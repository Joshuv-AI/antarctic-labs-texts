Small bowel diverticular disease: are we clinically aware?
Authors: Yujen Tseng, Jiayinaer Bolatihan, Xiao Li, Jiajing Yu, Dou Wang, Yuqin Jin, Lili Ma, Zhongguang Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background & objectiveSmall bowel diverticulum (SBD) is an outpouching of the small intestinal wall and is often neglected in clinical practice due to its undiscerning location. Majority of SBD can be asymptomatic but can also be associated with life-threatening complications. We hereby performed a clinical analysis to investigate the characteristics associated with SBD.Patients and methodsThis retrospective case series analyzed clinical data from two tertiary referral centers. Clinical data including baseline characteristics, clinical presentation, diagnostic methods and treatment were systematically collected and reviewed. Subgroup analysis based on distinct location of the SBD, such as the duodenum, jejunum and ileum were also performed.ResultsA total of 149 cases of SBD were ultimately included in the present study analysis. Among 114 duodenal SBD cases, 42 (36.8%) were asymptomatic, while 58 (50.9%) presented with pain/discomfort. In contrast, jejunoileal SBD (n = 35) were predominantly symptomatic: 21 (60.0%) required surgical intervention due to complications such as obstruction (n = 8), bleeding (n = 12), or perforation (n = 5). Endoscopic modalities (DBE/VCE) achieved a diagnostic accuracy of 92.3% for jejunoileal SBD, surpassing radiological methods (CT/BaFT) at 38.5% (p < 0.001). Distinctive case highlights also provided examples of SBD found at different locations.ConclusionSmall bowel diverticular (SBD) disease is often overlooked in clinical practice, due to its discrete location and limited access. With continuous diagnostic advancements, including endoscopic techniques, the detection of SBD is expected to rise, gaining clinical awareness amongst gastroenterologists.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04178-2.

Introduction

Diverticulum is an outpouching of the intestinal wall, which can occur anywhere throughout the gastrointestinal (GI) tract. Although majority of SBD can be asymptomatic and often discovered incidentally, it can also be associated with life-threatening complications such as infection, GI bleeding, and perforation. However, small bowel diverticula are often neglected in clinical practice due to its undiscerning location. Previous reports have estimated that the incidence of duodenal diverticula is approximately 7% in patients undergoing endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography.

(ERCP) and up to 20% in an autopsy series [1, 2]. While, jejunoileal diverticula are rare and have a reported incidence of 0.5 to 2.3% based on radiographic series and 7% in autopsy studies [3]. According to the pathogenesis, diverticulum can be divided into congenital diverticulum and acquired diverticulum. The most common congenital diverticulum is Meckel’s diverticulum, which is caused by incomplete atrophy of the vitelline duct during the embryonic period [4]. Acquired diverticulum can be divided into primary and secondary. Primary diverticulum is related to increased intra-abdominal pressure and is often accompanied by visceral and neuropathy. The main formation mechanism is intestinal smooth muscle atrophy and fibrosis, intestinal wall cystic dilatation, and weak muscle layer entering submucosa [5]. While secondary diverticulum is often caused by the involvement of adjacent tissue inflammation. Intestinal dysmotility and high intraluminal pressure may also play a role. Familial aggregation have been reported in case series, suggesting a probable genetic disposition [6, 7].

Clinical presentation of SBD can vary substantially, ranging from chronic abdominal discomfort to GI bleeding or perforation [8–10]. The reported complication rate associated with SBD ranges from 5 to 10% and is more common in jejunoileal diverticula compared to duodenal diverticula [11]. The clinical manifestation of SBD can vary substantially and have different degrees of severity. In milder cases, SBD can be asymptomatic, while some patients experience generalized abdominal pain and discomfort. More severe cases presented as small bowel obstruction, gastrointestinal bleeding, fever and infection, or acute abdomen, which often requires hospitalization. Due to the rare entity, SBD is not a usual suspect for clinical diagnosis, but often diagnosed incidentally on imaging or endoscopy. With the increased use and availability of diagnostic modalities for small bowel disease, such as double balloon enteroscopy (DBE) and video capsule endoscopy (VCE), SBD had been gaining clinical awareness [12, 13]. Asymptomatic SBD do not require active intervention, while treatment strategies of symptomatic disease is based on clinical presentation. Severe complications can be managed surgically, although recent reports have highlighted the role of endoscopic treatment for diverticular diseases [14, 15].

Currently, only few studies have analyzed and reported the characteristics of SBD in a patient cohort [2, 16]. More recent reports have been limited to individual case reports [13, 17]. This retrospective case series study aims to systematically analyze the clinical characteristics, diagnostic approaches, and therapeutic outcomes of SBD in a cohort from two tertiary centers. By focusing on the comparative efficacy of diverse diagnostic modalities for small bowel evaluation, our findings seek to enhance clinical awareness and optimize management strategies for this underrecognized condition.

Patients and methods

The inpatient hospital database of two tertiary referral centers, from January 2006 through December 2022 was retrospectively screened to identify all patients with small bowel diverticular disease. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients diagnosed with small bowel diverticula (SBD), located in the duodenum, ileum or jejunum, (2) Patients with available clinical data, including clinical presentation, diagnostic methods, and subsequent treatment. Patients with incomplete clinical or radiological data were excluded from the study analysis. Eligible patients were categorized into three subgroups based on the anatomical location of the diverticula (duodenal, jejunal, or ileal) to enable comparative analyses of clinical features and management strategies. Relevant clinical and radiological data were obtained for study analysis. Clinical data included clinical presentation of the patient upon admission, which can be classified into the following symptom groups: asymptomatic or incidental finding, pain or discomfort, bleeding, fever, obstruction, and acute abdomen. The patient history and hospital logs were meticulously screened to ensure that the chief complaint reported by the patient was associated with small bowel diverticular (SBD) disease. Patients were classified as asymptomatic if they had no clinical manifestations directly attributable to SBD (e.g., abdominal pain, bleeding, obstruction, or perforation) and no complications observed on imaging or endoscopic evaluation. Symptom relevance was determined through a multidisciplinary review involving gastroenterologists and radiologists, who assessed the temporal and anatomical correlation between reported symptoms and SBD findings. The location and number of diverticula were also recorded. Diagnostic methods used to identify the SBD, included radiological, endoscopic, or intraoperative diagnosis. Radiological diagnosis can be further divided into barium follow-through (BaFT) with X-ray or abdominal computer tomography (CT). While, endoscopic diagnosis was achieved by gastroscopy or duodenoscopy, double balloon enteroscopy (DBE), video capsule endoscopy (VCE) or in combination. All imaging and endoscopic findings were independently reviewed by two radiologists and two gastroenterologists to ensure diagnostic consistency. If more than one diagnostic approach was used, the specific modalities were documented. Treatment received were subsequently collected and can be divided into conservative therapy or surgical intervention. Conservative therapy included dietary modifications (low-residue diet), antibiotics for bacterial overgrowth, and analgesics for pain management. Any treatment associated complication were also detailed.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 27 (IBM Corporation, USA). Continuous variables were shown as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were shown as frequency (%). Comparison between continuous variables were achieved with the One-way ANOVA, while the χ2 test was used for categorical variables. All statistical analyses were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A retrospective analysis of the inpatient hospital database was conducted to identify eligible patients. A total of 543 cases of gastrointestinal diverticular disease were identified based on our hospital database from January 2006 through December 2022. Preliminary exclusion of 32 cases of esophageal diverticula, 10cases of gastric diverticular and 352 cases of colonic diverticula resulted in a total of 149 cases of small bowel diverticula and were ultimately included in the study analysis. Seventy-eight (52.3%)patients were male and seventy-one (47.7%) patients were female, with an average age of 62.70 ± 15.63 years old. Of the 149 cases of SBD, 114 (76.5%) patients had duodenal diverticula, 12 (8.1%) patients had jejunal diverticula, and 23 (15.4%) patients had ileal diverticula. Majority (133 cases, 89.3%) of SBD presented as a single diverticulum, while only 16 (10.7%) cases had multiple diverticula.

In order to further understand the distinctive clinical characteristics of SBD, an analysis was conducted based on different SBD locations. The study cohort was divided into duodenal SBD (n = 114), jejunal SBD (n = 12), and ileal SBD (n = 23). There was a significant difference in patient gender and age, specifically, patients with ileal SBD often present with clinical symptoms at a younger age (p < 0.001). Clinical manifestation varied significantly between SBD of different locations. Most duodenal SBD were asymptomatic and discovered incidentally or presented as nonspecific pain and discomfort. Duodenal SBD was rarely associated with severe sym

ptoms such as bleeding or perforation. In comparison, jejunal and ileal SBD were often symptomatic, prompting patients to seek medical attention. Obstruction, bleeding, and acute abdomen were common clinical manifestations of jejunal and ileal SBD. The two main types of diagnostic methods used to identify SBD were radiology and endoscopy. More than half of the SBD were diagnosed with endoscopy (57.7%), among which gastroscopy or duodenoscopy was the most commonly used (58.4%). A significant increase in diagnosis of SBD over the past decade was observed, which can be reflected by our experience (Fig. 1a). Due to the easily accessible location of duodenal SBD, a straight-forward diagnosis can often be made with direct visualization under gastroscopy or duodenoscopy. However, the horizontal and ascending segment of the duodenum remains a blind spot. Comparatively, the diagnosis of ileal and jejunal SBD is heavily reliant on targeted diagnosis with DBE and VCE (Fig. 1b). On the other hand, radiological studies can identify some SBD, however the low sensitivity hinders it clinical application, especially with the growing availability of endoscopic examinations.

Case highlights

The following case presentations demonstrates the different presentation and treatment of SBD found at different locations.

Patient A, 40-year-old, female, was admitted for mild acute pancreatitis. The patient’s symptoms and laboratory findings were consistent with the diagnosis of mild acute pancreatitis (MAP), however, no probable cause such as gallstones or hyperlipidemia, was identified. Her gastroscopy examination revealed a probable duodenal diverticulum. Subsequent EUS findings showed a large duodenal diverticulum and her duodenal papilla was located entirely within the diverticulum with an obscuring mucosal flap (Fig. 3). Therefore, her MAP could be explained by recurrent duodenal papillitis. Due to the congenital anomaly, surgical treatment was not indicated, and the patients continues to receive close follow-up at our outpatient service clinic.

Intra-diverticular papilla in a patient with recurrent chronic pancreatitis

Patient B, 59-year-old, female, complains of recurrent abdominal pain for over 20 year. She had received repeat gastroscopies and colonoscopies, but results were inconclusive. The patient was treated for functional dyspepsia and dysmotility, however, her symptoms were not alleviated. She sought further treatment at our hospital and received a VCE examination. Her results showed a single jejunal diverticulum (Fig. 4a, Video 1). A subsequent DBE examination, which revealed multiple diverticula throughout the proximal jejunum, with varying sizes (Video 2). The patient was recommended surgical treatment to remove the defect, intraoperative observations were consistent with DBE findings. A small intestinal segmentectomy from 10 to 70 cm below the Ligament of Treitz was performed. The largest diverticulum measured up to 4 cm in diameter (Fig. 4b). Pathology showed a break in the muscular layer of the diverticulum wall, confirming the diverticulum structed as a pseudo-diverticulum or “false diverticulum” (Fig. 4c). The patients reported relief of symptoms after the surgical procedure and continues with follow-up at our outpatient clinic.

Fig. 4Surgical imaging of multiple jejunal diverticula; pathology findings of surgical specimen; VCE imaging of SBD

Surgical imaging of multiple jejunal diverticula; pathology findings of surgical specimen; VCE imaging of SBD

Patient C, 38-year-old, male, complains of recurrent lower abdominal pain and bloating for 3 years. Small intestinal CT revealed thickening of the small bowel wall, with formation of ileal fistula and abscess, consistent with Crohn’s disease (CD) (Fig. 5a). The patient was admitted for DBE with a retrograde approach. Approximately 90 cm from the ileocecal valve, a diverticulum was observed with substantial scaring around the opening and a pinhole perforation at the base of the diverticulum (Video 3). The patient received small intestinal segmentectomy with complex adhesiolysis. The diverticulum measured 8 cm in length and 1 m in diameter (Fig. 5b). Adhesions was noted with the sigmoid colon on the posterior wall of the bladder. The patients reported relief of symptoms after surgery.

Patient D, 52-year-old, male, complains of recurrent melena for 7 days. CT imaging of the small intestine revealed no abnormalities, but capsule endoscopy showed a bleeding in the jejunum, suggesting a possible diverticulum with active bleeding. However, examination with a DBE, a 2.5 cm diverticulum was observed at the duodenal level, with exposed vessels at its base but no active bleeding was noted. Endoscopic suturing was commenced to treat the duodenal diverticulum (Fig. 6a). Five metal clips were used to secure a nylon suture to the diverticulum opening, and a grasping forcep was utilized to pull the free end of the diverticulum into the luminal cavity, allowing the nylon suture to tighten (Fig. 6b). Careful observations showed no significant bleeding or mucosal injury after the procedure. Often times, the location of lesion on CE can be difficult to interpret, resulting in the a false positive with regards to the location of the diverticulum. The patient experienced no unusual discomfort postoperatively and was discharged from the hospital. Follow-up small intestine endoscopy 3 months later revealed a diverticulum in the distal descending duodenum, with an enlarged opening, a flatter base, and scar formation (Fig. 6c). No significant hyperemia or exposed vessels were observed in the surrounding mucosa. The patient’s melena resolved postoperatively, and reported no other specific complaints.

Discussion

Small bowel diverticular (SBD) disease is often overlooked in clinical practice. Due to its discrete location and limited access, the true incidence of SBD remains unknown. Currently, many researchers have reported different clinical manifestation, treatment and complications of SBD, but has been limited to individual reports of cases studies or case series [18–20]. Available cohort studies from the past were unable to highlight the advancements of small bowel diagnostic modalities, such as DBE and VCE, in recent years [2, 16]. The present study is one of the few that demonstrates SBD and its associated characteristics in a single cohort population, with analysis of different clinical characteristics and diagnostic approach.

A total of 149 cases of SBD were included in the present study. Majority of the patients had duodenal SBD, while jeujunal ileal SBD were rare in incidence. SBD can be asymptomatic, or associated with nonspecific abdominal discomfort, due to bacterial overgrowth [9]. However, jejunoileal SBD are often associated with more severe clinical manifestations such as small bowel obstruction, bleeding or acute perforation. For instance, congenital small bowel diverticula like Meckel’s diverticulum are frequently linked to gastrointestinal bleeding. In contrast, acquired forms, such as pseudodiverticula (false diverticula lacking full bowel wall layers), are more commonly found in the duodenum and typically present with milder symptoms, including diverticulitis or complications like bacterial overgrowth. Due to the wide range of symptoms of SBD, it is not a commonly suspected disease in clinical practice, so it is still very difficult to diagnose SBD timely and accurately. Generalized abdominal discomfort may be dismissed as functional dyspepsia, as evident in our case highlights. GI bleeding may remain inconclusive after upper and lower GI endoscopic examinations. Therefore, the diagnosis of SBD is heavily reliant on diagnostic modalities of the small bowel, preferably with direct visualization. In clinical practice, traditional methods such as small intestinal CT or BaFT are usually used to diagnose SBD, but the above methods have poor diagnostic accuracy and cannot directly show the detailed characteristics of SBD. Double balloon enteroscopy (DBE) is an examination method that can directly visualize small intestinal lesions. Compared with traditional examination methods, double balloon enteroscopy has obvious advantages such as wide field of vision and clear image, and allows for biopsy and corresponding treatment at the lesion. Due to the lengthy anatomy of the small bowel, SBD may be easily missed in radiological studies, even with small intestinal CT or BaFT. Advancements in endoscopy has allowed for increased recognition of SBD in clinical practice, which is also an important tool to help differentiate common diseases of the small bowel, such as Crohn’s disease [17]. As presented in our case highlights, Patient C may have received treatment with corticosteroids or immunomodulators given the “severity” of his disease based on CT findings. Luckily, a DBE was performed for the patient and rectified his diagnosis, which was ultimately curative with surgery. Although DBE and VCE may not be available in primary or secondary healthcare facilities, we would like to emphasize the importance of considering small bowel disease, such as SBD, especially in patients with inconclusive diagnosis. The treatment for SBD should be directed based on clinical symptoms and location of SBD. Asymptomatic small bowel diverticulosis usually does not require treatment. Although ileal SBD often require surgical intervention, due to its association with more severe clinical presentation. The purpose of treatment is to relieve symptoms and prevent complications. The use of medicine and diet control can relieve dyspepsia and anemia caused by diverticulum. For those cases that respond to supportive treatment, surgery is not recommended to prevent recurrence [21]. Complications generally include bleeding, obstruction, and perforation, serious complications often require surgical intervention [22]. If Meckel’s diverticulum occurs during the operation as an incidental finding, the removal of Meckel’s diverticulum often requires further discussion [23]. Antibiotics can be prescribed for bacterial overgrowth, while surgery is reserved for SBD associated with severe complications. Several tertiary centers in China, with expertise in GI endoscopy and DBE, has been exploring the possibility of endoscopic resection of SBD with techniques modified from endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) and natural orifice transluminal endoscopic surgery (NOTES). According to the Ma LL’ case report, a novel technique called enteroscopic intestinal diverticulum dissection (EIDD) was proposed for the treatment of intussuscepted intestinal Meckel’s diverticulum [24].

There are several limitations to our study. First, this is a retrospective, two centers experience with limited number of patients. The incidence predicted by this study cohort may not be an accurate reflection of the general population. Selection bias may exist because the cohort included only hospitalized patients with prominent symptoms. Additionally, small-sized diverticula might be missed by current diagnostic modalities due to resolution limitations of imaging and endoscopic techniques, potentially affecting the estimation of the true incidence of SBD. While, asymptomatic SBD or outpatient SBD may be underestimated. The present study concluded that ileal diverticula may be associated with more severe symptoms and often present at a younger age. However, a subgroup analysis of Meckel’s diverticulum was not performed due to the limited number of patients. The diagnosis of Meckel’s diverticulum can only be confirmed with pathology, wherein all four layers of the intestinal wall are intact with ectopic gastric or pancreatic tissues. However, not all patients received surgical treatment for ileal diverticulum with available pathology reports. Due to the limited number of patients, we were unable to calculate the diagnostic rates of radiological and endoscopic examination, specifically the sensitivity, specificity, the positive and negative predictive values of each individual diagnostic modality. While our study provides insights into the clinical presentation and short-term management of SBD, the absence of longitudinal follow-up data limits the evaluation of long-term outcomes such as recurrence rates and delayed complications (e.g., diverticulitis, fistula formation). Future prospective studies incorporating standardized follow-up protocols are critical to establish evidence-based guidelines for long-term monitoring and secondary prevention.

SBD remains poorly recognized in clinical practice, due to its varying symptoms and discrete location. The diagnosis of SBD is heavily reliant on small bowel investigation such has VCE and DBE. Currently, there is no treatment consensus of small bowel diverticular disease. Future multi-center studies with a larger patient cohort is warranted for better understanding of the disease, including underlying mechanism and potential genetic disposition.
