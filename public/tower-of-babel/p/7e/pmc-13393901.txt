“Probably one of medicine’s best kept secrets”: preliminary qualitative insights into motivations and concerns regarding addiction medicine specialisation in Australasia
Authors: Yusra Tawfic, Ashlea Bartram, Jacqueline Bowden, Adrian Dunlop, Kirrilly Thompson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSubstance dependence is a significant health problem and major cause of preventable disease worldwide. Addiction Medicine (AM) specialists play an important role across numerous countries in the management and treatment of individuals with problematic substance use. However, there is currently a shortage of Fellows of the Australasian Chapter of Addiction Medicine (AChAM), with many reaching retirement age. This study aimed to inform AM attraction initiatives by identifying motivations, concerns, and suggestions for promoting the specialisation from the perspectives of current Fellows and Trainees. MethodsTwenty-two semi-structured interviews were undertaken with AChAM Fellows (n = 12) and Trainees (n = 10) across Australasia (Australia and New Zealand). Participants were asked about their motivations to specialise, concerns around specialising and suggestions for increasing the number of AM specialists. Transcripts were subject to qualitative data analysis and organised around an established four-factor framework for career choice.ResultsAll participants had followed a General Practitioner entry pathway. Seventeen different types of motivating factors were identified across four domains. For most, motivations included relationships with patients, support and encouragement from colleagues and supervisors, and an interest in AM. Concerns for specialisation were largely centred around generalist deskilling and pay reduction during training years. As many participants discovered AM in a serendipitous way, suggestions to grow the workforce are focused on increasing awareness and clinical placements.ConclusionsThis was the first study to consider Australasian General Practitioners’ motivations, concerns and attraction suggestions regarding AM specialisation. It identified a wide range of factors motivating General Practitioners across Australasia to specialise in AM, with limited concerns. To grow the workforce, there is a need for targeted promotional strategies that increase AM awareness while also addressing the motivations and concerns identified in this study.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09480-5.

Background

Substance dependence is a significant health problem and major cause of preventable disease worldwide. Its global impact has been described by the World Health Organization as “unacceptably high by any metric” [1]. Around one in 30 Australians (3.3%) met criteria for a 12-month substance use disorder in 2020–2022 [2]. Currently, there are effective treatments and services available which are not being implemented adequately on a global scale [1]. Addiction Medicine (AM) is one of these services, available in 47% of countries. It is an advanced medical specialisation in which doctors play a critical role in the management and treatment of individuals with problematic substance use [3]. Training and recognition of AM varies internationally, with some jurisdictions recognising addiction psychiatry but not AM as a distinct specialty [4–6]. Addiction psychiatry is an alternative subspecialty in which care focuses on co-occurring psychiatric and substance use conditions.

The Australasian Chapter of Addiction Medicine (AChAM) was established in 2001 and formally recognised by the Australian Government as a subdivision under the Royal Australasian College of Physicians (RACP) in 2009 [7]. Its aim is the ‘advancement of knowledge and expertise in addiction medicine’ as well as to provide an authoritative body for matters regarding alcohol, tobacco and illicit drugs [8]. The primary objective of its establishment was to support the health system’s engagement in addictive disorders, by legitimising this health condition and providing patients with specialist support [9]. AM is one of over 80 medical specialities in Australia and New Zealand. Each specialty has a specific training pathway to ensure clinicians have adequate knowledge and training in their area of interest. For AM, there are two main pathways: Basic Physician Training or completion of a fellowship of a wide variety of eligible faculties. Other disciplines are also considered on a case-by-case basis. After completion of either of these pathways, a three-year advanced training program is necessary to specialise in AM (see Fig. 1) [10]. The most common entry pathway is the General Practitioner pathway, through the Royal Australian/New Zealand College of General Practitioners. When AChAM was established, some clinicians with substantial prior experience were recognised as Fellows through a ‘grandparenting’ pathway.

In Australia, General Practitioners are funded through the national health insurance scheme, Medicare, with income derived largely from fee-for-service billings and, in some cases, supplemented by patient co-payments. Unlike salaried hospital-based roles, most General Practitioners operate as independent contractors, resulting in substantial variability in income depending on billing practices, patient population, and hours worked. Direct comparison of specific income figures is complicated by differences in data sources, employment models, and the structure of national occupational classification systems.1

In New Zealand, General Practitioners are funded differently, receiving primarily capitated payments from local health authorities rather than fee-for-service income. As Addiction Medicine is not yet recognised as a distinct specialty in New Zealand, clinicians with this qualification are not employed or remunerated accordingly. Consequently, there is no publicly reported salary for Addiction Medicine specialists with which to directly compare General Practitioner income.

There is a shortage of AM specialists in Australia, reflected globally [11, 12]. A 2021 audit of AChAM Fellows found that most were reaching retirement age [13]. As of 30 June 2024, there were 245 AChAM fellows and 68 trainees in Australia, with an average age of 62.0 years and 39.8 years respectively [pers. comm. John Saunders]. Concerns regarding the aging and small workforce have been raised by various state and national medical drug and alcohol groups in Australasia [14–16].

With limited research into motivating factors to undertake AM specifically, some insight is provided by studies that explore factors influencing medical students’ speciality selection more broadly. A common factor for specialty selection is the importance of a controllable lifestyle [17–22]. Additional factors influencing specialisation choice include experience on rotation [17, 20], role models [17, 18, 20, 21], career opportunities [17, 18], discipline interest [20–22], prestige [17, 18, 21], and the length and difficulty of training [17, 18, 22]. These factors are confirmed by both a systematic [21] and scoping review [22] which additionally demonstrate that personal, interpersonal, educational and life fulfillment influenced decisions to specialise. The extent to which these factors also contribute to motivations to specialise in AM are yet to be determined.

Some theories have been put forward to explain the shortage of AM specialists. In the United States of America, psychiatric residents told Soyka and Gorelick [23] that they were not interested in AM because of limited training and employment opportunities and concerns about long-term job security. In Australia, Van Beek [24] proposed three limiting factors for AM specialisation: little incentive (due to the social status of AM and financial limitations), stigma around patients (thought by some to be “criminals with self-inflicted problems”), and the long duration of training. Other contributing factors that have been proposed include, but are not limited to: limited recognition of the aging workforce, lack of specific strategies to help develop and recruit AM specialists, and a lack of strategies to continue to interest medical practitioners in this field [25]. While these factors may contribute to the lack of interest in the AM field, there is an equally important need to understand motivating factors. Accordingly, this study examined motivations, concerns and suggestions related to addiction medicine specialisation in Australasia.

Methods

We conducted a qualitative study using semi-structured interviews, appropriate for exploring unanticipated views, experiences and opinions of Addiction Medicine Fellows and Trainees in Australasia.

Sample and recruitment

As per the inclusion criteria, participants needed to be AChAM Trainees or Fellows. Invitations to participate were disseminated on behalf of the researchers by direct email from the RACP’s Member Engagement and Support department to all Fellows and Trainees of AChAM (approximately 300 members). We aimed for jurisdictional diversity across Fellows and Trainees. Preference was given to people currently working, although two semi-retired specialists were included in the sample to ensure jurisdictional representation. Specialisation pathway and prior recognition status were not inclusion or exclusion criteria. Participation was voluntary and informed consent was obtained from all participants. They were reimbursed with a $50 voucher in local currency (GiftPay for Australian participants, Prezzee for New Zealand participants). The desired sample size was approximately 20 participants as this is consistent with similar studies [26–29]. We continued to recruit participants until the research team agreed that sufficient depth had been provided in interviews to adequately address the research questions. This is consistent with the guiding principle of information power in qualitative studies [30].

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were undertaken with Fellows and Trainees online via Microsoft Teams. Two interview guides were created specifically for this study (provided in supplementary material) – one tailored for Fellows and the other for Trainees. Questions covered participants’ pathways to AM, hopes, motivations, concerns and suggestions for increasing recruitment. Interview guides were informed by the literature, refined with the assistance of an AM specialist (AD) and framed against the research aims. Interviews took place between November 2024 and March 2025, ranging from 28 to 71 min with a mean duration of 49 min. They were led by senior qualitative researcher (KT), who has experience in conducting qualitative research with different workforces [31], but does not have a medical background. On occasion, she was accompanied by a research trainee (YT), who is a post-graduate medical student. KT and YT debriefed after interviews to challenge interpretations and reduce analytic and hierarchy-related bias. All interviews were recorded and auto transcribed by Microsoft Teams. They were edited against audio recording and de-identified. Transcripts were returned to participants for review and optional redaction. No extensions were requested, and six transcripts were amended by participants (three Fellows and three Trainees).

Analysis and framework

Deidentified transcripts were imported into NVivo 15 to facilitate systematic qualitative data analysis (NVivo, released in March 2020 by QSR International Pty Ltd). Analysis used a directed qualitative content analysis approach [32] across three stages. In the first stage, transcripts were subject to deductive coding centred around the interview questions. In the second stage, subcodes were created inductively to collate similar responses. Stage three involved coding the motivations against Carpenter and Foster’s [33] framework for career choice which is comprised of four factors: intrinsic job characteristics, extrinsic job characteristics, interpersonal influences and choice by default. This framework was well suited to our study, as it enabled systematic organisation of influences on participants’ career decisions while preserving their perspectives. It has been applied in prior reviews of career choice [34].

In this study, we treated motivating factors as reasons participants described as influencing their decision to pursue (or continue) addiction medicine training. Intrinsic factors centred around personal interests, values and goals; extrinsic factors encompassed the desire for recognition and job security; and interpersonal factors related to the interplay of one’s career and their relationships, both within and external to the workplace. In Carpenter and Foster’s original formulation [33], ‘choice by default’ described entry via an alternative pathway when a preferred option was not pursued or not available. In the present study, choice by default refers to a dissatisfaction with current employment. Analysis was conducted by YT, with oversight from KT and regular review with KT and AB, reducing single coder bias.

The research protocol received ethics approval from Flinders University Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) (Project ID 7229), in accordance with the National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research.

They had experience working across all Australia jurisdictions (n = 18) as well as New Zealand (n = 4) and together had 355 years working in the AOD field (see Table 1). Intrinsic factors

Eight types of intrinsic factors were identified from interviews (Table 2). Most participants referenced their interest in AM generally, occasionally to their own surprise.“my first taste of the addiction medicine space …I wasn’t expecting much from it, but was really, really pleasantly surprised when I got there… I said to [an addiction medicine registrar]…I’m actually really enjoying this, like this is really, really interesting work.” (T1).

“my first taste of the addiction medicine space …I wasn’t expecting much from it, but was really, really pleasantly surprised when I got there… I said to [an addiction medicine registrar]…I’m actually really enjoying this, like this is really, really interesting work.” (T1).

The majority also discussed their intrinsic desire to work with vulnerable people facing the challenges of substance dependence.“The isolated people, isolated from society to a degree, and I always enjoy that - helping the people who need help essentially.” (T2).

“The isolated people, isolated from society to a degree, and I always enjoy that - helping the people who need help essentially.” (T2).

Participants valued building relationships with patients and being involved in their journey toward better health.“Working with alcohol and drug patients, they generally get well. And some of them made some big changes, they went from hustling and dealing through to I have teachers qualify as teachers, and people qualify in other professions …They’ve made some real changes.” (F1).

“Working with alcohol and drug patients, they generally get well. And some of them made some big changes, they went from hustling and dealing through to I have teachers qualify as teachers, and people qualify in other professions …They’ve made some real changes.” (F1).

Intrinsic factors were also altruistic, regarding the rewards that participants felt from seeing patients’ lives improve. Fellow 7 further valued their ability as an AM to have a positive impact on patients’ lives, noting:“Of all the areas of medicine, I think it’s the one that we can actually make one of the biggest difference to people’s lives.”

“Of all the areas of medicine, I think it’s the one that we can actually make one of the biggest difference to people’s lives.”

Some participants also appreciated the way in which AM allows people challenged by substance dependence to access care and support from a medical professional in a non-judgmental environment. Furthermore, specialists discussed their own desire for career progression – to increase their knowledge and formalise their training.“I also kind of realised that by doing the actual training program, it would probably enhance my knowledge and my skills.” (T7).

“I also kind of realised that by doing the actual training program, it would probably enhance my knowledge and my skills.” (T7).

AM was recognised as being quite different to ‘traditional medicine’, which appealed to many participants.“It is different to what, what I would probably consider to be traditional… mainstream areas of medicine. And when I say mainstream areas of medicine, the sort of things that you get exposed to routinely in medical school, you know, do you want to be a surgeon? Do you want to be a physician? Do you want to be a psychiatrist? …It just feels to me to be so pragmatic.” (F10).

“It is different to what, what I would probably consider to be traditional… mainstream areas of medicine. And when I say mainstream areas of medicine, the sort of things that you get exposed to routinely in medical school, you know, do you want to be a surgeon? Do you want to be a physician? Do you want to be a psychiatrist? …It just feels to me to be so pragmatic.” (F10).

One aspect of these differences was the diverse nature of AM work, providing a large amount of flexibility and ‘clinical freedom’ (F5), which many participants had not experienced in their previous roles.“It gives me quite a lot of flexibility in the work that I do… I am now doing different work and being involved in service development, training and supervision of our doctors…” (F3).

“It gives me quite a lot of flexibility in the work that I do… I am now doing different work and being involved in service development, training and supervision of our doctors…” (F3).

Extrinsic factors

Extrinsic factors included recognition, remuneration, job security and legitimisation of the AM field.

As a large proportion of Fellows and Trainees were already working in AM, formal recognition of their expertise was a key motivation for undertaking the specialisation. One Fellow described how they wanted:“that recognition that I had a specialised body of knowledge, and that I was working in a specialised area.” (F1).

“that recognition that I had a specialised body of knowledge, and that I was working in a specialised area.” (F1).

In addition, there was a desire for remuneration. In their prior role as General Practitioners, participants were often paid as sole traders which at times provided a level of financial instability. In contrast, they preferred a salaried role with “entitlements and paid annual leave” (F5).

Having an advanced specialisation also provided most participants with a level of job security. It ensured that in times of funding cutbacks, their job was prioritised and protected.“People who are the addiction medicine specialists are more likely to, you know, retain their jobs” (F11).

“People who are the addiction medicine specialists are more likely to, you know, retain their jobs” (F11).

While extrinsic motivations were centred around recognition and renumeration, they also included recognition of AM more broadly. This was in keeping with the altruistic nature of participants, that some were motivated to specialise as a political act – a way to legitimise the speciality and the issue of substance dependence. This allowed participants to advocate for social justice, particularly for a highly stigmatised patient group, as Fellow 7 argued:“I think as many of us, who can, should get certified in it so that it gains credibility as a speciality.” (F7).

“I think as many of us, who can, should get certified in it so that it gains credibility as a speciality.” (F7).

Interpersonal factors

Interpersonal factors influencing choice of specialisation were quite varied, with participants commenting both on the workplace itself as well as life outside of work. In reference to the workplace, peer and supervisor encouragement and support was discussed frequently. This encouragement often drew on AM being different to traditional medicine (intrinsic factor), with participants commenting on a sense of belonging, support and encouragement from supervisors.“It was definitely my first experience where someone in the hospital kind of showed an interest in my career and my future work, beyond me just being like like a paper monkey…I was in a, like, a small environment where people were like, what are you going to do? You should come and work with us, like writing references for me and kind of showing like an interest in me” (T1).

“It was definitely my first experience where someone in the hospital kind of showed an interest in my career and my future work, beyond me just being like like a paper monkey…I was in a, like, a small environment where people were like, what are you going to do? You should come and work with us, like writing references for me and kind of showing like an interest in me” (T1).

This notion of being included in a larger collective was also discussed regarding the opportunity to work within a multi-disciplinary team. This contrasted with prior experiences as General Practitioners, where some participants described feeling isolated.“We have this group of people that are supporting the individual and potentially their family … and you can’t do that on your own” (F9).

“We have this group of people that are supporting the individual and potentially their family … and you can’t do that on your own” (F9).

For many, there was a desire for a better work-life balance, as AM had more stable hours compared to other specialisations, requiring no shift work or on call hours.

“This is probably, if not the most work-life friendly job as a specialty” (T3).

“This is probably, if not the most work-life friendly job as a specialty” (T3).

Additionally, a few participants discussed their exposure to alcohol or drug dependence in their personal lives as a contributing factor in undertaking this work. For example, on fellow previous colleague struggled with addiction, and this “stimulated [their] interest in this addiction thing (F10).”

Choice by default

Choice by default was least commonly discussed as a type of motivation. This category reflected factors associated with dissatisfaction with general practice, more than positive attraction to addiction medicine specialisation. One of these factors was AM’s accessibility to those with a General Practice specialisation. Participants considered AM an advanced training that was “possible for a GP [General Practitioner] to get into” (F5). Trainee 8 stated:“without going back to do basic physician training, there’s really only two or three programs that you can do. I think you can do pain medicine, addiction and palliative care, maybe”.

“without going back to do basic physician training, there’s really only two or three programs that you can do. I think you can do pain medicine, addiction and palliative care, maybe”.

Concerns

The most frequently discussed concern about specialising in AM was deskilling. As all participants were General Practitioners prior to their specialisation, many had concerns around loss of the general skill set that they had developed over the course of their training and careers. However, this concern had not prevented them from choosing to specialise in AM. Some participants also reflected on a trade-off between their generalist specialisation and AM specialisation:“By not being generalist, you definitely do deskill. But then again, it’s like you deskill but then you upskill in other areas.” (T3).

“By not being generalist, you definitely do deskill. But then again, it’s like you deskill but then you upskill in other areas.” (T3).

However, when prompted to consider if their concerns had been borne out by their experience, most participants said that deskilling was inevitable even if they chose to stay a General Practitioner.“Just because your patient group will self-select to a degree that you will become better at something than others.” (T1).

“Just because your patient group will self-select to a degree that you will become better at something than others.” (T1).

Other concerns related to the financial and temporal commitment required to undertake the AChAM training program. For some, this consisted of a significant pay cut of about half (T3 and F3) and time spent studying outside of business hours. This had a greater impact on participants who were considering starting families or had young children. As such, participants in relationships where their partner was a high-income earner and could support the family during their training years were less impacted by these concerns. Most participants acknowledged that these sacrifices during training could be a deterring factor for other General Practitioners considering the specialisation. As Trainee 3 explained:“I’ve been privileged in my family to not need to be the primary income earner. But for many of my colleagues working as GPs [General Practitioners], I don’t think financially it would have been viable, especially when they’re up to this stage of their life when they’ve got a kid or multiple kids and other large, you know, mortgages to look after” (T3).

“I’ve been privileged in my family to not need to be the primary income earner. But for many of my colleagues working as GPs [General Practitioners], I don’t think financially it would have been viable, especially when they’re up to this stage of their life when they’ve got a kid or multiple kids and other large, you know, mortgages to look after” (T3).

In addition, the time commitment required for training (as shown in Fig. 1), particularly for those who were not previously working in full time roles was a consideration. This included working additional hours in combination with teaching and assignments.“You know I’ve got a family and kids and stuff at home too. And then just taking on the study as well, I was worried about how I’d fit that all in.” (T7).

“You know I’ve got a family and kids and stuff at home too. And then just taking on the study as well, I was worried about how I’d fit that all in.” (T7).

However, some participants noted they were fortunate enough to be well supported by their employer and the Chapter with recognition of previous learning.

“My training time has been cut down by 6 months in recognition of that prior learning.” (T4).

Suggestions

To increase attraction of medical professionals to the field of AM, participants suggested increased awareness and clinical placements, promotion of jobs, improvements and increased flexibility to training program and recognition of the speciality by the New Zealand Medical Council.

Most participants highlighted that their initial exposure to AM was serendipitous. They heard about AM specialisation incidentally.T9: I had no idea this existed. … It came about when I spoke to someone and they said you can get a fellowship in it and my heart leapt and I was like Oh my God, that would be amazing because I’ve always wanted to specialise.

T9: I had no idea this existed. … It came about when I spoke to someone and they said you can get a fellowship in it and my heart leapt and I was like Oh my God, that would be amazing because I’ve always wanted to specialise.

As for Fellow 6:“literally what happened is I went to a dinner party with some friends, at the dinner party was the husband of one of my friends who runs this addiction service and he suggested that I might consider coming to work for them.”

“literally what happened is I went to a dinner party with some friends, at the dinner party was the husband of one of my friends who runs this addiction service and he suggested that I might consider coming to work for them.”

It follows that the main suggestion made by participants to increase the number of AM specialists in Australasia was to increase awareness. For example,“No one hears about it and you can’t, you can’t be it if you can’t see it, if you don’t have any interaction.” (T1).

“No one hears about it and you can’t, you can’t be it if you can’t see it, if you don’t have any interaction.” (T1).

“I think if you plant the seed early, people will see it as a meaningful career.” (T10).

“I think if you plant the seed early, people will see it as a meaningful career.” (T10).

Participants were asked for their opinion about the most useful point in a medical career pathway to raise awareness of AM as a specialty pathway. They referred to career points for medical students, junior doctors and General Practice registrars. Participants also recognised the importance of AOD-related placements at these timepoints to provide experience of what AM entails and showcase the diversity of the role (e.g. community settings, inpatient settings). As one Fellow stated:“I think having someone sit down in a clinic with you for a week does way more than any stump speech about getting people on board” (F2).

“I think having someone sit down in a clinic with you for a week does way more than any stump speech about getting people on board” (F2).

Similarly, many participants suggested an increase in the promotion of AM jobs and provision of a clear career pathway. This would demonstrate the wide variety of opportunities available as an AM specialist.“Promoting having more jobs available out the other end that could stream them off into lab, different public health or great clinical positions – so there’s a professional pathway that’s in any of those areas.” (F8).

“Promoting having more jobs available out the other end that could stream them off into lab, different public health or great clinical positions – so there’s a professional pathway that’s in any of those areas.” (F8).

Others suggested improvements to the training program and support to engage in training. This included approval of external supervision to allow training to occur in areas where there are currently no AM specialists. Fellow 12 provided an example of how external supervision would increase both the number and geographical spread of AM Fellows.“There is no FAChAM in the South Island of New Zealand, or if there is they’re hundreds of kilometres away. So wouldn’t it be great to service the population of the South Island of New Zealand to actually train up someone to be a FAChAM?” (F12).

“There is no FAChAM in the South Island of New Zealand, or if there is they’re hundreds of kilometres away. So wouldn’t it be great to service the population of the South Island of New Zealand to actually train up someone to be a FAChAM?” (F12).

One Trainee provided insight into their positive training experience where they were undertaking a hybrid training model with external supervision (T9). They recommended making external training more accessible to other Trainees, as recognised by Fellow 12, quoted above.

Finally, participants from New Zealand described how the lack of recognition of AM as a speciality by the New Zealand Medical Council meant that AChAM Fellows were not required to be paid more, and therefore they must negotiate their wages with each new job opportunity.“And New Zealand because it’s not recognised as one of the recognised colleges within the country, I usually have negotiate that each time” (F1).

“And New Zealand because it’s not recognised as one of the recognised colleges within the country, I usually have negotiate that each time” (F1).

Whilst this had not deterred the four New Zealander participants from specialising, they reflected on its potential impact on other colleagues.“When we encourage Trainees in here, they can say, well, why should I, what’s in it for me?” (F1).

“When we encourage Trainees in here, they can say, well, why should I, what’s in it for me?” (F1).

Discussion

To provide a comprehensive understanding of factors impacting AM specialisation, we considered Fellow and Trainee motivations for - and concerns about - specialising, as well as their suggestions for increasing recruitment. Regarding motivations, we identified a complex interplay between being attracted to AM as well as looking to move away from General Practice. Our findings are broadly consistent with other literature on medical student specialisation, especially regarding the importance of work-life balance, exposure and discipline interest [17–22]. However, in contrast to previous research [17, 18, 21], prestige was not amongst the motivating factors discussed by our participants. Rather, participants tended to be altruistic and focused on patient care and advocacy. They aimed to support patients through recovery whilst also engaging in a political act to legitimise AM. Participants frequently framed their motivations in terms of advocacy, social justice and stigma reduction, suggesting values-based orientations that may differ from prestige-driven specialty choice. Our findings also provide further insight into motivations to specialise post-medical school. Most participants emphasised the importance of their relationships with patients, recognising that it was a key motivation for choosing AM. This likely reflects their prior experience working in the medical field which fostered a desire for patient focused care - an experience which medical students may not yet possess at the time of specialty selection.

Concerns around specialising in AM centred on the risk of deskilling and reduced income whilst training. This is consistent with Wilkinson et al. [35] who found that General Practitioners in Australia with special interests were also worried about losing their generalist skills. Given the limited literature on the concerns of undertaking advanced training, particularly post-General Practice, our identification of concerns about reduced income and time spent studying documents additional (albeit unsurprising) concerns that may have differentially impacted people at different stages of their life and medical career.

Importantly, the findings of this study also identify potential avenues for developing AM attraction strategies – especially regarding increased awareness of AM and considering the impact of potential losses to time and income whilst undertaking training. This is largely consistent with one scoping study that considered recruitment and retention strategies for rural generalism in high and low-middle income countries [36]. In that review, Noya et al. [36] found that education, increased exposure, and financial incentives were central to recruitment and retention for the rural and remote medical workforce. Their findings are supported by our study, with suggestions for increased awareness (particularly in medical school) and immersion (through clinical placements). However, unlike that review of the rural and remote medical workforce [36], the financial component to attraction for AChAM Fellows and Trainees was more focused on improving Trainee pay to offset income loss, as opposed to financial incentives. Participants were motivated by the job security and benefits of a salaried role, as opposed to an increase in their salary. This may reflect differences in the motivations emphasised within these workforces, with financial incentives appearing less central to the attraction of addiction medicine than to rural workforce recruitment. In addition, the call for formal recognition of AM by the New Zealand Medical Council was quite specific to the field, and therefore unique to our findings.

Increased awareness of AM is instrumental for attracting clinicians to the specialisation. This strategy was explicitly recommended by participants but also highlighted by their recollections of serendipitous encounters with AM specialists and/or encouragement from others. Findings suggest that awareness strategies should provide both information and experience, through clinical placements. Strategies should combine evidence-based research on the most successful awareness campaigns, with a focus on the motivations that led participants in this study to specialise in AM while also attending to concerns. Regarding to whom awareness initiatives should be targeted, participants suggested medical students, junior doctors and registrars, notably those in General Practice training. Focusing awareness campaigns on medical students and junior doctors supports existing research that early exposure increases interest [37, 38].

Awareness strategies should highlight the benefits of a career in Addiction Medicine reported by participants in this study. Rewarding patient care, work-life balance, and peer support are examples of pertinent motivations which could be used as a tool to promote AM. Participants repeatedly identified that the profound impact they have on patient’s lives was a key motivation for specialisation. As this sentiment was widely shared among participants, it suggests the altruistic nature of AM specialists. As such, this message could be intentionally incorporated into awareness campaigns to attract future specialists with similar values and motivations. Furthermore, our findings indicate that information about work-life balance and salaried benefits is likely to resonate with General Practitioner registrars. Vohra, Ladyshewsky and Trumble [39] found work-life balance was a key motivation for medical professionals to specialise as a General Practitioner. It could therefore be beneficial to highlight that this aspect is not lost with AM specialisation. As for remuneration, pay was a key ‘push’ factor away from being a General Practitioner. Thus, there could be an emphasis on the salaried benefits of this specialisation;a more stable income, leave entitlements and supported professional development leave. Furthermore, should General Practitioner registrars plan a pathway into AM training prior to the completion of their fellowship, they can choose to undertake AM related placements and receive Recognition of Previous Learning. This helps reduce the length of training and consequently the financial burden (less time will be worked at reduced pay).

Our finding that participants recognised increased clinical exposure as a crucial factor in attracting interest to the AM field reinforces existing literature which suggests that enjoyable rotation experiences and role models [17, 18, 20, 21] are key factors influencing specialty choice. This is further supported by Laurence et al. [40] and Vaidya et al. [41] who independently found that increased duration of clinical rotations had a positive correlation with medical student career decisions. Furthermore, clinical placements will expose trainees/students to the wide variety of potential AM roles – outpatients, rehabilitation facilities and consultant liaison. This could engage a broader range of potential specialists, recognising that each workplace appeals to different clinicians. Increasing awareness offers a promising foundation for supporting growth of the AM workforce.

Another approach that could be taken concurrently with increasing awareness of the AM specialty is addressing losses to time and income whilst undertaking AM training. As shown in Fig. 1, there is no direct pathway for the AM speciality, leading to increased training times. As such, promotion of recognition of prior learning, as discussed in relation to General Practitioner registrars above, could help address losses in time. It is also of note that specifically addressing losses of income while training – either raising trainee salaries or adjusting salary to reflect specific clinical experience – may resolve concerns which prevent some from undertaking this Fellowship. Another mechanism may be to focus promotion with Basic Physician Trainees (Fig. 1). Clinicians who enter advanced training immediately after Basic Physician Training are not qualified consultants (unlike General Practitioners). This means they will not experience a pay reduction in advanced training years, which may help reduce the financial burden of training.

Finally, a potential deterrent to specialising in AM in New Zealand is the lack of formal recognition by the New Zealand Medical Council. Gaining recognition could help remove this barrier for prospective Trainees, while also ensuring that current Addiction Medicine specialists are recognised for their training and expertise and thus appropriately remunerated.

Limitations and further research

There are two major limitations in relation to participant selection. First, all participants recruited were AChAM members. As participants had already entered training or Fellowship, views may reflect positive selection toward addiction medicine. We therefore recommend further research with those who considered but did not pursue the AM advanced training program. Second, an unintentional sample bias was that all participants had completed the General Practitioner training pathway prior to their specialisation in AM. The motivations, concerns and suggestions provided by participants may therefore have been reflective of their previous experiences as General Practitioners. While the General Practitioner pathway is the most common, findings of this study could be validated with AChAM Fellows and Trainees who have entered through alternate pathways (Fig. 1).

To optimise attraction strategies, further research with students and specialists should be undertaken to co-design AM awareness promotion material. In addition, further investigation into the barriers and enablers to AOD-related placement opportunities for medical students and junior doctors could be beneficial. Finally, the strength of this study was exploratory qualitative approach which identified factors which had been previously undocumented in Australasia. Further survey-based research with a larger and more diverse sample of clinicians would determine the generalisability of concerns and motivations recalled by this relatively modest sample.

Conclusions

This study offers novel insights into the interplay of values, experience, and exposure in advanced specialty decision-making, drawing on Australasian general practitioners’ motivations, concerns, and attraction suggestions to AM specialisation. It identified numerous motivating factors that could be incorporated into AM attraction initiatives. Overall, findings illustrated the ways in which AM provides a rewarding career pathway with a work-life friendly lifestyle, especially for those who are driven by social justice, advocacy and rewarding relations with patients. However, a basic lack of awareness that the speciality exists suggests that attraction strategies should prioritise promotion and increase opportunities for AOD-related clinical placements. Further research is needed to validate these findings with a larger and more diverse sample, especially including those from non-Generalist pathways and those who chose other specialisations over AM. Nonetheless, insight into the motivations, concerns and suggestions of AChAM Fellows and Trainees provided by this study reveal attraction strategies necessary to grow this instrumental workforce, and thereby support those impacted by substance dependence.
