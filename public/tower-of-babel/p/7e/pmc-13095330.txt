A nurse-led cross-sectional latent profile analysis of fear of cancer recurrence in patients with non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer
Authors: Yuanting Hao, Linyan Niu, Yi-An Shih, Pengxia Gao, Yongcai Wu, Wanyan Song, Chong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To identify distinct latent profiles of fear of cancer recurrence (FCR) among patients with bladder cancer and to examine sociodemographic and modifiable psychosocial correlates (self-management and social support) from a nursing perspective. We conducted a nurse-led cross-sectional study of patients with non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer in northern China (August 2023–October 2024). Participants completed a general demographic questionnaire, the fear of progression questionnaire-short form, the Self-management Assessment Scale for Cancer Patients, and the Social Support Rating Scale. Latent profile analysis was used to derive FCR profiles, and multinomial logistic regression examined differences in demographic and clinical variables across profiles. Three hundred thirteen bladder cancer patients recruited. Latent profile analysis revealed 2 distinct FCR profiles: “High-FCR Dysregulated Group” (66.5%) and the “Low-FCR Family-Concerned Group” (33.5%). Compared to the low-FCR class, the high-FCR class was more likely to be female, have higher educational levels, and report a lower monthly per capita household income. Furthermore, patients in the high-FCR group demonstrated significantly lower scores in both self-management and social support (P < .05). Fear of cancer recurrence among patients with non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer is heterogeneous. Latent profile analysis may assist nurses in identifying subgroups with distinct psychosocial characteristics. Lower levels of self-management ability and social support were associated with higher FCR profiles, suggesting potential targets for future nurse-led psychosocial interventions.

1. Introduction

Bladder cancer (BC), a malignancy arising from the urothelium, stands as the 9th most common cancer globally, with an estimated 614,000 new cases in 2022 alone.[1] A defining and challenging feature of this disease is its pronounced recurrence rate. For patients with non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC), which constitutes approximately 75% of all cases, the risk of recurrence within 5 years can be as high as 50% to 70%, creating a state of perpetual uncertainty and psychological vulnerability.[2] This clinical trajectory is inherently heterogeneous. Patients may undergo a range of treatments, from repeated transurethral resections of bladder tumors with intravesical therapy for NMIBC, to life-altering radical cystectomy for muscle-invasive disease. Regardless of the pathway, the threat of recurrence looms large. Those with NMIBC face the anxiety of potential progression to muscle-invasive disease,[3] while cystectomy patients must cope with profound alterations in bodily function and self-image.[4] It is within this context of pervasive and variable threat that fear of cancer recurrence (FCR) emerges as a critical, yet underexplored, psychological burden for BC patients.

Existing literature has identified multiple factors associated with FCR across cancer types, including younger age, higher disease stage, and lower social support.[5,6] Crucially, protective psychosocial factors such as self-management abilities and stronger social support have been recognized.[7] However, a significant limitation persists: the predominant reliance on variable-centered analyses.[8] These approaches assume population homogeneity and examine average relationships, which may mask the existence of distinct, clinically meaningful subgroups of patients with unique constellations of FCR symptoms.

This gap is particularly salient in BC. The diverse and often protracted treatment course suggests that patients’ experiences of FCR are unlikely to be uniform. Yet, research specifically examining the heterogeneity of FCR in this population is scarce. A person-centered approach, such as latent profile analysis (LPA), is urgently needed to move beyond population-level averages. LPA can identify homogeneous subgroups (“profiles”) based on their patterns of responses across multiple FCR dimensions, thereby capturing the nuanced reality of patient experiences.

This study directly responds to the call for “Nurses Advancing Oncology Research” by addressing this gap through a distinct nursing lens. While FCR is a universal concern, it is the oncology nurse who is uniquely positioned at the bedside to witness its varied manifestations.[9] The task of identifying this heterogeneity and investigating modifiable factors, such as self-management and social support, represents a critical step in translating clinical observations into systematic, evidence-based nursing practice.[10] It exemplifies the essential role of nursing science in bridging clinical problems with research-driven solutions, moving from merely identifying distress to proactively informing personalized care.

Therefore, to address these gaps and advance the role of nursing science in psycho-oncology, this nurse-led, cross-sectional study aims to: identify distinct latent profiles of FCR in a sample of BC patients using a person-centered analytical approach; investigate how demographic and clinical characteristics predict profile membership; and examine differences in key modifiable psychosocial factors (namely, self-management abilities and social support) across the identified profiles. The ultimate goal is to move beyond a one-size-fits-all approach and generate an empirical foundation for developing a tailored, feasible, and nurse-driven intervention model to effectively address the multifaceted fear of recurrence in this vulnerable population.

2. Methods

2.1. Study and sample

This nurse-led cross-sectional study was designed to identify latent profiles of FCR and examine associated influencing factors among patients with BC. Using a convenience sampling approach, participants were recruited from the urology departments of 2 tertiary grade-A hospitals in the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, China, between August 2023 and October 2024. Inclusion criteria required participants to be: aged 18 years or older; clinically and pathologically diagnosed with NMIBC; able to communicate in Mandarin Chinese; fully aware of their disease condition and medical treatment; and willing and able to provide written informed consent. Patients were excluded if they had: psychiatric disorders; hearing impairments; or multiple metastatic tumors. The design and reporting of this study followed the STROBE statement 14 (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology).

Sample size estimation was determined by the requirements for both LPA and multinomial logistic regression requirements. According to Nylund-Gibson and Choi,[11] a minimum of 300 participants is recommended to ensure robust profile identification. For multinomial logistic regression, a sample size of 10 to 15 times the number of independent variables is considered adequate.[12] With 26 potential predictors, the minimum required sample size ranged from 260 to 390 participants. Accounting for an anticipated 10% attrition rate, the target sample size was estimated to be between 289 and 429 participants.

2.2. Procedure

Data collection was led and conducted by a team of 3 nursing master’s students, who were specifically trained for this study and had previously established therapeutic rapport with patients through clinical placements on the urology wards. This preexisting relationship facilitated trust and engagement during the recruitment process. The trained nursing master’s students conducted daily screenings of potential participants by reviewing the hospitals’ electronic medical records against the study’s inclusion and exclusion criteria. Upon confirming eligibility, they approached the patients in person to obtain written informed consent. Questionnaires were completed anonymously in private ward settings. For participants with reading or vision difficulties, researchers read each item verbatim in neutral language without providing interpretation or guidance. Each questionnaire required approximately 20 to 30 minutes to complete. Upon collection, researchers checked for missing items and verified data completeness with participants. Socio-demographic and clinical data were cross-checked with medical records to ensure accuracy.

2.3. Measurements

2.3.1. Socio-demographic and disease characterization questionnaire

A self-designed form was used to collect demographic and disease-related information, including gender, age, marital status, religious belief, educational level, average monthly household income per capita, occupational status, presence of comorbid chronic diseases, and family history of bladder tumors.

2.3.2. Fear of cancer recurrence

The fear of progression questionnaire-short form was developed by Mehnert et al[13] based on the original FoP-Q by Herschbach and colleagues. The Chinese version was adapted and validated by Wu Qiyun et al,[14] demonstrating strong psychometric properties. The 12-item instrument assesses fear of disease progression or recurrence across 2 dimensions (physical health and social/family concerns) using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never to 5 = always). Total scores range from 12 to 60, with higher scores indicating greater fear. A Cronbach α of 0.92 in the Chinese version indicates excellent internal consistency, supporting its applicability in Chinese cancer populations.

2.3.3. Disease self-management ability

Disease self-management was evaluated using the Self-Management Assessment Scale for Cancer Patients, developed by Cheng Lingling and colleagues in China.[15] This original Chinese scale comprises 44 items across 6 domains (daily life, symptom management, psychological adjustment, communication, information management, and self-efficacy) rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Higher scores indicate better self-management ability. In the present study, the scale showed excellent reliability (Cronbach α = 0.959), confirming its suitability for assessing the self-management capacity of cancer patients during treatment and rehabilitation.

2.3.4. Social support level

Social support was measured using the Social Support Rating Scale developed by Xiao Shuiyuan in 1994.[16] This 10-item Chinese instrument assesses 3 dimensions: subjective support, objective support, and utilization of support. The total score ranges from 12 to 66, with higher scores reflecting stronger perceived social support. Previous studies have reported Cronbach α coefficients around 0.82, indicating good reliability. The Social Support Rating Scale has been widely applied in Chinese clinical and psychological research and is recognized as a valid and reliable measure of perceived social support.

2.4. Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk) and Mplus version 8.3. Descriptive statistics (mean ± SD, frequency, percentage) were used to summarize variables. LPA was conducted to identify distinct latent profiles of FCR. In this study, LPA was performed using the scores from the 12 items of the fear of progression questionnaire-short form to identify distinct FCR latent profiles. Model comparison proceeded sequentially for 1 to 5 profiles based on model fit indices: the Akaike information criterion, the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and the sample-size-adjusted BIC, where the smallest values indicate the optimal fit. Entropy values (range from 0–1) were used to evaluate classification accuracy; values ≥0.80 were considered to indicate good separation. Model improvement was tested using the Lo–Mendell–Rubin (LMR) adjusted likelihood ratio test and the bootstrap likelihood ratio test. A statistically significant P < .05 suggested that the k-profile model provided a better fit than the (k–1)-profile model.[17]

Following the determination of the optimal profile solution, one-way ANOVA and Chi-square tests were applied to compare socio-demographic and clinical characteristics across profiles. Subsequently, unordered multinomial logistic regression was used to explore predictors of FCR profile membership, with the lowest-FCR group serving as the reference category. Variables significant at P < .10 in univariate analyses were entered into the regression model. Statistical significance was set at P < .05 (two-tailed). Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors, with all variance inflation factor values <2, indicating no significant multicollinearity. To address sparse cell counts and ensure the stability of the regression model, educational attainment was collapsed into 2 categories (lower vs higher education) prior to inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression analysis.

Missing data on key study variables were minimal (5%). Cases with missing values were handled using listwise deletion.

3. Results

3.1. Characteristics of participants

Among the 313 BC patients, the gender distribution was as follows: 243 males (77.6%) and 70 females (22.4%). In terms of age: 56 patients (17.9%) were aged 18 to 49 years, 161 patients (51.4%) were aged 50 to 70 years, and 96 patients (30.7%) were over 70 years old. Regarding marital status: 278 patients (88.8%) were married, 7 patients (2.2%) were unmarried, 8 patients (2.6%) were divorced, and 20 patients (6.4%) were widowed. Regarding educational level: 74 patients (23.6%) had primary school education or below, 119 patients (38.0%) had junior high school education, 64 patients (20.5%) had senior high school or technical secondary school education, 19 patients (6.1%) had junior college education, 26 patients (8.3%) had undergraduate education, and 11 patients (3.5%) had postgraduate education or above. Occupational status was as follows: 125 patients (40.0%) were farmers, 67 patients (21.4%) were employed, 102 patients (32.6%) were retired, and 19 patients (6.1%) were unemployed. The average monthly household income per capita was as follows: 87 patients (27.8%) earned less than 2000 yuan, 159 patients (50.8%) earned between 2000 and 5000 yuan, and 67 patients (21.4%) earned >5000 yuan. Regarding comorbid chronic diseases: 219 patients (70.0%) had none, while 94 patients (30.0%) had 1 or more. Regarding family history of bladder tumors: 295 patients (94.3%) had no family history, while 18 patients (5.8%) did.

3.2. Latent profile analysis results

3.2.1. Establishment of the latent profile model for patients’ FCR

Before conducting LPA, Harman single-factor test was performed to assess common method bias. The results revealed that 13 common factors with eigenvalues >1 were extracted from the exploratory factor analysis, with the maximum common factor explaining 19.177% of the variance (the 40% threshold). Therefore, the relationships between variables in this study were minimally influenced by common method bias.

Initially, 4 models were fitted. As the number of profiles increased, the Akaike information criterion, Bayesian information criterion, and sample size-adjusted BIC continuously decreased. However, the fit indices from the LMR test were not significant for the 3-Profile and 4-Profile models, indicating that an excessive number of Profiles had been extracted. Considering the sample size per Profile, model parsimony, and interpretability of the Profiles, the 2-Profile model was deemed optimal, showing marked improvement in fit, clear profiling, and significant LMR and bootstrap likelihood ratio test results (Table 1). 3.2.2. Naming of each latent profile

Based on the determination of latent profiles, the 2 latent profiles were named according to the mean scores of the 2 profiles on the 12 items related to FCR (Fig. 1). Except for Item 11, “I worry about what will happen to my family if something happens to me,” the mean scores of Profiles 1 were all below 2.5. In contrast, the mean scores of Profiles 2 on each item were at a higher level compared to Profile 1, particularly for Item 1, “I become anxious when I think the disease may progress,” Item 2, “I feel nervous before doctor’s appointments and regular checkups,” Item 9, “I worry that there will be some major treatments during the course of the disease,” and Item 11. Excluding the comparability of Item 11 between the 2 Profiles, based on the other 3 items where Profile 2 had significantly higher scores and the overall mean score being at a high level, Profile 2 was named the “High-FCR Dysregulated Group,” which comprised 66.5% of the total study population. Correspondingly, Profile 1 was named the “Low-FCR Family-Concerned Group,” accounting for 33.5% of the overall study population.

General demographic information, scores for disease self-management ability, and scores for social support were treated as independent variables. 4. Discussion

Addressing the critical gap in understanding the heterogeneity of FCR among BC patients, this nurse-led study is the first to employ LPA to identify distinct psychological phenotypes. We successfully delineated 2 latent profiles: a “High-FCR Dysregulated Group” (66.5%) and a smaller “Low-FCR Family-Concerned Group” (33.5%). This empirical classification, grounded in Mishel Uncertainty in Illness Theory, suggests that FCR in this population is not a monolithic experience but manifests in qualitatively distinct patterns, with the majority of patients enduring significant emotional distress.

4.1. Psychological characteristics of the 2 Profiles

The 2 psychological profiles identified in this study diverge in how illness-related information is organized and appraised. The high-FCR dysregulated group shows marked anticipatory tension before surveillance, persistent treatment worry, and strong affective reactivity to the possibility of progression. Framed by Mishel Uncertainty in Illness Theory, these features indicate a compromised stimulus frame (low familiarity with symptom meanings, weak congruence between expectations and clinical signals, and unstable temporal patterning of care) which promotes an appraisal of uncertainty as danger and, in turn, hypervigilance and distress.[18] Limited “structure providers” and thinner social support further constrain coping resources, magnifying perceived uncertainty and undermining a sense of control, patterns consistently linked with higher FCR and worse mental health outcomes.[19]

By contrast, the low-FCR family-concerned group appears to maintain a more coherent stimulus frame characterized by predictable follow-up routines, consistent explanations, and accessible structure providers. Such communication-rich environments and supportive care processes are associated with lower distress and more adaptive appraisals of uncertainty as manageable risk or even opportunity.[20] Contemporary trials and practice guidance suggest that navigation programs and teach-back-based education can stabilize emotions, support self-regulation, and reduce symptom burden, thereby diminishing FCR-relevant uncertainty in day-to-day care.[21,22] Residual worry in this subgroup centers on role obligations and the family’s future rather than on uncontrollable disease trajectories, aligning with recent caregiver meta-analytic evidence that “family-burden” cognitions remain salient drivers of vigilance and worry.[23]

Taken together, these patterns map onto different points along Mishel pathway (stimulus frame and structure providers shaping uncertainty, uncertainty feeding into danger versus opportunity appraisals, and appraisals steering adaptation) clarifying why 1 subgroup remains locked in high FCR while the other constrains FCR to a manageable band despite ongoing family concerns.[24] The findings reinforce practice priorities emphasized in recent oncology guidance: reduce ambiguity, improve informational congruence and predictability in follow-up, and systematically embed supportive communication and navigation to strengthen patients’ cognitive and social resources.[25]

4.2. Influencing sociodemographic factors

Gender, educational attainment, and income were significant predictors of FCR profiles (P < .05). Female patients were more likely to be classified into the high-FCR group, consistent with recent evidence that women frequently report higher levels of FCR across cancer populations.[10,26] This pattern underscores the value of gender-responsive nursing strategies (e.g., oncology nurses providing brief emotion-focused counseling and mindfulness-based skills training) when preparing patients for surveillance and uncertainty management.

Notably, higher educational attainment (dichotomized as higher vs lower education) was strongly associated with high-FCR profile membership. Although counterintuitive, this paradox has been observed in oncopsychology research. Studies in stomach and breast cancer contexts have reported that higher education can co-occur with heightened fear of progression, possibly because greater information-processing capacity increases exposure to risk-laden details and heightens threat appraisal.[27,28] Contemporary communication science also shows that intensive health information seeking is associated with increased cancer worry and that “information overload” can escalate anxiety when clinical messaging is complex or incongruent.[29,30] These findings suggest that nurses should calibrate educational communication to minimize cognitive overload (using plain language, teach-back, and risk-graphics) to deliver accurate information without inadvertently amplifying perceived threat.

Higher monthly per-capita household income was associated with lower FCR, indicating that material security may buffer psychological distress through reduced financial toxicity and better access to supportive resources. Recent dyadic and patient studies link financial strain with greater FCR, partly via diminished social support, whereas economic stability promotes a sense of control and adherence.[31] In practice, routine screening for financial hardship and timely referral to social assistance or navigation services should be integrated into holistic FCR management for lower-income patients.

4.3. The role of self-management and social support

This study highlights self-management ability and social support as potentially modifiable psychosocial factors that may be considered in the development of future nurse-led supportive care strategies. Logistic regression analysis indicated that each one-point increase in self-management ability was associated with a 4% reduction in the likelihood of being classified into the high-FCR group, while each one-point increase in social support corresponded to a 10% reduction. These findings emphasize that strong self-management skills (covering symptom control, emotional regulation, information seeking, and healthcare communication) enhance patients’ sense of mastery and predictability, reducing fear and uncertainty regarding recurrence.[8]

Similarly, social support functions as an external coping resource, providing emotional comfort, informational guidance, and tangible assistance. Supportive relationships foster resilience and decrease the perceived isolation that often exacerbates FCR.[27] Although causal relationships cannot be inferred from this cross-sectional study, these results suggest that self-management and social support warrant consideration in the design and evaluation of future nurse-led supportive care interventions. The nurse team may thus consider both domains through structured self-management training and family-inclusive educational interventions.[32]

4.4. Theoretical and research significance

By applying LPA, this study transcends traditional variable-centered analyses and provides a person-centered understanding of FCR heterogeneity in BC patients.[33] It reveals that FCR arises from a complex interaction among individual, social, and contextual factors rather than a uniform linear process. This perspective aligns with holistic nursing models emphasizing individualized care and context-sensitive assessment.[29] Moreover, the identification of modifiable factors such as self-management and social support highlights potential direction for future intervention development and evaluation to enhance psychological adaptation and improve quality of life among cancer survivors.[12]

4.5. Strength and limitation

A key strength of this study lies in its use of LPA to capture hidden subgroups within the BC population, offering more precise insights into the diverse manifestations of FCR. The integration of self-management and social support further deepens understanding of the psychosocial dynamics shaping FCR, enriching the evidence base for nursing practice. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, data were collected from 2 tertiary hospitals in northern China, which may limit generalizability due to regional and cultural variability. Future research should expand to multi-center and cross-regional samples to enhance external validity. Second, reliance on self-reported questionnaires may introduce recall and social desirability bias. Employing mixed-methods approaches that combine quantitative assessment with qualitative interviews could yield a more comprehensive view of patients’ lived experiences. Finally, the cross-sectional design restricts causal inference; longitudinal studies are warranted to examine how FCR evolves over time and to assess the long-term effectiveness of nursing interventions. Additionally, educational attainment was dichotomized to ensure model stability in regression analysis, which may have reduced granularity in capturing nuanced educational differences across participants.

4.6. Implications for nursing practice

The findings of this nurse-led profiling study provide empirical insights that may inform the future development of tailored nursing support strategies for patients experiencing FCR. A person-centered profiling approach can assist nurses in recognizing patients who may require enhanced psychosocial assessment and support. Importantly, self-management ability and social support emerged as key differentiating factors between profiles, suggesting that these domains warrant attention in future intervention development and testing. However, given the cross-sectional design, causal inferences cannot be drawn, and intervention effectiveness should be evaluated through longitudinal or experimental studies.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, this nurse-led study provides initial empirical insights into distinct latent profiles of FCR among BC patients. By identifying a predominant high-FCR dysregulated profile alongside a low-FCR family-concerned profile, the findings enhance understanding of the heterogeneity underlying FCR experiences. Rather than advocating immediate practice change, these results offer an empirically grounded framework that may inform future development and evaluation of precision-oriented nursing assessment and supportive care approaches. By highlighting self-management ability and social support as key differentiating factors, this study contributes to hypothesis generation and supports the need for longitudinal and interventional research to determine effective, phenotype-sensitive nursing strategies.
