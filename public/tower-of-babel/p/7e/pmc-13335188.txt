Evaluation of Ag-, Fe- and Ni-exchanged mordenite for the reduction of 4-nitrophenol under different reduction pathways
Authors: Ikram Yssaad, Fatiha Hamidi, Denis Luart, Francesc Medina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mordenite (MOR) zeolite was hydrothermally synthesized and subsequently modified via ion exchange with Ag+, Fe3+, and Ni2+ ions. The resulting catalysts were evaluated for the reduction of 4-nitrophenol (4-NP) using two approaches: chemical reduction in the presence of NaBH4 and continuous-flow catalytic hydrogenation in an H-Cube Pro reactor (ThalesNano) using hydrogen generated by water electrolysis. The materials were comprehensively characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), nitrogen adsorption at 77 K, X-ray fluorescence (XRF), infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), UV-Vis spectroscopy, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Characterization results showed that the mordenite framework remained intact after metal incorporation, although slight decreases in textural properties were observed. Among the catalysts, AgMOR exhibited the highest activity for the NaBH4mediated reduction of 4-NP, achieving 98% conversion, which is attributed to the high dispersion and accessibility of silver species within the zeolite channels. Under continuous-flow hydrogenation, AgMOR, NiMOR, and FeMOR achieved conversions of 89%, 92%, and 60%, respectively, demonstrating the benefits of continuous-flow operation and the critical role of metal–support interactions in determining catalytic performance.

Introduction

Over the past few decades, industrial production has steadily increased, resulting in higher levels of pollution in the environment and leading to serious problems. Organic dyes and phenolic compounds are recognized as a crucial group of chemicals in global industry and are classified as priority pollutants because a potential hazard is posed when they are discharged and because they are difficult to biodegrade.1 As a consequence, risks may be induced for the environment and living organisms, particularly in aquatic systems, where eutrophication and a reduction in sunlight penetration into water bodies can be observed. This phenomenon leads to the inhibition of photosynthesis and aquatic flora activity and, consequently, the nutritional supply of aquatic life is hindered.2 Among the various phenolic compounds, 4-nitrophenol is identified as one of the most common and is listed as one of the 114 major organic pollutants among nitroaromatic compounds by the United States Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA).3 Its presence has been reported in surface water, groundwater, and agricultural water due to its extensive use in dyes, textiles, leather, pesticides, and pharmaceuticals. In addition, it is employed as a synthetic intermediate in the manufacture of protective agents.4,5 When released into rivers, water contamination and environmental degradation are caused, and risks to human health may be generated due to its persistence in biological systems.

On the other hand, 4-nitrophenol (4-NP) can be reduced to 4-aminophenol (4-AP), a less toxic derivative that is widely used in the pharmaceutical industry for the production of analgesic and antipyretic drugs such as paracetamol and acetaminophen. It is also applied in photographic developers, corrosion inhibitors, sulfur and azo dyes, agricultural products, rubber, and animal feed.4,6,7

Considerable research has been devoted to the development of methods that are both effective and economically viable for the reduction of nitroaromatic compounds. Several approaches have been proposed for the degradation of 4-nitrophenol (4-NP), including adsorption, photocatalysis, microbiological processes, electrochemical methods, and nitroreduction. Adsorption, although a simple physical process in which the pollutant is trapped on the surface of a solid, is often characterized by slow kinetics and incomplete removal.8 Photocatalysis is associated with the need for expensive light sources and complex operational control.9,10 Microbiological approaches are based on the transformation or degradation of pollutants by microorganisms but are frequently limited by the slow rate of biological processes.11 Electrochemical methods are considered promising alternatives; however, strict control of electrochemical parameters is required.12 Among these techniques, nitroreduction is distinguished by its efficiency and simplicity, allowing the selective reduction of nitroaromatic compounds under mild conditions through an environmentally friendly protocol.13,14

Noble metals such as palladium (Pd),13,15 platinum (Pt),16 gold (Au),5,17 and silver (Ag)18,19 are widely regarded as highly active catalysts for the reduction of 4-nitrophenol. Although high catalytic activity is exhibited, metal nanoparticles, owing to their small size and high surface energy, are prone to aggregation and leaching during reactions. As a result, a gradual loss of catalytic activity and stability is observed during the reduction of 4-NP.

To address these limitations, increasing attention has been directed toward catalysts based on non-noble metals such as nickel (Ni) and iron (Fe), due to their abundance, low cost, and satisfactory catalytic performance.20 Nevertheless, these nanoparticles are also affected by agglomeration and morphological instability during hydrogenation reactions, which leads to reduced catalytic efficiency. To mitigate these drawbacks, metal nanoparticles, whether noble or non-noble, are commonly dispersed or confined within support materials. Porous silica nanospheres,16 double-layer hydroxides,21 zeolites,22 CoS nanoparticles,23 nanolignin,24 and nanocarbons25 have been employed as supports to enhance nanoparticle dispersion, stability, and catalytic efficiency.

Zeolites are defined as microporous crystalline aluminosilicate materials that are extensively used in domestic, medical, and industrial applications. Their remarkable properties have enabled widespread use in catalysis, separation, adsorption, and ion exchange processes.26–28 Owing to their uniform pore structure, cage-like architecture, and high specific surface area, zeolites are considered particularly suitable for catalytic and adsorption applications.

Zeolites exchanged with transition metal ions are known to exhibit enhanced catalytic and adsorptive properties, making them effective materials for the removal of organic pollutants and pharmaceuticals from wastewater.29 Several strategies have been developed to disperse metal nanoparticles on zeolites, among which ion exchange is regarded as the most promising approach due to its simplicity and effectiveness.

Mordenite is characterized by an orthorhombic structure with lattice parameters a = 18.121 Å, b = 20.517 Å, and c = 7.544 Å, and is assigned to the Cmcm space group.30 High flexibility in regulating Lewis and Brønsted acidity is also exhibited, a feature that is considered crucial for tailoring catalysts to specific chemical reactions.31 However, diffusion within mordenite is relatively slow compared to other two- or three-dimensional molecular sieves, particularly when the molecular size of the adsorbate approaches the pore diameter.27

Despite the large number of studies devoted to the catalytic reduction of 4-NP, most investigations have focused on single-metal systems evaluated under conventional batch conditions using NaBH4 as the reducing agent. In addition, the influence of the reaction methodology on the observed catalytic performance has received relatively limited attention, even though growing evidence suggests that batch reduction may not always reflect the intrinsic catalytic behavior of a material.

In this context, mordenite represents an attractive support owing to its characteristic porous structure composed of relatively large channels that can facilitate the diffusion and accessibility of bulky organic molecules.

This study aims to analyze the impact of identical ion-exchange conditions on the incorporation of different metal cations (Ag, Ni, and Fe) into the mordenite structure. The catalytic performance of the resulting materials was subsequently evaluated in the reduction of 4-nitrophenol. Particular attention was given to the influence of the reduction pathway by investigating the catalytic behavior of the same catalyst under two distinct conditions: batch reduction using NaBH4 and continuous hydrogenation. This approach enables assessment of how the reaction mode influences the catalytic performance of metal-modified mordenite.

Experimental

Reagents and materials

The reagents used in this study were used without any additional purification. They are as follows: fumed silica, sodium aluminate (Haen Riedel), sodium hydroxide (>98%, Prolabo), silver nitrate, iron nitrate, nickel nitrate, sodium chloride solution (NaCl, 1 M), 4-nitrophenol (99%, Acros), sodium borohydride (NaBH4, 98.5%, Sigma-Aldrich). Demineralized water was used in all experiments.

Preparation of mordenite zeolite

Mordenite was synthesized in several steps using fumed silica as a source of silicon and sodium aluminate as a source of aluminum. The detailed synthesis parameters, as well as the influence of operating conditions on the structural properties of the material, were described in a previous study.32 In the present study, this method was adapted in order to obtain a suitable support for modification by cation exchange. The molar composition of the synthetic gel was as follows: 7Na2O : Al2O3 : 30SiO2 : 750H2O.

First, a silica solution was prepared according to the following protocol: a given amount of NaOH was dissolved in 65 mL of demineralized water while stirring at 1100 rpm for 10 minutes. An appropriate amount of fumed silica was then gradually added to this solution, followed by a 24 hour aging period with continuous stirring at the same speed.

Simultaneously, an aluminate solution was prepared by dissolving specific quantity of NaOH in 10 mL of demineralized water, with stirring for 10 minutes. A specific amount of sodium aluminate was then added, and the mixture was kept under stirring for an additional 30 minutes.

The two solutions (silica and aluminate) were combined, and the resulting mixture was stirred for 1 hour, forming a homogeneous white gel. This gel was transferred to a stainless steel autoclave for hydrothermal treatment at 170 °C for 48 hours. After rapid cooling, the resulting white solid material was filtered, washed with demineralized water until a pH of less than or equal to 9 was achieved, and then dried at 80 °C. The material obtained was designated MOR.

To prepare sodium-saturated mordenite (NaMOR), the MOR material was treated with a 0.1 M NaCl solution under agitation for 24 hours. After treatment, the solid was recovered by filtration, washed with demineralized water, and dried at 80 °C.

Preparation of mordenite modified by silver, iron, and nickel nanoparticles

The mordenite-based catalysts were prepared using the ion exchange method. An aqueous solution containing approximately 100 mmol of Fe(NO3)3·9H2O, AgNO3, and Ni(NO3)2·6H2O was obtained by dissolving these salts in 200 mL of demineralized water. After homogenization, 4 g of mordenite zeolite solids were introduced into the solution, and the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 hours. The initial pH of the exchange solutions was 7.1 for silver, 2.5 for iron and 7.15 for nickel, indicating significantly more acidic exchange conditions in the case of iron.

After filtration, the obtained were subjected to a second ion exchange by immersing them in a new solution containing the same metal precursors, following the same protocol. After a second filtration, the solids were carefully washed with demineralized water to remove salt residues and dried at 80 °C.

Evaluation of catalytic activity by chemical reduction

The catalytic reduction of the designed catalysts was studied for the reduction of 4-NP in the presence of NaBH4 as model reactions. The reduction processes were monitored by recording changes in the UV-Vis absorption spectrum.

Initially, we chose to study the reduction of 4-NP to 4-AP as a model reaction in the presence of NaBH4 to test the capacity of all the prepared catalysts. A concentration of 0.2 mM 4-NP was used in each test. First, 30 mL of 4-NP dye solution and 30 mL of freshly prepared NaBH4 (20 to 35 mM) and an amount of catalyst (1 mg to 7 mg) were mixed with stirring. The mixture was added to a quartz cuvette. The cuvette containing the reaction mixture was placed in a UV-vis apparatus in which the reaction was monitored.

Characterization

To confirm the formation of mordenite, all zeolite samples were analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) on a Malvern Analytical diffractometer with Cu Kα (40 kV, 15 mA). The analyses were performed at a scan speed of 5° min−1, over a 2θ range between 4 and 50°, at a wavelength of 1.54 Å and with a step size of 0.02°. In order to estimate the composition of the samples, an XRF analysis was performed using a portable Olympus device, using the three-beam geochemical mode. An FTIR analysis was performed using a Thermo Scientific Nicolet Summit LITE device for the wavelength range from 500 to 4000 cm−1. The UV-Vis spectra of the catalysts were recorded for the wavelength range of 200 to 700 nm using a LAMBDA 365+ UV-Vis spectrophotometer (double beam from PerkinElmer). The surface morphology was observed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) coupled with a focused ion beam (Scios 2 Dual Beam). XPS analyses were performed using an Omicron Argus X-ray photoelectron spectrometer equipped with a monochromatic AlKα radiation source (hν = 1486.6 eV) and a 280 W electron beam. The photoelectron emission from the sample was analyzed at a photoelectron collection angle of 45° under ultra-high vacuum conditions (≤10−9 mBar). The spectra were obtained with a pass energy of 100 eV for the overall scan and a pass energy of 20 eV for the fundamental level regions. The textural properties of the samples were determined by nitrogen adsorption–desorption at 77 K using a Micromeritics 3Flex surface analyzer, and the specific surface area was calculated according to the BET method.

Results and discussion

X-ray fluorescence

The chemical composition of the samples that underwent ion exchange was analyzed by X-ray fluorescence (XRF), and the results are presented in Table 1. The analyses reveal the incorporation of various metal cations into the mordenite structure following the exchange treatment. The AgMOR catalyst has a silver content of 14.51%, indicating significant incorporation of Ag+ ions into the zeolite matrix. For the NiMOR catalyst, a nickel content of 2.236% is observed, also confirming the introduction of the metal into the structure. In contrast, the measured content for the FeMOR catalyst is much lower (0.0443%), suggesting limited incorporation of ferric species under the experimental conditions employed.

This difference can be attributed to the distinct behavior of metal cations in solution during the ion exchange process in the zeolite. In particular, Fe3+ ions exhibit a strong tendency toward hydrolysis and the formation of hydroxylated or polymeric species in an aqueous medium. Under certain conditions, particularly depending on pH and oxidation state, these species can evolve into aggregates or oxides (FexOγ) outside the exchange sites.33,34 At the relatively high metal concentration used (0.1 M), iron may also preferentially undergo surface adsorption or compete with other cations rather than penetrate the structure. In contrast, Ag+ and Ni2+ ions exhibit greater stability in solution, which facilitates their mobility and incorporation into the zeolite through ion exchange.

For the NiMOR catalyst, the ion-exchange process carried out at near-neutral pH conditions is more favorable for nickel incorporation into the zeolite framework. Under these conditions. Ni2+ ions can more readily replace the original compensating cations and become anchored within the structure. However, the formation of hydroxylated nickel species may occur at this pH, potentially leading to less mobile species that could partially limit their diffusion within the zeolite pores.

The metal/Al molar ratios calculated from the XRF results confirm the incorporation of metals into the zeolite structure. The Ag/Al ratio, estimated at 2.77, indicates a significant metal loading in the AgMOR material. The Fe/Al and Ni/Al ratios obtained for the corresponding catalysts reflect the presence of metals in the zeolite matrix under the applied exchange conditions. Similarly, the metal/Si ratios calculated for the different samples confirm the presence of metal species within the mordenite structure. These observations are consistent with the results obtained by EDX (Table S1) and with previous work showing the affinity of zeolites for Ag+ cations in aqueous solution.35

X-ray diffraction

The XRD patterns of the parent material NaMOR and the modified samples AgMOR, FeMOR, and NiMOR, containing different amounts of silver, iron, and nickel, respectively, are presented in Fig. 1. All diffraction patterns confirm the presence of the characteristic crystalline structure of Mordenite. No additional diffraction peaks corresponding to secondary phases were observed either before or after modification with transition metals (Fig. 1a), which can be attributed to the relatively low metal loading in the catalyst.36

(a) XRD of virgin mordenite and its analogues; (b) enlarged view of selected diffraction peaks.

In the case of the AgMOR catalyst, a noticeable decrease in the intensity of the diffraction peaks is observed after modification, which suggests a good dispersion of silver species within the Mordenite matrix. The incorporation of these species may partially attenuate the diffraction signals due to increased X-ray absorption and scattering effects, resulting in an apparent reduction in the relative crystallinity of the material.37

Conversely, the FeMOR and NiMOR catalysts exhibit an increase in the intensity of certain diffraction peaks, particularly those associated with the (111) and (202) planes, compared with the parent zeolite (Fig. S1). However, the overall crystallinity of the FeMOR and NiMOR samples is slightly lower than that of the parent material (Fig. 1). These observations suggest that ion exchange with iron and nickel cations does not significantly alter the structural framework of Mordenite. Instead, the presence of these cations may locally enhance the structural ordering along specific crystallographic planes, while inducing a slight decrease in the overall crystallinity of the material.38 This apparent improvement may be attributed to local stabilization of the framework, a partial reduction of structural defects, or enhanced X-ray scattering contrast along specific crystallographic planes caused by the presence of the introduced metal cations.39,40 These results suggest that the modification does not significantly alter the structural framework of Mordenite, but rather induces slight variations in the crystalline ordering. Such behavior has also been reported in previous studies on transition-metal-modified zeolites, where metal incorporation leads to subtle structural heterogeneities without disrupting the overall zeolite framework.41,42

The enlarged section of Fig. 1b reveals a slight shift of the (202) and (350) diffraction peaks of the silver-modified mordenite (AgMOR) towards lower 2θ values compared with the parent NaMOR. This shift corresponds to an increase in interplanar spacing, indicating a slight expansion of the crystal lattice. Such lattice expansion can be attributed to the incorporation of Ag+ ions into the zeolite framework, which locally enlarges the unit cell and subtly modifies the structural parameters without compromising the overall crystallinity of the Mordenite.

In contrast, for the FeMOR catalyst, the (202) and (350) diffraction peaks shift towards higher 2θ angles, indicating a reduction in interplanar spacing and a slight contraction of the crystal lattice. This behavior can be attributed to the partial replacement of Na+ ions with smaller Fe3+ ions, which leads to a local tightening of the framework.

For the NiMOR sample, no significant peak shifts are observed, suggesting that the interplanar distances remain essentially unchanged. However, the increased peak intensity may reflect a slight enhancement in crystallinity, possibly due to the anchoring of Ni2+ ions within the zeolite framework without inducing lattice distortion.

FTIR

Infrared (IR) spectroscopy was employed to identify the functional groups of the parent Mordenite and its Ag-, Fe-, and Ni-modified counterparts, as well as to assess the structural changes induced by metal incorporation (Fig. 2). A broad band at 3620.96 cm−1 is observed in all samples, mainly arising from the asymmetric stretching of isolated silanol groups (Si–OH, Si–OH–Al) and hydrogen-bonded OH groups, as well as the stretching of physisorbed water molecules.43 The weak band at 1623.14 cm−1 corresponds to the bending vibration of residual water (H–O–H) trapped in the zeolite pores.44,45

FTIR spectrum of virgin mordenite and its analogues.

The absorption at 1217.06 cm−1 is associated with asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of the external TO4 tetrahedra (T = Si, Al),45,46 while the band at 1012.90 cm−1 corresponds to the asymmetric stretching of internal TO bonds. Low-intensity bands at 800.43 cm−1 and 720.93 cm−1 are attributed to the symmetric stretching of internal tetrahedra, and the narrow band at 626.95 cm−1 arises from the symmetric stretching of external T–O bonds.44 The band at 537.51 cm−1 is attributed to the elongation vibrations of secondary building units (SBUs).

After metal modification, a small band appears at 2976.50 cm−1, likely due to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching of C–H bonds. This feature is probably caused by the spontaneous adsorption of trace atmospheric volatile organic compounds or environmental carbonaceous residues, rather than the zeolite framework itself.

UV-Vis

UV-Vis spectroscopy (Fig. 3) was employed to investigate the coordination and electronic states of the metal species incorporated into Mordenite. The spectrum of the AgMOR catalyst displays a strong band at 229 nm, which can be assigned to an O2− → Ag+ charge–transfer transition, confirming effective ion exchange of silver at the acidic sites of the zeolite. In addition, a broad absorption feature between 393 and 457 nm is observed, characteristic of the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) of metallic silver nanoparticles (Ag0). This indicates that a portion of Ag+ has been partially reduced to Ag0 during the modification process.47

UV-Visible spectrum of modified mordenite.

For the FeMOR catalyst, a strong absorption band at 276 nm is assigned to Fe–O charge–transfer transitions, indicating the successful incorporation of iron into the Fe–O–Si framework of the Mordenite.48 In addition, a broad, low-intensity band in the visible region (∼397–550 nm) may be attributed to Fe3+ d–d transitions, or to the presence of oligomeric iron species and highly dispersed iron oxides. The absence of sharp, well-defined visible bands, which are characteristic of crystalline Fe2O3 nanoparticles, suggests that iron exists predominantly as amorphous or finely dispersed species rather than forming detectable bulk phases. These observations support the coexistence of iron species both incorporated into the zeolite lattice and as highly dispersed extra-framework species, consistent with the XRF and XRD results.

For the NiMOR catalyst, a band observed at 394–417 nm is assigned to the d–d transition (3A2 → 3T1g) of Ni2+ in octahedral coordination.49 The relatively low intensity of this band suggests that a portion of Ni2+ may have been partially reduced to metallic Ni0 nanoparticles, as further supported by the appearance of a band at 214 nm, attributed to O2− → Ni2+ charge–transfer transitions within the aluminosilicate framework.50 These features indicate the coexistence of both framework-incorporated Ni2+ and small, highly dispersed metallic nickel species in the zeolite.

XPS

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to examine the surface composition, oxidation states, and coordination environments of the metals (Ag, Fe, and Ni) in the modified Mordenite catalysts. The survey spectrum (Fig. 4a) confirms the presence of Ag, Ni, Fe, O, C, Si, and Al. The binding energy scale was calibrated using the C 1s peak as a reference. The XPS survey spectra of AgMOR and NiMOR clearly show characteristic Ag and Ni signals on the surface, indicating successful surface incorporation without additional processing (Fig. 4b and d). In contrast, no Fe peak was readily observed in the FeMOR survey spectrum (Fig. 4a), consistent with the very low Fe content measured by XRF (0.0443 wt%). However, a detailed regional scan of the Fe 2p region, after smoothing and baseline correction (Fig. S2), reveals the characteristic Fe doublet (Fig. 4c). Peak fitting using Voigt (or Gaussian) functions allowed deconvolution of the different electronic contributions.51

X-ray photoelectron (XPS) spectra of modified zeolites (a) survey spectrum, (b) Ag 3d, (c) Fe 2p and (d) Ni 2p.

In the Ag 3d region (Fig. 4b), a well-defined doublet with a spin–orbit separation of ∼6.0 eV is observed. The peaks at 368.72 eV (Ag 3d5/2) and 374.72 eV (Ag 3d3/2) correspond to metallic silver (Ag0),52 while secondary peaks at slightly higher binding energies (∼369.82 and 375.82 eV) indicate the presence of Ag+ ions. This coexistence of Ag0 and Ag+ confirms that the material is bifunctional, combining the catalytic properties of both metallic and ionic silver.

The Fe 2p spectrum (Fig. 4c) shows a doublet with the main peaks at 710.95 eV (Fe 2p3/2) and ∼724 eV (Fe 2p1/2), characteristic of Fe3+ species.53 Satellite peaks at ∼717 eV and 731 eV further confirm the dominance of Fe3+,54 and an additional peak at 713.52 eV suggests Fe3+ in slightly different chemical environments.55 The absence of a significant Fe signal in the survey spectrum is consistent with the low bulk iron content, while the regional analysis confirms its presence on the surface, in agreement with XRF and structural observations.

The Ni 2p high-resolution spectrum (Fig. 4d) shows two spin–orbit components at 856.33 eV (Ni 2p3/2) and 873.93 eV (Ni 2p1/2), separated by 17.6 eV, characteristic of Ni2+. Two distinct satellite peaks at 862.43 eV and 880.43 eV further support the presence of Ni2+ in the zeolite framework.56,57

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The morphology of mordenite zeolite (MOR) before and after ion exchange with Ag, Fe, and Ni precursors was examined by scanning electron microscopy (Fig. 5). No significant changes in crystal shape were observed after treatment, indicating that the characteristic prismatic morphology of mordenite, along with its generally smooth surface, was preserved.

Catalytic test

Reduction of 4-nitrophenol

The kinetics of the catalytic reduction of 4-NP in the presence of NaBH4 were monitored by UV-Vis spectroscopy, with spectra recorded at 2 min intervals. In aqueous solution, 4-NP initially exhibits a maximum absorption band at 317 nm. However, upon the addition of a freshly prepared NaBH4 solution, this band undergoes a red shift to 400 nm, corresponding to the formation of the 4-nitrophenolate ion (Fig. 6). This transformation is accompanied by a noticeable color change from pale yellow to an intense yellow.

UV-vis spectrum of 4-NP in the presence of NaBH4 without catalyst.

In the absence of a catalyst, the absorbance at 400 nm remains essentially unchanged even after prolonged reaction times, indicating that the reduction of 4-NP does not proceed with NaBH4 alone. This behavior is commonly attributed to electrostatic repulsion between the negatively charged 4-nitrophenolate ions and borohydride ions (BH4−), which hinders direct electron transfer between them.4

It is well established that metal nanoparticles efficiently catalyze the reduction of 4-NP by promoting electron transfer from the donor species (BH4−) to the acceptor (4-NP) through the metal surface. Accordingly, upon addition of the AgMOR catalyst to the NaBH4/4-NP solution, a rapid decrease in the absorbance at 400 nm is observed, accompanied by a progressive discoloration of the solution from yellow to colorless. This behavior indicates the conversion of 4-NP into 4-AP.

The formation of 4-AP is further confirmed by the appearance of a new absorption band at 298 nm (Fig. 7b).

Effect of catalyst type and mass

The catalytic reduction of 4-NP to 4-AP using metal nanoparticles is widely recognized as a model reaction to assess catalytic activity. Various parameters affecting this process have been systematically investigated, including the concentration of NaBH4, the type of catalyst and its metal loading, the amount of catalyst used, and the volume of the reaction medium, all of which significantly influence the reaction kinetics and overall conversion.

In this study, catalytic tests were performed using different catalyst masses (3 and 7 mg) in a fixed reaction volume (60 mL) with a NaBH4 concentration of 20 mM. The results indicate that both iron-based (FeMOR) and nickel-based (NiMOR) catalysts exhibit relatively low catalytic activity, regardless of the catalyst mass employed. For FeMOR, the conversion decreases from 32.42% to 9.43% as the catalyst mass increases from 3 to 7 mg, whereas for NiMOR, it increases slightly from 16.87% to 26% under the same conditions (Fig. 7a).

The silver-based catalyst (AgMOR) shows a markedly different catalytic behavior, reaching a 4-NP conversion of approximately 88.02% within only 10 minutes when using 3 mg of catalyst (Fig. 7b).

For both FeMOR and AgMOR catalysts, a decrease in catalyst mass was found to enhance the reduction efficiency, whereas increasing the mass led to a noticeable decline in catalytic activity. This behavior can be attributed to several factors. At higher catalyst loadings, particle agglomeration may occur, resulting in a reduction of the effective specific surface area and, consequently, a lower number of accessible active sites.

Furthermore, an excess of catalyst can introduce diffusion limitations within the reaction medium, hindering the transport of reactants to the active sites due to crowding effects. As a result, the overall catalytic behavior is affected despite the higher amount of material present.59

The rate constants obtained for AgMOR at 3 mg and 7 mg are relatively similar (k = 0.24 min−1 and 0.22 min−1, respectively), indicating comparable initial kinetics. However, at 7 mg, the conversion rapidly reaches approximately 75.03% and remains nearly constant, with no significant increase even after 30 minutes. In contrast, with 3 mg, the reaction continues progressively, reaching 88% conversion within 10 minutes (67.82% at 6 minutes). This behavior suggests that, despite similar initial reaction rates, diffusion limitations or particle aggregation at higher catalyst loading may hinder the overall reaction progress.

This effect is particularly evident for FeMOR, which shows a pronounced decrease in conversion at higher catalyst mass, suggesting limitations related to the dispersion or accessibility of active sites. The behavior observed for AgMOR can be related to the effective participation of silver species in electron transfer processes, contributing to the observed catalytic response under the studied conditions.

It is important to emphasise that, while these normalisation approaches provide complementary information, they are subject to specific limitations. In particular, metal-based normalisation is strongly influenced by metal loading. In cases of very low metal content, the normalisation may artificially amplify the apparent activity due to the mathematical effect of dividing by a small quantity. Therefore, the normalised rate constants should be interpreted with caution and considered as indicative parameters rather than direct measures of intrinsic catalytic activity.

Overall, these results highlight that catalytic behaviour is influenced by multiple factors, including metal distribution, the accessibility of active sites and the physicochemical environment provided by the zeolite framework. These factors will be discussed further in the following sections.

Based on these observations, the AgMOR catalyst, which exhibited the highest performance, was selected for further investigation. The catalyst mass was reduced to 1 mg while keeping all other reaction parameters constant (Fig. 7c), in order to evaluate the effect of decreased loading on catalytic efficiency (Fig. S4). Under these conditions, a higher conversion of approximately 95% was achieved; however, this came at the expense of a considerably longer reaction time, extending to around 1 hour and 10 minutes.

Effect of NaBH4 concentration

In the second part of the study, the effect of NaBH4 concentration on the reaction time and reduction efficiency of 4-NP was investigated using the AgMOR catalyst. Experiments were performed at different NaBH4 concentrations (20, 30, and 35 mM), with a fixed reaction volume of 60 mL and low catalyst loadings (1 mg or 3 mg).

The results show that all NaBH4 concentrations tested led to high 4-NP conversions, except for the combination of 20 mM NaBH4 and 1 mg of AgMOR, which resulted in relatively low conversion after 10 minutes. In contrast, the other conditions enabled rapid and significant conversion, reaching up to ∼98% (Fig. 8a).

(a) Variation of ln(Ct/C0) as a function of reaction time for different NaBH4 concentrations (20 mM, 30 mM and 35 mM). (b) UV-vis spectra recorded during the reduction of 4-NP to 4-AP catalyzed under the following conditions: [NaBH4] = 35 mM, catalyst mass m = 1 mg, volume V = 60 mL.

These observations suggest that, at very low catalyst loading, a minimum NaBH4 concentration is required to maintain an efficient electron transfer rate and achieve high reduction efficiency within a short reaction time.

The optimal balance between reaction time and conversion was achieved using 35 mM NaBH4 and 1 mg of AgMOR, leading to near-complete reduction within 10 minutes. Under these conditions, the apparent rate constant (k = 0.331 min−1) reflects a fast reaction process, further confirmed by disappearance of the characteristic absorption peak at 400 nm (Fig. 8b).

At higher catalyst loading (3 mg) and increased NaBH4 concentrations (30–35 mM), higher apparent rate constants were obtained (k = 0.537 min−1), (k = 0.546 min−1) respectively, indicating faster initial kinetics. However, the conversion rapidly reaches a plateau and shows no significant improvement thereafter. In contrast, at lower catalyst loading (1 mg), the reaction proceeds more gradually but ultimately achieves higher overall conversion.

This behavior indicates that increasing NaBH4 concentration or catalyst loading does not necessarily enhance the overall reduction efficiency. Although higher concentrations generally accelerate the reaction rate, they may also lead to reduced performance due to active site saturation, diffusion limitations, or nanoparticle aggregation, which decrease the effective surface available for electron transfer.

A deeper analysis of the apparent rate constants (kapp) reveals that optimal performance is achieved under balanced conditions, where electron transfer is maximized without inducing mass transfer limitations. These findings highlight the critical interplay between reactant concentration, catalyst loading, and nanoparticle dispersion in controlling both reaction kinetics and overall conversion. It is also noteworthy that even at lower NaBH4 concentration (20 mM) and slightly higher catalyst loading (3 mg), AgMOR still exhibits significant catalytic activity, confirming its intrinsic efficiency. Under the same experimental conditions, the behavior of FeMOR and NiMOR is presented in Fig. S5 and S6.

The superior performance of AgMOR can be attributed to the synergistic effect between hydride ions (BH4−) and electrons provided by silver nanoparticles (Ag0) supported on mordenite, which facilitates simultaneous adsorption of reactants and enhances hydrogen transfer while lowering the activation energy of the reduction process.18,60

Although FeMOR and NiMOR exhibit higher BET surface areas, their catalytic performance remains limited due to the low density of active metal sites. In contrast, AgMOR, despite its lower surface area, contains a higher loading of well-dispersed silver nanoparticles, providing a larger effective active surface and thus improving catalytic efficiency.61

Overall, these results demonstrate that achieving optimal 4-NP conversion within a short reaction time requires careful tuning of operating parameters, particularly the balance between NaBH4 concentration and catalyst loading.

Effect of volume

Variations in the solution volume significantly affect the effective concentrations of the reagents and their accessibility to the catalytic sites (Fig. 9). At 60 mL, an optimal balance is achieved between the adsorption of 4-NP and the availability of BH4− ions, thereby enhancing the conversion rate and promotes faster reaction kinetics. Conversely, reducing the volume to 40 mL increases the concentration of reactants, which can lead to saturation of the active sites by the substrate. This saturation limits the competition between the substrate and the reducing agent for the available catalytic sites. As a result, the efficiency of electron transfer decreases, as evidenced by the marked reduction in the rate constant (k).62

Variation of ln(Ct/C0) as a function of reaction time for different total solution volumes (40 mL, 60 mL, and 80 mL).

At the other extreme, a moderate dilution to 80 mL introduces diffusion limitations. The reactants, being more dispersed, interact less frequently with the catalytic surface, while their mobility within the solution becomes less effective in sustaining collisions with active sites. This ultimately slows down the overall reaction rate, without significantly affecting the final conversion, which remains high but is achieved over a slightly longer reaction time. Such behavior is typical in porous or supported catalytic systems, where mass transport rather than intrinsic catalytic activity becomes the rate-limiting step.14

Reaction mechanism of reduction

The reduction mechanism of 4-NP by NaBH4 over the AgMOR catalyst is illustrated in Scheme 1. This process can be described by a Langmuir–Hinshelwood mechanism,24,63 in which both reactants are first adsorbed onto the catalyst surface prior to reaction. The proposed pathway involves several key steps:

Plausible mechanism for the reduction of 4-NP to 4-AP.

(a) Upon addition of NaBH4, the solution pH increases, leading to the conversion of 4-NP into 4-nitrophenolate. Simultaneously, NaBH4 undergoes hydrolysis, generating active hydrogen species and electrons. These reactive species are transferred from BH4− to the AgMOR surface, where they become adsorbed.

(b) The 4-nitrophenolate species is subsequently adsorbed onto the AgMOR catalyst through chemical interactions, forming a surface–bound complex.14,64

(c) The catalyst continues to supply active hydrogen from BH4−, enabling the stepwise reduction of 4-NP. The reaction proceeds through intermediates such as 4-nitrosophenol and 4-hydroxylaminophenol identified as the first stable intermediate before ultimately yielding 4-AP (Scheme 2).14,65

Reaction pathway for catalyst reduction.

(d) Finally, the reaction product desorbs from the catalyst surface, regenerating the active sites.

Overall, the rate-determining step is most likely the surface reaction between adsorbed 4-nitrophenolate and hydrogen species, rather than a diffusion-controlled process.24

Catalysts recycle

Recycling tests revealed a progressive decrease in conversion over successive cycles (Fig. 10). This decline in activity can be partly attributed to the very low initial catalyst amount used (1 mg), which makes its recovery difficult after each cycle. Catalyst losses were observed during the washing steps, mainly due to adhesion to the surfaces of the filtration system. In addition, aggregation phenomena or alterations of the surface structure may occur under the mechanical constraints associated with repeated washing and drying processes. Therefore, the decrease in catalytic performance is not only due to possible intrinsic deactivation, but also to the physical loss of active material.

Hydrogenation of 4-nitrophenol in a continuous flow reactor

Following the synthesis of the catalysts described in the previous sections and the evaluation of their performance in the conventional reduction of 4-NP using NaBH4, an alternative approach was investigated to compare catalytic efficiency under different reaction conditions. Specifically, the hydrogenation of 4-NP was carried out in continuous flow using the ThalesNano H-Cube Pro reactor, a system widely employed for hydrogenation reactions due to its ability to generate dihydrogen in situ via water electrolysis.68

The objective of this study was to determine whether the catalytic performance observed under batch conditions could be reproduced or potentially enhanced in a continuous flow system that more closely mimics industrial catalytic processes. This approach aimed to evaluate whether the catalysts exhibit comparable activity under these conditions or display distinct behaviors as a result of differences in reaction environment, mass transport, and hydrogen availability.

The AgMOR, FeMOR, and NiMOR samples were individually packed into empty catalytic cartridges CatCart (70 × 4 mm) for AgMOR and NiMOR, and (30 × 4 mm) for FeMOR. To ensure proper sealing and prevent leakage, the cartridges were vacuum sealed using a mechanical assembly equipped with 8 µm stainless steel and Teflon filters. The resulting cartridges denoted as Cart-AgMOR, Cart-FeMOR, and Cart-NiMOR were subsequently installed in the ThalesNano H-Cube Pro system for continuous catalytic reduction experiments.

In this setup, H2 is generated in situ through the electrolytic decomposition of water within the flow reactor.69 The 4-NP solution is then pumped through the catalytic cartridge under controlled operating conditions, including an optimized temperature (e.g., 40 °C) and a defined flow rate (e.g., 0.3 mL min−1). As the solution passes through the cartridge, the generated hydrogen reacts with the substrate in the presence of the catalyst (Cart-AgMOR, Cart-FeMOR, or Cart-NiMOR), enabling efficient and continuous conversion of 4-NP to 4-AP (Fig. 11).

Schematic representation of a flow reactor (H-Cube) for hydrogenation reaction.

A series of experiments was carried out to evaluate the catalytic performance of AgMOR, FeMOR, and NiMOR in the ThalesNano H-Cube Pro system under continuous flow conditions. Key operational parameters including flow rate, temperature, and pressure were systematically varied to assess their influence on catalytic efficiency.

The progress of the 4-NP reduction was monitored by UV-vis spectroscopy. The absorption spectra (Fig. S7 and S8) show two main trends: the gradual decrease of the characteristic band of 4-NP (≈317 nm) and the concurrent emergence of bands associated with 4-AP, (≈290 nm), indicating the transformation of the substrate into the desired product.

However, for the AgMOR and FeMOR catalysts, partial spectral overlap between 4-NP and 4-AP hindered direct interpretation. To address this, a complementary analysis based on the second-derivative method was applied (Fig. 12a and b). This approach enabled clear differentiation between the contributions of both species, thereby providing more reliable confirmation of reaction progress under varying operating conditions.

The continuous flow hydrogenation tests carried out in the H-Cube Pro system highlight distinct catalytic behaviors depending on the nature of the cartridge used (Table 5, Fig. 12, S7 and S8).

In addition, operating parameters such as hydrogen pressure and temperature play a crucial role in determining hydrogen availability, reaction kinetics, and overall conversion efficiency.

In the case of the AgMOR catalyst (1165 mg, 25 °C), the conversion of 4-NP depends on both the flow rate and the hydrogen pressure. At a relatively high flow rate (0.7 mL min−1, 5 bar), conversion remains limited to around 50%, which can be explained by reduced contact time between the substrate and the active sites (Entry 1, Fig. 12a). However, when the flow rate is lowered to 0.3 mL min−1 and the pressure is gradually increased (5 → 20 → 50 bar, Entries 2–4), a marked decrease in the characteristic absorbance of 4-NP (∼317 nm) and the appearance of a new band around 285–293 nm, attributed to 4-AP, are observed (Fig. 12a and S7). The conversion reaches a maximum of 89% at 50 bar, reflecting better solubilization of H2 under high pressure and a longer residence time, thus promoting complete reduction of 4-NP. This confirms that increasing hydrogen pressure enhances its solubility and adsorption on the catalyst surface, thereby accelerating the hydrogenation process.

The FeMOR catalyst (362 mg, 25 °C) exhibits similar behavior but with more modest efficiency. At a flow rate of 0.3 mL min−1 and varying the pressure from 5 to 40 bar (Entries 5–7), the 4-NP band at ∼317 nm gradually decreases, while a new band appears between 285–295 nm, corresponding to the formation of 4-AP (Fig. 12b and S8). The conversion rate thus changes from 55 to 59%. An increase in temperature to 40 °C under 40 bar (Entry 8) leads to a slight improvement, reaching 60%. This effect can be attributed to enhanced molecular kinetics and a reduction in activation energy at higher temperatures, which facilitates the hydrogenation reaction. These results confirm that the effective residence time (∼45 s) and the intensity of the substrate/catalyst interaction are critical parameters for conversion.

The case of NiMOR stands out clearly (Fig. 12c). At 25 °C and 0.3 mL min−1, low-pressure tests (5–20 bar, Entries 9–10) mainly lead to the appearance of a peak at ∼400 nm, attributed to 4-nitrophenolate, indicating partial hydrogenation due to insufficient availability of dissolved hydrogen. However, above 40 bar (Entries 11–12), the reduction progresses efficiently and results in the formation of 4-AP, confirmed by a band between 285–293 nm. Raising the temperature to 40 °C (Entry 13) achieves the highest conversion (92%). However, a further increase in pressure to 60 bar (Entry 14) leads to a decrease in conversion (∼85%), probably due to saturation or partial blocking of the active sites by excess hydrogen. This suggests that while moderate increases in pressure and temperature promote hydrogen activation and reaction rate, excessive hydrogen pressure may hinder catalytic performance by limiting reactant access to active sites.

The catalytic performance can also be evaluated in terms of WHSV and productivity. Under the studied conditions, WHSV values remain nearly constant due to the fixed flow rate and catalyst mass for each system, while productivity follows a trend similar to that of conversion. These results indicate that the improvement in conversion at higher pressure or temperature is mainly related to enhanced hydrogen availability rather than changes in contact time.

Upon examining the above results, we found that the best optimized conditions are as follows: for Cart-AgMOR (1165 mg), a pressure of 50 bar of H2, a flow rate of 0.3 mL min−1 at a temperature of 25 °C for the reduction of 4-NP to 4-AP (Entry 4), for Cart-FeMOR (362 mg), a pressure of 40 bar H2, a flow rate of 0.3 mL min−1 at a temperature of 40 °C for the reduction of 4-NP to 4-AP (Entry 8), and for Cart-NiMOR (1165 mg), a pressure of 40 bar H2, a flow rate of 0.3 mL min−1 at a temperature of 40 °C for the reduction of 4-NP to 4-AP (Entry 13), it can be seen that when the pressure exceeds a certain threshold, the conversion decreases slightly (Entries 5, 6, 11, 12, 13, 14) due to a combination of mass transfer limitations, competitive adsorption of H2 on the catalytic sites, and hydrodynamic disturbances in the reactor.

During conventional chemical reduction in the presence of NaBH4, the UV-Vis spectra of the effluents for AgMOR, FeMOR, and NiMOR are dominated by the 4-nitrophenolate band at ∼400 nm. This behavior is expected, as borohydride induces medium alkalization, shifting the acid–base equilibrium toward the deprotonated nitrophenolate species.

In contrast, during continuous-flow hydrogenation (H-Cube Pro), the spectral evolution strongly depends on the catalyst. For Cart-AgMOR and Cart-FeMOR, the main observed change is a pronounced decrease in the band at 317 nm, corresponding to neutral 4-NP (Fig. S7 and S8). Conversely, Cart-NiMOR primarily shows a decrease in the nitrophenolate band around ∼400 nm (Fig. 12c).

Discussion of catalytic results

The UV-Vis and XPS analyses provide insight into the surface chemistry governing the catalytic behavior observed during 4-NP reduction. In conventional chemical reduction in the presence of NaBH4, the UV-Vis spectra of the effluents for AgMOR, FeMOR, and NiMOR are dominated by the 4-nitrophenolate band at ∼400 nm, as expected due to the alkalization of the medium induced by borohydride, which shifts the acid–base equilibrium toward the deprotonated species.

In contrast, under continuous-flow hydrogenation conditions (H-Cube Pro), the spectral evolution is strongly catalyst-dependent. AgMOR and FeMOR predominantly show a decrease in the band at 317 nm, corresponding to neutral 4-NP, whereas NiMOR mainly exhibits a decrease in the nitrophenolate band at ∼400 nm.

This divergence indicates that, in the absence of a strongly basic environment, the 4-nitrophenol/4-nitrophenolate equilibrium is governed by the surface microenvironment of the catalyst rather than by the bulk solution conditions.

This interpretation is supported by XPS (O 1s) analysis, which reveals a higher relative contribution of hydroxyl groups in NiMOR. Such surface hydroxylation is consistent with an increased ability to promote deprotonation and stabilize the nitrophenolate anion at the interface. In contrast, AgMOR and FeMOR display a greater proportion of lattice oxygen and/or physisorbed oxygen species, which are more favorable for the adsorption and interaction of the neutral molecular form of 4-NP.

The catalytic results further highlight a marked dependence of activity on the nature of the metal and the reaction pathway. During chemical reduction with NaBH4, AgMOR exhibits high conversion under the investigated conditions. This activity trend can be rationalized by considering the role of silver nanoparticles as efficient electron-transfer mediators between the hydride donor (BH4−) and the nitroaromatic substrate. Ag nanoparticles supported on mordenite facilitate interfacial electron transfer by acting as electronic relays, thereby lowering kinetic barriers and accelerating the reduction process.70 Their favorable redox properties, combined with high surface area and effective adsorption of reactants, account for the superior performance of AgMOR, which is consistent with the high conversions (>96%) commonly reported for Ag-based catalysts in nitro compound and dye reductions.

In contrast, during hydrogenation under H2 in the H-Cube reactor, NiMOR becomes the most efficient catalyst, followed by AgMOR and FeMOR. This reversal of activity is attributed to the need for efficient homolytic dissociation of molecular hydrogen (H2 → 2H*) at the metal surface, a process favored on nickel, even at low concentrations, whereas silver favors a less energetically favorable heterolytic activation, limiting its reaction rate for easily hydrogenated substrates.71 In addition, silver can undergo partial oxidation to Ag+ or Ag2O in the presence of water or oxygen, and its encapsulation in zeolite channels can reduce the accessibility and mobility of the active sites necessary for H2 dissociation.72,73

The FeMOR catalyst has limited chemical reduction activity (∼32%), but significantly improved conversion under hydrogenation (∼60%), indicating a better response to direct hydrogen activation. However, this performance remains inferior to that of NiMOR and AgMOR, due to both the predominance of oxidized iron species and the low amount of catalyst loaded in the cartridge used.

Therefore, the observed differences in activity and selectivity across catalysts and reaction conditions arise from a combination of (i) the dominant activation mechanism (electron transfer vs. Hydrogen dissociation), (ii) the electronic and chemical nature of the metal centers, and (iii) the microenvironment provided by the zeolite support, which governs substrate adsorption, speciation, and the availability of active sites. This synergy ultimately controls the catalytic efficiency in each reaction pathway.

Conclusions

Mordenite zeolite was successfully synthesized via a hydrothermal route and subsequently modified with silver (Ag), iron (Fe), and nickel (Ni) nanoparticles. The resulting materials were evaluated as catalysts for the reduction of 4-NP using two different approaches: the conventional chemical method employing NaBH4 and a continuous-flow hydrogenation system (H-Cube Pro, ThalesNano).

Characterization results confirmed that the modified zeolites preserved their crystallinity after metal incorporation and exhibited a generally homogeneous dispersion of the active metal species. XRF and EDX analyses indicated a significantly low iron content in FeMOR, which is attributed to the acidic conditions during ion exchange. Under these conditions, iron tends to form insoluble hydroxide species that hinder effective incorporation into the mordenite framework. In contrast, silver showed a high degree of incorporation, reflecting its strong interaction and affinity with the zeolite matrix, while nickel achieved an intermediate loading.

The catalytic performance in the NaBH4 mediated reduction of 4-NP demonstrated a strong dependence on catalyst mass, reducing agent concentration, solution volume, and, critically, the nature of the incorporated metal. Among the tested materials, AgMOR exhibited a good catalytic activity, which can be associated with its superior ability to mediate electron transfer between BH4− and the substrate, as well as its favorable surface properties for substrate adsorption and activation.

In the continuous-flow hydrogenation system, the reduction of 4-NP to 4-AP proceeded efficiently under H2 in the presence of Cart-AgMOR, Cart-FeMOR, and Cart-NiMOR. All catalysts were capable of activating molecular hydrogen and promoting the reaction, although with different efficiencies. Notably, FeMOR and NiMOR, which displayed relatively low conversions under NaBH4 conditions (32% and 16%, respectively), showed a substantial improvement under hydrogenation, reaching 60% and 92% conversion. This enhancement highlights the different mechanistic requirements of the two reduction pathways: while NaBH4 reduction is governed by surface-mediated electron transfer, H2 hydrogenation depends on the catalyst's ability to dissociate molecular hydrogen and generate reactive hydrogen species. In this context, Ni-containing catalysts are particularly effective due to their well-known hydrogen activation, whereas Fe-based systems benefit from direct hydrogen exposure despite the presence of oxidized species. It is also worth noting that the use of a larger cartridge for FeMOR could further improve its performance by increasing the available active phase and contact time.

Compared to the conventional batch method, the continuous-flow approach offers several advantages, including improved control over reaction parameters, enhanced safety when handling hydrogen, cleaner reaction profiles, and elimination of post-reaction separation steps. These features make continuous-flow hydrogenation a more practical and scalable alternative for catalytic reductions.

In the continuous-flow setup, the primary objective was to evaluate whether the change in reaction methodology could enhance the performance of FeMOR and NiMOR catalysts, which was successfully demonstrated. Future work will focus on optimizing the synthesis of FeMOR particularly the ion-exchange conditions and fine-tuning the reaction parameters in order to further improve its catalytic efficiency.

In conclusion, the results highlight that catalytic performance in mordenite based systems is governed by a complex interplay between synthesis conditions, metal incorporation, surface properties, and the chosen reduction methodology. Careful control of these factors is essential to optimize metal dispersion, active site accessibility, and the dominant reaction mechanism, ultimately determining the overall efficiency of the catalytic process.
