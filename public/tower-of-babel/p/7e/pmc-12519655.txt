Effect of a strength-based empowerment programme, using teach-back method, on caregiver burden and resilience among family caregivers of patients undergoing haemodialysis: a protocol for a clinical trial study
Authors: Nesa Reyhani, Ahmad Kameli, Tooba Hoseini Azizi, Mehdi Haresabadi, Sima Hejazi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFamily caregivers of patients undergoing haemodialysis experience significant psychological, social and physical challenges that contribute to caregiver burden and reduced resilience. Strengths-based empowerment programmes, combined with the teach-back method, may help reduce burden and enhance resilience. This protocol describes a randomised controlled trial designed to evaluate the effect of such an intervention among family caregivers of haemodialysis patients.Methods and analysisThis parallel-group randomised controlled trial will enrol 78 family caregivers of haemodialysis patients in Bojnurd, Iran, using a convenience sampling method. Participants will be randomised into intervention and control groups through block randomisation with a block size of four. The 10-week intervention will include 60 Short Message Service messages, five fortnightly teach-back phone calls and four structured inperson reinforcement sessions. The control group will receive the standard educational support provided by the dialysis unit. Outcomes will be measured at baseline, immediately post intervention and at the end of 8 weeks follow-up using the Caregiver Burden Questionnaire for Hemodialysis Caregivers and the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale. A blinded statistician will perform repeated measures Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) for the analysis.Ethics and disseminationThe participants will be assured that their data will remain confidential. Written informed consent will be obtained from the participants. The research proposal has been prospectively registered in the Iranian Clinical Trial Registration Centre (IRCT20250109064329N1; Date of full registration: 18 February 2025) and approved by the Ethics Committee of North Khorasan University of Medical Sciences (code: IR.NKUMS.REC.1403.128). All methods will be carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Findings will be disseminated to participants via a lay summary, to healthcare providers, policymakers and stakeholders through reports and presentations and submitted for publication in peer-reviewed journals. Deidentified participant data will be available from the corresponding author on reasonable request, in accordance with institutional policies and ethical approval.Trial registration numberIRCT20250109064329N1.Keywords: Caregiver Burden, Caregivers, Clinical Protocols, Dialysis

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) progresses through a five-stage pathway leading to end-stage kidney disease,1 a condition where kidney function is no longer sufficient to sustain life. At this stage, patients require renal replacement therapies, including dialysis (haemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis) or kidney transplantation, to survive.1 2

The global burden of CKD is rising, with projections suggesting it could become the fifth leading cause of reduced life expectancy by 2040.3 In Iran, by the end of 2016, approximately 60 000 patients with ESRD were undergoing treatment. Of these, around 29 500 were on haemodialysis, approximately 2000 were on peritoneal dialysis and about 28 500 had undergone kidney transplantation.2 Haemodialysis is the most common treatment for ESRD due to the shortage of kidneys available for transplantation.4

Family caregivers—whether relatives, friends or close acquaintances—play a central role in helping patients adapt to and manage chronic diseases. They provide essential physical, emotional and social care, often without financial compensation4 5 and are often referred to as ‘hidden patients’ due to the significant burden they bear.4 This caregiving role increases caregivers’ vulnerability to physical and mental health issues,6 including depression, burnout, physical illnesses,4 weight loss, sleep deprivation,7 fear of the patient’s death, role changes, disrupted weekly routines,8 tardiness or job loss.9 Furthermore, the patient’s dependence, the chronic nature of the disease, prolonged caregiving needs, complications of haemodialysis and lifestyle changes imposed on caregivers are all sources of stress for family caregivers.9 10 Caregiver burden refers to the physical, financial, psychological and social pressures experienced by those caring for a family member with a medical condition. When the stress and demands of caregiving exceed the caregiver’s available resources for coping, caregiver burden develops.11

Caregiving is a dynamic yet complex responsibility that can become debilitating. Conversely, it can provide renewed meaning to the caregiver’s life and strengthen their relationships.12 Caregivers who can recover, endure or adapt to the physical and psychological demands of caregiving are referred to as ‘resilient’.13 Resilience is defined as ‘positive adaptation in the face of adversity, flexibility, reduction of caregiver burden and satisfaction with social support’.14 Resilience is a modifiable psychological process influenced by factors such as biological, individual, social and cultural elements, environmental conditions13 and disease progression.14 Enhancing resilience levels can contribute to caregivers’ physical and psychological well-being while reducing caregiver burden.15

The concept of patient and family empowerment has recently gained prominence. Empowerment involves engaging family members in programmes that enhance their caregiving knowledge and understanding of the patient’s condition. This helps minimise their physical and psychological burdens and enables them to take effective steps towards resolving challenges and improving outcomes for both the patient and themselves.16 Strength-based empowerment is rooted in the belief that every individual, family or community possesses capabilities, skills and competencies. This approach provides individuals with the opportunity to recognise their own strengths and adapt to challenges based on their abilities.17 Evidence suggests that focusing on strengths improves resilience, hope, adaptability, psychological well-being and self-efficacy.18 Existing studies have examined strength-based approaches with family caregivers and patients with conditions such as stroke,19 dementia20 and those undergoing haemodialysis.17 Interventions in these studies have included emotional experiences, cognitive mastery,19 identifying strengths, self-care education and addressing caregiver challenges.20 Systematic reviews show that there is a relatively acceptable body of knowledge in the area of providing information and psychosocial support to informal caregivers of chronic diseases such as stroke, cancer and dementia, but caregivers of patients undergoing hemodialysis have been neglected and more research is needed on this population.21

The current interest in long-distance educational approaches (telemedicine) reflects their many advantages.22 Traditional learning methods are often time-consuming, costly and logistically challenging. In contrast, e-learning provides accessible content, eliminates the need for travel and reduces the time required for learning by 25–30%.23 One effective method of remote education is the use of text messaging services, which facilitate the delivery of educational content through text messages.24 25 Currently, text message-based education is considered one of the fundamental approaches for managing and controlling chronic diseases, competing equally with traditional face-to-face counselling systems.24

One of the key challenges in patient and family education is ensuring the retention and recall of information. Given the critical role of recall in adherence to educational interventions, the teach-back method serves as an effective strategy to address this challenge.26 This educational method engages learners by having them explain the material they have learnt27 in their own words.26 By doing so, misunderstandings and incorrect information are corrected, and new skills are reinforced.28 The teach-back method has demonstrated significant benefits, such as reducing hospital readmissions, enhancing knowledge retention, improving adherence to health measures in chronic patients29 and elevating levels of self-care knowledge and capability.26

Given the close interdependence between haemodialysis patients and their family caregivers and the paucity of research on strength-based empowerment using teach-back method in this context, this study aims to assess the impact of a strength-based empowerment programme using teach-back method on caregiver burden and resilience among family caregivers of patients undergoing haemodialysis. The intervention combines remote delivery (Short Message Service (SMS) and phone calls) with complementary inperson sessions. While SMS and telephone-based teach-back are the main delivery strategies, inperson sessions provide reinforcement, discussion and clarification.

Sample size

Sample size was calculated using G*Power software (V.3.1.9.2) based on data from Wang et al.30 A total of 31 participants per group were determined necessary. Accounting for potential attrition, 39 participants per group were estimated. Parameters: significance level: 0.05, power: 0.80, mean (group 1): 58.74, mean (group 2): 65.79, standard deviation (group 1): 2.77, standard deviation (group 2): 13.40.

Sampling method

Convenience sampling will be used to select participants. Random allocation to the intervention and control groups will be done using block randomisation with four-block permutations. Blocks of four with all permutations of ‘A’ (intervention group) and ‘B’ (control group) are created: AABB, ABBA, ABAB, BAAB, BBAA, BABA. These six permutations are numbered one to six. To determine the number of blocks, the sample size is divided by the block size (eg, 78/4=20 blocks). Random numbers (1–6) are selected from a random number table to assign blocks sequentially. For example, if the random number is ‘2,’ the ABBA block is selected. This process continues until all blocks are assigned. Participants are randomly assigned based on their unique codes and the block sequence. The first author (NR) will generate the random allocation sequence, while the last author (SH) will handle participant registration and assignments.

Blinding

The statistician will be blinded, but due to the intervention’s nature, blinding of participants and researchers is not feasible.

Data collection and intervention

After selecting research participants based on inclusion criteria and assigning them to the control or intervention groups, informed consent will be obtained. A pretest will be conducted during an introductory session prior to the intervention. The intervention details will then be explained to the caregivers in the intervention group. Additionally, 10 caregivers who are not part of the study will complete questionnaires to ensure the reliability of the tools.

To protect caregivers’ rights and prevent discomfort or perceived discrimination, the control group will be informed about the study process and assured of receiving a similar intervention after the study concludes.

The intervention will be empowerment based on strength through a feedback-oriented approach and is primarily delivered through SMS and teach-back phone calls, complemented by periodic inperson sessions to enhance understanding and engagement and will involve sending SMS via a group SMS panel, consisting of 60 messages over a 10-week period. Messages will be sent every other day (three days a week: Saturdays, Mondays and Wednesdays) at 8:00 and 20:00. Each intervention group member will receive two messages per day. The SMS content will consist of simple, concise, attractive, clear, fluent, organised and motivational sentences.24 The messages will be in Farsi, with a maximum length of 200 characters per message, and the content will be identical for all participants in the intervention group. The message content will include the following priorities: raising awareness and knowledge,17 improving resilience,17 31 reducing caregiver burden,32 building confidence and self-management,17 enhancing social relationships17 31 32 and improving access to support resources.17 Details of these messages are provided in table 1 . The content of tables1 2 is based on educational programmes outlined in three studies focusing on caregivers of patients with dementia31 32 and patients undergoing haemodialysis.17 Subsequently, a tailored programme was designed specifically for caregivers of patients undergoing haemodialysis.

SMS, short message service.

To ensure message delivery, the researcher will activate the ‘delivered’ notification option on her phone. Additionally, biweekly phone calls (weeks two, four, six, eight and 10 of the intervention) will be made to each member of the intervention group. Each call will last 10 min (a total of five calls over the 10-week intervention) and will occur on specific days (half of the participants will be called on Thursdays, and the other half on Fridays). During these calls, participants will be asked to articulate the content they have learnt via SMS in their own words, allowing the researcher to address any learning challenges.

Over the 10-week period, the researcher will also conduct four in-person sessions (weeks three, five, seven and nine of the intervention), each lasting 45 min, in a classroom at dialysis centres. Intervention group members will be invited to these sessions for educational discussions, question and answer and feedback. These sessions will include face-to-face training on topics requiring inperson instruction (outlined in table 2), along with counselling. Participants will also be asked to review the content they learnt during the week to ensure accurate comprehension (teach-back).

The teach-back method will be applied as an integral component of both the telephone follow-ups and the in-person sessions. Rather than simply repeating the educational content, these interactions will be used to check caregivers’ understanding of the SMS messages and class-based materials. Caregivers will be asked to restate the key messages in their own words, after which the researcher will provide feedback, clarify misunderstandings and repeat the process until accurate comprehension is achieved. For example, following SMS messages on dietary management, caregivers may be asked: ‘In your own words, what foods should be limited for your patient?’ Similarly, after a class on stress management, they may be prompted: ‘Tell me how you would apply the relaxation technique we discussed if you feel stressed’. This feedback-oriented cycle ensures that the intervention content is not only delivered but also internalised and applied in daily caregiving.

Incentives for consistent participation will be offered. Participant retention rates will be monitored to identify reasons for dropouts. Strategies will be adjusted based on common challenges reported by caregivers.

For the control group, the standard intervention will consist of the ward’s regular educational programme (traditional in person, individual training by a nurse and distribution of educational pamphlets).

Post-test (a questionnaire with three sections) assessments for the intervention group will be conducted immediately after the completion of the intervention (after the final phone call) for post-test one, and at eight weeks post intervention for post-test two. For the control group, Post-test one will be administered 10 weeks after the pretest and post-test two at 18 weeks after the pretest (figure 1).

Family caregivers may continue accessing general hospital support services that do not interfere with the intervention. Caregivers undergoing personal medical or psychological treatments may continue their treatment as long as it does not involve interventions targeting caregiver burden or resilience in ways that could overlap with the study intervention. Caregivers will not be allowed to participate in additional structured empowerment, stress management or resilience-building programmes during the trial, as these could confound the study results. Any formal education sessions or workshops that teach similar skills to the strength-based empowerment programme (eg, teach-back method training, resilience workshops) and are enrolled in other research trials involving interventions aimed at reducing caregiver burden or enhancing resilience will not be permitted. Participation in this study is entirely voluntary. Family caregivers may withdraw from the study at any time or choose to attend other supportive programmes. In such cases, they will be excluded from the trial to preserve data integrity, without any consequences for their usual care. Caregivers should maintain a stable role in providing care during the trial.

In cases of severe caregiver burden observed during either the pretest or post-test in both groups, caregivers will be referred to a psychiatrist in coordination with the technical supervisor of the centre.

Outcome measurement

The research tools include a demographic information questionnaire, a caregiver burden questionnaire for family caregivers of patients undergoing haemodialysis33 and the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC).34 These questionnaires will be administered to participants by a consistent researcher (a research assistant, AK, who is blinded to the randomisation and intervention). The tools will be completed with the participants’ collaboration during both the pretest and post-test phases.

Demographic Information questionnaire

This questionnaire collects information on age, gender, education level, marital status and more. It consists of 19 questions and will be completed by the family caregiver and the researcher.

The Caregiver Burden Questionnaire for Family Caregivers of Patients Undergoing Hemodialysis

In this study, caregiver burden is measured using a 21-item scale designed for family caregivers of haemodialysis patients. This questionnaire, developed by Hejazi et al, evaluates caregiver burden across four subscales: physical and psychological burden (nine items; items one–nine), social and time-related burden (five items; items 10–14), mental burden (four items; items 15–18), financial and support-related burden (three items; items 19–21). Each item uses a five-point Likert scale ranging from very low (one) to very high (five). A higher total score indicates a greater level of caregiver burden.33

The Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale

The CD-RISC measures resilience across five domains using 25 items. It employs a five-point Likert scale for responses from not true at all to true nearly all the time. The score for each item contributes to a total resilience score, with higher scores indicating greater resilience. This scale was translated by Sharifnia et al for use among healthy Iranian adults. For this study, a 23-item, five-factor version was used. Items two and 20 (pertaining to ‘Close and secure relationship’ and ‘Have to act on a hunch’) were removed during the adaptation process.34

The reliability of the research tools will be assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, which will be calculated with 10 questionnaires completed independently of the study samples. A Cronbach’s alpha greater than 0.7 will be considered indicative of acceptable consistency.

Data analysis

The data distribution will be evaluated through histogram charts, skewness and kurtosis assessments and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, alongside other statistical assumptions. For comparing caregiver burden and resilience scores between the two groups, repeated measures Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) will be used. Initially, Levene’s test will evaluate the equality of variances across the measured time points. If Mauchly’s test of sphericity rejects the assumption of sphericity (p>0.05), Greenhouse-Geisser corrections will be applied; otherwise, the sphericity interpretation will be used. Post hoc analysis with Bonferroni correction will be conducted for statistically significant results to examine differences in variable means. SPSS V.26 will be used for statistical analysis, with a significance level set at 0.05.

Ethics and dissemination

The participants will be assured that their data will remain confidential. Written informed consent will be obtained from the participants. The research proposal has been registered in the Iranian Clinical Trial Registration Center prospectively (IRCT20250109064329N1) (date of full registration: 18.02.2025). In addition, the ethics committee of North Khorasan University of Medical Sciences has approved it (code: IR.NKUMS.REC.1403.128). All methods will be carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study findings will be disseminated at several levels. A lay summary in simple, non-technical language will be provided to participants. Results will also be communicated to healthcare providers, policymakers and stakeholders (eg, the Nursing Office of North Khorasan University of Medical Sciences and hospitals with dialysis units) through official reports and presentations. In addition, findings will be submitted for publication in peer-reviewed journals. To ensure public access, the outcomes will also be uploaded to the University’s Research Results Database. Deidentified participant data will be made available on reasonable request from the corresponding author, in accordance with institutional policies and ethical approval.
