Identification of age-related biomarkers for meat quality in Kangle chickens using multi-omics
Authors: Qin He, Hanle Shang, Zhangfeng Wang, Liang Xiong, Xinyao Lai, Cong Yi, Xinwei Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As a complex biological trait, meat quality is regulated by multiple factors. This study aimed to systematically characterize the meat quality of Kangle chickens at three ages (D154, D182 and D365) using transcriptomics, metabolomics, and lipidomics. Breast meat from D365 chickens exhibited significantly higher intramuscular fat (IMF) and 24 h and 48 h drip loss than that from D154 and D182 chickens (P < 0.05). Transcriptomic analysis revealed that, compared with downregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the D154 and D182 groups, those in the D365 group were notably enriched in oxidative phosphorylation associated pathways. Metabolomic profiling showed that the D365 group had the lowest relative abundance of most meat quality-related amino acids, nucleotides, and lipid metabolites, whereas triacylglycerols accounted for the highest proportion among its upregulated lipids. In total, 21 metabolites and 10 DEGs were identified as key factors influencing IMF and pH. Collectively, these findings provide novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying age-related metabolic changes in Kangle chickens.

Introduction

As a most widely farmed domesticated livestock breed (Lawal & Hanotte, 2021), chicken provides meat with a tender and juicy texture, rich flavor, and high protein content(Xu et al., 2025). Rapid advancements in modern genetic breeding technologies have improved the economic performance of chickens (Sell-Kubiak, Wimmers, Reyer, & Szwaczkowski, 2017; Q. Wang et al., 2024), enabling poultry production to meet the growing demand for chicken meat. Selective breeding for increased breast meat yield has not only effectively enhanced carcass yield but also contributed to a higher incidence of meat quality abnormalities, such as wooden breast, white striping, and spaghetti meat (Baéza, Guillier, & Petracci, 2022). Socio-economic development, evolving consumption patterns, and an increased health awareness have further driven demand for high-quality chicken products, particularly those with superior sensory attributes, flavor, and safety. Consequently, improving meat quality has become a pressing challenge for the chicken industry worldwide. However, strategies such as extending the feeding period inevitably increase production costs, posing a commercial disadvantage for producers (Deng, Xing, Li, Xu, & Zhou, 2022).

Meat quality traits are complex quantitative traits governed by multiple factors, including genetics, age, feed composition, feeding management, and processing technology (Chaumont et al., 2025; Katemala, Molee, Thumanu, & Yongsawatdigul, 2021; Zhou et al., 2024). Notably, slaughter age exerts a significant influence on poultry meat quality (Y. Wang et al., 2023). As poultry age, meat tenderness generally decreases, resulting in a coarser texture (Schneider, Renema, Betti, Carney, & Zuidhof, 2012). Conversely, older chickens tend to accumulate higher levels of free amino acids (FAAs), intramuscular fat (IMF), and protein, which enhance meat flavor (Zhang et al., 2023). Moreover, aged native chickens exhibit better tenderness, higher inosine 5′-phosphate (IMP) content, and superior nutritional value than old laying hens (commercial breeds) (Liu et al., 2023). Therefore, Chinese consumers often prefer older chickens, particularly local breeds, for preparing chicken soups, despite their higher cost (Z. Xiao, Ge, Zhou, Zhang, & Liao, 2019). However, recent studies indicate that prolonged rearing may negatively affect meat quality traits (such as IMF, amino acids, and tenderness) while also increasing operational costs (Chen et al., 2024; Popova et al., 2023).

Native broilers are increasing recognized by consumers because of their unique flavor and quality and are often considered healthier than commercial broilers (Dalle Zotte et al., 2020; L. Xiao et al., 2024). The Kangle chicken, an indigenous breed from Jiangxi Province, China, is characterized by its typical “three-yellow” phenotype (yellow beak, yellow shanks, and yellow plumage) (Xiong et al., 2023). With the rapid development of high-throughput technologies, integrated multi-omics approaches including genomics, transcriptomics, metabolomics, and proteomics, have been widely applied to identify candidate genes and key functional metabolites associated with poultry meat quality traits (Chen et al., 2024; Molinero, Pena, Estany, & Ros-Freixedes, 2025; Zhao et al., 2024). In our previous study, we systematically compared the breast meat quality attributes and flavor profiles of Kangle chickens with those of commercial white-feathered broilers (Xiong et al., 2023). However, no studies have comprehensively investigated the metabolic characteristics of Kangle chickens at different ages, particularly changes in small molecule metabolites and lipids. Lipidomic profiling enables characterization of fatty acid composition and lipid class distribution, allowing a more comprehensive analysis of lipid metabolism in Kangle chicken breast meat. In this study, we aimed to systematically investigate meat quality traits of Kangle chickens at three ages (154, 182, and 365 days) using transcriptomics, targeted metabolomics, and targeted lipidomics.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval

All animal experimental protocols were carried out in strict compliance with Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals in China, and the full study was reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanchang Normal University.

Sample collection and meat quality assessment

Experimental chickens were provided by Jiangxi Nanshi Science and Technology Co., Ltd. (Nanchang, China). A total of 500 healthy 1-day-old female Kangle chickens were raised under uniform feeding and management conditions. The rearing conditions were as follows: stepwise reduction in ambient temperature from 36 °C to 22 °C, average relative humidity maintained at 70% ± 5%, and ad libitum access to complete feed and clean drinking water. The formulation and nutrient composition of the experimental diets are listed in Table S1. After rearing to the designated ages of 154, 182, and 365 days, eight hens were randomly selected from each age group for subsequent meat quality analysis. Following a 12 h overnight fasting period, the chickens were electrically stunned, exsanguinated, and slaughtered. All procedures were carried out in compliance with the aforementioned animal welfare. Breast muscle tissue was excised, and meat quality assessments were performed in the same anatomical region for each chicken. The following traits were evaluated: pH, drip loss, and color, as previously described (Xiong et al., 2022). Briefly, pH values were measured at three independent sites on the breast muscle using a Delta 320 pH meter (Shanghai, China) at 30 min post-mortem. Meat color parameters were similarly recorded at three representative locations using a CR-400 colorimeter (Konica Minolta, Osaka, Japan), also at 30 min post-mortem. Meat samples were trimmed into regular cuboids, and the initial weight of each sample was recorded as W1. Then the sample was placed in a sealed self-sealing bag and suspended vertically in 4 °C. After storage 24 h, the sample was reweighed (W2). The 24 h drip loss was calculated as: Drip loss (%) = (W1 − W2)/W1 × 100%. Subsequently, the sample was returned to the same conditions for an additional 24 h. After this period, the sample was weighed again (W3), and cumulative drip loss was calculated as: Drip loss (%) = (W1 − W3)/W1 × 100%. The Driploss_48h–24 h value was defined as the difference between the mean drip loss at 24 h and 48 h post-slaughter. The IMF and moisture contents of breast muscle samples were measured in accordance with Chinese National Food Safety Standards GB 5009.6–2016 and GB 5009.3–2016, respectively. Additionally, samples from the same anatomical region of the left breast muscle were collected, immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until further transcriptomic, metabolomic, and lipidomic analyses.

RNA extraction, sequencing, and alignment

Total RNA was isolated from breast muscle samples using TRIzol Reagent (Life Technologies, CA, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. The quality of the extracted RNA was systematically evaluated. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), while RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with an RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit (Agilent, CA, USA).

Qualified RNA was used to prepare a library for transcriptome sequencing performed by Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Sequencing was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq platform, and the raw reads were processed for quality control using the BMKCloud online platform(http://www.biocloud.net). The Hisat2 tool was used for alignment to the reference genome. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2, with P-values adjusted for multiple testing using the false discovery rate (FDR). Genes with FDR < 0.05 and fold change (FC) ≥ 2 were considered significant DEGs. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler package based on the Wallenius non-central hyper-geometric distribution. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs was also conducted using the clusterProfiler package in combination with the KOBAS database (http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn/home.do).

Widely targeted metabolomics of breast muscles

Chicken breast muscle samples were prepared and deproteinized using methanol. Briefly, 200 μL of pre-chilled 80% methanol water solution containing internal standards was added to 50 mg of muscle sample and homogenized at low temperature using a tissue homogenizer. Subsequently, an additional 800 μL of pre-cooled 80% methanol-water solution was added. After 20 min of sonication on ice and 1 h of incubation at −20 °C, the mixture was centrifuged at 16000 ×g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was carefully collected and lyophilized for subsequent analysis.

Ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry analysis was performed by BioProfile Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The LC-MS system consisted of a Shimadzu Nexera X2 LC-30 CE system (Kyoto, Japan) coupled with a 6500 QTRAP triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX, Singapore). Chromatographic separation was achieved using a Waters Acquity UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 50 mm and 1.8 μm; Waters, MA, USA). Metabolites were detected via electrospray ionization (ESI) with both positive and negative ion modes, with mass spectrometry conditions referenced to Zhou et al. (Zhou et al., 2024).

Raw multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) data obtained using the MT1000 kit were processed with MultiQuant 3.0.2 software to extract peak areas for each metabolite. The kit covers 16 major metabolite classes, including amino acids, peptides, and analogues, acyl carnitines, and bile acids. Differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) among the 154, 182, and 365-day groups were identified using the following criteria: variable importance in projection (VIP) >1 from partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), P<0.05 from two-tailed Student's t-tests on normalized data, and FC ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 2/3. These DEMs were subsequently subjected to cluster analysis using R software. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed to interpret biological functions and visualize key metabolic pathways.

Lipidomics of breast muscles

Lipid extraction was performed on eight biological replicates per group from the D154, D182, and D365 groups, with a total of 24 samples included in the analysis. After complete thawing on ice, 50 mg of each sample was mixed with two steel beads, followed by the addition of 200 μL of pre-cooled methanol/water solution and 825 μL of pre-cooled methyl tert-butyl ether. The mixture was vigorously vortexed and ultrasonicated under ice-cold conditions for lipid extraction. Subsequently, pre-chilled ultrapure water was added, and the mixture was incubated for 10 min. The samples were then centrifuged at 16000 ×g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the upper organic solvent layer was carefully collected and lyophilized for subsequent lipidomic analysis.

Lipid analysis was conducted by the BioProfile Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) using a Q Exactive Plus Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo, CA, USA) coupled with a Nexera X2 LC-30 CE UHPLC system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Mobile phase A consisted of 50% acetonitrile aqueous solution, while phase B consisted of 100% acetonitrile. The mass spectrometer and conditions were identical to those used for targeted metabolomics. MultiQuant software was used to determine the peak area and retention time of each lipid. Lipid identification was performed by matching ion-pairs and standard retention times.

SIMCAP software (version 14.0, Umetrics, Umeå, Sweden) was used for multivariate analysis. Data preprocessing included mean-centering followed by Pareto scaling. Models were constructed using PLS-DA. Differentially expressed lipids (DELs) were identified through pairwise comparisons among the D154, D182, and D365 groups, based on thresholds of P < 0.05 (Student's t-test) and FC ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 2/3. DEL identification and pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the same approach as used in the targeted metabolomics analysis.

Statistical analyses

Breast muscle texture traits were statistically analyzed via IBM SPSS statistics software (IBM Corporation, NY, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and a P < 0.05 was set as the threshold for statistical significance. To elucidate the biochemical regulatory mechanisms underlying variations in meat quality, spearman correlation analysis was employed to evaluate the correlation coefficients among meat quality-related DEMs, DELs and DEGs. The correlation-based interaction network was subsequently performed and visualized using Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1; https://cytoscape.org).

A total of 465 DEGs were identified in the D154 vs. D365 comparison including 329 downregulated and 134 upregulated expression genes (Fig. 1a, Fig. 1b, and Table S3). In the D182 vs. D365 comparison, 727 DEGs were identified, comprising 458 down-regulated and 269 up-regulated genes (Fig. 1a, Fig. 1c, and Table S4). GO analysis classified DEGs into three main categories: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). In the D154 vs. D365 comparison (Fig. 1d), the top BP terms included cellular process, metabolic process, and biological regulation. The top CC terms were associated with cellular anatomical entity, intracellular, and protein-containing complex, while the dominant MF terms included binding, catalytic activity, structural molecule activity, transporter activity, and molecular function regulator. KEGG enrichment analysis (Fig. 1f) identified several pathways related to meat quality and flavor formation among the top 20 enriched pathways. These included carbohydrate metabolism (fructose and mannose metabolism, galactose metabolism, and pentose and glucuronate interconversions), lipid metabolism (glycerolipid metabolism), and energy metabolism (oxidative phosphorylation and insulin signaling pathway). Intersection analysis of DEGs enriched in these pathways identified AKR1E2, HK2, TSTA3, LIPG, and PNPLA2 as key genes involved in multiple metabolic pathways (Table S5). In the D182 vs. D365 comparison (Fig. 1e), BP terms were primarily associated with cellular process, metabolic process, and biological regulation. The top enriched terms in CC and MF terms were consistent with those identified in the D154 vs. D365 comparison. KEGG enrichment analysis (Fig. 1g) highlighted pathways associated with lipid metabolism (linoleic acid metabolism, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, arachidonic acid metabolism, and ether lipid metabolism), carbohydrate metabolism (pentose and glucuronate interconversions), amino acid metabolism (lysine degradation), and energy metabolism (oxidative phosphorylation). Overlap analysis of DEGs across these pathways identified PLA2G10, PLA2G4B, PROCA1, and TPT1 as key genes involved in multiple metabolic pathways (Table S6). Compared with the D154 and D182 groups, the D365 group exhibited significant down-regulation of genes associated with oxidative phosphorylation and ribosome pathways, including ATP5I, COX4I1, COX5A, COX7A2, COX7B, NDUFA1, NDUFV2, UQCR1, RPL29, RPL31, RPL32, RPL34, RPL37A, RPL38, and RPL39L.

To investigate the influence of age on metabolite profiles, PLS-DA was performed (Fig. 2b and Fig. S2a). The PLS-DA models showed clear separation among age groups. Lipid profiles in the breast muscle of Kangle chickens at different ages

Although the D365 group exhibited the highest IMF content, targeted metabolomics indicated an overall decrease in most lipid metabolites. Therefore, to further characterize the lipid landscape and its relationship with meat quality differences across age groups, lipidomic analysis was performed on breast muscle samples from D154, D182, and D365 chickens. In total, 979 lipids were detected with triacylglycerols (TAGs, 41.98%) being the most abundant lipid class, followed by phosphatidylethanolamines (PEs, 12.16%), and phosphatidylcholines (PCs, 6.95%) (Fig. S4a). The distribution of lipid classes across age groups is shown in Fig. S4b. Higher proportions of TAGs and sphingomyelins (SM) were observed in the D365 group compared with the D154 and D182 groups.

PLS-DA models were constructed to identify DELs among the three age groups based on VIP scores and P (Fig. 4a-c and Fig. S5a-c). A total of 29 DELs were identified between D154 and D182 (Fig. 4d and Table S9), 413 DELs between D154 and D365 (Fig. 4e), and 443 DELs between D182 and D365 (Fig. 4f). The volcano plot (Fig. 4d) showed five up- and 24 down-regulated DELs in D154 compared with D182. In contrast, 376 of 413 DELs were up-regulated in D154 compared with D365 (Table S10, top 50 in Fig. 4h), and 411 of 443 DELs were up-regulated in D182 compared with D365 (Table S11, top 50 shown in Fig. 4i). Furthermore, the abundances of lysophospholipids including lysophophatidylcholine (LPC), lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE), lysophosphatidyiserine (LPS), lysophosphatidylinositol (LPI), and lysophosphatidylglycerol (LPG) was significantly reduced in the D365 group compared with the D154 and D182 groups. Notably, TAGs constituted the largest proportion of up-regulated DELs in the D365 group, accounting for 75.86% (vs. D154) and 68.18% (vs. D182). Discussion

High-quality poultry meat is essential to meet current market demands. Meat quality traits are complex quantitative traits influenced by multiple factors, with age playing a significant role, particularly in flavor development (Katemala et al., 2021; Y. Wang et al., 2023). Compared with commercial broilers, slow-growing indigenous chickens exhibit greater developmental potential owing to their superior meat quality and flavor (Dalle Zotte, Gleeson, Franco, Cullere, & Lorenzo, 2020; Mussa, Kibonde, Boonkum, & Chankitisakul, 2022). China possesses a wide variety of local chicken breeds (Jin et al., 2021), among which Kangle chickens are notable for their excellent quality and broad market potential, warranting further investigation and utilization.

The primary attributes of meat quality include pH, tenderness, meat color, IMF, and drip loss. In this study, no significant changes in the meat color were observed with increasing age. This may be because myofiber diameter reaches a growth plateau after 150 days of age, resulting in minimal structural differences and limited variation in light scattering and reflection within muscle tissue (Hughes, Clarke, Purslow, & Warner, 2020; Li et al., 2019). Previous studies have shown an inverse correlation between pH and L* values (Le Bihan-Duval et al., 2008), and consistent with this, a decrease in pH was observed in the D365 group. Chicken meat generally exhibits optimal quality when post-mortem pH ranges from 5.7 to 6.3, whereas values outside this range negatively affect meat quality (Bihan-Duval et al., 2018; Campbell, Walker, Bartz, Eckberg, & Pullin, 2025; Sujiwo, Kim, & Jang, 2018). The ultimate pH of chicken breast is strongly and inversely correlated with drip loss (Xiong et al., 2022). Accordingly, in this present study, the water-holding capacity of D365 group was lower than that of the D154 and D182 groups, although no significant differences in moisture content were observed among the three groups. Lower drip loss indicates high water-holding capacity, which contributes to improved tenderness and sensory quality (Zheng et al., 2023). Moisture loss in raw meat is influenced by multiple factors, including myofibrillar shrinkage, reduced inter-filament spacing, protein degradation, and altered membrane permeability (Hughes, Oiseth, Purslow, & Warner, 2014). In addition, the extent of glycolysis directly affects muscle pH (Listrat et al., 2016). Therefore, differences between our findings and previous studies may be attributed to variations in the genetic background and meat pH values of chicken.

High drip loss leads to substantial loss of key nutrients (Xiong et al., 2022). Among the three groups, breast meat from the D365 group showed lower levels of folic acid, L-carnosine, creatine, phenylalanine, lysine, L-selenomethionine, cysteine, tyrosine, glutamine, and glutathione, suggesting that meat from the D154 and D182 groups may have superior flavor. In the D182 vs. D365 comparison, the D365 group exhibited significantly lower levels of L-threonine, isoleucine, tryptophan, cysteine, lysine, tyrosine, phenylalanine, L-selenomethionine, glutathione, γ-aminobutyric acid, and creatine, but higher levels of histidine and alanine. Several peptides and FAAs in chicken meat act as important precursors for volatile flavor compounds (Monika, Małgorzata, & Teresa, 2017). Threonine, lysine, and alanine contribute to sweetness, whereas isoleucine, histidine, and phenylalanine are associated with bitterness (Ge et al., 2023). L-carnosine, a naturally occurring dipeptide in skeletal muscle, functions as an antioxidant, contributes to pH buffering, and is involved neurotransmission (Ali, Lee, Park, & Nam, 2021). Glutathione, a tripeptide abundant in muscle tissue, also acts as a key antioxidant and plays an essential role in maintaining metabolic homeostasis (Baldelli et al., 2019). Sulfur-containing amino acids can react with reducing sugars through Maillard reactions to produce sulfur-containing volatile compounds, which have low odor thresholds and significantly contribute to meat flavor (R. Zhou, Grant, Goldberg, Ryland, & Aliani, 2019). Creatine plays important roles in energy metabolism, skeletal muscle function, and neuroprotection (Li et al., 2022).

Apart from amino acids, nucleotides and their derivatives (IMP, GMP, CMP, UMP, and AMP) function as both umami enhancers and precursors of flavor compounds in meat. Previous studies have shown that IMP content in chicken muscles increases with age (Huang et al., 2022). By contrast, in the present study, breast muscle in the D365 group exhibited lower levels of UMP and IMP but higher levels of GMP and AMP than in the D154 and D182 groups. IMP and GMP are recognized as key taste-enhancing compounds influencing meat quality (Katemala et al., 2023), with IMP regarded as a major contributor to the flavor and palatability of chicken meat (T. Xu et al., 2025). Meanwhile, the levels of more than 50 organic acids and derivatives, including L-malate, pyruvate, and succinic acid, were decreased in the D365 group. Malate and succinic acid, key intermediates in the TCA cycle, play important roles in skeletal muscle remodeling (Wang et al., 2021). Dietary succinic acid supplementation enhances muscle antioxidant capacity and modulates amino acid and fatty acid composition (Cao et al., 2024). In this study, compared with the D154 group, the D182 group exhibited lower levels of succinic acid and sphingolipids but higher levels of acylcarnitines, fatty acids and phosphatidic acids, which was further supported by lipidomic analysis.

Although increasing age is generally associated with greater IMF deposition, examining the overall lipid metabolite profile provides more comprehensive insight than measuring fat content alone (Zhao et al., 2025). In this study, IMF content was higher in the D365 group than in the D154 and D182 groups. However, metabolomic analysis showed that the levels of most fatty acids, sphingolipids and phosphatidic acids were reduced in the D365 group, while lipidomic profiles indicated higher lipid metabolite levels in the D182 group than in the D154 group. Notably, the D365 group exhibited lower levels of glycerophospholipids and phospholipids but higher levels of TAGs and SMs than other groups, which may contribute to the elevated IMF observed in this group. Although this finding differs from that of previous studies (Xu, Feng, et al., 2025; W. Zhao et al., 2025), similar observations have been documented. For example, Zhang et al. (Zhang et al., 2025) reported that phospholipid levels (PE, PC and PA) in the breast muscle of Yangzhou geese decreased from D70 to D210, while TAG levels increased. Furthermore, integrative analysis showed that metabolites such as PC (20:0/20:4), PC (16:0/16:0), PC (16:0/18:1) + AcO, PE (18:0/20:4), and PC (P-16:0/18:1) were strongly negatively correlated with IMF, whereas SM 36:1;2 was positively correlated with IMF. Lipids not only act as solvents for volatile compounds formed during thermal processing but also generate key flavor compounds through lipolysis during cooking (Fu, Cao, Yang, & Li, 2022). However, lipid peroxidation can produce undesirable off-flavors (Shahidi & Hossain, 2022). Nie et al. (Nie et al., 2024) reported that TAGs, PCs, and PEs are major contributors to aroma retention. Additionally, phospholipids, particularly PE, PC, and PI, play critical roles in meat sensory properties (e.g., flavor, tenderness, and drip loss) owing to their function as major PUFA reservoirs in muscle tissues, thereby influencing water-holding capacity and texture (Cao et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2020). From a nutritional perspective, phospholipids also provide physiological benefits, including antioxidant activity, cognitive support, and immune regulation (Sun, Chen, Wang, & Lin, 2018). Although no significant difference in IMF content was observed between the D182 and D154 groups, the D182 group may have greater flavor potential, as lipidomic data showed higher levels of specific PAs, PCs, PGs, PIs, PSs, and carnitines. Seven fatty acylcarnitines were identified as DELs between the D154 and D182 groups, including carnitine-C20:1, carnitine-C20:2, carnitine-C18:1, carnitine-C18 OH, carnitine-C16 OH, carnitine-C18, and carnitine-C16. Carnitines help prevent the abnormal accumulation of organic acids within cells. As dietary supplements, carnitine and acyl-carnitine support cardiac function and may aid in managing conditions such as Alzheimer's disease, peripheral arterial disease, and metabolic disorders (Cao et al., 2019; Dambrova et al., 2022).

For the pairwise comparison between D182 and D365 groups, DEGs were predominantly enriched in lipid metabolism pathways, with PLA2G10, PLA2G4B, PROCA1, and TPT1 identified as key genes. The phospholipase A2 (PLA2) superfamily hydrolyzes phospholipids into fatty acids and lysophospholipids and act as a crucial regulator in the production of lipid mediators, particularly in arachidonic acid metabolism involved in eicosanoid synthesis (Murakami, 2017; Murakami, Sato, & Taketomi, 2020). However, accumulating evidence suggests that PLA2 have broader physiological roles, including phospholipid remodeling for membrane homeostasis, fatty acid β-oxidation for energy production, and barrier lipid formation on body surfaces (Murakami, 2023). Notably, PROCA1 has been proposed as a candidate gene influencing IMP deposition in the muscle tissues of Jingyuan and Pudong chickens (Zhang et al., 2021). Among the pathways shared by DEGs, DEMs, and DELs, glycerophospholipid metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation were particularly associated with Kangle chicken meat quality. ATP is generated through two main pathways: anaerobic glycolysis and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. Oxygen persists in muscle tissue following stunning and exsanguination and is gradually depleted during post-mortem metabolism rather than instantaneously (Ramos, Li, Elzo, Wohlgemuth, & Scheffler, 2020). Moreover, mitochondria maintain structural integrity and functional activity in early post-mortem muscle, potentially contributing to pH decline and thereby influencing meat quality development(England et al., 2018; C. Wang, Matarneh, Gerrard, & Tan, 2022). Oxidative phosphorylation has also been identified as a key pathway enriched with DEGs in skeletal muscle at different growth stages (Hu et al., 2021). In this study, compared with the D154 and D182 groups, the D365 group showed significant down-regulation of genes and metabolites associated with oxidative phosphorylation, including ATP5I, COX4I1, COX5A, COX7A2, COX7B, NDUFA1, NDUFV2, and UQCR1. ATP5I encodes subunit E of the mitochondrial F0 complex within ATP synthase, which is essential for proper assembly and ATP generation (Pícková, Potocký, & Houstek, 2005). Cytochrome c oxidase (COX), a multi-subunit complex and the terminal enzyme of the mitochondrial respiratory chain, couples electron transfer from cytochrome c to oxygen with the generation of a proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane. NDUFA1 and NDUFV2 are involved in electron transfer from NADH to ubiquinone, while UQCRC1 is a conserved core subunit of mitochondrial complex III interacts with cytochrome c and is implicated in apoptotic processes (Hung et al., 2021). The electron transport chain drives ATP production (Vercellino & Sazanov, 2022). In this study, ATP levels were higher in the breast muscle of the D365 group than in the other groups, which may be associated with limited oxygen availability in post-slaughter muscle. Although oxidative phosphorylation is highly efficient, ATP production in post-mortem muscle relies predominantly on glycolysis (Chen et al., 2020). We also observed an association between NADH and IMF in the integrative network analysis. The D365 group exhibited the highest NADH levels and the lowest NAD+/NADH ratio. A reduced NAD+/NADH ratio can promote lipid synthesis and tends to decrease with age (Yang et al., 2022). Overall, these findings suggest that the D154 and D182 groups maintain relatively stronger mitochondrial function in post-mortem pectoral muscle. However, mitochondrial function declines over time after slaughter. In the D365 group, reduced antioxidant levels (e.g., glutathione, L-carnosine, and succinic acid) combined with higher IMF content may increase susceptibility to lipid oxidation, thereby compromising meat quality (Dragoev, 2024).

Conclusion

In this study, breast meat from D365 chickens exhibited the highest IMF content and 24 h and 48 h drip loss, as well as the lowest pH, compared with that from D154 and D182 chickens. Metabolomic profiling showed that the D365 group had the lowest abundance of most meat quality–related amino acids, nucleotides, organic acids and derivatives, and fatty acids. By contrast, alanine, proline, ATP, GMP, and AMP levels were the highest in the D365 group. Lipidomic analysis further revealed that the D365 group had lower levels of glycerophospholipids and phospholipids but higher levels of TAGs and SMs than the other groups. Although no significant difference in IMF content was observed between the D182 and D154 groups, higher levels of specific PAs, PCs, PGs, PIs, PSs, and carnitines were detected in the D182 group. Integrated multi-omics analysis indicated that glycerophospholipid metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, and glycerolipid metabolism are closely associated with Kangle chicken meat quality. In total, 21 metabolites (DEMs and DELs) and 10 DEGs were identified as key candidates influencing IMF and pH. The D365 group showed significant downregulation of genes and metabolites involved in oxidative phosphorylation. Furthermore, the D154 and D182 groups appeared to maintain stronger mitochondrial function in post-mortem pectoral muscles. By contrast, in the D365 group, reduced antioxidant levels (e.g., glutathione, L-carnosine, and succinic acid), together with higher IMF content and declining mitochondrial function after slaughter, may increase susceptibility to lipid oxidation, thereby compromising meat quality. Overall, these findings suggest that slaughtering Kangle chickens at approximately 182 days of age may provide optimal meat quality. This study offers new mechanistic insights into age-related metabolic changes in Kangle chickens and identifies potential biomarkers for meat quality evaluation.
