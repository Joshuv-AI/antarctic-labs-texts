A BOPPPS-structured narrative medicine intervention to enhance empathy and reduce ageism among undergraduate nursing students
Authors: Shuai Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRapid population aging has intensified the complexity of gerontological nursing care, highlighting the need for educational approaches that foster empathy and reduce ageist attitudes among nursing students. Narrative medicine has been proposed as a humanistic pedagogy to enhance perspective-taking; however, its integration within structured instructional models in nursing education remains underexplored. Objective: This study aimed to describe within-cohort changes in empathy and ageist attitudes among undergraduate nursing students following participation in a narrative-medicine module structured using the BOPPPS instructional framework, and to examine the association between these two constructs.MethodsA quasi-experimental, one-group pretest–post test design was employed in a compulsory undergraduate nursing course. A total of 120 nursing students participated. Empathy was measured using the Jefferson Scale of Empathy–Health Professions Student version, and ageism was assessed using the Chinese version of the Fraboni Scale of Ageism. Paired-samples t tests were used to examine pre–post changes, and correlation and exploratory regression analyses were conducted to summarize associations between empathy and ageism.ResultsMean empathy scores increased from pre-test to post-test (mean difference = 0.45, 95% CI [0.33, 0.57]; t = 7.18, p < 0.001), while mean ageism scores decreased (mean difference = − 0.40, 95% CI [− 0.53, − 0.27]; t = − 6.02, p < 0.001), reflecting small-to-moderate effect sizes. Post-intervention empathy was moderately and negatively associated with ageism (r = − 0.42, p < 0.001). Exploratory regression analysis indicated that empathy accounted for a modest proportion of variance in ageist attitudes (B = − 0.34, p < 0.001).ConclusionsThese findings describe modest within-cohort changes and associations observed following a narrative-based instructional module delivered within a structured teaching framework. Given the one-group pre–post design and the compulsory course context, results should be interpreted cautiously due to predictable threats to internal validity, including testing and maturation effects. The proposed CARE-AGE framework is therefore presented as an evidence-informed conceptual curriculum model rather than a validated intervention. The study contributes descriptive insight into how theory-guided narrative pedagogy may be situated within gerontological nursing education.

Introduction

Population aging is accelerating globally and reshaping the demands placed on health-care systems. According to the World Health Organization, adults aged 60 years and above accounted for approximately 13% of the global population in 2020 and are projected to exceed 21% by 2050, based on estimates reported in the WHO Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) framework and related demographic projections [1, 2]. Using a different age threshold of 65 years and above, the United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs, Population Division, similarly estimates that older adults will represent approximately 16% of the global population by 2050 [3]. Although these sources differ in age definitions, both converge on a clear demographic trajectory: the proportion of older adults is increasing at an unprecedented pace. In China, the trend is even more pronounced, with adults aged 60 and above comprising 18.7% of the population in 2023 and projected to exceed 30% by 2050 [4]. As aging accelerates, the demand for high-quality geriatric care is growing rapidly, yet global health systems continue to struggle with capacity gaps in addressing the complex needs of older adults with multiple chronic conditions [5]. This demographic transition has substantive implications for the nursing workforce, which must be prepared to care for older adults with complex, chronic, and long-term care needs.

The rise in frailty underscores the growing clinical complexity associated with aging populations. Longitudinal data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) have documented a gradual but steady increase in frailty prevalence across successive survey waves, using multidimensional criteria that include functional limitation, comorbidity, and physiological decline [6]. Such trends imply that older adults will increasingly require care that is continuous, relational, and sensitive to both physical and psychosocial vulnerability. This heightened complexity directly affects nursing practice: caring for frail older adults demands not only technical competence but also strong empathic engagement, patience, and communication skills. As a result, population aging is not only a demographic challenge but an educational one, calling for pedagogical approaches that cultivate humanistic and relational competencies in nursing students.

Despite escalating needs, the geriatric-nursing workforce remains insufficient. Prior reports indicate that specialists in geriatric care represent only a small proportion of the national nursing workforce in China, highlighting both staffing shortages and a lack of advanced training pathways [7, 8]. Compounding this problem, several studies have found that nursing students often hold stereotypical or pessimistic views about working with older adults, perceiving geriatric care as less prestigious, emotionally taxing, or lacking career advancement opportunities [9, 10].

Empathy—understood across affective, cognitive, and behavioral dimensions—plays a central role in patient-centered nursing, influencing communication quality, therapeutic alliance, and patient satisfaction. Theoretical models such as patient-centered communication frameworks and moral imagination in nursing emphasize empathy as a foundational attribute for understanding patients’ lived experiences and tailoring care to individualized needs. In gerontology, empathy is especially critical because older adults frequently experience loneliness, functional dependence, and complex emotional needs. Yet empirical studies show that empathy among nursing students does not automatically increase with clinical exposure and may even decline over time due to stress, desensitization, or limited reflective practice opportunities [11]. This suggests a need for structured educational strategies that actively cultivate empathic understanding rather than assuming it develops naturally.

Narrative medicine, introduced by Charon (2001), provides a framework for strengthening narrative competence—the ability to recognize, absorb, interpret, and be moved by patients’ stories [12]. Existing studies in medical and nursing education suggest that narrative-based pedagogical approaches have been associated with improvements in empathy, reflective capacity, and communication skills under specific instructional conditions; however, reported effects vary widely across disciplines, intervention intensity, study designs, and outcome measures [11]. Most evidence is derived from short-term, self-report–based studies conducted in single institutional settings, and findings are therefore context-dependent rather than generalizable. Furthermore, studies examining narrative-medicine approaches in Chinese nursing education remain limited, with notable barriers including insufficient faculty training and inconsistent implementation across institutions [13]. Importantly, these studies do not establish a uniform or causal effect of narrative medicine on empathy, but instead indicate that empathy gains have been reported under specific instructional conditions. In nursing education, particularly within short compulsory modules and cohort-based classroom settings, the level of empirical support remains limited. Accordingly, the present study does not seek to evaluate the effectiveness of narrative medicine as an educational intervention, but rather to document observed educational responses within a single cohort and instructional context.

The BOPPPS model—comprising Bridge-in, Objective, Pre-assessment, Participatory Learning, Post-assessment, and Summary—was originally developed within Canadian higher education faculty development programs, most notably through the Instructional Skills Workshop (ISW), and has since been adopted more broadly in health professions education [14]. The BOPPPS instructional model was selected not because of its prevalence, but due to its structured alignment with the pedagogical requirements of narrative-based learning. Narrative medicine emphasizes staged engagement, guided reflection, and iterative meaning-making, all of which benefit from explicit instructional scaffolding. Within the BOPPPS framework, the Bridge-in component supports emotional engagement and perspective-taking through narrative prompts; Objectives clarify reflective learning goals; Pre-assessment elicits students’ baseline attitudes; Participatory learning provides space for narrative sharing and role-play; Post-assessment captures immediate educational responses; and Summary facilitates integrative reflection. This structural sequencing offers a coherent organizational container for narrative activities without presuming instructional effectiveness. Accordingly, in this study BOPPPS is treated as an instructional organizing framework rather than as an evidence-based intervention in itself. Despite its potential synergy with narrative medicine, the combined use of these approaches in nursing education—particularly in the context of geriatric care in China—has not been systematically studied. Moreover, existing applications often emphasize knowledge acquisition rather than humanistic competencies or attitudinal change [15].

Despite growing interest in humanistic approaches within health professions education, important gaps remain in the existing literature. Research on narrative medicine in undergraduate nursing education—particularly within the Chinese context—remains limited and often fragmented, with most studies relying on short-term workshops, elective humanities courses, or small-scale qualitative designs. Moreover, narrative-based teaching has rarely been embedded within a theoretically grounded instructional framework that ensures pedagogical structure, learner engagement, and outcome alignment. At the same time, although empathy and ageism are widely recognized as critical determinants of geriatric care quality, few studies have examined how structured educational interventions may simultaneously influence both constructs among nursing students. Existing work tends to address empathy or attitudes toward older adults in isolation, without exploring their interrelationship or implications for curriculum design. Consequently, there is a lack of empirically informed curricular models that integrate narrative medicine with established teaching frameworks to support systematic development of humanistic competencies in gerontological nursing education.

In response to these gaps, the present study examined observed changes in empathy and ageism among undergraduate nursing students following participation in a narrative-medicine module structured using the BOPPPS teaching model within a gerontological nursing course. Given the one-group pre–post design, the study does not seek to establish causal effects but rather to document within-cohort changes and associations. Based on these findings, a theory-informed and descriptively guided conceptual curriculum framework is proposed to guide future instructional innovation and research in empathy-centered geriatric nursing education.

Methodology

Research design

This study employed a one-group pretest–posttest quasi-experimental design embedded within a compulsory undergraduate nursing course. The design was used to document within-cohort changes in self-reported empathy and ageist attitudes over the instructional period, as well as to explore statistical associations between these constructs. The study was not designed to establish causal effects or to evaluate the effectiveness of a curriculum framework or instructional model. All findings should therefore be interpreted as descriptive and exploratory, reflecting observed educational responses within a specific classroom context.

Sample, sample size, and sampling method

Participants were recruited using convenience cluster sampling from intact undergraduate nursing classes at a single institution, yielding a total sample of 120 students. The study was conducted in an undergraduate nursing program in Dezhou, Shandong Province, China. All students enrolled in the selected classes were invited to participate. Randomization at the class or individual level was not feasible due to curricular constraints. Demographic information collected included gender and an academic year range; however, academic year level was not retained as an analytic variable in the final dataset and therefore was not included as a covariate in statistical analyses. As a result, potential differences related to training stage (e.g., second- versus fourth-year students) were not examined and may influence the interpretation of empathy and ageism scores. This limitation is acknowledged in the interpretation of findings.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) full-time enrollment in the undergraduate nursing program; (2) voluntary participation with signed informed consent; (3) no self-reported severe mental or physical conditions that would prevent participation in routine classroom activities and ability to complete the full course and data collection; and (4) no prior training in narrative medicine or the BOPPPS system. Exclusion criteria were: (1) prior participation in similar humanities education research; (2) attendance below 80%; or (3) incomplete scale measurements due to withdrawal. Eligibility criteria were based on self-report and course enrollment status. Students who indicated willingness to participate and were able to complete the questionnaires independently were included. No medical or psychological screening was conducted, and no health-related information was collected or verified. The reference to the absence of severe mental or physical illness reflects a self-reported participation condition rather than a clinically assessed exclusion criterion.

Sample size was determined primarily by course enrollment and feasibility. A post hoc power analysis indicated that the sample size was adequate to detect small-to-moderate within-group changes using paired-samples statistical tests at a significance level of 0.05 with a statistical power of 0.80.

Instrument

Two standardized instruments were used: the Jefferson Scale of Empathy–Health Professions (JSE-HP) and the Chinese version of the Fraboni Scale of Ageism (C-FSA). Both instruments have demonstrated strong reliability and validity in Chinese nursing education contexts. All questionnaires were administered anonymously via the Questionnaire Star platform to ensure standardization, confidentiality, and data security. For descriptive reporting, mean item scores were presented to facilitate interpretability across instruments with different item numbers and response ranges. For clarity, all correlational and regression analyses were conducted using consistently coded scale scores. Linear rescaling of scores (e.g., reporting mean item scores rather than total scores) does not mathematically affect Pearson correlation coefficients. This reporting strategy did not alter the underlying scale properties or analytic procedures.

Empathy was measured using the Chinese version of the JSE-HP, originally developed by Hojat et al. [16] and subsequently validated for Chinese nursing students by An et al. [17]. This 20-item scale uses a 7-point Likert format (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree), yielding total scores from 20 to 140, with higher scores reflecting greater empathy. The original three-factor structure—perspective taking, compassionate care, and empathic concern—is retained. Previous research in China has reported acceptable reliability and validity (Cronbach’s α ≈ 0.75; test–retest reliability ≈ 0.66).

The JSE-HP was used in this study with permission from Thomas Jefferson University (see Supplementary File: Permission letter.pdf).

Ageism was measured using the Chinese version of the Fraboni Scale of Ageism (C-FSA), adapted from the original developed by Fraboni et al. [18–20]. The 23-item instrument covers three dimensions: alienation, stereotype, and emotional attitudes. Prior studies demonstrated strong content validity (CVI = 0.81–1.00; average CVI = 0.92) and internal consistency for the total scale (Cronbach’s α = 0.92) as well as for subscales: alienation (α = 0.92), stereotype (α = 0.87), and emotional attitudes (α = 0.83). Test–retest reliability was 0.74. The scale uses a 5-point Likert format (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating stronger ageist attitudes.

Internal consistency for this study’s sample was also evaluated. For the JSE-HP (20 items), reliability was high: pre-test Cronbach’s α = 0.89, McDonald’s ω = 0.90; post-test α = 0.93, ω = 0.94. For the C-FSA (23 items), internal consistency was similarly strong: pre-test α = 0.88, ω = 0.89; post-test α = 0.90, ω = 0.91. These coefficients indicate acceptable to excellent reliability for both instruments in this study. It is noted that linear rescaling of scores (e.g., reporting mean item scores rather than total scores) does not mathematically affect Pearson correlation coefficients; therefore, all correlational and regression analyses were conducted using consistently coded scale scores.

Data gathering

Data were collected at two time points: immediately prior to the instructional period (pre-test) and within one week after completion of the final instructional session (post-test). Data collection took place during scheduled class time to ensure a standardized administration environment. Prior to participation, students received a written explanation of the study’s purpose, procedures, and voluntary nature. Participation was explicitly stated to be optional, and students were informed that choosing not to participate or withdrawing at any time would have no effect on their course grades, academic standing, or relationship with instructors.

To minimize any perception of coercion, course instructors were not involved in administering the questionnaires and did not have access to individual responses. Surveys were completed anonymously via the Questionnaire Star platform, and no identifying information was collected. Participants generated unique self-selected identification codes to allow matching of pre- and post-test responses while preserving anonymity.

Following completion of the pre-test, students participated in a structured instructional module delivered as part of the regular curriculum. The module incorporated elements of the BOPPPS framework and included learning activities such as case discussions, reflective writing, and role-playing related to older adult care and narrative medicine. After completion of the instructional period, the same instruments were re-administered to document within-cohort changes in empathy and ageist attitudes over time. Given the one-group pre–post design, the data are interpreted as descriptive of temporal change rather than as evidence of causal effects. Prior to analysis, data were screened for completeness, outliers, and scoring accuracy. Reverse-coded items were verified, and descriptive statistics were examined to ensure plausibility and internal consistency.

Experimental treatment: structure of the BOPPPS-based intervention

The instructional module was structured as an interactive learning experience emphasizing perspective taking and engagement with older adult care. Each of the six BOPPPS components was applied to address pedagogical components relevant to empathy development. Students were introduced to real-world cases and emotional narratives of older adult patients to introduce relevance and contextual grounding for learning. Clear cognitive, affective, and reflective learning objectives were presented, including strengthening understanding of older patients’ psychological needs and reflecting on personal biases associated with ageism.

The educational intervention was embedded within the existing gerontological nursing course and delivered over a four-week period, with one 90-minute session per week. The intervention integrated narrative-medicine activities into the BOPPPS instructional framework to align learning objectives, teaching activities, and reflective outcomes. All sessions were facilitated by an experienced nursing educator with more than ten years of teaching experience in gerontological nursing and prior training in narrative-based teaching methods. A standardized instructional guide was used to ensure consistency across sessions.

Each session followed the six stages of the BOPPPS model:

Bridge-in

Sessions began with brief narrative stimuli, included short written case vignettes, brief video clips, and first-person patient narratives related to older adults’ healthcare experiences. Written vignettes typically ranged from 200 to 400 words and focused on themes such as functional decline, communication barriers, dignity, and family dynamics in aging care. Video materials were limited to 3–5 min and depicted realistic clinical or community-based scenarios involving older adults.

Objectives

Learning objectives were stated explicitly at the beginning of each session. Objectives emphasized awareness of older adults’ lived experiences, recognition of age-related stereotypes, and reflective consideration of nurse–patient interactions in geriatric settings.

Pre-assessment

Informal pre-assessment activities were conducted using short reflective questions or open-ended prompts to explore students’ existing perceptions of aging and geriatric nursing.

Participatory learning

Participatory learning constituted the core instructional component. Students engaged with these narrative materials through guided small-group discussions and individual reflective writing. Reflection prompts encouraged students to consider the older person’s perspective, emotional experiences, and potential implications for nursing care. Representative examples of narrative vignettes, reflective prompts, and a worksheet template are provided in the Supplementary Materials to support transparency and replicability.

Post-assessment

At the end of each session, students completed brief written reflections or participated in whole-class feedback discussions to summarize key insights. These activities were used to reinforce learning objectives and encourage integration of narrative insights with course content.

Summary

Each session concluded with instructor-led synthesis, linking students’ reflections to relevant concepts in gerontological nursing, such as person-centered care, dignity, and communication with older adults.

To promote consistency in delivery, the instructor followed a session-level implementation checklist outlining the intended components of each BOPPPS phase (bridge-in, objectives, participatory learning, post-assessment, and summary). However, fidelity monitoring was limited to instructor self-adherence, and no independent observation or formal fidelity rating was conducted. This limitation is acknowledged, and future studies should incorporate independent fidelity assessment to strengthen implementation rigor.

Data analysis

The data analysis focused on describing within-group changes and associations rather than testing causal hypotheses. Prior to statistical analysis, all data were screened for completeness, plausibility, and scoring accuracy. Reverse-coded items were verified according to the respective scoring manuals, and descriptive statistics were examined to identify outliers or anomalous values. Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis (listwise deletion), and only participants with matched pre- and post-test responses were included in paired analyses. Following verification during manuscript revision, all analyses were conducted using a finalized dataset in which participant matching and item scoring procedures were rechecked to ensure consistency and accuracy.

Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and 95% confidence intervals, were calculated for empathy and ageism scores at both pre-test and post-test. Paired-samples t-tests were used to examine pre–post changes, as preliminary assumption checks indicated no substantial departures from normality in the difference scores; nonparametric alternatives (Wilcoxon signed-rank tests) were specified a priori but were not required for the primary analyses. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine the association between post-intervention empathy and ageism scores. Correlation coefficients were interpreted as indicators of association rather than prediction or causation. No multivariable regression analyses with covariate adjustment were conducted due to the exploratory nature of the study and the absence of a comparison group.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed). Results were reported in accordance with established guidelines for transparent reporting of quantitative educational research.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed by the authorized institutional office responsible for oversight of educational research at the participating nursing institution in Dezhou, Shandong Province, China, and received a written exemption determination from formal Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval. This determination was based on the fact that the study involved anonymous, voluntary questionnaire-based educational research and posed minimal risk to participants. Accordingly, in this institutional context, studies of this type are handled through an exemption/waiver determination rather than full IRB review.

All participants were fully informed about the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, and anticipated benefits, and written informed consent was obtained prior to participation. Participation was voluntary, and participants could withdraw at any time without academic or professional consequences. Confidentiality and data protection were strictly maintained. No personal identifiers were collected; participants were assigned anonymous codes, and all records were stored securely with access restricted to the research team. Data collected through the Questionnaire Star platform were password-protected, analyzed in aggregate form, and used solely for research purposes. All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 120 nursing students completed both the pre-test and post-test assessments and were included in the final analysis. Of these participants, 77.5% were female and 22.5% were male. Gender was the only demographic variable retained in the final dataset; information on year level, age, or other background characteristics was collected in broad ranges during administration but was not retained in the anonymized analytic dataset and therefore could not be analyzed.

At baseline, no statistically significant differences in empathy or ageism scores were observed by gender (all p > 0.05). Because other demographic variables were not available, no further subgroup comparisons were conducted.

All participants who completed the pre-test also completed the post-test using the same anonymous identification code, allowing complete matching of responses across time points. Partial or unmatched questionnaires were excluded prior to analysis and were not included in the final sample. As a result, no attrition was observed between the pre-test and post-test assessments within the analyzed cohort.

Changes in empathy and ageism scores over time

The paired-samples t-test revealed statistically significant changes in both empathy and ageist attitudes following the intervention. Empathy scores, as measured by the JSE-HP, increased from a pre-test mean of 4.12 (SD = 0.46) to a post-test mean of 4.57 (SD = 0.52). This change represented a mean difference of + 0.45 (95% CI [0.33, 0.57]), and was statistically significant (t = 7.18, p < 0.001), with a small-to-moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.46). Similarly, ageist attitudes, assessed using the C-FSA, showed a statistically significant decrease from a pre-test mean of 3.21 (SD = 0.50) to a post-test mean of 2.81 (SD = 0.47), yielding a mean difference of − 0.40 (95% CI [− 0.53, − 0.27]). This reduction was statistically significant (t = − 6.02, p < 0.001) and corresponded to a small effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.38) (Table 1).

These results indicate modest but measurable changes in students’ empathic orientation and attitudes toward older adults over the course of the intervention. Given the one-group pre–post design, the findings reflect observed within-cohort changes rather than definitive intervention effects.

Correlation between empathy and ageism

The correlation analyses were conducted to examine the relationship between empathy and ageist attitudes at the post-intervention time point. Pearson correlation analysis revealed a statistically significant negative association between empathy and ageism (r = − 0.42, p < 0.001). Spearman’s rank correlation yielded a similar result (ρ = −0.39, p < 0.001), indicating consistency across parametric and nonparametric methods (Table 2). This estimate was computed using the finalized and verified analytic dataset comprising matched pre–post cases (N = 120), in which participant matching was confirmed and all reverse-coded items were verified according to the scoring manuals. The magnitude of these correlations falls within the moderate range according to conventional benchmarks for psychosocial research, suggesting that higher empathy levels were associated with lower ageist attitudes while the two constructs remain conceptually distinct.

Discussion

Interpretation of the main findings

This study examined observed changes in empathy and ageist attitudes among undergraduate nursing students following participation in a narrative-medicine module structured using the BOPPPS teaching model. The results showed modest but statistically significant improvements in empathy and small reductions in ageist attitudes. Such effect sizes are consistent with previous educational studies indicating that short-term, curriculum-embedded humanistic interventions tend to produce incremental attitudinal changes rather than large-scale transformations [9, 10, 15].

Narrative medicine has been widely conceptualized as a pedagogical approach that enhances reflective capacity, empathy, and professional identity formation through engagement with patients’ lived experiences [13]. Empirical studies in health professions education have similarly reported modest gains in empathy following narrative-based or story-centered instructional strategies [14–16]. The present findings align with this body of literature, reinforcing the view that empathy development in nursing students is a gradual process shaped by repeated reflective exposure rather than a rapidly modifiable outcome.

The observed reduction in ageist attitudes may reflect increased awareness of age-related stereotypes and assumptions, a mechanism frequently emphasized in gerontological nursing education [10, 12]. Prior systematic reviews have shown that educational interventions can reduce ageism among health professional students, although reported effects are typically small to moderate and vary depending on intervention intensity and duration [9, 10]. In this regard, the present findings fall within the expected range for a single-course educational module.

Given the one-group pre–post design, these findings should be interpreted as within-cohort changes over time rather than definitive intervention effects, a limitation that has also been acknowledged in related narrative nursing and educational studies conducted in similar contexts [15, 17].

In addition, the compulsory nature of the course and the repeated measurement design may have contributed to demand characteristics and social desirability effects. Because empathy and attitudes toward older adults are socially valued attributes within nursing education, students may have been inclined to respond in ways they perceived as consistent with instructional goals, particularly at post-test. Repeated exposure to the same instruments may also have increased score familiarity or sensitization, independent of substantive attitudinal change. These factors are well-recognized sources of inflation in one-group pre–post educational studies and may partially account for the observed magnitude of change. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted with caution, as they likely reflect a combination of genuine reflective engagement and context-related response effects rather than isolated instructional impact.

Relationship between empathy and ageism

The moderate negative association observed between empathy and ageist attitudes following the intervention is consistent with theoretical assumptions and empirical evidence suggesting that empathic understanding may mitigate negative stereotypes toward older adults [9, 11]. However, the strength of the association in this study indicates that empathy and ageism are related but distinct constructs rather than inverse equivalents.

Existing literature has highlighted that ageism among nursing students is influenced by multiple factors, including cultural context, educational exposure, prior contact with older adults, and societal narratives of aging [10, 12]. While empathy-focused interventions may contribute to reducing ageist attitudes, they represent only one component of a broader educational strategy. The exploratory regression findings in the present study further support this interpretation, as empathy accounted for a modest proportion of variance in ageism scores. It should be noted that an earlier version of the analysis conducted during manuscript development yielded a comparatively stronger correlation between empathy and ageism. Following methodological review and data verification during the peer-review process, the analytic dataset was re-established to ensure accuracy and consistency. Specifically, participant matching between pre–post responses was verified, reverse-coded items were corrected according to the scoring manuals, and only complete matched cases were retained. The final correlation reported in this study (r = − 0.42) is based on this verified dataset (N = 120) and represents a more conservative and methodologically robust estimate of the association between these constructs. This magnitude is consistent with expectations for psychosocial variables measured via self-report and supports the interpretation that empathy and ageism are meaningfully related but not redundant constructs. This multifactorial perspective is consistent with prior research emphasizing that ageism cannot be fully addressed through individual-level attitudinal change alone but requires systematic curricular and institutional attention [9, 12].

Educational implications of integrating narrative medicine and the BOPPPS model

From an educational standpoint, integrating narrative medicine within a structured instructional framework such as the BOPPPS model may offer a feasible approach to embedding humanistic education into existing nursing curricula. Narrative medicine emphasizes attentiveness, reflection, and interpretive skills as core components of empathic clinical practice [13], while the BOPPPS model provides a pedagogically grounded structure that aligns learning objectives, participatory activities, and reflective consolidation.

Previous studies applying the BOPPPS model in nursing education have primarily focused on knowledge acquisition, skills training, and learning engagement. The present findings suggest that when narrative-based activities are embedded within such a structured framework, they may also support the development of humanistic competencies, including empathy and attitudes toward older adults, albeit with modest effects.

In this context, the CARE-AGE framework proposed in the present study should be regarded as a theory-informed conceptual curriculum model, rather than an empirically validated intervention. CARE-AGE was developed to organize and interpret observed quantitative patterns in relation to established theories of narrative medicine, empathy development, and gerontological nursing education. It was not subjected to formal needs assessment, stakeholder feedback, or independent evaluation. Similar conceptual approaches have been advocated in nursing education to guide curriculum design and inform future empirical evaluation, particularly in response to the challenges posed by population aging and increasing care complexity [1, 5].

Development of the CARE-AGE intervention program

Building on the educational context of the present study and established theory, rather than being empirically derived through needs assessment or outcome evaluation, we outline a proposed instructional framework titled CARE-AGE (Cultivating Empathy and Respect for the Aged through Guided Education). This framework is conceptually informed by existing literature on narrative-based education and by the educational context of the present study. CARE-AGE is designed to integrate interactive lectures, reflective journaling, simulation-based communication training, and community immersion with older adults. Each component is aligned with educational objectives such as fostering emotional and cognitive empathy, challenging stereotypes, and strengthening communication with older adults. Active engagement through standardized patients, guided reflections, and community visits is intended to humanize the aging experience and foster meaningful perspective shifts. This framework is intended as a conceptual reference and has not been empirically tested.

The framework aims to address commonly identified academic and clinical gaps in empathy and ageism education by emphasizing reflective and values-oriented learning. If implemented and empirically evaluated in future studies, CARE-AGE may serve as a structured reference model for examining changes in empathy, age-related attitudes, and student engagement in gerontological nursing education. By grounding its conceptual rationale in clearly articulated learning objectives and educational principles, CARE-AGE is presented as a proposed educational framework aligned with contemporary discussions in nursing education and healthy aging policy (Table 4). Future research should subject the CARE-AGE framework to systematic evaluation, including needs assessment, learner feedback, and outcome-linked testing across diverse educational contexts.

Conclusion

This study documented modest within-cohort changes in empathy and ageist attitudes among nursing students following participation in a narrative-medicine module structured using the BOPPPS instructional framework. These findings should be interpreted as descriptive observations rather than evidence of instructional effectiveness. The findings suggest that embedding narrative-based reflective activities—such as storytelling, role-play, and guided feedback—within a learner-centered and theoretically grounded teaching model may be associated with the development of humanistic orientations in undergraduate nursing education. Rather than establishing causal effects, the present study provides preliminary evidence that empathy represents a measurable and potentially responsive educational construct, and that higher levels of empathy are associated with lower ageist attitudes. These observations align with existing literature emphasizing the role of structured reflective pedagogy in addressing attitudinal dimensions of gerontological care.

The proposed CARE-AGE model should be understood as an evidence-informed conceptual approach rather than a validated intervention, offering a structured reference for integrating narrative medicine into gerontological nursing education. In this regard, the study contributes to ongoing discussions in health professions education regarding outcomes-oriented curriculum design and the intentional cultivation of humanistic competencies.

Future research should examine this framework using more rigorous and diverse study designs, including controlled and longitudinal approaches, to assess the sustainability and transferability of observed changes. Extending narrative-based pedagogies to other health professions and institutional contexts, as well as strengthening faculty development in narrative teaching methods, may further advance the integration of humanistic values into professional education.

Limitations and recommendations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, reliance on self-reported measures may have introduced social desirability bias, potentially inflating empathy scores or underestimating ageist attitudes. Second, the study was conducted at a single nursing institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other educational or cultural contexts. Third, the study employed a one-group pre–post design without a control group, restricting causal inference and limiting the ability to rule out alternative explanations such as maturation or contextual influences. In addition, outcomes were assessed only immediately after the instructional module, and the sustainability of observed changes over time remains unknown. Fourth, demographic information available for analysis was limited. Apart from gender, variables such as year level or age were not systematically collected, which may constrain interpretation of individual differences in empathy development or age-related attitudes. Potential cohort-related effects could therefore not be examined. Finally, although exploratory regression analysis indicated a statistical association between empathy and ageism, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. The cross-sectional and exploratory nature of the analysis does not support predictive or causal conclusions.

Future research should employ more rigorous designs, including controlled or longitudinal approaches, to examine the durability and transferability of narrative-based educational interventions. Incorporating mixed-methods designs, particularly qualitative analyses of student narratives, may provide deeper insight into the mechanisms underlying empathic development and attitudinal change. Moreover, empirical evaluation of conceptual frameworks such as CARE-AGE across diverse institutional and cultural settings would contribute to the refinement of gerontological nursing curricula and support evidence-informed educational responses to population aging.
