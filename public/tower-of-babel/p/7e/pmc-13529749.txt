CV19global: harmonized epidemiology, policy, mobility and weather indicators from two years and 54 countries
Authors: Stavros N. Moutsis, Christos Karapiperis, Lefteris Angelis, Christos A. Ouzounis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We introduce CV19global, a 3-million datapoint compilation of the COVID-19 pandemic tracking transmission patterns and response measures in 54 countries for the years 2020 and 2021. The harmonized dataset covers reports on epidemiological trends, environmental factors, socio-economic indices, vaccination efforts and government policies compiled from several primary data sources. CV19global maintains precision and high coverage across selected parameters, with a focus on seasonal elements, facilitating the discovery of insights into the pandemic and the effectiveness of interventions on a global scale. We describe the properties of 82 indicators and outline potential applications. CV19global is a data framework for COVID-19 that offers researchers and policymakers an additional capacity to benchmark responses, refine strategies and re-analyze pandemic patterns, enabling evidence-based decisions and improved public health outcomes.

Background & Summary

High-quality, standardized datasets have been essential for understanding and mitigating COVID-19. Modeling of transmission dynamics and the evaluation of intervention impacts have been enabled, for the first time, by accurate and comprehensive data, thus giving a basis for evidence-based decision-making and resource allocation1. Reliable data have also allowed the identification of risk factors, vulnerable populations and variations across demographics and regions, hence helping with effective public health planning and communication2. International collaboration, support for research and development and maybe improved preparedness against future public health challenges are ensured by transparent and well-curated datasets. As shown, such large-scale datasets underpin informed strategies and sustained pandemic response3.

We collected and combined data from reliable, publicly available data sources in order to create a harmonized COVID-19 dataset that captures various facets of response and documentation for COVID-19, with a focus on seasonality and environmental parameters4. More specifically, we have combined data from the World Health Organization (WHO) COVID-19 dashboard5, the Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker6 and Google COVID-19 open data3 as well as National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) (Global Historical Climatology Network daily, GHCNd)7, the Tropospheric Emission Monitoring Internet Service (TEMIS) ultraviolet (UV) Index8, OurWorldInData9 and socioeconomic indicators from the World Bank10, listed on the OSF project (https://osf.io/kgxuf/).

The main added value of the CV19Global dataset11, when compared to the existing primary sources and previous efforts, consists of extensive integration, certain intentional redundancy, multidimensionality and connection of environmental factors to the pandemic, following earlier efforts12,13. This approach generally decreases the time and complexity experienced when aggregating information by gathering several datasets into a single, structured data resource. Additional variables capture governmental policy measures and socioeconomic indicators, further enhancing the analytical context that enables deeper insights into pandemic dynamics. We deployed an automated Extract-Transform-Load (ETL) pipeline that ensures accuracy, uniformity and updates, hence making it a solid foundation for scientific research and public health analysis beyond the already significant capability of existing data sources.

Methods

The COVID-19 pandemic has resulted in an unprecedented increase in data from all types of organizations in the public domain and in real time. The raw numerical data emanating from multiple institutions, countries and regions, however, tells a complex story: it is difficult to aggregate, update and interpret14. We have implemented Apache Airflow (https://airflow.apache.org/), an open-source platform used for orchestrating computational workflows and data processing pipelines to import critical data resources into a single consolidated dataset. Our use of Airflow programmatically allows us to generate, schedule and monitor workflows, which facilitates the updating of particular data from disparate origins.

Airflow structures these computational pipelines as directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) of tasks, each interdependent and executed in a precise sequence. Our pipeline is hosted on the Hypatia Elixir-GR infrastructure (https://hypatia.athenarc.gr/) and employs a message queue to synchronize a diverse team of workers. The database server on Hypatia, serving CV19global, supports storage, a PostgreSQL database and robust compute capabilities.

The protocol is no longer active due to the cessation of consistent reporting across most sources around January 2022; CV19global is therefore a static, archived dataset. However, the Airflow pipeline remains fully documented and could be reactivated for similar data streams in future public health emergencies. This is also tagged in the corresponding README.md file (https://github.com/ouzounis/CV19global/blob/main/README.md).

We began with an established collection of open-access datasets, aiming to create two derivative corpora: one for single-country analysis and another for comparative analysis across countries. These datasets categorize variables into distinct classes (see Usage Notes) and are produced through an automated ETL pipeline (Fig. 1), orchestrated with Apache Airflow. Raw CSV files were programmatically downloaded from their primary data sources: WHO COVID-19 dashboards, Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker, Google COVID-19 Open Data, NOAA climate datasets, TEMIS UV Index and World Bank socioeconomic indicators, as listed above. Data were further standardized, including simple yet necessary metadata such as ISO country codes for interoperability.Fig. 1Data collection procedures using Airflow.

Data collection procedures using Airflow.

Country coding uses standards ISO 3166, ISO 3166-1-alpha-2 and ISO 3166-1-alpha-3 (as 3 variables) in order to merge and cross-index multiple datasets. After an initial compilation of data from up to 195 countries, some intrinsic limitations, specifically gaps in the mobility data from Google’s datasets in certain countries, required a refined country selection. The final dataset provides full data from 54 selected countries for the two-year period, corresponding to the pre-vaccine and post-vaccine phases of the COVID-19 pandemic. This enables detailed comparative analytics across these two periods and multiple regions. The country selection was primarily based on the intersection of TEMIS UV and Google mobility data availability.

First, raw CSV files are extracted and transformed, with added columns — these register the country code standards, mentioned above. Two datasets are created: the primary dataset for single-country analysis, including 76 variables as features and the augmented cross-country dataset, with an additional 3 variables (the three socioeconomic indicators, totaling 82 features). Descriptions of database tables and parameters used in constructing these datasets are provided below (see Data Records). Following transformation, the data are stored in PostgreSQL and delivered in CSV format for statistical analysis.

The datasets sourced from Google encompass information for 195 countries but also present notable limitations. For instance, UV data from the TEMIS service cover 86 countries8 and the Google dataset lacks mobility data for Switzerland. Although various techniques are available to address data gaps, we opted not to employ them because several countries exhibit extensive missing values for some features over the two-year period (see Technical Validation).

CV19global comprises 54 countries (Fig. 2) over a 2-year period, 704 days (15-Feb-2020 to 19-Jan-2022) and 82 features. This selection may give potentially a maximum of 3,117,312 data points. The actual dataset has 3,066,472 data points (37,396 country-days times 82 features), representing approximately 98.4% of the potential maximum. The difference arises because start dates are not synchronized across all countries, yielding those minor variations.Fig. 2The 54 countries covered by CV19global.

The 54 countries covered by CV19global.

Finally, capturing two years of COVID-19 available data was a deliberate but also expedient decision in order to cover the two distinct phases of the pandemic, namely the time before and after vaccine availability. This specific time frame allows for comparative analyses with respect to the impact of vaccination2, testing15 and cultural elements16. Also, several pandemic responses and public behaviors are recorded in the original data sources. A balance between data richness and computational efficiency in such a compact framework can advance the study of the pandemic dynamics across countries and conditions, with a focus on key environmental factors. Note that after this period, the monitoring strategies waned and were progressively abandoned, as COVID-19 was becoming endemic in the population17.

GDP per capita is a measure often used to compare the economic prosperity of different countries or regions and is a useful indicator of average economic output and wealth of the citizens. It gives a general indication of a country’s standard of living. However, it does not account for income distribution within the country.82 human_capital_index (numeric, time independent): degree to which a country or region mobilises the economic and professional potential of its citizens. It typically takes into account a variety of factors including the level of education, skills and health, which contributes to their ability to realise their potential as productive members of a society. The index is used to assess how well countries are developing and deploying their human capital and can have implications for a country’s economic growth and the well-being of its citizens.

Technical Validation

The validation of the CV19global dataset involved scrutiny checks for accuracy, completeness and consistency. Data preprocessing focused on discrepancies, missing values and outliers. Quality and reliability were controlled using several standard statistical methods, measures and plots. Cross-validation of the dataset against other independent sources was also performed in order to confirm its accuracy. In addition, we decided to report all the fields for which there is no missing data, yielding the set of 54 countries.

An automated ETL pipeline forms the basis for the continuous validation necessary to keep the dataset current and accurate. As a matter of fact, this further strengthens the robustness of the dataset and ensures its reliability for advanced analysis and decision-making related to the COVID-19 pandemic.

The seven primary sources were selected because they are official, widely used and independently validated elsewhere. The environmental data (NOAA for temperature/precipitation, TEMIS for UV indices) were chosen as the most complete and reliable publicly available sources for the 54 countries and 2020–2021 timeframe.

Analysis of the dataset indicated high completeness with no missing values in the selected 54 countries. The range across variables, although plausible, contains a number of negative values due to data corrections or adjustments. Outlier values may be common in pandemic-related data given the nearly real-time nature of the disease with variable reporting practices and continuous updates to prior data. These values were reviewed on a case-by-case basis to determine if they corresponded to true anomalies, data entry errors, reporting lags or unusual surges in case counts. The min/max values reported for each variable indicate plausible entries.

Statistical validation further confirmed the technical credibility of the dataset. However, technical assessment alone is not sufficient in assuring data quality. In-depth validation included domain-specific checks and contextual analysis for the reliability of the dataset to be fully endorsed. Other steps required include the assessment of the methodology of data collection, consistency of reporting across regions and any obvious influence of known events or interventions, such as lockdowns or testing campaigns, even from personal experience(s).

Simple statistical measures show that the dataset is giving a realistic and valid range of pandemic responses, gathered from the primary sources. The descriptive statistics, particularly the minimum/maximum values, confirm data point plausibility within COVID-19 reporting and response measures (Fig. 3). For instance, the logical minimum recorded values of zero for total cases and total deaths would have occurred before any case or fatality existed. On the other hand, the maximum values of total and new cases/deaths, while high, are realistic considering severe outbreaks in very densely populated areas. Similarly, maximum values for policy response indicators, such as school and workplace closures, fall within expected categorical limits that reflect a range from no measures to full restrictions. The average and median of temperature and environmental data are within established physical norms, further justifying the veracity of this dataset.

The collection encompasses a wide range of responses to the pandemic and environmental influences. Daily figures on COVID-19 cases and deaths, reported for example by Johns Hopkins University (JHU), WHO and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), do not always represent the actual number of cases or deaths that actually occurred on reported dates due to reporting delays. This is because of the lag between the occurrence of a case or death and its incorporation into public health statistics.

Because of this, there are times when the cases and deaths show negative values within the dataset due to retrospective adjustments that countries made to correct earlier over-reporting errors. While our dataset maintains these reported figures as is, it does mention these negative values as adjustments. We do this to ensure that this level of detail is captured for analysts to interpret correctly in making decisions on data integrity and transparency.

We report 30 such instances from 17 countries featuring negative numbers of new COVID-19 cases in our dataset (Table 1). These reflect efforts by national health authorities to rectify discrepancies in the case counts, largely caused by over-reporting or data entry errors. Usage Notes

Two illustrative sub-datasets are provided on OSF to demonstrate how users might explore CV19global: (A) Sub-dataset (A-pearson_new_deaths.csv) contains Pearson correlation coefficients between new_deaths and 39 factors across 54 countries; variables include epidemiological (e.g. reproduction rate), vaccination (e.g. new vaccinations), policy (e.g. school closures) and environmental (e.g. average temperature, UV indices) and are clustered in a heatmap of these correlations (Fig. 4); (B) Sub-dataset (B-pearson_new_vaccinations.csv) contains Pearson correlation coefficients between new_vaccinations and 38 factors across 49 countries (excluding new_deaths, cf. A), also clustered as above (Fig. 5). All correlation analyses and visualizations were performed using ClustVis19.
