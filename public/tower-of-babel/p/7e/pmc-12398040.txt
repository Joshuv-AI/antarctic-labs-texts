Temporal changes in the burden of type 2 diabetes mellitus across the BRICS, 1990–2021: a comprehensive analysis for the global burden of disease study 2021
Authors: Jinhua Wang, Huiwen Yang, Shuqi Chen, Sihan Lin, Huiyan Zheng, Beikun Hong, Huanyuan Luo, Yanwei Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe BRICS (Brazil, Russian, India, China and South Africa) was still in rapid development with great potential to promote the global governance of the type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). The study aimed to offer practical experience and strategies for global T2DM management, particularly for developing countries by evaluating the trends, cross-country inequalities of T2DM burden in the BRICS in the past and further predict their changes to 2035.MethodsAge-standardized rate of incidence, prevalence, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) of T2DM were calculated. Estimated annual percentage change, Joinpoint regression and age-period-cohort model were used to analyze trend, decomposition analysis were used to attribute changes in total DALYs to population aging, population growth, and DALYs change, and Bayesian age-period-cohort model was used to predict.ResultsThe cases number and ASR of T2DM incidence, prevalence and DALYs in the BRICS increased from 1990 to 2021, with obvious growth in Russia and South Africa. The highest incident relative risk (RR) in Brazil and China peaked at 55–59 years old with a notable RR in Chinese youths aged 20–24 years old, whereas it peaked at 60–64 years old in Russia, India and South Africa. The higher prevalent RR was after 65 years in the BRICS. The incident and prevalent RRs in the BRICS increased over time except for China where the incident RR decreased since 1995. Individuals born earlier had higher incident and prevalent RRs in Brazil, Russia, India and South Africa while individuals born later had higher incident RRs in China. Males bore a heavier burden of T2DM in India and China while females bore heavier in Brazil, Russia and South Africa. The impacts of population aging, population growth and epidemiological changes in China, Brazil, and Russia were the greatest respectively. The DALYs difference per 100,000 between countries with the lowest and highest SDI was 429.89 (95%CI: 187.62 to 672.17) in 1990, rising to 711.63 (95%CI: 416.73 to 1006.53) in 2021. The health inequality concentration index was − 9.60 (95%CI: -10.00 to -9.20) in 1990 and − 12.91 (95%CI: -13.71 to -12.10) in 2021. Until 2035, the incidence, prevalence and DALYs were projected to increase overall in the BRICS.ConclusionDeveloping comprehensive T2DM guidelines should be a key BRICS cooperation priority, leveraging proven aging population and chronic disease management experiences. Russia, India, and South Africa should prioritize older adults, whereas China and Brazil need to focus on middle-aged and older adults, with China additionally addressing younger-onset T2DM trends. Insulin shortages and severe infectious diseases pose major challenges, particularly in less developed countries, necessitating the expansion of early intervention programs, educational initiatives, and integrated hospital care to enhance T2DM control.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-23786-x.Keywords: Global burden of disease (GBD), Type 2 diabetes mellitus, BRICS countries, Trend, Prediction, Health inequalities, Disability adjusted life years (DALYs)

Introduction

Diabetes is a severe and chronic disease characterized by elevated blood glucose levels associated with the effect of abnormal β-cell biology on insulin action [1, 2]. The prevalence of diabetes has posed a significant challenge to public health systems globally. 537 million people worldwide lived with diabetes in 2021, leading to global health expenditure of $966 billion, which is predicted to exceed $1054 billion by 2045 [3, 4]. Notably, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) constitutes 90–95% of all diabetes cases worldwide [5]. Given its high prevalence and significant health impact, T2DM poses a substantial burden on healthcare systems and requires focused attention in global health strategies.

The BRICS (Brazil, Russia, India, China and South Africa) are the emerging markets of the global economy. They account for 41% of the global population, 24% of global GDP and over 16% of global trade [6]. Given their large population and rapid economic growth, the BRICS have become key players in shaping global health trends. Their significant demographic and economic influence means that health issues within these countries may have substantial global influence. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) in version 11, China (ranked 1 st), India (ranked 2nd), and Brazil (ranked 6th) were among the countries with the highest number of diabetes patients in 2024. Notably, China had the largest number of undiagnosed diabetes patients globally, with nearly half (49.7%) of adult diabetes patients remaining undiagnosed prior to screening. Other BRICS countries exhibit similar patterns of high diabetes prevalence and undiagnosed cases [7]. From the perspective of disease burden transition, the BRICS are experiencing a shift from communicable to non-communicable diseases. This trend is primarily attributed to rapid socio-economic development and urbanization. These changes have not only altered disease patterns but also induced lifestyle modifications, such as transformed dietary habits and decreased physical activity, which in turn worsen the diabetes epidemic [8, 9]. Furthermore, genetic susceptibility studies reveal that Africans and East Asians possess genetic backgrounds that render them more susceptible to diabetes than Caucasians [10]. The combined effect of the current enormous burden of T2DM, environmental and lifestyle changes driven by rapid development, and genetic predisposition may collectively explain that the BRICS are gradually emerging as the epicenter of the global diabetes pandemic.

Faced with this challenge, the BRICS had adopted a range of strategies to manage T2DM. Brazil’s Strategic Action Plan for Combating Non-Communicable Diseases was released in 2011 and updated in 2022 [11]. In Russia, the Federal Targeted Programme on Diabetes has been in place since 1996 to monitor the national diabetes care situation [12]. India launched the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Cancer, Diabetes, Cardiovascular Diseases, and Stroke (NPCDCS) in 2010 to provide early diagnosis and management for T2DM [13]. The Essential Public Health Service program launched in China in 2009 offers free screening, follow-up assessments, categorized interventions and examination for patients above 35 years old with T2DM [14]. Under the guidance of the SA Diabetes Declaration Implementation Strategy and the Long-term Care Model Implementation Plan, more diabetic patients in South Africa have gained control of the illness [15]. Despite national strategies, glycemic control in the BRICS was suboptimal: South Africa’s public health system saw only 10–30% of patients with HbA1c < 7.0%, Russia reported 20.7% with controlled blood sugar, India had a control rate of under 25% while Brazil and China both had control rates below 50% [16–20]. These disparities were due to significant differences in resource distribution, prevention, and disease management, highlighting the need for comparative analysis of T2DM epidemiology and disease burden across the BRICS. This study could offer scientific references for improving policies and management efficiency in the BRICS.

A previous study identified the upward trend of T2DM incidence within the BRICS from 1990 to 2019 [21]. However, this study predominantly relied on incidence as the sole metric and focused narrowly on temporal evolution. It had not adequately explored health equity disparities and had failed to conduct a demographic-epidemiological decomposition. Furthermore, it lacked predictions of disease trends after 2019, a critical gap for anticipating future healthcare demands. Meanwhile, current global-level T2DM studies often overlook regional analyses, hindering researchers from accurately grasping the unique influencing factors and specific contexts of T2DM in regions with similar development backgrounds [22, 23]. This oversight in turn impeded the integration of regional and global prevention policies. This study aimed to address these gaps by providing a more comprehensive evaluation.

This study will provide an updated comprehensive assessment of the burden, trends and prediction of T2DM. Specifically, this study includes (1) a descriptive analysis of the T2DM epidemiology among the BRICS; (2) a trend analysis from the overall, local, and multi-dimension scopes; (3) a decomposition analysis based on demographic and epidemiologic factors; (4) an inequality analysis according to the standard health equity analytic methods recommended by the WHO; and (5) a prediction of the BRICS burden of T2DM up to 2035. Notably, this study acknowledges that the validity of the data hinges on the quality of national vital registration, and that modelling in data-scarce regions risks introducing bias. There may also be a risk of ecological fallacy. By incorporating up-to-date data and analyzing the current situation and future trends of T2DM in the BRICS, this study aims to offer practical experience and strategies for global T2DM management, particularly for developing countries.

Methods

Data source

We obtained T2DM data from GBD 2021 via the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) results tool (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool) [24]. GBD 2021 provides new demographic estimates, information on disability for 371 diseases and injuries, and the comparative risks for 88 risk factors across 204 countries and territories and 811 subnational locations from 1990 to 2021 [25]. We extract incident cases, prevalent cases, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), and age-standardized rates (with 95% uncertainty intervals, UIs) to measure T2DM burden in Brazil, Russia, India, China and South Africa of the general population from 1990 to 2021. For age-standardized rates reported per 100,000 population, the GBD framework employed the following calculation [26]:

where is the age-specific rate in the ith age group, denotes the population (or weight) of the same age group in the GBD standard population, and N represents the total number of age groups. All rates were reported per 100,000 population. The 95%UIs were defined by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of 1000 estimates based on the GBD algorithm [27]. Our analysis followed the STROBOD reporting guidelines [28]. Detailed data processing workflow in GBD 2021 was demonstrated in (Appendix 1).

GBD 2021 defines diabetes mellitus as a fasting plasma glucose concentration of ≥ 126 mg/dL (7 mmol/L) or reported treatment for the condition. Crude years lived with disability were computed through multiplying the prevalence of T2DM by the respective disability weights for this health state [29]. T2DM was identified by ICD-10 codes E10 to E14.

DALYs were defined as the sum of years of life lost due to premature death (YLLs) and years of healthy life lost due to disability (YLDs) calculated with prevalence perspective, expressed as: DALYs = YLLs + YLDs. Introduced by the World Bank and WHO for measuring GBD. Individuals can interpret one DALY as equivalent to losing one year of life in full health [30].

The SDI, a composite metric integrating income, average years of schooling, and fertility for each GBD location and year, was first developed for GBD 2015 using Human Development Index (HDI) methodology to assess socio-demographic development, with classifications into five groups [30]: low SDI (0.00-0.45), low middle SDI (0.45–0.61), middle SDI (0.61–0.69), high middle SDI (0.69–0.81), and high SDI (0.81-1.00). For 2021, the BRICS countries’ SDI values and classifications are as follows: Brazil (0.65) is classified as middle SDI; Russia (0.81) and China (0.72) are both in the high middle SDI group; India (0.58) is classified as low middle SDI; and South Africa (0.68) is categorized as middle SDI.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis and trend analysis

The case number and age-standardized rate (ASR) of incidence, prevalence and DALYs of T2DM for the BRICS for both sexes in 1990 and 2021 were demonstrated. Firstly, we used the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) to quantify the overall trend of the burden of T2DM. An upward trend was identified by a positive EAPC and its 95%CI while a downward trend was identified by a negative EAPC and its 95%CI.

Then, we used Joinpoint regression analysis to detect the local trend of the burden of T2DM. Joinpoint regression analysis was also called segmented regression modeling as a statistical approach to the investigation of the trend of disease over time [33]. The Joinpoint software (version 4.9.1.0; National Cancer Institute, Rockville, Maryland, USA) was employed.

The temporal trends in T2DM were measured by the annual percentage change (APC), average annual percentage change (AAPC) and corresponding 95%CI. An upward trend was indicated by a positive APC/AAPC and corresponding 95%CI, while a downward trend was indicated by a negative APC/AAPC and corresponding 95%CI [34].

Furthermore, we examined the trend in the burden of T2DM associated with age, period and birth cohort effects in the BRICS. Age effects denoted the changes in the variables through an individual’s lifetime, while period effects represented the impact of environmental factors affecting the entire population. Birth cohort effects related to the alterations in the variables observed in children who underwent comparable life experiences as a consequence of their shared birth year. However, the inevitable identification problem was caused by the multicollinearity between age, period and birth cohort. To address this, the age-period-cohort model with intrinsic estimator (IE) method utilizing principal component regression analysis was employed to account for the variable effects across three temporal trends and provide a relatively efficient estimation [35]. The age-period-cohort model with IE method bases on the Poisson distribution can be expressed as follows: in which represents the incidence or prevalence in the (i, j, k) group; represents the intercept of the model; denote the age effect in the i-th age group, period effect in the j-th period group and birth cohort effect in the k-th birth cohort group respectively The model presented estimated coefficients for the age, period and birth cohort effects. These coefficients were subsequently transformed into exponential values to determine the relative risks (RRs) for the incidence and prevalence of a particular age, period or birth cohort relative to the average combined level of all ages, periods or birth cohorts [36, 37].

The incident or prevalent risk in the specific group exceeds the average combined level of the overall population if RRs > 1, and a larger RRs indicates a higher risk for that group.

Decomposition analysis

To explore the determinants of T2DM DALYs changes, we conducted a decomposition analysis by three dimensions: population size, age structure, and epidemiologic changes (defined as population- and age-standardized DALYs rates). DALYs were stratified by sex (female/male), and then decomposed into components attributable to population, age structure, and epidemiologic trends [38]. The number of DALYs for each country can be calculated as follows: , where refers to the number of DALYs according to the factors of age structure, population size and the DALYs rate in year y; denotes the proportion of population for the age category i of the n age categories in year y; denotes the total population in year y; and denotes the DALYs rate for the age category i in year y [39].

Cross-country inequality analysis

Health inequality monitoring can provide evidence for health planning and improve relevant policies, programs, and practices to enhance health equity. We used two indices to measure the distributive inequality of the T2DM burden: the slope index of inequality (SII) and the concentration index (CI). The SII was calculated by regressing national DALYs rates across all age groups on a socio-demographic development-associated relative position scale. The CI was computed by numerically integrating the area under the Lorenz concentration curve, which was fitted using the cumulative fraction of DALYs and the cumulative relative distribution of the population ranked by SDI. The 95% confidence interval for both indices was derived by fitting a Lorenz concentration curve to the cumulative distribution of the population ranked by SDI against the cumulative distribution of the T2DM burden. This dual-method analysis enables a detailed examination of how the T2DM burden is distributed across countries with varying socio-economic development levels and how these disparities have evolved over time [40, 41].

Predictive analysis

To help to establish better public health policy and health resource allocation, a further prediction of the burden of T2DM in the coming decades was performed. The Bayesian age-period-cohort analysis (BAPC) model with integrated nested Laplace approximation (INLA) [42, 43], which provides better coverage and precision than the age-period-cohort model, was used to predict the burden of T2DM up until 2035 in the BRICS. The utilization of an INLA with the BAPC model can approximate marginal posterior distributions and thus may avoid several mixing and convergence problems caused by the Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling technique traditionally used for Bayesian approaches. In this study, the Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling technique was implemented with 500 iterations to ensure sufficient sampling while minimizing computational burden.

The calculation procedures of all the methods in this study were demonstrated in (Appendix 2–7). All analysis was performed using R software (version 4.3.2) to ensure the accuracy and reproducibility of the results. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Overall trends in burden of T2DM using broad estimation method

In the BRICS, the most significant increases in the ASR of incidence and DALYs were observed in Russia and the most significant increases in the ASR of prevalence was observed in South Africa. Although increased trends are demonstrated globally in these three metrics, the level of increase in the incident and prevalent ASRs in Brazil, India and China were lower than that at the global level whereas Russia and South Africa had a higher increase than the global level. As for the ASR of DALYs, China was below the global level of increase, and Russia, India and South Africa were above the global level (Table 1).

Local trends in burden of T2DM using Joinpoint regression analysis

Between 1990 and 2021, continuously increasing trends in the incident, prevalent, and DALYs case numbers of T2DM occurred in the BRICS. As for the ASR, the BRICS kept increasing in incidence with the periods of most rapid increase being 2012–2021, 1990–1994, 1996–1999, 1990–1993 and 2001–2004 respectively (Fig. 1A). Sustained growth in prevalence was observed, with the periods of most rapid increase identified as 2011–2021, 1990–1994, 1996–2000, 1996–1999, and 1999–2005, respectively (Fig. 1B). Notably, the five countries showed fluctuating patterns in the ASR of DALYs. Russia experienced two sharp upswings in 1990–1994 and 2012–2015. India increased slowly until 2005 and then rose rapidly from 2005 to 2014. China primarily showed growth, with the fastest increase occurring from 1996 to 2000, followed by an upward rebound after 2015. South Africa, where a rapid increase occurred between 1994 and 1997, experienced a downward trend between 2015 and 2021 (Fig. 1C).

Age-period-cohort analysis on T2DM incidence and prevalence

As for the incidence, the highest RR was among those aged 55–59 years old in Brazil and China, whereas the peak RR was in those aged 60–64 years old in Russia, India and South Africa. Notably, the incident RR in China also rose obviously in those aged 20–24 years old. A rebound was seen in those aged 75–79 years old in India and South Africa respectively. As for the period effect, Brazil and South Africa showed continuous upward trends in the incident RR over time. China had a rising incident RR until 1995 and a consistent decline thereafter. India and Russia demonstrated increasing RR until 2020, followed by slight decreasing risk thereafter. As for the birth cohort effect, individuals born earlier had higher RR in Brazil, Russia, India and South Africa. Interestingly, the later an individual was born, the higher their RR in China. The RR remained relatively stable for those born before the 1980–1984 cohort while increased dramatically thereafter (Fig. 2A).

As for prevalence, there were single peak curves in the BRICS, with the highest RR being in those aged 65–69, 70–74, 70–74, 65–69 and 80–84 years old respectively. The prevalent RR was higher in older adults. After controlling the age and birth cohort effect, the prevalent RR in five countries increased over time. As for birth cohorts, the analysis revealed a higher prevalent RR for individuals born earlier in five countries, with the highest risk being in the 1895–1899 cohort. Remarkably, the RR experienced an increase in China after the 1970–1974 cohort (Fig. 2B).

Decomposition analysis on T2DM DALYs

The number of DALYs in the BRICS increased over time, with the highest increase detected in India. Population aging accounted for 36.61%, 19.19%, 15.50%, 38.13% and 13.01% of increase whereas population growth accounted for 67.06%, 4.33%, 53.94%, 37.64% and 47.00% in DALYs in the BRICS respectively. Epidemiological change accounted for 76.48%, 30.56%, 24.22% and 39.99% of the increase in Russia, India, China and South Africa. The overall T2DM burden was heavier for males in India and China whereas it was heavier for females in Brazil, Russia and South Africa. The impact of population aging was more pronounced for Brazilian, Indian, Chinese, and South African females. Epidemiological changes had a more evident positive effect on Russian females (Fig. 3).

Cross-country inequality analysis

As reflected by the slope index of inequality, there was an excess of 429.89(95%CI: 187.62 to 672.17) DALYs per 100,000 between countries with the lowest and highest SDI in 1990, which increased to 711.63(95%CI: 416.73 to 1006.53) in 2021. The relative gradient inequality, as measured by the health inequality concentration index, was − 9.60(95%CI: −10.00 to −9.20) in 1990 and − 12.91 (95%CI: −13.71 to −12.10) in 2021, showing a disproportionate concentration of the burden among the poorer population and a worsening over time (Fig. 4B).

The prevalent ASR was predicted to rise continuously across the BRICS (Fig. 5B). Discussion

From 1990 to 2021, Russia and South Africa demonstrated the most prominent T2DM burden among the BRICS, featuring both heavy burden and rapid growth. Prevention and control for the 60–74 age group in Russia and the 60–84 age group in South Africa required particular attention. Although the burden in China, Brazil, and India fluctuated, an overall upward trend was evident. The high-risk groups were 55–69 years old in China and Brazil, and 60–74 years old in India. Notably, China should also have attached importance to early prevention among young people aged 22–24. In terms of attribution, the burden growth in China, Brazil, and Russia was primarily related to population aging, population growth, and changes in disease spectrum, indicating that actively addressing aging was an important task for these three countries. Additionally, the burden gap between countries with different socio-economic statuses continued to widen, and the prevention and control needs of less developed countries urgently required attention. Projections showed that although the incidence growth would slow down by 2035, the prevalence would increase significantly. This suggested that the disease progression rate still exceeded population growth. Enhancing the effectiveness of T2DM health management in the next decade should have been a key focus to tackle this public health challenge, particularly in China and India.

Russia and South Africa demonstrated the most significant increases in T2DM burden. An early insulin shortage in Russia [44], combined with immature T2DM management and severe infectious disease burdens in South Africa [45], may contribute to their escalating T2DM burden. However, China, as a populous country, exhibited lower growth in these metrics than the global average, might have contributed to its community screening programs, regular blood glucose monitoring, and long-term primary care strategies for high-risk groups [46]. Such Chinese experiences might have offered valuable references for T2DM prevention and control strategies. Collectively, the BRICS also faced multifaceted other challenges, including fiscal austerity in Brazil [47, 48], environmental risks such as air pollution, artificial night lighting, and endocrine disruptors in India and China [49, 50]. Additionally, the BRICS confronted the rising prevalence of high fat/sugar diets and sedentary lifestyles driven by rapid urbanization. This lifestyle shifted fueled increases in overweight and obesity, which are key risk factors for T2DM [51, 52].

The age effect showed that incident and prevalent risks generally increase with age. This matched previous studies which indicated that aging raises T2DM risk in two ways: it directly lessens β-cell function, worsens insulin secretion deficiency, and indirectly boosts insulin resistance through obesity and other risk factors [53]. The population evidence from this study further supported this conclusion. Notably, Chinese youth at 20–24 years old demonstrated a significant upward trend in incidence risk, which might be linked to lifestyle shifts driven by fast-food expansion. Rising youth obesity became a key young T2DM risk factor [54, 55]. Additionally, higher health awareness and more screenings had increased early diagnosis, which might have contributed to identifying T2DM cases at a younger age. China should prioritize establishing early screening and behavioral intervention systems for youth while maintaining prevention strategies for older adults. As for the birth cohort effect, later cohorts indicated that earlier-born individuals in Brazil, Russia, India, and South Africa faced higher T2DM risks. This might stem from greater lifetime exposure to risk factors among earlier cohorts, while later-born individuals benefited from improved health literacy and medical care [39]. However, China demonstrated an opposite pattern, especially in 1970-74 cohort. This groups who grew up during Chinese reform and economic boom, experienced dietary shifts that increased overweight and obesity risks [56]. Furthermore, the older generation who survived famine provides additional insight that famine induced disruptions in the gut microbiome and may positively associate with T2DM risk suggested by a previous study. Our study further highlighted the long-term metabolic impact of early-life nutritional stress [57].

As for the BRICS, this study indicated that China, a high-middle SDI country, was significantly affected by population aging. Chinese older adult population which reached 254 million in 2019, was projected to 402 million, constituting 28% of the population by 2040 [58, 59]. This demographic shift underscored the need to enhance community and family-based care services to address health challenges such as multimorbidity and rural-urban disparities among older adults, aiming to promote active aging [60]. Brazil, classified as a middle SDI country, was primarily driven by population growth. This growth intensified healthcare demands and fiscal pressures on health and welfare systems, necessitating the development of a more systemic chronic care model and the improvement of the accessibility and quality of the national Family Health Programme [61, 62]. Russia, a high-middle SDI country, experienced profound impacts from epidemiological changes, which were closely associated with increasing rates of alcohol consumption, smoking, and a high incidence rate of cardiovascular diseases [63, 64]. The vastness of its territory also posed challenges for the delivery of chronic disease services. Future efforts should have prioritized the integration of telemedicine, remote monitoring, and the use of information technology and smart devices in healthcare. Populations in lower development countries, such as India (low-middle SDI), South Africa (middle SDI) and other global lower SDI countries, faced a higher T2DM burden possibly due to economic limitations and rapid urbanization, which fostered unhealthy diets and physical inactivity [65, 66]. This highlighted the necessity for lower development countries to reallocate public investment to enhance healthcare accessibility and strengthen health education initiatives. Furthermore, the burden of infectious diseases (e.g., tuberculosis, HIV, influenza) in lower development countries may have compounded the challenges posed by T2DM and other non-communicable diseases [67]. This study also provided a new perspective through DALYs, and previous studies demonstrated T2DM inequalities across incidence rates, HbA1c levels, care quality and complications [66, 68, 69], emphasizing the uneven distribution of T2DM burden across countries with varying socio-economic statuses collectively.

Aging disproportionately affected females in the BRICS. While longer life expectancy for women enabled more social engagement, it also amplified loneliness, disease burden, and chronic care demands [70]. However, policy discussions often overlooked the gender dimension of aging. Epidemiological change demonstrated male bear higher T2DM burdens across the BRICS, consistent with prior study [71]. However, Russian women faced greater burden, likely due to frequent T2DM screenings during pregnancy and menopause [72]. Integrating gender-differentiated strategies into T2DM management was crucial for national improvements.

Notably, the T2DM burden was forecasted to increase in the BRICS by 2035. The diabetogenic effects of COVID-19 during 2020–2021 might have represented a novel risk factor. The projected growth of T2DM burden in the BRICS suggested critical gaps in current global NCD strategies, which frequently lacked region-tailored interventions. This underscored the urgent need to align the BRICS-level policy frameworks with Global Noncommunicable Diseases Compact 2020–2030, enabling the global initiative to more effectively integrate region-specific needs and strategies. This alignment could both mitigate regional disease burdens and establish a model for developing countries experiencing analogous epidemiological transitions.

Limitations

This study also had some limitations. First, the validity of GBD data was contingent upon the quality and completeness of vital registration systems across countries, with GBD estimates in data-deficient regions relying on modeling approaches, predictive covariates, and temporal trends extrapolated from neighboring nations, which may introduce systematic bias. Second, the analysis was subject to ecological fallacy risks, as aggregate-level data may obscure individual-level variations in T2DM burden. Future studies could explore the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of T2DM management policies across different countries. The contributions of various interventions to reducing the T2DM burden could be assessed by comparing policy outcomes, providing data-driven support for rational resource allocation and prioritization of key interventions.

Conclusion

Developing comprehensive prevention and management guidelines for T2DM should emerge as a critical avenue for BRICS cooperation, drawing on successful experiences in addressing aging populations and enhancing chronic disease management. Specifically, Russia, India, and South Africa should concentrate on older adults, whereas China and Brazil need to pay attention to middle-aged and older adults, with China additionally addressing younger-onset T2DM trends. Concurrently, insulin shortages and severe infectious diseases pose major challenges, particularly in less developed BRICS countries. We recommend that the BRICS implement multi-faceted policy reforms and interventions to reverse the projected trend and mitigate health inequalities, such as expanding early intervention and education programs for T2DM and integrating hospital-based care.
