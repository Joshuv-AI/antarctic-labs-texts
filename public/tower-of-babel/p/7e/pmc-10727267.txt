Another Piece of the Complex Puzzle: Outcomes of Percutaneous Coronary Intervention in Older Adults
Authors: Rhian E Davies, Allison B Hall, Michael A Chen
Published: 2023

Coronary artery disease is highly prevalent in our rapidly aging population. Projections anticipate a 2‐fold increase in the size of the population ≥65 years old by 2050, with an estimated 1 in 5 in the United States being >65 years old by 2030. 1 CAD is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in older adults who tend to have more multivessel and left main CAD than younger adults. Because of the exclusion of older, particularly multimorbid and frail older adults from most of our clinical trials, the optimal treatment of CAD in this population is often unclear, but such information is critical to improving their outcomes. 2 A patient‐centered approach that takes into consideration the patient's overall condition including comorbidities, functional status, frailty, cognitive skills, and personal preferences should be the goal, with age itself being only 1 factor. 2

In this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), Hanna and colleagues evaluated outcomes in older adults undergoing complex percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) compared with those having noncomplex intervention. 3 The overall incidence of future target lesion revascularization (TLR) was low across the entire study cohort at 2.9%, without a statistically significant difference between groups, albeit individuals undergoing complex PCI had lower 12‐month risk of TLR after adjustment for a series of clinical characteristics. Individuals undergoing complex PCI were also noted, however, to have a much higher risk of all‐cause mortality 12 months from their index intervention. 3

The current study collected data on all older adults (defined as ≥75 years old) undergoing elective complex PCI for stable ischemic heart disease at a large academic health system, which included 5 network hospitals (513 patients; mean age, 81±5 years). Data were manually extracted from the electronic medical record by the authors. Excluded were all emergent or urgent procedures and anyone lost to follow‐up (61 patients). Complex PCI was defined as any procedure or lesion that may carry an inherently elevated risk of complications or PCI failure including multivessel PCI (≥3 vessels or ≥2 vessels if including an unprotected left main coronary artery or proximal left anterior descending artery), intervention on an unprotected left main coronary artery, saphenous vein graft, bifurcation (defined as at least 1 intervention to each branch at a bifurcation), or PCI involving atherectomy. Lesion‐based criteria included intervention on a lesion with severe calcification or a chronic total occlusion. Cases referred for coronary artery bypass grafting, as mentioned in cardiology notes, where the patient refused or was deemed ineligible for coronary artery bypass grafting, were also considered complex PCI. Noncomplex PCI was defined as any procedure that did not meet the definition for complex PCI. 3

The primary end point for this study was event‐free survival at 12 months following index revascularization, defined as freedom from all‐cause mortality, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, and major bleeding (bleeding was defined according to the BARC [Bleeding Academic Research Consortium]). 4 Secondary outcomes included 12‐month all‐cause mortality, TLR (defined as intervention on the lesion treated during the index PCI within the subsequent 12 months), and all bleeding events (BARC 1–3 and 5). During the study timeframe, 574 patients received elective PCI for stable ischemic heart disease. After excluding 61 patients who were lost to follow‐up, 513 patients (mean age, 81.3±4.6 years) were included in the final cohort (56.1%, N=288) who underwent noncomplex PCI versus 43.9% (N=225) who underwent complex PCI. 3 As expected, the most common indication to undergo PCI, whether complex or not, was symptom control.

The event‐free survival at 12 months for the entire cohort was 84.0% (95% CI, 80.9%–87.2%). There was no statistically significant difference seen in the event‐free survival for patients receiving noncomplex versus complex PCI. Additionally, there was no statistically significant difference between PCI groups on the risk of nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, or (importantly) cardiovascular death. 3

TLR was low at 2.9% and again, not statistically different between the complex PCI and noncomplex PCI groups. 3 Because TLR was so low, it suggests that regardless of whether or not the PCI was deemed complex, the patients had appropriate vessel preparation, and their intervention overall was deemed successful.

Although there was no statistically significant difference seen in bleeding between complex and noncomplex PCI at 1 year (25.3% versus 20.5%), there was an overall high rate for this age group. 3 Unfortunately, bleeding in the older population is common, and treatment with dual antiplatelet therapy exacerbates an increased risk of bleeding, particularly if patients are already on an anticoagulant. Balancing anti‐ischemic and bleeding risks is important to prevent adverse outcomes in all patients. 2 , 4 Any discussion of bleeding in patients undergoing PCI naturally leads to considerations about dual antiplatelet therapy adherence and an acknowledgment that older adults adherence to medications may be compromised due to cognitive impairment, side effects, physical limitations (eg, arthritis), or even the advice from another physician that may lead to adverse outcomes including stent thrombosis, acute coronary syndrome, or even death. 5

Perhaps the most notable finding was that all‐cause mortality for the entire cohort was 7.8% (95% CI, 5.4%–10.1%) at 12 months after index PCI. Patients receiving complex PCI had higher all‐cause mortality compared with patients receiving noncomplex PCI (10.2% versus 5.9%). 3 This was seen in the context of there not being any differences between the baseline patient or procedural characteristics and after statistical adjustment. This leads to the conclusion that there are other unmeasured/identified confounders that account for the difference, an area suggested by the authors as ripe for future research. These additional studies should inform causality and aid in decision making.

As noted by the authors, there is a lack of uniformity among the definitions of complex PCI, and there is variability of PCI procedures performed among operators across different institutions and countries. A component of this includes variability of PCI equipment, education on proper use of how to safely use the equipment, and subsequent operator comfort and experience with the equipment. To their credit, the authors clearly defined their criteria for complex and performed a sensitivity analysis using a more traditional definition of complex and found no statistically significant mortality difference. They logically call for a need for a uniform definition, but the findings beg the question of what exactly it is that differs between the definitions that may account for the difference in mortality. Another area of further investigation would be replicating the current findings in other populations of older adult patients receiving PCI.

In planning optimal PCI, it should be remembered that patients with stable ischemic heart disease may later become unstable, resulting in an urgent or even an emergent procedure. The FAME 2 (Fractional Flow Reserve Versus Angiography for Multivessel Evaluation 2) trial showed that patients with stable ischemic heart disease and fractional flow reserve <0.8 treated with goal directed medical therapy (GDMT) plus PCI were less likely to require subsequent urgent revascularization than patients with fractional flow reserve <0.8 treated with GDMT alone. 6 Patients with ≥50% stenosis of an unprotected left main, coronary stenosis ≥70%, or fractional flow reserve <0.8 who suffer from anginal symptoms may benefit from PCI, especially if these symptoms affect their quality of life. 6 Thus, an optimal PCI strategy, whether noncomplex or complex, may be appropriate regardless of age.

If the decision is made to move ahead with PCI in an older patient, a detailed conversation about potential risks, but also the benefits, should be performed. The benefits include but are not limited to the overall improvement in their quality of life, functional ability, and decrease in symptom burden (which may lessen the need for antianginal medications). Risk assessment should incorporate sociodemographics, signs, symptoms, risk factors, comorbid conditions, and test results.

Although we strive to have more multimorbid older adults included in clinical trials to help inform optimal treatment strategies including complex PCI, the authors are to be commended for carrying out a study that identified a large number of older adults with a high average age, adding information about this understudied population to the literature. The study required an intensive chart review to appropriately identify the baseline characteristics, intervention type, comorbidities, and outcomes, and resulted in relatively few patients lost to follow‐up. The inclusion of multiple variables important to the evaluation of older adults, including geriatric comorbidities such as falls and frailty, should be a model for other studies of older adults in cardiovascular science. Also laudable was the thoughtfully derived definition of complex PCI in this study, and especially for their performing the sensitivity analysis using a more traditional definition of complex, which should lead to further investigations. We look forward to further studies of complex PCI in older patients, along with elucidation of the specific mechanisms that might be affecting their long‐term outcomes, with the hope of improving care in this important patient population.
