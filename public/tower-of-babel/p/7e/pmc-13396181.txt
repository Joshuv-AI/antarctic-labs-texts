Mitochondrial RNA degradation regulates differentiation, stemness, and immune sensitivity in acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Geethu Emily Thomas, Veronique Voisin, Kazem Nouri, Rose Hurren, Karen Kai-Lin Fang, Ali Chegini, Marcela Gronda, Yongran Yan, Neil MacLean, Yulia Jitkova, Dakai Ling, Mary Ma, Xiao Ming Wang, Andrea Arruda, Vito Spadavecchio, Mark D. Minden, Li Zhang, Jong Bok Lee, Aaron D. Schimmer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Eukaryotic cells have separate genomes in the nucleus and mitochondria. Mitochondrial DNA is transcribed bi-directionally to generate mitochondrial RNA (mtRNA) and dsRNA as a by-product of this transcription. We demonstrate that mtRNA transcription and degradation are increased in AML (Acute Myeloid Leukemia) cells and stem cells resulting in higher rates of mtRNA turnover. We discover that the mitochondrial degradosome, SUV3 and PNPase, is upregulated in AML cells and stem cells and functionally important for degradation of mtRNA and mitochondrial dsRNA (double stranded RNA) in AML. Depleting SUV3 or PNPase impairs mtRNA degradation and promotes the accumulation of dsRNA. dsRNA that accumulates after depleting SUV3 or PNPase, stimulates IFN-I signaling that induces AML differentiation, decreases stemness and increases sensitivity to immune-mediating cytotoxicity. Thus, this work highlights mitochondrial RNA regulation in AML and identifies a mechanism by which mtRNA turnover influences AML differentiation, stem cell function, and immune sensitization.

Introduction

Mitochondria are unique cellular organelles with their own distinct DNA, a 16.6 kB double-stranded circular genome that is replicated independently of the cell cycle. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) lacks introns and encodes 2 rRNAs, 22 t-RNAs, and 13 of the 90 proteins involved in the mitochondrial respiratory chain1,2. Each cell contains hundreds to thousands of mitochondria, and within each mitochondria, there are multiple copies of this circular DNA3.

The mitochondrial genome is transcribed bi-directionally from both the heavy and light strands generating long polycistronic transcripts2. The long polycistronic transcripts are processed to excise mitochondrial tRNA and ribosomal RNA and thereby release the monocistronic RNA sequences that code for mitochondrial proteins4.

The mtRNA that code for respiratory chain proteins undergo posttranscriptional modifications at their 3’ end including polyadenylation that is mediated by poly(A) polymerases (PAPs)5,6. In a mechanism similar to prokaryotes, poly-A polymerase (mtPAP) transfers 40–50 adenines to mitochondrial RNA transcripts7,8. 3’ mtRNA polyadenylation completes the UAA stop codon and thereby regulates mtRNA translation. In addition, 3’ polyadenylation also facilitates mtRNA degradation9. The mechanisms by which poly-A tagged mtRNA are degraded are not completely understood, but, in one model, the SUV3/PNPase degradosome complex recognizes and degrades poly-A tagged mtRNA10–12. This heteropentamer degradosome complex consists of two copies of SUV3 (encoded by SUPV3L1) and three copies of PNPase (encoded by PNPT1). SUV3 is a mitochondrial RNA helicase that unwinds double-stranded and secondary-structured RNA11. PNPase is a polynucleotide phosphorylase that degrades mtRNA through exonucleolytic activity.

In addition to degrading poly-A tagged mtRNA, the SUV3 and PNPase complex also degrades the non-coding antisense RNA strands that are generated during bi-directional mitochondrial transcription11. By degrading antisense mtRNA, SUV3 and PNPase also control the accumulation of dsRNA13.

Here, we show that AML cells and stem cells have increased mtRNA turnover with increased mtRNA transcription and degradation. The mitochondrial degradosome consisting of SUV3 and PNPase, regulate mtRNA degradation as well as levels of mitochondrial dsRNA. Depleting SUV3 and PNPase leads to impaired mtRNA degradation and the accumulation of dsRNA in AML cells. dsRNA accumulation upon inhibition of the degradosome stimulates IFN-I signaling that promotes AML differentiation, decreases stem cell function and increases sensitivity of AML cells to immune-mediating cytotoxicity. Thus, this work highlights a novel mechanism by which the turnover of mtRNA regulates nuclear gene expression, differentiation and stem cell function.

Results

Mitochondrial RNA turnover is increased in AML

Previously, we and others demonstrated that AML cells have unique mitochondrial characteristics with increased mitochondrial mass, oxygen consumption, and reliance on mitochondrial pathways for survival14,15. Consistent with these findings, we demonstrated that expression of the 1136 genes encoding the mitochondrial proteome was increased in AML patients (n = 670) compared to normal hematopoietic cells (n = 19) (Beat AML 2.0). Although genes encoding the mitochondrial proteome were increased as expected, we were surprised to discover that expression of the 13 genes encoded by mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) was lower in these primary AML cells compared to normal (Fig. 1A, B). The upregulation of mitochondrial genes and down regulation of mitochondrial encoded genes remained robust across bootstrap analyses of size-matched AML subsamples and quantile-based thresholding. No differences in expression were noted across AML subgroups stratified by ELN2017 risk, FAB classification, or frequent molecular mutations. In contrast, analysis of other datasets (Lux et al. GSE158596)16 revealed increased expression of both the mitochondrial proteome and the 13 genes encoded by mtDNA (Fig. 1C, D, Fig. S1A-B). Interestingly, Beat AML 2.0 that showed lower levels of mitochondrial encoded genes in AML employed poly-A mRNA capturing to sequence the RNA, while Lux et al. used rRNA depletion in their sequencing method. To test whether differences in expression were related to the amplification method, we experimentally assessed the expression of mitochondrial-encoded genes in primary AML samples (n = 14), normal hematopoietic cells (n = 6) and normal CD34+ progenitors (n = 3) by qRT-PCR using primers directed at the internal coding sequence of the genes and primers that amplify the genes from the poly-A tag. When amplifying the genes with internal primers, expression was higher in AML compared to normal hematopoietic cells and progenitors (similar to Lux et al. GSE158596)16 (Fig. 1E). Yet, when amplifying the genes from the poly-A-tag similar to Beat AML 2.0, expression appeared lower in AML (Fig. 1F). Thus, primary AML cells have reduced poly-A-tagged mitochondrial RNA (mtRNA) despite having higher level of gene expression.

In mitochondria, like bacteria, poly-A tags facilitate RNA degradation12,17. Thus, lower levels of poly-A-tagged mtRNA could represent either decreased poly-A-tagging capacity or increased mtRNA turnover. Expression of genes associated with poly-A tagging and 3’ mRNA processing were increased in AML compared to normal hematopoietic cells, indicating that lower levels of poly-A tagged mtRNA is not related to reduced tagging capacity (Fig. 2A, S1E-F). Genes associated with mtRNA transcription, processing and degradation were also increased in AML compared to normal hematopoietic cells (Fig. 2A, S1E-F). Mitochondrial RNA transcription, processing and degradation were increased in stem-like/primitive AML compared to more differentiated/mature AML (Fig. 2B). Given these results, we functionally evaluated mtRNA stability and turnover in primary AML and normal hematopoietic cells. We measured changes in mtRNA over time after treating cells with Actinomycin D to inhibit RNA transcription. Expression of mitochondrial encoded genes declined more rapidly in AML compared to normal (Fig. 2C, D), indicating that primary AML cells have higher rates of mtRNA degradation and turnover.

The mitochondrial degradosome is upregulated in AML and mediates mitochondrial RNA turnover

Degradation of mitochondrial RNA is mediated by SUV3 (aka: SUPV3L1), an ATP-dependent helicase, and PNPase (aka: PNPT1), an exonuclease, that act together to form a heteropentameric mtRNA degradosome complex10–12. As mtRNA degradation was increased in AML, we measured the expression and functional importance of the mitochondria degradosome. In AML cell lines and primary patient samples, SUV3 and PNPase protein were increased compared to normal hematopoietic cells and normal CD34+ progenitors, although differences in mRNA expression were less robust (Fig. 3A, Fig. S2A–I). SUV3 and PNPase were higher in stem-like AML compared to more differentiated/mature AML (Fig. 3B). Increased SUV3 and PNPase protein expression was associated with decreased overall survival (Fig. 3C). To understand the functional importance of SUV3 and PNPase in mtRNA turnover, we knocked down SUV3 and PNPase in AML cell lines using shRNA in lentiviral vectors. Knockdown of SUV3 and PNPase increased total and poly-A tagged mtRNA (Fig. 3D–F) in AML cells. Thus, these results support the functional importance of SUV3 and PNPase in regulating mtRNA degradation and turnover.

The mitochondrial degradosome regulates levels of mitochondrial dsRNA in AML

mtDNA is organized as a small circular genome and is transcribed bidirectionally18. A by-product of this bidirectional transcription double stranded RNA (dsRNA) is formed19. Given the increased mtRNA turnover and given that SUV3 and PNPase also degrade the non-translating antisense strand of mitochondrial dsRNA13,20, we examined changes in dsRNA after SUV3 and PNPase depletion. Using confocal microscopy and flow cytometry, we observed increased levels of intracellular dsRNA upon knockdown of SUV3 and PNPase, in AML cell lines and primary AML samples (Fig. 4A, B, Fig. S3A–E). In contrast, dsRNA did not increase in normal hematopoietic cells after SUV3 knockdown (Fig. S3F). Of note, basal dsRNA levels were undetectable by confocal microscopy in both primary AML and normal hematopoietic cells (Fig. S3G). By confocal microscopy, the dsRNA that accumulated after SUV3 and PNPase depletion localized to both the mitochondria and the cytoplasm. To characterize the source of the dsRNA, we immunoprecipitated and sequenced the dsRNA after SUV3 or PNPase knockdown. The dsRNA was highly enriched for mitochondrial genes, consistent with a mitochondrial origin for the dsRNA (Fig. 4C). Thus, taken together, SUV3 and PNPase protect AML cells from the accumulation of mitochondrial dsRNA and depleting these enzymes increases mitochondrial dsRNA in both the mitochondria and the cytoplasm.

The mitochondrial degradosome regulates the type I interferon pathway in AML and controls stem cell function and differentiation

To understand the impact of increased dsRNA after depleting the mitochondrial degradosome, we performed gene-expression profiling of 579 immune and inflammatory genes at increasing times after knockdown of SUV3 and PNPase. Of these 579 immune related genes, 189 showed an increase over time after knockdown of SUV3 and PNPase (Fig. S4A-B). Pathway analysis of these upregulated genes revealed enrichment in immune signaling and viral response pathways (Fig. 4D, Fig. S4C). Using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR), we confirmed upregulation of immune and viral response genes after knockdown of SUV3 and PNPase (Fig. S4D). dsRNA can stimulate immune signaling and viral response pathways by activating RIG-I-like receptors21–23. Consistent with this mechanism, inhibiting TBK1 that is downstream of the RIG-I-like receptors with GSK861224, blocked upregulation of immune and viral response genes after SUV3 and PNPase depletion (Fig. 4E), without impacting the accumulation of dsRNA (Fig. S4E).

Upregulation of immune and viral response genes promotes cell differentiation and loss of stem cell function18. Therefore, we evaluated the role of SUV3 and PNPase in AML differentiation, proliferation and stem cell function. Depletion of SUV3 and PNPase decreased the clonogenic growth of AML cells (Fig. 4F, S5A). In contrast, SUV3 knockdown in normal hematopoietic cells did not reduce their clonogenic growth (Fig. S5B). Knockdown of SUV3 and PNPase also promoted monocytic differentiation of AML cells as evidenced by increased staining for non-specific esterase (Fig. 4G, Fig. S5C).

Knockdown and/or knockout of SUV3 or PNPase with multiple independent shRNA or gRNA reduced the growth and viability of human AML (OCI-AML2, TEX, K562, NB4, MV411) and murine AML cell lines (C1498 & WEHI-3) (Fig. 5A–F, Fig. S6A–G). SUV3 and PNPase knockdown also reduced the proliferation of low passage primary AML 8227 cells (Fig. 5C, D). Of note, SUV3 and PNPase ranked in the top 10% of essential genes in 26 leukemia cell lines in CRISPR screens, and the top 5% of essential genes in RNAi screens (https://depmap.org/portal) (Fig. S6H–I). To assess whether dependence on the mitochondrial degradosome was AML-specific, we analyzed the dependence on SUV3 and PNPase across cancer types in the DepMap database. The mitochondrial degradosome regulates the sensitivity of AML cells to T-cell mediated cytotoxicity

Upregulating the interferon response pathway can sensitize AML cells to T-cell mediated killing25. Therefore, we assessed the sensitivity of AML cells to T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity after SUV3 or PNPase knockdown. As a model for T-cell mediated cytotoxicity, we utilized double negative T cells (DNT). DNTs are a rare subpopulation of CD3 + CD4- CD8- T-cell found in the peripheral blood of healthy individuals. These cells can be expanded ex-vivo and have potent cytotoxic activity against AML cells without reactivity towards normal cells25,26. We knocked down SUV3 or PNPase with shRNA in AML cells and then co-cultured them with DNTs for 18 h. After incubation with DNTs, the viability of the AML cells was measured by flow cytometry. Knockdown of SUV3 and PNPase increased the susceptibility of AML cells to killing by these T cells (Fig. 6E, Fig. S7A). SUV3 or PNPase knockdown also sensitized AML cells to cytotoxicity by conventional cytotoxic T-cells (Fig. S7B). Sensitization to T-cell mediated killing after SUV3 or PNPase knockdown was dependent on the RIG-I-like receptor pathway as immune killing of AML cells was reduced by TBK1 inhibition (Fig. 6F).

Finally, we tested the importance of T cells on the growth of SUV3 depleted cells in vivo. As knockdown of SUV3 reduced the growth of human and murine AML cell lines, we used A20 murine lymphoma cells that increased intracellular dsRNA, but continued to proliferate in vitro, after SUV3 knockdown (Fig. S7C-D). A20 cells were transduced with shRNA targeting SUV3 or control sequences. After transduction, equal number of viable cells were injected subcutaneously into immunocompetent BALB/c mice followed by antibody-mediated T cell depletion that sustained T cell depletion for the duration of the experiment (Fig. S7E). SUV3 knockdown decreased the growth of A20 cells in vivo in mice with intact T cells. However, when T cells were depleted, A20 cells with SUV3 knockdown proliferated in vivo similar to controls (Fig. 6G). Thus, these data demonstrate the importance of the mitochondrial degradosome in mediating sensitivity to T-cell mediated cytotoxicity.

Discussion

Mitochondria have a unique genome that is replicated independently of the nuclear genome. The mitochondrial genome is organized as circular DNA and its transcription occurs bi-directionally. The bi-directional transcription leads to the formation of dsRNA as a by-product. In this study, we discovered that mtRNA transcription and degradation were increased in AML cells. Blocking mtRNA degradation at the level of SUV3 and PNPase, led to the accumulation of dsRNA, activated the interferon response pathway, caused AML differentiation, and sensitized cells to T-cell mediated cytotoxicity.

Through gene expression analysis and functional studies, we demonstrated that AML cell lines and primary samples have increased rates of mtRNA degradation. The increased mtRNA degradation may reflect increased rates of mtRNA damage and the need to eliminate damaged mtRNA to maintain the optimal mitochondrial function. mtRNA damage can result from mitochondrial ROS generated though oxidative phosphorylation27,28. We and others demonstrated that AML cells have increased oxidative metabolism14. Thus, we postulate that the increased mtRNA degradation and transcription are compensatory mechanisms to maintain the quality of mtRNA and preserve oxidative phosphorylation.

The degradation of nuclear encoded RNA occurs in the cytoplasm and is initiated by deadenylation followed by decapping and 5′ to 3′ exonucleolytic decay17,29. In contrast, mtRNA are degraded through a mechanism more closely related to prokaryotes. Similar to prokaryotes, mtRNA is tagged with poly-A residues by poly(A) polymerase5. The addition of the poly-A tag facilitates the termination of translation30, but also marks the strand for degradation9,31. The mechanism by which poly-A tagged RNA are degraded is not fully understood, but one model implicates the degradosome complex composed of SUV3 and PNPase13. In this model, the SUV3 helicase unwinds the mtRNA10 and the PNPase exonuclease further processes the mtRNA into dinucleotide sequences, termed nanoRNAs. The nanoRNAs are then degraded by REXO2 in the mitochondria32. Our data support this model as depletion of SUV3 and PNPase led to the accumulation of mtRNA and poly-A tagged mtRNA.

mtDNA is transcribed bidirectionally from both the G-rich H-strand and the complementary L-strand. Most functional RNAs are encoded by the H-strand, whereas the L-strand encodes antisense RNAs. Emerging data indicate that these antisense mtRNA are important regulators of mitochondrial gene expression and cell proliferation33. A by-product of the bidirectional transcription of mtDNA is the formation of dsRNA19,34. If not degraded, the mitochondrial dsRNA can exit the mitochondria and localize in the cytoplasm. Once in the cytoplasm, mitochondrial dsRNA are recognized as non-self molecules by pattern recognition receptors. Recognition of dsRNA by MDA5 or RIG-I results in the activation of type I interferon (IFN-I) signaling21–23 and an upregulation of genes that mimic the response to viral infection. Activation of the IFN-1 pathway promotes cell differentiation and loss of stem cell function15,35. In addition to degrading poly-A tagged mtRNA, we demonstrated that SUV3 and PNPase also degrade mitochondrial dsRNA. Depleting these enzymes led to the accumulation of mitochondrial dsRNA, and increased IFN-I signaling. Consistent with activation of IFN-I signaling, depleting SUV3 and PNPase in AML cells promoted differentiation and loss of stem cell function. Thus, we have shown a previously unrecognised mechanism for how AML cells regulate the accumulation of dsRNA to control nuclear gene expression, differentiation, and stem cell function.

The accumulation of intracellular dsRNA and activation of IFN-I signaling can sensitize cells to immune mediated cytotoxicity and we demonstrated that depletion of SUV3 and PNPase sensitized AML cells to immune mediated cytotoxicity. In this study, we used DNTs as a surrogate for leukemia-specific T cells. DNTs share similar effector function as conventional T cells but they are not alloreactive25,26. In addition, when administered to patients with relapsed AML, DNTs were well tolerated and had promising efficacy27. As such, they provide a clinically relevant model to study how depleting SUV3 and PNPase impacts sensitivity to T-cell mediated cytotoxicity.

Thus, in summary, the mitochondrial degradosome is upregulated in AML where it facilitates mtRNA turnover and prevents the accumulation of mitochondrial dsRNA. Depleting the degradosome in AML promotes differentiation, loss of stem cell function and sensitivity to immune surveillance. This work highlights a distinct mechanism by which mitochondrial biology controls nuclear gene expression and leukemic stem cell function.

Cell lines and primary samples

Dick’s lab36 were maintained in IMDM with 20% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine (Thermo Fisher Scientific 25030081), human recombinant stem cell factor (20 ng/ml; SCF) (Peprotech cat#300-07), and human recombinant interleukin-3 (2 ng/ml; IL-3) (Peprotech cat#200-003). 8227 cells obtained from J. Dick’s lab37 were cultured in X-VIVO 10 (Lonza 04-380Q) with 20% bovine serum albumin (BSA)–insulin-transferrin (STEMCELL Technologies 09500), human Fms-related tyrosine kinase 3 ligand (Flt3-L; 50 ng/ml) (PeproTech 300-19), IL-6 (10 ng/ml) (PeproTech 200-06), SCF (50 ng/ml) (PeproTech 300-07), thrombopoietin (25 ng/ml) (PeproTech 300-18), IL-3 (10 ng/ml) (PeproTech 200-03), and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF; 10 ng/ml) (Amgen 121181-53-1). All cell lines were maintained in humidified incubators at 37 °C, supplemented with 5% CO2.

Primary human AML samples were obtained from peripheral blood of consenting AML patients, with a malignant cell frequency of 80% among mononuclear cells (Supplemental Table 1). Cells were collected and cryopreserved by the Leukemia Tissue Bank at Princess Margaret Cancer Center/University Health Network. No financial compensation or incentives were provided to participants for their involvement in this study. Differential density centrifugation was used to isolate AML cells. The primary AML cells were frozen in 90% FCS + 15 U/mL of heparin + 10% DMSO. All specimens were de-identified prior to transfer from the Leukemia Tissue Bank. Normal hematopoietic cells were obtained from the peripheral blood of consenting donors receiving G-CSF to mobilize stem cells for allotransplant. We also obtained cord blood cells from Stem cell technologies (Cat # 70007). CD34+ control cells were isolated from G-CSF mobilized peripheral blood using Human Progenitor Cell Enrichment Kit from Stem cell technologies (Cat# #19356) and confirmed by flow cytometry using CD34 antibody (BD Biosciences Cat# 340441, RRID:AB_400514). The University Health Network institutional review boards approved the collection and use of human tissue for this study (REB #13-7163).

Analysis of gene expression in AML and normal hematopoietic cells

Bioinformatic analysis

BeatAML cohort: Batch corrected and normalized expression of BeatAML 2.0 polyA enriched RNA-seq samples were downloaded from https://biodev.github.io/BeatAML2/.

GSE158596 cohort16: Ribosomal RNA-depleted RNAseq of 61 AML patients and 16 healthy samples of mobilized CD34+ HSPCs were download from the Gene Expression Omnibus database under the accession number GSE158596. The logarithm of FPKM (fragments per kilobase of exon per million fragments mapped) was used as the estimation of the gene expression level. Genes with low counts were removed and only genes with FPKM > 100 were kept for further analysis.

Haferlach AML cohort38: Affymetrix gene expression data of AML (542 samples) and healthy bone marrow (73 samples) from the Haferlach dataset (GSE13159) was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus database. The platform used is Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array and the data values correspond to Robust Multichip Average (RMA) expression measure. Official gene symbols from the Hugo Gene Nomenclature Committee were retrieved from the Affymetrix probe identifiers using the R package biomaRt. Data was reduced at the gene level by selecting the probe with the highest median absolute deviation (MAD) across samples per gene.

TCGA cohort: Raw counts AML samples from the AML TCGA data were downloaded from the GDC portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). The raw counts were normalized and variance stabilized using DESeq2_1.42.1 package.

Human genes encoding proteins with strong support of mitochondrial localization were obtained from MitoCarta 3.0. Average expression levels of the 1136 MitoCarta genes and the 13 mitochondrial encoded genes were measured across all AML samples and mononuclear cells of bone marrow of healthy individual genes in the BeatAML 2.0 and GSE158596, respectively. In the Haferlach cohort, average expression levels of mitochondrial DNA replication (GO:0006264), mitochondrial transcription (GO:0006390), mitochondrial RNA processing (GO:0000963), mitochondrial RNA 3’ modification (GO:0031123) and mitochondrial RNA degradation (Reactome HSA-9836573.1) were measured across all AML samples and the bone marrow samples of healthy individuals. SUV3 and PNPT1 gene expression was measured in AML samples and healthy individual in the BeatAML 2.0, GSE158596 and the Haferlach cohort. A t test was used to estimate differential expression, and data were represented as violin plot using ggplot2 version 4.0.1.

For each AML versus normal comparison in each cohort, bootstrapping analysis was performed by 100 random samplings with replacement of AMLs chosen to be the same size as the normal group. For each random sampling, a t test was performed between the AML and the normal group. Results were visualized using a bar plot displaying the p value distribution using ggplot2 version 4.0.1.

For these AML versus normal comparisons, the list of AML samples with expression above the 90th percentile of the normal samples was retrieved. These AML samples were associated with clinical/lab data (FAB classification, ELN2017 risk frequent mutations and fusion proteins) and the distribution profile of each category was compared with the profile of total AMLs using a chi squared test. Data was visualized using a pie chart using ggplot2 version 4.0.1.

AML Proteomic dataset39: Imputed normalized data from Kramer et al. (10.1182/blood.2022016033) were used to split samples into 2 categories based on the median expression of SUV3 and PNPT1. Kaplan–Meier curves depicting overall survival were generated, and differences between groups were assessed using a log-rank test, with analyses performed using the survminer (v0.5.0) and survival (v3.8.3) R packages.

The proteomic dataset was also used to visualize the risk (ELN2017), FAB, and mutations categories in correlation with the expression of SUV3 or PNPT1 as well as the MitoCarta genes. A Wilcoxon rank-sum test assessed whether each category was associated with significantly higher or lower gene expression levels. Tests were corrected for multiple hypothesis testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Ridge plots were created using ggplot2 versions 3.5.2.

Stem versus myeloid gene expression analysis: Single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssgsea) was run using the R package GSVA_1.50.5 on the TCGA and Haferlach cohorts using AML derived gene lists obtained from the differential expression of LSC+ engrafting and LSC- non engrafting fractions known to be enriched in a stem and in a myeloid phenotype, respectively40. Enrichment scores were row normalized and visualized as a heatmap using the gplots R package. Samples with a positive ratio for the LSC+ versus the LSC- gene lists were classified as stem-like and the other cases were classified as myeloid-like. The gene expression of PNPT1 and SUV3 was assessed between the stem and myeloid categories in both the Haferlach and TCGA cohorts and represented as ridge plots. A Wilcoxon rank-sum test assessed whether each category was associated with significantly higher or lower gene expression levels. The gene expression of the mitochondrial DNA replication, mitochondrial transcription, mitochondrial RNA processing, mitochondrial RNA 3’ modification and mitochondrial RNA degradation was measured between the stem and myeloid categories in the Haferlach cohorts, the differential expression was estimated using a t test and the data represented as boxplots.

RNA isolation and real-time polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was isolated from AML cells using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (QIAGEN), and cDNA was prepared using SuperScript IV Reverse Transcriptase (ThermoFisher, MA, USA). Equal amounts of cDNA for each sample were added to a prepared master mix (Power SYBR Green PCR Master mix; Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). Quantitative Reverse Transcriptase Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT-PCR) was performed on the QuantStudio6Pro sequence detection system (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). The relative abundance of a transcript was represented by the threshold cycle of amplification (CT), which is inversely correlated to the amount of target RNA/first-strand cDNA being amplified. To normalize for equal amounts of cDNA we assayed the transcript amounts of 18 s rRNA gene. The comparative CT method was calculated as per manufacturer’s instructions. The primer sequences used are listed in Supplemental Table 2.

Immunoblotting

Total cell lysates from cell lines were lysed using RIPA buffer, and protein concentration was measured by the Bradford assay (Bio-Rad). Equal amounts of protein were analyzed on 10 to 12% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% milk or BSA in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 h and then incubated with primary antibody dissolved in 5% milk in TBST overnight at 4 °C. The membranes were washed three times before incubation for 1 h at room temperature with secondary horseradish peroxidase–conjugated donkey anti-rabbit antibody (GE Healthcare, Cat# NA934, RRID:AB_772206), sheep anti-mouse antibody (GE Healthcare; Cat# NA931, RRID:AB_772210). Primary antibodies: PNPase (14487-1-AP, Proteintech, IL, USA, RRID:AB_2165820), SUV3 (Bethyl Cat# A303-056A, RRID:AB_10895649, Texas, USA); β-Actin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology Cat# sc-69879, RRID:AB_1119529, Texas, USA).

DepMap CERES gene effect score analysis

CRISPR and RNAi gene effect score analysis was performed for AML cell lines across all the genes (version 22Q1). Gene effect scores for SUPV3L1 and PNPase across 1,128 cell lines representing 21 cancer types were obtained from the DepMap dataset (version 24Q2). Scores were grouped by cancer type, and the distribution of gene effect scores for each gene was visualized across cancer types using density plots.

Genetic depletion of the mitochondrial degradosome

Vectors and constructs

For the transduction of AML cell lines, SUV3 and PNPase shRNA constructs in hairpin-pLKO.1 vectors were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich® as glycerol bacterial stocks. The coding sequences of sgRNA’s targeting SUV3 and PNPase are listed in Supplementary Table 4 and the control LacZ genes is used as control. All sgRNA, shRNA, and overexpression plasmids were validated by Sanger sequencing.

Lentiviral packing

sgRNA and shRNA plasmids used in AML cell lines were isolated using the E.N.Z.A® Plasmid Midi Kit system (Omega bio-tek, GA, USA) from glycerol bacteria stocks, and then quantified by the NanoDrop (ThermoScientific, MA, USA) spectrophotometer. Lentivirus was made in a 25 cm2 flask format, by 293 T cells with a three-plasmid system (hairpin-pLKO.1 vector/ guide-pLCKO vector, packing plasmid with: gag, pol, tat and rev genes, and envelope plasmid, expressing VSV-G)42,43.

Lentivirus was made in a 175 cm2 flask format, by transfecting 293 T cells with a three-plasmid system (hairpin-pRS19 GFP vector, packing plasmid with: gag, pol, tat and rev genes, and envelope plasmid expressing VSV-G)42–44. The virus was concentrated using the Lenti-X Concentrator (Takara Bio USA, Inc.) as per manufacturer’s instructions.

shRNA knockdown in AML and lymphoma cell lines

Lentiviral infections with shRNA constructs were performed as previously described33. Briefly, 5 × 106 cells were centrifuged and resuspended in 5 mL of medium containing 5 μg/mL of protamine sulfate. 2 mL of virus was added to cells, followed by a 24-h incubation (37 °C, 5% CO2). On the following day, cells were centrifuged washed, and resuspended in fresh medium with puromycin (1.5 µg/mL for OCI-AML2, NB4, MV4-11, C1498, WEHI-3 and 8227 cells, 2 µg /mL for TEX, K562, and U-937 cells). After 3 days of selection, cell growth and viability was measured over time by cell counts and trypan blue exclusion staining.

Lentiviral transduction of sgRNA

1.0 × 106 CAS9 expressing OCI-AML2 cells were pelleted and resuspended in 3 mL of medium containing 5 μg/mL of protamine sulfate. 1 mL of virus was added to cells, followed by a 24-h incubation (37 °C, 5% CO2). On the following day, cells were centrifuged, washed, and then resuspended in fresh medium with puromycin (1.5 μg/mL). Cell growth and viability was measured over time by cell counts and trypan blue exclusion staining.

sgRNA nucleofection

AML cells were transduced with sgRNA using nucleofection. A total of 1×105–5×105 cells were rinsed with PBS and subjected to electroporation using the SF Cell Line 4D-Nucleofector X Kit L (V4XC-2024, Lonza, FL, USA) and Nucleofector 4D device (Lonza, FL, USA) following the manufactures protocol. Briefly, the electroporation mixture consisted of 2.5 µl of sgRNA (100 µM STOCK solution) and 1 µl high fidelity recombinant CAS9 (HiFi Cas9 Nuclease V3; IDT 1081061) and 21.5 µl of nucleofection mix from the kit.

Transduction of OCI AML8227 cells, AML patient samples, normal hematopoietic cells and cord blood cells

Transduction of 8227 cells, AML patient samples, normal hematopoietic cells and CD34 enriched cord blood cells were performed as described previously44,45. Wells in a 24-well non-tissue-culture-treated plate were coated with 500 μl of RetroNectin (Takara Bio USA, Inc.) (20 μg/mL in PBS) per well for 2 h at room temperature. The wells were blocked with 1 mL of 2% BSA (W/V) for 30 min at room temperature. The BSA solution was then aspirated, and concentrated lentiviral particles in HBSS with 25 mM HEPES were added to each well at a volume of 0.5 mL. The plate was then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 h at room temperature to promote the attachment of lentiviral particles to RetroNectin®. After aspirating 0.4 mL of the viral supernatant, 5 × 105 cells were added to each well in 1 mL of X-VIVO 10 (20% BIT, 50 ng/ml Flt3-L, 10 ng/mL IL6, 50 ng/mL SCF, 25 ng/mL TPO, 10 ng/mL IL3, 10 ng/mL G-CSF). The plate was then centrifuged again at 1300 rpm for 10 min at room temperature to promote the interaction between the cells and lentiviral particles, and then transferred to a 37 °C incubator to initiate lentiviral infection. Twenty-four hours afterward, the cells were resuspended in fresh media at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL and seeded in a 24-well plate at 1 mL per well. Three days later, transduction efficiency (% GFP + ) was determined by flow cytometry and expression confirmed by confocal microscopy.

Detection of intracellular dsRNA

Confocal microscopy

Cells were spread onto glass slides using Cytospin 4 (Epredia, Thermo Scientific, MA, USA). Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 3% BSA in PBS with 0.1% Triton X-10045. Then, cells were stained with primary antibodies against dsRNA46 (J2 antibody (Cat# 10010500), Scion, Budapest, Hungary) and Tom20 (Cat# 11802-1-AP, RRID, AB_2207530) for 1 h at room temperature and washed, followed by staining with a fluorochrome conjugated secondary antibodies Cy3 Donkey Anti-Mouse IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch Labs Cat# 715-165-150, RRID:AB_2340813) and Alexa Fluor488 Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch Labs Cat# 111-545-003, RRID:AB_2338046) for 45 min. Cells were stained with DAPI (5 μg/ml) to mark the nuclei. Cells were imaged with a SP8 confocal microscope from Leica SP8 Confocal Microscope (Leica Microsystems) at 60X. Intensity and ROI analysis were performed using LAS AF and ImageJ.

Flow cytometry

Cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% formaldehyde diluted in PBS for 20 min at room temperature. Cells were then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min followed by incubation in 1% BSA in PBS for 30 min. Primary anti-dsRNA (J2) (Cat# 10010500), Scion, Budapest, Hungary or mouse IgG antibodies were added at 1:100 ratio in 1% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Secondary anti mouse IgG conjugated with Alexa Fluor 700 was then added for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were washed three times. Cells were analyzed on a Fortessa X-20 (BD Biosciences, FL, USA) flow cytometer and Mean Fluorescence Intensity was measured with the FlowJo software (TreeStar, OR, USA). The gating strategy has been provided in the supplemental fig. 8.

Immunoprecipitation and sequencing

Cells were lysed in NP-40 lysis buffer (catalog #: J60766) for 30 min on ice. Protein A/G beads (catalog #: 88803) were incubated with J2 antibody(Cat# 10010500), Scion, Budapest, Hungary in NP-40 lysis buffer for 1 h at room temperature. Cell lysates were then incubated with antibody-conjugated beads for 2 h at 4 °C. Beads were then washed with a high-salt PBS buffer (1x PBS, 0.1% Tween, 1 M NaCl) followed by two washes with a low-salt PBS buffer (1x PBS, 0.1% Tween, 0.5 M NaCl). dsRNA was eluted from the immunoprecipitation beads by treating with 1 mg/ml Proteinase K (catalog #: P8044-1G) in NP-40 lysis buffer. The immunoprecipitated RNA was extracted with phenol–chloroform and then precipitated using ethanol.

Sequencing libraries were prepared using NEBNext Ultra Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina, v1.0 (cat. no. E7420) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq-2000 with 100-bp paired-end reads, v3 chemistry. The raw sequence data, in the form of FASTQ files, was aligned to the human genome (GRCh38, Homo_sapiens.GRCh38.84.gtf definition file) using the HISAT2 v2.2.1 (Kim et al., 2019) software tool. Accessory programs for the alignment stage include SAMTOOLS v1.14 (Heng et al., 2009). StringTie v2.1.4 (Pertea et al., 2015) was utilized for transcript assembly. Gene abundance was expressed as the logarithm of FPKM (fragments per kilobase of exon per million fragments mapped) across mitochondrial coded genes and all other protein coding genes. The dsRNA sequencing results has been submitted to GEO: GSE297113.

Nanostring gene expression

Expression of immune pathway genes after SUV3 and PNPase knockdown was measured using the NanoString NS_Immunology_v2_C2328, chip that contains probes for 579 genes involved in immune related processes. NanoString data were read using the R nanostringr_0.4.1 package and normalized using RUVSeq_1.36.0 using the combined positive and negative controls as reference. K-means clustering was utilized to partition the 579 Nanostring genes into 4 groups. Profiles of gene expression over each time point were observed and the centroid gene expression of the best profile showing a steady increase over the 4 time points was selected as reference for each sample. Pearson correlation to the reference profile was calculated for all genes. Genes correlated with the reference profile and in common between shSUV3 and shPNPase knockdown samples were extracted for further analyses. Pathway enrichment analysis of the profile genes was performed in g:Profiler https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/gost by selecting the Reactome database. Pathways significant at FDR 1e-08 were visualized using Cytoscape 3.10.2, EnrichmentMap 3.4.0 and AutoAnnotate 1.5.0. The dsRNA sequencing results has been submitted to GEO: GSE297053.

(Nunclon, Rochester, USA) to a final volume of 1 mL/dish in MethoCult H4100 media (StemCell Technologies, BC, Canada) supplemented with 30% FCS (OCI-AML2 and U937) or MethoCult H4100 media (Stem Cell Technologies, BC, Canada) supplemented with 30% FCS, 20 ng/mL SCF, and 2 ng/mL IL-3 (TEX cells). Non-specific esterase staining

Cells (1 × 105) were adhered to glass slides 5 days after transduction (SUV3 or PNPase shRNA plasmids) and then stained for nonspecific esterase (NSE) activity (Sigma-Aldrich), as per manufacturer’s instructions. The monocyte non-specific esterase inhibitor sodium fluoride was used to confirm the specificity of the reaction. Slides were scanned using the Aperio ScanScope AT2 (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), and the images analyzed by ImageJ to quantify the intensity of the staining.

Anti-leukemic activity of DNT cells

Ex vivo isolation and expansion of DNT: Healthy donor-derived PBMCs were depleted of CD4+ and CD8+ cells using CD4- and CD8-depletion cocktails (Stemcell Technologies) and cultured on anti-CD3 antibody-coated plates (5 μg/ml; OKT3; Miltenyi Biotec) in RPMI 1640 (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and IL-2 (250 IU/ml; Proleukin, Novartis Pharmaceuticals). The purity of DNTs was assessed by staining cells with fluorochrome-conjugated anti-human CD3, CD4, and CD8 antibodies and flow cytometry analysis.

Ex vivo isolation and expansion of conventional T cells (Tconv): Healthy donor-derived PBMCs were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS, IL2 (250 IU/ml; Proleukin, Novartis Pharmaceuticals), and ImmunoCult™ Human CD3/CD28 T Cell Activator (Stemcell Technologies). The phenotype of Tconv was assessed by staining cells with fluorochrome-conjugated anti-human CD3, CD4, and CD8 antibodies and flow cytometry analysis.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay: Target cells were cocultured with DNTs in triplicates in a 96-well plate at a 0.25:1 effector-to-target ratio for 18 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, cells were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated anti-human CD3 and CD33 antibodies and annexin V. Viability of the target cells was analyzed staining with Annexin V and by flow cytometry. Percent specific cytotoxicity was calculated using the formula: (% Annexin V+ with DNT - % Annexin V+ without DNT)/(100 -% Annexin V+ without DNT) x 100% as previously described25,26. DNT were enriched from healthy donor peripheral blood samples and expanded as previously described25,52.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 6.0 was used to perform analyses. Statistical analyses were performed by unpaired student’s t test or one-way ANOVA to compare mean values between multiple groups. Statistical significance values were indicated as follows: *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001, and ****p ≤ 0.0001. Data distribution was assumed to be normal but this was not formally tested. The investigators were not blinded. However, key experiments were reproduced independently by different individuals. Quantitative endpoints were used for measurements. No data has been excluded.
