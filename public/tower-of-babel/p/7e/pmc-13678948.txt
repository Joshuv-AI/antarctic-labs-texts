Demyelination of the central nervous system and peripheral neuropathy associated with sintilimab treatment for lung adenocarcinoma: a case report
Authors: Haibo Deng, Yu He, Hailong Wei, Qun Yi, Wei Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy unleashes the body’s natural immune system to fight cancer. Neurological immune-related adverse events (NirAEs) are rare but can be severe.Case presentationIn this report, we present a 47-year-old man of Han ethnicity who was diagnosed with lung adenocarcinoma with intracranial metastasis. Initially, the patient underwent intracranial radiotherapy followed by first-line and maintenance chemotherapy with pemetrexed, cisplatin and bevacizumab, and a partial response was achieved without significant toxicity. After progression, the patient received second-line chemotherapy with carboplatin and albumin-bound paclitaxel as well as an ICI, sintilimab, every 28 days. After completing nine cycles of sintilimab therapy, the patient suddenly experienced lower limb paresthesia, weakness and temporary confusion. Findings from diagnostic studies, including laboratory, electrophysiological and imaging studies, combined with the clinical presentation led to the diagnosis of NirAEs, including peripheral neuropathy and demyelination of the central nervous system. Sintilimab therapy was suspended, and intravenous corticosteroid therapy was initiated, resulting in a temporary rapid resolution of all neuropathic symptoms. However, the patient died within one month after the onset of neuropathic symptoms.ConclusionThis extremely rare case of NirAEs affecting both the peripheral and central nervous systems had the worst prognosis.Keywords: Immunotherapy, Immune checkpoint inhibitor, Neurological immune-related adverse events, Neuropathy

Background

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have played a central role in the treatment of non-small cell lung cancer with nondriver gene mutations during the past decade. ICIs can cause widespread autoimmune toxicity, including neurologic toxicity, that limits therapeutic potential and can progress rapidly, necessitating prompt recognition. Neurological immune-related adverse events (NirAEs) associated with ICIs are relatively rare, with an incidence estimated at approximately 1–12%, and the peripheral nervous system (PNS) is affected twice as frequently as the central nervous system (CNS) [1, 2]. Sintilimab is a monoclonal antibody that targets the programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) receptor on T cells, thereby increasing the antitumor immune response. In this article, we report a rare case of NirAEs that affected both the CNS and the PNS and were induced by the domestic PD-1 inhibitor sintilimab.

Case presentation

A 47-year-old man of Han ethnicity was diagnosed with stage IVb left lung adenocarcinoma, with intracranial, pericardium, and vertebral metastasis (Fig. 1), in November 2019. No driver mutations were detected, and PD-L1 expression was > 1%. He underwent intracranial radiotherapy at 60 Gy followed by chemotherapy with pemetrexed plus cisplatin. During the maintenance therapy stage, the patient received pemetrexed and bevacizumab. The patient did well until February 2021, when increases in the mass of the left lung lesion and the carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) level were observed. Second-line treatment was initiated with carboplatin and albumin-bound paclitaxel as well as sintilimab, a PD-1 inhibitor, at an intravenous dosage of 3 mg/kg every 3 weeks. After receiving 6 cycles of combined chemotherapy, the size of the lung lesion was well controlled until June 2021. Monotherapy with sintilimab was continued, and the patient experienced severe myelosuppression, hyponatremia and sepsis in July 2021. After recovery from this dangerous condition, persistent hyponatremia developed. Paraneoplastic syndrome was considered when other possibilities were excluded, and then 30 mg of prednisolone was started. Hyponatremia was relieved to some extent but then persisted. Two months later, after completing a total of 9 cycles of sintilimab, this patient experienced sudden lower limb weakness and paresthesia, which were more pronounced on the left side. A neurological examination revealed generalized hyporeflexia, distal hypoesthesia, paralysis of the lower extremities, and a manual muscle testing score of only 1. The remaining physical examination findings were unremarkable.

The diagnostic workup was negative for vascular, metabolic, infectious, and systemic autoimmune diseases and critical illness-related neuropathy. Electrophysiological tests revealed severe non-length-dependent sensorimotor polyneuropathy characterized by motor predominance and asymmetry (more pronounced in the left leg), axonal disorder and radiculopathy. Cervicothoracolumbar spine magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) without contrast revealed multiple ill-defined, foggy T2-weighted hyperintense lesions and isointense signal in T1-weighted images, which suggested demyelinating changes (Fig. 2). Lumbar puncture (LP) was carried out, and the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) protein, chloride, and glucose levels were within normal limits. Tumor cell was not detected in CSF. The diagnosis of NirAEs was made, sintilimab therapy was discontinued, and high-dose corticosteroid therapy (80 mg/day of methyl prednisolone for 5 days) was initiated after a comprehensive assessment and thorough discussion with the neurologist. Corticosteroid pulse therapy was not selected because of a fear of massive hemorrhage in the digestive tract because of the patient’s past medical history. Three days after the treatment, leg weakness was ameliorated, and 1 week later, the neurological symptoms of the patient were dramatically alleviated. Subsequently, the manual muscle test score recovered to 5, and the patient could stand on his own beside the bed. Corticosteroid therapy gradually tapered to 30 mg/day of prednisolone as maintenance therapy. Unfortunately, this patient experienced dyspnea, temporary consciousness disorders and confusion 20 days later, and his peripheral neurological symptoms, including paresthesia and leg paralysis, were exacerbated. Pulmonary embolism was excluded via computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA), and the lung lesion size was more stable than before. An emergency brain MRI revealed cerebral metastases in the left hemisphere, with findings similar to those of the previous scan, and the hyperintensity of the spinal cord lesion was greater than that before (Fig. 3). One day later, the patient died from respiratory failure (Fig. 4).

Discussion and conclusions

With the widespread use of immunotherapy, neurologic toxicity has become highly concerning among physicians. Diagnosing neurotoxicity can be particularly challenging in patients with brain metastasis. It is difficult to differentiate the presentations of tumor progression, radiation-related and chemotherapy-related adverse events, and paraneoplastic neurologic syndromes. Stable primary and existed intracranial lesion, negative of cytological analysis in CSF as well as the lack of a typical MRI appearance of peritumoral vasogenic edema and elevated signals of the leptomeninges along the cerebral sulci, cerebellar folia, and cranial nerves in fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images ruled out new metastatic lesions, including leptomeningeal metastasis (Figs. 2and 3). Radiation-induced myelopathy could be excluded because of the time interval longer than 17 months from radiotherapy to the onset of neurological symptoms. A low dose of corticosteroids was applied for hyponatremia two months before neurological symptom onset, which made paraneoplastic neurologic syndromes less likely. This patient was previously treated with neurotoxic chemotherapy, including albumin-bound paclitaxel, which complicated the differential diagnosis. Chemotherapy-related neuropathy is more likely to be length dependent, which means that symptoms start in the distal lower extremities and progress proximally [2]. In this patient, the limiting neurotoxic dose of chemotherapy drugs was not reached, and non-length-dependent and highly asymmetric presentations essentially ruled out chemotherapy-related neuropathy. Consecutive MRI showed the gradual progression of patchy diffusion and hyperintensity patterns within a short time interval, which is consistent with acute and active demyelination in the spinal cord [2].

NirAEs have mostly been reported to involve the PNS. For example, cases of ipilimumab-induced Guillain–Barré syndrome, durvalumab-induced peripheral polyneuropathy, and pembrolizumab-induced myasthenia gravis have been reported [1, 2]. CNS demyelinating disease can manifest as spinal cord and/or brain lesions; therefore, symptoms overlap with myelitis and encephalitis, and standard methods for differentiating them in published case series are lacking [1]. Maurice et al. reported that a 60-year-old man with metastatic melanoma who was first exposed to nivolumab and subsequently to ipilimumab developed contrast-enhancing demyelination of the CNS [3]. Cao et al. reported inflammatory CNS demyelination in a patient with melanoma treated with ipilimumab [4]. Although CSF findings of elevated protein levels and pleocytosis are common in immune-related meningitis, myelitis and encephalitis as well as in patients with demyelinating disease, these findings are not specific or considered as the gold standard. In an 18-case series of immune-mediated demyelination, 78% had elevated protein levels, 56% had pleocytosis, and 39% had oligoclonal bands [2]. To our knowledge, simultaneous NirAEs in both the CNS and the PNS caused by ICIs are rare [1]. This patient developed PNS symptoms first, and CNS symptoms emerged later. Abnormal electrodiagnostic, imaging and laboratory evidence and the absence of evidence for alternative diagnoses solidified the diagnosis of peripheral neuropathy and demyelination of the CNS.

NirAE-related symptoms, including the acute or subacute onset of neuropathy symptoms, usually occur within 3 months of starting ICI treatment. However, reports of late-onset symptoms after more than ten ICI cycles have also been described. Although NirAEs may occur anytime during treatment or after treatment completion, per the diagnostic criteria, NirAE-related symptoms must begin within 12 months of the last ICI infusion [2]. Ma et al. reported two cases of sintilimab-induced peripheral neuropathy, among which one patient presented with a more chronic course of more than 9 months [5]. Our patient had more than 8 months of ICI exposure along with a poor outcome. The exact cause of the patient’s respiratory failure remains unclear. Given the stable intracranial lesion and normal CTPA results, the most likely etiology is neuromuscular respiratory failure, which was secondary to either neuropathy or central causes due to CNS demyelination.

Several lessons can be learned from this case. First, we did not stop ICI treatment with the emergence of paraneoplastic syndrome, which might have worsened the prognosis. Second, enhanced MRI with contrast should be carried out, which could more easily differentiate tumor progression or leptomeningeal disease. Third, immunosuppressive therapies, including intravenous immunoglobulin and plasma exchange, were refused by the patient’s wife when symptoms relapsed. Fourth, LP with CSF studies could evaluate the evidence of CSF-restricted antibody production with oligoclonal bands to exclude other diagnoses. Examination of neural-specific antibodies in the CSF was not performed, which could have verified the diagnosis of CNS demyelination.
