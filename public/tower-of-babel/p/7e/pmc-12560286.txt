Matched analysis of traction-assisted posterior spinal correction in severe scoliosis with and without syringomyelia
Authors: Xiaobing Tian, Jin Zhou, Yingsong Wang, Jingming Xie, Zhi Zhao, Tao Li, Tingbiao Zhu, Jie Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate whether the presence of syringomyelia (SM) influences the efficacy of preoperative traction and final corrective outcomes in patients with severe scoliosis (SS) undergoing one-stage posterior spinal fusion.MethodsA retrospective matched study was conducted on 21 patients with severe scoliosis combined with syringomyelia (SS-SM) and 21 matched patients with severe idiopathic scoliosis (SIS), treated between 2007 and 2023. Patients were matched 1:1 based on sex, age, major curve type, Cobb angle, and number of curves. All patients underwent skull-femoral traction followed by one-stage posterior spinal correction. Radiographic parameters, surgical data, and neurological outcomes were compared between groups.ResultsNo significant differences were found between the SS-SM and SIS groups in kyphosis angle, curve flexibility, traction correction rate, total correction rate, or the respective contributions of traction and surgery to the final correction (p > 0.05 for all). Both groups achieved over 50% correction through preoperative traction. Neurological complications were minimal and reversible, with no permanent deficits reported. Notably, the presence of SM did not increase the incidence of intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring abnormalities or postoperative neurological dysfunction.ConclusionPreoperative traction followed by one-stage posterior correction is equally effective and safe for patients with SS-SM and SIS. The presence of syringomyelia does not adversely affect traction response or final surgical outcomes. These findings challenge the traditional view that prophylactic neurosurgical decompression is mandatory prior to scoliosis correction in SM patients, and support the feasibility of direct orthopedic intervention under strict selection criteria and neuromonitoring protocols.Keywords: Severe scoliosis, Syringomyelia, Traction, Surgical correction, Matched analysis

Introduction

Syringomyelia (SM) is a common intraspinal pathology characterized by fluid-filled cavities within the spinal cord and is frequently associated with spinal deformities such as scoliosis [1–4]. In patients with severe scoliosis accompanied by SM (SS-SM), the spinal curvature can progress rapidly, threatening cardiopulmonary function and neurological stability, thus often necessitating timely surgical correction. Conventionally, prophylactic neurosurgical decompression (PND) has been advocated prior to scoliosis correction to reduce the risk of intraoperative neurological injury [5–8].

However, growing clinical evidence challenges the efficacy of routine PND in preventing neurological complications. Cases of postoperative neurological deterioration have been reported even after prior decompression, raising questions about the necessity and optimal timing of neurosurgical intervention [9–14]. In contrast, several studies suggest that selected SS-SM patients without preoperative neurological deficits may safely undergo posterior correction without prior decompression [10, 11, 15–17]. Despite these observations, the specific impact of SM on preoperative traction outcomes and final surgical correction efficacy remains unclear.

Preoperative traction is a widely accepted strategy for managing rigid and severe scoliosis. It offers a gradual and controlled correction of spinal deformity while enabling continuous neurological assessment in an awake state [18–21]. However, it remains unknown whether SS-SM patients derive similar benefits from traction and whether the presence of SM modifies the therapeutic value or safety profile of this technique.

This study aims to investigate whether syringomyelia affects the traction response and final surgical outcomes in patients with severe scoliosis. We conducted a retrospective matched-pair analysis of SS-SM patients and those with severe idiopathic scoliosis (SIS), matched by sex, age, curve type, Cobb angle, and number of curves, all undergoing preoperative traction followed by one-stage posterior spinal correction.

Materials and methods

Patient selection and matching criteria

This retrospective study reviewed patients treated at the Department of Orthopaedics, Second Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University, between January 2007 and October 2023. A total of 40 SS-SM patients who had undergone preoperative skull-femoral traction followed by one-stage posterior spinal fusion were initially screened.

Inclusion criteria for the SS-SM group were as follows: Scoliosis with major Cobb angle >80°; Confirmed diagnosis of syringomyelia by routine admission MRI; No preoperative neurological deficits, defined as American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) grade E [22]; Complete preoperative and follow-up imaging and clinical data.

Exclusion criteria included

History of spinal surgery; Prior neurosurgical intervention (e.g., syrinx shunting, foramen magnum decompression, syrinx fenestration); Scoliosis secondary to congenital, syndromic, or connective tissue disorders; Syringomyelia due to non-idiopathic causes such as tumors, trauma, or infections.

Based on matching criteria including the same sex, identical major curve type, similar coronal Cobb angle, and the same number of spinal curves. Besides, age also served as a critical matching criterion, with each paired case requiring an age difference of less than 5 years. The final age range was 11–41 years in the SS-SM group and 12–41 years in the SIS group. A number of 21 SIS patients were 1:1 matched with the SS-SM group from the same treatment period at our institution who underwent the same treatment protocol. All procedures were performed by the same surgical team, and informed consent was obtained from all participants or their guardians. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee.

Traction protocol

All patients underwent preoperative traction using the skull-femoral traction (SFT) technique. After completing all required preoperative evaluations, patients were placed in the supine position. Gardner-Wells tongs were applied to the skull for cranial traction, and Kirschner wires were inserted bilaterally through the distal femoral condyles to establish femoral traction [19]. The initial traction force was set at no more than 15% of the patient’s body weight, distributed evenly between cranial and femoral ends.

In the absence of neurological symptoms or signs of intolerance, the traction weight was gradually increased by 3–6 kg per day, targeting a maximum of approximately 60% of the patient’s body weight. The total traction period lasted approximately 2 to 3 weeks, depending on the degree of deformity correction observed and the patient’s tolerance.

Patients receiving spinal traction necessitate constant observation of neurological function and correction of spinal deformity during the traction procedure, especially those with concomitant SM. As a consequence, all traction therapy must be administered on an inpatient basis. Daily assessments focused on cranial nerve function, peripheral motor and sensory function, and cauda equina function. In addition, routine nursing care, respiratory training, limb mobilization exercises, pin site disinfection, and nutritional support were provided. If patients developed symptoms of neurological compromise or were unable to tolerate the traction, the weight was reduced incrementally. Sudden removal of all traction force was strictly prohibited to avoid rebound effects or spinal cord stress.

Full-length spinal X-rays in the supine traction position were obtained weekly to monitor the progression of correction. The traction procedure was designed not only to provide preliminary deformity correction but also to assess spinal cord tolerance under gradual mechanical elongation, thereby improving spinal and thoracic compliance prior to definitive surgery.

Surgical technique

Following the completion of preoperative traction, all patients underwent one-stage posterior spinal correction and fusion under general anesthesia. After positioning the patient in the prone position, intraoperative skull-femoral traction was re-applied at 50% of the pre-traction weight. Standard intraoperative neuromonitoring (IONM), including somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEP) and transcranial motor evoked potentials (TcMEP), was utilized in all cases. Baseline amplitudes and latencies were recorded after positioning but prior to skin preparation to ensure optimal neuromonitoring reliability throughout the procedure.

A midline posterior incision was made to expose the posterior spinal elements subperiosteally. Freehand placement of pedicle screws was performed bilaterally, and screw positioning was verified using intraoperative fluoroscopy. Based on the severity and rigidity of the deformity, different levels of spinal osteotomies were conducted. In most cases, low-grade osteotomies (Grade ≤ 3) such as Ponte osteotomies or partial facetectomies were employed to achieve initial release. For more rigid deformities, high-grade osteotomies (Grade ≥ 4), including vertebral column resection and spinal shortening procedures, were performed. These involved resection of posterior elements, pedicle and vertebral body structures, and adjacent intervertebral discs to establish sufficient mobility and correction space.

Correction was initially achieved on the convex side through rod placement and compression maneuvers, followed by sequential adjustments on the concave side using derotation, translation, and in situ bending techniques. In high-grade osteotomy cases, spinal column shortening was achieved through controlled compression across the osteotomy gap. If a residual gap remained, titanium cages were placed and packed with bone graft. Where closure was complete, autologous bone was adequately impacted to promote fusion. All fusion segments were thoroughly decorticated, and both autograft and allograft were used to achieve a robust fusion bed [23, 24].

Throughout the surgical procedure, mean arterial pressure (MAP) was maintained above 75 mmHg to ensure spinal cord perfusion. Traction force was dynamically adjusted according to real-time changes in IONM signals. Stagnara wake-up tests were conducted after the accomplishment of critical procedural milestones (including the completion of screw implantation, osteotomy, and final correction) to verify intraoperative neurological safety.

Radiographic and clinical assessment

All patients underwent full-length standing spinal radiographs, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) before initiating traction therapy. During the traction period, weekly supine traction X-rays were obtained to monitor spinal deformity changes, and final imaging was completed postoperatively. Radiographic evaluations focused on several key parameters [25–29], including the Cobb angle and flexibility of the major scoliotic curve (flexibility calculated based on differences between standing and supine side-bending radiographs) [30], the number and classification of spinal curves, as well as the kyphosis angle and the corresponding number of affected vertebral segments.

To quantify the efficacy of treatment, we calculated the traction correction rate, total correction rate, and the relative contributions of traction and surgery to overall deformity correction. Specifically, the traction correction rate was defined as the percentage reduction in Cobb angle following traction, calculated as (Pre-traction Cobb−Post-traction Cobb) / Pre-traction Cobb × 100. The total correction rate was measured as the percentage change from pre-traction to postoperative Cobb angle, using the formula (Pre-traction Cobb – Postoperative Cobb) / Pre-traction Cobb × 100. Additionally, the contribution of traction to total correction was determined by dividing the correction achieved during traction by the total correction, and the surgical contribution was defined as the correction obtained during surgery relative to the total correction, with the sum of both contributions equating to 100%.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent samples t-tests. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient matching and baseline characteristics

A total of 21 matched pairs of patients were successfully enrolled, comprising 9 males and 12 females in both the SS-SM and SIS groups. Matching was performed based on sex, age, major curve type, coronal Cobb angle, and the number of scoliotic curves. All patients in both groups presented with thoracic major curves. The distribution of curve types (single, double, and triple) was identical across groups. The mean age was 18.05 ± 8.07 years in the SS-SM group and 17.52 ± 7.90 years in the SIS group. The average pre-traction Cobb angle of the major curve was 109.33° ± 18.10° in SS-SM and 109.48° ± 16.39° in SIS, with no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05) (Table 1).

Traction and surgical correction outcomes

Both groups demonstrated substantial improvements in coronal and sagittal deformities after preoperative traction and definitive surgery. There were no statistically significant differences in post-traction or postoperative Cobb angles, kyphosis angles, or their respective correction rates between the two groups (P > 0.05) (Fig. 1).In the SS-SM group, the mean total correction rate of the major curve was 65.68% ± 9.42%, with preoperative traction accounting for 55.01% ± 19.68% of the correction and surgical intervention contributing the remaining 44.99% ± 19.68%. Similarly, in the SIS group, the total correction rate was 63.70% ± 7.42%, of which 53.00% ± 12.30% was achieved by preoperative traction and 47.00% ± 12.30% by surgery. Similar proportions were observed in kyphosis correction, suggesting that the presence of SM did not significantly affect either the magnitude or distribution of correction achieved (Table 3; Fig. 2).

Surgical parameters and neurological safety

There were no significant differences in osteotomy grade distribution, number of fused vertebrae, estimated blood loss, or total operative time between groups (P > 0.05) (Table 4). Notably, two patients in the SS-SM group experienced transient numbness during traction, one of whom encountered intraoperative neuromonitoring challenges, including gradual SSEP baseline loss after paraspinal muscle dissection. However, TcMEP signals remained intact, Stagnara wake-up tests were negative, and no postoperative neurological deficits were noted. In the SIS group, one patient developed transient numbness during traction, which resolved without intervention. Across both groups, no patients exhibited postoperative motor deficits or permanent neurological impairments.

Discussion

Surgical treatment of SS-SM patients poses dual challenges: addressing rigid spinal deformity while ensuring neurological safety in the presence of intramedullary pathology. Traditional treatment strategies have long advocated for PND before scoliosis correction in patients with syringomyelia [6, 9, 13, 31, 32], aiming to reduce the risk of neurological complications during subsequent spinal surgery. However, there is still a lack of solid evidence proving that PND effectively decreases the incidence of neurological injury in later-stage orthopedic procedures [9, 13, 14]. Based on our previous clinical experience and findings, we observed that one-stage posterior spinal correction assisted by preoperative traction can be a safe and effective alternative for SS-SM patients without prior decompression [17]. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether the presence of syringomyelia affects the efficacy of traction or the final corrective outcomes in scoliosis surgery. This study intended to objectively evaluate the issue by matched analysis of SS-SM and SIS patients receiving identical treatment protocols. Given the relative rarity of both conditions and rigorous matching criteria, a 1:1 matching protocol was ultimately employed. By doing so, we aimed to clarify the clinical impact of SM on scoliosis correction and to provide stronger evidence supporting the feasibility and safety of traction-assisted one-stage posterior correction in this complex patient population.

Reconsidering the role of prophylactic neurosurgical decompression

Our results support the growing view that PND is not universally required in SS-SM patients, particularly those without preoperative neurological deficits (ASIA grade E). None of the SS-SM patients in our cohort underwent prior neurosurgical intervention, yet achieved comparable correction rates and neurological outcomes to the SIS group. This aligns with prior studies referenced in our manuscript, which indicate that there is insufficient high-level evidence proving PND reduces neurological complications in scoliosis correction. Notably, Welling et al. [14] reported delayed postoperative deficits despite prior decompression, questioning the protective value of PND. A retrospective matched analysis by Godzik et al. [13] reported that in scoliosis patients with concurrent syringomyelia, most individuals who underwent primary neurosurgical intervention experienced a reduction in syrinx size postoperatively. However, during subsequent spinal deformity correction, a considerable number of these patients encountered intraoperative neuromonitoring difficulties and perioperative neurological complications, with incidence rates significantly higher than those observed in the matched idiopathic scoliosis group. These findings suggest that while PND may effectively reduce syrinx size in selected cases, it does not necessarily translate into a reduced risk of neurological injury during secondary spinal correction procedures. Our findings, therefore, suggest that in the absence of symptomatic neural compression, direct correction with careful intraoperative management may be a viable alternative, potentially avoiding the risks and delays associated with neurosurgical procedures [15–17, 33].

The safety and value of preoperative traction in SS-SM

Preoperative traction has long been considered a valuable adjunct in the treatment of rigid spinal deformities [19, 20, 34, 35]. In our study, both SS-SM and SIS patients achieved significant curvature improvement through traction, with an average contribution exceeding 50% of the total correction in both coronal and sagittal planes. This demonstrates that syringomyelia does not impede the corrective response to gradual traction forces. Furthermore, only minor, transient neurological symptoms occurred during traction in a small number of patients (2 in SS-SM, 1 in SIS), all of which resolved spontaneously. These results support prior literature suggesting that traction provides not only mechanical benefit but also a functional test of spinal cord tolerance in awake patients [19, 36–38]—an especially valuable feature in the SS-SM population. Our findings reinforce traction as a safe, effective, and informative strategy for managing severe deformities even in the presence of SM.

Comparable correction outcomes in SS-SM and SIS patients

Despite SS-SM patients exhibiting slightly longer major curve segments than SIS controls (7.05 vs. 6.24, P = 0.037), both groups achieved statistically similar correction rates in the coronal and sagittal planes, whether from traction or surgery. This suggests that SM does not diminish spinal responsiveness to correction maneuvers. Moreover, the similar distributions of osteotomy grade, fusion length, surgical time, and blood loss between groups further indicate that the presence of SM does not necessitate more invasive or complex procedures when preoperative planning is adequate. The matched analysis design strengthens these conclusions by controlling for key confounding variables. These findings challenge the traditional belief that SS-SM represents a distinctly higher-risk surgical group [5–8], and instead support the view that with proper preparation, SS-SM patients can achieve outcomes equivalent to idiopathic scoliosis patients.

Strict case selection and intraoperative neuromonitoring are critical to surgical safety

Careful patient selection and IONM form the cornerstone of safe surgical correction in SS-SM patients. In patients with spinal deformity, greater Cobb angles correlate with more technical challenges in correction and elevated risks of neurological injury, particularly in those with concomitant SM. Generally, patients with Cobb angles over 80° require higher-grade osteotomies to achieve satisfactory correction. Therefore, we typically category patients with Cobb angles exceeding 80° as SS-SM cases, providing heightened preoperative attention and implementing thorough procedures to minimize surgical risks. We do not advocate for routine PND in all SS-SM patients, but equally, we do not support one-stage scoliosis correction without PND in all such cases. Instead, we emphasize the need for comprehensive preoperative neurological evaluation to identify candidates without clinical signs of spinal cord dysfunction—those who are most likely to tolerate correction safely.

Previous literature classifies syringomyelia into three morphological types based on MRI characteristics: swollen (expansile), fusiform, and slit-like syrinxes [39–41]. Based on our clinical observations, patients with swelling-type syringomyelia exhibit notable increases in intraspinal pressure due to large syrinx cavities, thereby rendering them more susceptible to neurological injuries, including sensory deficits, motor dysfunction, and sphincter impairments, during preoperative, traction, and intraoperative phases. For these patients, we recommend against single-stage spinal correction. In contrast, patients with fusiform or slit-like syrinx configurations tend to demonstrate minimal increases in spinal cord pressure. If no significant neurological deficits are observed during preoperative evaluation or traction, this indicates their pathological spinal cords can adequately tolerate tension changes caused by spinal realignment, cord displacement, and fluctuations in cerebrospinal fluid pressure during correction. In these carefully selected patients, one-stage posterior correction with preoperative traction can be both safe and effective(Fig. 3).

All patients in this study underwent comprehensive IONM, including SSEP and TcMEP assessments, with real-time adjustments to traction and surgical parameters based on neuromonitoring feedback. Although one SS-SM patient experienced SSEP signal loss after muscle dissection, TcMEP signals remained stable, and no postoperative neurological deficits were observed. This emphasizes the importance of multimodal monitoring and intraoperative flexibility in managing SS-SM patients. Our protocol required the maintenance of MAP above 75 mmHg. Stagnara wake-up tests were conducted upon the completion of critical procedural stages (including the completion of screw placement, osteotomy, and final correction) to eliminate potential IONM blind spots, thereby establishing a dual safety protocol that conjointly ensured intraoperative spinal cord protection. The absence of permanent neurological complications in either group further supports the reliability and necessity of these techniques, particularly in patients with underlying spinal cord pathology.

Limitations and implications for future research

This single-center retrospective study, with a restricted sample size, may be subject to potential bias. While the matched design enhances internal validity, the absence of subgroup analysis based on syrinx type (e.g., fusiform vs. slit-like), location, length or associated anomalies (e.g., Chiari malformation) limits external generalizability. Additionally, long-term follow-up data on syrinx evolution post-correction are not yet available. Consequently, the changes in SM post-spinal correction surgery remain indeterminate, representing a limitation of our study. Future multicenter prospective studies with MRI-based morphological stratification and extended follow-up could provide deeper insight into optimal patient selection and long-term outcomes.

Clinical significance and innovation

To our knowledge, this is the first matched analysis quantitatively comparing the contributions of preoperative traction and surgery to the overall correction in SS-SM versus SIS patients. Our findings provide new evidence that may reshape clinical decision-making by questioning the routine need for PND in selected cases and validating a direct orthopedic approach with appropriate monitoring and patient selection.

This study offers direct clinical evidence that traction-assisted one-stage posterior correction is both safe and effective for SS-SM patients without prior neurological impairment. It challenges the assumption that all such cases require neurosurgical decompression and affirms the value of integrating mechanical preparation (traction), real-time monitoring (IONM), and strategic correction (graded osteotomy) into a unified treatment algorithm. These findings support a more individualized, evidence-based approach to treating complex spinal deformities, with potential to reduce treatment duration, surgical burden, and neurological risk.

Conclusion

Our matched analysis demonstrates that preoperative skull-femoral traction followed by one-stage posterior spinal correction is a safe and effective strategy for SS-SM patients, provided that patients exhibit no preoperative neurological deficits. The presence of syringomyelia did not adversely affect traction response, surgical correction outcomes, or increase the risk of neurological complications compared to matched SIS patients. Both traction and surgery contributed similarly to deformity correction in SS-SM and SIS groups, and no significant differences were observed in intraoperative parameters or postoperative recovery.

These findings challenge the traditional assumption that prophylactic neurosurgical decompression is mandatory in SS-SM cases and support a more individualized treatment approach. With proper patient selection, thorough preoperative assessment, and rigorous intraoperative neuromonitoring, direct spinal correction without prior neurosurgical intervention is feasible and safe. This study adds valuable evidence to the evolving paradigm of scoliosis management and highlights the importance of multidisciplinary planning in treating complex spinal deformities.
