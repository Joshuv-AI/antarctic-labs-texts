Smoking-related interstitial fibrosis– a case series
Authors: Fernando Pereira da Silva, André Carvalho, Susana Guimarães, Conceição Souto Moura, André Terras Alexandre, Natália Melo, Patrícia Mota, Hélder Novais e Bastos, António Morais
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSmoking-related interstitial fibrosis (SRIF) is an increasingly recognized entity characterized by localized areas with respiratory bronchiolitis (RB), emphysema and interstitial fibrosis that occurs in smokers or former smokers. First described in 2010, the pathogenesis of SRIF remains unclear, although it is believed to be linked to inflammatory processes derived from smoking. The diagnosis is often accidental through pulmonary parenchyma samples obtained for other purposes, as it is not usually associated with significant respiratory symptoms. Transbronchial lung cryobiopsy (TBLC) is an accurate and safe procedure for diagnosing smoking-related interstitial lung diseases (ILDs).Case presentationThis study presents a case series of 13 patients diagnosed with SRIF through TBLC. Patients were predominantly male (76.9%), with a median age of 55 years and a median smoking history of 44 pack-years. Common symptoms included cough (53.8%) and dyspnoea (30.8%), while 38.5% were asymptomatic. Lung Function Tests enclosed normal lung volumes but an impaired diffusion capacity (DLCO). High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) revealed centrilobular and paraseptal emphysema in all patients, with bilateral ground‒glass opacities in 81.8% of them. Histologically, SRIF was characterized by hyalinized alveolar septal fibrosis and intra-alveolar pigmented macrophages. Over the follow-up period, clinical evolution was variable, however, despite this inconsistency, this series seems to corroborate that the majority of the patients have a stable clinical course. Nonetheless, it must be underline that a subset experienced radiological or functional progression, particularly a decline in DLCO, although any case of progressive fibrosis associated with respiratory insufficiency was noticed. Still, the small sample size and observed variability, limits definitive conclusions about the overall natural history of SRIF. Based on the available data, no therapeutic intervention is recommended at diagnosis besides smoking cessation.ConclusionsThis case series highlights the mostly indolent nature of SRIF, although progression can occur in a subset of patients. The variability in clinical evolution underscores the importance of accurate diagnosis and individualized follow-up. Further prospective studies with larger cohorts are needed to clarify the natural history of SRIF and guide management strategies. Introduction

Smoking-related interstitial fibrosis (SRIF) is an increasingly recognized entity characterized by localized areas with respiratory bronchiolitis (RB), emphysema and interstitial fibrosis that occurs in smokers or former smokers. The first time this condition was mentioned under the name SRIF was in 2010 by Katzenstein and colleagues, when areas of severe fibrosis associated with emphysema and RB were quite commonly found (45%) in extensive areas in the lung parenchyma of smokers who underwent lobectomy due to lung cancer [1].

The pathogenesis of SRIF is still unknown, although the authors suggested that inflammatory or other unknown processes derived from smoking could impact emphysema and fibrosis [2, 3].

The diagnosis is often accidental through pulmonary parenchyma samples obtained for other purposes, as it is not usually associated with significant respiratory symptoms [1]. When present, dyspnoea and cough are the most common symptoms. Functionally, patients can present mild/moderate obstruction and mild/moderate reduction in diffusion capacity (DLCO) [4].

The most frequent changes on high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) are micronodular and ground-glass opacities (GGO) [5, 6]. Histologically, it is usually characterized by thickening of alveolar septa by hyalinized fibrosis and intra-alveolar pigmented macrophages, mostly subpleural [1, 3–8].

The prognosis of SRIF is still unknown because of the short follow-up time and reduced number of cases reported in the literature. While progression towards more severe disease seems uncommon, some patients may experience functional or radiological worsening over time [4].

The authors present a case series of SRIF in which the final diagnosis was obtained after transbronchial lung cryobiopsy (TBLC), which was proven to be an accurate and safe procedure for diagnosing smoking-related interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) [9], to better clarify this entity’s spectrum of clinical evolution.

Materials and methods

Design

We conducted a retrospective review of ILD patients diagnosed at the Oporto Medical School multidisciplinary team meeting (MDM) between 2014 and 2023 to collect patients with a SRIF diagnosis. The study was approved by the local ethics committee.

Patients

All patients had a prior evaluation that commonly includes a detailed medical history, physical examination, laboratory and pulmonary function tests, chest HRCT, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) total and differential cell counts and TBLC.

TBLC was performed in cases where less invasive evaluation was inconclusive or when clinical/radiological features raised suspicion for alternative or coexisting diagnoses. The decision to pursue an invasive diagnostic procedure was based on the need for histological confirmation to ensure an accurate diagnosis and management, considering the SRIF overlap with other ILDs.

The final diagnosis was settled on the MDM meeting that includes pulmonologists, radiologists and pathologists with experience on ILDs.

After the SRIF cases were collected, the patients’ electronic medical records were retrospectively reviewed to obtain the following information: demographic data, smoking status, pulmonary function test results, chest HRCT findings, and BAL and TBLC features at baseline. Evolution based on clinical, functional, and imaging follow-up data were also assessed (if applicable and available).

Severity of lung function impairment was classified according to the percentage of predicted values, as defined by the 2005 ATS/ERS criteria.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics were analysed via descriptive statistics. Continuous data were described as means, whereas categorical variables were reported as percentages of total subjects. The software used was SPSS Statistics® (version 30.0).

Results

Patient characteristics at diagnosis

Thirteen patients with a TBLC histologic diagnosis of SRIF were included (Table 1). They were predominantly male (76,9%), and the median age at diagnosis was 55 years (42–67 years). All patients had a smoking history; 10 (76,9%) were active smokers, and 3 (23,1%) were former smokers. The median smoking load was 44 pack-years (20–62 pack-years).

The most frequent presenting symptoms were cough, described in 7 (53,8%) patients; dyspnoea, in 4 (30,8%); wheezing, in 3 (23,1%); sputum, in 2 (15,4%); and asthenia, in 1 (7,7%). Five patients (38,5%) did not present any relevant respiratory or constitutional symptoms, and 6 (75,0%) had more than one symptom. Lung auscultation revealed reduced breath sounds in 2 (15,4%) patients and crackles at the bottom fields in 3 (23,1%) patients.

Five patients had relevant comorbidities: 3 with ischaemic heart disease, 3 with arterial hypertension, 2 with diabetes, 1 with severe obstructive sleep apnoea and 1 with bronchiectasis.

Radiological data

With respect to the radiological data, we were able to review the baseline HRCT images of 11 patients. For the other 2 patients, we only had access to the reports, so we decided to exclude them from the analysis to avoid interindividual interpretation differences between radiologists. Some relevant findings can be seen in Fig. 1. Concerning imaging features, centrilobular and paraseptal emphysema was the predominant finding, occurring in all patients (mild in 8/11–72,7% - patients and moderate in 3/11–27,3% - patients). Bilateral GGO were also present in almost all patients (9/11; 81,8%). Centrilobular nodularity of ground-glass attenuation (most likely representing respiratory bronchiolitis) was present in 3/11 (27,3%) patients. Four patients presented with cystic lesions, with one having bilateral upper-lobe predominant thick-walled lesions resembling Langerhans cell histiocytosis. Interestingly, only one patient had signs of fibrotic lung disease manifesting as lower lobe volume loss, ground‒glass opacities with superimposed reticulation and traction bronchiectasis and bronchiolectasis, in a pattern resembling nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP). No patient presented honeycombing.

Lung function

Regarding lung function, at the time of diagnosis, none of the patients presented airway obstruction according to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease criteria. One patient presented with a mild reduction in total lung capacity (71,3%). Ten (76,9%) patients presented with DLCO impairment, half with mild impairment and the other half with moderate impairment.

BAL

Concerning BAL total and differential cell counts, neutrophilia and eosinophilia were found in all but one patient. Lymphocytosis was also observed in two patients.

Histologic data

In what concerns histologic features, all patients presented with marked alveolar septal fibrosis characterized by relatively acellular and hyalinized collagen bundles with minimal inflammation. Intra-alveolar macrophages with yellow- brown finely granular pigment in the cytoplasm were also present in all patients. Emphysema was present in 6 (46,2%) patients. Treatment

No specific treatment was prescribed on a regular basis for any patient. All smokers were advised on cessation, with only one successfully quitting.

Evolution

The median follow-up period was 38 (4–101) months. Among the 13 patients included, 9 had HRCT monitoring available for review. One patient subsequently diagnosed with urothelial carcinoma associated with diffuse lung metastases was excluded from this analysis. Among the remaining patients, half showed stable imaging features and extension, whereas the other half demonstrated an increase in ground‒glass opacification (n = 3) and progression of reticulation and traction bronchiectasis (n = 1). As depicted in Table 2, revaluation spirometry and DLCO were available for 11 patients. Regarding diffusion capacity, 5 patients remained stable regardless of the persistence of daily smoking habits. One patient progress from normal values to mild impairment, two patients from mild to moderate impairment and one from moderate to severe impairment. This decline of DLCO was observed in both former and active smokers, with smoking loads ranging from 40 to 50 pack-years. With respect to spirometry, only one patient had a worsening of the FEV1/FVC ratio, from 83 to 64%, preserving the FEV1 and FVC values in the normal range. Only one patient had concomitant functional and imaging worsening.

Discussion

Smoking is a recognized risk factor for lung disease, most commonly associated with lung cancer and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease but it has also been implicated in multiple ILDs [10–15].

It has been described in the literature that a considerable number of smokers, enrolled in some studies due to other reasons rather than ILD, can present functional and radiological abnormalities compatible with interstitial changes, such as restriction in pulmonary function tests, GGO and reticular opacities on HRCT scans [16, 17]. Additionally, the topic about fibrosis and emphysema has been dubious [18]. Some authors state that it can occur in two ways: as a diffuse form, in which emphysema is mixed with the fibrosis of an interstitial pneumonia (most commonly usual interstitial pneumonia - UIP), or as a localized form, in which fibrosis is part of emphysema/RB or both [3, 5–7, 14, 18, 19]. Since these interstitial changes are common in smokers and are most frequently asymptomatic, it is possible that SRIF has been under- or misdiagnosed as other entities, such as desquamative interstitial pneumonia (DIP), fibrosing NSIP or UIP [20].

The small number of SRIF cases reported in the literature might be due to the different designations used to describe comparable features of localized emphysema and fibrotic lesions: RB-ILD with fibrosis, airspace enlargement with fibrosis (AEF) and RB with fibrosis (RBF); despite that, AEF is the only designation mentioned in the differential diagnosis section of smoking-related ILDs in the update of the international classification of idiopathic interstitial pneumonias [3, 5–7, 10]. The described pathologic findings of SRIF vary slightly according to authors, but all have in common the presence of RB (pigmented macrophages within respiratory bronchioles usually numerous) and fibrosis, which is hyalinized, with eosinophilic collagen deposition, contributing to a ropy appearance and thickening of the alveolar septa, most prominently subpleural, but it can also affect deeper areas of the lung parenchyma with or without emphysema. Fibroblastic foci and/or architectural distortion might also be present, but they are usually mild [1, 3–8]. Thin-walled cystic lesions can also be present [7]. Collagen deposition is the most differentiating feature, as it presents an uniform pattern, lack of temporal heterogeneity or major architectural distortion (contrary to UIP), pigmented macrophages and no cellular inflammatory infiltrate (contrary to fibrosing NSIP) as well as emphysema, which is not characteristic of RB-ILD or DIP [8, 20]. As a manifestation of the same process of lung injury, different diffuse smoking-related lung diseases present overlapping histologic and radiological patterns, emphasizing the need for a multidisciplinary approach [21].

All patients described in this series had clinical and radiological features, as well as histopathological patterns compatible with SRIF. A review of the available data revealed that our patients presented a different median age comparing with the other two available studies, younger than one and older than the second [1, 5]. This discordance can be due to the small number of cases reported and the probable SRIF underdiagnosis. As expected, the patients were smokers or former smokers and presented mild symptoms. Regarding lung function, the most prominent finding was a global impairment in DLCO, with a significant variation in evolution regardless of smoking status or load. The main HRCT findings were emphysema and GGO, both of which were present in almost all patients; four patients presented with cystic lesions, in one resembling Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis; and only one patient manifested signs of fibrotic lung disease - all radiological features described previously in the SRIF scenario [3, 5, 6].

Regarding lung function, the evolution of the disease showed some discrepancy, apparently without any relationship with smoking. In fact, only one patient ceased smoking which can be related with the overall symptoms being mild and the evolution largely stable.

Our findings indicate that, while the majority of patients with SRIF demonstrate clinical, functional and radiological stability over time, a notable subset experienced progression, particularly expressed in DLCO declining. The small sample size and retrospective design limits definitive conclusions regarding the overall SRIF’s natural history. Nonetheless, the potential for progression in some patients underscores the need for individualized follow-up and careful monitoring.

To date, no therapeutic intervention is recommended at diagnosis besides smoking cessation.

Although this case-series includes a greater number of SRIF patients than the previous data, the study has several limitations. In addition to its retrospective nature, the follow-up period is relatively short and variable. These factors restrict the generalizability of our findings regarding SRIF’s apparent indolent nature, highlighting the need for additional studies, including a larger number of cases, a prospective approach and bias control to better elucidate SRIF clinical behaviour in terms of symptoms and clinical deterioration.

This information would help clinicians to decide the best timing for imaging and lung function re-evaluation, as well as the eventual benefit of specific treatments.
