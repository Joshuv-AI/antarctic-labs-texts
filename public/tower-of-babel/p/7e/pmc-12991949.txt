Active surveillance reveals common SARS-CoV-2 infections among cats and dogs from households with human COVID-19 cases in Texas, USA
Authors: Alex Pauvolid-Corrêa, Edward Davila, Lisa D Auckland, Italo B Zecca, Rachel E Busselman, Wendy Tang, Christopher M Roundy, Mary Lea Killian, Mia K Torchetti, Melinda Jenkins-Moore, Suelee Robbe Austerman, Kristina Lantz, Katherine Mozingo, Rachel Tell, Ailam Lim, Yao Akpalu, Rebecca SB Fischer, Ria R Ghai, Casey Barton Behravesh, Francisco C Ferreira, Gabriel L Hamer, Sarah A Hamer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHouseholds where people have COVID-19 are high-risk environments for companion animals to become infected by SARS-CoV-2. We sampled 579 pets from 281 households with one or more laboratory-confirmed persons with COVID-19 in central Texas from June 2020 to May 2021. Nineteen out of 396 (4.8%) dogs and 21 out of 157 (13.4%) cats were positive for SARS-CoV-2 by RT-qPCR. Additionally, 95/382 (25%) dogs and 52/146 (36%) cats harbored SARS-CoV-2 neutralizing antibodies. Twenty-six companion animals of ten other species were negative. Overall, 164 (29%) pets were positive for SARS-CoV-2 by molecular and/or serological tests; a total of 110 (39%) out of 281 households had at least one animal with active or past SARS-CoV-2 infection. Cats were more likely to be infected by SARS-CoV-2 and had higher endpoint antibody titers than dogs. We detected 11 SARS-CoV-2 lineages and isolated five of them from dogs and cats, including the B.1.234 lineage that was isolated from a cat a few weeks after the first detection in humans in the United States. Detection of SARS-CoV-2 RNA decreased by 10% per additional day post COVID-19 diagnosis of the human case in the household for both dogs and cats, whereas seropositivity odds increased with each additional day. Human-pet interactions associated with a higher risk of infection in dogs and cats included sleeping in the same room as an infected person, living with multiple infected persons, and sharing food with people. The frequency of clinical signs in pets with active infections did not differ from uninfected ones, suggesting that not all reported signs are attributed to SARS-CoV-2 infection. Characterizing animal infections using active SARS-CoV-2 surveillance in pets at risk of infection may aid in One Health pandemic prevention, response, and management.Keywords: One health, Risk factor, Human-pet interaction, Viral isolation, B.1.234 lineage

Introduction

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2; Betacoronavirus pandemicum) is a virus species that spread globally, and has generated thousands of variants [1], [2]. While the main driver of Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) transmission is human-to-human, cases of SARS-CoV-2 infection in animals resulting from spillback from infected people have been reported in multiple countries [3]. SARS-CoV-2 has a broad range of susceptible mammalian host species, and animal infections are presumed to result from close contact between infected people and animals [4].

Pets living in households with active human infections are particularly at-risk because of their prolonged, close proximity and indoor relationships with humans [5], [6], [7]. Therefore, it is critical to understand risk factors associated with human-to-pet transmission to protect the health of companion animals. Additionally, understanding the consequences of SARS-CoV-2 exposure in pets is important to inform owners and veterinarians about the risks of infection.

In summer 2020, we initiated an active household surveillance study in east-central Texas in homes with people with COVID-19, documenting several infected pets, including animals shedding infectious virus [8]. We further showed that household pets are infected with the same SARS-CoV-2 strains that circulate in humans with the first report of the Alpha variant in companion animals in the United States [9]. Here, we build upon this framework with the complete results of our study that spanned over a year and sampled 579 pets. Among the objectives were characterizing the exposure and clinical presentations of dogs and cats throughout the initial phases of the pandemic, as well as the risk factors of animal infections.

Materials and methods

Animal recruiting and sampling

We sampled companion animals in Texas, USA from June 2020 to May 2021, within 1.5 years of the pandemic onset. Briefly, households with COVID-19-positive individuals were identified by the Brazos County Health Department (BCHD) and pet ownership was disclosed during the case investigation after which pet owners were invited to participate. Owners completed a phone questionnaire (Supplementary Table S1) with animal information, including clinical signs of disease in pets within two weeks of human COVID-19 diagnosis. Pets were sampled at households as soon as convenient, 0–86 days after the self-reported date of the first COVID-19 diagnosis within each household. For confirmation, all self-reported dates have been cross-checked with BCHD data. Oral, nasal, rectal, and body (fur) swabs were collected in viral transport media (VTM) and stored at −80 °C. Initially, oral and nasal swabs were collected separately, but inserted into the same tube with VTM (referred to as respiratory swab). This strategy used in the earlier stages of the pandemic aimed to reduce the quantity of reagents used in a period that testing human samples was priority worldwide. Later in this study, oral and nasal swabs were collected and tested separately. A subset of cats also had conjunctival swabs collected. Blood was collected and serum was stored at −80 °C.

Resampling occurred in some households and was triggered by the detection of at least one positive animal (see criteria for determining animal infection below) in the household during the initial sampling event in all cases but in two households, and all pets in the house were sampled upon subsequent events. This project was approved by the Texas A&M University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and the Clinical Research Review Committee (2018–0460 CA). Written consent was obtained by pet owner prior to sampling.

Molecular testing

Swab samples in VTM were tested for SARS-CoV-2 viral RNA at TAMU and Wisconsin Veterinary Diagnostic Laboratory (WVDL). Viral RNA was purified using MagMAX CORE Nucleic Acid Purification Kit [8]. Samples were tested by RT-qPCR targeting SARS-CoV-2 genes, including RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp), envelope (E), nucleocapsid region 1 (N1) and/or nucleocapsid region 2 (N2) [10], [11], [12], [13]. From June–December 2020, all RT-qPCR-positive samples were sent to the National Veterinary Services Laboratories (NVSL) of the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) for confirmation following World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH) reporting procedures [14]. Starting in January 2021, only samples with Cycle thresholds (Ct) values <35 were sent to NVSL. This change was intended to monitor viral changes in strong sequencing candidates with lower SARS-CoV-2 reverse transcription qPCR (RT-qPCR) cycle threshold (Ct) values.

Viral isolation and plaque assay

Selected samples positive by RT-qPCR for at least two genes were subjected to virus isolation at TAMU and/or NVSL as described [8], [9]. Samples diluted 1:2 to 1:3 in cell culture medium were inoculated in Vero cell (ATCC CCL-81) and incubated at 37 °C for 4–7 days in a total of two to three blind passages. Samples presenting cytopathic effect (CPE) were confirmed SARS-CoV-2-positive by RT-qPCR and Sanger sequencing.

Whole genome sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

At NVSL, libraries for whole-genome sequencing were generated from RT-qPCR-positive samples prioritizing those with Ct < 30 using the Ion AmpliSeq Kit for Chef DL8 and Ion AmpliSeq SARS-CoV-2 Research Panel (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Libraries were sequenced on Ion S5 using the Ion 510™ & Ion 520™ & Ion 530™ Kits. Sequences were assembled using IRMA v. 0.6.7 and verified using DNAStar SeqMan NGen v. 14.

Virus neutralization

Serum samples were tested for specific neutralizing antibodies at NVSL by virus neutralization (VN) and/or SARS-CoV-2 Surrogate Virus Neutralization Test (sVNT; GenScript, N.J., USA). For VN, two-fold serially diluted sera from 1:8 to 1:2,048 were challenged in duplicate with 100 TCID50/mL of SARS-CoV-2 (2019-nCoV/USA-WA1/2020; ATCC NR-52281) in Vero 76 cells (ATCC CRL-1587) [8]. For sVNT, sera with percent inhibition values of at least 20% were regarded as positive [15]. Until November 10, 2020, sera were tested by both VN and sVNT in parallel. Subsequently, sera were screened by sVNT first, and only positive samples were tested by VN. Within the samples tested by both serological methods, only one out of 205 sVNT-negative sample was positive by VN (0.49%), demonstrating the reliability of our diagnostic strategy to test by VN only samples that were positive by sVNT.

Criteria for determining animal infection by SARS-CoV-2

Per NVSL guidelines, we defined as infected by SARS-CoV-2 those animals that had oral, nasal or respiratory swabs RT-qPCR-positive for ≥ 2 genes (Ct ≤ 40); and/or animals that were seropositive by VN. Animals with only body (fur) swabs testing positive, without serological evidence of exposure were considered negative.

Risk factors for SARS-CoV-2 infection

Risk factors associated with infection were categorized into four groups: sampling, household, animal, and human aspects. Sampling aspects included number of days between the human COVID-19 laboratory diagnosis and animal sampling. Household characteristics included number of pets per household and number of people with known COVID-19 in the household at the time of animal sampling. Animal aspects included age, sex, species, sterilization, travel history, and access to outdoors, assigning pets reported to spend 90% or more of their time indoors as ‘mostly/strictly indoors’ in the analysis. Human aspects included level of contact with pets, including whether people with COVID-19 shared a room, bed, or food with the pet; petted or kissed their pet; or if pets licked their owners during the self-isolation.

Statistical analysis

We compared VN endpoint titers between dogs and cats. Values not normally distributed (assessed using Shapiro-Wilk normality test) were compared using Wilcoxon signed-rank test, including the number of days between human diagnosis of COVID-19 and pet sampling. We compared the proportion of animals with owner-reported clinical signs (present/absent) between dogs and cats using χ2 test with Yates' correction. Comparisons were performed separately for RT-qPCR and VN serostatus.

We built logistic regression models to determine factors associated with the risk of dogs and cats being positive by either RT-qPCR or VN, using the first sampling event. The same approach was applied to the dataset combining dogs and cats, to assess risks for infection in pets (positivity by RT-qPCR and/or VN) overall. In this case, species was included as a risk factor to assess whether positivity likelihood varied between dogs and cats. For people-pet interaction factors, we used car [16] and detected high multicollinearity (> 0.85 and < −0.85). Therefore, we only included sleeping in the same room, sharing food, and petting as explanatory variables. We performed backward stepwise selection using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) to identify significant predictors using MASS [17].

To assess temporal variation in RT-qPCR and virus neutralization results, we modeled monthly counts using first sampling event with logistic regression with a logit link and quasi-binomial variance. Models were adjusted for the mean days from human COVID-19 diagnosis to pet sampling within each species–month. We report odds ratios (OR) per one-month increase with Wald 95% confidence intervals (CI) and two-sided P-values. Coefficients from the final models were used to calculate adjusted odds ratios and 95% CI. We performed all analyses using R 4.2.2 [18].

Results

General results

Between June 13, 2020, and May 18, 2021, we contacted 978 pet-owning individuals who were confirmed COVID-19 cases, resulting in successful sampling of 579 companion animals from 281 households in ten counties in Texas. This included 396 (68%) dogs, 157 (27%) cats, and 26 (4.5%) individuals of ten other species: guinea pigs (6), horses (5), rabbits (4), ferrets (2), rats (2), alpaca (2), a hamster, a hedgehog, bearded dragons (2), and a parrot. A total of 180 households had dogs only, 53 had cats only, 47 had both dogs and cats and one household had two horses. Median number of pets per household was two, ranging from one to 13. Mean number of days between human COVID-19 diagnosis and animal sampling was similar between dogs (12.1; SD = 12.0; 95% CI: 10.9–13.3) and cats (11.1; SD = 10.1; 95% CI: 9.56–12.7; P = 0.67).

A total of 49 animals had swabs test positive for SARS-CoV-2 RNA in at least one sampling event (Supplementary Table S2). From those, 19 out of 396 dogs (4.8%) and 21 out of 157 cats (13.4%) had respiratory and or/rectal swabs test positive and were considered infected. The six dogs and 3 cats positive for body swabs only were seronegative and not considered infected. When comparing swab types collected simultaneously, nasal swabs were most frequently positive (n = 29 animals), followed by body (n = 25), oral (n = 20), rectal (n = 7) and conjunctival (n = 2).

Neutralizing antibodies were detected in 24.9% (95/382) of dogs and 35.6% (52/146) of cats. Endpoint antibody titers were higher in cats (mean = 182.14, range: 8–2048) than dogs (mean = 34.8, range: 8–128; P < 0.001, Fig. 1).

Initial sampling of pets occurred 0–86 days after initial COVID-19 diagnosis in human household members (median = 8 days). The median days after human diagnosis for RT-qPCR-positive dogs was 7 (SD = 3.37; 95% CI: 4.74–7.99) and for RT-qPCR-positive cats was 6 (SD = 4.33; 95% CI: 5.32–9.25). Among seropositive pets, median days was 11 (SD = 14.1; 95% CI: 12.6–18.6) for dogs and 13 (SD = 13.4; 95% CI: 11.4–19.6) for cats after human diagnosis of COVID-19. These values did not differ between species (P > 0.05). No pet tested positive by RT-qPCR when sampled ≥ 18 days after human COVID-19 diagnosis; seropositivity was detected up to 86 days after human diagnosis, which was the longest interval in our study. Interestingly, three of four dogs tested from the same household sampled on day 0 were seropositive, suggesting previous exposure to SARS-CoV-2. None of the other 10 species tested positive by RT-qPCR nor by VN. At least one pet tested positive by RT-qPCR and/or VN in 110 of 281 (39.1%) households across six counties.

Monthly RT-qPCR positivity did not vary across the sampling period in cats (OR per month = 0.96, P = 0.62) or dogs (OR per month = 0.89, P = 0.14; Supplementary Fig. S1). However, seropositivity increased over time in dogs (OR per month = 1.18, P < 0.001), but not cats (OR per month = 1.10, P = 0.16; Supplementary Fig. S2).

Longitudinal sampling (2–8 events) included 28 dogs, 30 cats, and two horses from 28 households. Of those, eight dogs and 14 cats were RT-qPCR-positive, and 17 dogs and 27 cats had neutralizing antibodies in at least one sampling event (Supplementary Fig. S3). RT-qPCR-positivity was detected during the first sampling event, with two cats also testing positive in the second event 11 and 14 days apart. Ct values in the second sampling were higher than those from the first sampling, consistent with the natural course of a single infection in these cats. Eight dogs and 18 cats were positive at first sampling; seroconversion occurred during the second sampling event for nine dogs and nine cats. Three cats and 10 dogs remained seronegative despite being resampled and living in the same house with at least one other positive animal.

Factors associated with SARS-CoV-2 infection in dogs and cats

Our regression models showed that dogs had lower odds of RT-qPCR positivity (OR = 0.30, CI: 0.15–0.60, P < 0.001), seropositivity (OR = 0.57, CI: 0.34–0.95, P = 0.03) and infection (OR = 0.58, CI: 0.36–0.92, P = 0.02; Table 1) when compared to cats. Overall, living in households with more than one person with COVID-19 and sharing food with their owners and older ages increased likelihood of infection for pets (Table 1).

Detection of SARS-CoV-2 RNA decreased by 10% per additional day post COVID-19 diagnosis in both dogs (OR = 0.90, CI: 0.8–0.98, P = 0.032; Table 2) and cats (OR = 0.90, CI: 0.8–0.98, P = 0.023; Table 3). Conversely, seropositivity odds increased with each additional day for dogs (2%; OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1–1.04, P = 0.027) and cats (5%; OR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1–1.1, P = 0.035). Older dogs had a 13% increase in odds of testing positive by RT-qPCR per year (OR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1–1.27, P = 0.036). People sharing food with their pets increased seropositivity odds by more than five-fold for cats (OR = 5.40, 95% CI: 1.72–19.3, P = 0.005) and two-fold for dogs (OR = 2.06, 95% CI: 1.12–3.73, P = 0.018). Clinical signs of disease in pets

The proportion of owner-reported clinical signs did not differ between RT-qPCR-positive (31.6%; 6/19) and negative (18.9%; 71/375) dogs, and between RT-qPCR-positive (23.8%; 5/21) and negative (16.5%; 22/133) cats (χ2; P > 0.05). The proportion of clinical signs did not differ between seropositive (28.4%; 21/74) and seronegative (20.6%; 63/306) dogs, and between seropositive (36%; 9/25) and seronegative (27.1%; 32/118) cats. Additionally, of the pets reported to display respiratory, gastric or behavioral clinical signs, less than 50% of them were infected by SARS-CoV-2 (Fig. 2).

Virus isolation and whole genome sequencing

From 19 samples submitted for virus isolation (Supplementary Table S3), we isolated SARS-CoV-2 from six pets (Supplementary Table S4). Genome sequencing from 8 dogs and 16 cats revealed lineages Alpha (B.1.1.7), B.1.234, B.1.1, B.1, and B.1.609 (Supplementary Table S5).

Discussion

During the present investigation, the United States experienced its major peak of COVID-19 deaths, reaching over 3280 deaths in 7-day average between December 2020 and February 2021 [19]. The results described here show that active surveillance of pets in high-risk environments can provide key information on the frequency and timing of animal infections in relation to human infections. We confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infections in roughly one in every four companion animals (164 of 579; 28.8%) living with at least one person with COVID-19 from June 2020 to May 2021 in East-Central Texas, USA. Monthly seropositivity in dogs increased over the course of our study, showing the importance of long-term SARS-CoV-2 studies in pets. Overall, 110 of 281 households (39%) had pets infected with SARS-CoV-2. The surveillance described here contributed to place Texas as the state with the highest estimated number of confirmed cases of SARS-CoV-2 infection in pets in the USA [20]. The high prevalence for SARS-CoV-2 RNA and neutralizing antibodies in cats (35.7%) during the first stages of the COVID-19 pandemic in Texas corroborates previous studies in multiple countries [21], [22], [23], [24].

Only dogs and cats tested positive among 12 species investigated, but the representation of other species in our study was low. Infections in dogs and cats have been reported worldwide [24], [25], [26], making our data on 26 individuals of 10 non-canine/feline species relevant to understanding natural exposure of animals to SARS-CoV-2. As of 30 June 2023, 775 outbreaks caused by SARS-CoV-2 in 29 animal species have been reported to the World Organization of Animal Health [3]. Among pets, most outbreaks have been reported in dogs and cats, although there are reports of seropositivity in horses and rabbits naturally exposed to SARS-CoV-2 [27], [28], and among the hypotheses are reduced contact with humans and smaller numbers of individuals from these other species tested worldwide.

The odds of detecting SARS-CoV-2 RNA in dogs and cats declined over time following human COVID-19 diagnosis. Rapid active surveillance sampling of animals following human diagnosis likely resulted in the high frequency of active animal infections, for which the median days of SARS-CoV-2 detection by RT-qPCR was 7 days for dogs and 6 days for cats. This is aligned with median detection times reported in passive surveillance [29], further supporting the importance of early sampling to maximize detection likelihood.

In the present study, positive body swabs associated with negative respiratory and rectal swabs and absence of specific antibodies were found in six dogs and three cats. These results suggest pets were exposed to environmental contamination by SARS-CoV-2 without becoming infected.

Seroprevalence within households increased with the number of days since human diagnosis for both cats and dogs. VN represents the gold standard for assessing antibody-mediated protection in naturally infected and vaccinated individuals for SARS-CoV-2 [30]. The use of VN is also crucial for a reliable investigation of the diverse group of coronaviruses, reducing the chance of misclassification of an animal as seropositive.

We identified several pet-related and human-pet interaction factors related to infection risk. Sharing food with infected persons was a risk factor for seropositivity among dogs and cats. Dogs that slept in the same room as infected people had higher seropositivity. In contrast, Bienzle et al., [5] observed increased seropositivity in cats sharing owner's bed, but not dogs. Cats were just as likely to be infected regardless of whether there was very close contact with humans or not, possibly because of differences in biological susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 [31]. Overall, our results support the notion that people with COVID-19 should also isolate from animals to reduce the risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection in pets.

Living in households with two of more people with COVID-19 has been described as a risk factor for SARS-CoV-2 infections in pets [6], and we found similar results when considering overall infections in dogs and cats combined. In our study, seroprevalence among dogs living in households with multiple people with COVID-19 was higher than among dogs from households with a single person with COVID-19. However, this factor did not influence SARS-CoV-2 RNA detection in dogs, nor was it associated with higher odds of infection for cats. These findings may reflect higher susceptibility of cats, which could result in similar exposure regardless of the number of infected people [32].

Clinical signs were not more frequent among infected than uninfected animals. Reported clinical signs could relate to background illness (not related to SARS-CoV-2) or to a closer level of observation by owners of their pets during their time of self-isolation. A serological study assessing 1000 pets across the United States also found that exposure to SARS-CoV-2 was not associated with clinical signs of disease [33]. Experimental infection studies demonstrated that infected cats do not develop clear clinical signs [32], [34] and that the respiratory tract of cats is more severely affected by SARS-CoV-2 than that of dogs [35]. Field investigations have found evidence of illness in companion animals naturally infected with SARS-CoV-2 worldwide [9], [22], [24], [36], however the viral infection may not be the cause of illness in all cases.

Despite companion animal infections, confirmed cases of transmission from pets to humans remains rare [37], which is supported by genomic epidemiological data [38]. One confirmed case from a cat to a human in Thailand was reported in August 2021 [39]. The isolation of infectious SARS-CoV-2 from multiple pets in our study suggests potential infectivity. Pet-to-human and pet-to-pet transmission were not assessed here but cannot be fully ruled out.

We isolated multiple lineages of SARS-CoV-2 from respiratory swabs of six animals, including Alpha, B.1.234, B.1.1, B.1, and B.1.609, indicating potential capacity to act as sources of infection for their contacts. Isolation of SARS-CoV-2 from companion animals has not been reported frequently, as most diagnoses are made by RT-qPCR or serological kits later confirmed in reference laboratories [40].

In the present study, lineage B.1.234 was isolated from cat TAMU-013 on June 28th, 2020, just 25 days after the first detection of B.1.234 in humans in the country, and only 11 days after its first detection in Texas [41]. This finding highlights the timely nature of our study and the importance of a One Health approach during human outbreak responses. Lineage B.1 was isolated from a cat (TAMU-252) sampled six days after human diagnosis, and represented 34.4% of Texas sequences uploaded into GISAID considering samples collected up to October 15th, 2020 [42]. Lineage B.1.1 was isolated from cat TAMU-172 three days after human diagnosis. In Texas, this lineage represented 6.5% of sequences uploaded into GISAID for samples collected up to September 11th, 2020, suggesting a rarer lineage in this cat. Lineage B.1.609 was isolated from a dog (TAMU-392) that was sampled a week after human diagnosis in a household with two people with COVID-19 and five other companion animals, yet was the only positive pet in the household. Finally, we previously detailed the first detection of the Alpha variant B.1.1.7 in companion animals in the USA; this occurred in a pet dog and cat that lived together [9].

Among the limitations of our study are the non-testing of human household members to determine whether pet infections were acquired within households. However, previous studies [43], including one conducted by our group in Texas [44], showed that transmission usually happens within households between pets and people. Additionally, animals with initial negative results in households with other negative animals were not re-sampled, so we may have missed later infections and seroconversion.

Conclusion

Pet infections by SARS-CoV-2 were common in households where an infected human resided, affecting more than one quarter of dogs and cats. Over 39% of the 281 households sampled had at least one dog or cat exposed to SARS-CoV-2, which can be explained by close contact between people with COVID-19 and their pets during self-isolation. Cats were more likely to be infected than dogs. Given the short median interval (6–7 days) between COVID-19 diagnosis in humans and RT-qPCR-positive pet sampling, we suggest that future pet surveillance studies in such high-risk environments prioritize sample collection within a week of human diagnosis. Sharing food and sleeping in the same room with infected people were associated with increased risk in pets. Therefore, preventive measures including reduction of close contact between people with COVID-19 and pets should be taken. Despite reports of sick pets testing positive for SARS-CoV-2, our study shows that infected pets were not more likely to have clinical signs than uninfected pets. Therefore, passive surveillance pipelines that rely on testing only sick animals may be less useful than active surveillance. Pets serve as indicators of viral lineage circulation, highlighting their value in genomic surveillance. Active pet surveillance is a valuable One Health approach to address pandemic prevention, response, and management.
