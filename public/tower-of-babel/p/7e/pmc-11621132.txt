Disseminated Echovirus 11 infection in a newborn in the Province of Bolzano, Italy
Authors: Irene Bianconi, Laura Battisti, Alex Staffler, Elisabetta Giacobazzi, Elisa Masi, Elisabetta Incrocci, Marion Bellutti, Stefano Fontana, Paola Stefanelli, Gabriele Buttinelli, Elisabetta Pagani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeRecently, cases of serious illness in newborns infected with Echovirus 11 have been reported in Europe, including Italy. Here, we report the case of a newborn diagnosed with disseminated Echovirus 11 infection, which occurred in October 2023 in the Province of Bolzano, Italy.MethodsA molecular screening, by Real-Time RT-PCR, was employed to analyse the cerebrospinal fluid, blood and stool samples, and nasal swabs. The entire viral genome was sequenced using both Illumina and Nanopore technologies.ResultsThe patient was admitted to hospital due to fever. Molecular testing revealed the presence of enterovirus RNA. Typing confirmed the presence of Echovirus 11. The patient was initially treated with antibiotic therapy and, following the diagnosis of enterovirus infection, also with human immunoglobulins. Over the following days, the patient remained afebrile, with decreasing inflammation indices and in excellent general condition. Genomic and phylogenetic characterization suggested that the strain was similar to strains from severe cases reported in Europe.ConclusionsDespite the low overall risk for the neonatal population in Europe, recent cases of Echovirus 11 have highlighted the importance of surveillance and complete genome sequencing is fundamental to understanding the phylogenetic relationships of Echovirus 11 variants.Keywords: Echovirus 11, Newborn, WGS, Illumina, Nanopore, Phylogeny, Non-polio Enteroviruses

Introduction

Enteroviruses are primarily transmitted through the fecal-oral route and can cause a wide range of syndromes in humans [1, 2]. They are typically associated with mild and self-limiting diseases, such as common cold, hand-foot-and-mouth disease, and viral meningitis. However, in certain populations, such as neonates, especially those born prematurely, enterovirus infections can lead to severe clinical conditions with high mortality rates, ranging from 11.5 to 38.5%, with the highest incidence of mortality occurring in cases involving myocarditis [3, 4].

Echovirus 11, a virus belonging to Enterovirus B specie, has been identified as being particularly virulent in neonates [5–7]. Even if infections with Echovirus 11 are generally mild and self-limiting in healthy individuals, often manifesting as symptoms similar to those of common cold or gastrointestinal diseases, in certain populations, such as neonates, especially those born prematurely, the virus can lead to severe clinical conditions, including disseminated infections, myocarditis and encephalitis [8]. The severity of Echovirus 11 infections in neonates is ascribed to the immaturity of their immune systems and their limited ability to mount an effective immune response against the virus [5–7]. Additionally, factors such as low birth weight, prematurity, and underlying medical conditions can further increase the risk of severe illness.

Treatment for Echovirus 11 infections is primarily supportive, focusing on managing symptoms and providing care for complications as they arise [8]. There is currently no specific antiviral treatment available for Echovirus 11 and any other enterovirus infections. However, prevention measures, such as hand hygiene, proper sanitation, can help reduce the risk of infection.

In Europe, including Italy, there have been recent reports of severe cases of Echovirus 11 infections in neonates [5, 6]. These cases underscore the need for increased awareness and vigilance in monitoring and managing enterovirus infections, particularly in high-risk populations like neonates.

In this report, the case of a newborn, who was admitted to hospital in October 2023 for evaluation after developing a fever and subsequently diagnosed with disseminated Echovirus 11 infection, is described. Viral sequenced genome was compared with those reported from recent Echovirus 11 cases occurred in Italy and France.

Methods

A sample of cerebrospinal fluid underwent molecular screening using the BioFire FilmArray Meningitis/Encephalitis panel (BioMérieux, France). The presence of enterovirus RNA was determined via Real-Time PCR on cerebrospinal fluid, blood, and stool samples using the ENTEROVIRUS ELITe MGB kit (ELITechGroup, France). A molecular screening using the Allplex Respiratory Panel 1–4 (Seegene, South Korea) was performed on a nasal swab. Additionally, faecal samples were tested with the Allplex GI-Bacteria(I and II) Assay and Allplex GI-Virus Assay (Seegene, South Korea).

Virus typing by Sanger nucleotide sequencing of partial Viral Protein 1 (VP1) gene [9], was conducted at the National Reference Laboratory of the Istituto Superiore di Sanità.

Both Illumina sequencing and Nanopore technology approaches were employed for genomic analyses by the Laboratory of Microbiology and Virology in Bolzano, Italy, and the National Reference Laboratory of the Istituto Superiore di Sanità, respectively.

For Illumina sequencing, viral RNA extraction was conducted using the MagMAX Viral/Pathogen Nucleic Acid Isolation Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, US) on the KingFisher Flex instrument (ThermoFisher Scientific, US), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Viral RNA extraction was instead performed using the Magpure Virus DNA/RNA Purification Kit (Bigfish Bio-tech Co.) for the Nanopore method.

Illumina genomic libraries of the virus were prepared from stool samples with the RNA Prep with Enrichment kit (Illumina, US) coupled with the Respiratory Virus Oligo Panel v2 kit (Illumina, US); the libraries were sequenced on the MiSeq platform with the MiSeq Reagent Micro Kit v2 (300-cycles) (Illumina, US). The virus genome was reconstructed using the Megahit v1.2.9 program, available from the Galaxy platform (https://usegalaxy.eu/). Nanopore sequencing was conducted by initially amplifying near full-length enterovirus genomes [10], followed by library preparation using the Native Barcoding Kit (ONT, SQK-NBD114.24). The MinION Mk1C device and Flow Cell R9.4.1 (ONT, FLO-MIN114) were used for library sequencing. High accuracy basecalling was set up and performed directly on the Mk1C device. Fastq files were analyzed on the Galaxy platform (https://usegalaxy.eu/) using Minimap2 [11] and Ivar Consensus tools [12].

The genome sequence of the Echovirus 11 virus has been deposited in DDBJ/ENA/GenBank under the accession number PP279571.1.

Complete genome sequences of Echovirus 11 were retrieved from the NCBI GenBank [13]. A phylogenetic tree, based on the whole genome sequence obtained by Illumina sequencing, was generated using the MEGA11 software [14]. The phylogeny was constructed using the Neighbor-Joining and Kimura 2-parameter methods, bootstrap tests were conducted to assess the robustness of branches and the tree reliability. We constructed the phylogenetic tree using 49 complete genome sequences.

Results

In October 2023 an infant was admitted at the regional Hospital of Bolzano for evaluation after the onset of fever on the seventh day of life and otherwise good general conditions (Fig. 1).

Timeline of clinical and diagnostics events

Pregnancy had been uneventful with negative vaginal and rectal swabs for Group B streptococcal (GBS). At 39 + 6 weeks gestation the mother gave spontaneous home birth to a eutrophic healthy girl with a birth weight of 3080 gms. Amniotic fluid was clear. Subsequently, the mother and newborn were transported to the hospital for further observation and admitted to the mother and child ward. During the first postnatal days the baby adapted well, showed proper breastfeeding and weight gain and good overall conditions without clinical signs of infection. Therefore, on the 3rd day of life the neonate was discharged home.

The mother was in good clinical condition at the time of delivery, with no signs of infection. Post-discharge, she experienced a mild cold and subfebrile temperatures. On the third day at home, after the discharge, the newborn developed a fever of up to 38.4 °C. Upon admission to the Division of Pediatrics, the patient exhibited neutrophilic leukocytosis with a leukocyte count of 19,530/µl (neutrophils 83%, lymphocytes 14%, monocytes 2%). Additionally, there was a slight increase in C-reactive protein to 7.68 mg/dl, an elevated procalcitonin level of 4.22 ng/ml, and an elevated interleukin-6 level of 1093 pg/ml. In addition to the fever, the patient exhibited intermittent tachypnea and mild meteorism during hospital admission. Chest X-ray, abdominal ultrasound, and transfontanellar echocardiography were all normal. Despite an increase in B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) of 4088pg/ml, echocardiography and electrocardiogram (ECG) results excluded significant cardiac issues. ProBNP-values went back to normal after discharge. Blood and urine cultures were negative too. No laboratory signs of acute liver or kidney failure at any time.

Because of ongoing of fever and increased inflammatory markers, a lumbar puncture was performed. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed normal cell count, negative culture but reduced glucose levels. The neuropediatric evaluation revealed no clinical signs of meningoencephalitis.

However, molecular investigations detected the presence of enterovirus RNA in all biological samples analyzed, including CSF.

Blood was solely tested for enterovirus RNA. The CSF sample tested negative for the presence of cytomegalovirus (CMV), herpes simplex virus-1 (HSV-1), herpes simplex virus-2 (HSV-2), human herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6), human parechovirus, varicella-zoster virus (VZV), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), adenovirus (ADV), Escherichia coli (E. coli) K1, Haemophilus influenzae (H. influenzae), Listeria monocytogenes (L. monocytogenes), Neisseria meningitidis (N. meningitidis), Streptococcus agalactiae (S. agalactiae), Streptococcus pneumoniae (S. pneumoniae), and Cryptococcus neoformans/gattii (C. neoformans/gattii). The respiratory samples tested negative for the following pathogens: respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) types A and B, influenza types A and B, parainfluenza virus (PIV), human metapneumovirus (MPV), ADV, human coronaviruses, human rhinovirus (HRV), S. pneumoniae, Legionella pneumophila (L. pneumophila), H. influenzae, Mycoplasma pneumoniae (M pneumoniae), Chlamydia pneumoniae (C. pneumoniae), Bordetella pertussis (B. pertussis), and Bordetella parapertussis (B. parapertussis). Faeces tested negative for: astrovirus (ASV), sapovirus (SaV), rotavirus (ROV), norovirus (NoV), ADV, Aeromonas spp., Clostridium difficile (C. difficile), Campylobacter spp., pathogenic strains of E. coli, Shigella spp., Salmonella spp. Vibrio spp. and Yersinia enterocolitica (Y. enterocolitica).

Sanger sequencing, employed to analyze a segment of the gene coding for the capsid protein VP1 of the enterovirus, conclusively identified the causative agent behind the infection as Echovirus 11.

Treatment initially involved dual antibiotic therapy, and after the diagnosis of enterovirus infection, human immunoglobulin was also administered for five days. The patient’s condition improved over the following days, with a resolution of fever, decreasing inflammation markers, and overall excellent health. The infant presented stable vital parameters during the entire duration of hospitalization.

Discharge home was possible after a total of seven days of inpatient therapy (intravenous antibiotic therapy).

Whole genome (WGS) enterovirus typing was conducted by the Laboratory of Microbiology and Virology in Bolzano and by the National Reference Laboratory of the Istituto Superiore di Sanità and confirmed the presence of Echovirus 11. The comparison of the results obtained with the two alternative methods showed identical sequences, demonstrating that both technologies are suitable for genomic analysis of enterovirus. The sequences presented 2813x depth and 99% coverage for the Illumina method and 12124x depth and 94% coverage for the Nanopore method, respectively.

The genomic characterizations revealed that the complete genome of the Echovirus 11 strain detected in Bolzano shares up to 99% nucleotide sequence identity with the strains isolated in France between July 2022 and April 2023 [5], as well as in Italy in April 2023 [6]. In the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2), it clusters closely with these strains, which together form diverging lineage, including strains of Echovirus 11 that were associated with both severe neonatal infections non-severe neonatal, and viruses isolated from non-neonatal infections.

Phylogenetic relationships between the complete genomes of Echovirus 11

This clustering pattern suggests that the Echovirus 11 strains circulating in Europe probably share a common evolutionary history and were putative derived from a common ancestor. It also indicates that the Bolzano strain is closely related to those causing severe neonatal infections, despite the patient in this case not exhibiting severe symptoms, highlighting the complex relationship between viral genetics and clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

Despite the generally low risk posed by enteroviruses to neonatal population in Europe, recent cases of Echovirus 11 infections highlight the importance of surveillance for non-polio enteroviruses. Accurate and timely detection of these viruses is critical to avoid fatal outcomes or serious complications.

Complete genome sequencing is an essential tool in this regard. It not only helps in identifying specific strains of Echovirus 11 but also to establish their evolutionary relationships. This understanding is especially important given that Echovirus 11 variants can cause a broad spectrum of illness in newborns, ranging from severe, potentially life-threatening infections to milder, self-limiting cases. By identifying the genetic profile of these variants, researchers can better understand the factors that contribute to their virulence and transmission dynamics and design more effective preventive measures and therapeutic interventions.

Both Illumina and Nanopore sequencing methods are effective for identifying viral variants. Probe capture-based techniques, like the Illumina kit, exhibit robust tolerance to substantial target sequence variations, making them highly recommended for the analysis of viral variants. This approach is particularly beneficial for advancing our understanding of viral evolution and for applications in viral surveillance. Conversely, the Oxford Nanopore MinION system, which facilitates the sequencing of entire PCR products, is widely employed for enterovirus typing, offering precise discrimination between genotypes and sub-genotypes.
