Assessment of genotype-by-environment interactions for seed yield and yield-related traits in oilseed sunflower genotypes under normal and salinity stress conditions
Authors: Sanaz Khalifani, Reza Darvishzadeh, Nasrin Azad, Razgar Seyed Rahmani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextSunflower, as a significant source of edible oil worldwide, requires the development of stable and high-yield genotypes, particularly under salinity and adverse environmental conditions.ObjectivesThis research was conducted with the aim of assessing the stability of seed yield and yield-related traits in 61 sunflower genotypes under normal and salinity stress conditions across two cropping years (2015–2016).MethodsFor a more accurate assessment of genotype × environment interactions (GEI) in sunflower, robust statistical methods including mixed model analysis of variance, AMMI and GGE models, weighted average of absolute scores (WAASB), Multi-Trait Stability Index (MTSI), and parametric and non-parametric stability indices were used and addressed.ResultsMixed modeling ANOVA revealed the significant effect of environment, genotype, as well as GEI for seed yield and associated traits, demonstrating different responses of genotypes across environments. The outcomes of various statistical methods showed a high level of consistency. Genotypes 4, 6, 44, 22, and 59 demonstrated high stability and yield. The MTSI identified genotypes 4, 61, 25, 50, 55, 26, 32, 40, and 34 as the most desirable with maximum stability across various traits.ConclusionGenotype 4 (270-ENSAT) is introduced as a promising candidate for creating salt-resistant hybrid varieties.SignificanceDifferent stability assessment methods helped in the discovery of superior genotypes that can be used for developing salt-resistant and stable hybrids, thereby establish opportunity for exploiting salt-effected soils for crop production.Keywords: Adaptability Analysis, Multi-Environment Trials, Stability, Sunflower, Yield

Introduction

Sunflower (Helianthus annuus L.) is cultivated globally, primarily for oil extraction, and its high-fiber seed meal holds a special place in animal feeding [52]. Sunflower seeds are a rich source of nutrients and various phytochemicals including antioxidants, flavonoids, phenolic acids, procyanidins, phytosterols, amino acids, fiber, potassium, arginine, and unsaturated fatty acids [1]. Sunflower oil contains beneficial compounds such as linoleic acid, oleic acid, palmitic acid, stearic acid, omega-9, and omega-6, which have significant impacts on human health [8, 70].

Saline soils expanding at a rate of 10 percent per year, pose a serious threat to the adequate provision of food, globally, and it is predicted by 2050 more than 50 percent of cultivated lands will become saline [49, 57]. Salinity is a determining factor in crop productivity, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions. Salt accumulation in the soil limits water and nutrient uptake and causes osmotic stress, ionic toxicity, and nutrient imbalance [87]. These factors lead to metabolic disorders, reduced growth, and a significant decline in crop yields [87]. Sunflower is classified as a moderately salt-tolerant crop [31]. Reduced root proliferation and browning of root tips, leaf necrosis, reduced head diameter, delayed seed formation [30], reduced chlorophyll content [3, 19], decreased seed yield due to reduced pollen viability and stigma receptivity [55], decreased triacylglycerol (TAG) storage [30], reduced leaf area, dry matter, number of seeds per head, seed yield per plant, seed yield, and oil content are major morphological disorders in response to salinity stress in sunflower [40, 67]. Therefore, achieving high-yielding and stable genotypes is a primary objective of breeding programs. Climatic and ecological factors, as the main constituents of an environment, have positive or negative impacts on the quality and performance of genotypes. Consequently, developing stable genotypes that can grow under diverse environmental conditions is essential [33].

Genotypes do not always respond in the same manner to environmental conditions, a phenomenon known as genotype × environment (GE) interaction [34]. GE interaction appears in two types of multi-environment trials in plant breeding activities: (1) a change in the rank of genotypes across different environments, which is referred to as the severe type in multi-environment trials, and (2) a change in the absolute differences between genotypes without a change in ranking [12]. Yield, as an end product of the plant, is a complex quantitative trait that is highly affected by genotype-by-environment interaction [59]. Yield stability can be enhanced by combining agronomic traits that exhibit high stability [5].

Hühn [38] proposed various statistical models and approaches for analyzing and interpreting the GE interaction effects, which can be subdivided into two major groups: parametric and non-parametric approaches. In the non-parametric approach, based on trait mean values, the ranks of genotypes are estimated [65, 78]. In the examination and interpretation of genotype × environment interactions, these approaches can complement each other, leading to a coherent and logical picture of genotype-environment interactions [16]. Additionally, researchers use multivariate methods such as additive main effects and multiplicative interaction (AMMI) and genotype plus genotype-by-environment (GGE) biplot for effectively analyzing and interpreting the structure of multi-environment trial data in plant breeding programs [83]. Recently, a new approach called WAASB, which combines the AMMI and BLUP features, has been introduced by Olivoto et al. [60] that can facilitate identifying high-yield and quality as well as stable cultivars well-adapted to the region's climatic and ecological conditions. These cultivars can significantly increase production in unit area [33].

Some of the above-mentioned methods have been used to evaluate the GEI pattern and select stable genotypes in sunflower in terms of seed yield [7, 11, 13, 15, 32, 53, 54, 71, 77,80] however, there is limited information on selecting stable sunflower genotypes by simultaneously considering various traits. Therefore, the present study aims to analyze the G × E interaction in 61 sunflower genotypes based on seed yield and yield-related traits using a set of stability analysis methods under normal and saline stress conditions.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and experimental design

Sixty-one sunflower genotypes from an international collection (Table 1) were evaluated for yield and yield-related traits under both normal and 8 dS/m saline conditions over two growing seasons (2014–2015) (four environments). The experiment was conducted at the Department of Crop Production and Genetics, Urmia University, Nazloo, Iran (37°32’N, 45°5’E, 1313 m above sea level) in a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications. Seeds of each genotype were sown in 24 × 24 cm pots containing filed soil. Based on the initial soil salinity, 12 g of NaCl was dissolved in 500 ml of water and applied to each pot when plants reached the eight-leaf stage. Soil salinity in the pots was monitored throughout the experiment using an EC meter. Harvesting was carried out when the back of the sunflower head turned yellowish-brown. The measured traits included plant height (PH, centimeters), 100-seed weight (FSW), head diameter (HD), and grain yield per plant (GY).

Stability analyses

Parametric and non-parametric approaches

A combined analysis of variance was performed for yield and yield-related traits over four experimental environments. According to significance of genotype-environment interaction, genotypic stability was evaluated for grain yield using both parametric [Regression coefficient [25], Deviation mean square [22], Wricke's ecovalence [81], Shukla’s stability variance [75], Environmental coefficient of variation (S2) [27], mean–variance component [64], and Genotype × environment (GE) variance component [63]] and non-parametric [Mean absolute deviation (S1) and Variance or standard deviation (S2) [58], Sum of absolute deviations (S3), Sum of ranks squares for each genotype relative to the mean ranks (S6) [37], NP(i) statistic [76], and Kang’s yield stability [43]] methods.

Variance and AMMI analysis were performed using the agricolae package [56] in R software. GGE Biplot

The GGE biplot is particularly useful for simultaneously visualizing the stability and performance of genotypes. The 'average vs. stability' view, commonly known as Average Environment Coordination (AEC), aids in simplifying the assessment of genotypes based on their mean performance and stability across diverse environments. The horizontal axis of the AEC, indicated by an arrow and a small circle, passes through the origin and approximates genotype performance. The vertical axis, with two arrows and perpendicular to the AEC axis, represents the genotype × environment interaction and determines the stability of genotypes. The longer the projection of a genotype on this axis, the less stable it is.

After removing environmental effects in GGE biplot analysis, the first two principal components explained 54.97% and 30.16% of the total variability, respectively. Discussion

Sunflower is recognized as one of the most important oilseed crops, due to its high adaptability, suitability for agricultural mechanization, and low labor requirements [45]. As an environmental stress, salinity has multiple negative impacts on sunflower plants, notably reductions in yield and yield components. Various studies have reported decreases in seed yield, number of seeds per head, and oil content in saline conditions [17, 24, 26, 39, 40, 67]. Breeding high-yielding cultivars for saline conditions, like other quantitative traits, is affected by GEI. GEI can weaken the association between phenotypic and genotypic values [18]. Two strategies exist to mitigate the G × E interaction effects: (1) Partitioning diverse environments into smaller and more homogeneous environments to develop genotypes with specific adaptability to those environments, and (2) Identifying genotypes with general adaptability to diverse environments [22]. In the present study, the yield and stability of 61 oilseed sunflower genotypes were evaluated across non-saline (normal) and saline environments. Analysis of variance revealed significant GEI effects. This means the ranking of sunflower genotypes given studied traits varies depending on environmental conditions. These findings are per the previous studies that reported substantial GEI for seed yield in sunflowers [7, 9, 48, 50, 74, 85]. This highlights the need for comprehensive investigations into the nature and extent of GEI, which cannot be fully addressed using traditional analysis of variance [35]. Indeed, the observed variation in yield data necessitates further analysis to improve the efficiency of genotype selection. In other words, the GEI effect highlights the need to consider genotype adaptability alongside yield in breeding programs [73]. Because of being the environment as the main and primary source of variation in sunflower hybrids [29, 42, 77], G × E provokes the most significant challenges in sunflower hybrid breeding programs. This interaction results in yield variation among genotypes across different environments, thereby complicating the selection of suitable genotypes. To investigate GEI interaction, various statistical methods have been developed and proposed. While univariate statistics are simple to calculate and can straightforwardly interpret GEI interaction, they cannot reveal the complex patterns in GEI data [6]. Multivariate methods such as AMMI, GGE, and BLUP are introduced as powerful techniques for analyzing and detecting genotype-by-environment interaction patterns. These methods are suitable for the analysis of complex data from multi-environment trials and help researchers to identify stable genotypes. Previous studies have primarily focused on applying the AMMI and GGE methods to investigate GEI in sunflower, for example, the evaluation of sunflower genotypes in six locations because of oil content and fatty acid composition and the introduction of stable genotypes [80], assessment the stability and adaptability of 12 sunflower genotypes across five regions in Iran in view of seed yield [7], use of AMMI biplot to interpret genotype-environment interaction in 24 sunflower hybrids evaluated in 12 environments [42], use of GGE biplot for introducing stable and high-yielding hybrids in the experiments realized for evaluating 11 new hybrids beside four commercial hybrids in four environments [29], use of WAASB for stability analysis beside the evaluation of disease resistance of active sunflower germplasm against Plasmopara halstedii race 100 in four semi-arid locations in India [20]. In this study, we combined these methods with the BLUP method to achieve a more accurate assessment of genotype stability. The novelty of this study resides in the simultaneous application of several uni- and multi-variate methods for a thorough analysis of GEI data, aiming to optimize the selection of stable genotypes. As expected, the various statistical methods elucidated the different aspects of our dataset structure. These findings are consistent with previous reports on various crops [41], [79], [44], [36].

Genotypes 50, 59, 22, 24, 4, 6, 44, 31, 25, and 12 demonstrated high stability across various parametric and non-parametric methods. Genotypes 4, 6, 44, 22, and 59 exhibited high stability and yield. Genotype 4, demonstrating high yield and stability across all studied environments, is a promising candidate for salt-tolerant hybrid development. However, selecting high-yield and stable genotypes using parametric and non-parametric stability models remains challenging. Alternative approaches are required to identify genotypes capable of adaptation to diverse environments while maintaining high performance [79].

Considering the AMMI biplot, which illustrates the complex relationships between genotypes and environments, as well as the AMMI stability index that reflects the genotype's variability across various environments, the genotypes 24, 22, 50, 48, 40, 25, and 55 have been identified as a suitable source for genes associated with adaptability to a wide range of environmental conditions. Therefore, these genotypes are suitable parents for breeding yield stability. Similarly, the IPCA scores indicate the stability of genotypes, where an IPCA value close to zero reflects high stability. Consequently, genotypes 22 and 24, exhibiting IPCA values near zero, are classified as highly stable. The AMMI2 model effectively identifies high-yielding, stable genotypes and the correlation among environments [47]. In AMMI2 biplots, genotypes distant from the plot's center and near an environment-specific axis are considered environment-specific, typically yielding well in that environment [84].

AMMI2 biplot analysis revealed that certain genotypes exhibited superior adaptability to specific environmental conditions. Therefore, the proximity of genotypes 11 and 15 to the 'normal environment 2015' axis indicates their high yield and superior adaptation to that environment compared to the other three environments. Or being close to the saline environment on the 2015 axis of genotype 46 about yield and plant height, being close to the saline environment on the 2016 axis of genotypes 35, 30, and 58 about yield, genotype 30 about head diameter, and genotype 58 about plant height and yield, or being close to the normal environment on 2016 axis of genotypes 28, 44, 47, 38, 59, and 60 about seed yield and genotypes 44, 47, 59 and 60 about five-grain weight and genotypes 60 and 47 about plant height shows their good performance and adaptation to these environments. In other words, these findings highlight the superior adaptability of these genotypes to specific environmental conditions and their potential for use in breeding programs to develop cultivars with stable performance across diverse environments. Therefore, it can be concluded that the stability of grain yield can result from the stability of yield components. This finding is consistent with previous studies on other crops [5, 10]. determined that environmental conditions predominantly influence seed yield variability in sunflowers, indicating restricted adaptability and a preference for genotypes to excel in particular environments. The significant differences in the relative performance of genotypes across diverse environments attest to the presence of GEI and environmental heterogeneity. Based on mean yield and IPCA1 scores, genotypes with moderate yield exhibited higher stability than those with a high mean yield. This aligns with the findings of [2]. Furthermore, our results suggest that a genotype exhibiting high stability and performance for one trait may not necessarily show the same characteristics for others. This complex phenomenon can be attributed to the control of traits by different genes, variations in gene expression across genotypes, and the influence of variable environmental factors such as temperature and humidity [23]. GGE biplot analysis visually represents the relationships between genotypes and environments, effectively revealing genotype performance and stability [35]. The GGE biplot analysis indicated that genotypes 4, 46, 15, 54, 16, 7, and 47, located at the polygon vertices, exhibited high adaptability to the experimental environments they can be recommended for specific adaptability. In contrast, genotypes 50 and 55, located close to the origin, showed a lesser response to environmental variations and are more suitable for use in breeding programs aimed at developing genotypes with broad adaptability. The ideal experimental environment is characterized by the highest PC1 score, which enhances the ability to detect the main effects of genotypes. The salinity stress environments, in both years, exhibited the highest PC1 scores, indicating a greater capacity to differentiate between genotypes, thus presenting them as ideal settings for evaluating germplasm and identifying salt-resistant genotypes. Genotypes 4, 6, and 46 are identified as promising candidates for normal and salinity stress conditions in 2015 due to their superior performance. Notably, genotype 6 demonstrated remarkable adaptability to the saline environment in 2015. Given these findings, genotypes 4 and 6 are suggested as suitable parents for the development of new hybrids with high yield and wide adaptability across different environmental conditions. Previous studies have also confirmed the high potential of these genotypes for tolerance to environmental stresses, including salinity and phosphorus deficiency. Ahmadpour et al. [4] identified genotypes 4, 6, 46, and 59 with the highest single-plant yield under both normal and salinity stress conditions. In another study, Darvishzadeh et al. [14] identified genotype 4 as the most drought-tolerant. Furthermore, [68, 69] demonstrated that genotypes 4 and 59 exhibited higher yield and yield components under phosphorus deficiency stress conditions, with less reduction in quantitative and qualitative traits.

As highlighted by Yan [82], yield stability, while important, is not the sole criterion for genotype selection,it must be evaluated in conjunction with high mean yield to ensure optimal performance in breeding programs. The results of this study showed that genotypes 26, 22 and 44 possess a favorable combination of high yield and stability. However, for a more comprehensive selection of ideal genotypes, the Weighted Average of Absolute Scores from the Best Linear Unbiased Prediction (WAASB) model, which simultaneously considers both stability and yield, was used [28, 51, 65]. This method, proposed by Olivoto et al. [60], enables the concurrent selection for stability and yield by balancing the response variable (yield) with the WAASB stability score. Consequently, genotype 4 with a high WAASB index, had the highest grain yield and stability. Additionally, genotypes 6, 22, and 24 demonstrated high mean yields and acceptable stability levels. Genotypes 22 and 24 consistently demonstrated high stability and above-average yield across all statistical analyses, making them promising candidates for breeding programs focused on stable and broadly adaptable cultivars. Moreover, this study employed a MTSI to verify the simultaneous selection of stable genotypes based on GY and three key yield components: HD, FSW, and PH. These traits were selected based on previous findings by Khalifani et al. [46], which demonstrating their significant impact on sunflower grain yield. Based on the stability analysis of these traits, genotypes 4, 61, 25, 50, 55, 26, 32, 40, and 34, originating from various research centers, were identified as stable genotypes. Previous studies have shown that recommending cultivars based on both mean performance and the stability of multiple agronomic traits provides greater accuracy and reliability [61]. Consistent with this, our findings indicated that genotypes 4, 6, 22, and 24, due to combination of their desirable stability and high yield, are promising candidates for breeding programs aimed at developing high-performing, stable sunflower cultivars with broad adaptability. These findings underscore the necessity of multivariate approaches for selecting stable, high-yielding genotypes across various environments, which can enhance our understanding and interpretation of the complexities associated with genotype-by-environment interactions (GEI).

Conclusion

Combining numerical and graphical methods for assessing genotype stability allows for more accurate and reliable recommendations for selecting suitable genotypes for new cultivars. The results of this study indicated that although different stability methods vary in their computational details, they generally yield similar results. For instance, genotype 4 was consistently identified as a stable genotype with desirable performance across most methods and is thus recommended as a suitable parent in future breeding programs. Additionally, our findings indicate that Seed yield stability is closely linked to the stability of its component traits, reflecting the high genetic potential present within sunflower populations for enhancing multiple traits. Furthermore, genotypes selected based on the stability of multiple traits originated from different research institutions, highlighting the broad genetic diversity available for improving sunflower traits. The selected genotypes should be further evaluated under diverse environmental conditions, to validate these findings. Given the results of this study and previous research, genotype 4 is considered a promising candidate with high stability and desirable performance under various stress conditions (salinity, drought, and phosphorus deficiency). This genotype can be used as a parent in breeding programs to develop hybrid cultivars or as a foundation population to create genetic diversity. Overall, the results of this study demonstrate that combining multiple stability assessment methods and considering multiple agronomic traits is a powerful tool for selecting suitable genotypes for sunflower breeding programs. These genotypes can contribute to the development of cultivars with stable performance and broad adaptability.
