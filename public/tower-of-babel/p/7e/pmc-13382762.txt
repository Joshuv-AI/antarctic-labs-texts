Cyclic Mechanical Deformation of Denture PMMA Regulates Fungal Biofilm Virulence
Authors: Carolina Montoya, Ryan Yu-Sheng Chang, Dmitriy A. Dikin, Mike Sousanieh, Zhenting Xiang, Yuan Liu, Sumant Puri, Santiago Orrego
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cyclic mechanical deformation is a well-characterized phenomenon in polymeric biomaterials under physiological mastication loading, yet its role in regulating biological responses at material interfaces remains poorly understood. Here, we investigate whether cyclic mechanical loading of denture-base poly­(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) functions as a biomaterial-derived cue that regulates fungal biofilm behavior. This study establishes that mechanically induced Candida albicans virulence is strain dependent and extends to clinically relevant isolates, thereby improving the translational relevance of denture mechanobiology models. Biofilms of C. albicans strains with distinct filamentation capacities, including the laboratory reference strain SC5314, two clinical isolates (hyphae-defective (UR18) and hyperfilamentous (UR13)), and a hyphae-deficient (efg1ΔΔ mutant), were grown on polished and rough PMMA surfaces and subjected to physiologically relevant cyclic deformation. Results showed that cyclic loading significantly altered biofilm behavior in a Candida strain-dependent manner. Filament-competent strains exhibited increased viability, extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) production, hyphal formation, and protease secretion, with the hyperfilamentous clinical isolate showing the strongest mechanosensitive virulence response. Filament-incompetent strains still retained robust protease secretion triggered by cyclic mechanical loading, revealing that toxic enzyme production can be mechanically activated independently of hyphal growth. While surface roughness modulated response magnitude, cyclic deformation alone was sufficient to activate virulence even on polished/smooth PMMA surfaces. These findings identify cyclic mechanical deformation as a biomaterial parameter governing the biofilm behavior and virulence of clinical and laboratory fungal strains and highlight the importance of incorporating mechanical loading into the design and evaluation of polymeric biomaterials.

Introduction

Denture stomatitis (DS) is a common oral disorder affecting up to 70% of denture wearers globally. , It is characterized by inflammation and erythema of the oral mucosa underneath the denture. , The etiology of DS is multifactorial and has been associated with both local and systemic contributors. ,, Prolonged device usage, especially at night, xerostomia, poor device fitting and hygiene, and conditions such as diabetes, smoking, or immunosuppression can contribute to the progression of DS. , Fungal infections caused by Candida spp. are frequently implicated in DS due to the microbial ability to adhere to the denture, form pathogenic biofilms, and penetrate palate epithelial layers to cause infection. ,, Specifically, Candida albicans is an opportunistic fungus and is the most frequently isolated yeast in patients with DS. It is also listed in the World Health Organization fungal pathogen critical priority group. DS is one of several factors that may lead to denture replacement, along with reduced retention or stability, denture base fracture, wear or loss of prosthetic teeth, aesthetic concerns, and progressive material deterioration. ,, If left untreated, DS not only compromises oral comfort and prosthesis function but also increases the risk of systemic complications in vulnerable patients, underscoring its clinical significance.

C. albicans is a common opportunistic yeast that can become a serious pathogen. Its virulence arises from multiple traits, including strong adhesion to host tissues and biomaterial surfaces, robust biofilm formation, yeast-to-hyphae morphogenesis that enables tissue invasion, and production of hydrolytic enzymes that degrade host barriers and promote immune evasion. , These virulent processes can be triggered by environmental cues such as nutrient availability, oxygen levels, and pH. − For instance, hypoxia and alkaline pH atmospheres promote filamentation, enhancing mucosal invasion, immune evasion, and persistent inflammation. At the molecular level, virulence is coordinated by transcriptional networks involving regulators such as EFG1, CPH1, and TEC1. These pathways drive virulence, expressed through morphogenesis and biofilm development, which amplify tissue damage and mucosal inflammation. Altogether, these environmental signals converge on core transcriptional programs that amplify C. albicans adhesion, morphogenesis, and hydrolytic enzyme production, thereby increasing the aggressiveness and tissue-destructive potential of infection.

Denture material surface properties further modulate C. albicans pathogenicity at the biomaterial interface. Prior work demonstrates that surface roughness, stiffness, surface energy, and wettability can each influence early adhesion, biofilm maturation, morphogenetic switching, and secretion of tissue-damaging factors. ,− For example, rougher denture surfaces support greater microbial adhesion, biofilm formation, and overall virulence that renders more aggressive biofilms. Importantly, denture materials also experience cyclic deformation during mastication, introducing a mechanical cue that is rarely incorporated into conventional static biofilm assays. Dentures experience hundreds of thousands of masticatory loading cycles per year during clinical service. This repetitive deformation is an unavoidable in-service condition that may heighten biofilm aggressiveness at the denture–mucosa interface and thereby exacerbate DS. In fact, our recent studies showed that cyclic mechanical stress applied to dental biomaterials increases pathogen virulence, supporting those cyclic forces as an external mechanobiological trigger of adherent biofilms. , However, because this loading-induced virulence response is a relatively new paradigm, it remains unclear whether it generalizes beyond the laboratory reference strain to clinical, patient-derived isolates with broader phenotypic and genotypic diversity. Clinical C. albicans isolates are known to display substantial genetic and phenotypic heterogeneity, including differences in stress tolerance, hyphal formation, and biofilm development, which may influence their response to environmental and mechanical cues. − Therefore, evaluating multiple clinically relevant strains is necessary to determine whether mechanobiological responses observed in laboratory strains are conserved across diverse Candida populations. Accordingly, this study was designed to determine whether cyclic loading-induced virulence is strain-dependent and whether its effects are associated with filamentation capacity across rough and polished denture-base PMMA surfaces, thereby increasing the clinical relevance and translational significance of this novel mechanobiological response. Resolving these questions is necessary to define conserved, actionable design principles for denture materials that mitigate biofilm-driven disease.

In this study, we test whether cyclic deformation of denture-base PMMA triggers virulence across C. albicans strains spanning filamentation-competent, filamentation-impaired, and filamentation-deficient (pathway-inactivated) phenotypes. This strain-diverse design determines whether loading-induced virulence is clinically relevant rather than SC5314-specific and helps explain morphogenesis-dependent versus -independent mechanobiological pathways. Biofilms of SC5314, two clinical isolates representing hyperfilamentous and hyphae-defective phenotypes, and an efg1ΔΔ mutant were grown on saliva-conditioned PMMA with controlled surface finishes (polished versus rough) and subjected to denture-relevant cyclic loading. We quantify virulence-associated end points to define how strain background and surface state shape loading responses, establishing a mechanobiology-informed framework to translate denture “in-service” deformation into actionable material design parameters that limit pathogenic biofilm formation.

Materials and Methods

To evaluate the impact of cyclic loading on the pathogenicity of C. albicans clinical isolates, single-species biofilms were grown on 3D-printed poly­(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) specimens with two surface conditions (polished vs rough). During incubation, the PMMA beams were subjected to cyclic loading to mimic typical masticatory forces. Biofilms were then analyzed using microbiological, microscopic, and molecular approaches to assess strain-specific changes in virulence.

Fabrication and Characterization of PMMA Samples

Rectangular beams (20 × 1.5 × 2 mm3) were additively manufactured using a 3D-printable PMMA denture-base resin (Lucitone Digital Print Denture System, Dentsply Sirona). Beam designs were generated in 3D modeling software (Meshmixer, Autodesk Inc.) and printed at room temperature using an M3 carbon printer with a 50 μm slice thickness for maximum resolution. After printing, the samples were rinsed with 99% isopropyl alcohol to remove excess resin and then cured for 90 min at 27 °C in a curing unit (Digital Cure, Dentsply), followed by a 30 min boil at 100 °C, to ensure complete polymerization. One group of samples was polished sequentially with 400 to 2,000 grit paper until achieving a surface average roughness Ra < 0.2 μm (“Polished” group). The second group of samples was tested immediately after curing (“Rough” group). Physicochemical and mechanical properties of the PMMA specimens were characterized, including chemical composition (see Supporting Information 1, SI-1), morphology (SI-2), surface roughness profile (Ra, Rq, Ry) (SI-3), water contact angle (SI-4), and flexural mechanical properties (SI-5).

Cyclic Loading Applied to PMMA

In the biofilm model, beams were subjected to cyclic (repetitive) loading, which induced a surface biomaterial deformation (ε̃ x ) mimicking mastication patterns. The cyclic deformation was quantified using strain gauges (2 mm grid, BA200–2AA-A, Zemic Europe) bonded to PMMA beams in a three-point bending configuration. One gauge was placed at the midspan on the tension side to record maximum surface deformation (ε x(max)), while a second gauge was bonded to the compression side at the region of interest (ROI) where biofilms were assessed, providing the average surface deformation (ε̃ x ) (Figure d). Strain gauges were connected in a Wheatstone bridge (5 V excitation), and output voltages were recorded with a data acquisition system to calculate the experimental cyclic deformation. Beams were cyclically loaded in sinusoidal mode (P min = 0.5 to P max = 2 N at 2 Hz) using a fatigue testing machine (ElectroForce 5500, TA Instruments, 200 N loadcell). The load range was selected to approximate deformations observed during average mastication in denture wearers (ε̃ x = 0.2%). To validate measurements, the theoretical mechanical surface deformation was calculated using the Euler–Bernoulli beam equation ( εx=3PL2bh2E ), where P is the applied load, L the beam span, b the beam width, h the beam height, and E the Young’s modulus). A comparison between the theoretical and experimental evaluations was conducted.

Biofilm Model

Salivary Pellicle Formation

Unstimulated saliva (5–10 mL per donor) was collected from 15 healthy donors under 30 years of age (Temple University IRB-27791). Donors were screened to exclude systemic diseases, active carious lesions, and periodontal disease. Volunteers refrained from eating or drinking for at least 2 h prior to donation. The individual samples were pooled and centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was treated with dithiothreitol (2.5 mM final concentration; PRV3151, Promega) for 10 min, then mixed 1:1 with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). This saliva/PBS mixture was sterilized using a 0.2 μm membrane filter. The PMMA beams were sterilized by immersion in 70% ethanol for 15 min, followed by air drying under UV light in a biological safety cabinet. Finally, to form the saliva pellicle layer, the beams were incubated for 16 h in 1 mL of sterile saliva at 37 °C. This process rendered the beam’s surface coated with saliva pellicle.

Fungal Biofilm Culture

Four C. albicans strains were used as inocula: the laboratory reference strain SC5314, two clinical strains obtained from supragingival plaque (UR13, hyperfilamentous; UR18, hyphae-defective phenotype), , and a hyphae-deficient mutant (efg1ΔΔ). UR13 represents a highly invasive phenotype, whereas UR18 models a less filamentous (weakened version) but clinically relevant isolate. These isolates were kindly provided by Jin Xiao (University of Rochester) and have been previously characterized. The efg1ΔΔ mutant served as a nonfilamentous control to verify the role of triggering the EFG1 pathway in regulating filamentation and virulence. Together, these strains provided a spectrum of phenotypes for assessing the impact of cyclic loading on denture-associated C. albicans.

Microbial stocks were stored at −80 °C in tryptic soy broth containing 50% glycerol before use. To harvest a colony for biofilm culture, strains were cultured on yeast extract peptone dextrose (YPD) agar (10 g/L yeast extract, 20 g/L peptone, 20 g/L dextrose; DF0428, Thermo Fisher) at 30 °C for 24 h under aerobic conditions. Then, a single colony was inoculated into 10 mL of fresh YPD broth and incubated at 30 °C with continuous shaking at 200 rpm for 20 h. Before each experiment, the yeast structure of the cells was verified using optical microscopy. To prepare liquid culture, cells were centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 5 min, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in PBS to an OD600 of 1.0 (∼1 × 108 CFU/mL). The saliva-pellicle-coated beams were immersed in 2 mL of this calibrated liquid culture and incubated for 3 h to allow cell adhesion. After, the beams were gently washed with PBS (3×) to remove nonadherent cells and placed on the two pins of the bending fixture (span: 17 mm) with 2 mL of culture media. Beams were divided into two loading groups: a static control (no loading, ε̃ x = 0.0%) and a cyclic loaded group (ε̃ x = 0.2%) (Figure b). For the cyclic groups, specimens were subjected to continuous sinusoidal loading throughout the full 24 h biofilm incubation period at 2 Hz (P min = 0.5 N; P max = 2 N), representing average stresses experienced in dentures from masticatory loading. This beam surface deformation magnitude approximates those experienced by dentures under normal clinical mastication. To minimize morphogenetic variability induced by nutrients and isolate only the effects of cyclic loading on virulence, the SC5314 strain was cultured in yeast nitrogen base (YNB; 239210, Difco) with 2% glucose (G7021, Sigma-Aldrich) and incubated at 30 °C, conditions known to suppress hyphal growth under nitrogen- or carbon-limiting environments. , The clinical isolates strains (UR13, UR18) and hyphae-deficient mutant (efg1ΔΔ) were cultured in RPMI 1640 (R1383, Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 1.5% agar (BP1423, Fisher Scientific), 2% glucose (G8270, Sigma-Aldrich), and buffered with MOPS at pH 7.0 (M3183, Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C. After incubation, beams were gently rinsed with PBS (3×) to remove loosely attached cells, and the tension side and edges of the beams were carefully scraped to isolate the biofilm within the region of interest (Figure d). Biofilms were recovered by standardized mechanical scraping of the specimen surface. Because recovery from microtextured rough PMMA may not remove every cell retained within deeper surface irregularities, this procedure was used as a comparative and standardized collection method across all groups rather than as an absolute measure of total surface biomass. Microbiological experiments were conducted using two independent biological replicates, each analyzed with three technical replicates (six measurements per group per evaluation). Molecular analyses were performed using two independent biological replicates with two technical replicates each (four measurements per group per evaluation).

Evaluation of the Biofilm-Biomaterial Interactions

Microbiological Evaluations: Metabolic Activity and Cell Viability

To evaluate the fungal biofilm-biomaterial interactions after incubation, the metabolic activity and viability of the biofilms were assessed using alamarBlue and by counting the number of colony-forming units (CFU), respectively, following established protocols. For metabolic activity, the beams were submerged in fresh media containing 10% v/v of the alamarBlue reagent (Invitrogen A50100) and incubated for 6 h at the incubation temperature. Next, aliquots of this solution (100 μL) were transferred to a 96-well plate. Absorbance at 570 nm (A570 to detect oxidized alamarBlue) and at 600 nm (A600 to detect reduced alamarBlue) was measured to calculate the overall reduction of alamarBlue as instructed by the manufacturer. The calculated absorbance values were normalized to the surface area of the beam’s ROI. Cell viability was assessed by CFU after serial dilutions. Briefly, the beams were sonicated for 1 min at 40 kHz to fully detach the biofilm from the beam, then vortexed for 30 s in 1 mL PBS. Four 10-fold serial dilutions were prepared, plated onto YPD agar, and incubated at 30 °C for 24 h under aerobic conditions for all strains. Colonies were counted manually and normalized to the surface area of the beam’s ROI.

Enzymatic Activity Assay

C. albicans produces hydrolytic enzymes, including secreted aspartyl proteinases (SAPs), phospholipases (PLBs), and lipases, which facilitate host invasion by degrading structural proteins, promoting adhesion, nutrient acquisition, and immune evasion. , Enzymatic activity from biofilms was measured using the EnzChek Protease Assay Kit (Molecular Probes, E6638) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This assay quantifies overall proteolytic activity, serving as a measure of secreted enzymatic virulence from the biofilms. After incubation, the beams were placed in 200 μL of 1× digestion buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.8, 0.1 mM sodium azide), sonicated for 1 min at 40 kHz, and then vortexed for 30 s to detach the biofilm from the beams completely. Aliquots (50 μL) of the solution were mixed with 50 μL of a 10 μg/mL BODIPY FL casein solution and incubated in the dark for 1 h. Fluorescence was measured with a microplate reader at excitation/emission 485/528 nm wavelengths (BioTEK Synergy HTX).

Imaging of Biofilms

Fungal live cells and extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) of the biofilms were labeled with SYTO9 (S34854, Invitrogen) and Alexa Fluor 647-labeled dextran conjugate (D22914, Thermo Fisher), respectively, following established protocols. The EPS matrix was labeled with 1 μM of Alexa Fluor 647-labeled dextran conjugate after the adhesion period. After incubation, the fungal cells were stained with 2.5 μM SYTO9 and reincubated in a dark room for 20 min. The beams were then gently rinsed with filter-sterilized water to remove any excess dye. The stained biofilms were visualized using a fluorescence microscope (EVOS M5000). Images were collected in a 30 z-stack traversing the biofilms along their thickness. The color images were then converted to grayscale (unit 8, bitmap) to quantify the live cells (green image) and EPS (red image). A threshold was applied to obtain black-and-white images. The white pixels quantified above the threshold were counted as live cells/EPS. Thresholding and boundary selection were performed using identical parameters across conditions. To estimate the biofilm volume, the lower and upper boundaries of the biofilm within the z-stack were manually identified. Biofilm thickness was calculated by multiplying the number of slices within this range by the slice interval. Biofilm volume was calculated by quantifying the threshold-pixel area in each slice and integrating these values across the slices between the lower and upper boundaries (area × slice thickness), yielding a voxel-based estimate of biofilm volume (μm3). Image processing and analysis were performed using ImageJ. The yeast-to-hyphae transition was monitored under a light microscope (EVOS M5000) after 4 h of incubation. The hyphae formation (%) was calculated as the ratio of filamentous cells (i.e., hyphae and pseudohyphae) and the number of total cells. For each sample, five randomly selected microscopic fields within the ROI were analyzed. For each experimental condition six independent samples were evaluated. Image analysis was performed in a blinded manner to minimize observer bias.

Molecular Evaluations: RNA Extraction and Gene Expression Assays

After incubation, total RNA from the biofilms was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Bead beating and lysis were performed directly on the beams, using five cycles of 60 s at 6 m/s (MP Biomedicals FastPrep), with 2 min on ice between cycles to prevent overheating. RNA yield and quality were assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA synthesis was performed using the RT2 First Strand Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) was performed using a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) and the iTaq Universal SYBR Green supermix (Bio-Rad) with predesigned primer genes associated with cell adhesion (ALS3), proteolytic activity and biofilm-associated virulence (SAP4), and the yeast-to-hyphae transition (EFG1, UME6 and HWP1) (SI-6). These genes are key known regulators of C. albicans virulence. 18S rRNA was used as a housekeeping gene. RT-qPCR was conducted with an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 58 or 55 °C for 1 min, depending on the primer set. Data were analyzed using the ΔΔCt method. To evaluate the effects of surface roughness and cyclic loading within a consistent biofilm growth context, polished PMMA surfaces were used as the baseline control for comparison with roughened surfaces.

Statistical Analysis

For all evaluations data was expressed as the mean ± standard deviations. The assumption of normal distribution and homoscedasticity was assessed by Shapiro–Wilk’s and Levene’s tests. All microbiological and molecular data were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with surface roughness and cyclic loading as factors. Differences between surface finishes (rough vs polished) were evaluated using independent t-tests with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were completed using STATGRAPHICS Centurion XVII.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of the Different PMMA Groups

Figure summarizes the physical and mechanical characterization of the PMMA substrates used to decouple the effects of surface finish and cyclic deformation on biofilm behavior. Polished specimens exhibited feature-attenuated surfaces, whereas rough specimens retained the layer-line topography characteristic of additive manufacturing (Figure a). Stylus profilometry corroborated these observations (Figure c), with the rough condition showing a dominant peak–valley spacing on the order of ∼80 μm along the scan direction. Quantitative roughness metrics (Ra, Rq, and Ry) were markedly reduced in the polished group compared with the rough substrates (Table ), confirming separation of the two surface finishes. Static water contact angle was modestly higher for rough (∼65°) than polished (∼59°) surfaces, indicating slightly reduced wettability in the rough condition. During biofilm growth, specimens were subjected to cyclic deformation at ε̃ x = 0% (static) or ε̃ x = 0.2% (cyclic) a denture-relevant clinical strain magnitude applied at the region of interest (ROI) of the samples. Surface cyclic deformation was measured using strain gauges and agreed closely with theoretical predictions (deviation <5%; Figure d). For each experiment, loading amplitude was adjusted to maintain an average ROI strain of ε̃ x = 0.2% over the incubation period, enabling controlled evaluation of how surface finish and cyclic deformation jointly regulate fungal virulence outputs in subsequent analyses.

Cyclic Loading of PMMA Enhances C. albicans Viability and Metabolic Activity in a Strain-Dependent Manner

The effects of cyclic mechanical loading and surface roughness on C. albicans biofilm viability (CFU) and metabolic activity (alamarBlue reduction) were assessed on different isolates (laboratory and clinical isolates) and both PMMA surfaces subjected to cyclic loading (ε̃ x = 0.2%) or static (ε̃ x = 0%) conditions. For the laboratory reference strain SC5314, cyclic loading produced significant increases in both CFU counts and metabolic activity on both polished and rough PMMA surfaces (Figure a,e), indicating that repeated mechanical deformation on the biomaterial surface triggered increased yeast growth regardless of the PMMA surface finishing conditions. The responses of the hyperfilamentous clinical isolate UR13 were similar to the laboratory strain. Cyclic loading promoted increased yeast cells regardless of the surface roughness condition (Figure b). However, the metabolic activity response in this clinical strain was surface finish–dependent. Under cyclic loading, polished PMMA showed an increase in biofilm metabolic activity, whereas rough PMMA exhibited no appreciable change (Figure f). Across these two filamentation-competent strains (SC5314 and UR13), cyclic deformation increased viable yeast on both polished and rough PMMA, supporting repetitive loading as a dominant in-service cue that promotes colonization under dynamic denture conditions. For the hyphae-defective clinical isolate UR18, viable yeast cells remained similar across all loading and surface finish conditions, indicating no stimulation of yeast growth by mechanical loading (Figure c). The metabolic activity remained unchanged on rough PMMA under both loading conditions but significantly decreased on polished surfaces during cyclic loading (Figure g), suggesting that cyclic mechanical deformation suppressed metabolic activity on smoother substrates. The hyphae-deficient mutant (efg1ΔΔ) showed a similar response to UR18. A significant reduction in yeast viability and metabolic activity under cyclic loading on both rough and smooth surfaces was observed (Figure d,h), indicating that repetitive mechanical deformation impaired biofilm growth.

Together, these results show that cyclic mechanical loading of PMMA increases yeast viability and metabolic activity only in filament-competent strains (SC5314, UR13), while morphogenetically impaired isolates (UR18, efg1ΔΔ) fail to mount comparable growth or metabolic gains (Figure ). This pattern is biologically consistent because environmental virulence cues (e.g., nutrient shifts or hypoxia) typically enhance C. albicans biofilm fitness only when they engage the core hyphae/biofilm regulatory circuitry, particularly the EFG1-centered transcriptional network. , In contrast, filamentation-incompetent strains are classically biofilm-defective and often cannot translate external stimuli into expanded biofilm biomass or elevated metabolic output because loss of EFG1-mediated morphogenesis compromises adhesion, architecture development, and biofilm-associated metabolic adaptation. , Thus, cyclic deformation functions as a virulence cue that preferentially amplifies viability and metabolic activation when the filamentation/biofilm program is intact.

Cyclic Loading Enhances Live Biomass and EPS Matrix Production in Filament-Competent C. albicans Strains

The effects of cyclic mechanical deformation and surface roughness on biofilm structure, live cells, and EPS production were examined by fluorescence imaging for all C. albicans strains grown on rough and polished PMMA (Figure ). For the laboratory reference strain SC5314, cyclic loading significantly increased both live biomass and EPS accumulation on both polished and rough PMMA surfaces (Figure a,b), indicating that repetitive deformation of PMMA triggered the formation of thicker, matrix-rich biofilms. The hyperfilamentous clinical isolate UR13 exhibited the strongest response, with cyclic loading producing a marked rise in both live and EPS signals, specifically in the rough surfaces (Figure c,d), consistent with its enhanced filamentation and biofilm-forming capacity. In contrast, the hyphae-defective clinical isolate UR18 showed no significant changes in either live cells or EPS quantities under cyclic loading (Figure e,f), indicating weak mechanobiological responses. The hyphae-deficient mutant (efg1ΔΔ) showed low EPS and biomass levels in all testing conditions (Figure g,h), likely reflecting the loss of EFG1-mediated regulation of hyphal growth and matrix biosynthesis, which limits the strain’s ability to reinforce its biofilm architecture.

These findings indicate that cyclic loading promotes the development of thicker, matrix-enriched biofilms in filament-competent strains (SC5314, UR13), whereas UR18 and efg1ΔΔ (filament-incompetent) show little to no EPS or biomass amplification under the same mechanical cue (Figure ). This divergence is consistent with previous findings in which EPS accumulation and 3D architecture depend on functional hyphae/biofilm regulatory programs, particularly the EFG1-centered network and UME6-linked hyphal maintenance pathways, which coordinate matrix biogenesis. ,

Cyclic Loading Induces Proteolytic Activation Across C. albicans Strains, with Peak Activity in Hyphae-Incompetent Isolates

The effects of cyclic mechanical deformation on secreted proteolytic activity were evaluated for all C. albicans strains grown on polished and rough PMMA surfaces (Figure ). For the laboratory reference strain SC5314, cyclic loading (ε̃ x = 0.2%) increased proteolytic activity only on rough PMMA surfaces, while no difference was detected on polished surfaces (Figure a). The hyperfilamentous clinical isolate UR13 showed the opposite pattern, with an increase in proteolytic activity on polished PMMA subjected to cyclic loading and no significant change on rough surfaces regardless of the loading condition (Figure b). The hyphae-defective clinical isolate UR18 displayed significant increases in enzymatic activity on both polished and rough PMMA (Figure c), indicating that cyclic loading of PMMA induces protease secretion, a major virulence-associated response that persists even in strains with limited filamentation ability. Similarly, the hyphae-deficient mutant (efg1ΔΔ) exhibited a significant increase in proteolytic activity under cyclic loading (Figure d), demonstrating that mechanical deformation can trigger the upregulation of enzyme secretion even in the absence of hyphal formation. These findings indicate that cyclic loading promoted proteolytic activation across strains, but the direction and surface dependence were strain specific (Figure ). This overall pattern of enzyme secretion activated by mechanical loading is consistent with the broader concept that secreted proteases are a core C. albicans virulence output that is inducible by diverse environmental cues and is frequently heightened in biofilm-associated states, supporting nutrient acquisition, barrier disruption, and host modulation. , Notably, the persistence of strong protease induction in filament-incompetent strains (UR18, efg1ΔΔ) indicates that enzymatic virulence can remain biologically relevant even when filamentation-driven biomass/EPS amplification is weak, reinforcing the clinical concern that “low-filament” backgrounds may still express tissue-damaging potential under mechanical stress. , In addition, the tendency for UR13, UR18 and efg1ΔΔ to show higher induction of enzyme production on polished PMMA under cyclic loading suggests a potential trade-off between biomaterial architecture-driven retention and secreted offense. Rough topographies often favor physical retention/accumulation of biofilm mass, whereas smoother substrates may provide fewer stabilizing niches, prompting some strain backgrounds to compensate by increasing secreted proteolysis under cyclic loading. This hypothesis is consistent with clinical biomaterial literature noting greater biofilm retention on roughened surfaces, while emphasizing that smoothness alone may not be protective when the interface is mechanically dynamic.

Cyclic Loading Promotes Yeast-to-Hyphae Transition in Filament-Competent C. albicans Strains

The effect of cyclic mechanical deformation on C. albicans morphogenesis was examined by quantifying the proportion of hyphal cells formed on polished and rough PMMA (Figure ). For the laboratory reference strain SC5314, despite the hyphae-suppressive nutrient conditions, the application of cyclic loading stimulated the hyphal formation on both surface finishing types (Figure a,b), indicating that repetitive deformation of PMMA promotes filamentation and a transition toward a more invasive morphology. The hyperfilamentous clinical isolate UR13 exhibited extensive hyphal growth even under static (no loading) conditions, with further enhancement under cyclic loading (Figure c,d), particularly on rough surfaces, confirming its strong filamentation capacity and the amplification of this virulence-associated trait upon mechanical stimulation. The hyphae-defective clinical isolate UR18 displayed limited filamentation under static conditions but showed a slight but significant increase in hyphal formation under cyclic loading (Figure e). However, the induced filaments were short, sparsely branched, and morphologically underdeveloped (Figure f), indicating that mechanical stress partially activates, but does not fully induce, morphogenetic competence in this strain. In contrast, the hyphae-deficient mutant (efg1ΔΔ) remained locked in the yeast form regardless of surface or loading condition (Figure g,h), consistent with its genetic loss of the EFG1 transcriptional regulator required for hyphal development. These results indicate that the virulence activation triggered by cyclic loading is strongly linked to the EFG1-dependent filamentation pathway.

These findings show that cyclic mechanical loading acts as a potent signal that triggers C. albicans to induce or amplify yeast-to-hyphae transition in clinical and lab strains while leaving efg1ΔΔ expectedly unresponsive (Figure ), thereby suggesting the essential role of EFG1-mediated filamentation in mechanobiological adaptation on denture-base PMMA. This pattern is consistent with the current understanding that EFG1 is a central regulator of hyphal morphogenesis and biofilm development across diverse C. albicans genetic backgrounds, such that strains lacking EFG1-mediated circuitry cannot execute hyphal programs even when exposed to strong external stimuli. ,

Morphogenesis-Dependent Versus Enzyme-Dominant Mechanobiological Responses to Cyclic PMMA Deformation

Analysis across all our microbiological results revealed that cyclic deformation of denture-base PMMA triggered C. albicans virulence in a strain-specific manner. Filamentation-competent backgrounds (SC5314, UR13) converted loading into coordinated gains in viability/metabolic activity (Figure ), live biomass and EPS enrichment (Figure ), and hyphal amplification (Figure ). Filamentation-incompetent backgrounds (UR18, efg1ΔΔ) showed muted or negative responses yet retained strong protease induction (Figure ). Conversely, protease secretion represents a core virulence axis that can remain inducible even when morphogenesis-driven expansion is constrained, aligning with the central role of SAPs in pathogenesis and with clinical evidence linking biofilm proteinase activity/SAP expression to denture stomatitis severity. , Clinical surveys report that 86–97% of C. albicans isolates secrete high levels of tissue-damaging proteases, supporting our observation that enzymatic virulence is more prominent in patient- and niche-specific derived strains.

The UR13 (hyperfilamentous) showed the strongest virulence response under cyclic loading, with abundant EPS production, extensive hyphae, and high secretion of tissue-damaging proteases, aligning with prior reports that clinical strains often express stronger hydrolytic activity than SC5314. In contrast, the filamentation-incompetent strains showed reduced virulence levels. The UR18, an hyphae-defective isolate, displayed limited filamentation and biomass under cyclic loading, yet still secreted high levels of proteases, consistent with findings that C. albicans tooth isolates, maintain strong SAP activity that can persist despite impaired filamentation. Importantly, cyclic loading of denture substrate was still able to trigger measurable virulence responses in this muted clinical strain, suggesting that UR18 relies more on secreting proteases than on hyphal expansion to exert its pathogenic potential. Such strain-specific responses suggest that cyclic mechanical forces may amplify clinical virulence by enhancing SAP secretion, potentially worsening the severity of denture stomatitis. The efg1ΔΔ mutant showed the weakest virulence response under cyclic loading, with a significant decrease in cell viability, metabolism, and EPS production. However, application of cyclic loading was still able to trigger the secretion of proteases. These findings suggest that while EFG1-dependent filamentation is compromised in the filamentation-impaired strains, protease secretion may be preserved through filamentation-independent regulatory pathways, potentially including pH-responsive networks such as Rim101, which warrant direct investigation in future studies.

Cyclic Loading Differentially Regulates Virulence-Associated Genes in a Surface-Dependent Manner

To understand the cyclic loading-induced changes in C. albicans biofilm formation and virulence, the transcriptional response of C. albicans to cyclic mechanical deformation was evaluated for key genes involved in adhesion (ALS3), filamentation (EFG1, UME6), hyphal wall structure (HWP1), and proteolysis (SAP4) on polished and rough PMMA (Figure ). Overall, gene expression analysis revealed that cyclic mechanical loading produced distinct transcriptional profiles for adhesion, filamentation, hyphal wall structure and proteolysis pathways depending on strain and surface topography. Cyclic loading upregulated ALS3 in the filament-competent strains SC5314 and UR13 on both polished and rough PMMA (Figure a,b), indicating enhanced cell adhesion potential to PMMA surfaces under mechanical stimulation regardless of the surface finish. In contrast, ALS3 was significantly downregulated in the hyphae-defective UR18 on both surfaces finish conditions (Figure c), and in the efg1ΔΔ mutant, it decreased on rough PMMA but remained unchanged on polished surfaces (Figure d). Expression of EFG1 decreased on rough but increased on polished PMMA surfaces after the application of cyclic loading for both SC5314 and UR13 (Figure e,f). The UR18 isolate showed no change in the rough but an increase in the polished PMMA surface (Figure g). These results indicate that the activation of this master regulator depends on both surface finishing conditions and cyclic mechanical loading. In the efg1ΔΔ mutant, UME6 expression increased on rough but decreased on polished PMMA (Figure h), suggesting that downstream filamentation signals may still respond to mechanical stress in an EFG1-independent manner. Moreover, cyclic loading enhanced HWP1 expression on polished PMMA for SC5314, and on both surfaces for UR13 (Figure i,j), indicating the trigger of mechanically induced cell-wall remodeling for these two filament-competent strains. The hyphae-defective UR18 isolate showed increased expression only on rough PMMA, while the efg1ΔΔ mutant exhibited no significant changes for any condition (Figure k,l).

Activation of adhesion (ALS3) and hyphal-wall remodeling (HWP1) occurred primarily in filament-competent strains (SC5314, UR13), aligning with their strong increases in viability (Figure ), EPS production (Figure ), and hyphal formation (Figure ), which is consistent with activation of the filamentation program and sustained hyphal growth. , In contrast, ALS3 downregulation in the hyphae-defective UR18 and only weakly upregulated in efg1ΔΔ, indicates that ALS3 induction requires morphogenetic capacity. EFG1-UME6 showed a topography-dependent response: cyclic loading decreased EFG1 on rough PMMA but increased on polished surfaces in SC5314, UR13, and UR18, while UME6 was induced in efg1ΔΔ, highlighting EFG1’s role upstream of UME6 in driving hyphal initiation and maintenance. , These findings indicate that the filamentation network senses cyclic mechanical deformation, but its activation varies with both surface topography and fungal strain.

Cyclic loading strongly upregulated SAP4 in SC5314, UR13, and UR18 on both rough and polished PMMA (Figure m–p), consistent with the increased protease activity shown in Figure . In contrast, efg1ΔΔ was only upregulated in rough surfaces demonstrating that mechanically triggered protease activation can persist in filament-incompetent backgrounds but remains modulated by surface topography. This is consistent with evidence that yeast-associated SAP1–SAP3 remain inducible in nonfilamentous states, whereas hyphae-associated SAP4–SAP6 are preferentially activated during EFG1/UME6-driven hyphal programs. ,, Results from this work indicate that cyclic loading elicits distinct virulence-associated responses, including EFG1-dependent filamentation-related outputs and increased proteolytic activity, which together suggest multiple modes of mechanical responsiveness.

Traditionally, biomaterial surface roughness has been considered a key determinant of Candida adhesion and biofilm formation. , Our results show, however, that cyclic loading alone was sufficient to trigger virulence even on polished PMMA surfaces, and that mechanical deformation can partially override the reduced pathogenicity typically associated with smoother topographies. Rough PMMA selectively amplified tissue-damaging outputs, including protease activity, EPS production, and hyphal formation in clinical isolates, indicating that topography can potentiate specific virulence pathways. In contrast, polished surfaces generally suppressed virulence under static conditions, but when cyclic strain was applied, polished PMMA still elicited strong responses, including increased protease activity across most strains and upregulation of ALS3 and HWP1 in SC5314 and UR13. These findings align with evidence that cyclic loading of PMMA upregulates adhesins such as CSH1, RBT5, and ALS3, supporting a model in which C. albicans compensates for smoother surfaces by strengthening substrate adhesion when mechanically stimulated. C. albicans senses surfaces through thigmotropism, detecting topography features via physical cues. , This sensing mechanism, which involves the actin cytoskeleton and MAP kinase signaling pathways, triggers hyphal growth, increased adhesion, and biofilm formation, allowing the fungus to anchor more securely. , Higher surface roughness amplifies these responses by providing microscale features and protected niches that facilitate cell attachment and directional hyphal growth. In contrast, polished surfaces present fewer topographical cues and reduced sheltering sites for microbial retention, which may limit initial adhesion and biofilm stability. Clinically, the present findings may also have implications for the design of denture materials. Our data suggests that while careful polishing may reduce baseline colonization, mechanical loading during mastication can restore or even enhance virulence potential. These observations indicate that denture-associated Candida virulence is shaped by both PMMA’s topography and cyclic mechanical stress, in line with broader evidence that C. albicans adapts its virulence factors to environmental conditions. Future denture materials may benefit from surface designs that minimize microbial adhesion and reduce mechanobiological activation of virulence pathways through optimization of surface topography, material stiffness, or the incorporation of coatings that inhibit biofilm formation.

Despite the potential impact of our findings, this study has limitations. First, in this study, we used a single-species model that does not fully replicate the polymicrobial, immune-active oral environment found in denture wearers. Although recent work showed that cyclic mechanical loading also significantly changes subgingival microbiome, future studies should investigate this phenomenon using inocula derived directly from patients with denture stomatitis. Second, to evaluate the effect of cyclic loading in triggering the virulence of C. albicans, we cultured biofilms for 24 h, which reflects the early stages of biofilm development. Evaluation of the biofilms at different stages of maturation will allow us to capture the dynamic progression of virulence traits. Third, although a salivary pellicle was applied to the surface of the biomaterial, the presence and concentration of specific salivary proteins are affected by the donor and different sterilization processes. Proteins such as mucins, statherin, proline-rich proteins, and antimicrobial peptides exhibit varying abundance and activity levels depending on donor factors and handling methods. Fourth, our study focused solely on PMMA as the denture material, which excludes possible interactions with other commonly used bases and liners. These materials differ in surface chemistry, roughness, and mechanical properties, which can alter the mechanobiological responses observed in this study. Fifth, we evaluated a single cyclic loading magnitude (0.2%), which simulates normal mastication. Future studies should investigate how varying frequencies, intensities, and durations of cyclic mechanical loading influence C. albicans virulence to better reflect the range of clinical conditions. Future studies should quantify detached (dispersed) cells in the supernatant after cyclic loading to distinguish direct virulence induction from possible mechanical selection of strongly adherent subpopulations. Finally, while the present findings demonstrate statistically significant increases in C. albicans virulence-associated biofilm traits under certain surface and mechanical conditions, the extent to which these changes translate to clinical outcomes remains to be determined. Several of the virulence-associated traits evaluated here (i.e., hyphal formation, metabolic activity, EPS production, and proteolytic activity) are well-known contributors to host–pathogen interactions, such as epithelial invasion, immune activation, and biofilm persistence in vivo , Therefore, future studies incorporating epithelial cell models or in vivo systems will be necessary to further evaluate the clinical impact of these mechanobiological responses.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that cyclic mechanical loading applied to denture-base PMMA material is a powerful mechanobiological stimulus that activates virulence pathways in different C. albicans clinical isolates and strains. Results show that virulence activation was strongly strain-dependent and driven by each isolate’s filamentation capacity. Filament-competent strains showed the strongest mechanobiological responses, with significant increases in viability, EPS production, hyphal formation, protease secretion, and upregulation of adhesion and hyphal-wall genes. Findings also highlight that clinical isolates exhibit heightened virulence responsiveness to mechanical cues compared with the commonly used laboratory strain SC5314 except in enzyme production. In contrast, filamentation-incompetent strains showed selective virulent responses, yet still maintained strong SAP-associated protease activity under cyclic loading, indicating that mechanically triggered enzymatic virulence can persist even without robust filamentation. C. albicans integrates PMMA cyclic mechanical forces through both filamentation-dependent and filamentation-independent pathways, and that routine denture loading may contribute to conditions that favor enhanced virulence in vitro, particularly in hosts colonized with hyperfilamentous clinical strains.
