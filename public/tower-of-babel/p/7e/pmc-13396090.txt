Cenobamate: An Appraisal Five Years On
Authors: Eugen Trinka, John Paul Leach, Josemir W. Sander
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cenobamate is an antiseizure medication approved in 2021 as add-on therapy for adults with uncontrolled focal seizures. Early trials showed unusually high seizure freedom rates and a favourable tolerability profile, suggesting that cenobamate may represent an important therapeutic advance in the management of focal and other epilepsies. Here, we highlight the latest data from post hoc analyses and clinical studies suggesting that cenobamate is effective across all focal seizure types. It maintains efficacy and safety over the long term, allowing people to reduce their concomitant medications without compromising seizure control. While head-to-head studies are not available, these data, alongside indirect comparisons with other antiseizure medications, support cenobamate as a potentially important advance in the treatment of focal epilepsy.

Introduction

Epilepsy affects over 50 million individuals of all ages worldwide [1, 2], with negative impacts on the health, quality of life, and social and economic status of individuals and their caregivers [3–7]. Antiseizure medications (ASMs) are the mainstay of epilepsy treatment, and there has been a steady flow of new agents developed over the last 30 years [8–10]. However, despite this growing arsenal of ASMs with different mechanisms of action and efficacy and tolerability profiles, persistent seizures still occur in up to a third of people with epilepsy, even after treatment with multiple ASMs [11].

Cenobamate (CNB) is a novel ASM that has demonstrated efficacy in reducing focal and focal-to-bilateral tonic–clonic (FBTC) seizures, as well as broad-spectrum activity in animal seizure models [12]. CNB has at least two mechanisms of action which may underlie its antiseizure effects. First, it blocks voltage-gated sodium channels by selectively targeting the persistent component of the sodium current, reducing excitatory neurotransmission. Second, it acts as a positive allosteric modulator of GABA-A receptors, independently of the benzodiazepine binding site, to enhance inhibitory signalling [12]. Based on these complementary mechanisms, CNB has the potential to prevent seizure initiation and limit seizure spread at clinically therapeutic doses [13].

In 2021, CNB was approved by the European Medicines Agency for the treatment of adults who continue to have uncontrolled focal seizures despite treatment with at least two other ASMs [14]. The approval of CNB was based on positive efficacy and safety outcomes from one randomised controlled trial (RCT) and one open-label safety trial [15, 16]. When these studies were published in 2020, together with an additional supportive RCT [17], they generated significant enthusiasm for CNB as a potential breakthrough for the treatment of drug-resistant focal epilepsy. In that same year, a discussion arose about whether CNB could be the “game-changer” for which the epilepsy community has been waiting [18]. The initial clinical trials demonstrated high rates of seizure freedom and meaningful seizure reduction, alongside a favourable tolerability profile [15, 17]. While these results were encouraging, essential questions remained. Firstly, is CNB effective across a wide range of focal seizure types, or with different baseline seizure counts or concomitant ASM regimens? Secondly, do the promising results seen in RCTs translate into clinical practice, where participants may have substantial comorbidities that could prevent them from entering a clinical licensing trial [19]? Lastly, as serious, rare adverse events (AEs) cannot be reliably detected in licensing trials, would new safety concerns arise with wider exposure to CNB? Five years on, with multiple RCTs, long-term follow-up data, and clinical evidence available, we revisit these questions to evaluate whether CNB has lived up to its early promise. Most importantly, has CNB begun to reshape treatment approaches for people with drug-resistant focal seizures?

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Efficacy of Cenobamate

CNB has now shown strong and consistent efficacy and safety in adults with focal seizures across a range of clinical studies, including the first two placebo-controlled trials [15, 17], a corresponding long-term extension study [20], and an open-label safety study (Table 1) [16, 21–23]. In one RCT, adjunctive CNB significantly reduced seizure frequency compared with placebo in people with poorly controlled focal seizures [15]. During the 12-week maintenance phase, 40% of participants achieved at least a 50% reduction in seizure frequency with 100 mg/day CNB, 56% with 200 mg/day, and 64% with 400 mg/day. Significantly, up to a fifth of participants receiving CNB achieved seizure freedom compared with only 1% among those receiving placebo [15]. This was despite a high baseline seizure frequency, ranging from 8.4 to 11 seizures per month across groups [15]. Similar results were reported in another RCT, with almost a third of people treated with CNB becoming seizure-free in the 6-week maintenance period compared with 9% in those receiving placebo [17]. Similar rates of seizure response and seizure freedom were obtained at starting schedules similar to those in the long-term safety study [24]. While the clinical trials delivered promising results, they reported only the efficacy of CNB during a 6- to 12-week maintenance period. Longer-term efficacy has now been demonstrated in open-label extension studies. In one study, with a median treatment duration of over 2 years, over a third of participants achieved seizure freedom for at least 12 months [22]. In this study, 13% were seizure-free, while 40% experienced a reduction in seizures of at least 90% during their entire maintenance treatment duration (median 29.5 months).

Another open-label extension study [25] found that over 15% of participants remained seizure-free and almost 40% had at least a 90% reduction in seizures after 36–48 months of treatment [20]. Long-term retention rates (80% at 1 year; 72% at 2 years) from the pooled data of three clinical trials confirm the robust utility of CNB over the long term [26].

Is Cenobamate Effective Across a Wide Range of People with Focal Seizures?

People presenting with focal seizures vary in the type and frequency of their seizures, so it is essential to demonstrate who benefits from CNB treatment. In one study, reductions in seizure frequency were most significant for FBTC seizures. However, a similar degree of seizure reduction was observed in people with focal motor seizures with preserved consciousness (previously focal aware motor; FAM) and in those with focal seizures with impaired consciousness (previously impaired awareness; FIAS) [15, 27]. These findings are supported by a post hoc analysis of an open-label study, in which > 2-year seizure freedom rates of 48% for FAM, 54% for FIAS, and 91% for FBTC seizures were reported [21]. Thus, the evidence to date supports efficacy against a broad spectrum of focal seizure types.

The efficacy of CNB has also been demonstrated in people who initially have a relatively high frequency of seizures. In one study, people with more than 9.5 seizures per month at baseline showed a 48% reduction in seizure frequency with 200 mg/day CNB and a 71% reduction with 400 mg CNB [28]. In another study, response to CNB, defined as at least a 50% reduction in seizure frequency, was similar in those with three or more monthly seizures at baseline compared with those with fewer [22]. While 44% of participants with fewer baseline seizures achieved seizure freedom, almost a quarter of those with three or more seizures per month also became seizure-free [22]. These high rates of seizure freedom indicate the efficacy of CNB even in people with a high seizure burden. However, those with higher seizure frequency may require higher doses to increase their chances of achieving seizure freedom.

Initial experience with CNB, when it was provided as part of an Expanded Access Program (EAP), suggested that CNB’s efficacy also holds for people who may not have been eligible for inclusion in RCTs [29–32]. A multicentre study including 170 people with highly resistant focal seizures who had failed a median of 12 prior ASMs showed that 63% responded to CNB. At a median follow-up of 11 months, 14% were seizure-free.

Is Cenobamate Effective in Real-World Settings?

There is now a growing body of clinical evidence, including data from other EAPs, showing that CNB is just as effective in clinical practice as it is in clinical trial settings (Table 1).

In an early observational study, including almost 300 people with a median of nine prior failed ASMs, half of those treated with adjunctive CNB achieved a 50% reduction in seizure frequency during three months of maintenance, and over 10% achieved seizure freedom [32].

At 12 months of treatment, the proportion of individuals achieving at least a 50% reduction in seizure frequency ranged from 44% to 86% across clinic-based studies [33, 34]. The proportion achieving seizure freedom ranged from 10% to 33% [30, 33]. A meta-analysis of seven clinical studies involving 229 people with drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE), most of whom had focal seizures, reported that 68% had at least a 50% seizure reduction on CNB treatment and 16% achieved seizure freedom [35]. Similarly, some studies looking at efficacy by dose have shown that treatment with higher doses of CNB may offer higher rates of sustained seizure freedom [36], even in those with “absolute drug-resistant” epilepsy [37] or those with failure of higher numbers of prior ASMs.

Together, the regulatory and clinic-based studies highlight CNB’s potential to deliver meaningful and lasting seizure control, including seizure freedom, for a substantial proportion of people with focal seizures, including those with a history of drug resistance [38].

Is Cenobamate Effective Alongside Other ASMs?

A key finding from the clinical trial data is that CNB remains effective regardless of the number or type of concomitant ASMs used [28, 39]. In addition, CNB may reduce reliance on other ASMs. Polypharmacy poses a major challenge for people with epilepsy as it heightens the risk of AEs, reduces quality of life, and contributes to higher healthcare costs [40, 41]. Evidence from multiple sources suggests that CNB enables the reduction or discontinuation of concomitant ASMs, thus improving overall tolerability and long-term treatment retention [16, 23, 36, 42].

In a post hoc analysis of an open-label study, the total concomitant ASM drug load was reduced by an average of 32% over 24 months, regardless of ASM class, with no detrimental impact on seizure freedom rates [43]. Similarly, a long-term follow-up of 49 participants from three trials found that 73% were taking fewer ASMs at a median of 5.6 years after CNB initiation compared with baseline [44].

Reductions in concomitant ASMs have been reported in clinical settings [30, 33, 45]. Among 116 people taking CNB at two German tertiary referral centres, 44% reduced the dose of at least one concomitant ASM, while 59% discontinued at least one ASM [45]. In a Spanish study, carbamazepine, lacosamide, and clobazam were the ASMs with the most significant reductions in defined daily dose after 12 months of CNB treatment [33], as might be expected given their overlapping mechanisms of action with CNB. However, reductions in the use of other concomitant ASMs, including perampanel and SV2A modulators, have also been observed [23, 30, 33, 45], reflecting the potent antiseizure effects of CNB, which reduce the requirement for additional ASMs.

This ability to simplify treatment makes CNB a potentially valuable therapeutic option for people with uncontrolled focal seizures. Expert groups have recommended active management of concomitant ASMs when initiating CNB [13, 42, 46]. This includes close monitoring and gradual tapering of background ASMs during CNB titration [46]. For those taking clobazam or higher doses of sodium channel blockers, or multiple sodium channel blockers, early, proactive dose reductions of concomitant ASMs are recommended before AEs emerge (Fig. 1) [13, 42]. Other ASMs can also be adjusted reactively based on tolerability [13]. For people taking phenytoin or phenobarbital, serum level monitoring is recommended. Among the many individuals treated with adjunctive CNB, some may be able to reduce their concomitant ASMs and transition to CNB monotherapy [36]. A retrospective study in the USA among participants who converted to CNB monotherapy found that 77% had at least a 50% reduction in seizure frequency and 55% were seizure-free after 12 months [47].

Is Cenobamate Effective Earlier in the Treatment Pathway?

While the clinical use of CNB was initially prioritised for people with a lower probability of achieving seizure freedom, either due to the severity of their disease, the aetiology, or the number of previously failed ASMs, evidence for the use of CNB in earlier treatment lines is now increasing. A prospective, multicentre, observational study reported higher rates of sustained (6-month) seizure freedom in people treated with CNB after 2–3 ASMs (22%) compared with those treated later (9%). Similar results were obtained in other retrospective studies showing higher seizure freedom rates in people with epilepsy when treated in earlier versus later treatment lines. In a multicentre study of 465 working-age individuals in whom epilepsy likely had a substantial impact on health, well-being, and socioeconomic status, higher response rates were observed in those treated with CNB in earlier versus later treatment lines [48].

Safety and Tolerability

An early safety concern for CNB was the occurrence of drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), a rare but severe hypersensitivity reaction linked to treatment with ASMs [49]. Among other ASMs, cases of DRESS are reported to occur in 1–4/10,000 individuals receiving carbamazepine, 2.3–4.5/10,000 receiving phenytoin, and between 1/1000 and 1/10,000 receiving lamotrigine [50, 51]. Two cases of DRESS were reported during early CNB development, with one additional case reported in an RCT [15]. To reduce the risk of DRESS, a start-low, go-slow titration schedule was established, with CNB initiated at 12.5 mg/day and titrated no faster than once every 2 weeks, in increments of 50 mg, to the recommended target dose of 200 mg/day [14]. Using this approved titration schedule, no cases of DRESS were identified in a post-marketing analysis of over 100,000 people exposed to CNB [52]. Currently, with more than 223,000 people exposed to CNB, this has not changed. In addition to mitigating the risk of DRESS, the gradual titration of CNB also appears to reduce the incidence of common AEs, since somnolence, dizziness, and fatigue were less frequent during the start-low, go-slow titration phase of the open-label study compared with the two RCTs that used faster titration [53].

CNB has demonstrated a generally favourable safety and tolerability profile throughout clinical development. Most AEs reported in these studies were central nervous system-related, mild-to-moderate in severity, and resolved without intervention [14, 16, 26, 36, 53]. As with many other ASMs, the most commonly reported AEs with CNB are somnolence, dizziness, fatigue, and headache [14]. Psychiatric AEs have been reported infrequently in CNB trials and occur at low rates overall [16, 26].

The long-term safety profile of CNB is now well established, with no unexpected AEs emerging over time, leading to high retention rates [26]. In an open-label extension study, sustained tolerability was observed for up to 7.8 years in adults with treatment-resistant focal seizures on one to three concomitant ASMs [54]. The most commonly reported treatment-emergent AEs (TEAEs) were dizziness (33%), headache (27%), and somnolence (22%), and most of these were mild or moderate in intensity [54].

Most AEs reported in clinical studies occur during the titration phase. However, tolerability during this phase seems favourable. Using the approved start-low, go-slow titration schedule [14], only 7.6% of participants in a large, open-label study discontinued CNB due to a TEAE that emerged during titration. Among those who experienced TEAEs, most (85%) had complete symptom resolution [26]. In a clinical practice study evaluating the safety and effectiveness of CNB over the first 3 months of treatment, multivariate analysis demonstrated a greater effectiveness in reducing FBTC seizures among individuals receiving higher CNB doses at 3 months, with no statistically significant differences in safety outcomes [31]. This analysis suggests that titration schemes slower than those indicated on the label may affect effectiveness without significantly affecting tolerability.

Another sign of CNB’s favourable safety and tolerability profile is the high retention rate observed throughout the clinical development program, open-label extension studies and clinical studies. Pooled data from multiple clinical studies show retention rates of 80% at 1 year and 72% at 2 years, and an estimated 57% probability of continued treatment at 8 years [26]. Retention tended to be greater at higher CNB doses, and discontinuation due to lack of efficacy was relatively uncommon, occurring in just 7.7% of participants [26].

With stepwise titration and early, robust efficacy, there is potential to reduce or discontinue concomitant ASMs. Simplifying comedications provides further opportunity to maximise CNB dose and efficacy while minimising AEs, opening the door to individualised treatment [14, 25, 53].

How Does Cenobamate Compare with Other ASMs?

To date, no direct head-to-head trials of CNB versus other ASMs are available. However, several network meta-analyses (NMAs) based on RCT data suggest that CNB may be more effective than other newer ASMs, particularly in achieving ≥ 50% seizure reduction [55–58]. Across these analyses, CNB consistently ranked among the most effective treatments, while its tolerability and safety profiles remained broadly comparable to other ASMs [55–58].

In an NMA comprising 16 trials and over 6700 participants, CNB was associated with significantly higher odds of achieving ≥ 50% seizure reduction than brivaracetam, eslicarbazepine, lacosamide, and perampanel [56]. CNB had the highest probability of being the most effective ASM for achieving ≥ 50% seizure reduction and seizure freedom. In contrast, tolerability outcomes, including TEAE incidence, were broadly similar across drugs, with brivaracetam and lacosamide ranking highest for tolerability [56]. A review of 45 studies identified CNB as ranking highest for ≥ 50% responder rate among included ASMs, with brivaracetam ranked as the best tolerated [58]. Most recently, a NMA of 53 RCTs and 17 ASMs found that CNB was among the most effective in achieving a 50% response rate. Some of the comparators in this NMA, particularly topiramate and oxcarbazepine, were associated with higher rates of somnolence, and the long-term safety data for others remain limited or unavailable [57].

In people with DRE, CNB has been associated with the lowest Number Needed to Treat to achieve a ≥ 50% response rate and to achieve seizure freedom. This is based on clinical trial data of newer ASMs, including brivaracetam, eslicarbazepine acetate, lacosamide, and perampanel [59]. While NMAs and indirect comparisons cannot replace direct head-to-head trials, they provide valuable insights into the relative performance of ASMs. Collectively, these analyses suggest that CNB may offer a favourable balance of efficacy and tolerability in people with focal seizures, with a potential efficacy advantage over other ASMs.

Other Considerations

Beyond its established efficacy in focal seizures [15, 27], preliminary findings from preclinical models [60] and clinical investigations [61] suggest that CNB may also have therapeutic potential in reducing convulsive seizures. Early data from an RCT in individuals with generalised tonic–clonic seizures (NCT03678753) demonstrate efficacy, with a 71% responder rate (compared with 41% for placebo) and a median reduction in seizure frequency of 100% (compared with 44% for placebo; data on file, SK LifeSciences).

Emerging data also indicate downstream benefits of seizure reduction with CNB, including growing evidence of a treatment-associated reduction in sudden unexpected death in epilepsy incidence [62].

Lastly, the cost-effectiveness of CNB in treating focal seizures appears to be confirmed across Europe [63–65].

Conclusion

A growing body of evidence since 2020 suggests that CNB is a highly effective and well-tolerated treatment for focal seizures. The most recent data indicate that its efficacy is sustained over the long term and is consistent across different types of focal seizures, without additional safety concerns. A significant additional benefit of CNB is that it allows for the reduction of concomitant ASMs without compromising efficacy. In light of this evidence, we believe CNB may represent a potentially important therapeutic advance in the treatment of focal seizures.
