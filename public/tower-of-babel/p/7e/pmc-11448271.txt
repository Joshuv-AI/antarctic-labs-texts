Differences in transcriptomic responses upon Phytophthora palmivora infection among cultivars reveal potential underlying resistant mechanisms in durian
Authors: Wanapinun Nawae, Duangjai Sangsrakru, Thippawan Yoocha, Suparat Pinsupa, Phakamas Phetchawang, Sureeporn Bua-art, Orwintinee Chusri, Sithichoke Tangphatsornruang, Wirulda Pootakham
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPhytophthora palmivora is a devastating oomycete pathogen in durian, one of the most economically important crops in Southeast Asia. The use of fungicides in Phytophthora management may not be a long-term solution because of emerging chemical resistance issues. It is crucial to develop Phytophthora-resistant durian cultivars, and information regarding the underlying resistance mechanisms is valuable for smart breeding programs.ResultsIn this study, we conducted RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to investigate early gene expression responses (at 8, 24, and 48 h) after the P. palmivora infection in three durian cultivars, which included one resistant cultivar (Puangmanee; PM) and two susceptible cultivars (Monthong; MT and Kradumthong; KD). We performed co-expression and differential gene expression analyses to capture gene expression patterns and identify the differentially expressed genes. The results showed that genes encoding heat shock proteins (HSPs) were upregulated in all infected durians. The expression levels of genes encoding HSPs, such as ERdj3B, were high only in infected PM. A higher level of P. palmivora resistance in PM appeared to be associated with higher expression levels of various genes encoding defense and chitin response proteins, such as lysM domain receptor-like kinases. MT had a lower resistance level than PM, although it possessed more upregulated genes during P. palmivora infection. Many photosynthetic and defense genes were upregulated in the infected MT, although their expression levels were lower than those in the infected PM. KD, the least resistant cultivar, showed downregulation of genes involved in cell wall organization or biogenesis during P. palmivora infection.ConclusionsOur results showed that the three durian cultivars exhibited significantly different gene expression patterns in response to P. palmivora infection. The upregulation of genes encoding HSPs was common in all studied durians. The high expression of genes encoding chitin response proteins likely contributed to P. palmivora resistance in durians. Durian susceptibility was associated with low basal expression of defense genes and downregulation of several cell wall-related genes. These findings enhance our understanding of durian resistance to Phytophthora infection and could be useful for the development of elite durian cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05545-z.

Background

Durian fruit, the “king of fruits,” is one of the most economically important agricultural products exported by several countries in Southeast Asia [1]. Thailand, one of the largest durian producers, earned about 2.5 billion dollars from the exportation of fresh durian and derivative products [2]. Monthong, Kanyao, Kradumthong, Puangmanee, and Chanee are popular durian cultivars commercially grown in Thailand. Durian plants (Durio zibethinus Murr.) thrive in a tropical climate with temperatures ranging from 27 to 30 °C and relative humidity from 75 to 80% [1]. Unfortunately, the hot and humid climate in Southeast Asia is optimal for the spread of Phytophthora species [3]. Durians are inevitably prone to diseases caused by Phytophthora [3].

Phytophthora is a devastating oomycete pathogen of several economic crops [4] and might be originated in Southeast Asia [5]. More than 150 Phytophthora species have been identified [6]. The ability of Phytophthora to infect multiple hosts underscores the significant threat posed by this pathogen [7]. Several billion dollars are lost annually as a result of Phytophthora infection in cocoa, durian, rubber, coconut, pepper, potato, and citrus plants in Southeast Asia [3]. Within this genus, Phytophthora palmivora is the most destructive durian pathogen [8]. It attacks all parts of the tree, including the trunk, twigs, branches, fruits, leaves, flowers, and underground portions of the stem and roots [3, 9]. Pathogens enter the host through wounds or natural openings [3]. Fungicides can be used to control Phytophthora infections, but prolonged usage may lead to the development of chemical resistance [10, 11]. P. palmivora has been reported to be insensitive to metalaxyl after long-term use [11]. Resistance can be rapidly detected once fungicides have been reused because the oospores of fungicide-resistant strains can persist in soil for many years [12]. Therefore, the development of durian cultivars that are resistant to Phytophthora is crucial, and a comprehensive understanding of the genes associated with the resistance mechanism holds significant potential for cultivar development programs.

High-throughput RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) technology has been developed to compare gene expression levels under different conditions [13]. Recently, RNA-seq has been used to monitor changes in gene expression in sweet orange, potato, tobacco, and soybean infected with Phytophthora pathogens [14–17], and genes associated with resistant and susceptible phenotypes in these species have been identified. To date, there have been no reported studies on the transcriptomic responses of durians to Phytophthora infection. In this work, we performed RNA-seq experiments to probe the expression profiles of three economically important durian cultivars, namely Monthong (MT), Kradumthong (KD), and Puangmanee (PM). Varying levels of Phytophthora resistance were observed when these three cultivars were grown in the field, and our preliminary study indicated that the resistance levels in the greenhouse of these durians were in the following order: PM > > MT > KD (Supplementary Fig. S1). These preliminary findings align with the resistance levels typically found in durian orchards (the information provided by durian growers). We hypothesized that MT, KD, and PM exhibit different gene expression changes in response to P. palmivora infection. A recent study detected the expression of the haustoria-associated marker genes of a virulent P. palmivora isolate and the callose deposition in the durian host at 24 hours post inoculation (hpi), indicating a high interaction between host and pathogen at this time point [1]. In this study, we analyzed gene expression at 8, 24, and 48 hpi to investigate the early response of durians to P. palmivora infection. Examining the early response when durians perceive and initiate their responses to P. palmivora could enhance our understanding of the mechanisms that resistant durians possess to limit pathogen invasion, the insights that could be useful for the development of new disease-resistant durian cultivars.

Materials and methods

Plant materials , P. palmivora isolates and treatments

The P. palmivora used in this study was originally isolated from durian orchards in Chantaburi province, an area where outbreaks of the infection are common and extremely devastating. Another P. palmivora isolate from Chantaburi has been shown to be highly virulent [8]. According to these data, we selected to use P. palmivora from Chantaburi for our experiment. We obtained the P. palmivora Chantaburi isolate from the Plant Pathology Laboratory, Department of Agriculture, Thailand. The mycelial culture of P. palmivora was grown on potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium for 14 days at 25 °C. For inoculation, a wound was made on the stem of each durian plant (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Circular PDA plugs, 5 mm in diameter, were prepared using a cork borer. PDA plugs with P. palmivora mycelia were used for pathogen inoculation, while PDA plugs without mycelia were used for mock inoculation (control). These plugs were then affixed to the wound site on the plant stem (Supplementary Fig. S2B). To maintain high relative humidity, the inoculation site was covered with sterile cotton balls and parafilm, with 2 milliliters of sterile water injected into the cotton balls (Supplementary Fig. S2C-D). The durian plants were kept in plastic bags to maintain humidity and arranged using a randomized block design to mitigate the impact of uncontrolled environmental conditions on RNA-seq data analysis (Supplementary Fig. S2E). The experiment was conducted under a temperature range of 27 to 33 °C with an approximately 12-hour day-night cycle (Chantaburi typical climate), conditions under which durians thrive and infections are commonly found. The impact of P. palmivora infection on each plant was assessed by measuring the increase in wound size compared to the initial size (Supplementary Fig. S1 and S2F). ANOVA (parametric) and Kruskal-Wallis (nonparametric) tests were employed to compare wound spreads among multiple samples. For paired sample comparisons, the nonparametric Wilcoxon test was utilized. At 8, 24, and 48 hpi, the first two branches, leaves, and stems located 2 cm above the inoculation site were harvested and promptly frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent RNA extraction and sequencing.

RNA extraction and library preparation for MGI sequencing

Liquid nitrogen was used to pulverize the samples. Total RNA was extracted from each sample using the CTAB method. The DNA-free™ DNA Removal Kit (Invitrogen™) was employed to remove contaminating DNA. The fragment analyzer equipment (Agilent) was used to assess the quality and quantity of RNA. We used the Dynabeads™ mRNA Purification Kit to purify mRNA molecules from total RNA preparations. cDNA libraries were constructed using the MGIEasy RNA Library Prep kit. An MGISEQ-2000RS machine was used to sequence the libraries.

RNA read mapping and gene expression analysis

RNA reads were mapped to the durian pangenome [18] using HISAT2 software [19]. For each sample, the level of gene expression was measured by using StringTie2 [20] to quantify RNA reads mapped to the gene regions. HISAT2 and StringTie2 were run following the pipeline described in [21]. The standalone version of iDEP2.0, which served as the RNA-seq analysis environment [22], was employed for data filtering, using counts per million (CPM) ≥ 0.5 in ≥ 80% of all 54 samples as the cutoff. Regularized log transformation and principal component analysis (PCA) were also conducted using iDEP2.0. We conducted two different types of gene expression analyses: DGE and co-expression analyses. In the DGE analysis, DESeq2 was used to compare gene expression levels of samples under two contrasting conditions [23]. For each cultivar, the expression levels of triplicate samples under infected condition were compared with those under uninfected condition and the expression levels of infected samples were compared between time points. Additionally, at each time point, the expression levels of the infected samples were compared between two cultivars. The log2 of the fold change of 1.5 and the adjusted p-value of 0.05 were used in the DGE analysis. For co-expression analysis, we adopted weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA) with default parameters to identify networks of genes with highly correlated expression patterns [24]. The co-expression networks were displayed using Cytoscape software [25], and hub nodes were identified using the maximal clique centrality (MCC) scoring method in the cytoHubba plugin [26], focusing on the top 10 highest MCC score nodes and their first neighbor nodes.

Analysis of gene functions

We used OmicBox (Blast2GO) software version 3.0.29 [27] to find the gene ontology (GO) terms of the genes that were obtained as the results of the DGE and co-expression analyses. The software was run with an e-value of 1 × 10− 6 and the annotation cutoff of 55. The GO terms were also obtained from the BLAST searches of the gene sequences against the UniProt database. Mercator4 (or MapMan4) was used to classify genes into functional classes [28]. Mercator4 was run in protein sequence mode with Swissprot and Prot-scriber annotation options. The overrepresented functions of the genes from the DGE and co-expression analyses were identified using the STRING database [29], on which the protein sequences from the pangenome [18] were uploaded and used as a background set. We also searched the KEGG database [30] to find the pathways, in which the genes were involved.

Analysis of gene expression by quantitative PCR

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was used to validate the RNA-seq analysis. Fifteen DEGs were selected for validation, including genes encoding disease resistance protein RGA4 (infected MT vs. infected PM at 08 hpi), protein RESISTANCE TO PHYTOPHTHORA 1 (infected vs. uninfected MT at 08 hpi), receptor kinase-like protein Xa21 (infected vs. uninfected KD at 08 hpi, infected vs. uninfected MT at 08 hpi, infected vs. uninfected PM at 08 hpi), pathogenesis-related genes transcriptional activator PTI5-like (infected MT vs. infected PM at 08 hpi), LysM domain receptor-like kinase 4 (infected vs. uninfected PM at 24 hpi), LRR receptor-like serine/threonine-protein kinase FLS2 (infected MT vs. infected PM at 24 hpi), leucine-rich repeat extensin-like protein 4 (infected vs. uninfected KD at 08 hpi, infected KD vs. infected PM at 08 hpi), protein PAT1 homolog 1 (infected KD vs. infected PM at 24 hpi, infected MT vs. infected PM at 24 hpi), probable WRKY transcription factor 40 (infected MT vs. infected PM at 08 hpi), zinc finger protein ZAT10-like (infected vs. uninfected MT at 08 hpi), zinc finger protein ZAT10-like (infected vs. uninfected PM at 08 hpi), chlorophyll a-b binding protein (infected vs. uninfected PM at 08 hpi, infected MT vs. infected PM at 08 hpi, infected KD vs. infected MT at 08 hpi), photosynthetic NDH subunit of subcomplex B 5 (infected vs. uninfected MT at 08 hpi), pectinesterase (infected KD vs. infected PM at 08 hpi), and nitrate reductase (infected vs. uninfected KD at 24 hpi, infected vs. uninfected MT at 24 hpi). Actin, AP-2 mu subunit, and elongation factor 1-alpha were used as reference housekeeping genes. Primers were designed using the PrimerQuest Tool (Supplementary Table S1). For the RT-qPCR analysis, 50 ng of RNA was used as a template in a 20 µL total reaction volume. We used the EvoScript RNA SYBR® Green I Master (Roche, Germany), a one-step RT-PCR kit that enabled both reverse transcription of RNA into cDNA and qPCR to be done in a single reaction mixture. The reactions were performed using the QuantStudio™ 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System. Analyses were conducted with two or three biological replicates per condition, depending on RNA quality. Gene expression changes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [31]. A scatter plot comparing the expression values from RNA-seq and RT-qPCR data was generated using Microsoft Excel.

Results

NA-seq data quality and gene expression analysis strategies

In this study, we carried out RNA-seq experiments to investigate gene expression changes in response to P. palmivora infection in three economically important durian cultivars, one of which was a resistant cultivar (Puangmanee; PM) and the other two were susceptible cultivars (Monthong; MT and Kradumthong; KD). Our sequencing generated, on average, 3.6 × 109 bases per sample, covering about 26X of the total bases of all durian genes. The average percentages of bases with a quality score greater than 20 (Q20) and 30 (Q30) were 98% and 93%, respectively (Supplementary Table S2). The average number of reads across all cultivars at 8, 24, and 48 hpi was approximately 24, 23, and 26 million reads, with a mapping rate of 86%, 88%, and 86%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S3).

We generated a PCA plot based on the expression levels of all expressed genes (CPM ≥ 0.5) in 54 samples across 18 experimental groups (2 infection treatments x 3 cultivars x 3 time points x 3 replicates). The first and second principal component (PC1 and PC2) axes accounted for 31% and 9% of the total variation, respectively (Fig. 1B), which are slightly smaller than those reported in other studies [32, 33]. The PC1 axis revealed that the distance between the clusters of infected and uninfected samples of MT was greater than those of PM and KD, indicating earlier gene expression changes in MT. For all cultivars, the PC1 axis also showed a separation of both infected and uninfected samples at 8 hpi from those at 24 and 48 hpi. This variation suggested an effect of uncontrollable environmental factors on the expression at different time points. The effect was considered small because the PC1 axis explained only 31% of the variance. The within-group variations among the replicates were observed mostly within the small variance of the PC2 axis, suggesting the consistency of the expression under the same conditions. Overall, these PCA results indicate the high quality of our RNA-seq data for detecting gene expression changes in durians responding to P. palmivora.

We conducted two different types of RNA-seq data analyses: co-expression and differential gene expression (DGE) analyses. The aim of co-expression analysis was to group genes based on similarity in their expression patterns. DGE analyses, on the other hand, identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs), whose expression levels were significantly different in two contrasting conditions. We focused on the expression differences between samples of different cultivars or under different infection conditions at the same time point, rather than on differences across different time points, to minimize the variation attributable to environmental conditions. Through these analyses, we were able to obtain an overview of gene expression and concurrently pinpoint genes exhibiting significant changes in expression within a certain cultivar and/or time interval.

A significantly varied number of DEGs in response to Phytophthora infection among durian cultivars

In DGE analysis, comparisons between gene expression under infected and uninfected conditions at the same time point showed that the total number of DEGs in MT, especially at 8 hpi, was significantly higher than that in KD or PM (Fig. 1A). Approximately 88% and 60% of the total DEGs in MT and KD, respectively, were identified at 8 hpi, whereas 81% of the DEGs in PM were identified at 24 hpi. Compared to that at 8 hpi, the number of DEGs at 24 hpi exhibited an approximately 400% increase in PM and an approximately 27% and 86% decrease in KD and MT, respectively. For all cultivars, the number of DEGs was lowest at 48 hpi, suggesting that it was the late stage of the early response of durian to P. palmivora. At 8 hpi, infected MT and PM plants had more upregulated DEGs than downregulated DEGs, and vice versa for infected KD plants. At 24 hpi, the number of upregulated DEGs accounted for approximately 61, 70, and 74% of the total DEGs in the infected KD, MT, and PM, respectively. These results indicated that among all cultivars, MT exhibited the broadest spectrum of changes in gene expression in response to Phytophthora infection, whereas PM exhibited the narrowest range of gene expression profile changes.

The DEGs in the RNA biosynthesis class (based on the MapMan database) were found in high proportion in all cultivars, especially in MT (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. S4). Among them, DEGs encoding transcription factors (TFs) were the most abundant. The number of TF DEGs was in the following order: MT > > KD > PM, which was concordant with the total number of DEGs in these cultivars. From 8 to 24 hpi, the number of TF DEGs decreased significantly in the infected MT (from 170 to 25 DEGs) and ten times increased in the infected PM (from 3 to 30 DEGs). The number of TF DEGs changed slightly between the two time points in infected KD plants (from 27 to 26 DEGs). Additionally, only the infected MT had a high number of DEGs encoding TFs that were involved in RNA biosynthesis in the chloroplasts. Together, these results indicated that the responsiveness of the transcriptomic response to P. palmivora infection was highest in MT, followed by KD and PM.

To validate the results of RNA-seq data analysis, we compared the fold changes of 15 DEGs in 22 comparisons (detailed in the methods) from the RNA-seq data with those from the RT-qPCR expression analysis. The results showed the consistency of expression changes detected by two methods, i.e., the genes that were shown up- or downregulated in the RNA-seq data analysis were found changed in the same direction in the RT-qPCR data analysis. The scatter plot of the fold changes from two datasets showed a high correlation (R² = 0.96, Fig. 1C), indicating a similar amplitude of expression changes detected by these two independent methods. These results confirmed that our RNA-seq data analysis was valid.

Gene expression changes in response to P. palmivora infection of three durian cultivars

Co-expression analysis distinguished the genes (including DEGs and non-DEGs) into seven modules or groups based on their expression patterns (Fig. 3A). All cultivars had similar expression patterns of genes in modules I and II. Therefore, the correlation value of the expression patterns of these modules with the cultivar parameter was low (Fig. 3B). The expression patterns of modules I and II showed a high correlation with the parameters of time point (8 hpi) and treatment conditions (infected plants at 24 hpi), respectively (Fig. 3B). The expression patterns of genes in modules III-V were highly correlated with the cultivar parameter (Fig. 3B). In these three modules, the expression of genes in PM was different from that in MT and/or KD. For modules VI and VII, the expressions under infected and uninfected conditions at 8 hpi were different in MT and KD, respectively.

Common gene expression changes in three durian cultivars

All cultivars shared a common expression profile for the genes found in modules I and II (Fig. 3). Among all modules, module I had the highest number of genes (2,631), indicating that most genes were similarly expressed in the studied durian cultivars. GO enrichment analysis showed that the genes that were overrepresented in this module were involved in RNA modification, cytidine to uridine editing, response to light stimulus, and photosynthesis. Module I also contained genes that could participate in the defense response, although they accounted for only approximately 10% of the total members. Most of these genes encode receptor-like kinases (RLKs) and receptor-like proteins (RLPs), whereas others encode disease resistance proteins. The DEG encoding receptor kinase-like protein Xa21 was among the genes that were simultaneously upregulated in all cultivars.

Genes in module II displayed significantly different expression levels between the infected and uninfected plants at 24 hpi (Fig. 3). The DGE analysis indicated that most DEGs found in this module were upregulated. Enrichment analysis showed that most genes in module II encoded heat shock proteins (HSPs) that were involved in protein folding (Fig. 4A). Based on the KEGG database, the module contained genes encoding cytosolic HSP90 (heat shock 90-kDa protein), HSP70, HSP40 (DnaJ homolog subfamily A), small HSP (sHSP), and HSP70 nucleotide exchange factor Fes1, that were involved in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-associated degradation (ERAD). Genes encoding ER-resident HSPs, including luminal-binding protein (BiP; HSP70), Endoplasmin homolog (Grp94; HSP90), and DnaJ protein ERdj3B (HSP40), were shown to play a role in the protein recognition process. Module II also had genes that encoded calcium-dependent protein kinase 21 (CDPK21), respiratory burst oxidase homolog protein A (RBOHA), probable calcium-binding protein CML27, and heat shock protein 83 (HSP83; HSP90), which were involved in the recognition of pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP) in the PAMP-triggered immunity (PTI) that could lead to a hypersensitive response via reactive oxygen species (ROS) signaling. Additionally, GO enrichment analysis of all DEGs from the DGE analysis showed that the upregulated DEGs encoding heat shock factors (HSFs) and HSPs were enriched at 24 hpi in all cultivars (Fig. 4B). DEGs encoding HSP90 were more abundant in PM (five DEGs) than in KD (one DEG) or MT (one DEG). The number of HSP70 encoding DEGs was similar in all cultivars. In all cultivars, the DEGs encoding sHSP were present in the highest proportion. The upregulated DEGs encoding HSP60 (mitochondrial and chloroplastic chaperonin CPN60) were found only in MT and PM, whereas those encoding ERdj3B (HSP40) were found only in KD and PM. The number of upregulated DEGs that encoded ERdj3B in PM (six DEGs) was higher than that in KD (one DEG). These results indicated that the expression of HSP encoding genes was a core response of the studied durians to P. palmivora infection.

Different expression profiles of defense response genes between PM and the other two cultivars

For modules III-V, gene expression in PM differed from that in MT, KD, or both (Fig. 3A and B). Enrichment analysis indicated that pattern recognition receptor (PRR) and resistance (R) protein encoding genes were prevalent in these three modules (Fig. 4A). The PRRs were, for example, rust resistance kinase Lr10 (LRK10), LRR receptor-like serine/threonine-protein kinase FLS2, receptor-like protein EIX2, receptor-like protein kinase FERONIA, and several receptor-like proteins. The set of R proteins included disease resistance proteins RPS5, RPV1, RUN1, disease resistance-like protein DSC1, putative disease resistance protein RGA4, RPP13-like protein 1 (RPPL1), and probable disease resistance protein At4g27220. Other defense-related proteins encoded by the genes in these modules were EMSY-LIKE 3, PAT1 homolog 1 (PATL1), proteasome subunit beta type-1, phospholipase D beta 1 (PLDBETA1), yippee-like At4g27740, patatin-like protein 2 (PLP2), GDSL esterase/lipase At5g14450, prohibitin-1, cucumisin, and thioredoxin H-type. We also performed DGE to compare the expression of these genes in PM with those in MT or KD. The results showed that the genes encoding PATL1, At4g27740, and RGA4 were more highly expressed in PM than in KD and MT, whereas the expression level of DSC1 was lower. Additionally, the genes encoding EIX2, FERONIA, RPV1, and RUN1 exhibited higher expression levels in PM than in MT.

DGE analysis identified several defense genes whose expression levels were significantly altered in the infected plants. The DEGs that were assigned the defense response GO term were enriched in the set of upregulated DEGs in infected PM and MT (Fig. 4B). Among these, the response to fungus GO term was overrepresented among the upregulated DEGs in the infected MT. Examples of proteins encoded by these upregulated DEGs were receptor-like protein kinase THESEUS 1, pathogenesis-related protein PR-4, protein PMR5, UDP-glucuronate 4-epimerase 1, and endochitinase 1. The DEGs with the defense response GO term were also enriched in the set of downregulated DEGs in the infected MT (Fig. 4B). The DEGs with the response to chitin GO term were additionally found to be enriched in this set. Examples of proteins encoded by these downregulated DEGs were zinc finger protein ZAT10-like and probable WRKY transcription factor 40 (WRKY40). On the other hand, the DEGs with the response to chitin GO term were predominant among the upregulated DEGs in the infected PM (Fig. 4B). The upregulated DEGs included those encoding zinc finger protein ZAT10-like, probable WRKY40, and lysM domain receptor-like kinase 4 (LYK4). Additionally, we observed several genes with the signal transduction GO terms downregulated in MT. Examples were DEGs that encoded ethylene-responsive transcription factors (ERFs), pathogenesis-related genes transcriptional activator PTI5, lipase-like PAD4, mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase 5 (MKK5), probable WRKY transcription factor 50 (WRKY50), and DSC1. In contrast, several upregulated DEGs in infected PM were involved in signal transduction in the defense response mechanism. Examples were DEGs that encoded L-type lectin-domain containing receptor kinase S.4 (LECRKS4), LYK4, probable serine/threonine-protein kinase PBL19, PR5-like receptor kinase (PR5K), calmodulin binding protein PICBP, and major allergen Pru ar 1. Overall, the results indicated that the changes in the expression of several defense genes in PM were different from those in the other two cultivars.

We additionally analyzed the enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways of DEGs that were uniquely up- or downregulated in PM (Table 1). Significant GO terms were identified from DEGs detected at 24 hpi, while the number of DEGs at 8 and 48 hpi was too small to identify any significant terms. The analysis revealed that most of the DEGs uniquely upregulated in PM at 24 hpi were involved in protein folding, unfolded protein binding, response to chitin, response to stress, DNA-binding transcription factor activity, and protein serine kinase activity. A significant number of these DEGs were also involved in the protein processing pathway in the endoplasmic reticulum. Further similarity searches against the KEGG database showed an upregulated DEG at 8 hpi that encoded a basic endochitinase-like protein, which plays a role in the MAPK signaling pathway. At 24 hpi, upregulated DEGs of proteins, such as probable calcium-binding protein CML27, heat shock protein 81 − 1, and endoplasmin homolog, were mapped to the plant-pathogen interaction pathway. Conversely, the majority of DEGs downregulated only in PM at 24 hpi were genes encoding protein components of the chloroplast thylakoid.

PRR, signaling, and resistance genes with PM-specific expression patterns

We further examined the correlation between gene expression levels and the parameters that accounted for the cultivar, infection condition, and time point combination effects (Fig. 5A). The results showed that the representative expression of module III.I, which was a submodule of module III, was positively correlated with the PM/P/8hpi, PM/P/24hpi, and PM/P/48hpi parameters (together called the PM parameters). The correlation values were 0.34, 0.37, and 0.33, respectively. Meanwhile, module III.I had a weak negative correlation with the same group of parameters in KD and MT, with an average correlation value of -0.19. The heatmap of the expression levels confirmed that the expression pattern of the genes in this module in PM differed from those in KD and MT (Fig. 5B).

Each gene had an individual correlation value with each parameter. Genes in module III.I were classified into two groups by their correlation values. The first group included genes whose expression was negatively correlated with the PM parameters and was referred to as the negative group. The heatmap showed that the expression levels of genes in this group were overall lower in PM than in KD and MT (Fig. 5B). Among these genes, RLP6, RLP13, DSC1, PLDBETA1, putative disease resistance protein RGA1, and disease resistance protein At4g27190 were significantly lower expressed in PM. In contrast, the expression of genes in the second group was positively correlated with the PM parameters and was referred to as the positive group. The genes that were significantly higher expressed in PM than that in the other two cultivars were that encoding PATL1, LRK10, MLP-like protein 28 (MLP28), receptor-like protein Cf-9 homolog, LRR receptor-like serine/threonine-protein kinase FLS2, syntaxin-71, UDP-glycosyltransferase 79B6 (UGT79B6), protein PECTIC ARABINOGALACTAN SYNTHESIS-RELATED (PAGR), and probable acyl-activating enzyme 16, chloroplastic (AAE16).

The network analysis showed that the expressions of RLP6, RLP7, and PLDBETA1 encoding genes were correlated, either positively or negatively, with those of many other genes in the network, making them appear as hub genes in the co-expression network of module III.I (Fig. 5C). The disease resistance genes that were directly linked to these hub genes were DSC1, RGA1, and At4g27190 encoding genes from the negative group and FLS2, LRK10, PATL1 encoding genes from the positive group. Other defense response genes in the positive group, such as MLP28 encoding gene formed a subnetwork with the first neighbors (UGT79B6 and AAE16) of the hub genes. An additional subnetwork included the gene encoding the RUN1, which was linked to the hub genes through RGA1 and PATL1 encoding genes. The expression of RUN1 encoding gene significantly increased by time points in PM.

The increased expression of photosynthetic genes in P. palmivora infected MT

In module VI, most genes were involved in photosynthesis, as revealed by enrichment analysis (Fig. 4A). This module contained a gene that encoded protein RESISTANCE TO PHYTOPHTHORA 1 (RPH1), which was a chloroplastic protein. Genes that were co-expressed with RPH1 encoded other chloroplastic proteins, such as, PGR5-like protein 1 A, ferredoxin–NADP reductase, and the photosynthetic NDH subunit of subcomplex B 5. Other genes in this module could be involved in defense response. Examples included genes encoding PR5-like receptor kinase, protein ZINC INDUCED FACILITATOR-LIKE 1, lipase-like PAD4, pathogenesis-related genes transcriptional activator PTI5, ethylene-responsive transcription factor 1 A, PR5-like receptor kinase, and mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase 5. Consistently, the DGE analysis showed that several DEGs in MT were involved in photosynthesis (Fig. 4B). Based on the MapMan database, upregulated DEGs in the photosynthesis class accounted for approximately 10.5% of the total DEGs in infected MT (Fig. 2). This percentage was significantly higher than that in infected PM (4.8%) and KD (2.1%). These genes showed increased expression at 8 hpi and remained unaltered at 24 and 48 hpi. In infected KD and PM, we found a small number of photosynthetic DEGs, of which the number of downregulated DEGs was slightly higher than that of upregulated DEGs. The expression of RPH1, for example, was about 4.6 folds upregulated in infected MT but was not significantly changed in infected KD and PM. These results indicated that the upregulation of photosynthetic DEGs was unique to MT and involved in the response to P. palmivora infection. However, the expression levels of most photosynthetic DEGs in MT did not significantly differ from those in KD or PM at any time point. The average fold change (in the log2 function) over 1,763 DEGs in MT was 1.3, which indicated that most DEGs were upregulated in the infected MT. However, for the DEGs from the comparison between the expression levels in the infected MT and PM, the average fold change was negative (-1.5). We found that only 3% of 1,249 DEGs that were upregulated in the infected MT were expressed at a higher level than those in the infected PM. The results indicated that the expression levels of most genes under both normal and infected conditions in MT were lower than those in PM.

The downregulation of cell wall related genes in P. palmivora infected KD

In module VII, most genes were implicated in phenylpropanoid and lignin metabolic processes and plant-type cell wall organization or biogenesis (Fig. 4A). The genes involved in lignin and phenylpropanoid metabolic processes were laccase 4, 17, 22, caffeic acid 3-O-methyltransferase, cytochrome P450 84A1, dirigent protein 21, phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, trans-cinnamate 4-monooxygenase, and cinnamoyl-CoA reductase 1 encoding genes. The module also contained genes encoding cellulose synthase 4, 6, 7, 8, and CELLULOSE SYNTHASE INTERACTIVE 1, which were involved in cellulose production. Other genes that could be implicated in plant cell wall organization or biogenesis were expansin-A4, fasciclin-like arabinogalactan protein 1, 2, 7, 12, 17, probable xyloglucan endotransglucosylase/hydrolase protein 23, glucuronoxylan 4-O-methyltransferase 1 (GXM1), and protein IRX15-LIKE encoding genes.

Most genes in module VII were downregulated at 8 hpi in infected KD. Enrichment analysis showed overrepresentation of genes implicated in plant-type cell wall organization or biogenesis within the set of downregulated DEGs found in the infected KD (Fig. 4B). Approximately 8.3% of the total DEGs in infected KD were in the cell wall organization class (Fig. 2). This percentage was higher than that in infected MT (4.2%) and PM (2.9%). The downregulated DEGs were mainly detected at 8 hpi. Examples of DEGs that were downregulated in infected KD were those that encoded cell wall proteins, including expansin, fasciclin-like arabinogalactan protein, and LRR-domain extensin. There were also DEGs involved in the metabolism of pectin, cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. Examples included DEGs that encoded pectinesterase, cellulose synthase, protein REDUCED WALL ACETYLATION 2, and laccase. Several downregulated DEGs were expressed at lower levels in KD than in PM or MT. The downregulation of these DEGs could explain the difference in resistance levels between KD and the other two durian cultivars.

Additional genes were found in module VII.I, which was the submodule of module VII (Fig. 5A). At 48 hpi, the expression levels of genes in module VII.I were significantly higher in PM than in KD (Fig. 5D). Most of these genes were predominantly associated with fatty acid elongation and wax biosynthesis. Key interconnected genes in the co-expression network included 3-ketoacyl-CoA synthase 6 (KCS6), cinnamoyl-CoA reductase-like SNL6, long chain acyl-CoA synthetase 1 (LACS1), alkane hydroxylase MAH1, very-long-chain aldehyde decarbonylase CER3, and ABC transporter G family member 11 (ABCG11) encoding genes (Fig. 5E).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated gene expression in a large sample group (54 samples from 18 experimental groups) to comprehensively explore the genetic response of durian to the P. palmivora infection. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to employ RNA-seq to investigate genome-wide gene expression changes in durians in response to P. palmivora. Most existing gene expression studies in durian have focused on fruit ripening, while research on gene expression changes in response to pathogens remains scarce for this economically significant plant. A recent study on the interaction between durian and P. palmivora primarily focused on the expression of four pathogen genes and the physiological responses of the host [8]. In contrast, our study examined diverse genetic responses by analyzing gene expressions in three durian cultivars with different genetic backgrounds.

Heat shock protein encoding genes are required for the response of durians to P. palmivora infection

The upregulation of HSP genes was considered the core response because all durians exhibited this gene expression change. In this group of genes, the sHSP encoding genes were the most abundant. sHSPs were reported to be induced in both compatible and incompatible interactions between potato and Phytophthora infestans [34]. sHSPs are ancient stress response proteins that are evolutionarily conserved across domains of life [35, 36]. The common expression of sHSPs in both resistant and susceptible cultivars appeared to be linked to the conservation and chaperone roles of these proteins in plants. sHSPs do not work alone, but in collaboration with other HSPs such as HSP70 [37]. HSPs play an indispensable role in quality control of PRRs and R proteins [36]. Consistently, our examination of gene sequences in the KEGG database revealed that several genes encoded HSPs that were involved in PTI and the ER quality control (ERQC) mechanism. The upregulation of genes encoding HSP at 24 hpi might be related to the increased expression of other defense genes at 8 and 24 hpi. For example, we observed the expression of genes encoding the ER chaperone BiP (in module II) and receptor kinase-like protein Xa21 (in module I). These two genes were similarly expressed in all durian cultivars. The expression of these genes might be interrelated, as BiP has been suggested to play a role in the ERQC of Xa21 after pathogen infection [38, 39]. Despite sharing a similar expression pattern, some HSP encoding genes were expressed at varying levels among the durian cultivars, and some genes were upregulated in only one cultivar. The number of genes encoding HSP90 and ERdj3B was five and six times higher, respectively, in infected PM than in infected KD. HSP90 encoding genes have been shown to be essential for the resistance of Nicotiana benthamiana to P. infestans [40], whereas ERdj3B was involved in the proper accumulation of PRR [41]. The variation in the resistance level against P. palmivora infection between durian cultivars might be partly linked to the different expression levels of the HSP genes.

The resistance phenotype of PM is associated with elevated expression levels of several defense and chitin response genes

Several defense genes were differentially expressed in the three durian cultivars. Our results showed two groups of defense genes classified based on their expression patterns. The first group included genes in modules III-V. Most genes in this group were expressed differently between PM and the other two cultivars in both infected and uninfected conditions, indicating that the difference was not directly linked to the presence of the pathogen but to the genetic background. In a previous study, we discovered significant variation in the content of defense genes among PM, KD, and MT durians [18]. The difference in both the content and expression levels of defense genes might explain the difference in resistance levels among these three durians. The expression levels of several defense genes in the infected PM were significantly higher than those in the infected MT and/or KD. For example, the expression levels of genes encoding RUN1, RPV1, and RGA4 proteins in infected MT were 4.7, 22.5 and 162.3 times lower than those in infected PM, but not significantly different from those in infected KD. The first two proteins had a toll/IL-1R homologous region (TIR) domain, and the last one had an NB-LRR domain. The genes encoding RUN1 and RPV1 were shown to confer resistance to fungal and oomycete pathogens in grapevine [42]. RGA4 protein was reported to recognize multiple unrelated avirulence (Avr) proteins from Magnaporthe oryzae or rice blast fungus [43]. When fungi or oomycetes infect hosts, Avr proteins, which are effector proteins, can significantly impact the biology of the infected cells [44]. The expression patterns of these defense genes might contribute to the resistance phenotypes of PM.

The second group included defense DEGs whose expression under infected condition was altered compared to the expression under uninfected condition. All cultivars had DEGs in this group (Supplementary Table S3), indicating that they all perceived and responded to infection. However, different cultivars had different proportions of defense DEGs (Fig. 2). Enrichment analysis revealed that defense DEGs were enriched among the upregulated DEGs in infected PM, but not in infected KD. These findings explained the lower resistance level of KD compared to that of PM. For the infected MT, we found enrichment of defense genes in both up- and downregulated DEG sets. Most of the defense DEGs that were downregulated in the infected MT were involved in chitin response. In contrast, genes with similar functions were predominantly upregulated in infected PM. Chitin was found in zoospores and released sporangia of Phytophthora and could be detected as a PAMP by LYK5 in Arabidopsis [45]. In our study, the genes encoding LYK3 and LYK4 were upregulated only in infected PM. LYK4, for example, was essential for chitin recognition, which resulted in the activation of plant innate immunity [46]. The different expression patterns of chitin response genes might make the PM response more specific to Phytophthora than the MT response, and might contribute to a higher resistance level of PM.

Additional defense-related genes were detected in module III.I, which was positively correlated with the PM parameters (PM/P/8hpi, PM/P/24hpi, and PM/P/48hpi parameters). In this module, the expression levels of FLS2, LRK10, PATL1, MLP28, and Cf-9 homolog were specifically high in PM, suggesting that they contributed to the durian resistance phenotype. In all studied cultivars, these genes showed similar expression levels in uninfected and infected plants, suggesting the difference in the innate expression levels between PM and the other two cultivars. Additionally, their direct interactions were not reported in the STRING database, suggesting that they were independently regulated. Signal transduction and activation of defense genes are essential for plant resistance to Phytophthora [47]. In module III.I, several genes with a high-level expression in PM were involved in signaling pathways that were activated when the plant perceives a pathogen. For example, FLS2 was a well-characterized PRR in plant innate immunity [48], while RLP Cf-9 homolog played a role in Cladosporium fulvum fungal resistance [49]. The role of these genes in Phytophthora resistance has not been clearly reported. In this study, the high expression levels of these two genes and their co-expressed partners in PM suggested that they played roles in Phytophthora resistance. The overall expression pattern also implied that PM has evolved under higher disease pressure compared to KD and MT.

Multiple genes in the MT showed low basal expression levels

Among the three durian cultivars, the number of DEGs was the highest in MT, although it was less resistant to P. palmivora infection than PM (Supplementary Fig. S1). The number of DEGs was also higher in susceptible cultivars when resistant and susceptible melons [50] and tobacco [16] were treated with Phytophthora capsica and Phytophthora nicotianae, respectively. However, the expression levels of most genes in MT were lower than those in the other two cultivars, especially in PM. Several upregulated DEGs in infected MT encoded a chloroplastic protein. Several of these DEGs were photosynthetic genes (Fig. 2), which were usually downregulated in plants interacting with several pathogens [51], including Phytophthora [52]. In this study, photosynthetic genes were overrepresented in the set of downregulated DEGs in the infected PM. In tomato, the photosynthesis genes were also upregulated in the susceptible line and downregulated in the resistant line [53]. That study, however, did not provide any assumption on the upregulation of photosynthesis genes. In our study, based on module VI from the co-expression analysis, we found that the upregulation of photosynthetic genes was linked to the upregulation of the genes of chloroplastic proteins that could play a role in defense response. RPH1, which played a positive role in the response of plants to Phytophthora [54], was an example of such chloroplastic proteins. Similar to that of the other photosynthetic DEGs in the infected MT, the expression level of RPH1, despite the upregulation, was not significantly different from that in the infected PM or KD. The representative expression profile of module VI indicated that, at 8 hpi under uninfected condition, MT had a significantly lower expression of genes encoding chloroplastic proteins than PM did (Fig. 3A). Therefore, the upregulation of photosynthetic genes in the infected MT appeared to be linked to their low basal expression level and their co-expression with some defense genes. The results together implied that the innate expression levels of numerous genes in MT were lower than those in PM. Yet under the P. palmivora infection, the increased expression of most genes in MT was not enough to reach the expression level in PM. The lower resistance level of MT compared to PM was correlated with the lower expression levels of several genes in both infected and uninfected conditions.

Potential relationship between the low resistance level of KD and downregulation of cell wall-related genes

Several genes involved in phenylpropanoid and lignin metabolic processes and plant-type cell wall organization or biogenesis were downregulated in the infected KD (Fig. 2). The most prevalent downregulated DEGs were those encoding expansins, which played a role in cell wall loosening [55]. The expansin-like genes were necessary for Phytophthora to colonize plants [56]. Cellulose synthase genes have been identified in Phytophthora genomes and were required for appressorium formation and host penetration [57]. The genes encoding catalytic subunits of cellulose synthase were also downregulated or expressed at a lower level in infected KD compared to those in infected PM or MT. The impairment and downregulation of cellulose synthase genes have been shown to enhance plant resistance to soil-borne bacteria and necrotrophic or hemibiotrophic fungal pathogens [58, 59]. The downregulation of these genes might be one of the mechanisms for blocking P. palmivora progression in KD.

Another group of genes regulated in response to pathogens was involved in the metabolism of lignin, pectin, and hemicellulose, which were crucial for disease resistance in plants [60]. Several of these genes were implicated in the lignin and phenylpropanoid metabolic processes. Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway and cinnamoyl-CoA reductase (CCR1) encoding gene were shown to be important for the resistance of pepper roots to P. capsica [61]. CCR1 was shown to increase rapeseed resistance to fungi by increasing lignin content and reducing disease lesions [62]. Similarly, laccases were shown to contribute to the resistance of apple roots to Pythium ultimum oomycete [63]. For pectin metabolism, the genes that encoded proteins with a pectinesterase inhibitor domain were downregulated in the infected KD but upregulated in the infected PM and MT. Pectinesterase inhibitors (also known as pectin methylesterase inhibitor, PMEI) have been reported to be important for the resistance of Arabidopsis and pepper against fungal infection [64, 65]. Inhibition of methyl group removal from homogalacturonan by pectin methylesterase during pectin incorporation into the cell wall could enhance plant resistance against pectinase released by pathogens [60]. For hemicellulose, the gene that encoded mannan endo-1,4-β-mannosidase was upregulated in infected MT but downregulated in infected KD. This enzyme was reported to be involved in tobacco resistance against P. nicotianae [66]. The lower resistance level of KD compared to the other two cultivars might be linked to the downregulation or low expression of these genes.

At 48 hpi, the expression levels of KCS6, LACS1, MAH1, CER3, and ABCG11 encoding genes were significantly higher in PM than in KD. The expressions of these genes gradually decreased from 8 to 48 hpi in the susceptible KD cultivar. These genes, which were found co-expressed in this study, were involved in wax biosynthesis and transport [67]. The SNL6 encoding gene, found co-expressed with those genes, has been shown to play a role in lignin biosynthesis, pathogenesis-related genes expression, and rice resistance to Xanthomonas oryzae pathogen [68]. Collectively, all these genes could enhance plant structural reinforcement against Phytophthora infection [69]. The different expression levels in PM and KD suggested that these genes contribute to durian resistance to Phytophthora infection.

Based on these results, we speculated that KD had the downregulation of some cell wall-related genes to limit the primary growth of P. palmivora. However, the downregulation of other cell wall-related genes, especially those involved in lignin, pectin, and hemicellulose metabolisms, might adversely affect its resistance ability. Altogether, the low resistance level of KD seemed to be linked to the downregulation of genes involved in lignin, pectin, and hemicellulose metabolisms and the low expression of defense genes.

The model of Phytophthora resistance mechanisms in durian

The findings of this study elucidated the defense mechanisms employed by durians, particularly the resistant PM cultivar, in response to P. palmivora infection. Based on the findings, we proposed a model of Phytophthora resistance mechanisms in durians, which encompassed the sequential activation of pathogen recognition, signal transduction, defense gene expression, and structural reinforcement (Fig. 6). The model highlighted the key genes involved in each of these steps.

Initially, Phytophthora signals were recognized by specific receptors, particularly the chitin detectors LYK3 and LYK4. The involvement of other receptors, such as Xa21, LRK10, FLS2, EIX2, LECRKS4, FERONIA, and Cf-9 homologs, suggested a sophisticated network of pathogen recognition that enabled the PM cultivar to promptly detect and effectively respond to pathogenic signals. Following pathogen recognition, several genes were involved in the activation of complex signal transduction pathways. The expression of genes coding for proteins such as PBL19, PR5K, RBOHA, PICBP, CML27, PATL1, MLP28, and CDPK21 could play a role in these pathways. Additionally, HSPs, including HSP70, HSP83, HSP90, BiP, ERdj3B, and Grp94, along with HSFs such as HSFA2 and HSF30, contributed to signal transduction and Phytophthora resistance by maintaining the proper folding and functioning of PRRs and resistance proteins. The high-level expression of genes encoding stress response TFs, such as WRKY40, ZAT10-like, PTI5, ERF1A, and ERF105, in the PM cultivar suggests transcriptional reprogramming after signal transduction. The activation of defense gene expression was the next critical step in the resistance mechanism. The resistance proteins, including RUN1, RPV1, and RGA4, played pivotal roles in triggering hypersensitive responses and other immune reactions that limited pathogen spread. Additionally, the structural reinforcement of the plant cell wall and cuticle served as a physical barrier against pathogen invasion. The upregulation of genes encoding proteins such as LAC4, LAC17, COMT, CAD, SNL6, and PAL in PM indicates the role of lignin biosynthesis in fortifying the cell wall in resistant durians. Similarly, the upregulation of cellulose synthase genes (CESA4, 6, 7, 8 encoding genes) and hemicellulose biosynthesis genes (GXM1 encoding gene) further contributed to the structural reinforcement of the cell wall. The regulation of pectin metabolism, as evidenced by the upregulation of PAGR and PMEI encoding genes, preserved cell wall integrity during pathogen attack. Moreover, the expression of genes encoding KCS6, LACS1, MAH1, CER3, and ABCG11, which were involved in producing and transporting cuticular wax, could contribute to the formation of a protective barrier on the plant surface.

Conclusions

In this study, we investigated the early gene expression changes in response to P. palmivora of three durian cultivars. The selected durian cultivars had different genetic backgrounds and resistance levels. PM was the most resistant, followed by MT and KD, respectively. We investigated early gene expression responses in durians and found significant changes at 8 hpi in the susceptible MT cultivar and at 24 hpi in the resistant PM cultivar. In all cultivars, the total number of DEGs reduced to a minimum at 48 hpi. The upregulation of genes encoding HSPs was the core gene expression in response to P. palmivora infection in all durians. The high resistance of PM was found to be associated with the high expression of genes encoding HSPs, defense proteins, and chitin response proteins. Most genes were upregulated in MT, even though this cultivar had a lower resistance level than PM did. This is because the basal expression level of several genes was lower in MT than in PM. The least resistant phenotype of KD appeared to be linked to the low expression of defense genes and the downregulation of genes involved in cell wall organization or biogenesis. Our findings indicated that the upregulation of genes, such as photosynthetic genes and Phytophthora resistance genes observed in the MT cultivar, may not be a universal indicator of P. palmivora resistance. Conversely, the upregulation of genes involved in chitin response in the PM cultivar and the downregulation of genes encoding cell wall proteins in the KD cultivar appeared to be specific responses to P. palmivora. We proposed a model illustrating that the resistance mechanism of durians to P. palmivora involved a combination of pre-primed and specific pathogen recognition, particularly chitin recognition, coupled with robust signaling pathways, effective stabilization of PRRs and resistance proteins via HSPs, and strong structural defenses. The high-level expression of several defense-related genes in PM prior to the infection suggested that the ability of a durian to prevent the pathogen from establishing in its tissues is a key in Phytophthora resistance. Given the lack of comprehensive gene expression data on how durian perceives and initiates its response to P. palmivora, our findings significantly enhance the understanding of the interaction between this economically significant plant and one of its most destructive pathogens.
