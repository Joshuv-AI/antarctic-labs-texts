Liver fat quantification using deep silicon photon-counting CT: an in silico imaging study
Authors: Raj Kumar Panta, Zhye Yin, Fredrik Grönberg, Benjamin Wildman-Tobriner, Mridul Bhattarai, Ehsan Abadi, Paul Segars, Ehsan Samei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate liver fat quantification is essential for early diagnosis and effective management of fatty liver disease.PurposeTo investigate the potential clinical utility of a deep silicon-based photon-counting CT (dSi-PCCT), currently in development, for liver fat quantification using human models in an in silico imaging study.Materials and methodsdSi-PCCT is a cutting-edge photon-counting CT (GE HealthCare), with several investigational systems installed globally, used under IRB approval for imaging animals and human volunteers to support FDA clearance. We developed a dSi-PCCT simulator and benchmarked its imaging performance with respect to a prototype. We imaged a computational Gammex phantom with fat fractions (FF) ranging from 0% to 100%, along with five XCAT human models with liver FF ranging from 1% to 50%, using an abdominal CT protocol. The resulting spectral sinograms were processed using a material decomposition (MD) technique. We calculated HU-based Proton Density Fat Fraction (PDFF) from single-energy images in XCAT models and compared it against the MD-derived FF. The MD-derived FF of both datasets was assessed against the digitally defined ground truth values.ResultsWe observed a strong correlation (R2 = 0.98) between MD-derived, HU-based PDFF, and ground-truth FF in a Gammex and XCAT models. There was no statistically significant difference (P = .52) in FF quantification accuracy between Gammex and the XCAT human models. The root mean square errors were 4.7% for Gammex and 2.7% for XCAT. Bland–Altman analysis further confirmed good agreement between the ground truth and MD-derived FF, with differences in FF ranging from −6.9% to 7% for Gammex and −3.0% to 37.6% for XCAT.ConclusionThe results indicate that dSi-PCCT could enable accurate liver fat quantification across a wide range of FFs in multiple objects. These findings suggest that the potential utility of dSi-PCCT for accurate liver fat assessment should be explored in vivo.Keywords: liver fat quantification, photon-counting CT, in silico imaging study

Introduction

Hepatic steatosis, a pathological condition characterized by elevated liver fat content, is a growing global health concern driven by obesity, metabolic syndrome, diabetes, alcohol consumption, and viral infections.1,2 In the absence of specific causes such as alcohol abuse, steatogenic medications, or viral infections, the condition is classified as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)—the most common form of chronic liver disease1,3 affecting approximately 25% of the global population, with the highest prevalence reported in the Middle East (32%), South America (30%), North America (24%), and Asia (27%).1 NAFLD is strongly linked to metabolic syndrome and increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality,2 highlighting the urgent need for early and accurate liver fat quantification.4

While liver biopsy remains the existing diagnostic gold standard, its invasiveness, cost, and sampling variability limit its widespread use.5–7 Noninvasive imaging alternatives, particularly ultrasound, CT, and MRI are increasingly explored, but each has trade-offs. Ultrasound, though accessible and radiation-free, suffers from reduced accuracy and sensitivity in high-risk populations due to operator dependence and body habitus limitations.5 While MRI, particularly proton density fat fraction (PDFF) techniques, is considered the gold standard for liver fat quantification,8 but its use is limited by longer scan times, motion sensitivity, and accessibility challenges associated with the patient size and claustrophobia.2,8–10 Qualitative MRI methods lack quantitative reliability due to confounding factors like T1 bias and fat spectral complexity.2

Among noninvasive alternatives, CT-based methods show particular promise. Non-contrast CT, including single- and dual-energy CT (DE-CT), also enables fat quantification by detecting reduced HU values with increasing fat, showing good correlation with MRI-PDFF.12–14 However, HU accuracy is affected by scanner variability, patient factors, and contrast use, with limited sensitivity for early or mild steatosis.12,13 DE-CT enables fat quantification via material decomposition (MD), but its accuracy is limited by low spectral resolution and overlapping energy spectra. The similar attenuation of fat and water at two X-ray energies hampers separation, although DE-CT is useful for detecting confounding factors like iodine or iron overload.15–17

Recent studies18,19 have demonstrated that photon-counting CT (PCCT) using multiple energy thresholds can accurately quantify liver fat in material decomposed images, comparable to MRI, even in obese patients. Unlike MRI, PCCT is faster, better tolerated, and integrates seamlessly into existing CT workflows, making it a promising clinical alternative. Deep silicon-based PCCT (dSi-PCCT) from GE HealthCare (Waukesha, WI), with its low atomic number (Z = 14), enhances spectral fidelity, and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) by utilizing low-energy photons (5–30 keV),20 may enable accurate fat quantification. Given their potential for enhanced spatial and spectral resolution, along with more accurate MD enabled by reduced spectral interference, these systems represent a major advancement in clinical imaging.

While dSi-PCCT remains in the prototype stage with several investigational systems installed globally and used under IRB approval for imaging small animals and human volunteers to support FDA approval, in silico imaging studies play a crucial role in evaluating its clinical potential before widespread implementation. These studies provide a controlled environment for optimizing imaging parameters, assessing performance across diverse patient anatomies, and comparing with existing modalities, all while avoiding the logistical and ethical challenges of human trials.21

This study was designed to evaluate the feasibility of a deep silicon-based photon-counting CT (dSi-PCCT) for liver fat quantification using an in silico imaging framework. A previously developed and validated dSi-PCCT simulator was used to model the imaging process, incorporating key system characteristics such as spatial resolution, noise performance, and spectral response.22 Geometric phantoms and Extended cardiac-torso (XCAT) human models with varying fat fractions (FF) were imaged, and the results were compared against digitally defined ground-truth values to assess accuracy. This study provides a preliminary assessment of dSi-PCCT’s potential for liver fat quantification before widespread clinical implementation.

Materials and methods

Scanner technology

The prototype deep silicon-based PCCT scanners (dSi-PCCT) from GE HealthCare (Waukesha, WI) are currently deployed at selected research sites. These systems are actively collecting small animal and human volunteer images under IRB-approved protocols to support future regulatory clearance.

In silico imaging framework

This study employed a previously developed and validated in silico imaging framework22 to replicate real-world clinical imaging workflow of the dSi-PCCT prototype system. The imaging framework was used to scan both the phantoms and XCAT human models under identical image acquisition conditions using an abdominal CT protocol. Single-energy X-ray attenuation sinogram and material sinograms (water and fat) generated using MD were subsequently reconstructed using the manufacturer-provided CT reconstruction algorithm. The resulting single-energy and fat images were analyzed, with the MD-derived fat map used to evaluate the accuracy of FF quantification, and to demonstrate the clinical utility of the simulator in realistic imaging scenarios. The following sections outline the various components of the imaging framework utilized in this study.

Computational models

Geometrical phantoms

We developed a computational model of a commercially available Gammex phantom, composed of solid water-equivalent material (mass density: 1.021 mg/cm³) with in-plane dimensions of 40 cm × 30 cm. The phantom contained 15 inserts with ground-truth FF ranging from 1% to 100%, as annotated in Figure 1A.

(A) Computational geometric phantom (40 × 30 cm) with head and body sections, showing annotated fat fractions ranging from 1% to 100%. (B) Color-coded rendition of voxelized XCAT human liver model with hepatic blood vasculature, portal veins (shown in blue), and hepatic arteries (shown in red), alongside other abdominal organs. Five XCAT human models with liver fat fractions of 1%, 5%, 15%, 25%, and 50% were employed in this study.

XCAT human liver model

The XCAT human liver model23–26 used, as shown in Figure 1B, integrates anatomic, physiological, and computational components for a gross anatomical and hepatic contrast perfusion for conducting in silico studies. Anatomically, the XCAT liver model is based on detailed liver structures derived from high-resolution CT imaging data, including the hepatic vasculature and parenchyma, ensuring realistic spatial representation. Physiologically, the model incorporates contrast dynamics driven by blood flow patterns and tissue perfusion, capturing essential processes such as arterial and portal venous input and their interaction within the hepatic microenvironment.

For this study, we selected five XCAT human liver models (representing the adult male population of BMI 28) with liver FF with 1%, 5%, 15%, 25%, and 50%, and they were voxelized at an isotropic voxel dimension of 0.1 × 0.1 × 0.1 mm3, without the use of contrast agent. Physical densities and elemental compositions for various anatomical structures were defined according to the recommendations from the International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurements (ICRU).27

dSi-PCCT simulator

We developed the dSi-PCCT simulator by integrating the DukeSim28 and CatSim29 CT simulation platforms. We used DukeSim to generate primary spectral transmission CT projection images with 8 energy bins, replicating the imaging conditions of the dSi-PCCT prototype system, including scanning geometry (such as source-to-isocenter distance and object-to-detector distance), system architecture and robotics, detector column and row numbers and sizes, and bow-tie filter. We used a CT abdominal protocol with 120 kVp, 400 mAs, pitch of 1.

We used CatSim to apply a generic detector model of the dSi-PCCT system. This detector model simulates the physics of X-ray interactions with photon-counting detectors (PCDs), effectively replicating clinical CT protocol settings on the prototype system. Specifically, we employed a generic Si-PCD detector model with edge-on-irradiated geometry, consistent with the dSi-PCCT prototype system (GE HealthCare), as described in the literature.30,31

The detector model accounts for primary X-ray photon energy deposition across each energy bin, incorporating quantum detection efficiency and Monte Carlo tracking for each photon. This approach captures key physical phenomena such as X-ray fluorescence, scattering processes, and energy and spatial interference inherent to dSi-PCCT detectors. Poisson-distributed quantum noise, arising from statistical fluctuations in incident photon counts, was added to all eight energy bins, reflecting realistic irradiation conditions influenced by factors like object size and radiation dose levels.

Additionally, CatSim conditioned the data to ensure compatibility with the manufacturer-provided reconstruction engine, equivalent to a basic commercially available reconstruction system. This step enabled seamless integration and accurate replication of the clinical imaging workflow on the dSi-PCCT prototype system.

Material decomposition and reconstruction

The MD method used prior information about the spectral model of the X-ray source in open beam images and the detector model to map spectral information into material information. The MD algorithm was based on the maximum likelihood-based optimization between the observation and the model to find the material path length of a given basis material.31 The detector response, which represents how each energy bin reacts to different materials, was determined using a multi-material calibration phantom as described in previous studies.22,32,33

We applied the MD on pixel-by-pixel basis on sinogram space and the resulting water and fat sinograms were reconstructed into material-specific CT images using the manufacturer-provided reconstruction engine, based on a filtered back-projection algorithm. Single-energy gray-scale polychromatic images were also generated by integrating photon counts from all energy bins without energy weighting and reconstructing them using filtered back-projection algorithm.

Data analysis

Single-energy images of Gammex and XCAT were analyzed to assess the correlation between HU values and digitally defined ground-truth FF across various regions of interest (ROIs). MD-derived fat images were analyzed further by extracting the estimated FF values from the same ROIs and comparing them to ground-truth FF using linear regression. Multiple ROIs (three ROIs in both the left and right lobes) were sampled, and the ensemble mean of their FF was used for further analysis.

To compare MD-derived FF with a conventional liver fat quantification method, linear regression was performed using HU-based PDFF values extracted from XCAT models. PDFF was calculated from non-contrast single-energy images using the following literature-reported equation.8

Bland–Altman plots were generated to evaluate systematic bias and identify outliers, using 95% limits of agreement (±1.96 SDs). A statistical t-test was performed to determine whether there was a significant difference in FF estimation between the geometric phantom and the XCAT human models. Additionally, root mean square errors (RMSE) were calculated to quantify the estimation error in FF for both datasets. All data analysis was conducted using MATLAB (R2023b, The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA).

Discussions

Our study evaluated the potential clinical utility of dSi-PCCT for liver fat quantification, examining a wide range of FFs, from 0% to 100% in the geometric phantom and 1% to 50% in human models. This is the first kind of study to demonstrate the potential clinical utility of low atomic number-based dSi-PCCT for liver fat quantification, leveraging realistic human models and advanced in silico simulations.

Recent studies18,19 highlight the potential of PCCT for accurate liver fat quantification. Schwartz et al.18 compared PCCT and MRI in fat quantification in phantoms and obese patients, reporting no significant differences and an average FF difference of 1.1%, suggesting improved opportunities for opportunistic liver fat screening with CT in the future. Similarly, Lin et al.19 validated a standardized CT-to-MRI fat conversion formula across diverse protocols and patient groups, showing strong agreement between PCCT-derived FF and MRI PDFF. These results are encouraging and support the clinical potential of PCCT as a reliable and versatile modality for noninvasive liver fat assessment.

Our findings show that dSi-PCCT enables accurate liver fat quantification, with a strong correlation between the estimated MD-derived, HU-based PDFF and ground-truth FFs (R2 = 0.98) and low RMSE (of 4.7% for the Gammex phantom and 2.7% for XCAT models). The discrepancy between the MD-derived FF and ground-truth was quantified using RMSE. While RMSE is sensitive to outliers—as it squares errors before averaging, it remains a widely used conservative metric to assess quantification accuracy. While dSi-PCCT remains in its early developmental phase, further refinements to the technology are expected to enhance its accuracy (lower RMSE) in fat quantification. Continued validation studies will be essential as technology evolves. Nevertheless, the present findings underscore the promising potential of dSi-PCCT for addressing a growing clinical need in fatty liver disease, where surveillance with accurate fat quantification is critical for timely intervention in metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular disease.4 For clinical context, this level of accuracy would allow confident differentiation between normal (<5%), mild (5%–33%), and moderate-severe (>33%) hepatic steatosis.

To compare our MD-derived FF with the conventional approach, we used a previously published4 relationship between HU values and PDFF. The HU-based PDFF calculated from single-energy CT images of the XCAT models had an excellent correlation (R2 = 0.98) with MD-derived FF, as shown in Figure 5C. While MD-derived FF takes advantage of the spectral fidelity, HU-based PDFF relies solely on single-energy attenuation, both serving as different surrogates for liver fat quantification. However, the diagnostic utility of HU-based fat quantification remains limited due to reasons described previously. In contrast, PCCT-based fat quantification may offer a more accurate and clinically scalable solution for liver fat assessment.

Our results show that non-contrast PCCT HU values correlate strongly with liver fat content, achieving R2 values of 0.94 and 1.0 in the Gammex and XCAT models, respectively. These findings are consistent with previous studies.2,4,11 The lower R2 value observed in the Gammex phantom compared to XCAT is likely attributable to beam-hardening effects across its two concentric insert rings (inner and outer) and a broader range of FF (0%–100%). In contrast, XCAT ROIs were sampled from identical spatial locations with comparable beam-hardening effects, and it has a narrower range of FF (1%–50%).

A well-known limitation of HU-based fat quantification is that its accuracy and precision are influenced by scanner calibration, patient size, imaging artifacts, and the presence of contrast material, which can lead to inconsistent results. Additionally, CT-based liver fat assessment is susceptible to confounding from hepatic iron overload. Elevated iron content in the liver can increase the HU, potentially masking underlying steatosis and leading to misdiagnosis.4

The key advantage of dSi-PCCT is enhanced spectral and spatial resolution, along with reduced spectral interference (such as X-ray fluorescence-related artifacts) owing to its low-atomic number detector material compared to high-atomic number-based PCCT detectors.20 With its ability to measure low energy photons with high spectral fidelity in the 5–30 keV range, dSi-PCCT may improve CNR for improved MD. The adoption of monochromatic imaging based on spectral imaging has led PCCT to offer higher CNR and lower noise compared to conventional CT. These features position dSi-PCCT as a promising tool for opportunistic screening and could significantly enhance routine CT imaging in at-risk populations with a high incidence of liver fat. As FF mapping gains traction for surveillance of these groups. Considerations beyond accuracy, such as accessibility, low radiation dose, and suitability for repeated longitudinal assessments, become increasingly vital. dSi-PCCT may offer a scalable, safe, and clinically practical solution for routine liver fat quantification.

This study uses a cost-effective in silico imaging framework to assess the clinical potential of a dSi-PCCT system currently under active development. By imaging both a standard geometric phantom (Gammex) and anatomically realistic XCAT human models, using a previously validated dSi-PCCT simulator under a standard clinical abdominal CT protocol, this approach ensures both reproducibility under controlled conditions and clinical relevance. A key advantage of this in silico study is its ability to systematically evaluate a wide range of FFs across both geometric and human models, capturing population-level variability that would be challenging to achieve in clinical studies without a large patient cohort. However, the computational workload is intensive—particularly due to the MD process. The exact simulation time varies depending on the load and resource allocation within our high-performance computing cluster. This practical consideration is important for those planning and budgeting similar virtual imaging trials.

This exploratory study has some limitations. Prototype systems for direct validation of liver fat quantification are not yet available, and real-world imaging factors such as patient motion and respiratory motion were not modeled. Importantly, we evaluated only one body type (BMI 28). Future studies should validate performance across diverse body habitus, particularly in obese patients who represent the highest-risk population. Further, the study only evaluated one type of photon-counting technology. As PCCT technology, and particularly the dSi-PCCT technology, continues to evolve, there is a need to evaluate their hardware and image processing software (such as MD and reconstruction); processing optimizations can further refine the PCCT performance in liver fat quantification. Future extensions could include contrast-enhanced liver models, motion effects, varied imaging parameters (eg, dose, kVp), and low-dose protocol optimization for screening applications. Ongoing IRB-approved investigations using dSi-PCCT prototypes in animal and human imaging are expected to provide essential data for future clinical translation and regulatory approval.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates a novel in silico approach to evaluate the potential of using dSi-PCCT for liver fat quantification. The results demonstrate strong correlations between MD-derived, HU-based PDFF, and ground-truth FF across a wide range of FF in both geometric phantoms and computational human models. While these findings underscore the technical promise of dSi-PCCT for liver fat quantification, its clinical utility remains to be established. Future studies are warranted to validate its performance in vivo across diverse patient cohorts and imaging conditions that include abdominal organ motion and a range of body habitus.
