Development and psychometric validation of a first aid health literacy assessment questionnaire for older adults
Authors: Fatemeh Nasrolahi, Mohsen Adib-Hajbaghery, Mohammad Zare, Azade Safa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the critical role of health literacy in emergency management, no specific questionnaire exists to measure first aid health literacy in the older population. To address this gap, the present study aimed to develop and psychometrically evaluate a first aid health literacy assessment questionnaire for older adults in 2024–2025.MethodsThis methodological study focused on questionnaire development and psychometric evaluation based on the framework proposed by Boateng et al. Initial questionnaire items were generated through an extensive literature review. Content validity and face validity were assessed using quantitative and qualitative methods. A multi-stage random sampling method was employed to recruit 400 eligible older adults who were covered by comprehensive health centers affiliated with Kashan University of Medical Sciences. All participants completed a background information questionnaire, the Persian version of the Abbreviated Mental Test (AMT), and the Health Literacy Instrument for Adults-Short Form (HELIA-SF). Construct validity was evaluated using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and convergent validity. Internal consistency was assessed using the Kuder-Richardson Formula 21 (KR-21), test-retest reliability was measured via the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC), and stability was determined using the standard error of measurement (SEM) and the minimal detectable change (MDC). Ceiling and floor effects were also examined. Data analysis was performed using JASP version 0.19.3.ResultsThe content validity ratio (CVR) and content validity index (CVI) for all items exceeded 0.8 and 0.9, respectively, and the modified kappa coefficient was greater than 0.89. Item impacts were all above 4.5. PCA extracted three components, collectively accounting for 58.2% of the variance in the total score. Results revealed a significant positive correlation between the first aid health literacy assessment questionnaire for older adults and the HELIA-SF (p < 0.001). Reliability indices were as follows: KR-21 = 0.704, ICC = 0.955, SEM = 0.707, and MDC = 2.331. No ceiling or floor effects were observed.ConclusionThe first aid health literacy assessment questionnaire for older adults demonstrates good validity and reliability. This questionnaire can effectively measure first aid health literacy levels in the older population and can design targeted educational interventions to improve their health outcomes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: First aid, Psychometric, Health literacy, Aged

Background

Every year, millions of people worldwide require emergency medical services due to various accidents, resulting in over five million mortalities [1]. Among vulnerable populations, older adults are particularly at risk due to the high prevalence of chronic diseases, cognitive and balance impairments, musculoskeletal weakness, sensory disorders, and side effects from medications. These factors increase their susceptibility to severe injuries such as falls, fractures, heart attacks, burns, poisoning, and choking [2–4]. Such injuries not only require emergency care but also impose substantial costs on healthcare systems [5].

Research indicates that falls, burns, trauma, and poisoning are the most common types of accidents experienced by older adults, especially within the home environment [1, 6, 7]. Many older adults, particularly those in “empty nest” situations (living alone or with a spouse of similar age), often rely on themselves or their partners during emergencies [4]. In these situations, adequate health literacy and the ability to respond effectively can significantly reduce injury severity or even save lives [1].

First aid health literacy refers to the ability to acquire, understand, evaluate, and apply health information to respond effectively in emergencies. This skill is vital not only for self-care and prevention but also as a social responsibility [8]. The International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies emphasized the role of older adults in recognizing warning signs and administering first aid through its “First Aid and Aging of Population” campaign in 2015 [8]. The World Health Organization also recognizes health literacy as a key determinant of individual health outcomes [9]. Evidence shows that low health literacy levels are associated with adverse consequences, including increased healthcare services and diminished emergency response effectiveness [1, 9, 10].

Research indicates moderate awareness about first aid in countries such as Saudi Arabia [11], Scotland [12], and Spain [6]. Unfortunately, in Iran, most research in this area has focused on specific groups such as teachers, with very limited data available concerning older adults [13]. For example, a 2019 study on teachers in Kashan revealed that approximately 60% possessed average knowledge of first aid; however, their understanding of initial responses to accidents, such as fractures or electric shocks, was notably low [13].

Review of existing first aid health literacy assessment questionnaires reveals that although several have been developed for different contexts, most suffer from significant limitations in terms of target populations, content, conceptual framework, and cultural validity. Notably, none has been specifically developed to assess first aid health literacy among older adults comprehensively. For instance, Ganfure et al. in Ethiopia developed a questionnaire to evaluate kindergarten teachers’ knowledge of first aid, primarily focusing on scenarios involving children and educational settings, with little attention to the physiological needs and critical situations common among older adults [14]. Similarly, Dolenc et al. in Slovenia developed a questionnaire addressing the first aid needs of older adults. However, its focus was on chronic diseases and daily care skills rather than accidents, such as falls or burns [4]. Bakke et al. designed a questionnaire based on two CPR scenarios; however, it did not cover other common emergencies faced by older adults, such as poisoning or fractures [10].

Furthermore, the questionnaires used in certain domestic studies are often limited in content. Many of these questionnaires are tailored to specific populations, such as teachers [13], nursing students, or healthcare workers [15–17]. The conceptual frameworks underlying these questionnaires focus primarily on measuring “knowledge” or “attitude” related to specific events such as burns or heart attacks, thereby overlooking the multidimensional aspects of health literacy. It is important to develop a comprehensive, culturally appropriate questionnaire specifically for older adults that accurately assesses their health literacy in relation to common accidents [18]. These international and national findings emphasize the need for a specific first aid health literacy questionnaire tailored to older adults that captures age-specific emergency profiles (such as falls, burns, and poisoning), takes into account cognitive and sensory limitations that are common in older age, and incorporates culturally specific responses.

Given the rising trend of population aging, the high prevalence of accidents and injuries among older adults, and the absence of a specific, culturally congruent questionnaire to assess first aid health literacy, this study aimed to develop and psychometrically evaluate such a questionnaire. The goal is to introduce a comprehensive and suitable questionnaire for measuring first aid health literacy in older adults. Additionally, this questionnaire could serve as a foundation for assessing needs, designing targeted training, and enhancing the preparedness of older adults to respond effectively to accidents.

Methods

This methodological research focused on the development and psychometric evaluation of questionnaires. The research phases were conducted in accordance with the framework proposed by Boateng et al. (2018), which includes the phases of item development, questionnaire development, and evaluation [19]. The study was conducted in Kashan from 2024 to 2025.

Phase 1: item development

Identification of domain and item generation

A systematic review of the scientific literature was conducted using an operational definition of first aid health literacy related to older adults, focusing on common physical traumas such as falls, burns, injuries, and poisoning. Resources were searched across international databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Cochrane, and Google Scholar) and Persian databases (Magiran, SID, and IranDoc) using keywords, including “first aid”, “older adults”, “health literacy”, and “questionnaire”, and their synonyms. After analyzing the findings, an initial set of items and an item blueprint were developed.

Content validity was assessed both qualitatively and quantitatively through the participation of 10 experts in nursing, geriatrics, and trauma, all of whom were familiar with psychometric processes. To qualitatively evaluate content validity, experts provided feedback on the clarity, simplicity, grammar, scoring, and relevance of the content of each item [20]. To quantitatively evaluate content validity, the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) was calculated and interpreted according to Lawshe’s table (>0.79) [21], and the Content Validity Index (CVI) was computed following Waltz and Bausell’s criteria (>0.62) [22]. The Modified Kappa Statistic was calculated and interpreted based on Polit and Beck’s guidelines (>0.6) [23].

Phase 2: questionnaire development

Pre-testing of questions (face validity)

Face validity was evaluated both qualitatively and quantitatively with the participation of 10 older adults of varying ages, genders, and education levels. To qualitatively evaluate face validity, participants provided feedback on the clarity and comprehensibility of the items [24]. To quantitatively evaluate face validity, the item impact was calculated using a 5-point Likert questionnaire. Item impacts above 1.5 were considered acceptable [25].

Sampling and survey administration

The target population consisted of all older adults aged 65 and older who were registered with the comprehensive urban health centers in Kashan, Iran. The expert recommendations that a minimum sample size of 400 is sufficient for ensuring validity in psychometric studies [24]. As such, a multi-stage random sample of 400 older adults was obtained from comprehensive urban health centers. Inclusion criteria were age 65 years or older, Iranian citizenship, residency in Kashan, no history of mental illness (confirmed via medical records and self-report), no cognitive impairment (score of 8 or higher on the AMT), ability to communicate verbally, and informed consent to participate. The exclusion criterion was incomplete questionnaires.

Following the initial development and validity assessment of the questionnaire, an official introduction letter for sampling was obtained from the Vice Chancellor for Research and Technology at Kashan University of Medical Sciences. The first author conducted the multi-stage sampling between August 2024 and January 2025. Kashan was divided into three socioeconomic strata (upper, middle, and lower). Four health centers were randomly selected from each stratum.

Based on the sample size and the older population covered by each health center, eligible older adults were randomly selected from the Integrated Health System (SIB). If an older adult declined participation, another was randomly selected from the same stratum until the desired sample size was reached. Data collection questionnaires included a background information questionnaire, the Persian version of the Abbreviated Mental Test (AMT), and the Health Literacy Instrument for Adults-Short Form (HELIA-SF). These data were collected from the records of older adults in the SIB and through direct interviews conducted at the health centers.

The background questionnaire collected information such as age, gender, education level, marital status, job, economic status, living arrangement, and presence of comorbidities. The qualitative content validity of this questionnaire was confirmed by 10 faculty members of Kashan University of Medical Sciences.

The Persian version of AMT was introduced in Iran by Bakhtiyari et al. (2014). This test consists of 10 items that assess key areas, including important dates, time and year, notable personalities, occupation, place names, counting, and addressing. Each item is scored either zero or one, with a total score of 8 or higher indicating the absence of cognitive disorders. The Persian version of the test demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.76 and an intra-class correlation coefficient of 0.89 [26]. This test has been used as one of the inclusion criteria for cognitive impairment screening in our study.

The HELIA-SF was also adapted in Iran by Tavousi et al. (2022) to measure adult health literacy. It consists of two domains: Basic Skills (5 items) and Decision-Making Skills (4 items). Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert questionnaire, with higher scores indicating greater health literacy. The questionnaire’s face, content, and construct validity have been confirmed. Its reliability was reported with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.91 and an intra-class correlation of 0.81 [27]. In our study, the HELIA-SF was used to examine the convergent validity of the first aid health literacy assessment questionnaire for older adults, with a reported reliability of 0.79.

Item reduction

The correlation coefficients between each item and the total questionnaire, as well as between items, were calculated. Items with correlation coefficients below 0.30 or above 0.70 were considered candidates for deletion [24, 28].

Extraction of factors

The adequacy of the sample was evaluated using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. Due to the binary nature of the response options (true/false), a polychoric correlation matrix was utilized. Principal component analysis (PCA) served as a preliminary item-reduction procedure to summarize the total variance, employing equamax rotation. The number of components was determined based on eigenvalues greater than one, the scree plot, and parallel analysis. A minimum acceptable component loading of 0.40 was established.

Phase 3: questionnaire evaluation

Test of dimensionality

This assessment was conducted using convergent validity [24]. All participants completed the HELIA-SF [27] concurrently to serve as a comparative measure against the first aid health literacy assessment questionnaire for older adults, which focuses on a more specific domain. The Pearson correlation coefficient was calculated to examine convergent validity with HELIA-SF.

Tests of reliability

The reliability of the questionnaire was evaluated through assessments of internal consistency and stability. Internal consistency was determined using the Kuder-Richardson coefficient (KR-21), with coefficients exceeding 0.70 deemed acceptable. It is important to note that, since KR-21 assumes a roughly equal level of item difficulty, the true reliability may be lower than reported and could be underestimated if item difficulties vary. This standard aligns with initial validation processes for brief dichotomous questionnaires [29]. To assess test-retest reliability, 20 older adults completed the questionnaire on two separate occasions, with a two-week interval between tests. The absolute-agreement intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated using a two-way random model, with ICC values of 0.60 or greater considered satisfactory [30]. Absolute stability was evaluated using the standard error of measurement (SEM) and the minimal detectable change (MDC), taking into account the number of items and the response format.

Tests of validity

Ceiling and floor effects were examined by analyzing the distribution of scores. If more than 15% of participants obtained the maximum or minimum possible score, a ceiling or floor effect was considered [31].

Data analysis

All analyses were performed using JASP version 0.19.3. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using skewness and kurtosis indices, with values within the range of ± 2 indicating a normal distribution. A significance level of p < 0.05 was used in all statistical tests.

Ethical considerations

The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee (Code of Ethics IR.KAUMS.NUHEPM.REC.1403.011, dated May 5, 2024) and the Research Council (Research Code 403016) of Kashan University of Medical Sciences. The researchers adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were informed of the research objectives, the confidentiality of information, and the voluntary nature of their participation, and written informed consent was obtained from each of them.

Results

Phase 1: item development

After a comprehensive review of relevant literature, the initial draft of the first aid health literacy assessment questionnaire for older adults was developed with 21 items. The original version utilized a four-choice response format, consisting of one “True” option, two “False” options, and one “Don’t know” option. This structure aimed to discourage random guessing while minimizing the response burden. However, beginning with step 5 of the psychometric evaluation, the response format was modified to a true/false system to enhance analysis. Specifically, for scoring and all psychometric analyses, responses were dichotomized (Correct = 1; Incorrect or Don’t know = 0) to ensure comparability across items and to align with standard dichotomous psychometric procedures. This approach treats “Don’t know” as an absence of correct knowledge rather than a separate latent category, helping to reduce false-positive inflation from guessing and maintaining the integrity of dichotomous indices and item-level analyses.

In qualitative content validity assessment, 10 experts revised some items for clarity. For example, the item “What is the most important action after a blow to the neck?” was rephrased to “What do you do first if you see someone’s neck being hit?” The quantitative content validity results indicated that the CVR, CVI, and the modified kappa statistic were all above 0.80, 0.90, and 0.89, respectively. Additionally, the content validity of the overall questionnaire was 0.95, as determined by the S-CVI/Ave.

Phase 2: questionnaire development

In the face validity assessment, 10 older adults participated in the pre-test. Based on their qualitative feedback, no changes were made to the items. Additionally, the item impacts ranged from 4.5 to 5, indicating the maximum score achievable.

A total of 421 older adults were initially examined; however, 21 were excluded due to incomplete questionnaires. Finally, data from 400 eligible older adults were analyzed, resulting in a response rate of 94.9%. The mean age of the participants was 66.26 ± 5.51 years. Most of the participants were female (51%), married (78.5%), retired (41.8%), and had a lower or upper secondary education (60.5%). Additional demographic characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Phase 3: questionnaire evaluation

To assess the convergent validity of the questionnaire, the correlation between the questionnaire’s score and the HELIA-SF’s score was examined. The mean score on the developed questionnaire was 7.927 ± 3.322 (range: 0–15), and the mean score on the HELIA-SF was 67.316 ± 16.907 (range: 0–100). The Pearson correlation coefficient between the two questionnaires was 0.352 (p < 0.001). The internal consistency of the questionnaire, assessed using the KR-21, was 0.704. The average inter-item correlation across the entire questionnaire was 0.172, with values ranging from 0.167 to 0.394 for the extracted components. Test-retest reliability was evaluated in 20 older adults with a two-week interval, and the results showed an ICC of 0.955 (95% CI: 0.879–0.983, p < 0.0001). The SEM and the MDC were ± 0.707 and 2.331, respectively.

In assessing floor and ceiling effects, only 0.5% of participants scored the lowest possible score, and none reached the maximum score. Therefore, no significant floor or ceiling effects were observed in the questionnaire.

Discussion

The present study aimed to develop and psychometrically evaluate a first aid health literacy assessment questionnaire for older adults. The results demonstrated favorable psychometric properties of the designed questionnaire for older adults. While several studies have addressed trauma health literacy [31, 32], no specific questionnaire has been developed to suit the conditions of Iranian older adults. The initial version of the questionnaire was developed based on a thorough review of scientific resources from both international and domestic databases, as well as an analysis of local reports on the most common traumas among older adults. The initial draft consisted of 21 items.

Content validity assessments involved modifications aimed at ensuring content comprehensiveness and enhancing item clarity. Only a limited number of items were revised based on expert suggestions, reflecting the overall accuracy and relevance of the initial item development. The CVR (above 0.8) [21], CVI (above 0.90) [22], modified kappa statistic (above 0.89) [23], and item impact (above 4.5) [25] were within acceptable ranges. Considering the specific cognitive and linguistic characteristics of older adults, validating these indices is particularly important. Therefore, this questionnaire meets the necessary criteria regarding content and face validity [25, 33].

Based on the item reduction using the loop method, six items—1, 4, 8, 9, 12, and 16—were identified as either irrelevant or having low correlations with the overall questionnaire. These findings were consistent with the results of the questionnaire’s structural analysis, where removing these items contributed to achieving the desired final structure. While elements such as unconsciousness and syncope related to bleeding are clinically significant, the decisions regarding their deletion were based on empirical evidence. They did not compromise the questionnaire’s overall coverage. An expert review further established that the retained items effectively capture the recognition of life-threatening conditions and the corresponding escalation pathways; where redundancy was present, the item with higher discriminative ability was maintained. The content of the eliminated items included topics such as contacting the emergency department (item 1), initial actions in case of unconsciousness (item 4), appropriate response to nose bleeding (item 8), unconsciousness due to bleeding (item 9), joint injuries (item 12), and poisoning by toxic gases (item 16). Given the questionnaire’s primary focus on practical first aid skills, item 1 could be eliminated due to its general nature. Although items 3 and 4 covered similar concepts, removing item 4 would not compromise the questionnaire’s comprehensiveness. The exclusion of items 8, 12, and 16 may also be justified by the questionnaire’s emphasis on life-threatening conditions rather than minor injuries.

Construct validity was evaluated using PCA, which identified three components that collectively accounted for 58.2% of the total variance. Items were retained based on primary component loadings of ≥ 0.40, with cross-loadings differing by ≥ 0.30; these criteria were utilized for item selection rather than for inferring latent factors. As noted by Reio and Shuck (2015), achieving an explanation of more than 50% of the variance is deemed desirable in the structural analysis of educational and health-related instruments [34]; therefore, the results of this analysis are considered acceptable. The three components explained 22.2%, 19.8%, and 16.1% of the variance, respectively. Component labels were assigned through the integration of statistical criteria and expert panel review, grounded in principles of first aid practice and health literacy theory. The grouping of poisoning with physical trauma in Component 1 reflects a commonly used first aid protocol rather than a focus on etiology; it emphasizes rapid hazard control, initial protective measures, and prompt help-seeking. Similarly, Component 3 broadens the concept of “resuscitation” to encompass the wider stabilization responsibilities of first aid, which aim to maintain function and prevent secondary harm. This includes the management of embedded objects and ocular burns, in addition to airway and breathing support. Interpreting these components as action scripts aligned with Nutbeam’s functional, interactive, and critical dimensions enhances the clarity of their meaning without altering the empirical solution. The components were categorized as “Immediate Hazard Control & Injury/Poisoning Management”, “Recognition & Help-Seeking/Communication”, and “Physiologic Stabilization & Tissue Protection”. This classification elucidates how first aid health literacy functions in time-sensitive situations. By mapping these components, we enhance construct validity by connecting statistical groupings to established health literacy theories, thereby facilitating targeted interventions in procedural skills, navigation/communication, and decision-making. The three components obtained in this questionnaire correspond to the components of the Nutbeam model [35], such that the “Immediate Hazard Control & Injury/Poisoning Management” component is aligned with the functional literacy, the “Recognition & Help-Seeking/Communication” component represents interactive literacy, and the “Physiologic Stabilization & Tissue Protection” component is associated with critical literacy. Although several questionnaires have been developed to evaluate first aid knowledge across various populations, many lack construct validity, particularly among older adults [10, 13, 14].

The convergent validity analysis revealed a positive and significant correlation between the developed questionnaire and the HELIA-SF. Although these two questionnaires serve different purposes and target distinct populations, the content difference accounts for the observed small to moderate level of convergent validity; so that the HELIA-SF is aimed at adults and broadly addresses health literacy, while the current questionnaire specifically evaluates first aid health literacy in older adults. The HELIA-SF measures general health literacy, whereas the current questionnaire focuses solely on first aid specific literacy. As a result, the findings provide initial evidence of convergent validity and suggest that the current questionnaire captures aspects of health literacy not covered by the general health literacy assessment.

The internal consistency of the first aid health literacy questionnaire for older adults was acceptable, with a KR-21 coefficient of 0.704 [24]. Due to the small number of items in some components, the average inter-item correlation was also calculated, yielding acceptable values (ranging from 0.167 to 0.394) across all components [36]. These results support the internal consistency of the questionnaire. The internal consistency coefficients of the health literacy questionnaire in various studies have typically ranged from 0.7 to 0.9 [27, 37]; however, no specific studies have examined first aid health literacy among older adults.

The stability of the questionnaire, assessed via the ICC, was 0.955. Given that the lower bound of the ICC also falls within the acceptable range, it can be concluded that the questionnaire has good stability over time. This finding aligns with previous studies, such as those by Adib-Hajbaghery et al. (ICC = 0.95) [13] and Tavousi et al. (ICC = 0.81) [27]. Additionally, the SEM and the MDC were 0.707 and 2.331, respectively. Considering the scoring range (0 to 15) and the binary response format of the questionnaire, these values are small and acceptable.

The results indicated that the relative frequencies of participants with the minimum and maximum scores on the first aid health literacy assessment questionnaire for older adults were 0.5% and 0%, respectively. Since ceiling and floor effects are generally considered acceptable when below 15% [29], the questionnaire has no significant ceiling or floor effects. This indicates the questionnaire’s capacity to differentiate between different levels of first aid health literacy among older adults.

This research develops and evaluates a first aid health literacy assessment questionnaire specifically designed for older adults. Existing health literacy questionnaires often fail to address the unique needs of this age group, making this questionnaire a noteworthy advancement. The diverse sample used in this study enhances the questionnaire’s generalizability concerning gender, education, economic status, living arrangements, and comorbid conditions. However, it is important to note that the findings are limited to Iranian older adults and the Persian language. Therefore, validation in other languages is recommended to ensure broader applicability. The dimensionality assessment employed PCA for initial screening; however, future studies should utilize Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) to assess the latent structure. Convergent validity was evaluated using the HELIA-SF health literacy questionnaire rather than a first aid knowledge criterion. Future research should prioritize the validation of a first aid knowledge or skills assessment and explore a multitrait-multimethod approach to strengthen construct validity. Merging “Don’t know” responses with incorrect answers diminishes scoring granularity but helps prevent inflated accuracy due to guesswork, thus preserving dichotomous psychometric assumptions. In the context of diagnostic profiling studies, explicitly modeling “Don’t know” responses may prove beneficial. Future research should also explore the use of advanced techniques, such as structural equation modeling, to enhance the rigor of questionnaire validation. Also, in interpreting KR-21, we acknowledge its assumption of roughly equal item difficulty; thus, reliability estimations should be viewed with this caveat in mind, consistent with guidance for brief dichotomous questionnaires [29].

Conclusion

The first aid health literacy assessment questionnaire for older adults consists of 15 items and demonstrates strong psychometric properties. The present study confirms the questionnaire’s face, content, and convergent validity. In addition, principal component analysis identified three underlying components that collectively explain 58.2% of the total variance of the score. The questionnaire also exhibits acceptable reliability and shows no evidence of ceiling or floor effects.

In addition to its strong psychometric properties, the concise dichotomous format of this questionnaire allows for rapid screening in primary care and community health centers and facilitates the identification of older adults who could benefit from targeted first aid training. In addition, the questionnaire’s three-component structure can be used diagnostically to customize training content and ensure that training modules effectively address specific skill gaps. The questionnaire also supports program evaluation, longitudinal monitoring, and prioritization of high-risk subgroups for optimal resource allocation, such as individuals living alone or those with multimorbidity. Finally, the results can support the development of age-friendly policies and first aid curricula for older adults at the community and national levels.

In summary, the questionnaire is a valid, reliable, and feasible questionnaire for the purposes of education, evaluation of interventions, prioritization of services, and policy making.
