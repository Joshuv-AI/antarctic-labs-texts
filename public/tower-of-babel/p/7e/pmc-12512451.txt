Piloting lymphoedema management for lymphatic filariasis: experiences and perceptions of patients and stakeholders from Kwale county, coastal Kenya
Authors: Stella Kepha, Vincent Were, George Okello, Collins Okoyo, Wyckliff P Omondi, Doris W Njomo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe global program to eliminate lymphatic filariasis (GPELF) was started in 2003 with two strategies: mass drug administration (MDA) to interrupt disease transmission and the morbidity management and disability prevention (MMDP) to provide basic hygienic care to filariasis lymphoedema patients.MethodsWe conducted a mixed methods study with pre-intervention, intervention and post-intervention phases without control groups among 106 participants from two lymphatic filariasis-endemic wards of Lunga Lunga sub-county, Kwale county on the coastal Kenya between 2019 and 2021. During the intervention phase, a lymphoedema management care package was developed using participatory approaches. In the post-intervention phase, qualitative data was collected to assess perceptions of the impact of the intervention on patients’ knowledge of and access to lymphoedema management services.ResultsIn the pre-intervention phase, 86.8% of patients reported experiencing pain, swelling, or redness in their limbs, and 44.3% felt negatively about their condition. More than half (52.8%) did not know the cause of their limb swelling, and 73.6% did not use appropriate footwear. Additionally, 57.5% did not know how to care for their wounds to prevent acute attacks. Key challenges included financial constraints (39.6% spent up to 200 Ksh per visit), long distances to health facilities (24.5% lived > 5 km away), and stigma. In the post-intervention phase, participants reported improved knowledge and self-care practices, including washing, elevating, and exercising affected limbs. Community health volunteers (CHVs) were reported to play a critical role in raising awareness, identifying and referring patients, and improving health-seeking behavior, which contributed to increased service utilization and perceived improvements in quality of life.ConclusionTraining of health care providers, caregivers and patients using the developed intervention care package contributed to improved knowledge on cause and management of lymphoedema. The CHVs played a critical role in the identification and referral of patients, which contributed to improved health-care seeking behaviour and increased service utilization. Collaboration with the county health authorities contributed to improved quality of health care services provided for lymphoedema management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24303-w.Keywords: Lymphoedema, MMDP, Kenya

Introduction

The Global Programme to Eliminate Lymphatic Filariasis (GPELF) was launched over 15 years ago with the goal to interrupt transmission of Lymphatic Filariasis (LF) in endemic countries by 2020 [1]. The GPELF has two principal aims: (i) to interrupt LF transmission through an annual community-wide mass drug administration (MDA) of preventive chemotherapy to all at risk groups aged 2 years and above for 4 to 6 years [2, 3]; and (ii) to manage morbidity and prevent disability (MMDP) [2, 3]. Since the launch of the GPELF, there has been an estimated 46% reduction of the population living at risk for LF infection [4] and over 96 million LF cases cured or prevented [5, 6]. However, an estimated 40 million people worldwide have clinical manifestations of the disease, predominantly lymphoedema, elephantiasis, and hydrocele [7]. These clinical conditions contribute to an estimated 5.9 million disability-adjusted life years; are associated with significant social stigma and reduced economic productivity [8].

Most LF control programmes have mainly focused on interrupting LF transmission through mass drug administration. In 2014, only 34 out of 73 LF endemic countries had initiated MMDP service programmes [9]. The MMDP basic care package consists of individual treatment for episodes of adenolymphangitis (acute attacks), long-term skin care, psychosocial support, management of lymphoedema to prevent disease progression, and surgery for hydrocele [10]. The provision of these services requires capacity building of healthcare workers, supplies and equipment, and empowerment of communities and caregivers to appropriately care for patients. There are however many other factors that influence patient access to quality services and support [10, 11], which include low programme funds, lack of skills among providers, insufficient knowledge among patients, limited accessibility of health facilities due to disability or poverty, weak referral systems to increase access to surgeries, little to no funds to purchase medicines and supplies for basic home care, and social stigma and discrimination [12].

In Kenya, LF is endemic in the coastal counties of Kwale, Kilifi, Mombasa, Lamu, Taita Taveta and Tana River [13]. The LF elimination programme was launched in 2002 and aimed to eliminate LF as a public health problem in the country by 2020, currently extended to 2024. Mapping of LF morbidity patients has been conducted alongside with the MDA activities. Some of the strategies for eliminating LF in the country identified by the National Programme include: annual mass drug administration of recommended drugs in endemic areas; morbidity management and disability prevention, vector control, hydrocele surgery and health promotion and behaviour change communication.

In this study, we share the findings of a mixed-methods study with pre-intervention, intervention and post-intervention phases conducted as part of a large research project. The study was implemented by Kenya Medical Research Institute (KEMRI) scientists in collaboration with officers from the Kwale County department of health and the Division of Vector Borne and Neglected Tropical Diseases (DVNTD) Programme. The aim of the study was to demonstrate the impact of a comprehensive intervention care package in improving access to morbidity management and disability prevention (MMDP) services for patients with lymphoedema in coastal Kenya.

Methods

Study area

The study was conducted in Kwale County, which has four sub-counties: Lunga Lunga; Msambweni; Matuga and Kinango. Kwale County was selected based on data from the Kenya Neglected Tropical Diseases Programme that shows a high burden of lymphoedema (575) and hydrocele (401) cases compared to other counties that are endemic for lymphatic filariasis. Within Kwale county, the Programme data further showed that Lunga Lunga sub-county has the highest burden of hydrocele (240), lymphoedema (178) or both (51) compared to the other three sub-counties [14].

Study design and setting

This was a mixed methods study with pre-intervention, intervention and post-intervention phases without control groups that was conducted with lymphoedema patients, opinion leaders, community members including family members and caregivers of LF patients, community health volunteers, and health care providers. The variables of interest included: patients’ knowledge on cause of lymphoedema and patient knowledge of taking care of morbidity at home; experiences of pain and feelings about lymphoedema; barriers of access to and utilization of health care services at the pre-intervention phase; institution of the lymphoedema management programme, and were measured during the pre-intervention phase. The World Health Organization (WHO)-recommended minimum intervention care package [15] was implemented and assessed during the post-intervention phase to determine its effect on variables measured at the pre-intervention phase.

Sampling and study population

To select specific sites below the sub-county level, we examined the distribution of hospitals, dispensaries, and health centers across wards in the sub-county; and determined that a comprehensive intervention care package should be implemented and tested at all levels of the health system. Therefore, we selected Pongwe and Vanga wards in Lunga Lunga sub-county as they have the main referral hospital in the sub-county, a health center, and at least two dispensaries. Persons with visible signs of lymphoedema from Pongwe and Vanga wards were then purposively sampled from the villages of the two wards to represent a diverse range of participants, while maintaining convenience of access. Purposive sampling based on gender, age and ward of residence was further used to select participants for the qualitative component of the study.

Data collection

Pre-intervention phase

The pre-intervention phase was conducted in September to October 2019, quantitative and qualitative data were collected separately using the concurrent triangulation method to allow for comparison and divergence of views after analysis [23]. Quantitative data was collected from 106 patients with lymphoedema to; (1) assess their knowledge on the cause LF and lymphoedema; (2) understand their practices in taking care of swollen limbs; (3) document their experiences of pain and feeling about lymphoedema and; (4) explore the barriers of access to and utilization of MMDP services within the health care system. Qualitative data were collected through eight (8) FGDs (total of 96 participants) with community members stratified by age and gender. Separate IDIs were conducted with sixteen (16) opinion leaders (chiefs, village administrators/leaders, religious leaders, farmers, and business owners), eight (8) health workers and five (5) health authorities to gather more insights on barriers and facilitators of access to healthcare services.

Intervention phase

MMDP intervention activities began in February 2021 with the development of a training manual on home-based care for morbidity management and disability prevention due to lymphatic filariasis in Kenya [15]. The development of this manual was informed by the results of the formative assessment, a review of national and global guidelines on LF elimination and morbidity management, and consultations with national and sub-national health management teams. The final manual was validated in a workshop attended by stakeholders from the ministry of health, county department of health, and sub-county health management team. Training

A cascaded approach was used to train project implementing stakeholders at various levels. At the sub-national level, trained participants included public health officers, a dermatologist and project field officers. Training at the sub-national level were conducted by the study Principal Investigator with the support of national, county and sub-county Neglected Tropical Diseases (NTD) coordinators. These sub-national officers in turn trained community health volunteers who were responsible for the implementation of the MMDP programme at the community level. At the facility level, training targeted health workers involved in the provision of outpatient services, including morbidity management services. To improve the availability of commodities required for morbidity management, health facilities were supplied with antibiotics and ointments [16].

Lymphoedema patient tracing and recruitment

The first activity post training involved tracing lymphoedema patients who were part of the formative assessment conducted in 2019. This activity was conducted by the CHVs and field officers and took about 1 week. A preliminary list of patients was generated and used to register patients into the intervention activities. A total of 200 patients were registered during this exercise. Prior to data collection the Field officers were trained by the dermatologist on how to grade the lymphoedema. Each patient was visited by the field officer for recruitment who documented each patients’ socio-demographic characteristics and the type of lymphoedema (1 organ affected, 2 nature of swelling, 3, the lymphoedema was graded as mild, moderate or severe) they suffered from. Field officers assessed and ascertained the presence of lymphoedema in each of the registered patients Appendix I.

Training of LF patients

Patients who registered and agreed to be part of the study were taken through the MMDP manual using the local language, Swahili version. These trainings were conducted by field officers with the support of sub-county health managers and study PI. Patients were also provided with soap, a towel, a pair of sandals and petroleum jelly [16]. A total of 155 were enrolled into the intervention phase of the study and trained on morbidity management practices.

Follow up

Patients enrolled in the study were then followed up for 8 weeks (between April and May of 2021) to assess their adherence to the intervention. During the follow up the CHVs and/or Field officers recorded patients’ experiences related to severe attacks and frequency of adherence to management of their swollen limbs condition as trained (Appendix II).

Post-intervention phase

During the post-intervention phase, qualitative data was collected through FGDs (8 with patients’ groups, 4 with family members and 2 with CHVs) and key informant interviews with 24 stakeholders to assess experiences and perceptions of the impact of the intervention on the patients.

Data collection

FGDs were moderated by KEMRI-affiliated social scientists, with logistical and linguistic support provided by trained field assistants. These assistants, fluent in Swahili—the primary language used during data collection—documented key points and ensured comprehensive audio recordings of both FGDs and in-depth interviews (IDIs). To prepare for their roles, the field assistants completed a three-day orientation covering study objectives, qualitative interviewing skills, appropriate use of the Open Data Kit (ODK) platform, audio equipment handling, and protocols for accurate transcription and translation.

Statistical analysis

Data management and analysis

The quantitative data were transmitted wirelessly to a secure server at KEMRI Nairobi, where they were cleaned and prepared for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarize patient demographic characteristics and responses to structured survey questions. Data completeness was checked, and listwise deletion was used for missing values where applicable. Outliers were assessed through visual inspection of frequency distributions; no data points were excluded from analysis. Subgroup analyses were performed for variables such as sex, age, and ward of residence using chi-square tests for categorical variables. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All physical transcripts and notes from the qualitative interviews were securely stored in locked cabinets to safeguard confidentiality. The audio recordings were er transcribed and translated into English. Double transcription and translation and back translation was done among the investigators to ensure accuracy and minimize bias. To ensure quality control, digital copies of both quantitative and qualitative data were stored in computers with passwords, with access limited to the Principal Investigator authortity.

After transcription, the qualitative data was coded and entered into QSR NVIVO version 10 for management and analysis. The software facilitated determination of themes by running queries that allowed identification of patterns within the coded data. A framework analysis approach based on identified key themes that emerged from interviews iteratively up to saturation point was adopted with pre-determined themes [17]. The pre-determined themes included patients’ knowledge about LF and lymphoedema causes, knowledge and practices in taking care of their swollen limbs, experiences of pain and feeling about lymphoedema, distance to the health facility, ability to pay for health services and satisfaction with the health services received.

Socio-Demographic and economic characteristics

Background characteristics of the pre-intervention qualitative arm study participants

A total of 96 participants took part in 8 FGDs stratified by age and gender into adult men, adult women, youth men, and youth women. Their median age was 37.6 years. Their main economic activity was farming followed by self-employment. A majority, 62.6% of the FGDs participants had some primary level of education and a large majority, 84% were Muslims.

The 16 opinion leaders interviewed included chiefs, village administrators/leaders, religious leaders, farmers, and business owners. Their median age was 46.7 years. A majority, 80% were Muslims, 93.8% were married, and close to a half, 47.1% had never attended school.

Six of the 8 health workers interviewed were male and the duration of practice ranged from 1 to 25 years. Five of the health workers had been in practice for more than 5 years.

The five county health authorities included: Sub-County NTD Coordinator; County TB and Leprosy Coordinator; County NTD Coordinator, Sub-County TB, Leprosy and Malaria Coordinator, and the Chief Public Health Officer.

Patients’ knowledge about lymphoedema due LF

From both the IDIs and FGDs there was a mixture in levels of knowledge regarding the causes of lymphoedema. The reasons cited for swelling of limbs included witchcraft, the tsetse fly, walking on dirty water, consumption or lack of consumption of various types of food, and hereditary causes.

“Yes, it is believed to be caused by witchcraft especially among the Mijikenda community.” (IDI-PK-OL- 007).

“It is due to the food type that results to abnormal water in our body. Food like dagaa. Those foods which lack nutrients quantity (low nutrients).” (FGD-VG-YW-003).

“The community believes that these diseases are caused by witchcraft and so they go forth to seek intervention from traditional witchdoctors.” (FGD-PK-AM-002).

However, the participants in some of the FGDs did state that lymphoedema is as a result of LF which is transmitted by mosquitoes.

“It is caused by mosquitoes. There is specific time when mosquito bite you and contaminate your blood, leaving you with dirty blood.” (FGD-VG-YW-003).

Health authorities also indicated that cultural beliefs and lack of knowledge about the disease were some of the reasons for its neglect.

“For someone to seek services at the facility they have to be aware that the condition they have needs medical attention but there are those that are ignorant, who know they have a condition but assume it.” (IDI-HA-003).

“Cultural beliefs and lack of knowledge on the causes of the disease has made the community to neglect the disease as well as the ones affected by it too.” (IDI-HA-001).

Patients’ knowledge about taking care of LF morbidity/lymphoedema

The results in Table 4 show that 78 (73.6%) of the patients did not use appropriate footwear for their swollen feet. Although all the patients indicated that they were aware that washing and drying the swollen limbs helped prevent acute attacks and progression of swelling, 61 (57.6%) did not know how to take care of their wounds to prevent acute attacks and swelling progression. A large proportion were not aware that elevation and exercising of the swollen limbs helped prevent acute attacks and swelling progression: 90 (84.9%) and 103 (97.2%) respectively. Regarding awareness on how to treat the swollen limbs, all the patients knew that cooling their limbs was important. However, significant numbers − 40 (37.7%) and 73 (68.9%) respectively - were not aware that seeking care at the health facility, or resting the swollen limbs, were strategies for treating an acute attack.

Experience of pain and feelings about lymphoedema

The FGD participants expressed their feelings about caring for lymphoedema patients.

“To add on that. Like this disease of LF, it reaches a point where you cannot even look at that patient. So, the stage of the disease also matters even if you were at first giving help to that patient, but you just find you cannot do it anymore.” (FGD-VG-AW-001).

“The legs are too much swollen and fluids leaking. It takes someone who is very good hearted to take care of that patient.” (FGD-VG-AW-002).

The health workers reported that patients with lymphoedema feel stigmatized due to their condition resulting in delayed health-seeking behaviour and worsening of the condition.

“People suffering from lymphoedema perceive it to be a punishment from God and therefore they don’t feel comfortable seeking medical attention, they feel stigmatized, abandoned and discriminated.” (IDI-VG-HW-001).

“The major concern about LF patients, they prefer to stay at home and generally think that these things are caused by witchcraft. So they usually come to the hospital late to seek medications. You find that the disease has already progressed.” (IDI-VG-HW-002).

Access to and utilization of health care services

Distance to health facility for services

The findings showed that a majority 41 (38.7%) of the patients with lymphoedema live 1 to 2 km away from the health facility while about a quarter (24.5%) live more than 5 km away from the health facility. Slightly more than one-half, (57, 53.8%) of the patients had been visited by community health workers and a large majority (93, 87.7%) indicated that they had sought health care services at a health facility. The majority (72, 67.9%) of the patients use boda boda (motorbike) as means of transport while 15 (14.2%) walked to the health care facility. The results further show that on average, 28 (26.4%) of the patients spent Kshs 100 to 200 (0.9 to 1.8 US$) on transport while 41 (38.7%) encountered difficulties while going to the health care facilities.

The FGD participants and health care workers highlighted that issues around transport and movement to the health facilities were barriers that prevented access to healthcare and recommended the need to improve access by bringing the services to the communities to improve on reach and reduce cost.

“Some patients are too far from the health facilities. Some must cover up to 10–15 km to the nearest health facility.” (IDI-HA-005).

“Facilities are there but because they have no means to move faster, they have to be facilitated to get to the facility, distance is an issue for people with lymphoedema.” (IDI-HA-003).

“…we should set aside a place meant for patient suffering from elephantiasis where they will easily be accessing services near their homes instead of travelling long distances to access hospitals.” (FGD-PK-AM-002).

“They should reduce the cost or offer transport.” (FGD-VG-AW-001).

The healthcare workers further reported that transport to the health facilities is an issue for the patients.

“We can say in this area, it’s very vast. They cannot access our services due to the transport system. So transport is a real problem here. For us service providers to get to them, it’s an issue due to poor roads for transport.” (IDI-VG-HW-002).

“Some have problems walking, so they have to be picked from their homes.” (IDI-PK-HW-002).

Results from the health authorities further highlighted challenges related to mobility experienced by the patients.

“There are those cases where the condition is so advanced to an extent that it becomes a problem for the patient to move to a facility to seek for services.” (IDI-HA-001).

Ability to pay for health services

The findings displayed in Fig. 1 below show that 42 (39.6%) of the patients incurred an average cost of 50ksh to 200ksh (0.45 to 1.8 U$) for buying medication per visit. About one-third, 36 (34.0%) of the patients spent less than 50ksh (0.45u$) for consultation fees per visit while 30 (28.3%) incurred between 50ksh to 200ksh (0.45 to 1.8 U$).

Affordability: ability to pay

The FGD participants indicated that the cost of treatment was a barrier of access to health services for the patients.

“What I can say is, you never know the cost for medication for these diseases. A patient may need an operation for maybe Kshs 100,000 or Kshs 200,000 or Kshs 500,000 and money is a problem to the patient. So I suggest that the government should reduce the cost of treatment or make it to be free.” (FGD-PK-YM-004).

From the healthcare workers’ reports, among the factors that hinder access to health facilities reported included cost of treatment.

“And these patients cannot afford to pay for the services.” (IDI-HW-002).

The health authorities further highlighted that cost of treatment was a barrier to seeking health services.

“Most affected with LF are those at low-income levels and poverty levels are high. But in hospitals the services are charged, and these people cannot afford.” (IDI-HA-001).

Satisfaction with health care services

The results show that 68% of the patients spend about 2 to 8 h at the facility. The healthcare workers highlighted that there were issues with access to medical commodities.

“We don’t have enough drugs which means when a patient comes to the hospital and misses drugs, the patient will have to go and buy the drugs elsewhere. So, it’s an issue because most of these patients do not have money to buy these drugs.” (IDI-VG-HW-002).

Among other challenges reported by the healthcare workers that are barriers to health services included inadequately trained staff, lack of supportive supervision, low staff motivation, patients’ poor treatment-seeking behaviour, stigma, unavailability of drugs, understaffing, patients’ failure to follow instructions and limited resources and funding for LF.

“We have only one staff who was trained sometimes back, I think two years ago. The second one was transferred. Supervision and support are partial and more centered on the public health. Ok they will come do their activities and when they are done, they go. But remember the public health officers might have the knowledge but these clients they come to the facility and the clinician may have less knowledge in helping them”. (IDI-VG-HW-003)

“Human resource we cannot even start talking about it, understaffing is a very serious problem. It’s an obvious challenge because in most of the public health facilities, there is a problem with staffing.” (IDI-VG-HW-002).

“There are limited resources pumped towards addressing lymphoedema. Am talking about financial resources which can be used to procure equipment to manage patients.” (IDI-PK-HW-001).

Post-intervention phase study results

Background characteristics of the post-intervention qualitative study participants

Following delivery of the intervention, a total 14 FGDs were conducted, 8 with patients’ groups, 4 with family members and 2 with CHVs. Twenty-four key informant interviews were conducted with lymphoedema patients, their family members, health workers (including sub-county, ward officials and community health workers).

Patients’ knowledge and practices related to taking care of their swollen limbs

There were very positive views of the lymphoedema management programme across all patients FGDs. Most of them reported that they were taking care of their swollen limbs as trained by the programme because of the perceived benefits of hygiene, skin care, elevation and exercising.

“To control the condition, I am currently exercising, washing, applying jelly and elevating it when sleeping… “(Patient-FGD-002).

“What I have learnt and benefited from about this leg is making it clean, applying oil and raising it up.” (Patient-FGD-009).

The family members and healthcare workers also felt that the programme had greatly improved knowledge on strategies for lymphoedema management.

“If it was not for this programme, we could still be having the previous knowledge, we would not have known the importance of massaging the leg, washing it, and doing exercise. The programme has brought us the changes.” (K-MMDP-Family member-KII-005).

“Since you started the programme, we know that skin care is very important because many infections enter through the skin, so if you care for the skin, the infections reduce. At the moment the cases of attacks have really reduced.” (K-MMDP-Facility-KII-004).

“I did not know that lymphoedema can be managed by washing. I thought that after getting lymphatic filariasis, then that was it till death. I am happy because I got more information and can teach others what I know.” (K-MMDP-CHV-KII-002).

Patients experiences of pain and feeling about lymphoedema

A majority of the patients reported that the lymphoedema management programme had greatly improved their health outcomes. They further stated that the management practices had relieved them of the pain and suffering experienced before the programme, and that most of them were now able to go through their daily routines, including participating in some economic activities.

“I can feel a bit relieved because I can now walk. I could not walk before. If I tried to walk before, I had to stand until I am carried, but I can now walk well with my legs.” (Patient FGD, 008).

“But now, since this program began, I warm water for washing the leg with soap in the morning, applying oil after I’m done then go to the farm. When I get back in the evening I finish eating, and sleep on the bed with no pain on the leg. I even have close to a month before feeling pain, I don’t feel swollen. I feel very well.” (Patient FGD, 009).

Generally, there were positive views of the programme among family members. Most participants perceived that the programme had a positive impact on the health and wellbeing of lymphoedema patients as illustrated through the following quote.

“There is a very big difference. The fever that she used to experience now and then, or even if she had been walking for a long distance, she would come back when she is very tired because of the heaviness of the leg. Right now, she doesn’t feel any pain on her body. She does some tasks like cultivating in the garden and tethering the goats in the bush.” (K-MMDP-Family member-KII-003).

Knowledge on strategies for lymphoedema management

The CHVs further noted that the information on lymphoedema management was trickling down in the community since other patients who were not in the project were learning about lymphoedema management from those patients who had been enrolled in the study.

“I want to appreciate the fact that we got information, and we were able to serve our people who have lymphoedema. They feel good because it was like it was a cure for them. Even those who were not in the project have been forced to emulate them because they have seen that there has been some relief.” (K-MMDP-CHV-KII-001).

“Some people could not walk from this point to the road but at the moment, they can perform their duties, they can go to the field and till and when they get back, they wash their legs then sit. In the past, some of them could not go anywhere and in the evenings, they would have chills but now there is no one who chills.” (K-MMDP-CHV-KII-003).

Generally, there was a good understanding of the lymphoedema management programme among all study participants due to their involvement in its activities. The CHVs were involved in identification and enrolment of the patients into the programme, conducting home visits, leading community sensitization meetings, and referral of patients to health facilities. The health care providers gave facility-based health care to patients and provided health education while the family members played the caregivers ‘role at the household level.

Facilitators of access to the lymphoedema management services

Another factor that reportedly contributed to access to the services besides the training conducted for health care workers and CHVs and provision of guidelines to fill knowledge gaps and improve the lymphoedema management, was the provision of commodities such as ointments and antibiotics by the programme.

“There are drugs that you brought, the ointments and the orals. These have helped us greatly because as I said, sometimes the hospital would run out of stock or have few in stock and it would take a while for an order to be delivered but since you brought the antibiotics, especially orals, they can never miss drugs.” (K-MMDP- Facility-KII-004).

In addition, the involvement of CHVs in sensitization, identification, and referrals reportedly improved lymphoedema patients’ healthcare seeking behaviour and community acceptability of the morbidity programme.

“That program, I have seen it making some progress because the acceptance in the community is not like before. First, we used to direct CHVs to go look for those clients and they would be rejected by the community but after the first batch got assisted others also came and got help, they begun to appreciate our work.” (K-MMDP-Facility-KII-005}.

Challenges with the lymphoedema management

The patients anticipated challenges with procurement of required supplies and long distances to health facilities which undermined referrals and sustainability of the programme.

“You cannot lack challenges everywhere, I might run out of soap, yet I don’t have the money, already that is a challenge. Sometimes I am far away from a water source or maybe there is water, but it is too little to cater for my needs.” (Patient FGD, 002).

Despite the positive views about the programme, the CHVs also reported a few challenges which undermined effective implementation of the programme which included knowledge gaps among caregivers of lymphoedema patients who had a critical role in providing support.

“Some caregivers were not attending to these patients the way it should be. You may go there and explain to the patient, and they will tell you different things from what the caregiver had said. These caregivers need some sort of education. They should be educated for them to know how to live with these people.” (K-MMDP-CHV-KII-004).

Some participants reported that due to the short period of implementation, the programme did not reach all eligible patients in their communities.

“We were told that the programme had ended when we were not expecting. It was only a four-month programme and I know that you might have left some areas among the villages and if the time was longer, you could have been able to identify more patients.” (K-MMDP-Facility-KII-003).

In addition, health providers indicated that some key healthcare personnel such as medical doctors were left out of the training despite the critical role they play in the implementation of health interventions in their community.

“What happens, for implementation most programmes target only the PHOs. So, you find out the clinicians are being side-lined. They think it’s a PHO program and don’t understand that clinicians are the ones who handle the patients. Yeah, you see in a health facility everyone is as important as the disease. As much as you want to tackle a disease let it be inclusive.” (K-MMDP-Facility-KII-002).

Another reported challenge was stock outs of lymphoedema management drugs and commodities due to insufficient supply. As well as some of the drugs provided by the programme were not available in the list of drugs provided by Kenya Medical Supplies Agency to the health facilities hence could not be replenished once exhausted.

“They are not getting medicine at the health facility. It is only painkillers that are there for relieving pain. But there are no drugs for managing elephantiasis.” (K-MMDP-Family-Member-KII-001).

A few healthcare providers perceived that lymphoedema has not been prioritized as a public health concern and argued that drugs used to manage the disease are not included in the list of essential medicines. In addition, there was no funding allocation for the management of the disease.

“Previously the ministry had not factored in the element of LF in its planning. It is a disease which was somehow ignored. When we order for drugs, you will find that these drugs are not on the list. And when these people come for care, they cannot be attended to appropriately.” (K-MMDP-Facility-KII-003).

Most study participants lauded the study and supported its scale up. They however anticipated that some of the challenges experienced during the study would persist if not addressed. They provided several recommendations for scaling up the programme to ensure its sustainability (Table 6). At the health system level, they recommended the need for training and continuous medical education for all cadres of health workers. At individual and community levels, health education or sensitization with targeted messaging to dispel the myths and misconceptions on LF was proposed.

Discussion

The principal public health impact of lymphatic filariasis (LF) stems from the impairments and disabilities associated with lymphoedema, and hydrocoele. These chronic manifestations, particularly lymphoedema, contribute significantly to the disease burden and cause long-term physical, emotional, and economic hardship for affected individuals [18, 19]. In response, a minimum care package was developed to alleviate acute suffering, prevent disease progression, and reduce further disability. Implementation of this care package contributed to a positive impact on self-management of disabilities among patients, improved knowledge on cause and management of lymphoedema with CHVs playing a critical in its implementation. Morbidity management practices (proper limb hygiene, the elevation of the affected limb, proper foot‑ware, and exercises) play a critical role in reducing ADL attacks (frequency and duration), and the general wellbeing of the foot [19].

Generally, health workers had a better understanding of LF aetiology than family members interviewed, which may be attributed to their professional training and involvement in LF prevention programs, including the MMDP. While most family members were also aware that LF is caused by an infected mosquito, a few of them still linked causation to the prevailing myths and misconceptions about the disease in the community. Study participants reported that community members believed that the disease was caused by witchcraft or curses from elders after wrongdoing. Some believed that it was hereditary in nature, while others perceived that it was sexually transmitted or caused by excessive consumption of certain foods such as eating the jack fruit or drinking lots of young coconut fruit fluid. Several LF patients also explained that their conditions begun with a simple injury which rapidly progressed to elephantiasis. This finding is corroborated by similar studies that have shown perceived causes lymphoedema to encompass beliefs in witchcraft, direct contact with infected individuals or objects, and heritability [20–23]. The limited understanding of the link between mosquitoes as the cause of filarial infection and the advanced stages of the disease is not unique, echoing observations elsewhere [20].

Myths and misconceptions about LF causation contributed to delays in care seeking as some participants resorted to prayers for healing or visited witchdoctors for traditional medicine but ended up worse, although there are relatively simple medical interventions that can address these problems and assist patients such as washing [20, 21, 23]. It was perceived that the study had greatly improved knowledge of LF causes and on strategies for lymphoedema management among LF patients, family members and even healthcare workers which was lacking before the study.

In this study it was reported that LF patients did not engage in major economic activities in the community such as farming which increased their risk of injury and developing acute attacks. This made them dependent on other family members to perform household chores, provide for them, and support them with morbidity management. Beyond the physical sensations, the emotional impact is substantial, influencing their feelings and overall well-being. Another major challenge experienced by LF patients in the community was stigma and discrimination. Most participants opined that the LF patients were often isolated due to the myths and misconceptions surrounding the illness in the community. Self-stigma also limited health care seeking for LF since patients felt isolated and shied away from social and public places [19, 24]. However after the intervention provision of guidelines and commodities for lymphoedema management, family members and patients reported an observed reduction in episodes of pain and LF patients willingly joining social events. The experience of pain and the emotional aspects surrounding lymphoedema are significant components of patients’ perspectives. Individuals grappling with lymphoedema often contend with physical discomfort, with pain being a prevalent aspect of their daily lives [23–25]. Understanding the holistic nature of patients’ experiences, encompassing both the physical and emotional dimensions, is essential for providing comprehensive and empathetic care for those affected by lymphoedema.

Access to lymphoedema management services is a critical component of ensuring comprehensive care for individuals affected by this chronic condition [19]. The effectiveness of lymphoedema management—through interventions such as hygiene, skincare, simple exercises, and limb elevation—relies not on specialized healthcare resources but on empowering patients and their families to carry out consistent self-care. With appropriate training and ongoing support from community health workers, most aspects of lymphoedema care can be managed at home. This approach reduces the burden of traveling to distant health facilities and promotes long-term, sustainable management of the condition within the community setting [19, 25]. From our study, most participants agreed that LF patients routinely experienced several challenges which had an impact on their health, livelihoods, and social wellbeing. For example, these patients faced various access barriers such as distance and long waiting hours when accessing facility-based healthcare. Affordability is another key aspect, with considerations for and financial support to minimize economic barriers [24]. Geographical accessibility is crucial, necessitating the availability of services in both urban and remote areas as this was also observed in our study during the pre-intervention phase. During the intervention phase, the CHVs identified and enrolled LF patients into the programme, conducted home visits and referred patients to health facilities where health care providers provided further care to patients, provided health education reducing previous challenges that are mentioned above. Individuals should have ready access to well-equipped facilities staffed with trained healthcare professionals, ensuring that they can receive timely and appropriate care. Community awareness and education play pivotal roles in informing individuals about the existence and importance of lymphoedema management services [23]. In the current study, some of the factors that reportedly contributed to the success of the programme included training conducted for health care workers and CHVs on LF management, and provision of commodities for morbidity management and disability prevention. The integration of these services into existing healthcare systems, further enhances access by creating a supportive and inclusive environment for those seeking care. Overall, facilitating access to lymphoedema management services is paramount in improving the overall well-being and quality of life for individuals living with lymphoedema [19, 23, 26].

The inclusion of a particular health facility in training sessions was based on knowledge of the presence of these patients in the area based on the data collected as part of annual MDA following WHO recommendation of carrying out a situational analysis and assessment of local LF disease burden before including specific health facilities [27]. The CHVs and healthcare workers who participated in the training acknowledged that, prior to the sessions, they lacked the knowledge and resources needed to adequately care for patient with LFs. The training provided them with practical, cost-effective approaches that enabled them to deliver improved care, ultimately enhancing the well-being of patients and their families.

At the end of the study lymphoedema patients felt appreciated and accepted and had more confidence to visit health facilities as they received care when they went there.

While MMDP interventions for lymphatic filariasis are essential for improving the quality of life for affected individuals, their long-term sustainability faces several challenges. Maintaining consistent funding streams is critical, especially given that MMDP services such as lymphoedema management, and patient follow-up—require ongoing investment even after transmission has been interrupted [28]. Ensuring a stable supply chain for essential commodities, including antiseptics, bandages and antifungals, remains a persistent barrier in many endemic areas [10]. Additionally, integrating MMDP into existing health systems is often constrained by limited infrastructure, insufficiently trained personnel, and competing health priorities. Strengthening health system ownership, embedding MMDP within routine service delivery, and fostering multi-sectoral collaboration are necessary steps to ensure these services remain accessible and effective in the long term [28, 29].

Study limitations

First, the absence of a control group limits the ability to attribute observed outcomes solely to the intervention, as external factors may also have influenced the results. The COVID-19 pandemic caused delays between the pre-intervention and intervention phase making the tracing of patients of the pre-intervention phase for intervention activities tedious and time consuming. The pandemic may have also caused a recall bias among the respondents due to the prolonged period between pre-intervention and intervention phases. The reliance on self-reported data introduces potential biases, including recall bias and social desirability bias, which may affect the accuracy of responses, particularly in relation to behavior change or health outcomes. Although measures were taken to encourage honest reporting and maintain confidentiality, these limitations may have influenced the findings. Additionally, the short duration of follow-up of an 8-week interval was chosen to balance logistical feasibility and the need to detect early effects post-intervention. However, we agree that a longer follow-up period would allow for more comprehensive assessment of sustained outcomes. Future research should incorporate larger sample sizes, extended follow-up periods, and the inclusion of control or comparison groups to enable more robust evaluation of outcomes and causal inference. Additionally, studies using standardized quantitative tools to measure clinical and psychosocial improvements would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the intervention’s impact. Lastly, the data were collected in local language possibly contributing to some dialect-specific variations during the translation process.

Conclusion

The results of this study have shown that persistent myths and misconceptions about the cause of LF morbidity among the community members contribute greatly to stigmatization and delayed health care seeking among the lymphoedema patients. There is therefore a need to develop and implement social and behaviour change communication approaches to address the existing gaps. Involving all levels of health care workers and community members in lymphoedema management by providing them with the required information on how to take care of swollen limbs and ensuring a consistent supply of essential medicines and commodities at health facilities is critical for maintaining the long-term effectiveness of the intervention. The demand for lymphoedema management training is high among patients who are keen on taking care of their affected limbs and should be provided with the basic equipment and materials and follow-ups conducted to monitor their progress. The results presented in this study provide lessons that National LF Programme implementers can use to scale up the lymphoedema management programme in all LF endemic communities in Kenya and move to completing the writing of a dossier for WHO certification.
