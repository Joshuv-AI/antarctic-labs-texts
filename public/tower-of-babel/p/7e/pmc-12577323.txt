Nano-size tantalum particles suppress the osteogenic activity of osteoblasts and stimulate osteoclasts
Authors: Ashish Ranjan Sharma, Srijan Chatterjee, Yeon-Hee Lee, Garima Sharma, Jae Gyu Kim, Jin-Chul Kim, Sang-Soo Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTantalum (Ta) and its derivatives are inert, possess mechanical qualities such as corrosion resistance, and are biocompatible. They also offer structural support during surgical correction, such as bone grafts during surgery, in instances of dental or skeletal disabilities. However, various sizes of Ta particles could be expelled from the implant’s surface due to mechanical stress and load-induced wear caused by micromotion between loose implant surfaces during usage. Therefore, the study examined the effects of nano (25 nm) and micro-sized Ta particles (10 μm and 40–50 μm) on osteoblasts and osteoclasts.ResultsOsteoblasts efficiently phagocytosed 25 nm sized Ta particles compared to micro-sized particles and triggered significant biological effects. Only 25 nm Ta particles suppressed ALP activity, downregulated osteogenic regulators and markers, and inhibited collagen synthesis and mineralization. Moreover, 25 nm sized Ta particles induced inflammatory responses in osteoblasts by increasing Cox-2 expression and activating the NFkB signaling pathway. Nano-sized Ta particles induced intracellular ROS generation in osteoblasts and osteoclasts. Compared to micro-sized Ta particles, 25 nm sized Ta particles stimulated osteoclast formation, but ROS scavenging by N-acetyl cysteine (NAC) inhibited Ta particle-mediated osteoclastogenesis. Likewise, ALP activity of osteoblasts was partially restored after NAC treatment. 25 nm Ta particles suppressed Axin-2 reporter activity and protein levels of pGSK3β and β-catenin stability, implicating suppressed WNT signaling in treated osteoblasts. Expression levels of several families of antagonists like DKK, sFRP, and SOST of the WNT signaling pathway were found elevated several-fold in 25 nm-sized Ta particles treated osteoblasts, explaining suppressed WNT signaling pathway in exposed osteoblasts.ConclusionTa supports osseointegration and biocompatibility, but micromotion-induced nanoscale wear particles may disrupt osteoprogenitor function and enhance osteoclast activity, risking implant loosening. Thus, vigilant post-operative monitoring for nano-sized Ta particles is advisable and critical in detecting early osteolysis and ensuring implant longevity.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13036-025-00571-3.

Background

The aging population worldwide is going to be burdened with degenerative skeletal disabilities like osteoarthritis, osteoporosis, and rheumatoid arthritis. Dental and total joint replacement surgeries are a boon for contemporary medicine, offering consistent long-term pain relief and functionality [1]. It is anticipated that there will be an increase in the number of revisions in dental and total joint replacement surgeries because younger and more active populations are undergoing these surgeries due to inadequate implant durability. Over the past thirty years, numerous implant materials and designs have been utilized in dental and total joint arthroplasty (TJA) to attain biological fixation [2]. The predominant metallic materials are sintered titanium (Ti), cobalt-chromium-molybdenum alloy beads, diffusion-bonded Ti, fiber metal, and plasma-sprayed Ti. Regrettably, wear debris, predominantly produced from prosthetic joint articulations, modular interfaces, and non-articulating interfaces, continues to be the principal factor constraining the longevity of replaced implants by inducing osteolysis around the implant [3, 4]. Thus, there has been an effort to create inert and porous biomaterials with desirable properties such as a high resistance/weight ratio, biocompatibility in biological fluids, superior corrosion resistance, and osseointegration.

Tantalum (Ta), a refractory metal, has lately attracted interest as a viable implant material for orthopedic applications due to its remarkable strength, excellent biocompatibility, and corrosion resistance. Ta was introduced as Trabecular Metal™ by Zimmer Inc. (Warsaw, IN, USA) and, till now, has been utilized effectively in dental [5], prosthetic joint replacement [6], and femoral head necrosis treatment [7]. Ta possesses exceptional osseointegration and antimicrobial properties. The increased interest in porous Ta, a major Ta derivative, is attributable to its high elastic modulus and porous structure, which resembles cancellous bone [8]. Thus, porous Ta has demonstrated significant clinical application in restoring compromised bone tissues and managing diverse bone disorders. Due to the elevated surface energy and wettability, studies have elucidated that a nanostructured Ta surface could enhance the initial adhesion and diffusion of cells [9]. Studies demonstrated that Ta coating on implants could stimulate osteogenesis by increasing the amount of alkaline phosphatase (ALP), collagen type 1α (Col1α), osteocalcin (OCN), osteopontin, and cellular mineralization. Ta has also been reported to stimulate various established osteogenic pathways, including transforming growth factor, Wnt/β-catenin signaling, and bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling [10–13].

During the loading process, wear Ta particles from the coating may detach from the implant surface and interact with nearby cells, resulting in a multifaceted local cellular response that could alter the equilibrium between bone formation and resorption. Moreover, loose fixation of orthopedic/dental implants caused by micro motion between the Ta mesh or Ta bone grafts with implant surfaces might also lead to friction and subsequent release of wear debris. A release of approximately 2 µg/L Ta from Ta films was observed when placed on sand-blasted, immense grit, and acid-etched (SLA) titanium surfaces following immersion in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 28 days [9]. A case study reported that abundant debris from porous Ta was generated and accumulated at the periarticular soft tissues despite meticulous protection with gauze. The study suggested minimum use of motorized revision instruments to avoid generating third body wear particles from porous Ta during surgery and advised clinicians to follow up for recurrent osteolysis from the generated debris [14]. Another case study also observed the periprosthetic soft tissue metallosis after the dissemination of debris of Ta followed by delamination of porous coating of Ta from the tibial component [15]. Several studies have shown that implant wear debris particles adversely affect macrophages, osteoblasts, fibroblasts, and foreign body giant cells in the granulomatous soft tissue membrane at the bone–prosthesis interface [3, 4, 16, 17].

Thus, to address the issue of any adverse effect of Ta particles generated as wear debris on the osteoblasts and osteoclasts, Ta particles of various sizes (nano-sized (25 nm) and micro-sized (10 μm and 40–50 μm)) were treated, and analyzed for their effect on the osteoegenis and osteoclast formation along with their mechanism of action, ultimately contributing to a better understanding of metal particle-induced bone loss and potential treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Ta particles, 25 nm, 10 μm, and 40–50 μm were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Catalog #593486), Nanoshel (Catalog #NG01EM3204) and Nanografi (Catalog #7440-25-7), respectively. Chemicals including 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2yl)-2,5-diphenyl-tetrazoliumbromide (MTT), N-Acetyl Cysteine (NAC), dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA), cetylpyridinium chloride, ascorbic acid, sirius red S solution, Alizarin red S solution, and bouin’s fluid were bought from Sigma-Aldrich, USA. α-minimum essential medium (α-MEM) was sourced from Invitrogen, USA. Penicillin-streptomycin solution (P/S), Dulbecco’s Modification of Eagle’s Medium (DMEM), and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were obtained from Gibco, USA. RIPA buffer were procured from T&I, Korea. The Disodium 3-(4-methoxyspiro {1,2-dioxetane-3,2′-(5′-chloro)tricyclo [3.3.1.13,7]decan}-4-yl)phenyl phosphate (CSPD) substrate was obtained from Roche, Germany. Genefectine reagent was acquired from Genetrone, Korea. Antibodies, including β-catenin, IkBα, Cox2, Runx2, Iκbα, and pSer9-GSK-3β were procured from Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA. Col1α and β-actin were acquired from Bioss Inc. Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane, and Immobilon (chemiluminescence reagents) was bought from Millipore Corporation, USA. The Axin-2 reporter construct was acquired from Addgene, USA. The cell cytotoxicity lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) kit was obtained from Roche Diagnostics, USA, and the tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) stain kit from Takara Bio Inc., Japan. SYBR green qPCR MasterMix was obtained from Bioneer, Korea. SuperScript ІІ reverse transcriptase, trizol reagent, and the renilla luciferase thymidine kinase construct were bought from Invitrogen, USA. The protein assay kit was purchased from Bio-Rad, USA. The dual-luciferase assay kit was from Promega, USA.

Characterization and preparation of nano-sized and micro-sized Ta particles

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements across various sizes (25 nm, 10 μm, 40 μm) indicated a narrow, low-PDI distribution for 25 nm Ta nanoparticles, while microparticles exhibited broad/high-PDI profiles, which aligns with the limited suspension stability and DLS constraints for larger, non-spherical particles (DLS, a zeta sizer from Malvern (England). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis of all fractions revealed smooth, near-spherical primary particles measuring 25 nm, in contrast to irregular, aggregated particles of 10 μm and polydisperse, rough-surfaced particles of 40 μm (FE-SEM, JEOL, JSM-7900 F, Tokyo, Japan). This establishes a clear distinction in morphology and dispersibility across different sizes (Supplementary Figure S1). The nano- and micro-sized Ta particles were autoclaved at 121℃ for 20 min for sterilization and suspended in PBS and stored as 100 mg/mL stock solutions at -20 °C till further use. The particles were subjected to ultrasonication for 20 to 30 min before use.

Cell culture

Mice osteoprogenitor cell line, MC3T3-E1 cell line (CRL-2593, ATCC, USA), and murine macrophage cell line, RAW 264.7 cells (TIB-71, ATCC) were grown in α-MEM and DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% P/S. Subculturing of the cells was performed on confluence and was maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator.

MTT assay

After stimulating MC3T3-E1 cells with Ta particles at the following doses (0, 1.25, 2.5, 5, 10, 20 µg/ml) for 72 h, the cells were incubated with 5 mg/ml of MTT reagent for 2 h at 37 °C, followed by media removal and addition of DMSO (200 µl/well). To determine cell viability, the color absorbance of the lysed cells was taken at 570 nm (UV-Vis spectrophotometer, SpectraMax, Molecular Devices, USA).

LDH assay

After treating MC3T3-E1 cells with Ta particles at various doses (0, 1.25, 2.5, 5, 10, 20 µg/ml) for 72 h, 10 µl cell culture media from each well was collected separately in a fresh 96-well plate, followed by addition of PBS (40 µl) and LDH reagent (50 µl) and incubating in the dark for 45 min. The enzymatic process was then stopped by adding 50 µl of stop solution. The cell cytotoxicity, as indicated by LDH release, was determined by measuring optical density at a wavelength of 490 nm using UV-Vis spectrophotometer.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity

To determine ALP activity as an indicative of osteoblast differentiation, MC3T3-E1 cells (3 × 105 cells/well) were cultured in 48-well plates and treated with Ta particles in various concentrations (0, 1.25, 2.5, 5, 10, 20 µg/mL) for 48 h. Subsequently, after rinsing with cold, cells were lysed with cold RIPA buffer to collect cell lysate as supernatant after centrifugation at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 20 min. The 20 µL of cell lysates were then mixed with 100 µL of CSPD substrate for 30 min in the dark at RT, followed by measuring luminescence (Luminometer, Glomax, Promega, USA). Also, the protein concentration in the cell lysate was standardized using a protein assay kit.

Protein isolation and western blotting

The Ta particles, at varying concentrations (0, 1.25, 2.5, 5, 10, 20 µg/ml), were first treated to cultured MC3T3-E1 cells (6 × 105 cells/well) for 48 h, washed with cold PBS and cell lysates were collected after lysing the cells with cold RIPA buffer along with 1X fresh protease inhibitor cocktail. The collected cell lysates were quantified for protein concentration, loaded onto gels, transferred to PVDF membranes, and blocked with 5% skim milk at RT for a period of 1 h. The membranes were then treated with antibodies against specific proteins, such as Runx2, Cox2, Collagen1α, Iκbα, β-catenin, and pSer9-GSK-3β, and incubated overnight at 4 °C. β-actin was used as a loading control. The membranes were washed thrice with 1X TBST (Tris-buffered saline, 0.1% Tween 20) for 10 min each and then incubated for 45 min at RT with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugated secondary antibody (Jackson Immunoresearch, USA), to visualize the protein bands using a chemiluminescence reagent and quantify them using Image J software (NIH, USA).

RNA isolation and real-time RT-PCR

After 24 h of treatment of MC3T3-E1 cells by Ta particles at varying concentrations (0, 1.25, 2.5, 5, 10, 20 µg/ml), cellular RNA was collected in Trizol reagent, and the quality and purity of the RNA samples were evaluated using nanodrop. Subsequently, 2 µg of RNA was utilized with SuperScript II Reverse Transcriptase to synthesize the first-strand cDNA. Each PCR mixture contained 1 µl of cDNA, specific primers, and EXPRESS SYBR green qPCR Supermix. The Rotor-Gene 3000 system (Corbett, Germany) was used for real-time PCR analysis (10 min at 95 °C, 50 amplification cycles of 20 s each at 95 °C, 20 s at 60 °C, and 25 s at 72 °C. Quantification was performed using the ∆∆CT method, using glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as control. The primer sequences used for analysis are documented in Table 1.

Luciferase reporter assay

At first, reporter constructs, comprised of 1 µg of Axin-2 and an equal quantity of Renilla luciferase thymidine kinase, were transfected into the MC3T3-E1 cells in 48-well plates using Genefectine reagent. The cells were then exposed to Ta particles (10 µg/ml) for 48 h. The luciferase activity was assessed using a luminometer after normalizing the reporter activity identified in each sample against Renilla luciferase activity.

Sirius red S staining

After exposing MC3T3-E1 cells with Ta particles for 14 days, with Ta-containing medium replacement after every 3rd day. The cells were fixed using Bouin’s fluid at RT for 1 h, followed by several washes with PBS. Subsequently, the cells underwent staining with Sirius Red S solution for 1 h and were then observed using a microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany). To quantify the stained cells, they were dissolved in sodium hydroxide solution (0.1 N) for 30 min, and the optical density of the resulting solution was measured using a spectrophotometer at 550 nm, with 0.1 N sodium hydroxide serving as the blank solution.

Alizarin red S staining

MC3T3-E1 cells underwent treatment with Ta particles over a 14-day period, as described above. The cells were then fixed with 4% PFA for 1 h, rinsed, and stained with a 40 mM Alizarin Red S solution at RT for 30 min. The stained cells were first imaged using a microscope, and the the mineralization was quantified by dissolving the stained cells using 10% cetylpyridinium chloride solution and then transferring into a 96-well plate, to measure UV-vis absorbance 570 nm.

TRAP staining

To observe osteoclast formation, RAW 264.7 cells were cultured in RANKL (50 ng/ml) containing medium and treated with Ta particles for 7 days, with repeated treatments every 3rd day. Post-treatment, the cells were fixed and stained using a TRACP stain kit. The osteoclast-like cells, RAW 264.7 cells showing positive TRAP staining with more than three nuclei, were identified and quantified using a microscope.

To determine TRAP activity, hydrolysis assay of para-nitrophenylphosphate (pNPP) was performed. The cells were washed twice with PBS and dissolved in a 0.1% (V/V) Triton X-100 (200 µL). They were then incubated for 1 h with pNPP (12.5 mm) in a solution of 0.04 m tartaric acid and 0.09 m citrate (pH 5.8) at 37 °C. Later, 5 mM NaOH was added to stop the solution and UV-vis absorbance was recirded at 405 nm.

Confocal microscopy for intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection and lysosome staining

DCFH-DA is widely employed as a fluorescent probe, transforming into green fluorescence-emitting DCF upon ROS production. MC3T3-E1 and RAW 264.7 cells were cultured in a 8-well cell culture chamber slide (SPL Life Sciences, Catalog No. 30104), and treated with Ta particles.The cells were fixed with a 4% PFA for 20 min, 0.5% Triton X100 was used for 10 min to permeabilize the cells. Cells were stained with DCFH-DA (10 µM) in dark for 30 min. The nuclei were stained using 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Cell imaging was performed using a High-Resolution Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (Carl Zeiss (LSM880 with Airyscan), and fluorescence intensity was quantified using Zeiss ZEN lite software. For lysosome staining, cells were stained with lysosomeTracker 488, and nuclei were stained with Hoechst.

Statistical analysis

All the experimental data were analyzed statistically using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 software (Graphpad, USA), and the P-value was determined by using a two-tailed Student’s t-test or 2-way ANOVA (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001).

Results

Effect of different sizes of Ta particles on ALP activity, cell morphology, phagocytosis, cell viability, and cytotoxicity of osteoblasts

To assess the impact of nano-sized (25 nm) and micro-sized (10 μm and 40–50 μm) Ta particles on ALP activity, MC3T3-E1 cells were subjected to the following concentrations (0, 1.25, 2.5, 5, 10, 20 µg/ml) of these particles. After 48 h, the osteoblasts were measured for their ALP activity. The results demonstrated a dose-dependent reduction in ALP activity following treatment with nano-sized Ta particles, while no significant changes were observed with micro-sized particles (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, the impact of treating MC3T3-E1 cells with 10 µg/ml of nano-sized and micro-sized Ta particles for 24, 48, and 72 h revealed a time-dependent effect on the ALP activity of osteoblasts. Specifically, nano-sized Ta particles significantly reduced ALP activity in osteoblasts at 48 and 72 h. In contrast, micro-sized Ta particles did not induce any change in ALP activity over the same treatment time (Fig. 1B). Next, the effect of nano-sized and micro-sized Ta particles was observed on the morphology of MC3T3-E1 cells. The cells were subjected to Ta particle exposure for 48 h, and their morphology was analyzed via microscopy (Fig. 1C). Treating the cells with equal concentrations of 25 nm, 10 μm, and 40–50 μm Ta particles (10 µg/ml) also resulted in morphological changes in the MC3T3-E1 cells than in the control group. Furthermore, the phagocytotic ability of osteoblasts was evaluated for the different sizes of the Ta particles by fluorescence actin staining and visualized by confocal microscopy following 6 h of treatment (Fig. 1D). A disorganized arrangement of actin filaments was observed in Ta particle-treated osteoblasts, showing actin cup (U) and ring formation (O) compared to normal actin fiber alignment in non-treated osteoblasts. Ta particle’s attachment to the cell surface and internalization by actin remodeling was more evident in nano-sized Ta particles (25 nm) and in 10 μm micro-sized Ta particles. Phagocytic activity was relatively lower in the case of 40–50 μm micro-sized particles; however, as expected, the phagosome size was broader and bigger than the other two sizes (Fig. 1D). Untreated cells showed just a modest, normal amount of punctate green fluorescence, which was consistent with lysosomes in the perinuclear cytoplasm and undamaged nuclei. This set a baseline for comparison. Conversely, consistent with their diminutive, well-dispersed profile and smooth morphology (Supplementary Fig. S1), cells subjected to 25 nm Ta nanoparticles exhibited numerous green fluorescent puncta throughout the cytoplasm, primarily in the perinuclear region, with orthogonal projections validating their intracellular localization rather than surface attachment. The 25 nm Ta-treated cells had a lot more punctate green vesicles than the untreated or microparticle-treated groups. This suggests that Ta nanoparticles are easily engulfed by the cells and result in the formation of several new lysosomes. These nanoparticles exhibited a vesicular distribution pattern, suggesting endocytosis followed by transport into acidic endosomal/lysosomal compartments, while staying excluded from the nucleus. In contrast, 10 μm-sized Ta microparticles, which are irregular and prone to aggregation, formed brilliant green aggregates that were linked to the plasma membrane. Cross-sectional imaging showed that the particles were partially engulfed and the membrane was deformed, which is a sign of failed phagocytosis. These bigger particles did not completely colocalize with lysosomal compartments, indicating an uptake barrier at this size scale. At the extreme, 40 μm-sized Ta microparticles, the biggest and most polydisperse/rough proportion, stayed outside of cells. Green fluorescence was only seen at the edges of cells, and there was no sign of internalization or lysosomal trafficking. These findings collectively illustrate a distinct size-dependent uptake threshold, wherein nano-sized Ta particles are effectively internalized and transported into lysosomal compartments. Conversely, larger microparticles either cause membrane deformation or remain extracellular, indicating frustrated or unsuccessful phagocytosis, which emphasizes potential size-dependent cellular stress responses. Effects of Ta particles on the osteogenic activity of osteoblasts

Given the significant suppression of ALP activity by nano-sized Ta particles in osteoblasts, an attempt was made to investigate their impact on other osteogenic parameters. mRNA was collected from MC3T3-E1 cells treated with nano-sized (25 nm) and micro-sized (10 μm and 40–50 μm) Ta particles for 24 h. RT-PCR analysis revealed significant suppression of Runx2, OSX, Col1α, and OCN mRNA expression after exposure to nano-sized Ta particles (Fig. 2A). Ta particles were then exposed to MC3T3-E1 cells for 48 h, and whole-cell protein was collected. After treating MC3T3-E1 cells with nano-sized Ta particles, Western blot analysis revealed a significant decrease in the protein expression of the osteoblastic differentiation marker Col1α and the osteoblastic transcription factor Runx2 (Fig. 2B). The effects of nano- and micro-sized Ta particles on collagen synthesis and mineralization in MC3T3-E1 cells were evaluated using Sirius Red S and Alizarin Red S staining, respectively. After 14 days of treatment, cells were stained, and the quantification was conducted using a colorimetric assay. Collagen synthesis and mineralization were found to be significantly inhibited in cells treated with nano-sized Ta particles, as shown by the results (Fig. 2C). Nevertheless, neither micro-sized Ta particle impacted the osteogenic activity of osteoblasts when administered at the same doses.

Effect of Ta particles on osteoclastogenesis

In investigating the impact of Ta particles on osteoclasts, nano (25 nm) and micro size Ta particles (10 μm, and 40 μm) were treated to murine macrophage cell line RAW 264.7 cells. RAW 264.7 cells were exposed to Ta particle at a concentration of 10 µg/ml for both 3 h and 6 h. Following treatment, the induction of ROS was evaluated via confocal microscopy, with the emission of green fluorescent signals (DCFH-DA) serving as a marker for ROS induction. Results revealed a significant fluorescence activity specifically attributable to the 25 nm Ta particles at 3 h and 6 h of time points, suggesting elevated intracellular ROS levels within the RAW 264.7 cells. Conversely, the micro-sized Ta particles demonstrated no notable impact on ROS induction (Fig. 4A (a&b)).

Studies have indicated that ROS (hydrogen peroxide and superoxide) serve as crucial intracellular signaling molecules in the process of osteoclast differentiation. Thus, RAW 264.7 cells were exposed to Ta particles at a 10 µg/ml concentration for 7 days to investigate the effect of Ta particles on osteoclast formation (Fig. 4B). The presence of osteoclasts was assessed through TRAP staining, following established protocols (material and method section). RANKL, recognized for promoting osteoclast differentiation, served as a positive control. The results indicated that nano-sized Ta particles promote osteoclast formation by facilitating the formation of multinuclear cells. Conversely, micro-sized Ta particles did not induce osteoclast formation. This suggests a size-dependent effect of Ta particles on osteoclastogenesis, potentially mediated through ROS signaling pathways.

To further clarify the involvement of intracellular ROS induction by nano-sized Ta particles in osteoclast formation, RAW 264.7 cells were treated with or without prior exposure to 5 mM of N-acetyl-L-cysteine NAC for 7 days. NAC is frequently employed for detecting and assessing substances that induce ROS and is a well-known scavenger for ROS [18]. TRAP staining results revealed that NAC treatment prior to Ta particle treatment significantly reduced the formation of multinuclear osteoclasts formation compared to only Ta particles treated RAW 264.7 cells (Fig. 4C). Results highlight the role of intracellular ROS induced by Ta particles in promoting osteoclastogenesis and the formation of mature osteoclasts.

Intracellular ROS induction by Ta particles in osteoblasts

Next an assessment of induction of intracellular ROS by Ta particles was observed in osteoblasts. At a 10 µg/mL concentration, MC3T3-E1 cells were subjected to Ta particles of different sizes (25 nm, 10 μm, and 40–50 μm) for two separate time points: 12 h and 24 h. Utilizing confocal microscopy, the induction of ROS was examined using DCFH-DA, serving as a marker for ROS induction. Remarkably, cells treated with 25 nm Ta particles exhibited increased green fluorescence intensity at both time points, the maximum being at 24 h of treatment, indicating ROS induction. Conversely, this effect was notably absent in cells treated with Ta particles of micro sizes (10 μm and 40–50 μm) (Fig. 5A).

This observed induction of ROS prompted further investigation into the functional consequences of nano-sized Ta particle exposure on osteoblast osteogenic activity. Notably, earlier experiments showed that the ALP activity of osteoblasts decreased in a dose- and time-dependent manner after being treated with 25 nm Ta particles. Intriguingly, pre-treatment with a ROS scavenger, NAC (5mM), led to a significant recovery in ALP activity of MC3T3 E-1 cells even after nano-sized Ta particle treatment, implicating the role of intracellular ROS in the differentiation of osteoblasts (Fig. 5B).

Regulation of WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway by Ta particles

WNT signaling pathway holds significant importance in regulating the differentiation of osteoblasts and stimulating bone formation [19]. Therefore, any role of Ta particles in the regulation of the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway was examined in osteoblasts. Following a 24 h transfection with an Axin-2 luciferase reporter construct, MC3T3-E1 cells were exposed to Ta particles (10 µg/ml) with nano and micro sizes. The subsequent luciferase activity was evaluated after 48 h of treatment. Notably, nano-sized Ta particles demonstrated a marked reduction in Axin-2 luciferase activity compared to micro-sized particles (Fig. 6A).

Conversely, β-catenin stabilization and protein expression of p-GSK-3β remained unaltered in cells treated with 10 μm and 40–50 μm Ta particles (Fig. 6B).

Ta particles induce the expression of various antagonists of the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway

Next objective was to investgate the reason of suppression of WNT signaling pathway in Ta particles treated osteoblasts. The regulation of WNT/β-catenin signaling pathways is accomplished through various governing mechanisms, one of which is the utilization of secreted extracellular antagonists [20]. WNT inhibitors are part of several small protein groups, such as DKK, WISE, Sclerostin (SOST), WIF, sFRP and WISE. They all share the function of inhibiting the Wnt signaling pathway by either blocking the interaction between ligands and receptors or hindering the maturation of Wnt receptors [21]. Following exposure to nano- and micro-sized Ta particles, the mRNA expression levels of several families of antagonists of the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway were screened in treated MC3T3-E1 cells. The expression of numerous antagonists, including the DKK family members, sFRP family, and SOST, showed prominent increases. Notably, DKK2 (~ 5 fold), DKK3 (~ 6 fold), SOST (~ 5 fold), sFRP4 (~ 10 fold), and sFRP5 (~ 7 fold) exhibited a maximum increase in expression after nano-sized Ta particle treatment. Nevertheless, these inhibitors did exhibit an increase in expression in response to the treatment of cells with micro-sized Ta particles; however, induction by nano-sized Ta particles was more eminent (Fig. 7). Discussion

Following TJA, the routine use of implants often leads to clinical failures. This is typically due to the release of significant amounts of wear debris resulting from mechanical wear on implant surfaces. Debris generated commonly triggers an inflammatory and physiological response in various cell types present at the bone-implant interface, including osteoblasts, osteoclasts, fibroblasts, macrophages, and dendritic cells [22, 23]. To reduce the release of wear debris and the risk of osteolysis, several innovative materials have been developed for TJA, including carbon-carbon composite, carbon fiber-reinforced, polycarbonate-urethane, cross-linked polyethylene, titanium-based alloy, cobalt-chromium-based alloy, and ceramic-ceramic composite. Despite advancements, the increasing number of revision cases following TJA remains concerning, prompting further investigation into implant materials and their compatibility with the body [24, 25]. Characterizing wear debris is critical as its size, shape, and chemical composition influence biological reactions and potential complications. Most wear debris from prostheses is typically micron to sub-micron in size, with the majority being less than 5 mm in diameter [24]. Studies have reported that these particles typically measure around 0.5 μm [26, 27]. Research on wear particles generated from artificial joint simulations indicates that the average diameters vary from several tenths of micrometers. Conventional wear particles are often microparticles, while the presence of wear nanoparticles cannot be ruled out [28, 29].

Ta has emerged as a bone replacement material with notable biological properties. Specifically porous Ta, utilized across various clinical applications, has demonstrated efficacy in promoting osteogenesis [30]. Following implantation of the porous Ta, new bone tissue grows within its pores while retaining the same characteristics as natural bone, including the ability to remodel and preserve the Haversian system [31]. Due to its excellent biological inertness, Ta is widely recognized as a substance that cells naturally prefer. However, in medical applications, certain Ta particles at the micro- and nanoscale levels inevitably detach from the implant’s surface due to mechanical wear, potentially affecting the survival and physiological aspects of adjacent cells [27, 29, 32]. Ta implants may lead to loosening and increased blood Ta concentrations from mechanical wear, with some patients exhibiting levels notably higher than those in a healthy population. Although Ta is generally biocompatible, the long-term systemic effects remain unclear, necessitating further research and careful monitoring to fully understand the health implications of increased Ta exposure from implants [33]​. Because of this concern, a few studies have recently evaluated the effect of Ta wear particles on osteoblasts and osteoclasts. Potential cytotoxic and inflammatory effects have been observed in vitro, raising concerns about prolonged exposure [34]. Ta particles in the nanoparticle range of 8–15 nm were shown to stimulate osteoblast proliferation through induction of autophagy [29]. In contrast, Ta particles in the 1–10 μm size were shown to inhibit osteoblast differentiation and promote osteoclast formation in vitro [27]. Another study evaluated the effect of Ta particles in the size range of 41.89 ± 19.58 nm on osteoblasts and suggested that Ta nanoparticles can promote cell viability at lower concentrations. In comparison, higher concentrations increase cell cytotoxicity and oxidative stress, activating autophagy in osteoblasts [34]. Similar to our irregular shaped Ta particles (10 μm in diameter, 0–20 µg/ml), Huang et al. reported that spherical Ta particles (1–10 μm in diameter; 100 and 369 µg/ml) also exhibited much milder modulatory effects on human osteoblastic SaOS-2 cells and murine-derived RAW 264.7 macrophages, indicating that the shape and dose of the Ta microparticles (~ 10 μm in diameter) does not exert any variations in the modulatory effect on osteoblast and osteoclast [27]. In contrast to our study, Kang et al. showed that Ta nanoparticles (< 25 nm in diameter) increased cell viability in MC3T3-E1 cells at a concentration of 10 µg/ml and 20 µg/ml [29]. In this study, Ta particles underwent sterilization through 25 kGy of cobalt-60 irradiation. This process may enhance the cytocompatibility of Ta, likely attributed to a more stable chemical state characterized by a reduction in chemical defects, specifically oxygen vacancies, resulting from the effects of gamma-ray irradiation [35]. Interestingly, Wang et al. highlighted a dose-dependent effect on osteoblast viability in cobalt-60 at 25 k-gray radiated Ta nanoparticles-treated MC3T3-E1 cells via oxidative stress. They also reported that autophagy activation may be a key factor in the cellular response to Ta-nanoparticle toxicity and could have an important impact on determining the survival or death of osteoblasts. In addition, other irradiations, such as ultraviolet radiation (UV), are also favorable for the osteogenic potential of Ta particles due to increased surface hydroxyl groups [36]. Thus, since no irradiation was applied in the present study, the biological effect of unmodified nascent Ta particles was observed here. In contrast to previous studies, this work investigates and compares the osteogenic potential, phagocytic response, involvement of the WNT signaling pathway, ROS generation, and inflammatory mechanisms of non-irradiated nascent Ta particles of varying sizes (25 nm, 10 μm, and 40–50 μm), which may be generated as wear debris from Ta implants in clinical settings. The particle sizes used in this study were selected to represent clinically relevant Ta debris. Earlier case reports have demonstrated metallosis and increased serum Ta concentrations in patients with unsuccessful porous Ta hip implants, validating the in vivo spread of particulate debris [37]. Furthermore, the delamination of porous Ta coatings from total knee arthroplasty has been recorded, producing micro- to macro-scale fragments that align with our 10 μm and 40–50 μm groups [15]. Considering these observations, we incorporated 25 nm, 10 μm, and 40–50 μm particles to encompass both nanoscale debris, pertinent to phagocytosis, and microscale debris, relevant to frustrated phagocytosis, thus representing the clinical debris spectrum.

ALP activity is considered an initial indicator of osteoblast differentiation [38]. Research has revealed that local cellular reactions to metallic and ceramic nanoparticles from orthopedic joint implants can reduce the ALP activity of osteoblasts [23, 39]. In this study, treatment with various doses of nano- and micro-sized Ta particles on MC3T3-E1 cells demonstrated a notable decrease in ALP activity by 25 nm Ta particles. In contrast, there was no significant effect with the micro-sized Ta particles (Fig. 1A). Moreover, compared to micro sizes, nano-sized Ta particles time-dependently inhibited osteoblast ALP activity (Fig. 1B). In comparison to the untreated control group, MC3T3-E1 cells’ morphology changed noticeably when treated with nano- and micro-sized Ta particles at a concentration of 10 µg/mL (Fig. 1C). Previous studies have shown that approximately 2 µg/L of Ta concentration might be generated and present in the peri-implant fluid [9]. More recent studies indicate that metals can significantly accumulate in periprosthetic tissues despite low levels in circulating fluids, and there is often a weak correlation between tissue metal loads and serum or fluid concentrations. Therefore, serum or fluid measurements are not dependable indicators for adherent cells [40]. Moreover, in vitro systems often need higher concentrations compared to in vivo conditions to produce discernible biological effects, owing to the lack of intricate physiological processes like protein binding, metabolism, and clearance present in live organisms [41, 42]. The concentration range of 0–20 µg/mL for Ta particles was chosen to represent physiologically relevant levels observed in human periprosthetic tissues and documented in previous orthopaedic research studies [43, 44]. Osteoblasts demonstrate no toxicity at concentrations of 10–20 µg/mL of metal wear debris studies, whereas higher dosages might have a suppressive effect on cell viability, establishing a physiologically active but nonlethal range of 0–20 µg/mL for mechanistic investigations [4, 43]. Concurrently, in macrophage cell models (RAW264.7) Ta particles were used at low-to-moderate µg/mL doses to provoke quantifiable inflammatory/polarization responses, hence reinforcing the bottom boundary of our range [45]. In conclusion, similar order-of-magnitude concentrations are commonly employed to model wear-particle biology across various implant materials (e.g., Ti/TiO₂ typically at ~ 20–100 µg/mL for osteoblast/osteocyte and macrophage assays), thereby reinforcing the translational relevance of our selected range while preventing supra-toxic conditions [44]. The observed decrease in ALP expression at 1.25 µg/mL, significantly above physiological Ta levels (2 µg/L), indicates a sensitive biological reaction. To thoroughly describe dose-response relationships, elucidate mechanistic insights, and provide a margin for detecting effects at many doses, fundamental studies are often conducted at elevated, supra-physiological levels. Thus, in this study, a dose of 10 µg/mL was selected as a relevant in vitro dose to simulate a high yet clinically plausible particle burden, ensuring a distinct and detectable biological response to clarify the underlying mechanisms, as evidenced by prior research on orthopedic biomaterial particles [46].

Evidence from earlier studies suggests that the osteoblasts’ ability to engulf debris of specific sizes may be responsible for this morphological change [23, 47]. These changes are likely due to internalization processes, specifically phagocytosis. Phagocytosis analysis after Ta particle treatment revealed that the osteoblasts effectively engulfed 25 nm and 10 μm sized Ta particles, while 40–50 μm of Ta particles showed the formation of large-sized phagosomes (Fig. 1D). Additionally, LysosomeTracker 488 staining further confirmed that Ta particles are differently phagocytosed by the osteoblasts and the phenomenon is size-dependent (Fig. 1E). Nano-Ta particles exhibit significantly higher cellular uptake and bioactivity than their micron-sized counterparts at the same mass concentration due to their much larger surface area-to-mass ratio, enabling enhanced interactions with cell membranes and activation of multiple endocytic pathways [43]. This nanoscale size enables more efficient cell entry, enhanced modulation of cellular signaling, and more substantial bioactive effects, including improved regulation of the immune microenvironment and osteogenic activity [44]. In contrast, micro-sized Ta particles remain largely biologically inert and are less efficiently internalized by cells [45]. The osteoblasts did not exhibit any effect after 72 h when the cell viability and cytotoxicity of various Ta particle sizes were analyzed (Fig. 1E&F). Exposure to wear particles hinders the bone-forming process and mineralization in osteoblasts. A reduction in the expression of genes associated with early and late indicators of bone formation, such as ALP, Runx2, OSX, and OCN, has been observed [4, 23]. Additionally, there’s a loss in the ability of osteoblasts to produce type I collagen and facilitate mineralization [4, 23, 47]. Treatment of MC3T3-E1 cells with nano-sized Ta particles reduced the expression of key transcription factors and markers involved in bone formation, such as Runx2, OSX, Col1α, and OCN (Fig. 2A). The suppression of Runx2 and Col1α protein expression increased with higher doses of 25 nm Ta particles (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, nano-sized Ta particle treatment inhibits collagen synthesis and mineralization of MC3T3-E1 cells (Fig. 2C & D), confirming the anti-osteogenic property of nano-sized Ta particles on bone formation. No such effect of micro-sized Ta particle was observed on the analyzed osteogenic properties of osteoblasts (Fig. 2).

Macrophages at the membrane interface become activated when they come into contact with wear debris, whether or not they engulf it. In the microenvironment surrounding the bone implants, this activation causes the release of inflammatory mediators [48]. An upregulation of Cox2 and downregulation of IĸBα confirmed inflammation and activation of the NFĸB signaling pathway in MC3T3-E1, indicating an inflammatory response to the nano-sized Ta particles. In contrast, the micro-sized Ta particles exhibited no such effect (Fig. 3). Numerous studies, including bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing as well as targeted analyses, have identified NF-κB activation as a key signaling mechanism in periprosthetic tissues and myeloid cells exposed to particles. The pharmacologic and genetic inhibition of NF-κB has been shown to alleviate debris-induced osteolysis [27, 49, 50]. Another study on single-cell investigations of loosening tissues reveals the enrichment of immune pathways, including NF-κB/TNF signaling, and highlights osteoimmune interactions, thereby providing a comprehensive, pathway-level context for signaling induced by debris [51]. Wear debris has been shown to generate ROS in bone cells and has been implicated in developing several ailments, such as bone loss during osteolysis [52]. Nano-sized wear debris is considered the most damaging size category due to its ability to increase free radical generation and cause chromosomal damage [34, 53]. Our results showed that the nano-sized Ta particle elicited intracellular ROS in RAW 264.7 cells, followed by the formation of mature osteoclasts (Fig. 4A & B). Moreover, scavenging the intracellular ROS by NAC inhibited the Ta particle-induced osteoclast formation, implying a potential influence of nano-sized Ta particles on osteoclastogenesis, possibly via the ROS signaling pathway (Fig. 4C). In addition to RAW 264.7 cells, the nano-sized Ta particle also significantly induced intracellular ROS in MC3T3-E1 cells (Fig. 5A). Prior to nano-sized Ta particle treatment, pre-treatment of NAC significantly restored the ALP activity of MC3T3 E-1 cells (Fig. 5B). This suggests that ROS generated by the Ta particles might be responsible for the observed decrease in ALP activity. The results emphasize the potential role of ROS in mediating the effects of nano-sized Ta particles on cellular function in osteoblasts and osteoclasts.

The inhibition of Runx2, OSX, and other osteogenic markers (Col1α and OCN) in MC3T3-E1 cells treated with nano-sized Ta particles necessitated an evaluation of Ta’s impact on crucial osteogenic signaling pathways, notably WNT signaling. The WNT signaling pathway controls embryonic development, including the synthesis of bone and cartilage and the osteogenic differentiation of adult mesenchymal stem cells [54]. Suppressing Axin-2 reporter activity, phosphorylation of GSK-3β, and a decrease in β-catenin stability confirmed the adverse effect of nano-sized Ta particles on the WNT signaling activity in MC3T3 E-1 cells (Fig. 6). Furthermore, the marked increase in the expression of WNT signaling antagonists (DKK2, DKK3, SOST, sFRP4, and sFRP5) by several folds in nano-sized Ta-treated MC3T3-E1 cells elucidates the possible mechanism for the suppressed WNT signaling activity and subsequently, the osteogenic potential of osteoprogenitors (Fig. 7). On the contrary, such effects on the WNT signaling pathway were not observed in the micro-sized Ta particles treated with osteoproginators. In vivo studies demonstrate that the inhibition of sclerostin (SOST, a WNT antagonist) reinstates β-catenin signaling and mitigates particle-induced osteolysis. Clinical and sequencing investigations in aseptic loosening further link miRNA/mRNA alterations to the WNT signaling pathway. This explains that a similar mechanism of action might be involved in the case of nano-sized Ta particles in the regulation of Wnt signaling pathways in osteoblasts [55].

Nano-sized Ta particles demonstrated unique antagonism of ROS/Wnt owing to their elevated surface area-to-volume ratio, rapid particle dissolution kinetics, and specific intracellular trafficking [56]. Moreover, the elevated surface area of nano-sized Ta particles offers more reactive sites for engagement with cellular proteins that regulate oxidative and Wnt signaling, hence enhancing their influence on intracellular ROS levels and Wnt pathway activity. Nanoparticle dissolution affects ion concentrations, which in turn influence ROS generation and Wnt signaling via regulators such as Dishevelled (Dvl) and β-catenin [56, 57]. Nano-sized Ta particles effectively facilitate endocytic uptake and trafficking inside cells, potentially modulating ROS scavenging systems and mitochondrial ROS production, which reduces pro-inflammatory signals and Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation. In contrast, larger Ta particles lack similar biophysical and biochemical characteristics. Therefore, a viable strategy for mitigating the effects of metal particle-induced osteolysis may involve targeting therapeutic interventions at the inflammation (Cox-2 and NFĸB signaling), as well as ROS generation and WNT signaling pathways. This approach could facilitate the development of effective treatment options. However, this study utilizes monoculture systems, which allow for the clear attribution of cell-intrinsic effects. Nevertheless, these systems fail to replicate the paracrine and juxtacrine signaling, as well as the multicellular interactions among osteoblasts, osteoclasts, stromal cells, and immune cells that regulate bone remodeling in vivo. Further development of physiologic co-/tri-culture and 3D platforms under controlled mechanical loading, along with validation in animal or clinical peri-prosthetic models, may further validate these in vitro findings and translate them into clinical interventions to prevent peri-prosthetic osteolysis.

Conclusion

An investigation into the wear debris obtained from periprosthetic tissues has revealed a notable distinction in biological activity between nano- and micro-sized particles. In this comparative study, nano-sized Ta wear particles appear to exert a significantly adverse effect on the osteogenic activity of osteoblasts and the osteoclast formation ability compared to micro-sized wear debris. This disruption leads to changes in bone remodeling processes, characterized by an increase in osteoclastogenesis and a concurrent decrease in osteoblastogenesis, which often results in implant failure and osteolysis. These findings underscore the importance of careful analysis of wear debris size and quantity generated by implants in joint replacement surgeries or during revisions. The micro-motion caused during loose implant fixation between Ta bone graft or Ta mesh used during joint arthroplasty should be carefully monitored to avoid generating wear debris of various sizes. Such examination is necessary to mitigate the potential long-term complications associated with implant failure. Furthermore, future investigations should broaden the scope by examining the effects of nano-sized Ta particles on various cell types involved in implant-induced osteolysis, including macrophages, fibroblast-like synoviocytes, and osteocytes.

While our study focused solely on murine osteoprogenitor and osteoclast cell lines, the need for validation in more clinically relevant in vitro models incorporating human primary osteoblasts, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), osteoclasts, or other in vivo osteolysis models is required. Notably, micro-sized Ta particles in our investigation had insignificant impacts on osteoblasts and osteoclasts; nevertheless, as previously discussed, a few references have reported otherwise, underlining the different impacts of particle size on cellular responses. This study offers valuable mechanistic insights into the impact of nano-sized particles on osteoblasts and osteoclasts, highlighting potential therapeutic targets, including ROS, NF-κB, and Wnt signaling pathways, for addressing metal particle-induced osteolysis. Therefore, to enhance the durability and effectiveness of orthopedic implants, further studies are necessary to understand the complexities of implant wear debris size and production quantity in relation to cellular absorption and long-term physiological effects.
