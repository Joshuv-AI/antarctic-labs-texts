Development and validation of the synthetic index of palliative care integration into primary health care
Authors: Carolina Muñoz Olivar, Juan Sebastián Gomez Quintero, Fernando Pastrana Rendon, Carla Taramasco Toro, Miguel Antonio Sanchez-Cardenas, Carlos Javier Avendaño-Vásquez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntegrating palliative care (PC) into primary health care (PHC) is essential to achieve universal health coverage and equity. However, most countries lack concise and validated tools to monitor this process. This study developed and validated a composite index to measure the degree of PC integration into PHC at the departmental level in Colombia.MethodsAn ecological, cross-sectional study was conducted across 33 territorial units in Colombia (32 departments plus Bogotá D.C.) in 2024. Eight of 15 candidate indicators were retained to construct the index. Each indicator was normalized using a Min–Max linear transformation [0–1] and analyzed through principal component analysis with varimax rotation. The Synthetic Index of Palliative Care Integration into Primary Health Care (SIPCI) was built using a hierarchical weighting scheme combining domain-level and within-domain weights. Stability was assessed through jackknife leave-one-out, alternative normalizations methods, and four robustness scenarios including Monte Carlo perturbations. External validity was examined through correlations with socioeconomic and service-related variables.ResultsSIPCI scores ranged from 0.04 to 0.71 (mean 0.38, SD 0.13). Overall, 52% of departments showed low or medium-low integration, while 48% showed medium-high or high integration. A clear spatial gradient was observed, with higher integration in central departments and Bogotá, and lower integration in Amazon and Orinoquia regions. Sampling adequacy was good (KMO = 0.742). Information loss was low (NMSE = 0.13; 0.10 after indicator reduction). Leave-one-out analyses showed strong concordance with the baseline index (Spearman > 0.94; Kendall > 0.86). Five subcomponents were identified, which rural accessibility and undergraduate PC training contributing most. The correlation between SIPCI and departmental income was positive but moderate, indicating that economic capacity may facilitate, but does not determine integration.ConclusionsThe SIPCI provides a reproducible, system-level measure for monitoring and benchmarking territorial patterns of palliative care integration within primary health care in Colombia. The findings describe marked geographic heterogeneity and support the use of SIPCI for routine monitoring and planning, without implying causal mechanisms or patient-level effects. Although the index showed good internal coherence and robustness, further studies may help strengthen its construct validity and assess its applicability in other settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15191-w.

Background

Primary Health Care (PHC) is recognized by the World Health Organization (WHO) as the cornerstone of health systems and a public health strategy to achieve Universal Health Coverage (UHC) and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) [1, 2]. Within this strategy, palliative care (PC) is considered an ethical responsibility of health systems and an essential function of PHC [3, 4]. The integration of PC into PHC ensures that people with serious or life-limiting conditions receive continuous, person-centred, and equitable care across all stages of life and all levels of the health system [5].

The Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) has emphasized that PHC is not limited to the first level of care but rather represents a comprehensive approach that coordinates the full range of health services, from health promotion to specialized interventions. The “30–30–30 PHC for Universal Health” commitment, launched in 2019, urges countries in the Americas to reduce access barriers by 30%, allocate at least 6% of GDP to health, and invest 30% of public expenditure in first-level care to strengthen integrated service networks [6, 7]. In this framework, integrating PC within PHC is fundamental for ensuring that equity, accessibility, and continuity become operational realities rather than policy aspirations.

Despite global recognition of PC as a public health priority, its development and integration remain highly uneven across regions. In Latin America, only a few countries have achieved generalized provision of PC (Global Development Level 3b), and significant gaps persist between urban and rural areas [8]. Colombia, an upper middle–income country with a Gini index of 53.9 in 2023 [9], illustrates these disparities. Although national legislation and public policies support PC as part of the essential health benefits, service availability and human resources remain concentrated in large urban centres, leaving peripheral regions underserved [10, 11].

Assessing the degree of PC integration into PHC requires a multidimensional approach that extends beyond service counts or financial inputs. It should include indicators reflecting system capacity, policy implementation, professional training, and equity in access and continuity of care [2, 12]. Composite indicators can capture this complexity by summarizing multiple dimensions into a single, transparent measure. When properly designed, they enhance comparability and support evidence-informed policymaking, but if poorly constructed, they can mislead interpretation or promote simplistic conclusions [13, 14].

This study addresses this gap by developing and validating the Synthetic Index of Palliative Care Integration into Primary Health Care (SIPCI), a composite measure designed to quantify the extent of integration across Colombia’s departments. The SIPCI combines palliative care development indicators and PHC system metrics to provide a comprehensive, context-sensitive evaluation of how effectively PC is embedded within the broader PHC strategy.

In this study, integration of palliative care into primary health care is conceptualized as a multidimensional, system-level capacity through which palliative care resources, services, competencies, and organizational processes become embedded within primary health care structures and functions. Accordingly, integration is operationalized through dimensions reflecting: (i) workforce training and availability of essential resources, (ii) provision of palliative care services and population access, (iii) territorial and rural accessibility, (iv) continuity and responsiveness across levels of care, and (v) contextual conditions that influence the need for and delivery of palliative care within PHC systems.

From this perspective, these dimensions are not viewed as isolated determinants or outcomes, but as observable manifestations of the extent to which palliative care has been incorporated into primary health care. This conceptualization was informed by the WHO framework for palliative care development, the WHO–UNICEF Primary Health Care Measurement Framework, and two complementary national studies. The first identified and prioritized indicators of palliative care integration through a modified Delphi process supported by expert stratification procedures and self-organizing maps, while the second explored how experts conceptualized integration through qualitative analysis of expert comments. Together, these studies contributed to the conceptualization of integration as a system-level construct encompassing workforce preparedness, service availability, accessibility, continuity of care, policy implementation, and health system responsiveness within primary health care settings [15, 16].

Accordingly, SIPCI is intended as a departmental-level monitoring tool to describe and compare territorial patterns of palliative care integration within PHC; it is not designed to evaluate clinical outcomes, rank providers, or support causal inference.

Methods

Study objective

The objective of this study was to design and validate a synthetic indicator to assess the degree of integration of PC into PHC in Colombia at the departmental level in 2024.

Study design

We conducted an observational, ecological, and cross-sectional study. The units of analysis comprised all territorial entities of Colombia, including 32 departments and one capital district. All territorial units were eligible and included; no exclusions were made. This configuration represented a complete national census rather than a sample. All data corresponded to the year 2024, the most recent period with complete departmental information available.

Accordingly, the unit of inference is the department (health system level); therefore, all results should be interpreted at the territorial level and do not imply individual-level effects or causal relationships.

The methodological process followed the OECD/European Commission–JRC handbook for constructing composite indicators [14]. The analytical flow included conceptualization, indicator selection, normalization, multivariate analysis, weighting, aggregation, sensitivity and robustness assessment, and external validation. Conceptualization

Integration of PC into PHC was understood as the effective incorporation of palliative principles and services within the essential components of PHC, ensuring continuity, accessibility, equity, and quality of care throughout the life course. This approach seeks to articulate health care across levels and service modalities, in both urban and rural settings, to provide a comprehensive and coordinated response to the palliative needs of the population.

The framework also recognizes three strategic and operational levers that strengthen PHC: integrated health services, multisectoral policy and action, and empowered people and communities [2].

Indicator selection and data handling

The main outcome was the Synthetic Index of Palliative Care Integration (SIPCI), constructed from normalized quantitative indicators grouped in structural, service, and outcome domains.

Partial indicators were derived through a sequential mixed-methods process. An initial pool of candidate indicators was identified during a pre-Delphi stage and subsequently evaluated through a modified two-round Delphi study supported by expert stratification procedures and Self-Organizing Maps (SOM) to identify representative and discriminative indicators of palliative care integration within primary health care [15, 19]. A complementary qualitative analysis of expert comments was then conducted to refine the conceptual framework and indicator set, providing additional insight into how integration was understood and operationalized by experts [16]. Only quantitative indicators with departmental data were included in SIPCI construction; indicators available only at the national level, lacking territorial variability, or not operationalizable at the departmental level were excluded.

Variables were inspected for distributional properties, extreme values, and polarity consistency (all aligned as “higher = better”). No missing data were identified, and outliers were retained as they represented real observations. For multivariate analysis, binary (dichotomous) variables were also excluded, retaining only continuous quantitative indicators.

All retained indicators were grouped a priori into structural, service, and outcome domains, representing system determinants, service delivery processes, and system-level outcomes related to palliative care integration within PHC.

It is important to note that SIPCI combines indicators reflecting system capacity and readiness, such as workforce training, service availability, and access to essential medicines, with indicators reflecting contextual need or demand, such as the burden of chronic conditions requiring palliative care. Therefore, higher SIPCI values should not be interpreted as indicating better performance or higher quality of care, but rather as higher measured levels of system integration and responsiveness in the context of population needs.

Dimensionality reduction and weighting

Principal component analysis (PCA)

PCA was used to concentrate multidimensional information into a reduced set of components explaining at least 85% of cumulative variance. We then applied orthogonal Varimax rotation to enhance interpretability and assigned each indicator to the component where its absolute loading was highest. Indicators were selected using a global 85th percentile threshold on the absolute rotated loadings; duplicated indicators across components were kept only in the component with the highest loading. The 85th percentile loading threshold was applied to retain the most informative indicator(s) per component, ensuring that each retained indicator was strongly associated with a single latent dimension. This criterion helped avoid double weighting of indicators conveying overlapping information when loadings exceeded the threshold across multiple components. As a result, a parsimonious set of indicators was obtained while preserving most of the information relevant for routine monitoring and territorial comparison.

Representativeness and reconstruction error

We computed communalities (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_i^2$$\end{document}) and the normalized mean squared error (NMSE) of reconstruction to quantify information loss after dimensionality reduction. These ranges are informed by guidance on stability and information loss in composite indicators and PCA reconstruction error [14, 20, 21].

External validity

External validity was assessed via Pearson correlations between SIPCI and theoretically related variables: Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita, Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI), health system coverage, share of home-care services offering PC, opioid consumption per capita, pediatric serious health-related suffering index, Human Development Index (HDI), and number of PC-specific services per population.

Data sources

Data were obtained from official public sources: the National Administrative Department of Statistics (DANE) [24], the High-Cost Account (CAC) [25], the Ministry of Health open data platform (OTIC) [26], and the Colombian Palliative Care Observatory (OCCP) [27], complemented by peer-reviewed literature and technical reports [2, 12].

Data extraction occurred between1 February and 1 July 2025, using the dataset versions available on those dates. All sources correspond to the most recent 2024 departmental releases. All indicators were extracted from standardized administrative databases, ensuring consistent measurement definitions and full comparability across departments. No measurement procedures differed among territorial units or across data sources.

Detailed definitions, data sources, numerators and denominators, and classification of indicators as reflecting system capacity or contextual need for the eight indicators retained in the final SIPCI are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Results

Indicator selection and internal consistency

Descriptive statistics were computed for all 33 territorial units. No missing data were found for any indicator. Fifteen candidate indicators were initially identified through the Delphi process. Eight belonged to the Palliative Care Development Indicators domain (53%), mainly distributed across subdomains such as essential medicines (n=1), training and education (n=4), and health service delivery/needs estimation (n=3). Seven indicators (47%) originated from the PHC Components framework, corresponding to determinants and health burden, service delivery outputs, integrated services (access/equity, effective coverage, and utilization of services), Impact (suffering reduction / avoidable mortality), and multisectoral policy or financial protection. According to Donabedian’s model, these indicators included three structural, two process, and ten outcome measures. The weight distribution was balanced across domains, supporting the multidimensional nature of the index. Although Access to Health Services in Rural Areas showed the largest individual contribution, SIPCI should not be interpreted as a measure of rural accessibility alone. Rather, rural accessibility represents one of several complementary dimensions of palliative care integration within primary health care. The final index combines indicators reflecting workforce preparedness, service availability, territorial accessibility, population needs, and system responsiveness, Consequently, SIPCI is intended to reflect the overall degree of palliative care integration within PHC systems rather than the performance of any single domain.

Domain weights reflect the relative contribution of each latent dimension to the overall SIPCI, based on the proportion of variance explained by the corresponding principal component. Within each domain, indicator weights represent the relative influence of each indicator within that dimension, derived from the squared rotated loadings and normalized so that weights sum to one. This hierarchical weighting approach preserves the multidimensional structure of palliative care integration while improving transparency and interpretability for health system users.

How to interpret SIPCI scores

SIPCI values range from 0 to 1 and reflect relative levels of palliative care integration into primary health care at the departmental (system) level, based on the eight indicators and their fixed weights. Values closer to 1 indicate higher observed integration relative to other departments, whereas lower values indicate a position at the lower end of the national distribution; importantly, they do not imply the absence of palliative care services. No department reached the theoretical maximum value of 1, indicating that none simultaneously achieved the highest observed values across all indicators. For descriptive purposes, departments were grouped into quartiles (Q1–Q4), which represent relative positions within the national distribution rather than absolute performance thresholds. As a reference, a difference of 0.10 in SIPCI corresponds to a 10-percentage-point change on the index scale and should be interpreted as a meaningful shift in the combined weighted indicator profile, particularly when changes occur in higher-weight dimensions.

Sensitivity, robustness, and uncertainty analyses

The leave-one-out (Jackknife) sensitivity analysis indicated strong stability of SIPCI. When each indicator was removed in turn and weights were re-normalized, correlations with the base index remained very high (Spearman = 0.96; Kendall’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau_b = 0.86$$\end{document}; mean absolute rank error [MAE] low (1.81)). The least influential indicators were 24/7 Pharmacies Dispensing Opioids (Spearman = 0.99; Kendall = 0.94; MAE = 0.91) and Proportion of Population with Access to Health Services (Spearman = 0.98; Kendall = 0.89; MAE = 1.33). The most influential was Access to Health Services in Rural Areas (Spearman = 0.89; Kendall = 0.72; MAE = 3.51). Overall, the findings confirmed that no single indicator dominated the structure of SIPCI and that the index remained stable to individual exclusions Supplementary Table 6.

Alternative normalization methods (z-score, winsorized Min–Max, robust Min–Max, median/IQR, and rank scaling) showed high consistency with the baseline SIPCI (Spearman > 0.94). The IQR, and rank-based variants presented slightly higher ranking errors (MAE = 2.36 and 2.67, respectively), while z-score, winsorized, and robust Min–Max maintained excellent stability (MAE < 1.1) Supplementary Table 7.

Four alternative weighting scenarios further confirmed structural robustness: R1: Equal weights, high stability (ρ = 0.98, MAE = 1.58); R2: Equal weights by domain with intra-domain allocation by squared loadings, moderate stability (ρ = 0.92, MAE = 2.73); R3: Geometric aggregation weighted by explained variance, lower stability (ρ = 0.83, MAE = 3.75); R4: Monte Carlo simulation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm 20\%$$\end{document} perturbation, n = 1000), median ρ = 0.99 (p5–p95 = [0.98–1.00]) Supplementary Table 8. These results confirmed that SIPCI was robust to alternative assumptions regarding weighting and normalization.

Across plausible methodological choices, including indicator exclusion, alternative normalization, weighting schemes, and Monte Carlo perturbations, departmental rankings changed minimally, indicating that SIPCI is unlikely to be an artifact of any single analytic decision.

External validity

To guide interpretation of the external validation analyses, external variables were conceptually grouped into two categories. Capacity-related validators (e.g., opioid availability and share of home-care services offering palliative care) were interpreted as proxies of service availability and health system capacity for delivering palliative care. In contrast, context- or need-related validators (e.g., poverty indices, suffering indicators, and number of patients with palliative-relevant diagnoses) were interpreted as reflecting underlying population needs and disease burden rather than higher-quality or better-performing integration. General socioeconomic indicators (GDP per capita, Human Development Index, and overall health system coverage) were treated as contextual benchmarks that characterize the broader development environment in which palliative care integration occurs, rather than as direct measures of palliative care capacity or need.

All associations were estimated with Pearson’s correlation coefficient r. SIPCI showed a strong positive association with GDP per capita (r = 0.62, p < 0.000) and strong negative association with the MPI (r = -0.64, p < 0.000), indicating that higher levels of palliative care integration are more frequently observed in departments with greater economic development and lower poverty. In contrast, the association with overall health system coverage was weak and non-significant (r = 0.25, p = 0.155). The correlation with the HDI was also weak and non-significant (r = 0.19, p = 0.296), suggesting that general development indicators alone do not capture specific service capacities required for palliative care integration (Supplementary Table 9).

Regarding capacity-related service indicators, SIPCI showed a strong positive association with opioid availability (r = 0.70, p < 0.000) and with the proportion of home-care services offering palliative care (r = 0.68, p < 0.000), supporting the interpretation that higher SIPCI values reflect better access to essential palliative care services.In contrast, the association with the number of specialized palliative care services per population was weaker (r = 0.39, p < 0,026), suggesting that numerical service availability alone does not fully capture territorial or functional integration.

For context- and need-related validators, SIPCI showed a strong positive association with the number of patients attended for palliative-relevant diagnoses (r = 0.71, p < 0.000) and a moderate positive association with the pediatric serious health-related suffering index (r = 0.58, p < 0.000). Given that higher values of these indicators represent greater burden or suffering, these associations were interpreted as reflecting alignment between higher levels of integration and greater underlying need, rather than improved outcomes. All estimates were unadjusted correlations, as no individual-level confounding factors were applicable in this ecological design.

Discussion

This study aimed to design and validate a new composite indicator, the SIPCI Index of Palliative Care Integration into Primary Health Care, to assess the degree of integration of PC within the PHC system in the departments of Colombia in 2024. The methodological process followed international guidelines for developing composite indicators [14, 28–30], including internal consistency tests, PCA with varimax rotation, and extensive sensitivity and robustness analyses. The final model showed good adequacy (KMO = 0.742) and preserved nearly 90% of the original information (NMSE ≈ 0.10), confirming internal coherence and parsimony. High Spearman ( > 0.94) and Kendall’s ( > 0.86) coefficients confirmed the structural stability of the index [31, 32].

About 52% (17 of 33) of departments were classified as having low or medium-low integration, and 48% (16 of 33) as medium-high or high. Integration showed a marked spatial gradient: central regions and Bogotá scored highest, while Amazon and Orinoquia departments remained lowest. These disparities mirror the territorial inequities already reported in Colombia’s health-service distribution [23, 33].

The positive but moderate correlation between SIPCI and departmental GDP per capita (r = 0.62) should be interpreted cautiously. GDP reflects productive capacity, not the allocation or efficiency of health expenditure. Economic development may facilitate integration but does not ensure it. These findings suggest that the translation of financial resources into effective service integration depends on governance, workforce distribution, and intersectoral coordination. Several departments with intermediate GDP; such as Cundinamarca, Atlántico, and Arauca, had limited integration, suggesting that governance, workforce distribution, and service coordination matter more than macroeconomic wealth alone. Conversely, departments with both low GDP and low SIPCI, including Vichada and Guainía, combine economic and institutional barriers [24, 34, 35].

These findings are consistent with the interpretation that economic capacity may facilitate palliative care integration, but it does not reliably predict it. Differences observed across departments may reflect variation in governance, workforce distribution, and service organization, which SIPCI captures descriptively at the system level. In this context, existing national legislation and regulatory frameworks provide a reference, but the degree of integration observed across territories remains heterogeneous [27, 36, 37]. At the policy level, SIPCI can help identify domains where implementation appears weaker, such as workforce training or rural accessibility, and can inform prioritization and follow-up within existing regulatory frameworks.

From a public-health perspective, these results align with the WHO position that the integration of PC into PHC is essential to achieve UHC and continuity of care [2]. The SIPCI captures this multidimensional nature through its five thematic axes (TAPS): training and resources (TAPS-1), service availability and access (TAPS-2), rural accessibility (TAPS-3), chronic-disease needs (TAPS-4), and continuity and responsiveness (TAPS-5). The indicator “Access to health services in rural areas,” which showed the highest communality (0.99), emphasizes the importance of territorial accessibility as a structural condition for integration rather than a direct measure of equity. Together, these dimensions portray integration as a systemic rather than a purely clinical phenomenon [2, 12, 38].

At the same time, it is important to distinguish between statistical validity and construct validity. The analyses conducted in this study provide evidence of internal coherence, dimensional structure, and robustness under alternative weighting and normalization scenarios. However, these findings alone do not fully establish construct validity. As highlighted in the OECD Handbook on Constructing Composite Indicators, statistical techniques can support indicator selection, weighting, and dimensionality reduction, but the conceptual validity of a composite index also depends on the theoretical framework underlying its construction [14]. In the present study, construct validity was supported through the use of internationally recognized frameworks for palliative care and primary health care, a structured Delphi process, self-organizing maps, and complementary qualitative analysis of expert perspectives [15, 16]. Taken together, these conceptual and empirical procedures support the interpretation of SIPCI as a measure of palliative care integration within primary health care. Nevertheless, integration remains a multidimensional and context-dependent concept, and future studies should continue examining the performance of SIPCI in other settings and against additional external criteria.

Education and workforce capacity emerged as critical drivers. Undergraduate training in PC for nursing and medical students contributed substantially to SIPCI performance, confirming evidence that early inclusion of PC in general curricula strengthens PHC responsiveness and community care [39–42]. In Latin America, integration efforts remain fragmented; PC services often coexist with PHC programmes but are rarely functionally aligned [8, 43]. Strengthening professional competencies at the primary-care level is therefore a realistic entry point for advancing integration across the region [44, 45].

Service availability also influences integration. The inclusion of “home-care services offering palliative care” shows that first-level service capacity contributes to functional integration. However, the indicator “hospitalizations for chronic conditions” aggregates all levels of care, which limits its interpretability. For this initial analysis, it is considered a proxy indicator suggesting that, at a minimum, people with chronic conditions who require health care are in contact with the health system, rather than a direct measure of integrated or high-quality care [46–48]. Future analyses should examine patient-level trajectories to assess whether higher integration correlates with fewer preventable hospital admissions. This aligns with the notion that integrated networks, rather than isolated services, drive continuity across levels of care, as also emphasized in the PAHO Strategic Plan on resilient health systems based on primary health care [48, 49]. When patients can move seamlessly among home-based, ambulatory, and hospital care, the system demonstrates the degree of integration that palliative care requires to be both timely and equitable. The SIPCI thus offers an initial framework to identify territorial differences in service organization and to guide future monitoring of integration across levels of care.

Beyond service organization, system financing and financial protection also shape integration. According to the World Bank, out-of-pocket health expenditure in Colombia represented 15% of current health expenditure in 2023 [50]. Values approaching the 20% threshold identified by WHO as high financial risk level [51] suggest growing pressure on households and potential barriers to effective access. When families must assume increasing direct costs, the capacity of PHC services to deliver integrated palliative approaches is undermined. These financial and organizational barriers converge to explain why economic capacity alone does not translate into integration. This evidence situates the SIPCI within a broader context where financial protection is declining despite nearly universal insurance coverage.

Departments with relatively high GDP but modest SIPCI scores, such as Atlántico or Cundinamarca, likely reflect persistent fragmentation: hospital-centred models, weak coordination between service levels, and limited implementation of national PC policies. These challenges are consistent with previous analyses showing that palliative care in Colombia and Latin America remains concentrated in urban tertiary settings rather than embedded within PHC [5]. The SIPCI captures how such structural gaps translate into measurable disparities.

From a policy perspective, findings suggest that improving integration is likely to depend on the operationalization of existing national PC legislation through effective implementation within PHC programmes, particularly in rural areas. Priorities include workforce development, equitable distribution of essential resources, and monitoring of integration progress through composite metrics such as the SIPCI. Because national policies already define regulatory frameworks, departmental actions should focus on execution and coordination rather than new legislation [47].

The SIPCI is intended as a system-level monitoring tool. It can be used to support routine follow-up of palliative care integration within primary health care, to identify territorial gaps in domains such as workforce training, rural accessibility, and service organization, and to inform the implementation of national palliative care policies at the departmental level. SIPCI is not a patient outcome index, not a hospital performance score, and it is not intended for causal inference. It provides a descriptive view of integration and system responsiveness at the health-system level, rather than a measure of individual-level quality of care.

From an implementation perspective, SIPCI can be used by departments to identify which system domains are most strongly associated with lower levels of palliative care integration. By examining domain-specific contributions, decision-makers can distinguish whether gaps are primarily driven by workforce training, rural accessibility, service availability, or system responsiveness, and can prioritize context-appropriate actions within existing policy frameworks. For example, low scores in training-related domains may support expanding undergraduate palliative care curricula, while deficits in rural accessibility may highlight the need to strengthen outreach pathways or service coverage outside urban centers.

The practical applicability of SIPCI is supported by several facilitators, including the use of existing administrative data sources, open access to data and code, and stable weighting derived from internal structure rather than normative assumptions. At the same time, potential barriers should be acknowledged, such as uneven data quality or under-registration across territories, limited subnational granularity for some indicators, and the risk of misinterpretation if the index is used as a punitive ranking rather than as a descriptive monitoring tool.

Limitations

This study has limitations. Being ecological and cross-sectional, causal inferences cannot be made. No missing data were detected, although under-registration of some indicators may persist.

Nevertheless, sensitivity and robustness analyses confirmed both internal and external consistency. Some indicators, such as hospitalizations for chronic diseases and the number of patients attended, should be interpreted with caution. Their positive correlation with SIPCI likely reflects greater demand and need for care rather than higher integration or performance. In this first measurement of palliative care integration, hospitalizations were used as a proxy for system contact and service demand; however, future studies should incorporate disaggregated data to differentiate home-based, ambulatory, and inpatient care. Tracking SIPCI scores over time and linking them to patient-level outcomes, such as hospital readmissions, symptom control, or place of death, could further strengthen its interpretive validity.

Correlations used for external validation were exploratory and were interpreted primarily in terms of magnitude and theoretical coherence, rather than formal hypothesis testing.

Although multiple procedures were used to support the conceptual basis of SIPCI, including international frameworks, expert consensus, self-organizing maps, and qualitative analysis, the extensive statistical validation performed in this study does not by itself guarantee that the index fully captures all dimensions of palliative care integration. As with other composite indicators representing complex health-system constructs, SIPCI should be interpreted as an evidence-informed operationalization of integration rather than as an exhaustive representation of the concept. Further studies in different contexts and using additional external criteria may help strengthen its construct validity.

The transferability of the SIPCI to other countries depends on the availability of comparable subnational indicators and on contextual adaptation of domains and weights. Differences in health system organization, data infrastructure, and palliative care policy frameworks may limit the direct application of the index without contextual calibration.

In addition to these data-driven extensions, future research may complement this system-level approach by examining contextual and operational factors that affect the implementation of palliative care, such as facility-level constraints, management of controlled medicines, and ethical considerations related to resource allocation, using qualitative or policy-oriented study designs.

Conclusions

The SIPCI provides an operational and reproducible monitoring and benchmarking instrument, offering moderate-strength evidence for system-level assessment of palliative care integration into primary health care at the departmental level in Colombia. The results describe marked territorial heterogeneity, indicating that higher levels of economic development are not consistently associated with higher levels of measured integration.

SIPCI should not be interpreted as a measure of clinical quality, patient outcomes, or causal impact. Its primary value lies in supporting routine monitoring, identifying territorial gaps, and informing planning and follow-up across specific system domains, such as workforce training, access to essential medicines, service availability, and rural accessibility.

By making multidimensional patterns of integration visible at the system level, SIPCI offers a transparent tool to support health system analysis and policy discussion within existing regulatory frameworks. Future research may complement this approach by examining patient-level trajectories and outcomes using designs suited for causal inference.

Although the results support the internal coherence, robustness, and practical applicability of SIPCI, further research is needed to continue examining its construct validity and performance in other settings. As with other composite indicators representing complex health-system constructs, SIPCI should be interpreted as an evidence-informed operationalization of palliative care integration rather than as its only possible representation. Future applications in different contexts may help refine and strengthen its interpretive validity.
