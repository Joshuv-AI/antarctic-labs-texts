Leveraging data-driven risk-adapted surveillance strategies after disease progression in locally advanced rectal cancer
Authors: Jianjian Qiu, Yuling Ye, Yilin Yu, Zhiping Wang, Liang Hong, Lingdong Shao, Baihua Yang, Junxin Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-progression survival (PPS) is a critical endpoint in oncology, yet predictors of PPS and data-driven strategies for post-progression management in locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) remain undefined. This study aimed to identify prognostic factors for PPS, develop a predictive tool, and formulate risk-adapted surveillance strategies based on conditional survival to optimize long-term patient management.MethodsThe relationship between PPS and overall survival (OS) using spearman analysis. Independent predictors of PPS were identified through a combination of LASSO regression, machine learning approaches, and Cox regression, and a PPS prediction model was constructed. Model interpretability was elucidated using SHAP. Conditional PPS survival rates and annual recurrence risks were evaluated using Kaplan-Meier curve. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were employed to explore the association between a risk score and PPS.ResultsAmong the cohort, 161 patients experienced disease progression. PPS was positively correlated with OS. Vascular tumor thrombus, perineural invasion, extramural venous invasion, body mass index, tumor size, and lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio were identified as independent prognostic factors. The model achieved AUCs of 0.767, 0.801, and 0.802 at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively, outperforming individual variables and TNM staging. The C-index of the model is 0.745. RCS analysis indicated a nonlinear relationship between higher risk score and worse PPS. After adjusting for other confounders, multi-model analysis demonstrated further supporting the positive association between risk score and adverse outcomes. Conditional survival analyses revealed that, across risk groups, longer PPS corresponded to higher survival probabilities and lower recurrence risks. Based on these findings, follow-up strategies should implement intensive surveillance for high-risk patients while adopting relatively streamlined monitoring for low-risk patients.ConclusionThis study provides the first integrated, data-driven framework for predicting PPS and personalizing surveillance in LARC. The validated nomogram and conditional survival-based strategies enable risk-adapted follow-up, supporting a shift from empirical to evidence-based management after disease progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16433-0.Keywords: Post-progression survival, Predictive model, Conditional survival, Individualized follow-up strategies, Locally advanced rectal cancer

Introduction

Rectal cancer (RC) is one of the most common malignant tumors in China [1]. In 2022, approximately 260,000 new cases were diagnosed, with a trend toward younger age at presentation [2, 3]. About 30% of patients initially present with locally advanced disease, a subgroup for whom combined treatment strategies are essential to improve cure rates and quality of life [4]. The current therapeutic paradigm centers on neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (CRT) followed by total mesorectal excision (TME), with the aim of achieving curative resection while preserving function [5]. Although immunotherapy significantly reduce recurrence risk and improve clinical objective response rates in patients with mismatch repair deficiency or high microsatellite instability, these represent only about 5–10% of RC cases [6, 7]. Moreover, nearly 35% of patients experience disease progression after treatment, and the median overall survival (OS) typically remains less than 24 months [8–10].

In oncology clinical research, OS and progression-free survival (PFS) have long served as primary endpoints [11, 12]. However, the factors underlying these endpoints are complex and multifactorial, and they have direct implications for therapeutic strategies and follow-up decisions. In this context, post-progression survival (PPS) has emerged as an increasingly valuable complementary metric, offering a more nuanced reflection of patient survival benefit and treatment effect across multiple dimensions [13]. First, as a key efficacy indicator, PPS is closely associated with OS in the research on tumors and other chronic diseases. In studies of locally advanced non-small cell lung cancer and other conditions, the impact of PPS on OS is even greater than that of PFS, suggesting that PPS can better reflect the OS benefits of patients [14]. Second, PPS helps evaluate the effectiveness of treatments administered after disease progression. For patients who relapse following first-line therapy, PPS can reveal survival gains attributable to subsequent interventions, such as targeted or immune-based treatments [15, 16]. Third, PPS compensates for the limitations of PFS and OS. Some drugs perform well in terms of PFS, but if the treatment effect after progression is poor, there may be no significant improvement in OS; in this case, PPS can reveal the real survival outcome [17, 18]. Fourth, PPS promotes the optimization of clinical research design. In trial design, PPS can serve as a secondary endpoint or an auxiliary analysis indicator [19]. Fifth, it aids in guiding individualized treatment. By analyzing the influencing factors of PPS and combining the baseline characteristics of patients (such as physical condition, gene mutation status, etc.), more precise treatment can be formulated for different patients [20].

Despite its clinical relevance, research on PPS in locally advanced (LARC) and its application to personalized follow-up planning remains limited. The present study therefore aims to identify adverse prognostic factors for PPS in LARC by integrating demographic, pathological, inflammatory, and nutritional variables through a combination of LASSO regression, machine learning, and Cox regression analysis. Based on these factors, we will construct a predictive risk model for PPS and propose an individualized follow-up strategy to enhance evidence-based clinical decision-making and optimize healthcare resource allocation.

Materials and methods

Patients

We retrospectively collected data from patients diagnosed with LARC at our center from 2014 to 2020. Inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) histologically confirmed RC; (3) post-operative pathological stage II or III; (4) Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) score ≥ 70. Exclusion criteria: (1) history of other malignancies; (2) presence of severe infection or other major comorbidities; (3) incomplete clinical data; (4) loss to follow-up. A total of 557 patients met the inclusion criteria. All patients were staged according to the jointly developed TNM staging system by the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) and the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration and approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Provincial Tumor Hospital. As this study is a retrospective study, there is no need to seek further informed consent from the patients related to this study.

Treatment

All the enrolled patients in this study received a comprehensive treatment based on CRT combined with TME. Specifically, the CRT protocol strictly followed clinical guidelines, mainly consisting of two standardized modes: long-course radiotherapy (total dose of 45–50.4 Gy, completed in 25–28 sessions, with each session of 1.8–2.0 Gy) and short-course radiotherapy (total dose of 25 Gy, completed in 5 sessions, with each session of 5 Gy). All patients received concurrent chemotherapy based on fluorouracil drugs during radiotherapy, aiming to increase the rate of pathological complete response, enhance local control, and reduce pathological stage. The timing of surgery was determined according to the radiotherapy plan: patients receiving long-course radiotherapy usually underwent surgery 6–8 weeks after the completion of radiotherapy to ensure that the tumor had fully regressed; while patients receiving short-course radiotherapy could undergo surgery within 1 week after the completion of radiotherapy. All surgeries were performed by experienced colorectal surgeons in accordance with the standard TME principles. The surgical approach (open, laparoscopic, or robot-assisted) will be determined based on the patient’s condition and the surgeon’s experience. Ultimately, all patients who underwent surgery successfully achieved R0 resection, and the postoperative pathological examination confirmed that the margins were negative.

Data collection

We retrospectively collected data from patients with LARC at our center using the electronic medical records system. Demographic features included age and sex. Tumor pathological data encompassed postoperative stage, tumor markers, and the following histopathological invasion characteristics: extramural vascular invasion (EMVI), mesorectal fascia invasion (MRF), perineural invasion, vascular tumor thrombus, tumor deposits, and anus preservation. Inflammatory markers included the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), the systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR), neutrophil-to-monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (NMLR), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR). Nutritional indices included body mass index (BMI), prognostic nutritional index (PNI), platelet-to-albumin ratio (PAR), and advanced lung cancer inflammation index (ALI. The formulas for inflammatory indicators and nutritional indicators are as follows: SII=neutrophil*platelet/lymphocyte; SIRI= neutrophil*monocyte/lymphocyte; LMR= lymphocyte/monocyte; NMLR=(monocyte+lymphocyte)/neutrophil; NLR=neutrophil/lymphocyte; PLR= platelet/lymphocyte; BMI=weight/height2; PNI=albumin + 5* lymphocyte; PAR= platelet/albumin; ALI = BMI × albumin/NLR.

Study endpoints

The primary endpoint is PPS, defined as the time from the occurrence of tumor progression to death from any cause or to the last follow-up. Secondary endpoints include OS and PFS. OS is defined as the period from the start of treatment until death due to any cause or until the end of follow-up; PFS is defined as the period from the start of treatment until disease progression, death due to any cause, or until the end of the last follow-up, whichever occurs first.

Conditional survival

Conditional survival (CS) probability refers to the probability that a patient will survive a certain future period after receiving treatment. For example, for cancer patients who have survived for one year, CS can predict the probability of how much longer they will live in the future. CS focuses on the survival status of patients after a specific time point and can dynamically reflect the changes in patients’ prognosis over time. Based on CS, the probability of conditional PPS can be derived.

Follow-up strategy

According to guidelines, follow-up frequency for patients with LARC is as follows: every 3–6 months within the first 2 years after completion of treatment; every 6 months from years 2 to 5; and annually thereafter. Based on the annual recurrence risk distribution of the low-, moderate- and high-risk groups in this research cohort, combined with the frequency range recommended by clinical guidelines, 25% and 12.5% were selected as the thresholds for differentiating intensive, routine and low-frequency follow-up. Follow-up assessments include: physical examination, tumor markers, endoscopy, chest and abdominal CT, and pelvic MRI.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26) and R software (version 4.5.2). The optimal cutoffs for continuous variables were determined by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The associations among PPS, local-regional recurrence-free survival (LRRFS), distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS), PFS, and OS were evaluated using Spearman analysis. Key factors influencing PPS were screened using an integrated approach combining LASSO regression, multiple machine learning methods, and Cox regression. A PPS predictive model was subsequently constructed. We employed several machine learning algorithms, including Decision Tree, Generalized Linear Model (GLM), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), and Random Forest. The entire process encompassing model training, hyperparameter tuning (via cross-validation and grid search), and validation was conducted on a pre-split dataset, with 70% allocated for training and 30% for testing. Model performance was evaluated using standard metrics such as the AUC and accuracy. Interpretability of the optimal model was achieved through SHAP analysis. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were employed to explore the association between a risk score and PPS. Finally, Kaplan–Meier analyses were used to compare survival differences for PPS, PFS, and OS, and conditional survival analyses were performed. All tests will be two-sided, with a significance level set at p < 0.05.

Results

Clinical characteristics

A total of 557 patients were stratified by PPS (< 2 years, n = 176; ≥2 years, n = 381), with their baseline characteristics summarized in Table 1. Demographic features, including a male predominance and a majority of stage II and T1-3 disease, were well-balanced between the two groups. Regarding pathological indicators, the PPS < 2 years group exhibited higher proportions of EMVI positivity (30.1% vs. 7.9%), perineural invasion (44.9% vs. 22.6%), and low PNI (62.5% vs. 22.6%). In contrast, the PPS ≥ 2 years group showed a higher prevalence of MRF positivity (79.5% vs. 60.8%) and vascular tumor thrombus (38.6% vs. 29.5%). Additionally, the two groups also showed different distribution characteristics in systemic inflammatory and nutritional indicators (such as SII, SIRI, etc.). The optimal cutoff values for the variables (tumor size, SII, SIRI, LMR, NMLR, NLR, PLR, PNI, PAR, BMI, ALI) are: 6.90 cm, 370.72, 0.77, 5.48, 0.71, 1.87, 137.93, 52.35, 5.13, 20.59, and 230.26. The median follow-up period was 44.53 months, and during the follow-up period, a total of 161 patients experienced progression (28.9%).

Spearman rank correlation analysis

To explore the relationships between OS and other survival-associated indicators, Spearman analyses were conducted to evaluate the associations between OS and LRRFS, DMFS, PFS, and PPS. The results showed significant positive correlations between OS and all four indices, with correlation coefficients: R = 0.728 for LRRFS, R = 0.514 for DMFS, R = 0.572 for PFS, and R = 0.516 for PPS, all reaching statistical significance (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1).

Using LASSO regression, machine learning and Cox regression to screen the key factors affecting PPS

This study comprehensively analyzed prognostic factors affecting PPS from four dimensions-demographic characteristics, pathological factors, inflammatory markers, and nutritional indicators-incorporating a total of 23 variables. The workflow was as follows: first, LASSO regression was used for dimensionality reduction to identify four variables with nonzero coefficients (vascular tumor thrombus, perineural invasion, BMI, and EMVI) (Fig. 2A), with their coefficient trajectories shown in Fig. 2B. Subsequently, all variables were used to train and compare five machine learning models: Decision Tree, XGBoost, Generalized Linear Model (GLM), Random Forest, and Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM). Model performance was evaluated using metrics including AUC, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score to determine the optimal predictive model. The results indicated that the XGBoost model exhibited the best overall phenotype prediction performance (Fig. 2C). SHAP was employed to interpret the model and reveal the importance and directionality of the features. The SHAP feature importance bar plot identified the top five contributing variables as vascular tumor thrombus, LMR, perineural invasion, BMI, and EMVI (Fig. 2D). The SHAP swarm plot showed that vascular endothelial thrombosis, neural invasion, BMI, and EMVI acted as risk factors for PPS, whereas LMR appeared as a protective factor (Fig. 2E). The SHAP dependence plot highlighted the most important variable (positivity for vascular tumor thrombus) exerting a negative effect on PPS (Fig. 2F). The SHAP force plot illustrated the contribution of the top five variables to the prediction for individual samples, with positive SHAP values indicating positive contributions to PPS and negative values indicating negative contributions (Fig. 2G).

To further identify the most prognostically valuable factors, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed. Univariate analysis showed that T stage, tumor size, CEA, MRF, EMVI, vascular tumor thrombus, perineural invasion, tumor deposits, SIRI, LMR, PLR, BMI, and ALI were associated with PPS (p < 0.05). Multivariate analysis identified tumor size [(Hazard Ratio), HR], 1.504; 95% Confidence interval (CI), 1.037–2.183; p = 0.032], EMVI (HR, 2.614; 95%CI, 1.639–4.170; p < 0.001), vascular tumor thrombus (HR, 2.882; 95%CI, 1.922–4.321; p < 0.001), perineural invasion (HR, 1.796; 95%CI, 1.271–2.537; p = 0.001), and BMI (HR, 1.919; 95%CI, 1.219–3.021; p = 0.005) as independent prognostic factors for PPS (Table 2). Taken together, integrating LASSO regression, machine learning, SHAP visualization and cox regression indicates that vascular tumor thrombus, perineural invasion, EMVI, BMI, tumor size and LMR are the most important factors for PPS. The Kaplan–Meier curves illustrate the survival differences of the aforementioned variables on PPS, OS, and PFS (Figure S1).

Build a nomogram model to predict PPS and risk stratification

Based on the six independent factors that most strongly influence PPS, we developed a nomogram model for predicting PPS (Fig. 3A). Calibration curves at 1, 3, and 5 years showed a high degree of agreement between predicted and observed values (Fig. 3B). Decision curve analysis at 1, 3, and 5 years indicated that, compared with single variables, the predictive model yielded a higher net benefit, suggesting greater potential clinical usefulness (Fig. 3C). ROC analyses at 1, 3, and 5 years demonstrated that the model achieved greater areas under the curve than the individual variables, reflecting superior predictive accuracy (Fig. 3D). The C-index of the model is 0.745. Furthermore, we compared the model with tumor staging; although tumor stage retained some predictive value, the model demonstrated superior performance in overall PPS prediction (Fig. 3E).

Based on the contribution of each variable in the nomogram model, we calculated the overall risk score for each study participant. The median total risk score was 156, with a range from 0 to 390. The cohort was subsequently stratified into three risk groups: low risk (0–130), moderate risk (131–260), and high risk (261–390). The survival outcomes were as follows: 1-year PPS was 94.0%, 3-year PPS was 90.5%, and 5-year PPS was 86.3% in the low-risk group; 1-year PPS was 82.3%, 3-year PPS was 68.2%, and 5-year PPS was 61.2% in the moderate-risk group; 1-year PPS was 42.7%, 3-year PPS was 23.7%, and 5-year PPS was 7.9% in the high-risk group. Kaplan–Meier analyses showed that, compared with the low-risk group, the moderate- and high-risk groups had significantly worse survival for PPS, OS, and PFS (Figure S2).

The association between risk scores and prognosis

To analyze the relationship between the risk score and PPS, OS, and PFS, we conducted a prognostic restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis. The results showed that the higher the risk score, the worse the prognosis, and there was a non-linear relationship between them (overall p < 0.05) (Fig. 4). To further validate the association between risk scores and prognosis, we performed multi-model analyses (Table 3). In the simplified model (no adjustment for other variables), the PPS adverse prognosis risk for the moderate-risk group versus the low-risk group was 3.164 (HR = 3.164; 95% CI 2.114–4.734), and for the high-risk group versus the low-risk group was 13.534 (HR = 13.534; 95% CI 8.483–21.591). After adjusting in Model 2 for age, sex, TNM stage, T stage, N stage, CEA, MRF, tumor deposit, and anus preservation, the relative risks increased to 3.035 (HR = 3.035; 95% CI 2.011–4.581) for the moderate-risk group and 12.267 (HR = 12.267; 95% CI 7.181–20.957) for the high-risk group. Model 3 further adjusted for indices such as SII, SIRI, NLR, NMLR, PLR, PNI, PAR, and ALI, after which the PPS adverse prognosis risks rose to 3.127 (HR = 3.127; 95% CI 2.064–4.736) for the moderate-risk group and 10.225 (HR = 10.225; 95% CI 5.923–17.652) for the high-risk group. In multi-model analyses of OS and PFS, the same trends were observed, indicating that irrespective of the adjustment strategy, the PPS adverse prognosis risks for the moderate- and high-risk groups remained significantly higher than those for the low-risk group, thereby further supporting a positive association between risk scores and adverse outcomes.

Development of individualized follow-up strategies

To optimize the allocation of medical resources, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the overall population and different risk groups focusing on conditional PPS and annual risk of subsequent progression or death, and we developed individualized follow-up strategies accordingly. The overall PPS 5-year survival rate was 68.4%, and the conditional PPS 5-year survival rates at 1, 2, and 3 years were 81.8%, 88.2%, and 92.0%, respectively, indicating that survival probability increases with longer PPS (Fig. 5A); the conditional PPS for each risk group showed a similar trend (Figs. 5B-D). Moreover, recurrence risk analysis demonstrated that extending PPS reduces subsequent progression or death risk (Figure S3). Based on these findings, we quantified and compared the annual risk of subsequent progression or death and the number of recurrences across different risk groups (Figs. 5E, F). Specifically, the subsequent progression or death risks by year were: low-risk group—0–1 year: 78.9% (15/19), 1–2 years: 31.5% (6/19), 2–3 years: 5.0% (2/40), 3–4 years: 10.5% (4/38), 4–5 years: 8.8% (3/34), 5–6 years: 2.9% (3/101); moderate-risk group—0–1 year: 88.0% (44/50), 1–2 years: 43.1% (19/44), 2–3 years: 25.0% (11/44), 3–4 years: 15.1% (5/33), 4–5 years: 23.5% (4/17), 5–6 years: 3.2% (2/62); high-risk group—0–1 year: 96.9% (32/33), 1–2 years: 53.8% (7/13), 2–3 years: 25.0% (1/4), 3–4 years: 50.0% (2/4), 4–5 years: 50.0% (1/2). Based on annual risk of subsequent progression or death, we established individualized follow-up frequencies: follow-up every 3 months if the annual risk of subsequent progression or death exceeds 25%; every 6 months if the risk is between 12.5% and 25%; and annually if the risk is below 12.5%. The specific follow-up schedule and content are shown in Fig. 5G. This strategy facilitates precise resource allocation and individualized management across different risk groups.

Discussion

This study is the first to focus on the PPS and its predictive factors in patients with LARC, comprehensively considering multiple dimensions of variables: demographic, pathological features, inflammatory markers, and nutritional indicators. Through a combined analysis of LASSO dimension reduction, machine learning, and Cox regression, six independent predictive factors were identified and a nomogram for PPS prediction was constructed. Based on this nomogram, we proposed individualized follow-up strategies and resource allocation plans. The research results showed that vascular tumor thrombus, perineural invasion, EMVI, BMI, tumor size, and LMR all had significant and independent predictive value for PPS. The nomogram built in this study outperformed single variables and TNM staging in predicting PPS at 1, 3, and 5 years. The RCS curves and multi-model analysis both indicated that the higher the risk score, the shorter the survival time of PPS. Finally, the follow-up strategy based on risk stratification can achieve precise allocation of resources and individualized management. These findings have potential clinical significance for evidence-based treatment decisions and follow-up management, especially in clinical practices where resources are limited and the intensity of follow-up needs to be optimized.

As an independent outcome variable, PPS not only compensates for the limitations of OS and PFS, but also more comprehensively reflects the survival benefit and treatment effect of patients [21, 22]. Previous studies have mainly focused on the following aspects of PPS. First, exploring the correlation and robustness between PPS and OS, comparing the predictive strength of PPS and PFS for OS, and revealing the independent contribution of PPS to survival after disease progression [17]. For example, Imai et al. found that PPS was more significantly positively correlated with OS than PFS [14]. Interestingly, the spearman analysis results of this study indicated that both PPS and PFS were positively correlated with OS. Second, analyzing the prognostic factors affecting PPS, including baseline characteristics (such as age, gender, etc.), disease characteristics (pathological type, recurrence site, progression pattern, etc.), and treatment factors (first-line treatment, post-progression treatment strategy, etc.). Studies have pointed out that the Glasgow prognostic score at recurrence and liver metastasis status are significantly associated with PPS, suggesting that these factors can be used as potential indicators for predicting PPS [23]. Different progression patterns (classified by recurrence time, the relationship between new lesions and primary lesions, and imaging assessment, etc.) are also closely related to PPS [24–26]. In a study of advanced non-small cell lung cancer with high PD-L1 expression, where first-line Pembrolizumab monotherapy was the baseline, post-progression combined local treatment prolonged PPS compared to monotherapy, indicating that post-progression treatment strategies have a significant impact on PPS [27]. Third, exploring the application of PPS in clinical trials. By stratifying PPS, patients who may benefit from specific treatments after progression can be identified, thereby optimizing the second-line and subsequent treatment pathways [28]. Therefore, as an independent and comprehensive prognostic endpoint for end-stage survival, PPS not only helps improve the assessment of survival benefit after disease progression but also provides theoretical and clinical basis for individualized treatment decisions and optimization of second-line and subsequent treatment pathways.

Different from previous studies, this study is the first to focus on PPS in the LARC population, comprehensively considering multiple dimensions of variables to establish a predictive model and form an operational clinical tool (nomogram), achieving an integrated application from “prediction-risk stratification-follow-up strategy”. Through SHAP interpretation and multi-model consistency analysis, the relative importance and directionality of the six core variables were clarified, enhancing the clinical interpretability of the model. The presence of tumor thrombus in blood vessels may indicate that tumor cells have invaded the blood vessels, increasing the risk of distant metastasis. In a retrospective study, the 12-month mortality rate of patients with tumor thrombus was 52.5%, and there was no significant difference in mortality between those who received anticoagulant therapy and those who did not, suggesting that the tumor thrombus itself has a significant impact on prognosis [29]. The occurrence of perineural invasion is the result of the interaction between tumor cells and the neural microenvironment. Tumor cells attract nerve fibers by secreting neurotrophic factors (such as NGF), while the signal molecules released by nerve fibers can also promote the proliferation and invasion of tumor cells, leading to a poor prognosis [30]. EMVI positivity indicates that tumor cells have broken through the muscularis propria of the intestinal wall and invaded blood vessels, increasing the risk of local recurrence and distant metastasis. For example, the local recurrence rate of EMVI-positive patients is significantly higher than that of EMVI-negative patient, and is often accompanied by adverse events such as peritoneal metastasis [31, 32]. In addition, EMVI-positive patients are more likely to have lymph node metastasis and distant metastasis, and are mutually influenced by indicators such as tumor invasion depth and differentiation degree, further worsening the prognosis [33]. Larger tumors are more likely to have lymph node metastasis or distant metastasis, thereby increasing the difficulty of treatment and the risk of recurrence. Studies have shown that compared with patients with a tumor diameter of less than 2 cm, patients with a tumor diameter of 2 cm or more have a higher risk of recurrence and a shorter survival period [34]. LMR can reflect the balance between the body’s immune status and tumor burden. A low ratio often indicates a poor prognosis for the patient. Studies have shown that patients with a lower LMR have a relatively poor prognosis, while those with a higher LMR have a better prognosis [35]. Overweight and obesity are associated with overall mortality and cancer mortality. The most common obesity-related malignancies are breast cancer and colorectal cancer [36]. A higher average BMI (an increase of 1 kg/m2) is associated with a 6% increase in the risk of death in colorectal cancer patients [37]. In conclusion, this study reveals the importance and interrelationships of factors such as vascular tumor thrombus, perineural invasion, EMVI, BMI, tumor size, and LMR in the prognosis of LARC, laying a solid foundation for the construction of a predictive PPS model.

At present, there are several limitations in the follow-up strategies for RC after treatment. Firstly, CEA is a commonly used follow-up indicator, but in some patients (especially those with normal baseline CEA), CEA may remain normal at the time of recurrence, leading to insufficient monitoring sensitivity. This means that relying solely on CEA may miss some early recurrence cases, especially for patients with normal baseline CEA [38]. Secondly, the existing follow-up plans involve a trade-off between frequency and cost [39]. It is usually recommended to have a follow-up every three months within two years after surgery, every six months between two and five years, and once a year after five years [40, 41]. For high-risk patients (such as those with lymph node metastasis or poorly differentiated tumors), more frequent monitoring is required, which increases the economic burden and time cost for patients and may affect their compliance. More importantly, the follow-up strategies are relatively uniform and do not fully consider individual differences. For example, elderly or patients with poor physical condition may not tolerate frequent examinations, while low-risk patients do not need such intensive follow-up. In addition, the impact of molecular subtype differences on recurrence risk and patterns has not been targeted and optimized in the existing follow-up plans. Moreover, the current strategies lack precise intervention nodes driven by molecular markers, resulting in approximately 44% of patients with recurrence and metastasis missing the best treatment window [42].

To address these limitations, the present study proposes a data-driven, risk-adapted surveillance strategy. The thresholds of 25% and 12.5% were established based on the observed annual recurrence risk distribution in our cohort (Fig. 5E), where the low-risk group fell below 12.5% after two years while the high-risk group remained above 25% at most time points. These thresholds were further selected for clinical plausibility: 25% aligns with the maximum follow-up frequency (every 3 months) recommended by NCCN, ESMO, and CSCO guidelines, while 12.5% provides a pragmatic boundary between every-6-month and annual surveillance. Consequently, this strategy introduces a quantitative, individualized layer within the fixed-interval framework of current guidelines (every 3–6 months for the first 2 years, every 6 months for years 2–5, and annually thereafter). For example, low-risk patients in our cohort could safely transition to annual follow-up after two years (risk 5%–10%), whereas high-risk patients required continued trimonthly surveillance in years 3–4 (risk > 25%). This approach reduces unnecessary follow-up for low-risk patients while intensifying monitoring for high-risk patients, optimizing resource allocation without compromising early detection.

To address these issues, we developed a risk stratification based on the PPS prediction model and formulated individualized follow-up strategies, thereby filling the gap in the PPS dimension. The research results show that for the high-risk group with an annual risk of subsequent progression or death > 25%, a follow-up every three months is recommended; for the moderate-risk group with a subsequent progression or death risk > 25% in the 0–3 and 4–5 years, a follow-up every three months is also recommended; for the 3–4 years with a recurrence risk between 12.5% and 25%, a follow-up every six months is recommended; for the 5–6 years with a subsequent progression or death risk < 12.5%, a follow-up once a year is recommended; for the low-risk group with a subsequent progression or death risk > 25% in the 0–2 years, a follow-up every three months is recommended, and then a follow-up once a year when the subsequent progression or death risk is < 12.5%. This stratification strategy is based on the distribution of annual risk of subsequent progression or death in long-term follow-up data and can achieve precise allocation of resources and individualized management in different risk groups. Through a PPS-driven stratified follow-up framework, intensive follow-up can be implemented in high-risk patients and relatively simplified monitoring in low-risk patients, thereby improving the quality of follow-up and the efficiency of resource utilization. Future work will further verify the operability of this follow-up plan in real clinical settings, its impact on resource utilization, and the actual improvement in long-term outcomes, and optimize the intensity of follow-up based on the dynamic changes of biomarkers and imaging.

Although this study fills the research gap of individualized follow-up strategies centered on PPS, there are still several limitations. Firstly, the samples come from a single center, which inevitably leads to selection bias. External validation in multi-center cohorts covering different populations is needed to evaluate the generalization ability of the established nomogram. Secondly, although 23 variables were included, potential key factors (such as microsatellite instability, recurrence patterns, post-progression treatment strategies, etc.) may still be missed. Future studies should include these variables to build a more comprehensive prediction model. Thirdly, the current model is based on baseline characteristics. Future research can explore dynamic update prediction frameworks (such as time-dependent predictions in the follow-up stage) to further improve the accuracy and operability of individualized follow-up.

Conclusion

This study presents a reliable predictive model that not only enables accurate prediction of PPS in LARC but also translates risk stratification into personalized surveillance strategies, thereby addressing a significant void in the management of progressive disease.
