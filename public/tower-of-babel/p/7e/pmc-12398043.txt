Factors associated with female genital mutilation/cutting in Tanzania: insights from Tanzania demographic and health survey 2022
Authors: Tumaini Nyamhanga, Oliva Kapinga, Brian A Muro, Pankras Luoga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobally, female genital mutilation (FGM) remains a significant public health concern. The practice is disproportionately high in African countries. In Tanzania, FGM poses serious health risks to both women, girls and children. However, there is limited empirical literature on the factors associated with FGM in Tanzania. This study intended to fill the gap.MethodsIn this study, we analysed secondary data from a cross-sectional survey, involving a weighted sample of 7,678 women aged 15–49 from the 2022 Tanzania Demographic and Health Survey (TDHS). In this study, the binary dependent variable indicates whether the respondent is mutilated or not mutilated while the independent variables include various demographic characteristics of women, such as age, education level, socioeconomic status, and region of residence. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted. A threshold of p-value < 0.05 at 95% Confidence Interval (CI) was used to determine a statistically significant association.ResultsThe prevalence of FGM in Tanzania is 8.2% and types I and II (a cut with or without removal of flesh) were the dominant types of FGM practice by 89.2%. After controlling for other variables, higher odds of being mutilated was reported in; older ages 45–49 years (adjusted Odds Ratio(aOR));3.09, 95%CI: 1.72, 5.54), in rural areas (aOR;2.30, 95%CI:1.4,3.6), in women in unions (aOR;1.60, 95%CI:1.20,2.10), in Northern zone (aOR;9.10, 95%CI: 4.60, 17.80), those who ever heard about FGM had 2.27 times (aOR; 2.27, 95%CI: 0.82, 6.29), those who said FGM required by religion had 8.3 times (aOR; 8.30, 95%CI: 4.30,16.03), those who supported FGM had 5.29 times (aOR; 5.29, 95%CI: 2.69, 10.40) higher odds of reporting having undergone FGM compared to those who said the practice should be stopped. Conversely, lower odds of experiencing FGM was reported in; women with at least secondary education (aOR;0.40, 95%CI:0.20,0.60), those from richest households (aOR;0.40, 95%CI: 0.20, 0.60) and those who said distance to a health facility was not a big problem (aOR;0.70, 95%CI: 0.50, 0.90).ConclusionOur study found that prevalence of FGM in Tanzania is 8.2%. The factors associated with experiencing FGM included woman’s socio-demographic factors like older age, rural residency, lower or no education, poorest wealth quintile, supporting FGM to continue and being in unions. This calls for collaborative efforts between the government and other stakeholders to design targeted interventions as ending FGM require a multisectoral approach addressing aforementioned determinants across multiple levels including education and wealth creation programs particularly to uneducated and poorest women from rural areas.Keywords: Female genital mutilation, Women, DHS, Tanzania

Background

The World Health Organization defines female genital mutilation (FGM) as “all procedures that involve partial or total removal of the external female genitalia, or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons” [1, 2]. FGM takes place in over 40 countries worldwide, and has affected more than 230 million girls and women [3]. Furthermore, an estimated 3 million girls are at danger of female genital mutilation annually; the majority of these girls have their genitalia cut before the age of 15. About 28 African nations practice female genital mutilation (FGM), the majority of which are located in the sub-Saharan Africa [4–6].

The WHO and UNICEF recognize 4 main types of FGM [7, 8]. Type 1 involves clitoridectomy which is total or partial removal of the clitoral glans or the prepuce/clitoral hood. Type 2 is shortly known as excision which involves total or partial removal of the clitoral glans and the labia minora, with or without removal of the labia majora. Type 3 is infibulation, which involves narrowing of the vaginal entrance by creating a covering seal. The seal is created by cutting and realigning the inner or outer labia through stitching, with or without removal of clitoris. Type 4 comprises all other dangerous procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical reasons. Such harmful procedures include: pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterizing the vaginal area [7]. Across Africa, types I and II are the most common, as most women reported having experienced “a cut with or without removal of flesh (flesh removed)”; but there is variation among countries. Type III (Infibulation) is mostly performed in North Eastern Africa, particularly Djibouti, Eritrea, Somalia and Sudan [4].

FGM in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is driven by strong socio-cultural factors [4, 9]. The practice is primarily influenced by two key drivers. Firstly, FGM is frequently seen as a vital component of a girl’s upbringing and a means of preparing her for maturity and marriage, with the motive of restricting her sexuality. Secondly, FGM is considered to be a social convention or standard. That is, the practice is strongly encouraged by social pressure to follow the example set by others, the need to fit in, and the fear of rejection from the community [10–14]. For instance, in some societies, unmutilated women cannot talk sense in front of mutilated women and less likely to be married by men from their communities [10, 14].

Additionally, in the communities that practice FGM in Tanzania, the practice is closely tied to the perception of a girl’s readiness for marriage [15]. Moreover, the practice is sometimes seen as a way to suppress female sexuality, reduce perceived promiscuity, and ensure women’s sexual “purity” for marriage. The practice is rooted in the belief that it maintains the control of women’s bodies and sexuality within male-dominated structures (16,17).

The consequences of FGM in Tanzania, like in other countries, include immediate negative health effects and long term health complications [7, 16]. The immediate health effects include: severe pain, excessive bleeding (haemorrhage), genital tissue swelling, fever, infections, injury to surrounding genital tissue, shock, and death. Moreover, the long term health complications include: urinary problems (painful urination, urinary tract infections); vaginal problems (discharge, itching, bacterial vaginosis and other infections); menstrual problems (painful menstruations, difficulty in passing menstrual blood, etc.); scar tissue and keloid; sexual problems (pain during intercourse, decreased satisfaction, etc.); increased risk of childbirth complications (difficult delivery, excessive bleeding, caesarean section, need to resuscitate the baby, etc.) and newborn deaths; need for later surgeries. For example, the sealing or narrowing of the vaginal opening (type 3) may lead to the practice of cutting open the sealed vagina later to allow for sexual intercourse and childbirth (deinfibulation); psychological problems (depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, low self-esteem, etc.); reduced sexual desire and enjoyment [16–18].

Currently, in Tanzania, the prevalence of FGM is inconsistently reported. For instance, UNFPA estimates that 1 in every 10 women, has experienced some forms of FGM [18–20]; another study report that 3 in every 20 women has undergone some forms of FGM in Singida region, Tanzania [16]. A report by UNFPA indicates that 1 in every 10 women aged 15–49 has undergone FGM in Tanzania [18]. It is worth noting that, these previous estimates of the magnitude of FGM in Tanzania are based on studies that covered small area and limited sample; and they consequently lacked national representation [10, 19, 21]. The key findings from the 2022 TDHS revealed an overall prevalence of 8.2% and that the leading regions for FGM are Arusha (43%) and Manyara (43%), followed by the Mara region (28%) [10, 15, 20]. The most common type of FGM practices are types 1 and 2, as in 2022 TDHS most of the victims (89%) reported to have experienced a cut with or without removal of flesh [22]. Nevertheless, research evidence about factors associated with FGM is limited. Previous quantitative studies [11, 20, 21] on FGM in Tanzania were limited to small sample, subpopulation and conducted at sub-national level. To the best of our knowledge, only one study has reported factors associated with FGM among women aged 15–49 years in Tanzania using DHS data [23]. However, this study [23] was limited both in terms of time and scope. That is, it based on the 2015/16 TDHS data, analyzed data from a sub-group of women who participated in the survey– only those who had given birth five years before the survey. Moreover, that study [23] tested a limited number of independent variables including marital status, wealth status, zonal location of place of residence, place of childbirth, parity, and opinion on whether FGM should continue or not. It is worth noting that FGM is shaped by a wide range of cultural, socio-economic, and demographic factors. By testing fewer variables, the study [23] missed out critical factors might influence the practice of FGM. For example, factors like education and media exposure (listening to radio, watching television) which may influence whether or not the individual makes a decision to undergo FGM. Therefore, this study intended to assess the association between a wide range of independent variables (as seen in the conceptual framework) and FGM among women in Tanzania using national representative data of TDHS 2022 [19]. Testing a larger set of independent variables has resulted into a more comprehensive and accurate understanding of the factors influencing FGM. While the term “female genital mutilation” is widely accepted by international human rights organizations to emphasize the harm caused by the practice, DHS uses “female genital circumcision” in its questionnaires probably in an attempt to be culturally sensitive (neutral) to facilitate more non-confrontational interviews in communities where the practice is common. However, in the context of this article, apart from usage of the terms “circumcised” or “not circumcised” in description of the dependent variable, the term “female genital mutilation” is used in the rest of the sections to call attention to the severe and harmful consequences of the practice.

Theoretical framework for understanding the factors associated with FGM in Tanzania

The study was guided by a socioecological model (SEM) in conceptualization, selection of the variables, analysis and interpretation of the findings. The socioecological model (SEM) provides a comprehensive analytical framework for examining the multifaceted and interrelated factors from the individual level to wider societal level and how they may influence healthy or unhealthy practice of an individual [24]. Individual’s decision to practice or not to practice FGM is influenced by a number of factors falling at different levels; at the individual level including age, education, religion, marital status and beliefs [25, 26], at interpersonal level like economic status and decision-making power for women, at community and societal levels; things like norms, ethnic group and area of residence [27, 28]. The aforementioned factors are linked to the practice of FGM among Tanzanian women. Examining factors at the individual, interpersonal, community, and societal levels provides a holistic perspective and a nuanced understanding of the drivers of FGM [29]. As summarised in Table 1.

Methodology

Study design

In this study, we analysed secondary data of a weighted sample of 7,678 women aged 15–49 collected using a cross-sectional design during the 2022 TDHS. The survey was conducted by the National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) and the Office of the Chief Government Statistician (OCGS) in Zanzibar. It was funded by United States Agency of International Development (USAID), with technical support from Inner City Fund (ICF), and carried out in collaboration with various national and international partners. The sample selection for the 2022 TDHS took place in two stages. First, 629 clusters (enumeration areas or EAs) were chosen using probability proportion to size sampling, with 211 located in urban areas and 418 in rural areas based on geographical proximity and rural/urban diversity. In the second stage, 26 households were systematically selected from each cluster, resulting in a total target sample size of 16,354 households. A detailed explanation of the survey methodology, data collection process, and questionnaires can be found in the final report of the 2022 TDHS [19]. The survey included 15,699 eligible women aged 15–49, with 15,254 completing the interview. For our analysis, we focused on women aged 15–49, who responded to the question: “Have you yourself ever been mutilated?”

Study sample

The study involved a weighted sample of 7,678 women. Since the population of Tanzania is not evenly distributed across its region; some regions, like Dar es Salaam, have a high population, while others, such as Lindi, have fewer people. As a result, regions with larger populations were underrepresented in the actual sample that participated in the survey, while those with smaller populations were overrepresented. This imbalance in the sample does not reflect the actual population distribution. To obtain accurate statistics that are representative of the entire country, the sample distribution of women was adjusted (or weighted) to match the true population distribution in Tanzania. This adjustment ensured that women from smaller regions, like Lindi, contribute proportionally less to the national total, while women from larger regions, like Dar es Salaam, have a larger representation. Consequently, DHS statisticians calculate a “weight” to adjust the number of women from each region, ensuring their contribution aligns with the actual population size of each region [19].

Specifically, for this data involving women 15–49, all data analysis applied individual weight for women(v005/1000000) to account for complex sampling design and non-response rate. Another reason why weighting was important was a need to adjust for non-response rate. In DHS, some individuals may not respond, either due to refusal or inability to participate, leading to missing values. Weighting helped to correct for this nonresponse bias by giving more importance to data from respondents who are similar to those who did not participate, ensuring the final results are representative.

Finally, the study sample comprised 7,678 weighted drawn from the total sample of 15,254 women of reproductive age (15–49 years). We remained with 7,678 weighted sample after excluding from the analysis all cases with missing in the outcome variable. The excluded cases due to missing values that accounted for 49.67% of the original sample size.

Dependent and independent variables

Dependent variable

Female genital mutilation status among women in Tanzania. The variable originated from the question asked to a woman “Have you yourself ever been mutilated?” and the answer was “Yes” or “No”. These responses were coded as follows: no = 0, yes = 1. In addition, we further described the types of FGM practice among those who underwent mutilation, as per the categories in the DHS questionnaire (0 = No flesh removed, 1 = Flesh removed, 2=. Genital areas narrowed, 3 = Don’t know/missing).

Independent variables

The independent variables in this analysis included: age, education level, marital status, type of residence, geographical zones, wealth quintile, current working status, distance to the health facility, getting permission to go to access healthcare, frequency of watching/listening the mass media, ever heard about FGM, FGM required by religion and FGM should continue or be stopped. These variables have been used in other similar studies on the topics related to FGM [11, 25–28, 30].

The influence of these variables on FGM/C is depicted by socioecological model as occurring at multiple levels, namely: individual, interpersonal, community, and societal. Individual Level include: age, level of education, working status, marital status, attitude, religious beliefs, working status. Age of the woman undergoing FGM matters. The younger the age, the higher the likelihood of being persuaded/forced by parents/guardians to have the FGM done. Lack of/low level of education translates into a woman not knowing the harmful effects of FGM and limited understanding of the fact that FGM is a violation human [women’s] rights [31]. Moreover, illiterate or semi-literate woman may not be aware of sources of support to avoid FGM [6]. Working status is a determinant of FGM as it determines socioeconomic position which may have a bearing on literacy about negative effects of FGM. Marriage limits the woman’s freedom of determining her own sexual and reproductive health destiny, since the practice is a cultural phenomenon, the type of residence rural/urban may have an influence whether one undergoes FGM or not [15]. Attitude toward continuation or stopping FGM is key to its elimination [31]. If people perceive FGM as harmful practice and that the negative effects outweigh cultural benefits, if any, then there is high likelihood of eliminating the practice [2, 6, 13]. Additionally, in some communities, FGM is seen as a religious requirement or a practice that aligns with religious teachings [24]. When individuals believe that FGM is mandated by their faith, it gains a level of social legitimacy. The practice becomes not only a cultural tradition but also a perceived religious obligation. This belief strengthens the adherence to FGM, even in the face of evidence regarding its harmful consequences [2].

Moreover, interpersonal factors included: household wealth index, household decision making power. Wealth quintile is a determinant of FGM because the poorer the person the lower the literacy about FGM and higher the likelihood of her clinging to cultural practices including the harmful ones such as FGM [10]. However, rich households also may engage in medicalization of FGM practices [32]. Furthermore, in societies where women and girls face restrictions on their autonomy and decision-making, they need permission to access healthcare. Consequently, they may be less likely to seek out information about the harmful effects of FGM. Restricted access to healthcare means they might not receive education on the dangers of FGM, including its physical and psychological consequences. This lack of information can perpetuate the practice, as individuals may not understand its harmful effects.

At community level, the factors include distance to health facilities and media exposure. Health facilities are sources of health education. The further one resides from the health facility, the more difficult it becomes for her to access reliable health education about FGM. Moreover, frequent media exposure can lead to increased knowledge about the health, legal, and human rights aspects of FGM, thereby encouraging individuals to reject the practice. However, the influence of media can be complex, as it can both challenge harmful practices or, in some cases, perpetuate them, depending on the contents and messages being disseminated. Hearing about FGM raises awareness about its harmful effects, including potential complications such as infections, infertility, and psychological trauma. This increased knowledge can discourage individuals from practicing or supporting FGM, particularly when they are aware of its medical risks and legal consequences.

Societal-level factors include place of residence (rural versus urban) and geographic zones. It is well known that cultural homogeneity in the rural settings provides a fertile ground for persistence of FGM practices, compared to more heterogeneous urban settings [33]. The geographical zones comprise regions where communities or tribes which practice FGM are located [15]. It is known that certain tribes/ethinic groups like Kurya in the Lake zone and Maasai in the Northern zone do embrace the culture of FGM [15].

Data analysis

Descriptive analysis using descriptive analysis to show the distribution of the respondents in terms of frequencies and percentages, inferential analysis using Chi-square was conducted to determine the association between independent and dependent variables. Before entering the variables from Chi-square in the univariate and multivariable models, we tested for multicollinearity between independent variables. Worth noting that there were no variables which showed to have multicollinearity. In addition, univariate and multivariable binary logistic regression models were conducted to determine the magnitude of association between categories of independent and dependent variables. The magnitude of association was determined using crude and adjusted odds ratios along with their respective 95% Confidence Intervals (CI). Statistical significance was determined at a threshold of p-value < 0.05. All analyses were conducted with applications of women’s individual sample weight to account for complex survey design and non-response rate.

Results

Socio demographic characteristics of the study respondents

The analysis involved a total weighted sample of 7,678 women aged 15–49 years. Of these, more than a quarter (20.70%) were in the 15–19 age group, with mean age 29 years (SD = 10). The majority (53.65%) had primary education, and only 35% were from urban areas. A significant proportion (37.90%) resided in the Lake Zone, 60.10% were married or in a union, 42.00% reported being unemployed, and only 22.70% came from the richest households, Of the respondents, 8.20% reported having experienced FGM. Among those who experienced FGM, 89% had type I or type II FGM (had a cut with or without removal of flesh) as described in Table 2.

Factors associated with FGM in Tanzania

Individual level factors: age, level of education, working status, marital status, attitude, religious beliefs, working status, ever heard of FGM were significantly associated with FGM at p-value < 0.001. Moreover, the respondent’s current working status was significantly associated with FGM at p-value = 0.003. Moreover, while one interpersonal level factor, namely: household wealth index, was significantly associated with undergoing FGM at p value < 0.001; another - household decision making power (needing permission to access health care)-getting permission to access health care had no association with FGM, with p value < 0.766.

Regarding community level factors, distance to the health facility and media exposure had significant relationship with undergoing FGM, with p values of 0.001 and < 0.001 respectively.

Additionally, societal level factors including rural versus urban residence and geographical zones were significantly associated with undergoing FGM (p = 0.001) as indicated in Table 3.

On interpersonal factors, women who said getting permission to access healthcare was not a big problem had 1.2 times (aOR;1.20, 95% CI: 0.70, 2.00) higher odds of experiencing FGM compared to those who said permission to access healthcare was a big problem. Those from richest households had 0.4 times (aOR;0.40, 95%CI: 0.20, 0.60) lower odds of experiencing FGM compared to those women from poorest households.

On the community level factors; women who said distance to the health facility was not a big problem had 0.7 times (aOR;0.70, 95% CI: 0.50, 0.90) lower odds of experiencing FGM compared to those who said distance to health facility was a big problem. Discussion

The aim of this study was to determine the factors associated with FGM among women of reproductive age (15–49 years) in Tanzania. Our analysis showed that the current national prevalence of FGM is 8.2%. This prevalence is slightly lower than the previous prevalence reported in the 2015/16 TDHS (10%). This downward trend is a reflection that Tanzania is making progress toward ending FGM, albeit slowly, as recorded in the previous DHS survey reports from 1996 to 2022 [15, 22, 29]. The current prevalence of 8.2% is about 50% decline from 17.9% in 1996. This achievement is attributed to FGM elimination efforts by the Government and non-Governmental actors. The UNFPA documents that as a result of the FGM elimination efforts: more girls are participating in alternative rites of passage, and more cases are reported and successfully handled by the police as a result of systematic training on gender-based violence and FGM response by law enforcement officers [20].

Our analysis revealed that among the mutilated women; the three commonest types of FGM include: types I and II (a cut with or without removal of flesh, 89.2%) followed by genital areas narrowed (5.9%) and no flesh removed (2.3%). However, (2.6%) said that did not know the kind of FGM performed to them. The findings are in line to findings reported in other studies [13, 23]. The studies report that majority of mutilated women underwent flesh removed than the remaining types of FGM.

On the individual level factors, this study found that older women aged 45–49 years had higher odds of experiencing FGM compared to women in the younger age bracket of 15–19 years. This finding aligns with the fact that older women (aged 45–49) likely underwent FGM during their youth, in the years shortly after the practice was criminalized in 1998 [18, 20]. The positive impact of criminalization of FGM and related elimination efforts is being realized gradually as years go by thereby benefitting the younger generation [20]. Similar findings are reported elsewhere in SSA whereby women aged 45–49 years had higher likelihood to have undergone FGM [10].

The study revealed that women with at least a secondary education had lower percent of experiencing FGM compared to those with no formal education. Therefore, attaining secondary or higher education serves as a protective factor against FGM. This finding suggests that providing women with more educational opportunities could fasten ending FGM in the society as they get enlightened on negative effects of the practice [6]. Since the current DHS shows that about two thirds of women underwent FGM before they were 15 years old, i.e. before completing secondary education; the reduced likelihood of FGM among women with higher educational achievement has a reflection on the effects of education on FGM. Such strategies of increasing education opportunities to women brings about the necessary health literacy that empowers them to overcome pro-FGM sociocultural pressures. This observation is in agreement with the findings reported in Tanzania and elsewhere whereby lack of formal education or lower education level among women was linked supporting and undergoing FGM, as such women have lower knowledge of the FGM and its negative health complications [6, 34].

Moreover, women who are in union showed to have higher odds of experiencing FGM compared to those who are not in union. This finding adds valuable insights into how union status might intersect with the practice of FGM. In many communities where FGM is practiced, it is often considered a traditional or cultural requirement for women, and it may be seen as a rite of passage into womanhood [10]. In such contexts, marriage or being in a union may increase the pressure to undergo FGM, particularly if it’s believed to be a prerequisite for social acceptance or marriageability [16]. Similar findings are reported in Ghana whereby women who were married reported higher prevalence of FGM than unmarried women [35].

Furthermore, our analysis indicates that women who supported continuation of FGM had higher odds of experiencing FGM compared to those who opposed the practice. Similarly, those who were uncertain about whether FGM should continue or be stopped had higher odds of reporting that they had undergone FGM, compared to those who advocated for stopping the practice. These findings suggest that women who support the continuation of FGM may come from communities where the practice is deeply ingrained in cultural or social norms [13, 16, 36]. In such environments, FGM is often seen as a necessary rite of passage, an important part of tradition, or a key to social acceptance [6, 14]. For these women, supporting FGM might reflect an internalization of these norms, making them more likely to undergo FGM as part of their lived experience [14, 16, 21, 36]. Moreover, these findings have significant implications for intervention and policy-making. It indicates that simply addressing the physical harms of FGM may not be sufficient; efforts to eliminate FGM also need to challenge the cultural and societal support for the practice [14]. Interventions could focus on education and community dialogue to alter attitudes toward FGM and to empower women to question and resist the practice [6].

Regarding interpersonal factors, richest wealth quintile was protective against FGM as women who were from richest and richer households had lower risk of experiencing FGM compared to those who were from poorest households. These results may be suggesting that poor economic status of the woman and that of her parents could limit their accessibility to education, thereby impairing their literacy on the effects of FGM and consequently putting the woman at higher risk of undergoing the practice [30]. Similar findings are reported in a study done in SSA [10]. However, contradictory findings are reported in a study in SSA, whereby being in a rich wealth quantile was linked to medicalization of FGM [32].

In addition, at the community level, our analysis indicates that respondents who did not perceive distance to a health facility as a major problem had lower odds of experiencing FGM. This could be due to the fact that women living near or at a place not very far from the health facility have higher likelihood of visiting the health facility and get educated about negative effects of FGM, thereby having higher chances of refraining from the practice [34].

Regarding societal factors, women residing in rural areas had twice the odds of experiencing FGM compared their counterparts living in urban areas. Pro-FGM socio-cultural norms are known to be more entrenched in the rural settings [16] partly because of dominance of one ethnic group in particular localities with limited educational opportunities. A study conducted in a rural district of Itigi (Singida Region), Tanzania, where the dominant tribe is Nyaturu, reported that 51% of women had undergone FGM [16]. Similar findings are reported by other studies done in Tanzania and other parts of SSA [15, 21, 33]. Inconsistent findings are reported in some parts of SSA whereby women from rural settings had less likelihood of experiencing FGM [10]. Moreover, women from Northern and Central zones had higher likelihood of experiencing FGM respectively, compared to those from Lake zone. These findings reflect regional/zonal variation of the magnitude of FGM across the country, as reported in the 2022 TDHS final report [19] that the leading regions in FGM are Arusha (43%), Manyara (43%) located in the Northern zone; followed by Mara region (28%) located in the Lake zone. The observed differences may be attributed to cultural variations among the local communities residing in the different zones. Therefore, designing tailored interventions specific to each geographical zone is essential for effectively eliminating FGM.

Moreover, women who at least once a week watched/listened to Mass media had no significant association with undergoing FGM. This finding could potential be explained by the reason that only 45.70% of the study respondents had at least once a week watched or listened to mass media. This was unexpected findings due to the fact that women’s exposure to mass media is linked to lower possibility of undergoing FGM as reported in the literature [21, 37]. In summary, this study has highlighted important variables associated with experiencing FGM including women supporting continuation of FGM in Tanzania and residing in Northern and Central zones. On the other side, women who did not see the distance to a health facility as a big problem were less likely to have undergone FGM. These variables link to the socioecological model on the individual, interpersonal and wider-social factors. To best of our knowledge, no previous study in Tanzania indicated the association of these variables in relation to FGM.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The strength of the study lies in its use of a large DHS sample, which yields findings that are both generalizable and nationally representative. However, it was difficult to demonstrate causal-effect linkages between the independent and dependent variables due to the cross-sectional nature of the study. Some variables such as religion and ethnicity, could not be obtained in Tanzania’s DHS data set, even though other studies have reported them as being linked to the occurrence of FGM both in Tanzania and elsewhere. In addition, the fourth type of FGM, which includes all other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical reasons as categorized by WHO, was not available in the DHS dataset, and therefore, our study was unable to report on it. The study may have inherited errors that occurred during the collection or recording of the primary data. For instance, self-reported data leading to underreporting of the practice among women during primary data collection was expected due to FGM being illegal since 1998 in the country as well as a sensitive topic in the society. Exclusion of 49.67% cases with missing data in the outcome variable in the analysis may have impacted our overall results especially if the cases with missing comprised a significant number of women who underwent FGM. However, preliminary analysis indicated that the cases with missing values were at random hence they had no any observed patterns. Therefore, their exclusion had no influence on the findings. Furthermore, a limitation of difference in the timing of the dependent variable and the independent variables is acknowledged. The concern is related to temporal misalignment between when FGM occurred versus when covariates like education and wealth were obtained. Additionally, DHS being purely quantitative, reasons why FGM practice remains in Tanzania were not explored. Likewise, although the 2022 DHS had recorded the FGM prevalence of 8.2%, a decline of 50% from 17.9% of 1996, the survey did not involve probes on how and why this decline was achieved. However, the study draws its strengths from using data collected through a standardized questionnaire used in more than 90 countries worldwide. Also, the use of most recent nationally representative data and to be able to indicate the common types of FGM practices in Tanzania. This had not been well documented prior to the current study.

Conclusion

The study found that the overall prevalence of FGM among Tanzanian women is 8.2% and among those mutilated women, Types I and II (a cut with or without removal of flesh) were the dominant form of FGM practice by 89.2%. The study has revealed multiple women’s socio-demographic factors associated with FGM. The individual level factors that increased likelihood of undergoing FGM included older age, being married, lower or no education, ever heard of FGM, a belief that FGM is required by religion, and the perception that FGM should continue. Moreover, the interpersonal level factor of belonging to the poorest wealth quintile and the societal factor of rural residency were associated with experiencing FGM. Nevertheless, the community level factor of living near or at a place not very far from the health facility was protective against FGM. Therefore, the government, in collaboration with other stakeholders, should develop a multilevel strategy to combat FGM. That is, the strategy should have targeted interventions as follows: Firstly, individual level factors need to be addressed through increasing educational opportunities for women so most of them may have secondary level and beyond. Since a large number of married women who support continuation of FGM - based on religious beliefs - are out of school, community-based educational programmes highlighting negative effects of FGM may help to changing their attitudes and those of their spouses toward eliminating the practice. Likewise, interpersonal and societal factors need to be addressed by improving economic status of women in the rural settings, particularly in the zones with high FGM prevalence, enabling them to effectively combat FGM. There is a need for future researchers to conduct further research on FGM using advanced statistical analyses like multilevel or interaction to uncover the influences of wider contextual factors on the practice. In addition, a qualitative study is recommended to explore reasons why FGM persists in Tanzania and explain how and why the decline of 50% - from the prevalence 17.9% in 1996 to that of 8.2% in 2022 - was achieved.
