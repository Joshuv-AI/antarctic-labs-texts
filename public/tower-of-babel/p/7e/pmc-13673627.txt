Contact with older adults as a factor shaping the attitudes of students of healthcare professions
Authors: Izabela Sowińska, Sylwia Kocur, Klaudia Dubis, Daria Buźniak, Gabriela Mirek, Alicja Kamińska, Iwona Malinowska-Lipień
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe progressive ageing of the population reinforces the need for appropriate preparation of future healthcare professionals to provide geriatric care. Previous research indicates that students’ attitudes towards older adults vary, which may affect the quality of care provided. The aim of the study was to determine the impact of contact with older individuals, place of residence, and stage of education on the attitudes of students of healthcare professions towards older adults.MethodsThe study was conducted among 203 healthcare professions students between January and February 2025. The Scale of Attitudes Towards Older Adults (Polish: Skala Postaw Wobec Seniorów, SPWS) and an author-designed questionnaire were used in the study.ResultsWomen constituted the majority of the respondents (73.89%), and the mean age of the respondents was 22.51 ± 1.98 years. Respondents demonstrated mostly positive attitudes towards older adults, particularly on the Respect and Support subscale (M = 4.49 ± 0.61). More frequent contact with older adults was associated with an increase in the intensity of this attitude (R = 0.29; t = 4.32; p < 0.001). Negative attitudes showed no significant differences with respect to frequency of contact, year of study, or place of residence (p > 0.05).ConclusionsThe attitudes of students of healthcare professions towards older adults are positive, particularly in the Respect and Support subscale. Frequent contact with older adults is an important factor in fostering positive attitudes. Year of study and place of residence did not have a significant impact on the attitudes presented. Background

Population ageing is a well-recognised demographic trend, characterised by a growing proportion of older individuals compared with other age groups [1]. As a result, increasing emphasis is being placed on the appropriate preparation of future healthcare professionals. The scientific literature analyses, among other things, the attitudes of students of healthcare professions towards older patients and their readiness to work with this group in the future. Scientific reports indicate that despite the predominance of positive attitudes among students toward older adults, interest in specializing in geriatrics remains limited [2]. Meanwhile, older patients constitute a large proportion of those seeking specialist help in various areas of medicine. Therefore, in addition to training new specialists, the need for continuous improvement of the qualifications of medical staff working with older patients is also emphasised [3].

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), older adults are commonly defined as individuals aged 60 years and over in public health and ageing research [4, 5]. Therefore, the term “older adults” used throughout this study refers to persons aged 60 years and over.

In response to the growing scale of this phenomenon, international organisations such as the World Health Organization (WHO) and the United Nations have undertaken coordinated actions aimed at reducing ageism. The WHO Global Report on Ageism identifies education, intergenerational contact and policy interventions as key strategies for combating age-based discrimination [4]. Similarly, the Decade of Healthy Ageing (2021–2030) highlights the importance of transforming societal attitudes towards ageing and strengthening the competencies of healthcare professionals working with older adults [5]. Despite these global initiatives, there is still a need for empirical research examining how such factors operate at the level of education and influence students’ attitudes towards older people.

Ageism refers to positive or negative stereotypes, prejudices and discriminatory actions that are directed towards – or in favour of – older people solely because of their age or the belief that a person is “old” [6]. This phenomenon can manifest itself both covertly and overtly, and its effects can occur at the individual (micro), institutional (meso) and societal (macro) levels. Entrenched perceptions of older adults including viewing these individuals as weak, dependent or less capable – affect both the general public and healthcare professionals [7]. Older adults should be treated with due respect, as ageist attitudes can negatively affect their physical health, emotional well-being and social functioning [8].

Recent studies have shown that students’ attitudes towards older adults deteriorate during their healthcare education, particularly in terms of reduced interest in working with older patients and an increase in age-related stereotypes. This change has been attributed to factors such as limited exposure to positive geriatric role models, the predominance of hospital-based training focused on acute care, and the perception of geriatrics as a less attractive or less prestigious specialty [8]. As attitudes are complex and encompass many dimensions, both quantitative and qualitative research is required for their full understanding. At the same time, a major challenge is posed by the fact that there are many different instruments for assessing attitudes, each with its own limitations, which makes it difficult to compare results and draw clear conclusions [8, 9].

Contact between students of healthcare professions and older adults plays a key role in shaping their attitudes towards this population, as direct experiences can reduce prejudice and build greater understanding of the needs of this patient group. Place of residence – especially differences between urban and rural environments – can also influence the frequency of contact with older people and perceptions of old age [8]. The stage of education is also an important factor, as progression through the course of study is often associated with greater knowledge, clinical experience, and professional maturity. These findings can serve as a basis for a well-thought-out geriatric education programme tailored to the individual characteristics and competences of students [4, 9]. These findings align with Allport’s Contact Theory, which suggests that structured, positive interactions between different groups can effectively reduce prejudice and improve intergroup relations [10, 11]. In the context of healthcare education, this framework implies that frequent and meaningful contact with older adults may mitigate ageist attitudes by fostering empathy and challenging negative stereotypes. Consequently, understanding these dynamics is essential for developing educational strategies that promote age-sensitive care. In Poland, geriatric education is gradually being incorporated into undergraduate curricula for healthcare professions; however, substantial variability remains between universities with regard to the scope, number of teaching hours, and timing of geriatric training [12, 13]. The healthcare programmes represented in the present study differ in duration, ranging from three-year undergraduate programmes (e.g. nursing, midwifery, paramedicine and public health) to five- and six-year programmes (e.g. physiotherapy, dentistry and medicine). Furthermore, the timing and scope of geriatric education vary considerably across programmes and universities. Consequently, students at the same year of study may differ in their exposure to geriatric content and clinical experience with older adults [13]. Previous analyses have indicated that geriatrics is often introduced only at later stages of education, which may limit opportunities for sustained development of competencies and attitudes towards older adults. Despite ongoing demographic changes and increasing healthcare demands related to population ageing, there is still limited evidence regarding factors shaping attitudes towards older adults among Polish healthcare students. The aim of the study was to determine the impact of contact with older adults, place of residence, and stage of education on the attitudes of students of healthcare professions towards older adults.

Methods

Study design

The cross-sectional study was conducted between January and February 2025 using a diagnostic survey method with an online questionnaire.

Setting and sample

The survey was conducted online by making the questionnaire available via a Google form. The survey link was posted on online forums for students of healthcare professions and shared through social media platforms, allowing participation from students enrolled in various medical universities across Poland. As the distribution channels were not restricted to specific institutions, the exact universities attended by participants were not recorded. Therefore, the sample should be considered heterogeneous but not institution specific.

Ultimately, 203 correctly completed questionnaires were analyzed. Inclusion criteria for the study included voluntary consent to participate in the study, age over 18, being a student of a healthcare field, i.e. nursing, midwifery, physiotherapy, paramedicine, medicine, dentistry, public health. The exclusion criteria were refusal to participate in the study, age below 18 years, and being a student of a non-healthcare field. The questionnaire did not contain any data that allowed the identification of participants.

Research tools

The study used the Scale of Attitudes Towards Older Adults (Polish: Skala Postaw wobec Seniorów, SPWS) adapted by Łukasiewicz and Kowalski [9], which enabled the assessment of the intensity of three dimensions of attitudes: Respect and Support, Rejection and Lack of Understanding, and Social Distance. The SPWS is an instrument developed for use in the Polish population to assess attitudes towards older adults. The scale was selected due to its cultural and linguistic suitability for the Polish population and its previous use in studies conducted among Polish respondents, including healthcare-related populations and studies on ageism in Polish society [9, 14]. The standardised questionnaire comprised 60 statements, which respondents rated using a six-point Likert scale (where “1” means “strongly disagree” and “6” means “strongly agree”). The instrument comprised three subscales: “Respect and Support” (α = 0.93), “Rejection and Lack of Understanding” (α = 0.86) and “Social Distance” (α = 0.7). High scores on the Respect and Support scale reflect a positive approach towards older adults, who are perceived as a source of life wisdom and experience. The Rejection and Lack of Understanding scale identifies attitudes characterised by irritation, lack of empathy, and social isolation of older adults, with higher scores indicating a negative attitude towards older people. In turn, a high score on the Social Distance scale indicates emotional detachment and the belief that older adults are excessively focused on their own material comfort. The study also used an author-designed questionnaire containing six questions and covering age, sex, place of residence, field of study, year of study and frequency of contact with older adults. An English-language version of the author-designed sociodemographic questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Ethical approval

The study was conducted with the approval of the Bioethics Committee of the Jagiellonian University Medical College in Krakow (No. 118.0043.1.402.2024) and in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the STROBE guidelines for observational studies. All respondents were informed that participation in the study was voluntary and anonymous, and that they could withdraw at any time.

Statistical analysis

The collected data was statistically analyzed using the Statistica v.13.1 PL. programme. Quantitative variables were described using basic descriptive statistics, whereas qualitative variables were presented as frequencies (n) and percentages (%). Differences between two groups were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test, whereas comparisons of three or more groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or its non-parametric equivalent, the Kruskal–Wallis test, with post hoc analyses. Associations between variables were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. A composite attitude score was calculated as the mean of all items included in the SPWS scale. Negatively worded items were reverse-coded so that higher values consistently reflected more positive attitudes towards older adults. The composite score was used as the dependent variable in multivariable regression analyses. A multivariable linear regression model was performed to assess factors associated with attitudes towards older adults. The composite attitude score was used as the dependent variable, and selected sociodemographic variables (age, gender, and frequency of contact with older adults) were included as independent variables. A significance level of p < 0.05 was adopted in the study.

Results

A total of 203 students from selected healthcare fields took part in the study. Women constituted the majority of respondents (150; 73.89%), and the age of participants was most often between 21 and 25 years old (144; 70.94%). Almost half of the respondents lived in cities with a population of over 100,000 (96; 47.29 per cent). The largest group consisted of nursing students (30.54%). About one third of respondents were fifth-year students. A detailed description of the study sample is presented in Table 1.

Sociodemographic characteristics of respondents

Over one third of the respondents (38.42%) reported frequent contact with older adults. Slightly over one quarter of the respondents reported very frequent contact with older adults (27.59%), while slightly fewer respondents reported infrequent contact (24.63%). 9.36% of respondents reported having no contact with older adults (Fig. 1).

Frequency of contact between students and older adults

The frequency of contact between students and older individuals significantly influenced the attitude based on respect and support, for which a statistically significant positive correlation was noted (R = 0.29; t = 4.32; p < 0.001). This indicates that higher frequency of contact was associated with higher levels of this attitude (from MNever = 4.04; SD = 0.39 to MVery often = 4.68; SD = 0.61). For the attitude based on rejection and lack of understanding, the results in different contact groups varied only slightly. (from MVery often = 3.29; SD = 0.88 to MOften = 3.43; SD = 0.87), and the correlation was not significant (R = -0.02; p = 0.75). Similarly, the attitude of social distancing showed little difference between groups (from MVery often = 2.41; SD = 0.44 to MRarely = 2.58; SD = 0.49). The correlation between contact frequency and social distance also proved to be insignificant (R = -0.13; p = 0.071). The results therefore suggest that more frequent contact with older people is associated only with a stronger positive attitude but does not influence the intensity of negative attitudes towards older individuals (Table 2).

Students' attitudes towards older adults did not depend on their year of study. The intensity of the attitude based on respect and support ranged from 4.33 (SD = 0.67) for the fourth year to 4.64 (SD = 0.72) for the third year. The correlation analysis confirmed the absence of statistically significant relationships between the year of study and the attitude of respect and support (R = 0.12; p = 0.087), attitudes of rejection and lack of understanding (R = 0.04; p = 0.538) and social distance (R = -0.04; p = 0.53). The results of the study indicate that the intensity of attitudes based on respect and support was similar among respondents living in towns of different sizes, ranging from 4.48 (SD = 0.63) in towns with up to 100,000 residents to 4.50 (SD = 0.59) in towns with over 100,000 residents. Similarly, slight differences were observed in the case of attitudes based on rejection and lack of understanding, with averages ranging from 3.31 (SD = 0.89) in cities with up to 100,000 residents to 3.45 (SD = 0.78) in rural areas. Correlation analysis showed that there was no statistically significant relationship between place of residence and attitudes of respect and support (R = -0.02; p = 0.773). There was also no significant correlation with regard to rejection and lack of understanding (R = -0.01; p = 0.905). However, it was observed that the larger the place of residence of the respondents, the less often they declared social distance towards older adults, which was confirmed by average values ranging from 2.59 (SD = 0.48) in rural areas to 2.41 (SD = 0.47) in cities with more than 100,000 residents. However, this trend did not reach statistical significance (R = -0.14; p = 0.052). Overall, the results indicate that no significant association was observed between place of residence and attitudes towards older adults (Table 4).

Multivariable analysis of attitudes towards older adults

To provide a more comprehensive assessment of factors associated with attitudes towards older adults, a composite total attitude score was calculated based on all scale items and used in the multivariable analysis. A multivariable linear regression model was then conducted, with the total attitude score as the dependent variable and selected sociodemographic variables as predictors. The overall model was statistically significant (F = 5.00; p = 0.002), although it explained a relatively small proportion of variance (R² = 0.07). Among the analyzed variables, gender was significantly associated with the overall attitude score (β = 0.099; *p* < 0.001), indicating that attitudes differed between male and female students. Women demonstrated significantly more positive overall attitudes towards older adults compared with men.

In contrast, no statistically significant associations were observed for frequency of contact with older adults (β = −0.004; p = 0.769) or age (β = −0.002; p = 0.315). These findings suggest that, after adjustment for other variables, the previously observed relationship between contact frequency and attitudes may not represent an independent association. Thus, the association observed in bivariate analyses may have been influenced by other sociodemographic factors included in the regression model (Table 5).

Discussion

The present study identified several key findings. First, more frequent contact with older adults was associated with more positive attitudes in bivariate analyses, particularly in the domain of respect and support. However, this association was not confirmed in multivariable analysis. Second, most sociodemographic variables, including year of study and place of residence, were not significantly associated with attitudes towards older adults. Third, gender was the only variable significantly associated with the overall attitude score. Female students demonstrated more positive attitudes towards older adults, which is consistent with previous studies reporting greater empathy and more favourable attitudes towards ageing among women in healthcare-related professions [15, 16].

The findings of the present study are also in line with previous Polish research. Łukasiewicz and Kowalski [9] reported that attitudes towards older adults were generally characterised by respect and acceptance, although some age-related stereotypes remained present. Similar observations were made by Łukasiewicz and Dziewulska [14], who found predominantly positive attitudes towards older adults among nurses. These results correspond with our findings, which indicate a predominance of positive attitudes, particularly in the domain of respect and support.

The changing demographic structure means that health professions education needs to be particularly focused on developing competencies and attitudes that promote high-quality care for older adults. The population of people aged 60 years and over is now a rapidly growing demographic group worldwide, and according to United Nations projections, by 2030 it will exceed 1.4 billion, reflecting the unprecedented pace of population ageing and significantly affecting the structure of health needs and the organisation of care systems [17]. This process significantly affects the functioning of health systems, the structure of families and the need to adapt health professions education to new epidemiological and social realities [18].

The WHO Global Report on Ageism highlights that ageism is one of the key social determinants of health and is present at all levels of care, from clinical relationships to institutional therapeutic decisions [19, 20]. Numerous strategies developed by the WHO and the United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs show that negative attitudes towards older adults may lead to lowered therapeutic expectations, excessive medicalisation or, conversely, to neglect of symptoms in older patients [21–23].

The results of this study show a prevalence of respectful and supportive attitudes, which is in line with observations from other countries. Alqahtani et al. reported moderately positive attitudes among medical and nursing students but highlighted the prevalence of ambivalence and lack of confidence in caring for older adults [24]. Similar results were obtained by Castro et al., who demonstrated that despite a willingness to work with older patients, students felt concerns regarding communication competence, professional workload and the functional limitations of geriatric patients [25].

Analyses carried out in Europe, Asia and North America indicate that attitudes towards older adults are determined by both personal experiences and the structure of educational programmes, particularly the number of hours of geriatric practice [25–27]. A systematic review by Ayalon et al. highlights that tools used to measure ageism vary significantly in scope (covering stereotypes, prejudice, and discrimination), target populations, and psychometric properties. Many existing scales fail to capture the multi-dimensional nature of the phenomenon. The authors emphasize a lack of standardization and limited validation for several instruments, which hinders the comparability of research findings and underscores the need for more comprehensive and reliable assessment methods [28]. While frequency of contact with older adults was associated with more positive attitudes in bivariate analyses, this relationship was not confirmed in multivariable models. This suggests that the effect of contact frequency may not be independent, but rather intertwined with other sociodemographic characteristics, particularly gender. Therefore, although contact with older adults appears to support positive attitudes, its influence may be more complex than initially indicated by the bivariate analyses alone.

The significant positive correlation confirms that more frequent interactions favour stronger pro-social attitudes towards older adults, with no effect on levels of negative attitudes and social distance. This finding may be understood within the framework of Contact Theory, first introduced by Allport, which suggests that direct interactions between members of different groups—when occurring under favourable conditions—can help reduce prejudice and foster more positive attitudes. This interpretation is further supported by recent meta-analytic evidence demonstrating the effectiveness of contact-based approaches in mitigating ageism [10, 11].

Apriceno and Levy showed that most effectively designed interventions include an educational component combined with direct, long-term contact with older adults [23]. Studies conducted in Canada and the UK show that intergenerational contact improves not only attitudes but also students' self-assessed clinical competence in geriatric care [29, 30]. Programmes such as "Geriatrics Connect", "Intergenerational Service Learning" or the "Senior Mentor Program" play an important role in reducing stereotypes of dependency and inactivity associated with older people and in increasing understanding of phenomena such as loneliness, frailty or multimorbidity [28–30].

In the present study, however, more frequent contact did not correlate with a decrease in negative attitudes or social distance, which may indicate that not only the frequency, but also the quality of contact is crucial. The observed pattern, in which more frequent contact was associated with stronger positive attitudes but not with a reduction in negative attitudes, is consistent with findings from recent literature. A scoping review by Huynh et al. indicates that positive and negative dimensions of ageism may coexist, and that contact-based experiences tend to enhance supportive attitudes without necessarily reducing more deeply rooted negative perceptions. Furthermore, the lack of association between attitudes and year of study aligns with evidence indicating that standard health professions education does not consistently lead to attitudinal change towards older adults [31, 32].

These findings are consistent with the analysis of Drury et al. who highlighted that positive student attitudes develop primarily when contact is peer-to-peer, informal and free from the structural burdens typical of the clinical environment [33]. In a study by Kusumastuti et al, contact during clinical placements – conducted under conditions of high workload and limited support – was found to lead to a deterioration in students’ evaluations of ageing [34]. This finding indicates the need for deliberate planning of the educational environment.

The lack of correlation between the year of study and the attitudes analyzed suggests that the current model of geriatric education is not having a sufficient impact on attitudinal change. Reviews of medical curricula show that geriatrics is rarely prioritised in education and that these classes have a high degree of variability in terms of number of hours, scope of content and degree of integration with the practical pathway [34, 35]. Additionally, in Poland, substantial variation between universities has been demonstrated, and in many of them geriatrics is included only at the final stage of studies [36, 37].

The lack of progression of attitudes between cohorts may also reflect a "ceiling effect", where positive attitudes at the early stages of education make it difficult to detect further improvement. The results regarding the lack of influence of place of residence on attitudes towards older adults are in line with the trends observed in international studies. While previous studies suggested that people raised in rural environments may have greater exposure to intergenerational relationships, more recent analyses indicate that ultimately personal experience, rather than geographic background, is the main predictor of attitudes towards ageing [27, 38]. The increasingly homogenised lifestyles of young adults, together with educational mobility, may further reduce regional differences [39].

Research by Suberry et al. demonstrates that ageism can be effectively reduced when interventions are carefully designed and evaluated using multidimensional assessment approaches. Furthermore, anti-discrimination education should be introduced early and implemented through interactive strategies that incorporate direct intergenerational contact. The use of diverse measurement methods revealed that changes in ageist attitudes are not always explicitly observable, underscoring the importance of employing multiple, complementary assessment tools to capture both overt and implicit shifts [39].

In Poland, geriatric content is gradually being incorporated into the curricula of health profession programmes, although the scope and depth of such training vary considerably across institutions. Current national educational standards emphasize core competencies related to the biology of aging, principles of comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA), multimorbidity management, and effective communication with older adults, as outlined in the guidelines of the Centre of Postgraduate Medical Education [40, 41].

he Polish Society of Geriatric Medicine similarly highlights the need to strengthen geriatric competencies at the undergraduate level, citing demographic trends and the persistent shortage of geriatric specialists as key drivers for curricular expansion [41 ,42]. National reports further indicate that insufficient knowledge about aging processes contributes to the perpetuation of negative attitudes, reinforcing the need to integrate antiageist training modules into the education of future healthcare professionals [43].

These findings also point to the importance of considering the broader cultural context in which attitudes towards ageing are formed. The National Center to Reframe Aging emphasises that dominant societal narratives—embedded in language, metaphors, and media representations—play a key role in shaping perceptions of older adults [44].

The pattern observed in this study—where more frequent contact with older adults was associated with stronger positive attitudes (respect and support), but not with a reduction in negative attitudes or social distance—may be interpreted in light of recent research on intergenerational learning [44, 32]. These findings suggest that contact primarily strengthens relationship-based, positive dimensions of attitudes, while having a more limited impact on more deeply rooted negative perceptions.

One possible explanation is the “humanisation” mechanism, whereby direct interaction shifts perceptions from abstract stereotypes to more individualised and nuanced understandings of older adults. At the same time, increased exposure may lead to a more realistic appraisal of ageing and caregiving, contributing to more complex, rather than uniformly positive, attitudes. This interpretation is further supported by our finding that the year of study was not associated with attitudes, suggesting that educational exposure alone—without meaningful interpersonal contact—may be insufficient to drive attitudinal change.

The absence of a relationship between attitudes and year of study in the present study may be linked to the way geriatric education is organised in Poland. Existing analyses indicate that undergraduate curricula differ substantially across institutions in terms of content, number of teaching hours, and the stage at which geriatric training is introduced [13, 35, 37]. Such variability may limit the cumulative impact of education over time, which is consistent with our finding that progression through the course of study did not translate into measurable differences in attitudes.

In summary, it is important to note that the results obtained fit into the broad context of research on the attitudes of future health care professionals towards older adults. The relationship between the frequency of intergenerational contact and the intensity of positive attitudes remains one of the most frequently described phenomena, which is also confirmed in this study [31, 13]. At the same time, the lack of association between attitudes and year of study or place of residence indicates the complexity of the determinants accompanying the formation of attitudes towards ageing and old age. The results discussed here are part of the current scientific reflection on the need for a more coherent approach to geriatric education, especially in the context of global demographic change and a growing awareness of the consequences of ageism in clinical care. Juxtaposing the data with the literature and our own observations makes it possible to understand the areas in which the educational process fosters the development of positive attitudes, as well as those where significant gaps remain that require further reflection.

The study has several limitations, including its cross-sectional nature, the self-reporting nature of the data and the lack of an assessment of the quality of contact with older adults. Participants may also have been influenced by social desirability bias when answering the questionnaire, which may have resulted in responses perceived as socially acceptable rather than fully reflecting their actual attitudes or experiences. Another limitation of the study is the gender imbalance of the sample, with women constituting the majority of participants. This may limit the generalisability of the findings and may have influenced the overall distribution of attitudes towards older adults. In future research, it would be worth extending the analysis to include the environments in which contact with older adults most commonly occurs (e.g. work settings, home environment, care facilities), as this context can significantly influence the nature and quality of intergenerational relationships and the formation of attitudes towards older people. Despite these limitations, the study provides up-to-date and important insights into students' attitudes in an ageing society and may serve as a reference point for further research and for modifications to educational programmes. Furthermore, the lack of data on participants’ institutional affiliation precluded analysis of potential differences between universities and may additionally limit the generalisability of the findings.

Conclusions

The results presented here indicate that students of healthcare professions. hold generally positive attitudes towards older adults, and that these attitudes are strongly associated with individual experiences of contact with this population. Frequency of contact with older adults proved to be a significant differentiating factor – the more frequent the contact, the higher the level of positive attitudes reported. At the same time, the lack of significant differences by stage of study and place of residence suggests that these variables are not key determinants of attitudes towards older adults in the study group. These findings point to the complexity of how attitudes towards older adults are formed and indicate that providing students with opportunities for regular, meaningful contact may support the development of more positive attitudes.

Implications for practice

Given these results, several practical implications can be formulated. Firstly, geriatric education should be integrated into the curriculum in all years of study, rather than being limited to a single course. Secondly, great emphasis should be placed on creating stable intergenerational relationships, which show the greatest effectiveness in reducing ageism. Practically, this may be achieved through longitudinal, curriculum-embedded initiatives such as senior mentor programmes, intergenerational service-learning, or continuity-based clinical placements. Key elements include regular contact over time, structured reflection, and collaboration with community partners. Thirdly, it is necessary to increase the number of practical hours in older adult care settings, in a manner that ensures support and positive experiences for students. Finally, the findings of the present study suggest that regular contact with older adults is associated with more positive attitudes. Therefore, educational strategies should focus on creating opportunities for meaningful interactions with older people. Innovative approaches, such as virtual geriatric simulations, role-play-based experiential learning, telecare programmes, or digitally supported interactions, may complement direct contact, particularly when real-life exposure is limited.
