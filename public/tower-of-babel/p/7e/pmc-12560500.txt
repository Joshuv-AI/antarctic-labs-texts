Multilevel drivers of tobacco use among people living with HIV in West Nile and Karamoja Regions in Uganda: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Ezekiel Musasizi, Heather Wipfli, Jim Arinaitwe, Lynn Atuyambe, Kyra Guy, Ronald Kusolo, Elizeus Rutebemberwa, David Guwatudde
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTobacco use is a significant public health problem, especially among People Living with HIV (PLWH) yet evidence on its prevalence and multi-level drivers in Uganda remains limited. This study assessed the prevalence and multilevel drivers of tobacco use among PLWH in two regions in northern Uganda.MethodsWe conducted a mixed-methods study involving a survey of 439 PLWH randomly sampled from eight health facilities in West Nile and Karamoja regions and Focus Group Discussion (FGDs) of 47 PLWH to understand multi-level drivers of tobacco use. Data was collected from August 15th to December 20th, 2024. Survey data was collected electronically using Open Data Kit (ODK) platform. Tobacco use was biochemically verified using cotinine urine dipsticks. We conducted quantitative analysis to identify factors associated with tobacco use using multinomial regression model using STATA v14. Qualitative data was analyzed using thematic analysis in NVivo V15.ResultsAccording to self-reports, Tobacco use was more prevalent in Karamoja than in West Nile, with smokeless tobacco dominating in Karamoja and smoking more common in West Nile. Nicotine dependence was higher among smokers (49.1%) than smokeless users (32.1%). Smoking was associated with being male (RRR = 11.13; 95% CI, 5.08–24.41) and having lower tobacco realted - knowledge (RRR = 0.39; 95% CI, 0.17–0.90). Smokeless tobacco use was associated with district of residence (RRR = 177.57; 95% CI, 29.36–1074.02) and other substance use (RRR = 4.08; 95% CI, 1.06–15.64). FGDs revealed multi-level drivers spanning the intrapersonal level (addiction, HIV-related distress, gender norms, and limited knowledge), interpersonal level (peer and family influence), community level (cultural practices and stigma), organizational level (tobacco affordability and inadequate cessation services), and the policy level, where limited awareness and weak enforcement of existing smokeless tobacco regulations contributed to continued use of tobacco.ConclusionTobacco cessation strategies should be mode-specific and context-sensitive. In West Nile, where smoking and dual use are common, interventions should target men with low tobacco-related knowledge. In Karamoja, where smokeless tobacco is culturally entrenched and enforcement of existing bans is weak, cessation requires community-based education, facility-based treatment, and post-care support. Addressing multi-level drivers is essential for tobacco use cessation among PLWH in similar settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24938-9.Keywords: Smoking; Smokeless tobacco use; Tobacco use; HIV, Uganda

Introduction

Tobacco use remains a leading cause of preventable death globally, accounting for over 8 million deaths annually, and its burden is particularly severe in low-and middle income countries (LMICs) which host more than 80% of the world’s tobacco users [1]. Among people living with HIV (PLWH), tobacco use is particularly concerning, with prevalence rates double that of the general population (20% vs. 10%) [2]. Tobacco use among PLWH is associated with hastier advancement from HIV to AIDS, poorer outcomes in HIV-related opportunistic infections, and lower adherence to antiretroviral therapy (ART) [3]. The high prevalence of HIV in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) exacerbates the burden of tobacco use in the region [4]. In 2023, Eastern and Southern Africa accounted for 260,000 of the 630,000 global HIV-related deaths [5]. While region-specific data are lacking, modeling from a large multinational HIV cohort found that approximately 24% of all-cause mortality among PLWH were attributed to cigarette smoking [6]. In Uganda, the high prevalence of HIV [7], elevated tobacco use among PLWH and diverse patterns of smokeless and smoked tobacco use [8] make it an ideal setting for research on the intersection of these two health issues.

Uganda has made important progress in establishing a legislative framework for tobacco control. The Tobacco Control Act (2015) operationalizes the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control and prohibits tobacco advertising and public smoking [9]. However, enforcement has been inconsistent, with literature pointing to under-resourced institutions, limited awareness among enforcers and the public [10]. Importantly, the Act does not mandate provision of cessation services, leaving HIV programs and other health platforms with few tools to respond to the high burden of tobacco use among PLWH.

Despite these known risks, research on tobacco use among PLWH in SSA, particularly in Uganda, remains limited. Although studies have identified intrapersonal and interpersonal factors such as gender, stress, alcohol use, manual labor, lower educational attainment, and having friends who smoke as key determinants of tobacco use among PLWH [2, 11], these tend to focus solely on combustible tobacco use, neglecting the broader spectrum of tobacco products, such as smokeless tobacco [12] which is common in tobacco growing regions [13]. Notably, smokeless tobacco is prevalent in tobacco-growing regions like West Nile (Arua district) and Karamoja (Moroto district) due to cultural acceptance and easy access through local production [13].

Tobacco use in Uganda remains highest in the Northen districts of Arua (14.5%) and Moroto (24.8%) [14], well above the national average of 10.1% [8]. Both districts bear a significant HIV burden and are part of Uganda’s major tobacco-producing regions, where social and economic ties to tobacco cultivation may normalize its use. Arua and Moroto districts were therefore purposively selected as study sites due to their high tobacco use prevalence, substantial HIV burden, and entrenched tobacco-growing economies. However, no study has been conducted to estimate the prevalence and drivers of smokeless and smoked tobacco use among PLWH in these tobacco-growing regions. This knowledge gap limits the development of effective and culturally appropriate tobacco cessation interventions for this population group.

To address these gaps, this study aimed to (1) estimate the prevalence of both smoked and smokeless tobacco use among PLWH receiving care at HIV clinics in West Nile (Arua district) and Karamoja (Moroto district) regions in Uganda, and (2) examine the multi-level drivers of tobacco use in this population. The findings are intended to inform the development of tailored, evidence-based behavior change interventions that promote long-term tobacco cessation among PLWH in similar settings in Uganda and beyond.

Methods

Study design

The study used an explanatory sequential design, nested in an ongoing clinical trial registered on clinical trials.gov [clinical trial No: NCT05487807; Registration date: 27 November 2023]. Details of the clinical trial have been published elsewhere [15]. Survey data were collected from 439 participants between August and September 2024, followed by qualitative data from 47 participants between November and December 2024 to further explore tobacco use behaviors. The study was informed by the Social Ecological Model (SEM), which recognizes the multiple levels of influence on behavior. The SEM considers several factors, including policy, community, organizational, interpersonal, and intrapersonal influences on health behavior [16]. The SEM framework informed the study questionnaire to determine how these multilevel drivers shape smokeless and combustible tobacco use behavior.

Study sites

The study was conducted in two districts, Arua in West Nile region and Moroto in Karamoja region, both located in the northern part of Uganda, as described in the Quit4Life + trial protocol [15]. These two sites were selected because they have the highest rate of tobacco use in the country, yet differ in cultural context and tobacco use practices. Participants were those enrolled in HIV treatment programs at health facilities within these districts. In Arua district, the study was carried out at Arua Regional Referral Hospital, Adumi Health Centre IV, Omugo Health Centre IV, and River Oli Health Centre IV. In the Moroto district, the study facilities were Moroto Regional Referral Hospital, Nadunget Health Centre IV, Kosiroi HC II, and Nakaperimen HC II.

Study population

We recruited a random sample of PLWH from eight HIV clinics between August 15th and September 15th, 2024 for the quantitative component and between November 15th and December 20th, 2024 for the qualitative component. Eligible participants were HIV-positive individuals receiving ART at the selected facilities, aged 18 years or older, and capable of completing the survey in the Lugbara language (Nga’karimojong), or English. Individuals who were very ill or had cognitive impairments were excluded from the study. Cognitive impairment was assessed using the Standardised Mini Mental-State Examination (SMMSE) with a score < 24 indicating exclusion [17–21]. The tool was administered by trained clinicians in the participant’s preferred language (Lugbara, Nga’karimojong, or English).

Quantitative procedures

Sample selection

We employed a multi-stage sampling approach to select respondents for this study: First, Arua and Moroto districts were purposively selected due to their high tobacco use prevalence. Second, four health facilities per district were chosen based on their role as Quit4Life + implementation sites [15]. Third, at each facility, the ART register served as the sampling frame, and simple random sampling was conducted using STATA v14 to select PLWH aged 18 and above. The ART registers across the eight facilities had PLWH on ART ranging form 23 in Nadunget Health Centre III to 6850 at Arua Regional Referral Hospital.The number of participants selected at facility was distributed proportionate to the size of the ART register at the time of data collection. Selected individuals were contacted by clinic staff, provided with study details, and invited for eligibility screening. Eligible participants received written study information in English or the local language, with verbal explanations provided for those with literacy challenges. Using a formula for cross-sectional studies, we estimated a sample size of 439 participants, allowing for a 5% significance level and a 3% margin of error [22].

Study variables

The dependent variable, smokeless and/or smoke tobacco use, was assessed through self-report and biochemical verification. Biochemical verification was done using urine cotinine dipsticks [23, 24]. A cotinine cut-off value of >15 ng/mL, previously validated among African populations [25–27], was used to detect current tobacco use. While this threshold has demonstrated validity in general African cohorts, variability in nicotine metabolism among PLWH and differences in local tobacco products may influence the accuracy of biochemical verification. Self-reported tobacco use status was assessed by asking respondents whether they currently use any form of tobacco (yes/no) and if they had ever used tobacco before (yes/no) [28]. Among current users, nicotine dependence was evaluated using two specific scales: (1) The Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence (FTND) for those who smoked, as described by Fagerström and Eissenberg [29], and (2) the Severson 7-item Smokeless Tobacco Dependence Scale (SSTDS) for those who used smokeless tobacco, according to Mushtaq and Beebe [30]. Tobacco use patterns were assessed using the Smoking Timeline Follow-Back (TLFB) procedure, which has been demonstrated to be a reliable and valid measure [31].

The exploratory variables were intrapersonal and interpersonal characteristics outlined in the SEM. Intrapersonal characteristics included sociodemographic variables such as age, sex, marital status, and education level, which were assessed using questions adapted from the Uganda Demographic and Health Survey (UDHS) [32]. Socioeconomic status was assessed using a wealth index constructed through principal component analysis (PCA) based on ownership of 15 household assets. These included items such as a radio, television, mobile phone, bicycle, motorcycle, livestock, bed, mattress, sofa set, table, lantern, and access to electricity or piped water. This approach has been widely validated for assessing relative household wealth in low-resource settings [33].

Respondents were also asked about their temptation to use smokeless and smoked tobacco using the Temptation Scale [34]. Individual factors, such as tobacco related health knowledge and risk perception, were assessed among respondents. Respondents ranked their beliefs about the negative health effects of tobacco on a Likert scale ranging from 0 (not harmful) to 3 (very harmful) and indicated the correlation between tobacco use frequency and health impact, categorized as any use, at least weekly, daily, or multiple times per day. Specific questions were asked about perceived health risks to the lungs and heart. Respondents also weighed their risk perception of second-hand smoke through three questions: their awareness of its harmful effects on others, the importance of having a smoke-free workplace (rated on a scale from 1, “Not at all,” to 4, “Very important”), and their attitudes toward second-hand smoke exposure in their homes [35].

Respondents were also asked questions regarding their social networks, friendship networks, and social support systems, as outlined by Blok et al. [28]. Respondents provided information about their social networks, including household members, up to five close friends, and siblings and parents living outside the household. Close friends were defined as those in contact within the past six months. The total network size was calculated by adding all reported ties. Respondents specified whether each network member currently used tobacco, allowing for the calculation of tobacco use proportions across all ties and by type (household, close friends, family). Additionally, social support was assessed by probing whether network members encouraged the respondent to use tobacco, quit, or neither [28].

Data collection

Prior to data collection, the questionnaire was pre-tested with 15 PLWH at two non-study HIV clinics (one in Karamoja and another in West Nile region) to ensure clarity and flow, with minor adjustments made accordingly. The finalised tool was administered by 15 trained research assistants, all of whom were clinicians working within HIV clinics at the selected health facilities to ensure confidentiality. The survey was conducted through face-to-face interviews using the Open Data Kit (ODK) platform (https://getodk.org/) on tablets. At the end of each survey, we checked for missing data to ensure completeness and consistency throughout the data collection process. The survey tool is attached as supplementary file 1.

Data analysis

Respondents’ demographic characteristics were analyzed using frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations. The associations between respondents’ characteristics and tobacco use were tested using Pearson test chi-square for categorical variables and Fisher’s exact test in cases of small cell counts. The continuous variables were analysed using the analysis of variance (ANOVA) [36] in cases of normality and Kruskal-Wallis tests where the normality assumption was violated. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to test for normality [37].

Multivariate multinomial regression was used to assess the association between intrapersonal and interpersonal factors with the various tobacco use modes (smoking, smokeless use, and dual use). This model was chosen because it allows for the simultaneous analysis of multiple categorical outcomes. Relative risk ratios (RRR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were computed to estimate the strength of association between independent variables and each tobacco use mode relative to non-use [38]. Predictors such as age, gender, socioeconomic status, alcohol and other substance use, and psychosocial factors were included in the model, adjusting for the district of residence. The reference category for tobacco use was non-use, and the analysis adjusted for potential confounders identified through prior literature. Data were analyzed using Stata version 14.0, and statistical significance was determined at a p-value of less than 0.05.

Qualitative procedures

Participants and recruitment

PLWH were recruited from two study districts for the focus group discussions (FGDs), with separate groups for men and women to address gender differences in stigma. They were eligible if aged 18 or older, receiving ART at the clinic during recruitment and had been confirmed as tobacco user in the quantitative phase. FGD participants were purposively selected from this pool of confirmed users to ensure diversity in age, gender, and mode of tobacco use. Each FGD included 8–12 participants, for a total of five groups (N = 47). Clinic staff assisted with participant recruitment, ensuring a diverse range of voices in the study.

Data collection

Two facilitators moderated each FGD: one led the discussion, while the other managed logistics and notetaking. Sessions lasted 1.5 h, were conducted in the local language, and recorded with participant consent. Audio recordings were transcribed into English, with two bilingual transcribers cross-checking for completeness and accuracy. The transcribing process was validated for consistency, ensuring high-quality data for analysis. The data collection, analysis and results reporting follow the COREQ guidelines [39]. The FGD guide is attached as supplementary file 2.

Qualitative measures

The study used structured discussion guides to ensure consistency across sites. These focused on the tobacco use drivers at the different levels of the SEM. Participants completed a brief anonymous survey on demographics before engaging in discussions about tobacco use initiation and behavior, HIV status, moods and emotions, tobacco’s impact on health and HIV treatment. Other dimensions of the discusion involved the role of social and cultural influences, tobacco affordability, and policies affecting tobacco behavior.

Data management and analysis

Transcripts were coded and analyzed using Nvivo 15 to identify themes related to tobacco use behavior. The coding process was iterative, with an initial codebook developed from moderator guides and refined as new codes emerged. The codebook was developed by two study team members, with discrepancies resolved through a meeting with a third investigator, all of whom are Public Health graduates. Scripts were coded at sentence level with Kappa scores for each theme ranging from 0.81 to 0.93. Nine hundred Ten excerpts from the FGDs were coded into themes, providing a detailed basis for understanding the multilevel drivers of tobacco use among PLWH.

Associations of smokeless and smoked tobacco use with intrapersonal and interpersonal factors

The multivariate multinomial regression analysis in Table 4 identified several significant intrapersonal and interpersonal factors associated with smoking. Males were more likely to smoke compared to females (RRR = 11.13; 95% CI, 5.08–24.41). Respondents knowledgeable about tobacco-related health effects were less likely to smoke (RRR = 0.39; 95% CI, 0.17–0.90). Alcohol users were more likely to smoke (RRR = 3.57; 95% CI, 1.70–7.47) and use dual tobacco forms (RRR = 11.93; 95% CI, 2.42–58.78). Similarly, respondents using other substances were more likely to smoke (RRR = 3.08; 95% CI, 1.30–7.27) or use smokeless tobacco (RRR = 4.08; 95% CI, 1.06–15.64). Having close friends who used tobacco increased the likelihood of smoking (RRR = 8.81; 95% CI, 4.27–18.18) and dual use (RRR = 16.46; 95% CI, 1.74–156.14). Those receiving family support for tobacco use were more likely to smoke (RRR = 7.25; 95% CI, 2.67–19.68), use smokeless tobacco (RRR = 7.32; 95% CI, 2.07–25.93), or engage in dual use (RRR = 16.29; 95% CI, 3.15–84.33).

Qualitative drivers of tobacco users

Intrapersonal drivers (433 excerpts)

The most commonly discussed topic was emotional struggles and the perceived benefits of using tobacco. Stress related to HIV status, including societal rejection and mandatory testing, drove many to use tobacco as a coping mechanism (Supplementary file 3, Quote 1). Tobacco use increased after HIV diagnosis for some, driven by the stress of treatment initiation (Supplementary file 3, Quote 2), while others started due to cravings and frustration (Supplementary file 3, Quote 3). Misconceptions about smokeless tobacco being less harmful and helping cure ailments such as flue perpetuated use (Supplementary file 3, Quote 4 & 5). Others attributed their tobacco use to addiction with many reporting difficulty functioning without tobacco (Supplementary file 3, Quote 6). Gendered pressures shaped behavior, with men using tobacco while undertaking roles like guarding cattle, while women faced societal restrictions on its use (Supplementary file 3, Quotes 7 & 8).

Interpersonal drivers (114 excerpts)

PLWH frequently discussed the influence of family members on their tobacco use. Women often prepared tobacco for their husbands as part of household routines (Supplementary file 3, Quote 10). Others were exposed to tobacco use in childhood, particularly in homes involved in tobacco cultivation, where its use was normalized (Supplementary file 3, Quote 11). Peer interactions further reinforced use, with social contexts encouraging shared consumption (Supplementary file 3, Quote 12). Friends often supplied tobacco to those unable to afford it, sustaining dependence within social networks (Supplementary file 3, Quote 13).

Community-level drivers (185 excerpts)

Tobacco use was entrenched as a generational legacy, with its consumption central to ceremonies and rites of passage (Supplementary file 3, Quotes 14 & 15). Elders played a significant role in normalizing use, creating expectations for participation in traditional practices (Supplementary file 3, Quote 16). Participants highlighted that snuffing tobacco was culturally mandated during events, regardless of education or status (Supplementary file 3, Quote 17). Stigma varied by form of use; smokeless tobacco was socially accepted (Supplementary file 3, Quote 18), while public smoking was often criticized (Supplementary file 3, Quote 19).

Organizational-level drivers (104 excerpts)

Tobacco’s affordability and availability, supported by the tobacco industry, made it highly accessible (Supplementary file 3, Quotes 20 & 21). Workplace norms also encouraged use, with employers favoring smokers for short-term jobs due to perceptions of increased productivity (Supplementary file 3, Quote 22). Participants criticized cessation programs as inadequate, noting they failed to address the underlying drivers of tobacco addiction (Supplementary file 3, Quote 23).

Policy-level drivers (74 excerpts)

Participants frequently mentioned their limited awareness of regulations on smokeless tobacco, with many unsure whether any policies existed (Supplementary file 3, Quote 24). Some also observed that its use remained largely unregulated, underscoring a significant policy gap (Supplementary file 3, Quote 25).

Discussion

This study found a high prevalence of tobacco use among PLWH in Arua and Moroto districts, with notable regional differences. Tobacco use was shaped by factors across all levels of the SEM. At the intrapersonal level, tobacco use was associated with male gender, limited knowledge of tobacco-related harms, alcohol and substance use, and high nicotine dependence. These findings were reinforced by participant narratives describing tobacco as a coping mechanism for HIV-related stress, a perceived remedy for common ailments, and a habitual behavior shaped by addiction and gender norms. At the interpersonal level, tobacco use among family members and peers was a strong predictor of individual use, corroborated by qualitative accounts of early exposure within tobacco-growing households and routine sharing within social networks. At the community level, residence in Moroto district was associated with increased smokeless tobacco use, explained by deep-rooted cultural practices that normalize tobacco use during courtship, and traditional ceremonies, At organizational level, the absence of structured cessation support in HIV clinics, alongwith widespread availability and affordability of tobacco shaped use. At the policy level, participants reported limited awareness of tobacco regulations, reflecting broader gaps in enforcement and public communication.

In our study population of PLWH on ART, the prevalence of tobacco use was notably high, surpassing both regional and national estimates reported in previous surveys. This finding aligns with global and sub-Saharan African evidence showing elevated tobacco use among PLWH [40–43], with prevalence reaching up to 50% in some settings [26]. However, the levels observed in Moroto district, particularly for smokeless tobacco, are substantially higher than those documented elsewhere in the region. This contrast may be attributed to the widespread cultural acceptance and accessibility of smokeless tobacco in northeastern Uganda, especially in tobacco-growing communities where local production and social norms normalize its use. Our findings also suggest that self-reported measures may underestimate tobacco use, as biochemical verification identified more users than reported. This discrepancy likely reflects social desirability bias or recall limitations, particularly among women or users of smokeless products in culturally conservative settings. Further, regional differences in tobacco use modes are consistent with prior studies [44] and highlight the need for context specific interventions.

At intrapersonal level, our findings suggest that smokers exhibit higher nicotine dependence compared to smokeless tobacco users as measured by the FTND and SSTDS, respectively. This is consistent with existing evidence that smoking delivers nicotine more rapidly and in higher doses, reinforcing dependence [45] and addiction [46, 47]. The strong association between male gender and smoking aligns well with FGD reports of men using tobacco to fulfil roles like guarding cattle. Some PLWH initiated or increased tobacco use after diagnosis, citing it as a coping mechanism for HIV-related distress, including depression. Similar trends in high-income settings link emotional distress to higher tobacco use [48, 49]. The belief in tobacco’s protective properties against infections also has deep historical roots in Western cultural traditions [50] and may have been passed down to participants in this study or could show misconceptions about the safety of smokeless tobacco. Other substance use significantly increased the likelihood of smokeless and smoked tobacco use which is also consistent with previous research [2, 51] and highlights the need for mode-specific cessation strategies that address nicotine dependence with dimensions of knowledge enhancement and psychosocial support.

Further, our findings reveal that PLWH who use alcohol were more likely to use dual forms of tobacco (both smoked and smokeless tobacco). These findings suggest that alcohol may reinforce tobacco use not only through social and contextual overlap, but also through shared psychological mechanisms such as craving. This observation aligns with previous laboratory [52] and ecological momentary assessment studies [53, 54] demonstrating that alcohol intensifies tobacco craving, which in turn is a robust antecedent of smoking behavior. These patterns underscore the need for integrated cessation interventions that address alcohol use and craving management as part of comprehensive support for dual users.

Interpersonal factors, such as family support for tobacco use increased tobacco use across across all modes likely due to reinforced social pressures. This aligns with evidence suggesting that peer and family influences are key determinants of tobacco use [55]. The influence of interpersonal factors underscores the importance of integrating family-centered and peer-based approaches into cessation programs. Smokeless tobacco use was deeply embedded in cultural traditions, particularly in Moroto district, where it is widely accepted and often used in social settings. Similar findings have been reported in other low-resource settings where smokeless tobacco is viewed as a norm rather than a health risk [56]. Additionally, stigma, both related to HIV and tobacco use played a complex role. Our findings reveal that social settings contributed to tobacco use, for example, by enforcing mandatory HIV testing prior to marriage, which heightened feelings of rejection and stress-related tobacco reliance. The role of interpersonal factors in driving tobacco use emphasizes the need to engage communities and local leaders in tobacco control efforts.

At organizational level, PLWH continued to use tobacco because of limited access to tobacco cessation services within HIV clinics. This reflects broader gaps in integrating tobacco control into HIV care. While previous studies have documented the underutilization of cessation support among PLWH [11, 57], our study highlights that existing services are not tailored to the diverse needs of smokers, smokeless tobacco users, and dual users. Addressing these gaps requires integrating personalized cessation support into the differential service delivery models for HIV care.

At policy level, our findings highlight a disconnect between the legal framework and enforcement practices regarding smokeless tobacco in Uganda. The Tobacco Control Act (2015) explicitly prohibits the importation, manufacture, distribution, and sale of smokeless tobacco products [9]. However, enforcement of this provision remains weak, especially in rural and border regions such as Moroto, where smokeless tobacco remains widely available and culturally embedded. This enforcement gap contributes to public perception that smokeless products are less harmful or legally permissible. Further, the absence of specific taxation mechanisms for smokeless tobacco has contributed to its normalization and high prevalence. Similar trends have been observed in other low-resource settings where weak enforcement of smokeless tobacco regulations has led to widespread use [44]. Strengthening enforcement of tobacco control policies focused on smokeless products is essential for comprehensive tobacco cessation efforts.

We acknowledge four key limitations in this study. First, we interviewed only adults aged 18 years and above who were receiving HIV care at eight treatment sites in Arua and Moroto districts. Second, as a facility-based study, our sample included only individuals already enrolled in HIV care. While the inclusion of both urban and rural facilities enhances the relevance of our findings within these regions, the exclusion of minors and those not yet in care limits generalizability to younger or undiagnosed populations. Third, focus group recruitment may have been subject to selection bias, as individuals more comfortable discussing tobacco use or with stronger views may have been more likely to participate. We minimized this risk through purposive sampling across diverse sites and continued interviews until thematic saturation was achieved. Lastly, although we employed cotinine dipsticks to reduce self-report bias. The limited sensitivity of these tests, particularly for infrequent users of smokeless tobacco, may have led to underestimation of true prevalence.

Despite these limitations, our study has several methodological strengths. It is among the few in low-resource settings to assess tobacco use among PLWH across all three modes: smoking, smokeless, and dual use, using a mixed-methods explanatory sequential design. Biochemical verification enhanced the accuracy of prevalence estimates, while qualitative data added contextual depth to the quantitative findings. The use of standardized tools and a theory-driven analytical framework based on the SEM allowed for a comprehensive understanding of multilevel drivers of tobacco use. These strengths reinforce the value of our findings for informing context-specific, integrated cessation interventions for PLWH in similar settings.

Conclusion

Our findings lead to five main conclusions. First, tobacco use among PLWH is highly prevalent in both Karamoja and West Nile regions of Uganda. Second, modes of use differ by region, with smoking and dual use more common in West Nile region, while smokeless tobacco predominates in Karamoja region. Third, at the intrapersonal and interpersonal levels of the SEM, tobacco use was shaped by male gender, alcohol and other substance use, low tobacco-related knowledge, stress, and strong peer and family influence. Fourth, at the organizational, community, and policy levels of the SEM, limited access to cessation services within HIV clinics, entrenched cultural norms, and weak enforcement of smokeless tobacco regulations sustained use, particularly in Karamoja. Finally, while tobacco control strategies should be mode-specific and context-sensitive, they should also address the multilevel nature of the SEM by strengthening individual knowledge and motivation, fostering supportive peer and family environments, building organizational capacity within HIV care, addressing community norms, and reinforcing policy enforcement to effectively reduce tobacco use among PLWH.
