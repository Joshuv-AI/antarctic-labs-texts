Failed Back Surgery Syndrome Patients Undergoing Flexion Distraction Chiropractic Treatment: A Case Series
Authors: Ralph A. Kruse, Bret A. White, Casey J. Rogers, Jason Klamson, Maruti R. Gudavalli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe purpose of this case series is to describe the outcomes of chiropractic therapy using Cox technic flexion distraction decompression spinal manipulation for patients with cervical and lumbar failed back surgery syndrome (FBSS) who were experiencing pain in the areas where they had previously undergone surgery.MethodsThis case series includes clinical outcome measures from 61 patients with FBSS treated by 21 certified Cox Doctors of Chiropractic from multiple clinic locations. Primary outcomes were pain severity and function, assessed using Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement System-29 v1.0, Neck Disability Index, and Numerical Pain Rating Scale (NPRS) questionnaires. Data were analyzed with SPSS software (version 28, IBM Corp). Demographic data were summarized with descriptive statistics. NPRS for neck pain was analyzed using the Friedman test. Repeated measures ANOVA was used to compare initial visit data with data at 3 and 12 months after the onset of care.ResultsThe cohort received a mean (SD) of 12.9 (8.0) treatments over 56.3 (35.2) days. Pain relief reported by doctors was 67.2% (28.5%) and by patients was 65.9% (29.3%). Neck Disability Index scores had reduced by 6.4 (SD 6.9) and 2.67 (5.1) at 3 and 12 months, respectively. NPRS showed reduced pain for cervical and lumbar patients at 3 and 12 months. Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement System-29 v1.0 scores improved in physical function, anxiety, depression, fatigue, pain interference, average pain, spine pain, leg and arm pain, sleep disturbance, and social roles.ConclusionPatients with FBSS who received specialized chiropractic flexion distraction spinal manipulation in this study experienced relief of pain and disability.

Introduction

Up to 50% of postsurgical spine patients experience ongoing or recurrent pain and disability after undergoing spine surgery.1 Chronic pain following surgery is termed postsurgical continued pain, chronic postsurgical pain, or “Failed back surgery syndrome (FBSS).”2 For this study, FBSS includes individuals who have undergone surgery on either the cervical or lumbar spine. Patients with FBSS report conditions such as chronic neck and upper extremity pain, sacroiliac joint pain, internal disc disruption, zygapophyseal joint pain, recurrence of herniation, adjacent segment disease, pseudoarthrosis, spondylodiscitis, and fibrosis.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Consequently, they might seek alternative treatment options such as conservative chiropractic care.

Several recent studies have investigated the effectiveness of chiropractic therapy for postsurgical spine cases.11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 Studies have shown that patients undergoing spinal manipulative therapy were less likely to require additional spinal reoperations.13 Among the various nonthrust manipulative therapies, flexion distraction was found to be the most commonly used therapy for postsurgical cases.12

Case reports have documented the use of chiropractic manual therapy, specifically Cox technic flexion distraction decompression (CTFDD). Two case reports have shown positive clinical outcomes after treatment with CTFDD: 1 in a patient with a history of an L5-S1 surgical fusion and another in a patient with a prior C6 to C7 surgical fusion and spondylotic myelopathy.17,18 Coulis and Lisi reported on 3 postoperative spine pain patients managed with chiropractic therapy. One of these patients underwent flexion distraction therapy and reported subjective improvements and satisfaction with the treatment outcomes.19

Two retrospective studies have documented the outcomes of flexion distraction therapy on postsurgical lumbar spine pain and radiculopathy. One study by Kruse and Cambron reported on 32 postsurgical lumbar spine pain patients who had undergone flexion distraction. These patients reported experiencing improvements with chiropractic care, with no adverse events noted.20 The other study by Gudavalli et al21 involved 69 postsurgical continued pain patients who were treated with Cox Technic flexion distraction. In this study, 15 Doctors of Chiropractic (DCs) reviewed their records and found that 81% of patients experienced more than 50% pain relief after receiving CTFDD with a mean of 11 visits over a 49-day period. However, Daniels et al conducted a systematic review of the literature on manipulative and manual therapies for managing patients who had prior lumbar surgery. Out of the 51 studies meeting the inclusion criteria, the authors found no conclusive evidence supporting the majority of manual therapies following postlumbar spine surgery.22

Given these findings, it is important to focus on changes in outcomes that are meaningful to patients. An approach to identifying patient-centered outcomes is the minimal clinically important difference (MCID), which helps in determining the smallest change in a treatment outcome that patients recognize as beneficial.23

Our study aims to add evidence for conservative care, such as chiropractic treatment of patients with FBSS. The purpose of our study was to investigate the clinical outcomes of patients with postsurgical continued pain/FBSS who experienced pain in the lumbar, thoracic, or cervical regions following surgery in those regions, who sought care from CTFDD-certified DCs in private practice.

Methods

Ethics

Patients who agreed to participate after being informed about the study signed a written informed consent form. A copy of the consent form was provided to the participant. Permission to conduct the case series was obtained from the Keiser University Institutional Review Board (IRB—IRB000OC18MG72).

This case series is registered under the clinical trial registration identifier NCT05401682 and was first posted on June 2, 2022. Though it is not mandatory to register case series studies on clinicaltrial.gov, this project was registered following participant recruitment to provide transparency and integrity regarding the study design and to share the information with the scientific community.24,25 This case series was conducted in accordance with the PROCESS guidelines.26

Doctors of Chiropractic

A total of 21 DCs were recruited via email and lectures from a pool of those certified in CTFDD and had been trained on study protocols. To receive certification, DCs attended Cox seminars on CTFDD. These postgraduate seminars include didactic and hands-on practice. Certification is earned if the DC passes the written case-based examination and demonstrates proficiency in proper hands-on protocol applications.

Protocols for this study were explained to DCs who agreed to participate. They then signed a written consent form detailing their role and the methods for confidential data collection. All DCs received training through webinars/conference calls, which covered their roles in the study, the inclusion and exclusion criteria, blinding to outcome measures, and the data collection forms to be completed by both the DCs and participants.

Participants

Participant recruitment was conducted at each participating DC’s office over a period of 6 months from the beginning of the study. The participating doctor (or a staff member) described the study and invited all patients seeking chiropractic treatment whom the doctor felt would benefit from CTFDD. All participants had previous surgery on the area of the spine that was causing them pain.

Intervention

Participating doctors performed physical examinations and collected data on the orthopedic, neurologic, and other routine clinical tests as part of the regular care of their patients. No special examination procedures were performed as part of this case series.

Chiropractic Care

The patients received CTFDD chiropractic care following 1 of 2 protocols based on their specific symptoms. During lumbar spine CTFDD, the patient was positioned prone, with the segment being treated positioned between the thoracic and movable caudal piece of the Cox table. For the cervical spine, the segment being treated was positioned above the opening between the thoracic piece and the movable cervical headpiece of the table. Before performing the manipulation, all motions were tolerance tested by initially applying CTFDD starting at low force and gradually increasing to pain-free treatment levels. During the application of CTFDD, the doctor’s hand contacted the spinal segment above the treated level. If there was no spine fusion, each vertebral segment that was distracted and manipulated was tolerance tested and treated. In cases of spinal fusion, the unfused levels of the spine adjacent to the spinal fusion were treated as clinically indicated.

Protocol I

Protocol I was used to treat patients with radicular pain. Protocol I CTFDD consisted of manual flexion or long y-axis distraction of the table’s caudal or cervical headpiece section, delivered to the patient’s tolerance. Protocol I CTFDD involved applying 3 sets of 5 4-second distractions to the vertebral segment being treated.

Protocol II

Protocol II CTFDD was performed on patients without initial radicular symptoms and/or those who experienced at least 50% relief of their radicular pain following the administration of protocol I. Protocol II included the addition of the clinically indicated mobilization of the facet joints into the physiological ranges of motion, including flexion, lateral flexion, extension, and circumduction, as well as rotation in the cervical spine. The choice of protocols was at the discretion of the treating doctor and was based on clinical findings and published algorithms.27

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome variable of pain severity and function was recorded by all participants who completed the self-reported Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement System (PROMIS)-29 v1.0 and Numerical Pain Rating Scale (NPRS) paper questionnaires. The secondary outcome variable of neck pain and disability was recorded by all participants who underwent prior surgery to the cervical spine and had completed the Neck Disability Index (NDI). All measured outcomes were recorded at baseline (initial visit) and 3- and 12-months following the onset of care. Patients completed the outcome forms, sealed them in an envelope, and sent them to the research coordinator to ensure the information remained blinded.

NDI

The NDI assesses how neck pain affects a patient’s daily life and measures the level of disability caused by neck pain on a 0 to 5 scale, with higher scores indicating greater disability. The NDI consists of 10 items, such as pain intensity, personal care, lifting, reading, headaches, concentration, work, driving, sleep, and recreation.

PROMIS-29 v1.0 and NPRS

To assess participants’ perception of the overall change from the initial visit (baseline) to the 3-month assessment and the 12-month follow-up, they completed the PROMIS-29 v1.0 questionnaire. The PROMIS-29 v1.0 is a self-reported measure of the patient’s quality of life. This questionnaire assesses 7 domains and includes 4 questions with an additional pain intensity NPRS.28 The NPRS-1 refers to the least pain in the last week, and the NPRS-2 refers to the most pain in the last week. Aside from the NPRS question with a 0 (No pain)—10 (Worst imaginable pain) rating scale, each question included 5 response options (1-5) with each domain having raw scores ranging from 4 to 20. The scores were converted to T-scores using conversion tables provided by the PROMIS developers, based on population reference values for adults in the US (mean = 50, standard deviation = 10). Higher PROMIS scores on each domain scale indicated a greater level of the measured concept. If a response within a domain was missing for any given respondent, that domain was not scored. The single NPRS item was reported in raw form.28 At the endpoint of care, both patients and doctors completed a written questionnaire to report the perceived percentage of pain relief experienced by the patients.

Statistical Analysis

All data were analyzed with SPSS software (version 28, IBM Corp). Data analysis included descriptive statistics with means and standard deviations. A 0.05 level of significance was applied. The NPRS for neck pain was analyzed using the Friedman test. Repeated measures ANOVA with Bonferroni correction was used to compare the PROMIS T-score data from the initial visit with data collected at 3 and 12 months after the onset of care.

Results

Study Population

This study included 61 patients who had undergone spinal surgery and consented to receive treatment in the regions where they had surgery from 21 CTFDD-certified DCs. The prior surgeries included 14 in the cervical region and 47 in the lumbar region.

The demographic characteristics of the patients (n = 59) included 57.6% females and 42.4% males, with a mean age and standard deviation (SD) of 59.5 (13.7) years, ranging from 35 to 92 years of age. The mean (SD) of height was 174.5 (10.0) cm, or 5.72 (0.33) feet, and the mean weight was 90.9 (21.4) kg, or 200.4 (47.2) pounds. Demographic data were not provided for 2 of the 61 patients.

Data from 14 of the initial 61 patients were missing at the end of the study and, therefore, were not included in the descriptive analysis. Of these 14 patients, one discontinued the study to undergo another surgery, and one was deployed on military duty and could not continue care. The remaining patients did not complete the study due to attrition for various reasons and did not respond to follow-up phone calls from the doctors.

The patients received a mean (SD) of 12.9 (8.0) treatments over 56.3 (35.2) days. The participating doctors reported a percentage relief of pain experienced by the patients of 67.2% (28.5%), and patients reported a relief of pain of 65.9% (29.3%). This data represents all study participants regardless of the location of their spinal surgery.

Cervical and Lumbar Spine Patients’ NPRS Scores

The least pain in the last week (NPRS 1) was analyzed using the Friedman test, a nonparametric test for comparing the 3 visits. The NPRS 1 median values were 3 at the baseline visit, 0.5 following 3 months of care, and 1.0 at 12 months of follow-up. The observed changes between baseline and 3 months of care were statistically significant (P < .001). The values increased from 3 months of care to 12 months of follow-up.

The most pain in the last week (NPRS 2) was analyzed using the Friedman test. The NPRS 2 median values were 7 at the baseline visit, 3.5 at 3 months of care, and 4.0 at 12 months of follow-up. The observed changes between the baseline visit and 3 months of care were statistically significant (P < .001). The values increased from visit 3 months of care to 12 months of follow-up.

Discussion

The findings of this study describe that 61 patients with FBSS reported experiencing pain and disability relief through specialized CTFDD. FBSS remains a challenge that plagues patients and clinicians.29, 30, 31, 32 Although surgical groups have demonstrated somewhat better clinical outcomes after 1 year, the higher complication rates associated with surgery are statistically significant compared to conservative care. Furthermore, there is no evidence to recommend any specific surgical intervention for lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) patients.33 Zaina et al34 observed side effects in 10% to 24% of the participants in the surgical group for LSS, while no side effects were reported for those treated conservatively. Notably, the study did not determine whether surgical or nonsurgical treatments were better for individuals with LSS. Considering these similar outcomes, a patient-centered care approach would be helpful. This approach would involve a thorough discussion with the treating doctor about the viability of surgery, considering the risks of known side effects in surgical recipients versus those receiving conservative care.

A 2016 systematic review found that up to 50% of lumbar spine surgery patients eventually develop FBSS, often leading to further surgical procedures, increased costs, and a decrease in success rates.1 With such high recidivism rates associated with low back pain and surgery, higher risks encountered among surgical groups versus nonsurgical groups, and no statistical difference in overall outcomes, conservative care options must be considered as a viable option for the treatment of FBSS.

Currently, there is a paucity of published information on standardized conservative treatment protocols, especially concerning the efficacy of chiropractic care for patients with FBSS. In support of conservative care options, Rushton et al, using qualitative patient diaries, identified 3 distinct recovery trajectories following lumbar spinal fusion: meaningful recovery (engagement in physical and functional activities to return to functionality), progressive recovery (small but meaningful improvement in physical ability with increasing confidence), and disruptive recovery (limited purpose for meaningful recovery). Adopting a patient-centered approach to identifying various recovery pathways could enhance follow-up care, recovery processes, and overall patient outcomes.35

The MCID serves as a method for categorizing research study data to assess “success” or “failure” based on changes that are clinically meaningful for individuals.23 Parker et al36 found that the MCID threshold for the NDI was 17.3% (23.2% ± 19.7%) following anterior cervical discectomy and fusion. An analysis of 13179 patients who underwent cervical spine surgery for degenerative disc disease concluded that a 30% reduction in NDI scores is a valid method for determining the MCID in this patient population.37 A secondary psychometric study of 107 patients who had neck pain without upper extremity symptoms determined the MCID for the NDI to be 5.5.38 In addition, Vernon found that the NDI has strong psychometric properties, is proven to be highly responsive in clinical trials, is endorsed by a number of clinical guidelines, and is strongly validated as an instrument for assessing self-rated disability in neck pain patients.39 Notably, in our study, the decrease in the NDI scores at 3 months (32%), was in-line with the published MCID threshold of 30%.

The PROMIS Profiles have been shown to effectively provide a concise, multidimensional measure of core domains related to patient-reported health-related quality of life.40 The PROMIS-29 v1.0 is a collection of short forms covering 7 PROMIS domains, including physical function, anxiety, depression, fatigue, sleep disturbance, satisfaction with social role, and pain interference.41,42 Each domain in the PROMIS-29 v1.0 has 4 measures, asking responders to rate the symptom or item using a 1 to 5 scale. The short forms of the domains included in the PROMIS-29 v1.0 have demonstrated strong correlations (0.83-0.90) with the full PROMIS domain item banks.41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46

The MCIDs for the PROMIS-29 v1.0 scales have been evaluated in patients with low back pain, with 5.0 points considered a reasonable threshold for MCID.47 The literature also supports the use of PROMIS to determine MCIDs in cervical spine patients.48 Numerous studies have validated PROMIS as a clinical outcome measure for spine pain patients.39,49, 50, 51, 52, 53, 54 In our study, the following PROMIS T-scores were 5 points or higher, meeting the published MCID threshold.47 For cervical spine patients, anxiety, fatigue, physical function, and social interference met this threshold from baseline to 3 months. From baseline to 12 months, anxiety, depression, fatigue, pain interference, and physical function also met the threshold for MCID. Although the lumbar spine patients reported improvements in PROMIS-29 v1.0 scores, these improvements did not reach the MCID threshold.

The NPRS is valuable for effectively managing and understanding cervical and lumbar pain. A study on MCID values in spinal cord injury patients determined that health professionals and researchers can use 1.6 as the MCID for the NPRS.55 Another study on the psychometric properties of the NPRS established the MCID threshold as 1.5 for patients with neck pain and no upper extremity symptoms, and 2.2 for those with neck pain and upper extremity symptoms.55 In our study, patients with pain in the cervical and lumbar regions reported reduced pain in those regions at 3 and 12 months according to the NPRS-1 and NPRS-2. However, only the NPRS-2 patient-reported values met the MCID threshold for the NPRS.

Limitations

A strength of this multisite observational study, involving 21 DCs, is that it offers greater generalizability compared to a single-site study conducted by fewer DCs. However, the authors acknowledge several limitations of this study, including insufficient documentation of presurgical and postsurgical treatments, lack of a control or comparison group, lack of patient health status and comorbidities, a small sample size, and patient attrition. Additionally, patients may have received ancillary treatment modalities in addition to CTFDD. Furthermore, information regarding patients who may have been screened and did not meet the inclusion criteria was not provided in the data collected.

Future Studies

Implementing electronic data collection may improve patient recruitment, increase the amount of information collected, and reduce attrition in future studies. Further research on patients with FBSS should include larger sample sizes, control groups, longer-term follow-up, and more detailed data on demographics and characteristics of the participants, including time since surgery. Additionally, future clinical outcome studies on chiropractic treatment for patients with FBSS are needed to further validate its benefits for this chronic and often disabling condition.

Conclusion

This multicenter case series of 61 patients with FBSS treated with CTFDD manipulation has shown statistically significant patient-reported pain relief and improvements in physical function. PROMIS-29 v1.0 scores showed reductions in physical function limitations, anxiety, depression, fatigue, pain interference, average pain, spine pain, leg and arm pain, along with improvements in sleep disturbance and social roles. Additionally, NDI scores decreased at both 3 and 12 months of care, although there was an increase from 3 to 12 months.
