The development of residential gut microbiota in mice at different phases in early life
Authors: Yu Meng, Yani Yin, Chunhui Ouyang, Fanggen Lu, Xiaojun Li, Yu Ren, Rong Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGut microbiota develops to an adult-like mode in early life and stays comparatively stable since then. There are 3 stages in early life: delivery, breastfeeding, and post-weaning nutrition period. In our study, we ascertained the forming of adult-like microbiota pattern in early life time by establishing antibiotic-treated mice model and analyzed the residential microorganism of whole gastrointestinal tract using 16S rRNA sequencing. The antibiotic-treated mouse model showed antibiotics could influence gut bacterial development and breastfeeding is the critical period for gastric and ileal microbiota development. In this study, we were the first to investigate the residential bacteria in the whole gastrointestinal tract in early life time. With our approach of exploration of gut microbiota evolution, we hope to provide a better view of how residential microflora develop in early life and influence the future gut microbial system, which help us better understand gut microbiota and related diseases.Subject areas: microbiology, developmental biology

Introduction

Accumulating evidence has revealed that compositional and metabolic alterations of gut microbiota are closely related to various diseases, including obesity, atherosclerosis, type 1 diabetes mellitus, and cardiovascular diseases.1,2,3 A vast number of microbial life thrives in our gastrointestinal (GI) ecosystem and functions intertwined with other parts of our body like a “microbial organ”. However, the causal relationship between gut microbiota and the development of these diseases is still lacking clarity.

In recent decades, researchers have developed various theories and hypotheses about the underlying relationship of gut microbiota and health. Hygiene hypothesis stated that early childhood exposure to microbes could reduce the incidence rate of allergies and inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD).4 It was later updated to the microflora hypothesis which elaborated that perturbations in gut microbiota in early life, such as antibiotics use in early childhood, could lead to an increase in allergic diseases by disrupting immunological tolerance in gut mucosa.5,6 Increasing evidence has pointed out that gut microbiota development in early childhood could result in lifelong influence on our health, especially with the incidence of autoimmune diseases.

After birth, the gut microbial system begins to develop toward an adult-like profile, which is typically established by the end of the first three years of life and stays comparatively stable for the following decades.7,8 There are 3 key periods of GI microbiota development in early life time: delivery, breastfeeding (birth to around 12 months), and post-weaning nutrition when infants begin consuming solid food (12 months to around 3 years old).9,10 During these 3 stages gut microbiota develops from newborn pattern to adult pattern and factors influencing these stages may affect intestinal microbial system development.

Numerous studies showed that different modes of delivery could impact the composition and diversity of microbiome in newborns and furthermore influence on their health. Unlike vaginal birth infants who inherit bacteria from birth canal and fecal contact when struggling through the mother’s vagina, cesarean delivery infants are thought first colonized with microbes from skin and hospital environment, instead of from their mothers.11 This discrepancy in the initial gut microbiota can be attributed to the different sources of seeding bacteria. The gut of vaginally delivered infants is initially dominated by Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Firmicutes. Conversely, in infants born via C-section, Actinobacteria often does not establish a significant presence until 7–15 days postnatally.

Breast milk is not only a dynamic source of nutrients for infant growth but also influences on the establishment of infant’s gut microbiota by providing human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs).12,13 HMOs are the third most abundant component in breast milk and could shape intestinal microbiota composition by promoting and inhibiting specific bacteria in different ways. HMOs serve as a carbohydrate and food source for Bifidobacterium, thus favoring the growth of Bifidobacterium infantis and Bifidobacterium longum, which are the major gut bacteria in breastfeeding infants. HMOs also work as anti-adhesive antimicrobials by preventing pathogen from adhering to intestinal mucosal surface.14

By the end of the 1st year, the next important period of gut microbiota development occurs when weaning and adding solid foods for infants. The introduction of complementary foods into diet leads to an increase in alpha diversity and change of major flora in gut microbiota.15,16 Studies have shown that the relative abundance of Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae is increasing gradually while that of Bifidobacterium is decreasing.17 Research has shown that after 5 days of weaning, increasing relative abundance is observed within Blautia and Bacteroides.18,19

In summary, gut microbial system develops to an adult-like mode in early life time and stays comparatively stable since then. After the seeding bacteria colonizing in gut, the microflora is sculpted and modulated during breastfeeding and post-weaning food period. In our study, we ascertained the forming of adult-like microbiota in early life time by establishing antibiotic-treated mice model and analyzed the residential microorganisms of whole GI tract using 16S rRNA sequencing. We also examined the source of seeding bacteria of infants and gut microbiota changes during different periods of early life as well as their characteristics in different longitudinal space of gut. Through our exploration of gut microbiota evolution, we hope to provide a better view of how residential microflora develop in early life and influence on future gut microbial system, which could help us better understand gut microbiota and related diseases.

Results

Gut microbiota composition at birth

Mice were sacrificed within 2 h after birth and their meconium and mucus from stomach ileum and cecum were collected and analyzed with 16S rRNA sequencing (details of grouping strategy were shown in Figure 1). Figure 2A shows the relative abundances of the distinct bacterial phyla in the gastric mucus, ileal mucus, cecum mucus, and meconium. Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, and Bacteroidetes were dominant bacteria groups in gastric mucus, ileal mucus, cecum mucus, and meconium while Proteobacteria was the overwhelmingly dominant phylum of newly born mice. The relative abundances of Proteobacteria in the gastric mucus, ileal mucus, cecum mucus, and stool were 70.75 ± 8.8%,76.32 ± 4.6%,62.55 ± 3.9%, and 81.24 ± 3.7%, separately. In ileum mucus and meconium, the relative abundance of Proteobacteria was higher than in cecum (p = 0.000,p = 0.001) while there was no significant difference between gastric mucus and cecum mucus. The relative abundance of Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes was near equal, with 15.51 ± 7.8% (gastric mucus), 10.31 ± 2.3% (ileal mucus), 18.12 ± 2.4% (cecum mucus), 8.66 ± 2.1% (stool) for Firmicutes and 11.58 ± 0.7% (gastric mucus), 10.47 ± 1.0% (ileal mucus), 16.82 ± 1.2% (cecum mucus), and 8.03 ± 2.1% (stool) for Bacteroidetes. As for Actinobacteria, Verrucomicrobia, and Deferribacteres, which were less dominant bacteria composition in meconium and GI tract mucus, the distribution of microbiota was also different in different locations. The relative abundance of Actinobacteria was relatively high in ileal mucus (1.92 ± 2.9%), comparing to 0.50 ± 0.2% in gastric mucus, 0.52 ± 0.2% in cecum mucus, and 0.64 ± 0.6% in stool. For Verrucomicrobia the relative abundance was highest in cecum mucus (1.29 ± 0.3%), with 0.56 ± 0.1% in gastric mucus, 0.51 ± 0.2% in stool, and 0.18 ± 0.1% in ileum mucus. The percentage of Deferribacteres was comparatively low in all mucus and meconium samples.

In Figure 3A, the Chao1 richness index of gastric mucus, ileal mucus, cecum mucus, and meconium were 551.52 ± 101.45,574.15 ± 62.41,602.15 ± 58.92, and 523.80 ± 93.35, and there was no significant difference between groups (p > 0.05). Simpson’s index and Shannon index of ileal mucus were both lower than that in cecum mucus (p < 0.05, p < 0.05), suggesting higher diversity in cecum. Simpson’s index and Shannon index of gastric mucus seemed lower than that in cecum but there was no significant difference between them. In summary, the microbiota diversity of newly born mice was lowest in meconium and followed by ileum mucus, with cecum mucus and gastric mucus higher but with no difference. Beta diversity, analyzed by PCoA (principal coordinate analysis) in Figure 3B, showed that the gastric and ileal mucus samples were closest to each other, while the cecum mucus and meconium samples were the most distant.

Gut microbiota development in different periods of early life

Figure 4A showed at week 2 the 3 dominant majority bacteria were still Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, and Bacteroidetes, while the percentage of them changed during the 2 weeks of breastfeeding. As for Proteobacteria, its relative abundance in gastric mucus, ileal mucus, cecum mucus, and stool was 27.69 ± 5.72%, 85.45 ± 1.53%, 1.35 ± 0.21%, and 39.00 ± 13.03%, respectively. Proteobacteria was the absolute dominant bacteria in ileum mucus but it lost its major role in cecum mucus with a decrease from 62.55 ± 3.9% at birth to around 1% at week 2. The relative abundance of Proteobacteria was highest in ileal mucus and lowest in cecum mucus, with significant difference comparing with the other 2 groups. The relative abundance of Bacteroidetes in gastric mucus, ileal mucus, cecum mucus, and stool was 47.36 ± 2.01%, 5.73 ± 0.76%, 44.84 ± 1.31%, 24.26 ± 2.90%, indicating that the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes was lowest in the ileal mucus compared to the other three groups (p < 0.05). The relative abundance of Bacteroidetes in gastric mucus and cecum mucus was higher than in ileum mucus and stool (p < 0.05) but showed no difference between each other. Firmicutes in gastric mucus, ileal mucus, cecum mucus, and stool were 21.06 ± 6.06%, 8.12 ± 0.87%, 36.13 ± 0.88%, 23.75 ± 15.96%, separately.

Discussion

It was long believed that fetus develops in a sterile environment in uterus and the microbial flora start colonizing as soon as they go through birth canal or contact with the environment in the situation of C-section.20,21 Maternal bacteria from vagina and rectum was considered to be the seeding bacteria in infant’s gut until the newly developed gene sequencing detection technology revealed that the difference between maternal vaginal microbiota and meconium microbiota was extremely limited.22,23 Furthermore, bacteria detected in placenta and umbilical cord blood shared considerable similarity with maternal GI microbiota, indicating that bacteria colonization may start from fetus period when baby obtains bacteria from mother’s GI tract through blood.24,25

We found that microbiota in gastric mucus, ileal mucus, cecum mucus, and meconium shared huge similarity in the level of phyla, with the finding that most dominant phyla in those locations were all Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, and Bacteroidetes, which together took more than 95% of microorganism. However, in the level of genus, the relative abundance of bacteria in different locations of GI tract as well as meconium was apparently variant from each other. These suggested that the seeding bacteria which colonized in infants were not only from maternal birth canal. The consistency at the phylum level also does not support the theory that seeding bacteria were obtained from vagina considering mucus and meconium samples were collected within 2 h after birth and it would be less likely that the bacteria could be swallowed by infants and distributed equally in the whole GI tract within 2 h. The analyses of microbial groups in meconium showed there were abundant bacteria in meconium, which indicated that the bacteria in meconium were not only from mother’s vagina since it would be impossible for infants to swallow bacteria together with vaginal effusion during delivery and form meconium within the limited time before they were sacrificed. Alpha diversity showed highest values in mucus of cecum and stomach while had lowest in meconium, supporting that there were more diverse bacteria resident in mucus than in stool. However, if the seeding bacteria were all from maternal birth canal and colonized to infants when they swallowed during the process of delivery, the diversity in meconium should be higher than that of mucus since the bacteria which reside in intestine mucus and go pass GI tract should all be observable in stool. The low diversity in the meconium was against the theory that seeding bacteria was inherited from maternal vagina during delivery and in accordance with the evidence of bacteria existence in placenta and amniotic fluid.26,27 In summary, our data inferred that the seeding bacteria of infant are not obtained solely from the vagina during delivery but could also be inherited from mother in other ways including blood transmission in potential. What’s more, in our study, we investigated on residential microbiota, which should better represent and reflect the gut bacterial environment than bacteria from stool.

After the seeding bacteria arrive at GI tract, they will be selected and foreign bacteria may be introduced in the following breastfeeding and solid food period until the adult-pattern gut microbiota formed around year 3. In breastmilk, there were exogenous bacteria as well as abundant sIgA, which could bind to specific bacteria, preventing those bacteria from being resident in gut mucus and resulting in excretion via stool. IgA was produced in maternal immune responses when mother’s B cell recognize bacteria as pathogen and resident bacteria in mother’s gut mucus will not induce the production of IgA.28,29 In that way, mother’s identification of pathogen pass to newborns via breastmilk and sculpt the resident bacterial community in neonatal gut mucus. HMO in breastmilk could also impact gut microbiota by influencing bacteria residency in gut mucus. Considerable amount of research has reported the difference of breastmilk and formula could result in variations of gut microflora composition. However, most of the previous studies were focused on the passing travelers when detecting bacteria in stool, neglecting mucosal microbiota, which are the real residents in gut and impact our health in long-term. After weaning, infants obtain impressive large number of bacteria from different types of solid food and more extensive environment in their daily life, which makes it more complicated in the development of gut micro flora in early life period.30,31 How do breastmilk and solid food after weaning impact gut microbiota, especially on gut mucus resident bacteria is in need of clarification.

In our research, we explored the influence of breastmilk and solid food on gut bacteria development using mice model, which underwent breastfeeding for 2 weeks and weaning at week 3, following with solid food till week 6. Mice were sacrificed at week 2 and week 6 to investigate the influence of breastmilk and solid food, separately. Variations of intestinal microflora were observed during the period of breastfeeding in our research. The composition and abundance of gut bacteria were largely similar with each other in different parts of the GI tract and meconium at birth. The 3 dominant bacteria were Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, and Bacteroidetes. Proteobacteria took the overwhelming percentage in gastric mucus, ileal mucus, cecum mucus, and meconium. However, after 2 weeks of breastfeeding, the microbiota in gut mucus and stool changed to different degree. In stomach mucus, Proteobacteria dropped and Bacteroidetes increased during breastfeeding, in accordance with the increase of Bacteroides and Parabacteroides and decrease of Acinetobacter, in the level of genus. In ileal mucus, the microflora was changed from 3 major bacteria groups to 4 in phyla and the abundance as well as diversity of microbiota decreased sharply during breastfeeding. In cecum mucus, there was a sharp fall with Proteobacteria from 62.55% to 1.35%. All these changes indicated breastfeeding influence the colonization of gut bacterial groups which resident in the mucus and impact on long-term health. Breastmilk provides newborns with nutrition as well as protections from mothers’ immunological memories. We also noticed the changes in microbiota richness and diversity happened during the 2 weeks of breastfeeding. Microflora richness in different locations kept stable during breastfeeding and diversity presented with alterations toward separate directions, indicating that some original bacteria were replaced by bacteria groups which suit better in mucus, with or without the selection from breastmilk. The diversity of stool was promoted significantly during, which is in accordance with the fact that neonate ingest abundant extrinsic bacteria while breastfeeding and most of the bacteria would be excreted via stool. We also found the diversity and richness of ileal bacterial communities were apparently decreased which might be related with the single-layer structure of the ileal mucus, compared to the double-layer structure in the stomach and cecum. In a word, during the period of breastmilk feeding there was microbiota changes in different location of gut, which may be related with the selection of mucus and breastmilk. The diversity and richness change in stool microflora presented differently with that in gut mucus, suggesting research restricted to fecal samples cannot fully represent the bacteria variations in gut and exploration on the mucus microbiota was requested when studying gut microflora development and evolution in early life period. When investigating the richness and diversity at mice at week 6, we found the diversity sharply decreased in stomach and ileum, with no significant difference of richness. The relative abundance of dominant bacteria rose from less than 30% to more than 80% in stomach in genus level. In ileum, the dominant bacteria rose to more than 90% with the decrease of second and third dominant groups in genus level. These findings suggested with the intake of solid food, Proteobacteria seemed more suitable in gut and became more dominant in gut mucus. Unlike stomach and ileum, the microflora diversity apparently increased in cecum. Most nutrition from food has been digested and absorbed in small intestine and more digestive enzymes from bacteria are needed to digest the content in cecum, which may be related with the increased diversity in cecum microbiota. We are surprised to find that the diversity in stool dropped, considering there should be more bacteria coming with solid food. Plenty research has proved the food-dependent pattern in stool microbiota, with the evidence that high-fat food could promoted the bacteria diversity while high-protein and high-fiber diet could reduce the bacteria diversity in stool.32,33 Our data of microbiota diversity presented decreased in stool and increased trend in cecum, bringing the question that with opposite directions of microflora changes in cecum mucus and stool, which one could be more precise to represent gut microbiota and more closely related to general health? Further research is needed to demonstrate this question.

We have demonstrated how gut microbiota develop and change during the 3 important period when at birth, breastfeeding, and intaking solid food. However, which period is most critical in the development of gut microbiota toward to the adult-like pattern is still unknown. To answer this question, we interfered mice with antibiotics during breastfeeding, solid food period and the whole process (breastfeeding and solid food period) and documented gut microbiota changes with antibiotic interventions at different timing. With antibiotics treatment in breastfeeding, Lactobacillus became dominant bacteria community in stomach and ileum in the level of genus, suggesting that antibiotics intervention in breastfeeding could exert an influence on gut microbiota building till adult stage. An interesting finding in our research showed that in stomach and ileum mucus, mice treated with antibiotics in breastfeeding (AA and AF group) presented with impressive similarity, whereas naive mice and mice treated antibiotics after breastfeeding (WA group) shared likely pattern in relative abundance and diversity of micro biota, bringing the hypothesis that breastfeeding is a key period in the forming of gut microbiota. Antibiotics interfere in breastfeeding period resulted in increased abundance and diversity in stomach and ileum, which may introduce instability in the process of forming adult gut microbiota. Antibiotics interfere after breastfeeding showed very limited influence on microflora in stomach and ileal mucus, which also supported the hypothesis that breastfeeding is the critical period in the development of micro communities’ resident in stomach and ileal mucus. We also found that in cecum mucus, the development of microflora seemed different. Mice interfered with antibiotics after breastfeeding shared more similarity in abundance and diversity with those treated with antibiotics for the whole process, suggesting solid food period may be more critical in the micro bacteria development in cecum mucus. Considering stomach and ileum are where food digestion and absorption happened mostly, it seemed breastfeeding is the most significant in the development of gut microbiota and solid food period is more critical in the development of cecum microbiota.

Limitations of the study

Although this study provides a comprehensive analysis of residential gut microbiota development across the entire GI tract in early life, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the use of mice model, while enabling controlled longitudinal sampling of mucosal sites inaccessible in human infants, may not fully recapitulate the complexity of human gut microbiota development due to inherent physiological and dietary differences between species. Second, our antibiotic intervention model, though effective in perturbing microbiota at specific developmental windows, does not perfectly mimic the nuanced and varied nature of antibiotic exposure in humans (e.g., type, dose, and duration). The observed effects represent a response to a broad-spectrum disruption and may not translate directly to all clinical scenarios. Third, although we identified the breastfeeding period as critical for gastric and ileal microbiota development, and the post-weaning period for the cecum, the exact mechanisms (e.g., specific milk components, dietary nutrients, or host immune factors) driving these region-specific effects remain to be elucidated. Further studies are needed to establish causality.

Experimental model and study participant details

Ethics statement

All animal experiments and procedures were approved by the Animal Care Committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital, Central South University.

Animal models

This study used C57BL/6 mice. Pregnant females (one week before the expected delivery date) were purchased from Hunan Slack Jingda Laboratory Animal Technology Company. All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12-hour light-dark cycle and provided with unlimited access to food and water. A total of 48 mouse pups were used in this study, distributed across six experimental groups (n=8 per group, both males and females were included). Pups from different litters were housed in individually ventilated cages to prevent cross-contamination. The detailed grouping and treatment timeline are illustrated in Figure 1 and described below:

0w group: Pups were sacrificed within 2 hours after birth.

2w group: Pups were raised with normal breastfeeding and sacrificed at 2 weeks of age.

6w (WF) group: Pups were breastfed normally for 2 weeks, weaned onto a normal chow diet, and sacrificed at 6 weeks of age.

WA group: Pups were breastfed normally for 2 weeks. From day 15 to day 21, the nursing dams were treated with a daily gavage of antibiotics (cefazolin 160 mg/kg/day, ampicillin 340 mg/kg/day). After weaning at 3 weeks, the pups themselves received the same antibiotic treatment via gavage from week 4 until sacrifice at 6 weeks of age.

AF group: Nursing dams received antibiotic gavage (cefazolin 160 mg/kg/day, ampicillin 340 mg/kg/day) for the first 2 weeks postpartum. Dams stopped receiving antibiotics but continued nursing until weaning at day 21. Pups were then fed a regular chow diet after weaning and sacrificed at 6 weeks of age.

AA group: Nursing dams received antibiotic gavage (cefazolin 160 mg/kg/day, ampicillin 340 mg/kg/day) for the first 3 weeks until weaning. After weaning, the pups received the same antibiotic treatment via gavage from week 4 until sacrifice at 6 weeks of age.

A schematic diagram of the experimental timeline is provided in Figure S1.

Method details

Sample collection and dissection procedure

Mice were anesthetized with ether and euthanized by cervical dislocation. Dissection was performed immediately on a sterile clean bench. The mouse was placed ventral side up, with limbs fixed using pins. The abdominal skin was disinfected with 70% alcohol. Using sterile scissors, the skin and peritoneum were sequentially incised along the ventral midline to expose the abdominal cavity. The small and large intestines were gently exteriorized, and the mesenteric fat was trimmed away to completely free the gastrointestinal tract from the stomach to the distal colon.

Stomach

The pyloric sphincter was cut with sterile scissors to separate the stomach from the small intestine. The stomach was opened longitudinally, and the gastric contents were gently removed with a sterile cotton swab. The gastric mucosa was gently rinsed with sterile saline to remove any residual contents. A sterile cotton swab was then used to gently scrape the mucosa to collect the gastric mucus sample (G). The swab tip was cut off directly into a sterile cryotube, which was sealed with plastic film and immediately placed in liquid nitrogen.

Small and large intestine

An incision was made at the ileocecal junction to completely remove the small intestine. Following the same principle as for the stomach, ileal mucus (I) and cecal mucus (C) samples were collected by gentle scraping with a sterile cotton swab.

Feces

Fecal samples (S) were collected directly into sterile cryotubes.

All collected samples in cryotubes were immediately placed in liquid nitrogen for temporary storage. After the collection procedure was complete, all samples were transferred to a -80°C ultra-low temperature freezer for storage until further processing.

DNA extraction, quality control, and quantification

Bacterial genomic DNA was extracted from the samples using the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit strictly following the manufacturer's instructions.

For DNA quality control and quantification, 2 μL of the DNA sample was diluted with 98 μL of double-distilled H2O (ddH2O) in a 96-well plate. DNA concentration was measured using an ultra-microspectrophotometer by reading the absorbance at 260 nm (OD260) and 280 nm (OD280). The purity of the extracted DNA was assessed by the OD260/OD280 ratio, with values between 1.6 and 2.0 indicating good purity.

DNA integrity was further verified by 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis:

0.60 g of agarose was mixed with 50 mL of TAE (Tris-Acetate-EDTA) buffer and heated in a microwave for 2-5 minutes until completely dissolved.

The solution was cooled to approximately 65°C, and 3 μL of ethidium bromide was added and mixed gently. The mixture was poured into a clean casting tray, and the comb was inserted carefully.

The gel was allowed to solidify at room temperature, then placed at 4°C for about 30 minutes. The comb was gently removed.

The gel was placed in an electrophoresis tank, and 1× electrophoresis buffer was added until the gel was completely submerged.

DNA samples (1 μL per well) were gently loaded into the wells, ensuring the pipette tip did not puncture the gel.

Electrophoresis was performed at 120 V. The run was stopped when the marker bands had migrated to approximately 1/2 to 2/3 of the gel length.

The gel was visualized and analyzed using a gel imaging system.

All samples that passed quality control (showing good integrity and purity) were diluted with sterile ultrapure water to a working concentration of 10 ng/μL and stored at -80°C until use.

16S rRNA gene amplification

Primer design

The V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was targeted for amplification. Primers were synthesized with specific barcodes for multiplexing. The primer sequences used were: 515F (5'-GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3') and 806R (5'-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3').

PCR reaction

PCR amplification was carried out in a 50 μL reaction volume containing: 2× PCR Buffer, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.4 μM dNTPs, 1.0 μM of each forward and reverse primer, 0.5 U of Taq DNA polymerase, and 10 ng of template DNA.

PCR conditions

The cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 94°C for 1 minute; 30 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 20 seconds, annealing at 55°C for 30 seconds, and extension at 72°C for 30 seconds; followed by a final extension at 72°C for 5 minutes.

Technical replicates and product purification

To account for PCR bias, three separate PCR reactions were performed for each sample, and each reaction was stopped during the linear amplification phase. The three replicate PCR products for each sample were then pooled. The pooled products were analyzed by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel (5 V/cm for 20 min). The target DNA bands were excised and purified using the E.Z.N.A.® Gel Extraction Kit, eluting the final product in TE buffer.

Library preparation and sequencing

Quantification for pooling

Purified PCR products were quantified using a Qubit™ 2.0 Fluorometer or a NanoVue Plus Spectrophotometer, referring to the preliminary results from gel electrophoresis.

Library construction

Based on the quantification results, equimolar amounts of amplicons from each sample were pooled. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the TruSeq DNA PCR-Free Sample Prep Kit according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Sequencing

Paired-end sequencing (2 × 250 bp) was performed on an Illumina HiSeq platform using the Hiseq Rapid SBS Kit v2.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Paired-end reads were merged by using FLASH4 and the sequence quality was ensured by removing low-quality bases and contaminated sequences. Sequences were matched to samples with specific barcodes.

PCR products were tested with Qubit 2.0 (ThermoFisher) or GE NanoVue System (GE Healthcare) and mixed as required. Library was constructed with TruSeq DNA PCR-Free Sample Prep Kit (FC-121-3001/3003) from Illumina Company. Sequencing was performed to paired-end sequencing (2 by 250 bp) with Hiseq Rapid SBS Kit v2 (FC-402-4023 500 Cycle) from Illumina Company. Sequences were analyzed according to the QIIME pipeline.34 Sequences were clustered to operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% identity threshold using UPARSE algorithms with USEARCH (http://drive5.com/uparse/) software. The representative OTU sequences were selected and chimeras were removed with Uchime9.Taxonomy was assigned using the SILVA ribosomal RNA database (https://www.arb-silva.de/) and OTUs labeled with chloroplasts, mitochondria, non-bacterial and archaea kingdoms were removed based on SILVA database.The representative sequences were compared and filtered with PyNAST algorithm12 and then phylogenetic tree was reconstructed using FastTree13. OTU table was rarefied and all samples had the same number of sequences. Alpha diversity was assessed using the Shannon and Chao1 indices, which evaluated the evenness and richness of the community, respectively. The Simpson's index reported throughout this study was calculated as 1 - λ, representing the probability that two randomly selected individuals belong to different species. An increase in this Simpson's index value indicates greater diversity. Beta diversity was assessed using the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, which simultaneously evaluated community membership (presence of taxa) and composition (relative abundance of taxa). Both alpha and beta diversities were assessed at a depth of 16000 sequences per sample. QIIME2, USEARCH and Mothur6 were used for the bioinformatics analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 24.0 and between-group variance was analyzed with One-way ANOVA (normal distribution) or Mann-Whitney test (abnormal distribution). Data were presented as mean ± SEM. T test and variance analysis were applied between groups. P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.
