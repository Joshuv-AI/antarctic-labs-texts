Intensity of nursing work in a primary healthcare center: An observational study
Authors: Melita Peršolja
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesIn the sphere of occupational intensity, nursing stands recognized for its inherently demanding nature, marked by a rapid succession of tasks. Our primary aim was to assess the level of job intensity within nursing by analyzing the frequency and duration of activities performed by nurses, instances of task overlap, and the distribution of break times within a primary healthcare center.MethodsThis study was conducted using a descriptive working method. Nursing activities were recorded with the observation of six nurses, each monitored for 37.5 h. We used the Maribor System for measuring Quantity in Nursing Care in Primary Health Care Settings to measure the nursing care activities about direct patient care, indirect patient care, and other unproductive aspects through direct non-participant observations.ResultsAbout 41.5% (n = 1,640) of all nursing activities involving direct contact with patients, about 38.2% (n = 1,508) was indirect patient care, where a large amount worked with paper files (n = 666, 16.9%). In addition, about 15.0% were other nursing activities (mostly computer work), and just 5.3% (n = 210) of tasks were unproductive. The observational findings indicate a pronounced level of work intensity experienced by nurses in primary health clinics. Significantly, nurses were predominantly engaged in direct patient care tasks, often managing multiple activities simultaneously. Their transitions between tasks occurred approximately every 3 min, frequently without adequate intervals for breaks.ConclusionsThis study highlights the need to shift nurses’ focus from a task-centric approach to one centered on patient care. The prevailing emphasis on tasks may contribute to enduring fatigue and professional dissatisfaction. Consequently, there is an urgent need to redefine the scope of a nurse’s role and implement a comprehensive computer information system as an integral part of this redefined approach.

Introduction

Rising work intensity in recent decades is a widely experienced phenomenon around the world, across both public and private sectors [1]. Overall, job demands fall into two broad categories: quantitative job demands, which refer to the amount and pace of work (sometimes called workload); qualitative job demands, which refer to cognitive/mental and emotional demands and the effort needed at work [2,3]. According to Kubicek et al. [4], the quantitative facet includes a need to work faster, reduce downtime, and perform different work tasks simultaneously. Highly intensive jobs demand focus and attention, adhere to specific rules or habits, and, in the technological process, limit opportunities for creativity and control over work, resulting in workload, working to tight deadlines, and working at speed [5,6].

Previous research mostly estimated nursing intensity in hospitals by calculating nurse-to-patient ratios, nursing hours per patient day, or volume of nursing tasks based on patient complexity classifications [7]. Nursing intensity is described as the amount of nursing care that is needed to give safe care [8]. Nursing is a complex and rigorous profession, with tasks swiftly following one another and overlapping [7,9]. Moreover, the intensity of the job is still increasing, due to the following factors [10]: a) the increased need for nurses due to more doctors being available; b) a growing population of older adults requiring more flexible healthcare services that cover a wide range of needs; c) nurses being given more tasks and responsibilities, broadening their roles; and d) more focused and concentrated patient care being provided in shorter periods. High work intensity has a number of negative consequences for the work of nursing staff, such as poor decision-making and more mistakes and accidents, less empathic—or even hostile—attitudes towards patients and colleagues, limited nurse-patient contact, an increased amount of care left undone, more overtime and greater work pressure, reduced work efficiency, and poorer patient outcomes [11,12].

The appropriate ratio between direct and indirect activities for a nurse in primary healthcare is suggested as 20 (direct) to 80 (indirect) [13]. A direct care intervention is a treatment performed through interaction with the patient, direct social actions, and counseling. An indirect care intervention is a treatment performed away from the patient, but on their behalf, where these actions support the overall effectiveness of direct care interventions [14]. Typical direct patient care nursing activities in primary healthcare include telephone triage, care management, patient education, medication counseling, collaborative work with primary care providers, wound management, sexual health screening and advice, ear syringing, vaccines and immunization, blood tests, smear tests, swabs, recording concerns and historical health information, administering medications ordered by a doctor, monitoring, assessing, and interpreting basic vital signs, and so on [15,16]. Among indirect patient activities, documentation occupies a large part of nurses’ working time [[17], [18], [19]]. The literature also shows that nurses in primary healthcare use their work time ineffectively with a substantial among of time wasted while waiting for patients [20,21]. The data also reveal that nurses in primary healthcare are under-used, as many of their activities could be eliminated, combined or delegated [22].

With the increasing demand for primary health services, it is important to ensure sufficient time is available for nursing care. Moreover, if we want to identify the optimal areas for improvement, it is essential to obtain information on how much time is actually spent on different nursing activities. While there is research available on nursing work intensity in hospitals (mostly addressing stress, workload, or absenteeism among nurses) [17,23,24], a significant gap in understanding this problem in primary healthcare remains. The contribution of this study is the empirical evidence that it presents, which could be used to increase interest in exploring the main activities of primary healthcare nurses, as well as the need for reorganization and optimization of their tasks.

Methods

This study was conducted using a descriptive working method [25].

Study aims

We aimed to ascertain the intensity of work conducted by nurses within a primary healthcare center with regard to: a) the time nurses spend on different activities; b) the time spent on overlapping activities; and c) the time spent on unproductive work.

Study setting

Slovenia is a country with a little over two million inhabitants, with universal compulsory health insurance, and all of its 212 municipalities have primary healthcare centers. Primary healthcare activities are thus conducted at the local level within these community healthcare centers, where essential medical and dental services are provided. Despite efforts to increase the number of staff, the number of physicians in Slovenia remains below the European Union (EU) average. In 2019, there were 3.3 doctors per 1,000 inhabitants, one-fifth being general practitioners in family medicine clinics. However, the number of nursing professionals, including registered nurses (RNs) and nursing technicians (NTs), is 10.3 per 1,000 population, exceeding the EU average of 8.4 per 1,000 inhabitants [26]. In primary care, in particular, task shifting from medical doctors to RNs was introduced in 2019. Further, due to the low density of these professionals, NTs have assumed responsibilities that are formally the competencies of RNs. RNs are nurses who meet the EU criteria for trained and licensed nurses according to Directive 2005/36/EC [27], while NTs have an educational background of four years of secondary school for health technicians, which corresponds to level four of the European Qualifications Framework.

Study participants

The researched primary healthcare center was chosen based on accessibility and long-term cooperation with the research organization. The primary healthcare center covers approximately 60,000 people, and employs around 250 staff. The study participants were the population of nurses in 25 family medicine clinics, which were equipped with 31 full-employed nursing staff (6 RNs and 25 NTs). The head nurse selected eight (32%) clinics for research based on the number of employees (nurses) complaining about the workload and the feasibility of the research process. Before starting this study, we held a meeting where the researcher informed the nursing staff regarding the aims and methods of the study, all nurses working in the proposed clinics at the time of the study were invited to collaborate in the research. We did not have any other admission criteria, as this was the first time that any of these nurses had been included in a study or were observed during work for research purposes. Finally, seven nurses (2 RNs and 7 NTs) from five clinics employing agreed to cooperate in the research.

Instrument

We used the Maribor System for measuring Quantity in Nursing Care in Primary Health Care Settings [28] to assess the nursing care activities performed through direct non-participant observations. The observer explicitly focused on counting the frequency and intensity of specific behaviors. The observer, although present in the environment, remained visible but almost imperceptible and distant. This approach ensured that they did not intervene or disrupt the everyday functioning and activities of the observed population. At the same time, everything being observed could be directly recorded. The observed nursing care activities were divided into four main categories: a) direct patient care, which includes all hands-on care, one-to-one observation or support for patients, and direct communication with patients or their relatives; b) indirect patient care, which includes patient documentation, professional discussions to plan patients’ care, and ordering investigations; c) other nursing activities, which includes other patient-focused activity (completing nursing audits, checking clinical equipment), staff-focused activity (student support, giving and receiving training sessions, personal development reviews), and clinic-focused activity (ensuring environmental safety and cleanliness, ordering or unpacking stock); d) unproductive time, which include personal staff time (staff meals, breaks) and wasted time (waiting for equipment, waiting for colleagues, waiting for patients, etc.). The instrument also collected data concerning the place where the staff member was at the moment of the observation (e.g., in the nursing station, providing ambulatory care, in the archive, waiting room, and rest area).

Maribor System for measuring Quantity in Nursing Care in Primary Health Care Settings was selected for the following reasons: a) it was found to be valid and reliable in previous studies that tested the questionnaire format, assessed content validity through evaluation by a group of independent nursing experts, and examined interrater reliability and internal consistency [28]; b) it detects the number of different nursing activities; and c) Slovenian nurses are familiar with it. Consent to use the instrument was obtained from the author via email.

Data collection

To achieve the aims of this study, all the care activities performed by nurses were recorded by observers. Measurements were carried out over a span of four weeks, with the predetermined nine observation days based on the day of the week. Observations were performed in such a way that all working days of the week were included in a range of one month and randomly selected by the principal investigator of the study. Observations took place at the clinics in September 2022, mainly in the morning shifts between 07:00 and 14:30 or in the afternoon shifts between 13:00 and 20:30. The observations were conducted by measuring the activity and the time required by the nurse for each activity. Activities were recorded every time a nurse changed tasks. When nurses performed multiple tasks at once, observers only registered the first task and marked it as overlapping. We anticipated four general categories of activities, further divided into 20 subcategories.

Data collection was performed on paper forms, using a timer, by ten third-year nursing students who were familiar with the structure and processes of nursing care in the clinics, given that they previously had at least two weeks of clinical practice in the researched unit. Every student was responsible for observing one nurse at a time for five or six observation days. At the time of the observation, the nursing students were dressed in their own clothes rather than uniforms, and they were not involved in nursing care. Student nurses were instructed to interrupt the observation only in the case of critical events (e.g., cardiopulmonary resuscitation), although no such events occurred during the observations.

On a preliminary basis, given that one independent variable regarding the measurement of the nursing care activities was based on direct observation of behaviour, the researcher considered the possible influences of the Hawthorne Effect. According to the literature, awareness of being observed or having behaviour assessed engenders beliefs about researcher expectations, while conformity and social desirability considerations can lead to behavioural changes in line with these expectations [29]. Therefore, out of the nine research days, only five were included in the final analysis, with the intent of increasing the reliability of the study. These days were selected randomly in advance before the data collection phase and reported in a closed envelope that was opened only at the end of the study process. Only the principal investigator was aware of this.

Data analysis

Exploratory data analyses were first performed to inspect the data and identify any inconsistencies. The dataset of one observed nurse was excluded from the analysis due to deficiencies in the data quality. The final analyzed sample, therefore, included six nurses, each observed for five working days, a total of 37.5 h.

IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 software was used for data analysis. Quantitative data was presented using descriptive methods, namely the Mean (M), standard deviation (SD), frequency (n), percentage (%), Minimum (Min.), and Maximum (Max.). Pearson’s correlation (r) and ANOVA (F) were used to compare the data. Correlation strengths were valued as follows: 0–0.09 is not correlated, 0.1–0.3 is weak, 0.31–0.6 is medium, and 0.61–1 is a strong correlation [30]. We also used the Chi Square (χ2) test to assess differences. Multiple linear regression (β) was used to predict the dependent variable. We analyzed five observation days, six nurses, and 7.5 observation hours per day.

The duration of activities in different locations was calculated on the basis of percent per observation hours in one day (450 min). The intensity of work was defined as high based on the following criteria: a) time allocated for each activity; b) the degree of overlap between activities; and c) the volume of unproductive time. The average time for all nursing activities, 3.4 min, was used as the critical threshold for the “time per activity” variable. Given that 30 min of unproductive time per working day is a legal right for workers, the critical threshold for unproductive time was set at a total of 150 min (30 min across five observational days). Work intensity was classified as high if one or more of the following conditions were met: activity duration was less than 3.4 min, there was overlapping of activities, and/or unproductive time totaled less than 150 min. The significance was set at P < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

Institutional review board approval was obtained prior to the start of the study from the Faculty of Health Sciences, University of Primorska (Slovenia), and from the primary healthcare center’s administration. The study was conducted in accordance with the Code of Ethics for Nurses and Nurse Assistants as well as the Declaration of Helsinki: Ethical Principles for Medical Research Involving Human Subjects [31]. Specifically, nurse consent was obtained on-site. The nurses were informed that a “study on nursing care” was being conducted, offered the name of the person responsible and their contact details when necessary, and provided with information regarding what would happen to the data collected.

Duration of activities

The average duration of all activities performed was just over 3 min (n = 3,948) (Table 2), with a minimum of less than one and a maximum of 50 min per activity. The average direct activity time was 3 min (n = 1,640, SD = 3.6, Min. < 1, Max. = 50). The most time-consuming tasks were nursing procedures, which exhibited a time range of one to 50 min, averaging about 5.3 min per procedure. Direct communication with the patient lasted about 2.3 min on average, constituting the third briefest activity in terms of duration. The average time for the indirect activities was 3.6 min (n = 1,508, SD = 3.6, Min. = 1, Max. = 30). Working with files took up to 30 min, but on average, it took about 4 min. The mean time spent on other nursing activities was 3.6 min (n = 590, SD = 3.9, Min. = 1, Max. = 50), and the longest was computer work (up to 50 min). Considering unproductive time, with an average of 3.6 min (n = 210, SD = 3.9, Min. = 1, Max. = 20), small talk took up to 20 min (on average 2.7 min), and nurses spent on average 13.3 min on a general pause, meal.

Location of activities and work shift

In general, nursing staff predominantly performed tasks at the nursing station, accounting for 74.2% (n = 2,927) of all tasks. Within the ambulatory area, nurses undertook approximately 14.9% (n = 589) of the total workload, which was roughly an hour per day (67.5 min). Around half an hour per day was spent in the file archive (n = 260, 6.6%). Nurses accomplished 2.0% of all tasks in the waiting room (n = 79), equal to approximately 9 min of work per day. Work intensity was measured with a constructed variable in which the predictors together explained 78.3% of its variance. The data are highly significant, as indicated by the F-value (F = 4842.92, P < 0.001). An examination of the Beta values indicates that three constants contribute significantly to the prediction of work intensity (β [time per activity] = 0.56; β [overlapping] = 0.49; β [unproductive time] = 0.30). Work intensity exhibited significant variation depending on the day of the week (χ2 = 203.6, df = 4, P < 0.001), peaking on Wednesday and reaching its lowest level on Friday.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to describe the work intensity of nurses in a primary healthcare center. The results indicate that the observed nurses have mostly high intensity work, where activities last on average about 3 min and frequently overlap. The observed nurses also took just short breaks.

The nurses in this study spent more than 40% of their time on direct patient care, which is more than that reported in some previous time-motion studies [32] and higher than the suggested ratio [13]. This imbalance is likely a result of the high-pressure environment prevalent in clinics, driven by the intense number of patients, and thus, the recommended ratio of direct/indirect patient care might not be attainable in this context. The results could also be the consequence of differences in the classifications used in different studies – some time-motion studies include communication in the category of indirect patient care activities [22], while we classified it as a direct activity.

The results are intriguing, revealing notable differences in average time durations. Specifically, the variations deviate from previous study [33], indicating that the nurses in the current work operated at a considerably faster pace. As the length of tasks in this study is short, the data show that the role of the nurse necessitates multitasking, often engaging in the concurrent execution of two tasks (commonly involving a combination of making phone calls, observing, and documenting procedures or patient information). Multitasking is the nature of nurses’ work [34], but these rapid transitions between short activities demand exceptionally high levels of concentration from the individual, making the job highly demanding and stressful. Work intensity also reduces the possibility of meeting employees’ personal needs at work, as their ability to decide what they will do and how they will do it is particularly impaired under such conditions [35].

It is already known that nurses spend about half of their time on documentation [34,36,37], while some studies state that they are most involved in health education and counseling patients and their family members on healthcare matters [38]. In our research, the nurses spent nearly a fifth of their time organizing paper files, a non-demanding activity that could have been done more appropriately by an administrator. Moreover, the use of paper files can also be seen as an outdated practice that should have been abandoned years ago, although it is likely that the problem originates with doctors, who continue to document their work on paper, and so nurses have to find, prepare, and store the documents as part of their regular activities. Overall, this takes too much time, and thus the introduction and use of a computer information system are urgently needed. Indeed, several studies have reported an increase in patient care time due to a decrease in the amount of time needed for administrative tasks and documentation after implementing an electronic documentation system [34]. However, the implementation of such a system should be carried out carefully, as research also shows that dissatisfaction among nurses can increase with the use of a healthcare information system. In particular, poorly functioning electronic health records have been found to induce stress and cognitive workload. Organizations need to prepare and offer comprehensive training before, during, and after any implementation of a healthcare information system [39].

One of the briefest activities identified was direct communication, i.e., nurses engaged in direct communication with patients or their family members, which occurred numerous times during a typical workday. However, despite the high frequency of these interactions, the actual amount of time that a nurse allocated to each of these was notably brief. The goal of the nursing profession should be to help people reach their full health potential, cope with illness and disability, and promote overall health. As noted by Auserhofer [40], activities such as talking to patients and educating patients and family members are often neglected and seem to receive the lowest priorities from nurses. Nurses need time to do their job, and communication is an essential part of all aspects of nursing. In their work, nurses should focus on developing long-term therapeutic relationships, being more patient-centered, efficient, and cost-effective, and building partnerships with both patients and their family members [34]. A 2-min conversation is likely too short to achieve this. Nurses also conducted the majority of their activities at the nursing station, often remaining stationed there rather than visiting the waiting room, where valuable opportunities for patient engagement exist. By being more present in the waiting area, nurses could actively address patients’ questions, provide timely information, and offer education on health matters, thereby enhancing patient satisfaction and care quality. However, it has been observed that nurses also spend a portion of their time at the nursing station engaged in unproductive activities. This downtime, whether due to administrative bottlenecks or task inefficiencies, can create the perception that nurses are not fully occupied with their work. Such misconceptions may lead to unrealistic expectations from management or colleagues, resulting in an overload of tasks beyond their capacity to manage effectively.

The nurses in our research did not take a 30-min break for a meal or even to just temporarily relieve their work-related stress. Even though a half-hour lunch break after four working hours is the right of every employee in Slovenia, the nurses in this study spent a maximum of 20 min taking such a break. Over the long term, this could be reflected in lower satisfaction at work, lower quality of work, and impaired health. The pressure to complete overlapping duties means that nurses often skip essential breaks, including those for meals, further exacerbating physical and mental fatigue. For example, fatigue could accumulate over time and negatively impact job performance, increase the risk of workplace injuries, and lead to musculoskeletal disorders, stress-related illnesses, and general dissatisfaction [41]. Moderate or severe fatigue is common among nurses [42] and could be the consequence of the high intensity of work activities.

To ensure quality primary healthcare, institutions must ensure adequate organization and human resources [43]. Since the healthcare environment is changing and becoming increasingly complex, future research should re-examine current nursing practices. The results of this study show a tendency within the organization to focus more on tasks and work hard, with limited time to think, reflect, focus, and grow professionally with regard to caring for patients. Nurses may be underestimating the importance of lifelong learning and the urgency of maintaining up-to-date knowledge and skills. The limited focus on self-development may be linked to the persistence of non-digitalized practices. To address these issues, healthcare systems need to streamline administrative processes, adopt more efficient digital tools, and implement better workload management strategies that allow nurses the time they need for rest and recovery.

We observed a significant variation in work intensity based on the day of the week. This finding aligns with the results of Surate, Hederman, and Martin [44], who reported that planned visits to primary healthcare clinics peaked on Mondays and declined steadily through Fridays. Conversely, other studies [45] found no significant differences in nurses’ activities across workdays. This discrepancy is noteworthy, as it suggests that while many patients require care over the weekend, they may avoid visiting the emergency department on these days. The significant variation in work intensity across weekdays also has important implications for nursing management, indicating that staffing adjustments could be beneficial on the predicted busiest days.

Limitations

This study has a number of limitations, as follows. First, it was performed in a single primary healthcare center, and only a small number of nurses were involved in the study due to both the decisions of the management and some of the nurses’ reluctance to take part in this research. We also did not collect the demographic characteristics of the nurses who were observed, which would have enhanced the explanatory power of the results. Observations were performed by nursing students, and information biases could also have occurred due to this. We analyzed only five days of observation data out of the nine that were collected in order to increase the reliability of the data, but because of this, we also wasted a lot of data and time. We suggest that future studies add relevant details on nurses and a bigger sample or longer observation time to enhance the explanatory power of the study.

Conclusions

The work intensity among nurses in the focal primary healthcare center is high, and the nurses tend to move between activities approximately every 3 min, often juggling multiple tasks without adequate breaks. There is thus a pressing need to revise the content of a nurse’s role and implement a computer-based information system to reduce the time spent dealing with paper documentation, as the task-centric focus of the current duties may result in long-term fatigue and dissatisfaction. This study highlights the need for job analysis to enhance current practices in nursing management and professional development initiatives and could pave the way for a more organized approach in research related to nursing management.
