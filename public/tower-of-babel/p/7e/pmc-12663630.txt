Characterization of difficult-to-remove host cell proteins in adeno-associated virus downstream processing
Authors: Thomas M. Leibiger, Lie Min, Kelvin H. Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A scalable, serotype-agnostic, and fully chromatographic downstream platform was developed for the purification of adeno-associated virus (AAV) based on affinity chromatography (AC), anion-exchange chromatography (AEX), and multi-modal polishing chromatography (MMC). Quantitative proteomic profiling across each purification stage was performed using sequential window acquisition of all theoretical fragment ion mass spectra (SWATH-MS) to characterize residual host cell protein (HCP) retention for four AAV serotypes (AAV2, -5, -8, and -9) produced with suspension HEK293 cells. The downstream process showed cumulative vector genome (VG) yields ranging from 44.6 to 69.2% with full capsid enrichments ranging from 2.62- to 5.93-fold across all AAV samples. A total of 880, 99, and 21 HCPs were identified in all AAV samples after AC, AEX, and MMC, respectively. Vector-mediated mechanisms of retention are proposed (i.e., capsid or genome association) based on enrichment in protein- and nucleic acid-binding properties of difficult-to-remove HCPs, little clearance across multiple modes of separation, and divergence of HCP profiles for AAV-containing samples vs. null lysate material. The characterization of HCP retention described here advances the understanding of process-related impurity clearance in scalable AAV downstream systems, with implications for both purification process design and AAV interactions with host cell factors.

Introduction

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) is the leading viral vector platform for in vivo gene therapy applications.1 There are currently seven US Food and Drug Administration-approved AAV gene therapies on the market with over 200 completed or ongoing clinical trials evaluating AAV therapies as long-term treatments for genetic diseases.2 Despite the clinical and commercial success of AAV gene therapies, there has been a barrier to widespread patient access due to the low productivity, difficult scalability, and high cost associated with current expression systems.3,4 Approaches to boost manufacturability have been reported in recent years, including the adoption of large-scale suspension bioreactors,5 enhanced transfection reagents for transient systems,6 rigorous design space evaluations for optimization of transfection processes,7,8 and stable packaging and producer cell lines for improved process cost and scalability.9,10 Enhancements in AAV production capability must be met with improvements in downstream processing technologies to purify higher-titer and larger-volume feed streams, while robustly clearing process- and product-related impurities. Traditional purification techniques with poor scalability and throughput such as density gradient ultracentrifugation are increasingly being replaced by fully chromatographic downstream processes that rely on affinity capture and charge-based separation of full and empty (F/E) capsids.11,12 Purification of recombinant AAVs by multiple steps of column chromatography has been demonstrated in different formats with high product recovery, efficient separation of F/E capsids, and substantial advantages in processing time and scalability.11

Despite increasing development and implementation of chromatographic AAV downstream processes, there have been few studies examining the ability of these purification techniques to remove process-related impurities such as residual host cell proteins (HCPs). Residual HCP content is a critical quality attribute (CQA) for the clinical and commercial manufacture of biologics with noted impacts on product stability, aggregate formation, and immunogenicity in patients.13 For AAV products specifically, HCP content has been demonstrated to impact in vivo transduction efficiency and has been linked to adverse side effects in animal models such as increased incidence of chorioretinal atrophy in non-human primates after ocular AAV administration.14,15 Several studies have reported residual HCP profiles after AAV purification by density gradient ultracentrifugation or column chromatography, but none have comprehensively tracked HCP content by individual species and abundance across a full downstream process.16,17,18,19

In this work, sequential stages of column chromatography as a platform for AAV purification is demonstrated with the development of a three-stage process consisting of affinity chromatography (AC) with POROS CaptureSelect AAVX, anion-exchange chromatography (AEX) with Capto Q, and multi-modal polishing chromatography (MMC) with Capto Core 400. The downstream platform was applied for the purification of AAV2, -5, -8, and -9 produced by transient transfection of suspension HEK293 cells, showing high capsid recovery and enrichment of packaged vectors. Sequential window acquisition of all theoretical fragment ion mass spectra (SWATH-MS) data were collected for quantitative proteomic profiling of residual HCPs across each purification stage. Proteomic characterization highlights the ability of AEX and MMC to clear most HCPs after AC. Difficult-to-remove (DTR) HCPs were detected, which persist across the full downstream process for all four AAV serotypes. DTR HCPs are observed to be highly persistent across multiple modes of separation including affinity, electrostatic, hydrophobic, and size-based purification. Characterization of DTR HCPs by relative abundance normalized to capsid protein content, Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment, and comparison to HEK293-null lysate material are performed to discern the potential mechanisms of retention. This work highlights the development of a serotype-agnostic three-stage chromatography platform for scalable AAV downstream processing and identifies DTR HCPs that co-purify with AAV capsids. The process described in this work can be applied for laboratory or large-scale AAV purification to support analytical workflows in research settings or serve as a starting point for clinical process design. Characterization of DTR HCPs represents a step forward in the overall understanding of impurity retention in AAV systems with implications for both downstream process development and AAV capsid interactions with host cell factors.

AAV purification

The column chromatography sequence developed in this work fits into an industrial AAV downstream platform analogous to that of a monoclonal antibody (mAb), which includes cell debris removal, affinity capture, polishing chromatography, ultrafiltration/diafiltration, and sterile filtration (Figure 2). The chromatography stages consisted of AC, AEX, and MMC, which cumulatively exploit affinity binding (AC), electrostatic (AEX and MMC), hydrophobic (MMC), and size (MMC) properties of AAV capsids for purification. As shown in Figures 3A and 3B, VG recoveries ranged from 44.6% (AAV9 R1) to 69.2% (AAV2 R1) with full capsid enrichment ranging from 2.62-fold (AAV5 R2) to 5.93-fold (AAV2 R2) across the three chromatography stages. AEX and MMC both contributed to additional impurity clearance following AC. Affinity chromatography: POROS CaptureSelect AAVX

Capsid recoveries were variable and appeared to be influenced more strongly by AAV serotype than by capsid load amount (Table S1). AAV8 cycles showed high average capsid recovery at 80.7% ± 5.3% despite having the highest average load ratio (7.7 × 1014 capsids/mL resin), while AAV9 gave the lowest average capsid recovery at 59.7% ± 7.3% with the lowest average load ratio (1.7×1014 capsids/mL resin). Anion-exchange chromatography: Capto Q

Capto Q salt gradient screening showed a wide divergence in sodium acetate concentration needed for F/E capsid binding and elution (Figure 2). AAV5 had the strongest retention with a 30% step of 250 mM sodium acetate buffer at pH 9.0 required for empty capsid elution. AAV9 binding was highly sensitive to salt concentration, with empty capsid elution occurring between 3–4% of 250 mM sodium acetate and full capsid elution occurring at <10% of 250 mM sodium acetate. Step gradients were set based on the screening runs, which achieved F/E capsid separation as indicated by a shift in relative integrated peak areas of UV absorbance at 260 and 280 nm (Figures 4B and S3). Additional analyses on fractionated F/E capsid peaks for each cycle were performed, including real-time PCR (qPCR) and AAVX-Octet, which further supported the separation of F/E capsids (Figure S4). Across all AEX processing runs (N = 28), a median of 92.2% of VGs were recovered, while removing a median of 73.2% of total loaded capsids (Table S2). AEX process performance varied by serotype, likely due to differences in the full capsid percentages of the load material and serotype-specific capsid properties that influence electrostatic effects at varying ionic strength. Average full capsid enrichments across AEX based on AAVX-Octet and qPCR were 5.3-fold for AAV2, 2.7-fold AAV5, 3.7-fold for AAV8, and 3.3-fold for AAV9.

Multi-modal polishing chromatography: Capto Core 400

Capto Core 400 resin contains an octylamine ligand-activated core that is both hydrophobic and positively charged. The core is shielded by a size exclusion outer shell with approximate pore radii of 19 nm.20 Salt concentration in the load and running buffer is crucial for balancing electrostatic and hydrophobic effects driving the separation, which impact both impurity clearance and process yield. Salt buffer screening performed with EGFP lysate and AAV2 capsids indicated that the optimal NaCl concentration of load material and running buffer is within 150–300 mM. Operation of Capto Core 400 within this salt concentration range at 1 min residence time achieved 4–5-fold reduction in HCP content with approximately 90% AAV2 capsid recovery (Figure S5). AAV2 capsid recovery was strongly dependent on operating salt concentration, with the highest recovery in the load flowthrough occurring for operation at intermediate NaCl concentration (150–300 mM). Average capsid yields in the flowthrough for Capto Core 400 processing runs were 90.4% for AAV2 (N = 2), 83.6% for AAV5 (N = 2), 90.1% for AAV8 (N = 2), and 87.9% for AAV9 (N = 2) measured by AAVX-Octet. Representative chromatograms for MMC are shown in Figure 4C, and complete datasets for all processing cycles are listed in Table S3.

Residual host cell protein content across purification stages

Residual HCPs were examined based on total identifications, identifications for each AAV serotype group (N = 2), and universal identifications in AAV samples (NAAV = 8) (Figure 5A). A breakdown of HCP identifications within each AAV serotype group showing overlapping protein hits is detailed in Figure S6. As seen previously,18 a broad range of cellular impurities were identified in POROS CaptureSelect AAVX AC eluates. Abundance profiles for the affinity-purified samples were dominated by several highly prevalent impurities, which were conserved across serotypes, including heat shock 70-kDa protein, hsc-70-interacting protein, and heat shock protein HSP-90-beta, which was consistent with previously observed trends.18 Both AEX and MMC provided substantial HCP clearance on a species basis (i.e., number of uniquely identified HCPs) and a normalized HCP content basis (i.e., ng HCP/μg capsid protein) (Figures 5A and 5B). Uniquely identified HCPs across the sample set (NAAV = 8) decreased 76.8% across AEX and an additional 79.4% across multi-modal polishing to give a total reduction of 95.2% for the two-column stages after AC. Normalized HCP content (ng HCP/μg capsid protein) across the sample set (NAAV = 8) declined by an average of 87.8% across AEX and an additional 75.9% across MMC for a total reduction of 97.1% for the two-column stages after AC. Difficult-to-remove residual host cell proteins

A total of 146 DTR HCPs were identified (DTR-146), defined by their detection in both biological replicates of at least one AAV serotype group after the three-stage purification. Twenty-one HCPs were detected in all AAV-containing samples (NAAV = 8) following the three-stage purification (DTR-∩-21), which are listed in Table 1. DTR HCPs did not have consistently high abundances following AC despite appearing across multiple samples in the final purified AAV products (Figure 5C). Several DTR-∩-21 HCPs were detected in high amounts across all AAV samples, including serine/threonine-protein kinase Nek9 (NEK9), small ubiquitin-related modifier-2 (SUMO2), peptidyl-prolyl cis-trans isomerase A (PPAI1), small ubiquitin-related modifier 1 (SUMO1), and transgelin-2 (TAGLN2). Tracking the abundance of these HCPs relative to AAV capsid protein content across the downstream process revealed that their levels remain relatively unchanged despite total HCP content decreasing approximately 2-logs across AEX and MMC stages (Figures 6 and 7). SUMO2, SUMO1, and NEK9 relative abundances (ng HCP/μg capsid protein) increased in some cases, particularly across the AEX stage, indicating preferential association with packaged vectors. Serotype-specific DTR HCPs were also observed after the three-stage purification process, which appeared in both replicates of a given AAV serotype but were absent or detected at much lower levels Parallel processing of EGFP control material that contained no AAV capsid proteins provided a background reference for vector-independent HCP retention. The proportion of residual HCPs detected in AAV-containing samples that were not detected in the EGFP control samples increased across the purification stages. HCPs identified in AAV-containing samples (both replicates of at least one serotype) that were not identified in EGFP control samples increased from 20.3% (649 of 3,199) to 47.7% (271 of 568) to 69.2% (101 of 146) after AC, AEX, and MMC, respectively (Figure S9). Small amounts of capsid proteins were detected in several EGFP control samples, which are attributable to minor carryover from the column hardware, tubing, or eluate fraction collector combined with sample concentration prior to trypsin digestion. Chromatograms for EGFP control processing show minute levels of retained material compared to AAV-containing samples (Figure S10).

Functional and physical properties of difficult-to-remove host cell proteins

GO functional enrichment analyses performed for HCPs detected after AC and AEX showed a statistically significant enrichment for proteins that have protein-binding and nucleic acid-binding molecular functions (Figures 7A–7E). The DTR-∩-21 group of HCPs did not provide a sufficient sample size relative to the background to perform GO enrichment. The proportion of HCPs retained in all AAV-containing samples (NAAV = 8) with protein-binding properties increased throughout the downstream process from 59.1% to 80.4% to 100% after AC, AEX, and MMC, respectively (Figure 7F). Similarly, the proportion of HCPs possessing nucleic acid-binding properties increased from 52.4% to 59.8% to 66.7% after AC, AEX, and MMC, respectively (Figure 7F).

There was a statistically significant shift in median molecular weight (MW) when comparing HCPs identified after AC to HCPs measured after AEX (Kruskal-Wallis test, p < 0.001) (Figure S11). No difference was found in median HCP MW between AEX and MMC eluates. Median HCP MWs were 44.1, 24.4, and 23.4 kDa following AC, AEX, and MMC, respectively. A shift in median HCP pI was observed when comparing AC and AEX eluates (Kruskal-Wallis test, p < 0.001) with no difference in median pI measured between AEX and MMC eluates (Figure S11). Median HCP pIs were 6.3, 7.7, and 6.2 following AC, AEX, and MMC, respectively.

Discussion

Density gradient ultracentrifugation has been widely applied for purification of AAV vectors,22 but this technique is not suitable for large-scale processing due to the excessive number of tubes, cycles, and manual manipulation required. Although large-scale purification has been demonstrated using short-term ultracentrifugation with a zonal rotor,23 multiple five-hour cycles may be required with rotor volume limits less than 2 L.23 Sequential stages of column chromatography has substantial advantages for AAV purification process scalability,11 offers operational benefits in manufacturing due to short-duration cycles, and provides an opportunity for reduced material requirements for process development with high-throughput workflows.24 Qu et al. extensively explore chromatographic methods for scalable AAV purification, and other researchers have reported the successful development of multi-stage chromatography systems that exploit different physiochemical vector properties for purification.11,25,26 These studies focus primarily on AAV product recovery and full capsid enrichment without consideration of process-related impurity clearance. Several studies dedicated to examining HCP retention and clearance in AAV chromatography processes have been reported,16,17,18,27,28 but they are limited to the analysis of a single purification stage or focus on characterizing commercially acquired material with little information on production and purification processes. Additional research has been dedicated to developing mass spectrometry tools capable of overcoming dynamic range limitations for HCP analyses with AAV samples21,29,30,31; however, these methods have not been rigorously applied for characterization of commercially relevant systems. Significant knowledge gaps remain for linking AAV downstream process design to the CQA of HCP content. In this work, a fully chromatographic, scalable, and serotype-agnostic downstream AAV platform was developed and used as a model for characterizing cellular impurity retention across vector purification to identify DTR HCPs and examine physical and functional properties that may be driving their persistence.

AAV chromatography platform considerations and throughput

POROS CaptureSelect AAVX provides a convenient platform fit for serotype-agnostic purification, but the AAVX ligand may allow for non-specific binding and elution of impurities.18 The observation of serotype-specific yield variability across AC suggests differential dynamic binding capacities at the process operating conditions for different AAV serotypes. Additional immunoaffinity resins can be considered including AVB Sepharose (Cytiva), Capto AVB (Cytiva), POROS CaptureSelect AAV8 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), or POROS CaptureSelect AAV9 (Thermo Fisher Scientific).32 Peptide affinity ligand resins have been described for serotype-agnostic AAV purification, which offer advantages in resin sanitization with base stability and allow for capsid elution at more moderate pH conditions.33

AEX operation for AAV processing can be challenging due to the narrow physicochemical properties shared between empty and full capsids and the need to operate at low ionic strength to promote capsid binding. Solution conditions with ionic strength >200 mM have been shown to prevent AAV aggregate formation, which can also be impacted by additives, AAV serotype, or capsid concentration.34,35 Salt gradient evaluations allowed for adjustment of load materials to the highest ionic strength that still promoted capsid binding for reduction in aggregate formation. Operation of AEX for AAV9 was performed such that empty capsids did not appreciably bind to the resin, while full capsids were retained, which allowed for slightly higher ionic strength of the load material (Figure S12). This strategy can be applied to other serotypes to allow for higher loading salt concentrations and increased load capacity of the column by allocating binding spots to only fully packaged capsids. The Capto Q separation described in this work is intended to serve as a model AEX chromatography step for impurity characterization and is not rigorously optimized for full capsid enrichment, as indicated by greater UV absorbances at 280 nm compared to 260 nm for some full capsid elution peaks (Figure 4). The AEX step can be further optimized for operational advantages or more desirable product quality through additional salt gradient evaluations,36 screening process parameters such as residence time and load ratio, use of flowthrough processes instead of bind and elute,37 or continuous recycling for higher-resolution separation of F/E capsids.38

Capto Core 400 resin has pore radii of 19 nm, slightly larger than most AAV capsids, which have been measured to have hydrodynamic radii ranging from 9–14 nm.20,39 However, the relatively thick resin shell (∼3.6 μm) hinders the diffusion of large species such as capsids, while smaller DNA and HCP impurities diffuse more easily into the ligand-activated core and are adsorbed. The recovery of AAV capsids in the load flowthrough was dependent on operating salt concentration where the range of 150–300 mM NaCl was optimal for capsid recovery, likely driven by the balancing of weak electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions between capsids and the outer shell of the resin (Figure S5). The recovery of AAV capsids was also strongly dependent on operating flow rate, with shorter residence times corresponding to greater capsid recovery due to reduced diffusion (data not shown). In this work, the Capto Q AEX step was run before the Capto Core 400 step to avoid significant conductivity adjustment of the load material. It is not expected that the order of the polishing stages would substantially impact HCP clearance given the independent charge-, hydrophobicity-, and size-based mechanisms of impurity separation.

AAV dosing varies widely by specific treatment and whether systemic or targeted administration is used.40 Targeted administration of AAV therapies rarely exceeds 1015 VGs, and greater than half of systemic administration treatments are dosed at 1015 VGs or less (compiled clinical trial data, assuming 70-kg patient).40 The purification workflow described in this work processed >1015 capsids for each AAV serotype, with 8 mL of resin for each chromatography stage. Assuming 10% packaging of capsids and a 50% cumulative downstream processing yield, more than 50 doses can be purified at 10-L column scale and more than 500 doses can be purified at 100-L column scale. Batch processing time across the three stages was approximately 6 h and is assumed to be scale independent.

Retention mechanisms of difficult-to-remove HCPs

Characterization of HCP retention in mAb downstream bioprocessing has elucidated several dominant retention mechanisms including product association,41 resin interactions, high-MW heteroaggregate formation,42,43,44 and network-mediated retention.45 Quantitative profiling of AAV-containing and EGFP control samples across vector purification in this work supports two HCP retention mechanisms—non-specific resin interactions and product association.

In previous work, we showed that a wide range of cellular impurities are retained across POROS CaptureSelect AAVX AC, which predominantly appeared in both AAV-containing and null HEK293 lysate control samples.18 We observed similar trends here despite applying more stringent washing conditions with extended duration and higher salt concentration. There were 3,199 unique HCPs detected in both replicates of at least one AAV-containing sample after AC. Approximately 80% of these HCPs (2,550 out of 3,199) were detected in EGFP control material after AC, indicating that their carry-through is primarily vector independent. The relatively lower number of unique HCP identifications with AAV2 samples may be related to the lysate-only harvest process, compared to AAV5, -8, and -9, which were purified from pooled lysates and supernatants (Figure 5A). Additional serotype-specific variability in impurity profiles after AC may be driven by differences in upstream process performance such as peak VCD, which could influence the relative proportion of HCPs entering the purification process. For instance, AAV5 was observed to have the highest peak VCD and the highest number of unique HCPs detected after AC (Figure S13). The observation of visible elution peaks for EGFP control material despite the modified washing procedure suggests that non-specific binding and elution of HCPs to the AAVX ligand is occurring (Figure S10), a mechanism further supported by the binding of AAVX-Octet biosensors to background impurities.46 Robust clearance of these impurities in subsequent AEX and MMC downstream steps suggests that the majority of HCPs carried through AC do not strongly interact with capsids, highlighting the importance of applying multiple modes of separation in AAV purification. HCPs retained by resin interactions are more prominent in AC eluates as indicated by the decreasing overlap in HCP identifications for AAV-containing and EGFP control material throughout the downstream process (Figure S9). While not evaluated in this work, the non-specific binding of HCPs with the AAVX ligand may be dependent on load ratio. Operating the AC step close to or exceeding the maximum binding capacity may reduce retention of HCPs through competitive binding displacement. This potential mechanism assumes that capsids have higher binding affinities for the AAVX ligand compared to HCPs, and that capsids and HCPs can equally access the resin’s ligand with minimal impacts of their differential sizes.

The complexity of AAV products allows for a wider diversity of interactions with cellular proteins that can include protein-protein interactions with the capsid surface, incorporation in the viral shell, packaging inside the vector, or association with the ssDNA genome or other packaged residual DNA.47 Results obtained here indicate that product interactions may be driving the persistence of DTR HCPs. The significant enrichment in HCPs displaying protein- and nucleic acid-binding molecular functions highlights the tendency of these species to co-purify with AAV products, which could occur through interactions with the capsid (i.e., protein-protein) or packaged genome (i.e., protein-nucleic acid) (Figure 7). 100% of HCPs detected in all AAV-containing samples after the three-stage purification possess protein-binding properties, and 66.7% of these HCPs possess nucleic acid-binding properties (Figure 7). Comparatively, 52.4% and 34.8% of HCPs detected in a crude sample prior to purification show protein- and nucleic acid-binding properties, respectively. The poor clearance of DTR HCPs compared to overall HCP content (Figures 6 and S7) highlights a strong mechanistic basis for retention, which is likely vector mediated based on divergence of HCP profiles for EGFP control samples compared to AAV-containing samples (Figure S9) and the tracking of individual DTR HCP abundances with capsid protein levels (Figure S14). Interestingly, a notable proportion of HCPs detected after MMC for each respective AAV sample was measured below the 50th percentile abundance threshold in AC eluates, demonstrating that DTR HCP profiles are more strongly influenced by selective retention as opposed to input concentration further upstream (Figure 5C). Only 47 of the 146 DTR HCPs identified in this work were also measured in EGFP control material following the same purification scheme, suggesting that most cellular impurities are selectively retained with capsids. Overall, we propose that protein-protein interactions (i.e., HCP-capsid interactions), which have been extensively demonstrated in mAb purification, and protein-nucleic acid interactions (i.e., HCP-genome interactions), which have been demonstrated in AAV purification by Satkunanathan et al. on a limited basis,17,27 both contribute to the persistence of the DTR HCPs identified in this work.

Problematic DTR HCPs

HCPs that pose notable risks to product quality or safety have been described in other bioprocessing contexts.13 These high-risk impurities have been shown to pose immunogenicity risks to patients and have significant impacts on CQAs including aggregation,13 product degradation through enzymatic activity,48 or degradation of excipients impacting formulated product stability.49,50 In this work, we identified 7 residual HCPs that are both DTR in at least one AAV serotype and have been previously described as problematic due to their notable impacts to product safety or quality. These species include alpha enolase (drug quality, modification), cathepsin D (drug quality, fragmentation), heat shock 70-kDa protein 1A (drug quality, aggregation), heat shock cognate 71-kDa protein (drug quality, aggregation), peptidyl-prolyl cis-trans isomerase (drug quality, aggregation), pyruvate kinase (immunogenicity), and peroxiredoxin-1 (immunogenicity) (Table S4; Figure S15).13

Serine/threonine protein kinase Nek9 protein

NEK9 was measured in all AAV-containing samples and had the highest median abundance after the full three-stage purification process (Table 1). NEK9 is a member of the NIMA-related kinase family and plays key roles in the regulation of mitosis and interphase progression.51,52,53 Interestingly, NEK9 has been previously described for its role in adenovirus replication where it localizes with viral replication centers and influences gene regulation through suppression of transcription via viral and cellular promoter association.54 NEK9 has been shown to play multiple roles in viral growth and replication through direct interactions with E1A and through transcriptional control by blocking activation of a p53-inducible gene (GADD45A) involved in stress response and cell-cycle arrest.54,55 We observed that NEK9 protein is present for all AAV samples after MMC, and the relative abundance of NEK9 normalized to capsid protein content remains relatively unchanged across a rigorous purification sequence encompassing affinity-, ionic-, hydrophobic-, and size-based separation (Figure 6). The detection and notable persistence of NEK9 suggests a serotype-independent interaction with the AAV capsid or VG, a phenomenon previously undescribed for AAV capsid assembly, genome replication, or viral packaging processes. NEK9 association with AAV capsids may be driven by localization during viral replication, interactions with viral helper elements, or interactions with VGs before or during virus packaging. It is possible that NEK9 plays a role in AAV production and replication processes similar to adenovirus, which could be exploited in a production system for tuning of viral gene expression to improve VG titer or packaging efficiency.

Small ubiquitin-related modifier proteins

Small ubiquitin-related modifier (SUMO) proteins (SUMO1 and SUMO2) were detected as highly abundant DTR HCPs across all biological replicates and serotype conditions. The relative abundance of SUMO2 normalized to capsid protein content showed a notable increase across AEX purification, indicating a selective or preferential co-purification of this HCP with fully packaged vectors (Figure 6). SUMO1 and SUMO2 HCP amounts (ng) measured in final samples were also observed to track with capsid protein amounts (μg) (Figure S14). The SUMO pathway has been previously explored in various virology contexts for AAV and other viruses. Mary et al. detected SUMOylation by SUMO1 and SUMO2 at various capsid sites through a comprehensive assessment of AAV post-translational modifications by mass spectrometry. Further, Chen et al. reported that members of the SUMO pathway restrict AAV transduction and examined SUMOylation mechanisms for AAV capsids, showing that SUMOylation of VP2 occurs during vector production and contributes to enhanced capsid stability.56 The observation in this work that full AAV capsids are associated with higher relative proportions of SUMO proteins indicates that these cellular proteins and their modifications may modulate vector packaging processes in the host cell (Figure 6). SUMOylation has been described as a critical post-translation modification for successful replication of other viruses, including herpes virus, human immunodeficiency virus-1, and coronaviruses, among others.57 SUMOylation of AAV capsids may influence subcellular localization, nuclear transport, capsid stability, or interactions between viral and host replication factors, which impact packaging efficiency during production; however, additional studies are needed to examine these potential mechanisms.

Conclusions

In this work, a scalable, serotype agnostic, three-stage AAV purification platform was developed based on AC, AEX, and MMC. The downstream platform was applied across AAV2, -5, -8, and -9 to characterize residual HCP profiles across each stage of purification. Groups of DTR HCPs were identified for each serotype with a subset of impurities appearing universally across all samples (DTR-∩-21). Vector-mediated HCP retention is proposed based on GO functional enrichment analyses, quantitative proteomic profiling across purification stages, and comparisons to EGFP control material void of AAV capsids. The downstream platform can be readily applied in small-scale purification workflows or serve as a starting point for large-scale vector purification process development. HCP profiling across the full downstream process represents a notable step forward in the understanding of AAV process-related impurity retention and clearance, broadening the understanding of scalable AAV purification process performance and offering new insights into AAV interactions with cellular factors. The identification of DTR HCPs and those that carry particularly high risks can guide downstream process design through targeted monitoring or removal strategies, ultimately contributing to the improved delivery of safe and efficacious therapies to patients.

Materials and methods

AAV production

AAV2, -5, -8, and -9 lots were produced by transient three-plasmid transfection of suspension HEK293 cells as described previously.18 Expi293 cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used along with Expi293 Expression Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Detailed transfection parameters are summarized in Table S5, and plasmid information is listed in Table S6. Eight individual 1-L shake flasks were transfected per serotype. Cells were cultured for 72-h post-transfection in a Multitron HT (Infors, 25-mm throw) incubator at 135 rpm, 37°C, 80% relative humidity, and 5% CO2. Samples (0.5 mL) were taken at 24-, 48-, and 72-h post-transfection and were analyzed with a Vi-CELL XR (Beckman Coulter) instrument for measurement of cell viability and VCD. At 72-h post-transfection cultures were harvested by centrifugation at 1,000 relative centrifugal force (RCF) for 10 min using a 5920 R benchtop centrifuge (Eppendorf). Supernatants were decanted from each culture for separate measurement of VG titer. Cell pellets for each 1-L shake flask were resuspended in 25 mL mammalian lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, pH 8.5) and lysed with three consecutive freeze/thaw cycles. Cell debris was removed from lysates by centrifugation at 3,428 RCF for 30 min. An additional 8 × 1-L shake flasks were transfected with only pEGFP plasmid to produce “EGFP control” material lots, which contained no AAV-production genes. The plasmid delivery for these flasks (μg pEGFP) matched that for AAV5, -8, and -9 conditions. The transfection, culture, harvest, and post-harvest processing for these EGFP control conditions matched that of the AAV production cultures. All steps, reagents, and equipment for quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) were performed as previously described,18 including Benzonase Nuclease (Sigma Aldrich), DNase I (New England Biolabs), and Proteinase K (Thermo Fisher Scientific) treatment. After qPCR, lysate and supernatant harvest materials from shake flasks 1, 3, 5, and 7, and shake flasks 2, 4, 6, and 8 were pooled together, respectively, for AAV5, -8, -9, and EGFP control conditions. Only lysates were pooled together for AAV2 flasks due to low supernatant VG titer. In total, 10 material lots were created (N = 2 for AAV2, -5, -8, -9, and EGFP) each composed of 4 × 1-L shake flask harvests.

AAV purification

All purification runs were performed using an ÄKTA AVANT fast protein liquid chromatography system. Unless otherwise specified, all cycles of POROS CaptureSelect AAVX, Capto Q, and Capto Core 400 were performed using 8 mL of fresh resin packed into a TRICORN 10/100 column following the manufacturer’s recommended packing guidelines for each resin. 1-mL aliquots of load and elution (AC, AEX empty, AEX full, MMC flowthrough) material were taken for each chromatography cycle for measurement of VG and capsid content.

Affinity chromatography: POROS CaptureSelect AAVX

Harvest materials (lysate and supernatant pools for AAV5, -8, -9, and EGFP, and lysate pools for AAV2) were 0.22 μm vacuum filtered (ThermoFisher Scientific) immediately prior to load. One cycle was performed for each replicate of AAV2, -5, -8, and EGFP material. Four cycles per replicate were performed for AAV9 material due to lower observed binding to the AAVX ligand.46 The following method was used for each run: equilibration with 10 ColV of AAVX-equilibration buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 0.1 M NaCl, pH 7.2), loading, washing with 20 ColV of AAVX-wash buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 0.25 M NaCl, pH 7.2), elution with 5 ColV AAVX-elution buffer (0.1 M glycine-HCl, pH 2.8), column stripping with 5 ColV AAVX-strip solution (200 mM acetic acid), and column re-equilibration with 5 ColV AAVX-equilibration buffer. All product contact steps were performed at 5 mL/min flow rate (1.6 min residence time). Eluates were collected in 50-mL tubes placed in the fraction collector at 6°C containing 10 v/v% AAVX-neutralization buffer (1 M Tris-HCl, pH 8.7). Neutralized elution pools were stored at -80°C immediately after sampling.

Anion-exchange chromatography: Capto Q salt gradient screening

Salt gradient screening for optimal F/E capsid separation was performed for each AAV serotype using 20 mM Bis-Tris propane, 2 mM MgCl2 (Q-buffer A) and 20 mM Bis-Tris propane, 2 mM MgCl2, and 250 mM sodium acetate (Q-buffer B) for separation. 1 mL of affinity elution material for AAV2, -5, and -8 was diluted with 40 mL Q-buffer A. 1 mL of AAVX elution material for AAV9 was diluted with 20 mL Q-buffer A + 20 mL deionized (DI) water since it was observed that AAV9 required lower conductivity for binding to the Capto Q resin compared to the other serotypes. The following salt gradient screening was performed: column equilibration with 5 ColV Q-buffer A, loading, then sequential 5% (AAV2, -5, -8) or 2% (AAV9) isocratic gradient steps of Q-buffer B for 3 ColV per step. UV absorbance data collection was set to 260 and 280 nm wavelengths. Relative peak integration of absorbance at UV 260 and 280 nm was used to determine the optimal single isocratic gradient step conditions for F/E capsid separation of each serotype (Figure S2).

Anion-exchange chromatography: Capto Q processing

F/E separation of AAV capsids was performed using Capto Q AEX with a single isocratic step gradient. Replicate groups were split across multiple cycles to prevent column overloading—three cycles were performed for each biological replicate (N = 2) of AAV2, -5, and -8 and four cycles were performed for each biological replicate (N = 2) for AAV9. Each EGFP control condition (N = 2) was run in a single cycle. In total, 38 cycles of Capto Q were performed. Load material was diluted in 190 mL Q-buffer A prior to each cycle and then adjusted to target loading conductivity by addition of 10X-PBS. Poloxamer-188 (Sigma Aldrich) was added to load materials to a concentration of 0.05% to reduce aggregate formation at low ionic strength. AEX separation for AAV9 was operated at solution conditions that allowed for binding of full capsids, with most empty capsids appearing in the load flowthrough (Figure S12). The following method was used for each cycle of AEX processing: column equilibration with 5 ColV Q-buffer A, sample loading, 5 ColV washing, 10 ColV empty capsid removal, and up to 4 ColV full capsid elution (truncated at UV 280 nm absorbance <10 mAU). Detailed methods used for each serotype including load dilution and step gradient composition are summarized in Table S7.

Multi-modal polishing chromatography: Capto Core 400 buffer screening

Running buffer salt concentration with Capto Core 400 resin was screened to determine impacts on AAV capsid recovery and HCP clearance. Two sets of runs were performed—one with affinity-purified AAV2 material and one with EGFP lysate material. Purified AAV2 loads were created by diluting purified capsids (3 × 1013 capsids per run) into 7.5 mL volumes of 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.3 at seven different NaCl concentrations—0, 50, 100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 mM. EGFP lysate loads were created by diluting EGFP lysate (2 mL per run) into 6 mL volumes of 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.3 at nine different NaCl concentrations—0, 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300, 400, and 500 mM. Capto Core 400 runs were performed for each purified AAV2 and EGFP load material lot using the following method: 5 ColV equilibration with respective running buffer (Tris-HCl, pH 7.3, NaCl 0–500 mM), load, 5 ColV wash with respective running buffer (Tris-HCl, pH 7.3, NaCl 0–500 mM), 2 ColV DI water, and 5 ColV strip with CC400-strip solution (20% 2-propanol in 1 M NaOH). Capsid recovery for AAV2 runs was measured using AAVX-Octet for each run. HCP content in the flowthrough for EGFP runs was measured by Bradford assay.

Multi-modal polishing chromatography: Capto Core 400 processing

Full capsid elution material from Capto Q AEX cycles was pooled within each replicate group to give 10 total material lots (N = 2 for AAV2, -5, -8, -9, and EGFP). Each material lot was 2-fold diluted in CC400-buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 250 NaCl, pH 7.4) followed by pH adjustment to 7.40 with 2 M HCl and conductivity adjustment to 31 mS/cm with 2 M NaCl to match CC400-buffer. The following method was used for each cycle of Capto Core 400: Equilibration with 5 ColV CC400-buffer, sample loading, washing with 3 ColV CC400-buffer, 2 ColV DI water, and 3 ColV column stripping with CC400-strip solution. Processing was performed at 7 mL/min flow rate (approximately 1.14 min residence time). Load flowthrough was collected from >1 mAU after sample load to <1 mAU during washing at UV 280 nm.

AAVX-octet, Bradford, SDS-PAGE, and negative stain transmission electron microscopy

Capsid titer measurements were performed before and after each chromatography cycle using Octet AAVX biosensors (Sartorius) with an Octet R8 instrument (Sartorius) as previously described.46 Freshly prepared internal reference standards for each AAV serotype were used for calculation of capsid concentrations. Data analysis was performed using dose response 4-parameter logistic regression (Weighted Y) standard curves in Octet Analysis Studio software (version 12.2). All samples were run in duplicate with averaged capsid concentration values used for calculation of chromatography yields.

Elution pools for each replicate group were concentrated and measured for total protein content using Pierce Bradford Plus Protein Assay Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as described previously.18 For chromatography steps with multiple cycles (AC for AAV9, AEX for all serotypes) samples were measured for protein concentration after pooling together elution material across all cycles. Eluates were concentrated either 10-fold (AC and AEX) or 20-fold (MMC) using AMICON Ultra-15 10-kDa spin filters (Sigma Aldrich) prior to protein measurement. Spin filter units were pretreated by sequential 5-min incubation and flushing cycles with 0.1% Pluronic F-68 in 1X PBS, 0.01% Pluronic F-68 in 1X PBS, and 0.001% Pluronic F-68 in 1X PBS +200 mM NaCl. Flat bottom 96-well plates (CELLTREAT) and a SpectraMax M5 plate reader (Molecular Devices) were used. A six-point albumin standard (BSA) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) standard curve was used, and all samples and standards were run in duplicate. Absorbance was measured at 595 nm with blank correction performed by subtracting absorbance measured for each respective elution buffer.

SDS-PAGE was used to visualize viral capsid protein banding and the relative abundance of protein impurities in each sample. Concentrated samples were 1:1 diluted in 2X Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad) and heated at 85°C for 15 min. 2 μL of each diluted sample was loaded to lanes of a polyacrylamide gel (4%–15% Mini-PROTEAN TGX Precast Protein Gel, 15-well, 15 μL, Bio-Rad). The gel was run for 50 min at 150 V in Tris/glycine/SDS running buffer (Bio-Rad). After running, the gel was fixed with 50% methanol +7% acetic acid for 40 min, stained with SYPRO Ruby Protein Stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 3 h, and then washed with 10% methanol +7% acetic acid for 1 h. Imaging was performed with an Azure 600 instrument (Azure Biosystems) with the default SYPRO Ruby channel (472 nm excitation/684 nm emission).

nsTEM was performed for each AAV sample after MMC (NAAV = 8). Eluates were 4-fold concentrated using AMICON Ultra-15 10-kDa spin filters as described above. 400-mesh copper grids with carbon films (Electron Microscopy Sciences) were rendered hydrophilic with a Pelco easiGlow glow discharge system. Samples were applied to the freshly glow-discharged grids, excess fluid was removed with filter paper, and the grids were stained on two drops of 2% aqueous uranyl acetate. Grids were blotted with filter paper and dried prior to imaging. Samples were examined using a Thermo Scientific Talos L120C TEM microscope, and images were acquired with a Thermo Scientific Ceta 16 M camera.

LC-MS/MS sample preparation

Protein digestion and desalting methods were performed as previously described.31,42,44,58 For each sample, 25 μg of measured protein in 100 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) buffer was reduced with 1.25 μL 100 mM TCEP (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated for 1 h at 60°C for denaturation. Alkylation was performed by addition of 2.5 μL 150 mM iodoacetamide (Sigma Aldrich) followed by incubation for 30 min in the dark at room temperature. Digestion was performed with trypsin (Promega) enzyme addition at a 1:50 enzyme-to-substrate mass ratio followed by incubation at 37°C for 16 h. Digestion was terminated by 4 μL addition of 20% formic acid (FA, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Of the 30 total samples analyzed, there were 11 samples that had a protein availability of less than 25 μg (Table S8). Digestion was performed for these samples with reagent amounts scaled accordingly.31 Samples were desalted with Omix C18 tips (Agilent), dried using a SpeedVac (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and redissolved in 22.5 μL 2% acetonitrile (ACN) with 0.1% FA as described previously.18 Pre-digested ADH standard (Waters) was spiked into samples at a concentration of 5 fmol/μL.

LC-MS/MS data acquisition

Samples equivalent to 2 μg digested materials were injected for each liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) data acquisition. Retention time standards (iRT, Biognosys) were prepared following the manufacturer’s protocol, and 0.1 μL was added with each injection. LC-MS/MS was performed with an ACQUITY UPLC M-class (Waters) and Sciex ZenoTOF 7600 instrument (Sciex) with an OptiFlow source. Samples were injected onto a C18 microtrap column (Phenomenex, 10 × 0.3 mm) followed by washing with 0.1% FA at 8 μL/min for 2 min. A C18 column (Phenomenex, Kinetex 2.6 mm XB C18, 100 Å, 150 × 0.3 mm) was used for peptide separation with the following gradient of mobile phase A (0.1% FA in water) and mobile phase B (0.1% FA in ACN): 3% mobile phase B for 1 min, 3%–32% mobile phase B over 44 min, 32%–80% mobile phase B over 1 min, column regeneration, and column re-equilibration. The column temperature was set to 30°C. SWATH-MS was performed with a survey scan within the 400–1,200 m/z mass range followed by 32 variable-size isolation windows for MS/MS with 25 ms accumulation time. Ion source gas 1 was set to 30 psi, and ion source gas 2 was set to 60 psi.

SWATH-MS data processing

SWATH-MS data processing was performed using an in silico spectral library constructed in DIA-NN (version 1.8.1) with a local copy of NCBI: Hu_RefSeqGRCh38 database.31,59 The reference database was supplemented with ADH, retention time calibrants, common contaminants, and AAV-related proteins.59 Data processing in DIA-NN was performed with default settings, except for protein interference, which was set to isoform IDs. Protein and peptide false discovery rates (FDRs) were set at 1% and match-between-runs was specified. The main output report from DIA-NN was processed in MSstats for summarization of protein peak areas and calculation of protein amounts in ng based on ADH response factors and protein MWs.31 Relative HCP amounts (ng) were determined with the assumption that all proteins consistently generate a concentration-dependent response. Data are displayed and discussed in terms of total amount (ng) detected for each protein, individual HCP content normalized to total HCP content (ng individual HCP/μg total HCP), and individual HCP content normalized to capsid protein content (ng individual HCP/μg total capsid protein).

Gene Ontology functional enrichment analysis

HCPs detected universally in all AAV-containing samples (NAAV = 8) after AC (AAVX-∩, n = 880), AEX (Q-∩, n = 99) and MMC (CC400-∩, n = 21) were characterized in the String (version 12.0) protein database. Physical subnetwork interaction maps were constructed for each group of HCPs using default interaction sources (text mining, experiments, and databases) and minimum required interaction scores (0.400). StringApp (version 2.2.0) within Cytoscape (version 3.10.1) was used for GO enrichment analysis.60 To establish a representative enrichment background, one crude AAV sample (AC load for AAV5 R1) was characterized using the LC-MS/MS proteomic workflow described above. The enrichment background was defined as the full union of all identified HCPs in the experiment, including the 30 chromatography eluates and the one crude sample. GO molecular function enrichment was performed with FDR <0.05 considered significant.
