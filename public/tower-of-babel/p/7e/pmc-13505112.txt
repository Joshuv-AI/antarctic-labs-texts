Latent profiles of advance care planning self-efficacy among clinical nurses in China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Wen Sun, Yiwen Liu, Nan Zhao, Chunhe Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdvance care planning (ACP) can help align care with patients’ values and preferences, but its implementation in routine practice remains challenging. Nurses are well placed to support ACP, although their confidence in ACP-related communication may vary. This study aimed to identify latent profiles of ACP self-efficacy among clinical nurses in hospital settings in China, examine factors associated with profile membership, and explore whether attitudes towards death differed across profiles.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted from August to September 2025 among registered clinical nurses in nine hospitals in two provinces in northeastern China. Data from 531 nurses were analysed. ACP self-efficacy was measured using the Chinese version of the Advance Care Planning Self-Efficacy Scale, and attitudes towards death were assessed using the Death Attitude Profile–Revised. Latent profile analysis was used to identify ACP self-efficacy profiles. Factors associated with profile membership were examined using the R3STEP approach, and differences in attitudes towards death were tested using the BCH method.ResultsThree ACP self-efficacy profiles were identified: low (37.5%), moderate (49.7%), and high (12.8%). The profiles differed mainly in overall confidence rather than item-specific response patterns. Across profiles, nurses reported relatively lower confidence in discussing living will documentation and communicating bad news. Prior awareness of ACP was consistently associated with membership in higher-confidence profiles. Palliative care training was associated with lower odds of belonging to the low rather than the moderate profile, whereas resuscitation experience was associated with higher odds of belonging to the low rather than the moderate profile. Selected work-related factors, including professional title and department, were also associated with profile membership. Attitudes towards death did not differ significantly across profiles.ConclusionsClinical nurses in hospital settings showed heterogeneity in ACP self-efficacy. Profile membership appeared to be more closely linked to ACP-related exposure, training, and selected work-related factors than to general attitudes towards death. These findings support targeted strategies to strengthen nurses’ confidence in ACP communication, particularly through early ACP exposure, practical training in difficult communication tasks, and supportive clinical workflows.Trial RegistrationNot applicable.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-05024-w.

Introduction

Advance care planning (ACP) is a process that helps patients clarify their values, discuss future care preferences, and prepare for situations in which they may no longer be able to speak for themselves [1]. It has been linked to care that is more consistent with patients’ wishes and to fewer unwanted aggressive interventions near the end of life [2]. Nevertheless, ACP remains difficult to implement in routine practice. In China, conversations about death and future care are often sensitive for patients, families, and health professionals [3, 4]. In addition, ACP is not yet part of routine care in many settings, and its implementation may be limited by low public awareness, limited institutional support, and insufficient professional training [5–7].

Nurses are well positioned to support ACP because they spend substantial time with patients and families and are often involved in ongoing communication about illness, treatment, and care needs [8, 9]. In ACP, nurses often contribute through supportive communication, such as helping patients express their values, understand care options, and navigate difficult conversations [10]. These roles complement, rather than replace, physician-led discussions about prognosis and treatment decisions [11, 12]. In practice, however, ACP conversations can be difficult. Nurses may need to respond to emotional distress, family disagreement, prognostic uncertainty, and time pressure [3]. In such situations, some nurses may feel able to engage in ACP-related communication, whereas others may feel unsure about how to begin or continue such discussions.

In China, ACP-related communication is shaped by cultural sensitivities around death, family-centred decision-making, and established patterns of physician–patient communication [4, 7]. ACP-related practices remain at an early stage, and national-level legislation specific to ACP is still limited [13]. In this context, nurses are more likely to support ACP through everyday communication about patients’ values, preferences, and care needs than to lead formal ACP decision-making [9–11]. Recent policy attention to hospice and palliative care reflects a broader shift towards patient-centred end-of-life care [14, 15], which may create a more supportive context for nurses’ involvement in ACP-related communication. Nurses may therefore facilitate ACP-related communication, although the scope of this role may vary across institutions and settings. Existing Chinese studies have documented variation in nurses’ ACP-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices [16, 17], and studies from other settings have identified self-efficacy as a factor associated with ACP engagement [18, 19]. Together, these findings support the need to examine ACP self-efficacy among clinical nurses in hospital settings in China.

One way to understand such variation is through self-efficacy, which refers to a person’s confidence in their ability to perform a specific behaviour [20]. In the present study, ACP self-efficacy (ACP-SE) refers to nurses’ confidence in their ability to engage in ACP-related behaviours, such as discussing patients’ values and preferences for future medical care, helping patients or families consider surrogate decision-making arrangements, and explaining or supporting the completion of advance directive documents where applicable. Given the current Chinese clinical context, these behaviours are interpreted as communication- and facilitation-related aspects of nurses’ ACP role rather than as clinical decision-making responsibilities [12, 21].

Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) provides a useful conceptual perspective for considering why nurses may differ in ACP self-efficacy [22]. According to SCT, self-efficacy is shaped by mastery experience, vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, and physiological and affective states associated with a given task [20]. Nurses who are already aware of ACP, have received palliative care training, are engaged in ACP-related conversations, or observe how experienced colleagues handle end-of-life discussions may feel more confident in ACP-related tasks [17, 22, 23]. Supportive feedback and encouragement from supervisors or peers may also reinforce nurses’ sense of competence [20]. Conversely, nurses who work in environments where ACP is not routine, or who have been repeatedly exposed to emotionally demanding end-of-life situations without adequate communication support, may feel less confident in initiating or sustaining ACP-related discussions [12, 18]. These mechanisms may contribute to heterogeneity in ACP self-efficacy among nurses, making it important to examine whether distinct subgroups can be identified. Identifying these subgroups was the primary rationale for adopting a person-centred analytic approach. In this study, SCT served as a conceptual guide for selecting and interpreting potentially relevant factors rather than as a formal framework for theory testing.

Research on ACP self-efficacy in China remains limited. Existing studies have focused mainly on the translation and psychometric testing of ACP self-efficacy measures [21, 24], whereas other surveys have examined nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to ACP [16, 17]. These studies are informative, but they mostly describe average levels or associations between variables. Although continuous scores can capture individual variation in ACP self-efficacy, they do not directly show whether nurses form distinct subgroups with different levels of confidence or support needs. A person-centred method such as latent profile analysis (LPA) can help identify such unobserved subgroups [25]. It may also help examine whether related factors, such as ACP awareness, palliative care training, and end-of-life care experience, are associated with these subgroups in different ways. This may offer a more practical basis for targeted training and support.

After identifying ACP self-efficacy profiles, it is also important to examine which factors are associated with membership in different profiles. Nurses’ personal characteristics, work setting, and prior clinical experiences may all be relevant. In particular, prior awareness of ACP, palliative care training, and end-of-life-related experiences may help explain differences in confidence. We were also interested in attitudes towards death. Because ACP often involves discussion of illness progression, dying, and future care choices, attitudes towards death may be relevant to nurses’ confidence in ACP-related communication [26]. However, it remains unclear whether broader attitudes towards death differ across subgroups of nurses with different levels of ACP self-efficacy. In this study, attitudes towards death were therefore included as an exploratory comparison rather than as a main explanatory variable.

Accordingly, this study aimed to (1) identify latent profiles of ACP self-efficacy among clinical nurses in hospital settings in China; (2) examine factors associated with membership in these profiles; and (3) explore whether attitudes towards death differ across the identified profiles.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional survey was conducted between August and September 2025 using convenience sampling. Registered clinical nurses were recruited from nine hospitals in two provinces in northeastern China. The participating hospitals comprised seven tertiary Grade 3A hospitals, one secondary Grade A general hospital, and one primary-level township hospital. Six were general hospitals, including the township hospital, and three were specialised hospitals, including two oncology hospitals and one maternal and child health hospital. The survey link was distributed through internal communication channels in each hospital. Participation was voluntary, and nurses could decide whether to complete the questionnaire. This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [27].

Nurses were eligible if they were aged 18 years or older, held a valid nursing licence, were engaged in clinical nursing work during the study period, and provided electronic informed consent. Clinical nursing work was defined as direct patient care in inpatient wards, outpatient departments, operating rooms, and other clinical units within hospitals, such as vascular access or catheter placement rooms. Nurses in full-time administrative, information management, computer-related, or other non-clinical roles were not included. Nurses were excluded if they were on sick leave or maternity leave during the survey period, were interns or trainees, or were temporarily away from routine clinical duties because of full-time continuing education or advanced training during data collection. The sample included nurses caring for adult patients as well as nurses working in gynaecology and paediatric departments, including those caring for neonates and infants. For descriptive and analytic purposes, nurses working outside inpatient wards were classified as the non-ward clinical services category.

There is no single accepted minimum sample size for LPA, but previous methodological work suggests that samples of approximately 300 or more are often adequate when multiple observed indicators are used [28]. In the present study, 14 ACP self-efficacy items were included as profile indicators. A total of 558 questionnaires were returned, and 531 valid responses were retained after data screening. The final sample size was therefore considered acceptable for the present analysis.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital (Approval No. KY2023-67). All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants before they completed the online questionnaire.

Instruments

Participant characteristics questionnaire

A self-administered participant characteristics questionnaire was used to collect sociodemographic, work-related, and experiential information that might be related to ACP self-efficacy. Using SCT as a conceptual framework [22], these variables were grouped into three domains for descriptive and analytic purposes. Personal characteristics included sex, ethnicity, religious belief, marital status, educational attainment, bereavement experience, and family discussions about death. Work-related and environmental factors included hospital type, hospital level, department, professional title, and administrative position. Departments were self-reported and classified into five categories: internal medicine, surgery, gynaecology/paediatrics, critical care/emergency, and non-ward clinical services. Experiential factors included years of working experience, resuscitation experience, palliative care training, and prior awareness of ACP.

Advance care planning self-efficacy scale (ACP-SE)

The Advance Care Planning Self-Efficacy Scale (ACP-SE) was used to assess nurses’ confidence in their ability to engage in ACP-related behaviours, such as discussing patients’ values and preferences for future medical care, helping patients identify surrogate decision-makers, and explaining or supporting the completion of advance directive documents. The scale was originally developed by Baughman et al. [29] and later translated and adapted into Chinese by Yang et al. [21]. It contains 14 items across three dimensions: preference discussion and assessment, information guidance and disclosure, and content assessment and determination. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“not at all confident”) to 5 (“extremely confident”). Total scores range from 14 to 70, with higher scores indicating greater ACP self-efficacy. The Chinese version showed good internal consistency in the validation study (Cronbach’s α = 0.896) [21]. In the present study, Cronbach’s α was 0.960. All 14 item scores were used as indicators in the LPA.

Death attitude profile–revised (DAP-R)

The Death Attitude Profile–Revised (DAP-R) was used to assess attitudes towards death. The scale was originally developed by Wong et al. [30] and later translated and culturally adapted into Chinese by Tang et al. [31]. It contains 32 items and measures five dimensions: death avoidance, fear of death, neutral acceptance, approach acceptance, and escape acceptance. The items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”). Higher scores on each subscale indicate stronger endorsement of the corresponding attitude towards death. The Chinese version showed good internal consistency in the adaptation study, with a reported Cronbach’s α of 0.875 [31]. In the present study, the overall Cronbach’s α was 0.934. The five subscale scores were used as exploratory distal outcomes in the supplementary BCH comparisons across the identified latent profiles.

Data collection

Data were collected online through the Wenjuanxing platform between August and September 2025. Study information and instructions were shown on the first page of the questionnaire. Participants could proceed to the survey only after providing electronic informed consent. To reduce duplicate submissions, each device or IP address was allowed to submit the questionnaire only once. All items required a response, so there were no item-level missing values in the final dataset. After data collection was completed, two researchers independently screened all returned questionnaires. Responses were excluded if they showed obvious straight-line answering patterns or if the completion time was less than 120 seconds. Of the 558 returned questionnaires, 531 were retained for the final analysis.

Data analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0. LPA and auxiliary analyses were conducted using Mplus version 8.3.

All 14 ACP-SE items were treated as continuous indicators. LPA models were estimated sequentially, beginning with a one-profile solution and then increasing the number of profiles one at a time [25]. Models were estimated using the robust maximum likelihood estimator (MLR). To reduce the risk of local solutions, 1000 random sets of starting values were used in the initial stage, and 250 optimisations were used in the final stage (STARTS = 1000 250), with 20 initial-stage iterations (STITERATIONS = 20). For the likelihood ratio tests, LRTSTARTS was set to 0 0 1000 250. The best log-likelihood value was replicated.

Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and sample-size-adjusted BIC (aBIC), with lower values indicating better fit [25]. Classification quality was assessed using entropy, with values closer to 1 indicating clearer separation between profiles; values of 0.80 or above were considered acceptable [25, 32]. The Lo–Mendell–Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMRT) and the bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT) were used to compare models with k profiles and k − 1 profiles. A significant p value indicates that the model with k profiles provides a better fit to the data [25, 33]. We also conducted sensitivity analyses by estimating separate LPA models for nurses from general hospitals (n = 335) and specialised hospitals (n = 196), as shown in Supplementary Table S2. The stratified analyses revealed broadly similar model-fit patterns across hospital types. In both subgroups, the three-profile solution had high entropy and acceptable class proportions, although the LMRT values for the three-profile solutions were not statistically significant. The BLRT and information criteria also indicated acceptable model fit. In the full sample, the three-profile solution had a significant LMRT value (p = 0.019). The full-sample three-profile solution was retained as the primary model. In addition to these statistical indices, profile size and substantive interpretability were considered when selecting the final model. Profiles containing less than 5% of the sample were considered potentially unstable and were interpreted with caution.

After the final profile solution had been selected, factors associated with profile membership were examined using the R3STEP approach [34]. All retained covariates were entered into the model simultaneously. Sex, ethnicity, and religious belief were excluded from this analysis because of category sparsity. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used for pairwise comparisons between profiles. For transparency, the full R3STEP results for all entered covariates are provided in Supplementary Table S1, whereas Table 4 presents the key estimates discussed in the text.

As a supplementary analysis, differences in attitudes towards death across the identified ACP-SE profiles were examined using the BCH method, which accounts for classification error when comparing distal outcomes across latent profiles [34]. The five DAP-R dimensions were entered as distal outcomes, and Wald chi-square tests were used to assess overall mean differences across profiles. Because these comparisons were exploratory, the findings were interpreted cautiously. All tests were two-tailed, and a p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 531 nurses were included in the final analysis. The mean age was 33.9 ± 7.2 years (range: 20–57 years). Most participants were female (96.0%), Han Chinese (95.3%), and married (63.3%); 82.5% had a bachelor’s degree or above. Religious belief was uncommon in this sample, with 98.1% reporting no religious belief. Nearly two-thirds of the participants had experienced bereavement (63.8%).

In terms of workplace characteristics, 63.1% worked in general hospitals, and 62.0% worked in tertiary Grade 3A hospitals. The largest departmental groups were internal medicine (33.0%), surgery (23.2%), and gynaecology/paediatrics (20.2%), while 13.6% worked in non-ward clinical services. Most nurses held junior (48.4%) or intermediate (45.6%) professional titles, and 86.8% did not hold an administrative position.

Regarding family discussions about death and clinical experience, 30.9% reported discussing death with family members when necessary in a relaxed manner, whereas 23.4% reported never discussing death with family members. More than half of the participants had 11 years or more of working experience (54.8%). Most reported having resuscitation experience (78.2%) or palliative care training (71.8%), while 58.0% reported no prior awareness of ACP. Discussion

This study identified three latent profiles of ACP self-efficacy among clinical nurses in hospital settings, indicating that ACP-SE varied across participants in this sample. The profiles differed mainly in overall confidence level rather than in item-specific response patterns. Profile membership was associated mainly with ACP-related exposure and selected work-related factors, whereas general attitudes towards death did not differ significantly across profiles.

The three-profile solution showed that clinical nurses in hospital settings differed mainly in their overall level of confidence in ACP-related tasks rather than in sharply distinct item-specific patterns. This pattern may be interpreted in relation to the integrated nature of ACP practices. In routine care, initiating discussions, exploring values, sharing information, explaining future care options, and supporting decision-making are closely connected rather than separate tasks [9, 35]. When nurses feel underprepared in one part of the process, reduced confidence is likely to affect the rest of the conversation as well [20]. In this sample, ACP-SE appeared to reflect a general level of ACP-related communication readiness rather than a set of isolated task-specific strengths and weaknesses. This also helps explain why the moderate profile accounted for nearly half of the sample, whereas the high-confidence profile was much smaller: many nurses were not completely unprepared for ACP, but neither did they feel fully confident across the full range of ACP-related tasks. Although the three profiles were largely parallel, the relatively lower scores on A3 and A10 indicate that some ACP tasks remained challenging across profiles, even when the main difference lay in the overall confidence level. These lower scores for A3 and A10 are clinically meaningful. A3, discussing how to complete living will documentation, requires nurses to explain future-care planning in a clear and practical way, which may be particularly challenging in settings where ACP documentation remains unfamiliar or unevenly implemented [13, 36]. A10, communicating bad news to patients and families, requires sensitivity, appropriate timing, and emotional regulation [37, 38]. Rather than representing subgroup-specific deficits, these items appear to be shared pressure points in ACP communication.

Among the experiential factors, prior awareness of ACP was the most consistent correlate of profile membership. Nurses with prior awareness of ACP were less likely to belong to lower-confidence profiles across all pairwise comparisons. This finding suggests that unfamiliarity itself may be an important barrier, consistent with studies showing that limited awareness or understanding of ACP can hinder its acceptance and implementation [36]. In settings where ACP is not yet routine, simply knowing what ACP is and what it involves may reduce uncertainty and make ACP-related tasks feel more approachable [6, 36]. Awareness is not the same as competence, but it may be the starting point for developing confidence in ACP-related communication [18, 19]. This interpretation is also consistent with recent Chinese evidence showing that death-related attitudes and ACP-related understanding are linked to nurses’ willingness to engage in ACP implementation [26]. Palliative care training was also associated with profile membership, particularly in distinguishing the low profile from the moderate profile. This result suggests that training may help nurses avoid very low confidence, even if it does not fully explain who reaches the highest confidence profile. Prior studies likewise suggest that palliative care education and ACP simulation training can improve nurses’ perceived self-efficacy or self-confidence in difficult conversations [39, 40]. However, training alone is unlikely to be sufficient [9, 41]. Higher ACP-SE may require repeated use in practice, feedback from experienced colleagues, and a work environment in which ACP discussions are expected and supported [18, 41].

Resuscitation experience showed a different pattern. In this study, it was associated with higher odds of belonging to the low rather than the moderate profile. This finding may appear counterintuitive because resuscitation experience reflects exposure to severe illness and end-of-life situations. Repeated exposure to resuscitation may not necessarily build confidence in proactive ACP communication [42, 43]. Resuscitation is usually reactive, time-critical, and technically focused, whereas ACP requires earlier, proactive, and value-oriented communication with patients and families [35, 42]. Resuscitation experience may also expose nurses to situations in which earlier conversations have not occurred, including rushed decisions, treatment escalation under pressure, family conflict, and care that may no longer reflect the patient’s values [42, 43]. In this sense, resuscitation experience may not function as a confidence-building experience for ACP communication, because it does not necessarily provide opportunities to practise proactive, value-oriented communication. From an SCT perspective, such experiences may offer limited mastery experience or vicarious learning for ACP communication [20, 43]. This interpretation is in line with recent literature on moral distress and ethical challenges in critical care nursing, which shows that end-of-life situations often involve perceived medical futility, ethically difficult treatment decisions, and a sense of burden rather than professional control [42, 43]. This pattern may therefore be specific to ACP-related communication rather than reflecting a general decline in clinical confidence. Nurses may feel competent in technical or emergency care while still feeling underprepared for proactive, value-oriented end-of-life communication when prior planning is absent or delayed. Notably, this association was observed only in the low- versus moderate-profile comparison, and this limited pattern should be interpreted cautiously given the cross-sectional design.

Professional title was also associated with profile membership, although this pattern was less consistent than that observed for ACP awareness and training. This finding may appear counterintuitive, but a higher professional title does not necessarily indicate better ACP-specific preparation [17, 23]. Rather, previous evidence suggests that ACP readiness and confidence are influenced more directly by dedicated training opportunities, communication support, and organisational facilitation than by seniority alone [12, 23, 41]. This may be particularly relevant in the Chinese context, where ACP remains insufficiently recognised by patients and families, end-of-life discussions are often shaped by family-centred decision-making, and institutional support for ACP implementation is still developing [4, 36]. Under these circumstances, nurses with intermediate titles may occupy demanding frontline roles with substantial clinical and family communication responsibilities yet still work within role-related and organisational constraints, which may undermine their confidence in ACP [4, 12]. Differences across departments may reflect variations in patient mix, communication opportunities, and exposure to end-of-life care [12, 17], but these effects appeared to be more limited than those related to ACP awareness and training.

Together, these findings highlight the practical value of the profile-based approach. While regression on a total score is useful for estimating average associations across the sample, it does not directly identify groups of nurses with different levels of readiness for ACP-related communication [25]. In this study, the three profiles provided interpretable groups that may be relevant for educational planning, as nurses at different confidence levels may have different support needs [9, 39]. The profile comparisons further suggested that related factors were not associated with profile membership in the same way across the confidence range. Prior awareness of ACP was associated with profile membership across all three pairwise comparisons, whereas palliative care training appeared to be more relevant for distinguishing low-confidence nurses from moderate-confidence nurses. Resuscitation experience was associated with greater odds of belonging to the low-confidence profile than to the moderate-confidence profile, suggesting that exposure to end-of-life crises may not necessarily translate into greater confidence in ACP-related communication. Although these findings should be interpreted as exploratory rather than as evidence of formally tested effect heterogeneity, they indicate that a profile-based approach may provide a more differentiated basis for understanding ACP self-efficacy and considering tailored educational support than aggregate associations alone.

As a supplementary comparison, general attitudes towards death did not clearly distinguish the ACP self-efficacy profiles in this sample. This null finding is still informative. Although a recent Chinese study showed that attitudes towards death were associated with nurses’ willingness to engage in ACP [26], our results suggest that these broader attitudes may not directly translate into clinical confidence. From the perspective of SCT, self-efficacy is a task-specific evaluation rather than a general psychological trait [20]. While a positive orientation towards death reflects personal values, ACP communication requires confidence in navigating specific and often sensitive interpersonal tasks, such as responding to family concerns and linking prognostic information with patients’ values and care goals [12, 35]. This finding may also be interpreted within the cultural and organisational context of end-of-life care in China. End-of-life communication is often shaped by family-centred decision-making, where family members are closely involved in information disclosure and care decisions [4, 7]. In such settings, nurses’ confidence may be influenced less by their personal comfort with death and more by situational factors, such as family readiness for discussion, team consensus, and the timing of communication [12]. Furthermore, in clinical environments where prognostic discussions are commonly physician-led, nurses’ confidence in participating in ACP may depend more on their defined role within the team and the available communication support than on their individual death-related values [11, 12]. These contextual conditions may help explain why individual attitudes towards death were not clearly reflected in ACP communication confidence in this sample. This suggests that, in the developing landscape of ACP in China, task-specific exposure, training, and organisational facilitation may be more closely related to nurses’ confidence than broader attitudes towards death.

These findings have practical implications for nurse education and organisational support. Because the profiles differed mainly in overall confidence level, a tiered approach may be more useful than a one-size-fits-all training model. Nurses in the low-confidence profile may benefit from basic ACP orientation, clearer role expectations, and foundational communication training [9]. In practical terms, this means helping them understand when ACP should be introduced, how nurses can appropriately participate in these discussions, and how to introduce future-care conversations using simple and practical communication prompts [35]. Nurses in the moderate-confidence profile may benefit from more advanced and practice-oriented educational strategies, such as case-based discussion, simulation, rehearsal, and feedback [39, 40], particularly for tasks such as explaining living will documentation and communicating bad news. In the Chinese context, where family involvement, traditional values, and implementation barriers may shape ACP discussions, nurses may face additional difficulty when discussing documents such as living wills and clarifying patients’ individual preferences [4, 13, 36]. These nurses may already have some familiarity with ACP, but they may still struggle when conversations become emotionally complex or when patients and families need both information and support. Nurses in the high-confidence profile may be well placed to serve as peer mentors, role models, or local champions within team-based ACP implementation [9, 41]. Their role is important because ACP becomes more sustainable when confidence is reinforced within teams rather than left to individual effort alone [41]. At the organisational level, clearer ACP workflows, documentation pathways, and interdisciplinary support may also help nurses translate awareness and training into more consistent practices [12, 41], which is particularly relevant in the Chinese context, where ACP implementation remains uneven and patients and families may still hold substantial misconceptions about ACP [36].

These findings should be interpreted in relation to the healthcare system in which ACP is implemented. ACP practices depend on legal recognition, institutional routines, professional role expectations, cultural norms around death, family involvement, and patient autonomy [4, 9, 13]. In settings where ACP is not yet routine, limited awareness of ACP may remain an important barrier to nurses’ confidence, regardless of their general clinical experience [18, 36]. Similarly, experience with resuscitation or other end-of-life crises may not by itself prepare nurses for proactive ACP communication. Such experience can force nurses to confront the consequences of delayed planning, but it does not necessarily provide the communication practices, role clarity, or team support needed for proactive ACP discussions [35, 41]. The finding that attitudes towards death did not clearly distinguish ACP self-efficacy profiles also suggests that general comfort with dying should not be conflated with task-specific ACP self-efficacy [20, 26]. Moreover, some findings should be interpreted within the Chinese context. Associations related to professional title may reflect local nursing career structures, role expectations, and institutional hierarchies, although this interpretation should be considered exploratory [4, 12]. Family-centred decision-making, protective approaches to information disclosure, and limited public familiarity with advance directives may also shape how nurses participate in ACP-related communication [4, 36]. In healthcare systems where ACP is more formally established, nurses may have clearer institutional roles but may face different challenges, such as helping with legal documentation, supporting patient autonomy, or working within structured ACP pathways [9, 41]. The profile-based approach may therefore be useful beyond this setting, not because the same profiles will necessarily appear elsewhere, but because it offers a structured way to examine how nurses’ ACP readiness is distributed within a given healthcare system and to identify where training and organisational support are most needed [9, 25].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design means that causal relationships cannot be established. Although prior awareness of ACP, palliative care training, and resuscitation experience were associated with profile membership, it remains unclear whether these factors influence ACP self-efficacy or whether nurses with different levels of self-efficacy are more likely to report these experiences. Second, participants were recruited through convenience sampling from nine hospitals in two provinces in northeastern China. Although the sample included clinical nurses from different hospital levels and from both general and specialised hospitals, the findings are likely to be most applicable to nurses working in similar hospital-based settings. They should be generalised cautiously to nurses in other regions, community health service centres, long-term care facilities, other non-hospital care settings, or exclusively non-clinical roles. Because the sample included nurses caring for adult patients as well as nurses working in gynaecology and paediatric departments, ACP-related communication may differ across adult, gynaecological, paediatric, and neonatal care settings; these differences could not be examined in detail in this study. Third, nurses who were more interested in ACP or more willing to engage with end-of-life issues may have been more likely to participate, which could have introduced self-selection bias and may have led to some overestimation of ACP self-efficacy. Fourth, all variables were measured using self-report, which may have introduced reporting bias. Fifth, the high ACP self-efficacy profile was relatively small (n = 68), and some estimates from the multinomial logistic regression, particularly those involving subgroups with few cases, should therefore be interpreted with caution. For example, the odds ratio for senior professional title in the moderate versus high profile comparison (OR = 14.541, 95% CI: 1.632–129.588) had a wide confidence interval, reflecting limited precision in this estimate. Finally, prior awareness of ACP was assessed with a simple yes/no item and could not capture the amount, source, or quality of ACP-related exposure. Future research should use longitudinal designs, larger and more balanced samples, and more detailed measures of ACP-related exposure, clinical role, care setting, patient population, and organisational support to better understand how ACP self-efficacy develops over time.

Conclusions

ACP self-efficacy varied among clinical nurses in hospital settings, with three profiles identified: low, moderate, and high. These profiles differed mainly in overall confidence level rather than in sharply distinct task patterns. Profile membership was more closely related to ACP-related exposure, training, and selected work-related factors than to general attitudes towards death.

Efforts to strengthen nurses’ ACP self-efficacy should therefore be practical and targeted. Early exposure to ACP concepts may help reduce unfamiliarity. Communication-focused training on living will documentation and communicating bad news may help nurses handle common difficult tasks more confidently. Practice-based approaches such as case discussion, simulation, rehearsal, and feedback may be particularly useful for nurses with moderate confidence. At the organisational level, clearer workflows, documentation pathways, and team-based support may help translate awareness and training into more consistent ACP practices.
