The influence of higher education based on machine learning on subjective well-being
Authors: Ting Qin, Rui Fu, Pengjian Yao, Pingqiang Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As higher education becomes increasingly prevalent and accessible in China, a growing number of residents are afforded the option to pursue advanced studies. Can higher education genuinely enhance residents’ subjective well-being? The response to this enquiry necessitates additional investigation. This study selected 5 wave data of Chinese General Social Survey (CGSS), a total of 53,874 samples. Machine learning methodologies, including XGBoost and GBDT, were utilised for the inaugural correlation investigation between higher education and subjective well-being in China. Feature importance sorting elucidated the nonlinear correlations and interaction effects, such as the threshold effect of social fairness cognition on happiness, that typical regression models struggle to capture. (1) The average subjective well-being of the higher education group (4.005309) was significantly higher than that of the non-higher education group (3.835478), and the education level had a significant positive predictive role on subjective well-being (p = 0.000 < 0.05); (2) Machine learning uncovers substantial correlations between higher education and subjective well-being (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document}=0.008, p < 0.01), with social justice cognition (feature weight=0.32) and self-rated health (0.28) identified as primary mediators. (3) The proportion of women in the highest level of well-being ([4.0,5.0)] was slightly higher than that of men. Higher education can markedly enhance the subjective well-being of individuals. Furthermore, it improves residents’ subjective well-being via social justice perception, self-assessed health, social class identity, job satisfaction, and socio-economic status. This establishes a scientific foundation for the government and all societal sectors to augment investment in education, enhance the distribution of educational resources, and foster the comprehensive development of individuals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19116-1.

Introduction

Subjective well-being is a complex structure, covering many aspects such as life satisfaction, balance between positive and negative emotions. Diener defines it as a variety of subjective evaluations of a person’s life, including cognitive evaluations and emotional feelings1. With the rise and development of positive psychology, there are more and more researches on subjective well-being. Especially in the field of education. Lai et al. conducted a study on the elderly in Hong Kong, and the results showed that the elderly with insufficient disposable income and lower education were more likely to have low subjective well-being2. Herke et al. analyzed the trajectory of subjective well-being of German adolescents throughout secondary education, taking into account differences in socioeconomic status, gender, and family structure3. Liu et al. confirmed the positive predictive effect of online education on subjective well-being, and affirmed the multiple mediating effects of social class mobility and social tolerance between online education and subjective well-being4. Ergün and Demirdag proved that positive language education can improve students’ subjective well-being, and then improve the level of positive emotions in the language classroom5. Wang and Sohail’s results show that long-term estimates related to education are positive and important, which means that an increase in average years of schooling contributes to higher levels of happiness6. Bien and Bien-Barkowska demonstrated that higher levels of education improved self-assessed health status and reduced the severity of loneliness and depressive symptoms, independent of the general predictors of beneficial effects on all three SWB indicators7. Qin et al. analyzed the predictive role of women’s education level on subjective well-being. The results showed that the longer the women’s education years, the stronger the subjective well-being8. According to Fig. 1, based on the Material pathway, this study proposes hypothesis 1: higher education associates with elevated SWB by increasing socioeconomic resources.

Higher education may have a predictive role on subjective well-being through various mechanisms. Sortheix et al. explored the relationship between personal values, value congruence, interpersonal relationships and subjective well-being of students from different countries. They found that personal values are not directly related to subjective well-being, but rather how these values adapt to the social environment9. This shows the importance of social relations and values consistency in determining subjective well-being outcomes. Ma et al. empirically analyzed the social justice cognition of Chinese people. The results showed that most respondents’ cognition of fairness was prior to their cognition of justice. Demographic factors such as education level and income level caused group differences in people’s cognition of fairness and justice10. Zhang et al. analyzed the cognition of informal employment on residents’ social justice. The results show that informal employment further reduces residents’ cognition of the overall fairness of society by reducing residents’ cognition of rights fairness, opportunity fairness and rule fairness11. Ma et al. analyzed the floating population ‘s perception of social equity. The results showed that in terms of social equity perception, compared with rural residents and urban residents, the floating population had a lower sense of fairness in the current overall social situation, compulsory education and health care policies12. These studies have shown that education may be associated with subjective well-being through social cognition.

Self-rated health status is the subjective evaluation of an individual’s physical condition. Sun et al. investigated how the subjective well-being of the Chinese population changes with subjective health status, age, gender, region, and socioeconomic characteristics, emphasizing the importance of these factors in determining overall subjective well-being13. Rivenbark et al. found that adolescents’ perceptions of family social status were linked to various indicators of health, including depression, anxiety, and life chances14. Feng et al. found that when affected by epidemic diseases, the subjective well-being, social class identity and self-rated health of the elderly were still significantly positively correlated. The mediating effect of self-rated health on subjective well-being and social class identity of the elderly increased from 9.6–12.4%15. In addition, Zhang and Wu found that the level of education has a significant predictive role on individual physical health (both physical and psychological), and individual subjective well-being has a significant positive predictive role on their physical health16. These studies have shown that education may be associated with subjective well-being through health.

Social class identity is the reflection of social structure at the individual psychological level. The research on the subjective well-being of the elderly shows that social class identity, gender, age, household registration, education level, social trust and social justice have a significant positive predictive role on the subjective well-being of the elderly17,18. Li et al. studied the relationship between income level, social class and subjective well-being. The results show that the curve relationship between income and subjective well-being is regulated by social class19. Wang et al. analyzed the relationship between socioeconomic status, social class identity and subjective well-being. The results showed that socioeconomic status was measured by income and education, and the indirect effect of socioeconomic status on subjective well-being mediated by social class identity was 0.351 (95% CI: 0.721, 1.587)20. These studies have shown that education may be linked to subjective well-being through social class identity.

The degree of education is directly correlated with the job happiness of inhabitants. Studies have shown that people with higher education are more likely to report higher levels of hedonic subjective well-being, indicating that they think their work and life are more meaningful and experience more positive emotions21. Zhang et al. conducted an empirical study on the subjective well-being of professional women in China’s Dalian High-tech Industrial Zone (women who have received formal education and currently have full-time stable work). The results show that the positive emotions of professional women come from the company of family and friends in the family’s internal life and the satisfaction of the working environment and treatment in the family’s external life22. The study found that the professional identity of college sports technical teachers will have a positive impact on their subjective well-being through the four dimensions of professional values, role values, professional sense of belonging and professional behavior tendency, directly or indirectly through the intermediary role of job satisfaction23. Voces and Caínzos have shown that educational/employment incongruities reduce job and economic satisfaction, but do not associate with overall subjective well-being or the image of social stratification24. In addition, a study of doctoral students in mainland China has shown the importance of psychological capital (including self-efficacy, hope, resilience, and optimism) in influencing subjective well-being25. In short, education is associated with residents’ subjective well-being through various ways, including job satisfaction.

The level of education of parents is related to the subjective well-being of offspring in adulthood, which indicates that subjective well-being is transmitted from generation to generation through education26. Rivenbark et al. found that adolescents’ perceptions of family social status were associated with health and well-being outcomes, indicating the importance of subjective perceptions of socioeconomic status14. Sun and Seo found that both subjective and objective socioeconomic status were associated with subjective well-being, but the correlation varied by socioeconomic status type and age group27. The results of Gao et al. showed that subjective socioeconomic status moderated the predictive role of life stressors on perceived neighborhood security, emphasizing the complex dynamic relationship between objective life stressors and social environmental background in shaping mental health outcomes28. In addition, subjective socioeconomic status has been identified as an indicator of intimate partner violence in middle-aged people. Higher education, lower subjective socioeconomic status, childhood stress and marriage are significantly associated with intimate partner violence29. These studies have shown the complex relationship between education, socioeconomic status and subjective well-being.

In general, education is associated with subjective well-being through social equity cognition, health status, value consistency, social relations, job satisfaction, and perceptions of income inequality. By shaping individuals’ perceptions of these factors, education may play a key role in determining subjective well-being outcomes. Further research in this field can provide valuable insights into the mechanism by which education affects subjective well-being. Based on Fig. 1, grounding in social/individual pathways, this study proposes hypothesis 2: higher education associates with SWB through social justice cognition (H2a), self-rated health (H2b), class identity (H2c), and job satisfaction (H2d).

Materials and methods

Data sources

This study selected five waves of data from the Chinese General Social Survey (CGSS), which were 2013,2015,2017,2018 and 2021, respectively. CGSS is implemented by Renmin University of China, which conducts a continuous cross-sectional survey of more than 10,000 households in various provinces, municipalities and autonomous regions in Mainland China. After screening, cleaning and eliminating invalid samples, 53,874 valid data were obtained. Among them, 10,901 in 2013,10577 in 2015,12220 in 2017,12447 in 2018, and 7729 in 2021.

Owing to the cross-sectional form of the CGSS, this study is limited to examining statistical relationships among variables. Subsequent study ought to authenticate causal pathways by the utilization of panel data or natural experiments (e.g., policies for the extension of higher education).

Variables measurement

Variable being explained

According to the existing research, a single question can be used as a measure of subjective well-being30,31. In this study, the question of “in general, do you feel happy in your life” was selected as the explanatory variable32,33. The answer is Mainly divided into 5 grades, 1 is very unhappy, and 5 is very happy. When the value is larger, it indicates that the individual’s subjective well-being is stronger. In this study, 3 is taken as the critical point, 1 to 3 is divided into low happiness, and 4 to 5 is divided into high happiness, so as to judge whether you are happy.

Explanatory variables

This study selects the level of education as an explanatory variable. 1 represents no education, and 13 represents graduate students and above. The larger the value is, the higher the education level is. We define a college degree or above as higher education, that is, 9 to 13 represents individuals who have received higher education, and 1 to 8 represents individuals who have not received higher education.

Control variable

This study sets up control variables at three levels: individual, family and society. At the individual level, gender, marital status, self-rated health status, job satisfaction and self-ability were controlled. Analysis method

Gradient boosting decision tree classification

Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (GBDT) is an ensemble learning algorithm that constructs a strong learner by combining multiple weak learners (decision trees). In the classification problem, GBDT works similarly to the regression problem, but in the final output, the result is converted to a probability value through a sigmoid function. For the binary classification problem, the model of GBDT can be expressed as formula (1).1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(y=1\mid{x})=\frac{1}{1+e^{F_M(x)}}$$\end{document}

When constructing each decision tree, GBDT searches for the optimal cut points and thresholds to minimize the loss function. For the binary classification problem, the commonly used loss function is log loss, which is in the form of formula (2).2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L(y_i,f(x_i))=y_i\:log(p_i)=(1-y_i)log(1-p_i)$$\end{document}

Compared with CatBoost, it is usually necessary to manually process category features, such as converting category features into numerical features by single-hot coding. The latter automatically processes category features without manual conversion.

XGBoost classification

XGBoost is an efficient implementation of GBDT. It is optimized on the basis of GBDT and introduces a regularization term to control the complexity of the model, thereby improving the generalization ability of the model. In the classification problem, the objective function of XGBoost can be expressed as Formula (3).3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$obj(t)=\sum^T_{(j=1)}[\sum(i\epsilon{I}_j)g_i{w}_j+\frac{1}{2}(\sum(i\epsilon{I}_j)h_j+\lambda)w_j^2]+\gamma{T}$$\end{document}

In the formula (3), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{g}_{i}$$\end{document} is the first derivative (gradient) of the i th sample under the current model, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{h}_{j}$$\end{document} is the second derivative of the i th sample under the current model, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{j}$$\end{document} is the score of the j th leaf node, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{j}$$\end{document} is the index set of all samples in the j th leaf node. λ and γ are the parameters of the regularization term, which are used to control the complexity of the model and prevent overfitting. T is the total number of leaf nodes.

In the classification problem, XGBoost finds the optimal decision tree structure by minimizing the above objective function. For the binary classification problem, the final prediction output of XGBoost can convert the linear score into a probability value through the sigmoid function, and the formula is (4).4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(y=1\mid{x})=1\mid{x}=\frac{1}{1+e^{(-\sum^M_{(n=1)}f_x(x))}}$$\end{document}

In Eq. (4), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{m}\left(x\right)$$\end{document} is the predicted score of the m-th tree for the sample x, and M is the total number of trees.

L1 and L2 regularization terms are added to the loss function. The greedy algorithm is used to select the split point of the tree, and the pre-pruning strategy is used, that is, pruning is performed during the growth of the tree to prevent overfitting. GBDT usually uses a depth-first strategy, namely Level-wise growth. XGBoost uses a breadth-first strategy, known as Leaf-wise growth, which helps to build trees faster, and reduces the amount of computation and supports parallel processing, which can significantly increase the training speed.

Mediation effect analysis

We tested mediation effects using the bootstrap method with 5,000 resamples. The mediation effect is significant if the 95% confidence interval (CI) excludes zero. Effect decomposition was calculated as:

Total effect = Direct effect + Indirect effect.

Proportion mediated = Indirect effect/Total effect.

Changes in the model. The R² of the control layer model is 0.652, and the adjusted R² is 0.649. The R² of the level 1 model is 0.752, and the adjusted R² is 0.746. This shows that the hierarchical 1 model of Self-rated health and Social justice recognition better explains the change of subjective well-being than the control layer model. From the control layer to layer 1, R² increased by 0.079, △F value was 2476.841, and p value was 0.000***. This shows that the newly added independent variables (self-rated health status and social justice perception) have significant additional explanatory power for subjective well-being.

In summary, job satisfaction, social class identity, education level, and household registration have a significant positive predictive role on subjective well-being. In addition, at level 1, individual self-rated health status and social equity perception have an additional significant predictive role on subjective well-being, with an △R² of 0.079.

Discussion

Theoretical verification

Our machine learning findings validate the tri-pathway framework:

Social justice cognition (weight = 0.32) dominated feature importance, confirming Sortheix’s social pathway.

Self-rated health (0.28) outweighed education (0.15), supporting Diener’s affective dimension.

SES showed weaker mediation (β = 0.014), aligning with resource saturation effects in China.

Main findings

This study mainly discusses whether higher education is associated with residents’ subjective well-being, and how higher education associates with residents’ subjective well-being. Through the empirical analysis of the 5-wave data of CGSS, there are three main findings.

First, the average subjective well-being of the higher education group (4.005309) is significantly higher than that of the non-higher education group (3.835478). The education level has a significant positive predictive role on subjective well-being (p = 0.000 < 0.05), that is, receiving higher education can significantly improve the subjective well-being of residents. Hypothesis 1 is verified, which is consistent with the existing research results34–36. In China, the focus of higher education expansion policy is to stimulate the upward mobility of individuals and ultimately improve their overall well-being37. For highly educated groups, general social trust has a significant positive predictive role on subjective well-being38. University education improves well-being by raising personal political status, perceived personal status, health, and family social status39. Higher education has a complex predictive role on residents’ subjective well-being. In general, by raising awareness, improving economic returns, improving health status, improving living conditions, etc., to enhance residents’ subjective well-being.

Second, higher education is associated with subjective well-being through social equity cognition, self-rated health status, social class identity, job satisfaction and socioeconomic status. Moreover, higher education, social equity cognition, self-rated health status, social class identity, job satisfaction and socioeconomic status have a significant positive predictive role on residents’ subjective well-being. Hypothesis 2 is verified. Previous studies have shown that social justice cognition is conducive to improving residents’ subjective well-being. For example, the higher the level of social equity, the stronger the happiness of low-income groups due to higher income40. Participants who support egalitarianism and are more aware of social inequalities show a greater awareness of racism, and in turn, are more likely to support a higher direction and commitment to social justice41. There is evidence that higher education is associated with subjective well-being through self-rated health status. For example, both self-rated health status and subjective well-being decreased significantly from the senior year of college to one year after graduation42. Living in rural areas and lower education level are negative predictors of subjective well-being in the elderly. Although income still associates with the subjective well-being of the elderly, self-rated health status and psychological capital may be the key mediators43. Previous studies have found that the age, low education level, poor health and low income of the elderly are negatively correlated with subjective well-being44. Previous studies have shown that job satisfaction may have a good subjective well-being for residents. For example, Korean workers with high job satisfaction and job security are more likely to have good subjective health and well-being45. Job satisfaction has a significant positive predictive role on farmers’ subjective well-being, and non-agricultural job satisfaction has a more significant predictive role on farmers’ subjective well-being46. There is evidence that subjective socioeconomic status and objective socioeconomic status are positively correlated with mental health, physical health and well-being47. Therefore, we should attach great importance to the popularization and development of higher education, and provide more people with equal educational opportunities and resources to promote social fairness and harmony and enhance the overall well-being of residents.

Third, the proportion of women in the highest happiness level ([4.0,5.0]) is slightly higher than that of men, which may indicate that women have a slight advantage in subjective well-being. Previous studies have supported this view to varying degrees. For example, gender can regulate the direct path of cognitive failure affecting mobile phone addiction, and cognitive failure has a greater negative predictive role on girls’ subjective well-being48. Gender moderated the correlation of psychological symptoms on subjective well-being, and girls had a higher moderating correlation49. The subjective well-being scale score of male college students was lower than that of female college students (t=−3.218, P < 0.01)50. However, some studies have shown that male subjective well-being is higher than female subjective well-being. For example, the level of subjective well-being of older men is significantly higher than that of older women51. In areas where women tend to be more gender-biased, women’s subjective well-being is lower, but men’s well-being is not affected52. Does this mean that age differences and urban-rural differences lead to gender differences in subjective well-being. Which factors lead to different subjective well-being due to gender differences remains to be further studied.

Advantages and limitations

This study has the following advantages. First of all, through large-scale data analysis, it is verified that receiving higher education has a significant correlation on improving residents’ subjective well-being. It provides data support for policy makers. Secondly, the study found that residents receiving higher education are more likely to achieve social class transition, so as to obtain higher social status and respect. It provides an important reference for the government and all sectors of society to promote the flow of social strata and promote social fairness and justice. Machine learning models identified social justice cognition (feature importance weight: 0.32) and self-rated health (0.28) as stronger predictors of well-being than education level (0.15). This challenges the conventional view that education directly dominates well-being and provides new evidence for multidimensional policy interventions.

This study also has the following shortcomings. First, although higher education can improve residents’ subjective well-being, this improvement is not universally applicable to all groups of people. For example, through data analysis, we are also thinking about whether there are gender differences in subjective well-being due to age differences and urban-rural differences. Second, although higher education has a positive predictive role on residents’ subjective well-being, there is still an imbalance in the distribution of educational resources. This imbalance not only affects the education level of residents in these areas, but also limits their possibility of improving happiness through education.

Conclusions

Utilising five waves of CGSS data and employing XGBoost and GBDT to elucidate nonlinearities and interactions, our research illustrates that elevated educational attainment markedly improves subjective well-being (mean SWB: 4.0053 vs. 3.8355, p < 0.001) and contributes to this outcome both directly and indirectly through five principal mediators: social equity cognition, self-assessed health, social class identity, job satisfaction, and socioeconomic status. Significantly, social equity cognition and health perception collectively constitute more than 70% of the overall indirect effect, highlighting their crucial functions in converting educational achievement into enhanced life satisfaction. These findings underscore the necessity for policymakers to broaden and equalise access to quality higher education, enhance resource allocation, and fortify supportive social and health services, thereby facilitating greater segments of the population in attaining upward mobility and enduring psychological well-being through higher education.
