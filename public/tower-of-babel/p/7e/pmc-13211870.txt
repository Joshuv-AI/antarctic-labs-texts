End‐of‐Life Loneliness, Social Isolation, and Symptom Burden: A Nationally‐Representative Study
Authors: Mara Rosenberg, Irena Cenzer, Alexander K Smith, Ashwin A Kotwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLoneliness (subjective feeling of lacking connection) and social isolation (objective deficit in number of relationships or contact with others) are common at the end‐of‐life and can be detrimental to quality of life. Investigating the association between symptoms and end‐of‐life loneliness and social isolation could help inform targeted interventions.MethodsWe used nationally‐representative, cross‐sectional Health and Retirement Study data, including adults > 50 years old (N = 2385) who died while enrolled. Respondents self‐reported on validated loneliness and social isolation measures within 1 year of death; 12 physical and psychological symptoms were determined via after‐death interviews with proxies. We use multivariable logistic regression to determine the adjusted probability of end‐of‐life loneliness or social isolation by each symptom, adjusting for socio‐demographic covariates.ResultsRespondents were on average 76 years old, 50% female, 82% identified as White, 10% Black, and 5% Latino. Loneliness was more common among the following symptoms (p < 0.05): pain (30% vs. 20%), depression (35% vs. 18%), fatigue (29% vs. 22%), drowsiness (32% vs. 25%), and agitation (38% vs. 24%). Social isolation was more common in decedents who had difficulty breathing (22% vs. 14%, p = 0.03), drowsiness (30% vs. 17%, p = 0.006), and persistent cough (24% vs. 15%, p = 0.007).ConclusionsIn the last year of life, multiple psychosocial symptoms were associated with experiences of loneliness whereas physical symptoms were associated more with social isolation. While these relationships are complex, addressing the social sequela of physical and psychological symptoms may be an opportunity to improve overall well‐being at the end‐of‐life.Keywords: end‐of‐life, loneliness, social isolation, symptom burden

Introduction

Social isolation and loneliness have emerged as growing public health challenges, with the U.S. Surgeon General's Advisory in May 2023 declaring these issues national epidemics [1]. Social isolation, the objective lack of social connection, and loneliness, the subjective feeling of insufficient social relationships, have significant psychological and physical consequences including worse mental health and higher morbidity and mortality [2, 3, 4]. People in the last years or months of life appear especially vulnerable to experiencing loneliness and social isolation. Specifically, rates were twice that of the general public; 19% met criteria for social isolation, 18% experienced frequent loneliness, and 5% experienced both in the last 4 years of life [5]. Effective interventions to address end‐of‐life social isolation and loneliness are needed to improve well‐being of those within the last year of life.

Despite growing recognition of the importance of social connection at the end‐of‐life, there remains a knowledge gap on the interplay between social isolation, loneliness, and end‐of‐life symptoms. Clinicians and hospice providers often focus on physical and psychological symptoms, however there is an under recognition of how these symptoms may be intertwined with, and potentially impact, the social lives of their patients. A better understanding of the relationship between loneliness and/or social isolation and symptom burden might inform clinical interventions to improve person‐centered quality of life at the end‐of‐life by addressing the social sequelae of common end‐of‐life symptoms. In addition, limited research has explored the relationship between end‐of‐life symptom burden and social isolation, which is a distinct experience from end‐of‐life loneliness. Examining social isolation and loneliness separately is important to help identify targetable and impactful interventions for two distinct social needs. Lastly, while prior research has demonstrated that loneliness can predict symptom burden in the last 4 years of life [4], there is limited data on end‐of‐life loneliness occurring in the last 12 months prior to death, an important timeframe of even higher symptom burden and psychological distress [6].

In this study, we address these gaps by leveraging a large, nationally representative cohort of older adults to examine the relationships between end‐of‐life symptoms and social isolation and loneliness. We hypothesize that symptoms more closely tied to psychological well‐being (i.e., depression) are associated with loneliness, whereas predominantly physical symptoms (i.e., emesis) are more closely associated with objective social isolation. This hypothesis is informed by a conceptual framework that recognizes loneliness and social isolation as separate but related social experiences with distinct mechanisms by which they impact health. Loneliness reflects emotional distress from inadequate social relationships and so may be amplified by other forms of psychosocial distress. In contrast, social isolation may reflect a lack of available sources of support in one's social network (e.g., for caregiving, complex medication regimens) [7, 8] which may impact the ability to manage and cope with physical symptoms. While many symptoms can have both physical and psychological components, we categorized symptoms based on previously described symptom clusters, with the symptom of pain having both psychological and physical features [9].

Methods

Setting and Participants

We used nationally representative data from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) 2006–2018 [10]. The “core” interview includes nationally representative sampling of adults age > 50 years interviewed biannually until death, with an overall response rate ranging from 74% to 89% depending on the interview year [11]. After the death of respondents, proxies (who are typically next of kin) are interviewed as part of an “exit interview” including responses to an end‐of‐life symptoms questionnaire. Our analyses included HRS participants who participated in and self‐completed a core interview between 2006 and 2018, died within 12 months of their interview, and had an exit interview completed by a proxy after their death. We used interviews identifying loneliness and social isolation within 1 year of death to ensure we could match the response window of proxy reported symptoms. Three thousand six hundred and seventy‐one respondents died within 12 months of their last HRS interview; 2547 of these completed the last interview on their own without a proxy, and 2432 also had exit interviews after their death. Excluding 42 decedents who had missing data on loneliness led to a final cohort size of 2385 subjects for the primary cohort. The mean duration of time from death to completion of the after‐death interview was 7.4 months (SD 3.3 months).

In addition, HRS participants are invited to respond to a written “psychosocial leave behind questionnaire” (LBQ) which has a comprehensive measure of social isolation not present in the core; it is administered to alternating halves (50%) of the core sample every 2 years. Of our overall sample, 800 participants also had complete data in the LBQ and were included as the LBQ cohort (Figure S1).

Social Isolation and Loneliness

Loneliness was measured from the core data using a previously validated single question assessment for loneliness [12]: “Now think about the past week and the feelings you have experienced. Please tell me if you felt lonely much of the time during the past week.” A “yes” response was classified as “lonely,” a “no” response as “not lonely.” We chose this measure as it has been commonly used in previous studies, has high correlation with cutoffs used in longer loneliness scales [12, 13, 14], and because use of a single item loneliness measure in clinical settings may be more practical due to the time‐limited setting [15]. In addition, using this measure allowed us to maximize sample size as it was administered to the full HRS sample. In addition, we performed a sensitivity analysis using the 3‐item UCLA Loneliness Scale [16]. This scale was asked of a subset of the sample via the LBQ and included a composite score based on the following questions: the frequency participants reported lacking companionship, felt left out, or felt isolated from others. Response options were: “Hardly ever or never” (0 points), “Some of the time” (1 point), or “Often” (2 points) for a total potential score of 6 points. Loneliness was determined if the respondent scored at least 4 points on the scale.

Social isolation was defined using a previously published composite social isolation scale derived from survey questions in the LBQ. Additional details on the development of this scale and its rationale for use in end‐of‐life cohorts are described in previous work [5, 17]. The available sample for this scale was smaller than the full cohort (n = 800 vs. n = 2385); however, this scale includes a comprehensive evaluation of social networks, a feature hypothesized to correlate with symptom burden [5, 18, 19, 20, 21]. The social isolation scale uses a composite score derived from 15 distinct survey questions that address household and core contacts, community engagement, and social network interaction. The final scale ranges from 0 to 6 points, with 0–2 points classified as “socially isolated.” The specific survey questions and numerical scale are included in Figure S2.

End‐of‐Life Symptoms

Symptoms were reported by respondent proxies via after‐death interviews. Proxies were asked to assess symptoms experienced by HRS participants for one or more months (yes/no) during the last year of the decedent's life and included both psychological symptoms (depression, agitation, and confusion) and physical symptoms (poor appetite, difficulty breathing, difficulty controlling arms/legs, frequent vomiting, incontinence, persistent cough, drowsiness, fatigue, and pain) (the wording of each question is provided in Table S1). Sensitivity analyses were done using self‐reported symptoms that occurred in the past year by decedents during their final core interview. Self‐reported symptoms included: depression, pain, difficulty breathing, fatigue, cough, and incontinence (question wording in Table S1). We focused on the proxy‐reported symptoms for the primary investigation rather than self‐reported as there were more symptoms reported by proxies and the question wording focused on symptoms occurring for at least a month within the last year prior to death.

Sociodemographic and Health Measures

Sociodemographic factors included age, sex, marital status, race/ethnicity (White, Black, Hispanic/Latinx, and Other), and education. Clinical factors included self‐reported chronic conditions, including diabetes, heart disease, lung disease, cancer, hypertension, and stroke. Functional measures included difficulty (yes/no) in performing six Activities of Daily Living (ADLs) (bathing, dressing, transferring, toileting, eating, walking across a room) and difficulty (yes/no) performing five Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADLs) (using a phone, managing finances, taking medications, shopping for groceries, and preparing hot meals). Cognitive impairment included Cognitive Impairment Not Dementia (CIND) or Dementia using the previously validated Langa–Weir methodology [22]. A 27‐point summary score was derived from 3 items: (1) immediate and delayed 10‐noun free recall, (2) serial sevens, and (3) counting backwards test. Respondents were grouped as Normal (12–27 points), Cognitively Impaired but not Dementia (CIND) (7–11 points), and Dementia (0–6 points) based on diagnostic information from the HRS Aging, Demographics, and Memory Study cohort.

Statistical Analysis

We conducted separate multivariable logistic regression analyses to determine the association between each proxy‐reported end‐of‐life symptom and loneliness or social isolation while accounting for sociodemographic and health covariates. The primary cohort was used for loneliness (N = 2385), while the LBQ cohort (N = 800) with the extended measure of social isolation was used for social isolation. The sociodemographic and health covariates included: age, gender, race and ethnicity, marital status, and college education as a proxy for socioeconomic status. These covariates were chosen based on previous determinants of loneliness and social isolation [3, 17]; other correlates of physical health (e.g., chronic diagnoses) were omitted to avoid multicollinearity with symptom burden [23]. Given the subjective nature of many of these symptoms, we performed sensitivity analyses using self‐reported symptoms instead of proxy‐reported symptoms to determine consistency of findings.

We used a p value cutoff of less than 0.05 to determine statistical significance. We did not adjust for multiple comparisons due to the exploratory nature of these analyses. We present adjusted probabilities of social isolation or loneliness from each regression model. All analyses included survey design weights to account for complex sampling and non‐responses. Analyses were performed with STATA 17.0 (using the margins command to convert odds ratios to adjusted probabilities) [24] and R version 4.4.1 for figures.

Results

The primary cohort included 2385 decedents, with an average age at last interview of 76 years (standard deviation 12). Approximately 50% were female, 81% identified as White, 11% Black, 5% Latino, 46% were married. Regarding health characteristics, 44% screened positive for either mild cognitive impairment or dementia (30% and 13% respectively), 44% had three or more co‐morbidities, with the most frequent co‐morbidities as hypertension (73%) and heart disease (49%). Within the primary cohort, approximately 27% (653) reported feelings of loneliness. The LBQ subset included 800 decedents with a similar demographic profile to the core, of which 19% (134) were socially isolated based on the extended social isolation measure (Table 1).

First, we used the primary cohort to explore the relationship between specific end‐of‐life symptoms and loneliness. After adjusting for demographic and clinical covariates, the following psychosocial and physical end‐of‐life symptoms had an associated higher adjusted probability of experiencing loneliness: depression (35% vs. 18%, p < 0.001), agitation (38% vs. 24%, p < 0.001), trouble with pain (30% vs. 20%, p < 0.001), fatigue (29% vs. 22%, p < 0.001), and drowsiness (32% vs. 25%, p = 0.02). Confusion and cough were near significance: cough (adjusted probability 29% vs. 24%, p = 0.08), confusion (29% vs. 24%, p = 0.06). In comparison, the following physical end‐of‐life symptoms had no significant difference in adjusted probabilities: poor appetite (27% vs. 25%, p = 0.3), vomiting (26% vs. 27%, p = 0.92), difficulty controlling limbs (28% vs. 26%, p = 0.32), difficulty breathing (28% vs. 25%, p = 0.23), and incontinence (27% vs. 26%, p = 0.75) (Figure 1, Table S2).

We conducted a sensitivity analysis restricting the sample to only those with data available in the LBQ cohort (N = 800) which demonstrated consistent results (Table S3). We then performed a sensitivity analysis comparing the 3‐item UCLA loneliness scale in the LBQ to the single item measure from the core. The 3‐item scale yielded similar results for depression and fatigue (adjusted probability 26% vs. 12.6%, p < 0.001 and 21% vs. 14.1%, p = 0.04, respectively). However, while a positive trend, there were no significant differences in the previously identified symptoms of agitation, drowsiness, or pain (Table S4).

Next, we focused on the LBQ cohort to characterize the relationship between end‐of‐life symptoms and social isolation. Multiple physical symptoms including difficulty breathing, drowsiness, and persistent cough had higher adjusted probabilities of social isolation (difficulty breathing: 22% vs. 14%, p = 0.03, drowsiness: 30% vs. 17%, p = 0.006, and cough: 24% vs. 15%, p = 0.007). Decedents with poor appetite, vomiting, difficulty controlling limbs, and incontinence did not have an associated increased likelihood of social isolation: poor appetite (adjusted probability 19% vs. 17%, p = 0.51), vomiting (15% vs. 19%, p = 0.51), difficulty controlling limbs (22% vs. 17%, p = 0.11), and incontinence (19% vs. 19%, p = 0.85) (Figure 1, Table S2).

As an exploratory analysis, we further identified the relationship between each symptom and respondents who reported only loneliness, only social isolation, both, or neither. We identified similar trends in the data as seen in our primary analysis. Additionally, we found that those experiencing both loneliness and social isolation tended to have more proxy reported symptom burden, although the small sample sizes limit interpretation (Table 2).

Sensitivity analyses using self‐reported symptoms prior to death rather than after‐death proxy reports revealed mostly consistent results with some exceptions (Table S5). For loneliness, similar, albeit stronger, associations were present between depression, pain, and fatigue and experiencing loneliness (adjusted probability for depression: 55% vs. 19%, pain: 32% vs. 22%, fatigue: 41% vs. 21% respectively, p < 0.001). In contrast to proxy‐reported symptoms, we found significant associations between loneliness and self‐reported incontinence at the end‐of‐life (adjusted probability 31% vs. 24%, p < 0.001), difficulty breathing (35% vs. 23%, p = 0.002), and cough (36% vs. 24%, p = 0.001). With respect to social isolation, self‐reported difficulty breathing had a similar association (adjusted probability 25% vs. 15%, p = 0.05), while self‐reported cough trended towards significance (adjusted probability 24% vs. 17%, p = 0.15).

Discussion

In a large, nationally representative sample of older adults, we examined the relationship between several end‐of‐life symptoms and social well‐being. Several proxy‐reported symptoms—including pain, depression, severe fatigue, drowsiness, and agitation—were associated with loneliness, while persistent cough, drowsiness, and/or difficulty breathing were strongly associated with social isolation. By investigating a wide array of physical and psychological symptoms, we highlight the importance of simultaneously exploring an individual's symptom burden in parallel with their goals for social connection. In this way, a provider can potentially address modifiable factors to improve an individual's overall well‐being near the end of their life.

Prior literature has identified an association between loneliness in the last 4 years of life and burden of end‐of‐life symptoms [25]. Literature on chronic symptom management has also shown that chronic pain, fatigue, and/or depression both predispose individuals to loneliness and, conversely, are also exacerbated by it [26, 27, 28, 29, 30]. We build on these findings in several ways. First, through focusing on a sample in the last year of life we provide confirmatory evidence of the linkage between end‐of‐life symptoms and loneliness. Second, we highlight the distinct pattern of end‐of‐life symptoms that are associated with loneliness versus social isolation which may prompt different interventions [3]. Third, we newly identify symptoms such as end‐of‐life agitation and cough that are associated with loneliness and social isolation, respectively.

Our findings were generally consistent with our hypothesis that symptoms with a strong psychosocial component (e.g., pain and depression) were associated with greater experiences of loneliness whereas physical symptoms (such as cough and difficulty breathing) had stronger associations with social isolation. The mechanisms behind these are likely multifactorial and could derive from a shared underlying process, directional or reciprocal relationships, or a combination of these. While the presented study was limited in its cross‐sectional design, a previously published longitudinal study suggested that loneliness could directly increase aggressive behaviors and emotional dysregulation [31, 32]. Alternatively, agitation behaviors could also amplify loneliness, perhaps as a result of difficulty socializing or surrounding social networks avoiding uncomfortable behaviors. In addition, we highlight persistent cough, difficulty breathing, and drowsiness at the end of life as potential contributors to social isolation. This may be because respiratory symptoms can make it harder to communicate or join activities due to physical discomfort, embarrassment, and/or stigma [25, 33, 34, 35]. Families may also withdraw from their loved ones due to difficulty communicating [36]. Taken together, these symptoms may be uniquely isolating and suggest the importance of addressing the social sequelae of these symptoms to improve quality of life at the end‐of‐life.

Sensitivity analyses with self‐reported symptoms had similar results to proxy‐reported; however, there were notable differences. For example, individuals who self‐reported a persistent cough experienced loneliness, whereas a similar association was not seen for proxy‐reported cough. Perhaps proxies were less aware of milder or subtle symptoms like cough. Additionally, self‐reported incontinence was associated with higher loneliness, whereas proxy‐reported incontinence was not. This may reflect differences in reporting and symptom awareness at the end of life. Proxy‐reported incontinence may be in the context of the end‐of‐life when people have decreased alertness and awareness of incontinence and so does not have the same social impact. In contrast, self‐reported incontinence may be a private experience less visible to proxies, which is more closely tied to loneliness due to stigma, embarrassment, or activity limitations. Differences in findings may also be related to differences in the timeframe of symptoms; due to interview question phrasing, self‐reported symptoms could have occurred up to 2 years prior to death, whereas proxy‐reports focus on symptoms in the last year of life. The proxy report may have been further influenced by the severity and visibility of the symptom more so than the individual reports.

In addition, we conducted sensitivity analyses comparing results from a 1‐item loneliness measure to a longer 3‐item UCLA loneliness scale; this yielded similar results, with some distinctions. Specifically, the single item measure identified differences in end‐of‐life agitation, drowsiness, and pain, which were not seen when using the extended loneliness scale. Multiple reasons may account for these differences. First, the single item question may reflect a broader measure of loneliness as it allowed respondents to self‐interpret the term “lonely” rather than guiding respondents through focused questions that tend to highlight the intimate relationship aspects of loneliness [13]. Second, the measures use different timeframes; the single item focuses specifically on symptoms in the previous 7 days whereas the 3‐item UCLA Scale does not reference a specific timeframe. As a result, the 1‐item measure may reflect more situational rather than chronic loneliness experiences. Third, the 3‐item UCLA Scale was only asked of a subset of the sample who completed the LBQ, reducing overall power.

There are multiple clinical implications for this study. First, clinicians should be aware of the potential link between specific end‐of‐life symptoms and unmet social needs at the end‐of‐life. For example, as part of the assessment of cough or difficulty breathing in a person who is seriously ill, clinicians could consider screening for and addressing co‐occurring social isolation. Similarly, in addressing common psychosocial end‐of‐life symptoms (e.g., depression, pain, or fatigue) clinicians may similarly address co‐occurring loneliness. In these contexts, interventions could include exploring an individual's goals for social connection, referrals to local community programs for volunteer visitors, recommending that family or friends coordinate a schedule for regular visits, or providing connection to online support groups. Clinicians should be further aware of potential tradeoffs between aggressive symptom treatment and impacts on social lives. For example, while opioids can alleviate severe pain, they may reduce alertness and limit ability to engage in activities. Time‐limited clinical encounters could benefit from a streamlined approach in which patient‐reported symptoms serve as triggers for further exploration of psychosocial needs as part of routine end‐of‐life care.

Our study has several limitations. First, end‐of‐life symptoms were determined by respondent proxies through after‐death interviews. This approach introduces the potential for recall bias, particularly given the subjective nature of many symptoms. However, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using symptoms, including pain, shortness of breath, fatigue, cough, and depression, self‐reported by the respondents prior to their death and had similar findings. Second, because of the interview format, feelings of loneliness, social isolation, and end‐of‐life symptoms may not have occurred at the same time, even though various reports were in relative proximity to the last year of life. Third, we used a single rather than an extended 3 or 11‐item measure of loneliness to maximize our sample size, and so we are unable to examine different dimensions of loneliness or severity. Lastly, this was a cross‐sectional study which limits the ability to draw causal relationships between the predictor and outcome variables, and the underlying relationship is likely multifaceted.

In conclusion, this study highlights the interplay between end‐of‐life symptoms, loneliness, and social isolation. The presence of such symptoms could serve as a prompt for healthcare providers to explore loneliness and social isolation in their patients and subsequently provide appropriate referrals or interventions.
