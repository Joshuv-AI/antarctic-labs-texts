Development and psychometric validation of a knowledge–attitudes–practices questionnaire for virtual reality training in tennis: a cross-sectional study among Chinese university students with tennis training experience
Authors: Rui Liu, Hazwani Ahmad Yusof, ZiMei Zhong, Wen Jie Wu, Rohayu Hami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAt present, there is no validated instrument to assess athletes’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) concerning virtual reality (VR) in sports training. This study developed and psychometrically validated a VR Tennis KAP questionnaire for Chinese university students with tennis training experience.MethodsA cross-sectional survey involving 480 collegiate tennis players was executed. The 30-item scale, developed through expert consultation and pilot testing, was subjected to reliability and validity evaluation utilising exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses (EFA/CFA).ResultsEFA revealed a three-factor structure accounting for 47.4% of variance. CFA verified exceptional model fit ($\chi^2/df = 1.08$; $CFI = 0.994$; $RMSEA = 0.013$). Internal consistency was excellent across all subscales (total α = 0.918). Participants with prior VR experience demonstrated significantly higher scores ($d = 0.72, p < .001$) than those without prior VR experience.ConclusionsThe questionnaire demonstrates strong psychometric properties, serving as a valid tool for assessing perceptions of VR technology in sports research and interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-026-01858-8.Keywords: Virtual reality training, Knowledge-attitudes-practices, Questionnaire validation, Psychometric properties, Confirmatory factor analysis

Introduction

Virtual reality (VR) technology has become a promising and new way to train for sports. It can create realistic environments that make it feel like you’re in real-life athletic situations and competitions [1–3]. The global VR market for sports applications has expanded substantially over the past decade, with continued growth anticipated as hardware becomes increasingly accessible, affordable, and technologically advanced [4, 5]. The application of VR in sports has expanded rapidly across multiple domains. There is growing evidence that it can help with skill acquisition, perceptual-cognitive training, improving decision-making, developing tactical awareness, and getting mentally ready for competition [6–10].

In racket sports, particularly tennis, VR training systems have been developed and implemented to enhance technical skills, tactical awareness, anticipatory performance, and competitive readiness [11–13]. Tennis poses distinct challenges and opportunities for virtual reality application due to its intricate perceptual-motor requirements, such as swift ball tracking, anticipatory timing informed by opponent body kinematics, and strategic decision-making under intense time constraints [14, 15]. Recent technological advancements have facilitated progressively authentic tennis simulations that replicate the fundamental kinematic and perceptual attributes of actual gameplay, encompassing realistic ball physics, opponent movement patterns, and court environments [16, 17]. Research indicates that VR-based tennis training can enhance anticipation skills, decrease response times, and improve decision-making accuracy in both novice and intermediate players [18–21].

Despite the growing interest and investment in VR-based sports training technologies, the successful implementation of such technology depends largely on athletes’ perceptions, acceptance, and readiness to adopt these innovations [22, 23]. Technology implementation research consistently demonstrates that even the most effective interventions will fail if end users are not receptive to or prepared for the technology [24, 25]. Research in technology adoption consistently demonstrates that users’ knowledge about technology capabilities, attitudes toward technology use, and behavioral intentions significantly influence actual technology adoption and sustained engagement [26, 27]. Understanding these psychological precursors to technology adoption is therefore essential for maximizing the potential benefits of VR in athletic training contexts.

The Knowledge-Attitudes-Practices (KAP) framework is a well-known and theoretically sound way to understand how people see and interact with new technologies, health behaviours, and educational interventions [28–30]. The KAP framework enables the concurrent evaluation of knowledge deficiencies, attitudinal predispositions, and behavioural preparedness, in contrast to technology acceptance models like TAM [31] or UTAUT [32]. This is especially pertinent for nascent training technologies such as VR, where factual comprehension, evaluative perspective, and practical readiness may each serve as distinct obstacles to adoption. Originally designed to evaluate health-related behaviours and readiness for intervention, the KAP framework has been effectively modified for technology acceptance research in various fields [33, 34]. In this framework, knowledge pertains to the factual comprehension of VR technology and its applications in sports contexts, encompassing awareness of VR capabilities, limitations, and potential advantages for athletic performance; attitudes include beliefs, evaluations, and emotional reactions toward VR training, such as perceived utility, enjoyment, and apprehensions; and practices signify behavioural intentions, actual engagement patterns, and adoption behaviours associated with VR systems [35, 36].

This three-part structure has been successfully used to study the use of technology in healthcare [37], the use of educational technology [38], and a number of other professional fields [39, 40]. It has also demonstrated predictive utility for actual behaviour change. The KAP framework offers a valid and theory-grounded approach to assessing VR readiness in sports because it looks at both cognitive (knowledge) and affective (attitudes) factors that affect behaviour. It also recognises the difference between what people say they want to do and what they actually do, and it gives useful information for designing interventions by finding specific knowledge gaps or attitudinal barriers that might make it harder for people to adopt [41].

There is currently no validated tool for measuring KAP toward VR technology in sports training settings. The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) [31] and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) [32] are examples of existing measures of technology acceptance that were made for general information technology settings. TAM and UTAUT are still widely used and useful for understanding how people adopt digital systems in general, but they may not be as useful for embodied training settings where domain-specific knowledge, physical immersion, and sport-specific self-efficacy are important to the user experience [42, 43]. Most technology acceptance frameworks, like TAM, focus on how useful and easy to use something is. But VR sport training adoption may also depend on how much you know about the sport and how familiar you are with immersive technology, which are not the main focus of traditional technology acceptance models. Studies that have used TAM-based tools in immersive training settings have found that traditional adoption models only partially explain how people act in these settings, especially when users have to use their bodies to interact with the system instead of just passively using it [44, 45]. This gap in measurement makes it hard for researchers to look at baseline perceptions, find barriers to adoption, figure out training needs, and see how well VR training works in controlled experimental designs. The KAP framework is more useful than TAM-derived measures because it looks at knowledge, evaluative attitudes, and behavioural readiness all at once. This lets practitioners figure out which specific aspect is the biggest barrier to VR adoption. People have said that the KAP framework suggests a linear progression from knowledge to attitude to behaviour, which may not always be true when it comes to adopting complex technology [28]. This study deals with this by treating the three dimensions as conceptually separate but empirically connected constructs instead of as stages.

China is an especially important place for this study because the country has put a lot of money into improving sports technology and training for athletes as part of its overall strategy for improving sports in the country [46, 47]. Chinese universities are becoming more and more places where new training technologies are tested. There is a growing interest in using VR in physical education classes and athletic training programs [48]. However, there is a lack of research on how Chinese athletes feel about VR-based training systems, and there are no validated Chinese-language tools for this. To make the most of the benefits of VR technology in Chinese sports education, it is important to understand these perceptions so that you can plan how to use it.

This study fills in the gap by creating and testing a bilingual (Chinese-English) VR Tennis KAP questionnaire. The specific goals were: (1) to create a content-valid KAP questionnaire for VR technology in tennis training by systematically generating items and having experts review them; (2) to use strict psychometric methods to test the questionnaire’s factor structure, reliability, and validity; and (3) to find out what the baseline KAP levels were among Chinese university students who had trained in tennis and see if there were any differences based on their previous VR experience. The validated tool will be the basis for more research on the use of VR in sports and will help professionals figure out if athletes are ready to train with VR before they start using it. Theoretically, by operationalising the KAP triad within VR-based sport training, this study extends the KAP framework beyond its traditional health and educational applications into the domain of immersive sport technology. The construct of VR readiness is reconceptualised as a tripartite psychological construct in which knowledge reflects cognitive representations of VR capabilities, attitudes capture affective and evaluative orientations toward VR adoption, and practices index behavioural-intentional readiness to engage with VR training environments. This reconceptualisation provides a theoretically grounded framework for understanding technology adoption in digital sport training systems. The questionnaire was applied in an unpublished companion RCT examining the psychological effects of VR tennis training [49], demonstrating its utility for baseline characterisation and contextual interpretation of intervention outcomes.

Methods

Study design and participants

The VR Tennis KAP questionnaire was tested using a cross-sectional survey design. The study took place at Hezhou University, which is in Hezhou City, Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, in southwestern China, from September to December 2025. The four-month data collection period made it possible to recruit a wide range of participants during the spring and summer academic terms, making sure that students at different points in their academic programs were included.

Undergraduate students who were 18 years old or older, enrolled in Physical Education or Sports Science programs, and had at least one semester of formal tennis lessons were eligible to participate. Students who had visual impairments that couldn’t be fixed and would make it impossible to use VR, or who had a history of photosensitive epilepsy or severe motion sickness, were not allowed to take part [50]. These exclusion criteria were established to safeguard participant safety and ensure data quality, given that these conditions may affect both VR tolerance and questionnaire response patterns.

Sample size determination followed established guidelines for factor analysis, which recommend a minimum of 10–15 participants per item for stable factor solutions [51, 52]. The final questionnaire had 30 questions, so at least 300 to 450 people had to take it. Enrolment of 500 participants was targeted to accommodate potential attrition and ensure sufficient sample size for stable factor analysis solutions. A total of 500 questionnaires were distributed to students enrolled in tennis classes at a single university via convenience sampling. Of these, 485 were returned (97% response rate), and 480 were deemed valid for analysis following the removal of five incomplete or ineligible responses. The full 30-item VR Tennis KAP Questionnaire (in both Chinese and English) is included as Supplementary Material so that it can be used in future research. Participants were chosen from physical education programs at one school using convenience sampling, which may limit the ability to apply the results to a larger group of student athletes. Future studies should use probability sampling across several universities.

Questionnaire development

The VR Tennis KAP questionnaire was created using a methodical, multi-step process that followed standard guidelines for developing scales [53, 54]. The first step was to make a list of 45 possible items based on a thorough review of the literature on VR in sports training, existing KAP tools in health and education, theories of technology acceptance, and talks with experts in VR technology and sports science. The initial item pool was sourced from previous VR training literature [1–3, 18, 19], technology acceptance studies [31, 32], and recognised KAP instruments in related fields [37, 38]. The items were meticulously crafted to pertain specifically to VR technology within tennis training contexts, ensuring clarity for participants lacking prior VR experience. Items were allocated into three theoretical subscales: Knowledge (factual comprehension of VR technology and its applications), Attitudes (beliefs, assessments, and emotional reactions towards VR training), and Practices (behavioural intentions, readiness to participate, and adoption preparedness).

We checked the content validity by having a panel of six experts review it. Two of them were VR technology experts who had worked with sports applications, two were sports science researchers who were experts in motor learning and training methods, and two were physical education teachers who had taught directly. Each expert rated all of the items on a 4-point scale for relevance (1 = not relevant to 4 = highly relevant) and clarity [55]. Items that had an Item-level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) of less than 0.78 were either changed based on feedback from experts or thrown out. After the expert review, 30 items were kept in the final questionnaire (10 items per subscale), and the Scale-level Content Validity Index averaging method (S-CVI/Ave) was 0.85.

All items kept used a 5-point Likert scale, with 1 being “Strongly Disagree” and 5 being “Strongly Agree.” To lessen the bias of acquiescence response, four items were reverse-scored: K9, K10, A9, and P8 [56]. To see if there was a link between prior VR experience and baseline KAP levels, it was recorded as a yes/no variable. The questionnaire was created in both Chinese and English through strict forward-backward translation methods [57]. Two bilingual experts translated all the items from English to Chinese on their own. Any differences were worked out through discussion, and a third bilingual expert translated the Chinese version back to English. The back-translation was checked against the original for semantic equivalence, and small changes were made to make sure that both languages were clear.

Statistical analysis

Given the exploratory nature of this newly developed instrument and the requirement to retain sufficient statistical power for stable latent variable estimation, the full sample (N = 480) was used for both EFA and CFA, consistent with precedents in psychometric validation research [58]]. The sample of 480 substantially exceeds the 300–500 case minimum recommended for stable CFA solutions [51]]. Sensitivity analyses on random 50:50 splits (n = 240 per subsample) confirmed equivalent three-factor structures, supporting solution robustness. We acknowledge that independent-sample cross-validation would provide stronger confirmatory evidence and is recommended for future replication studies. The sample size is much larger than the 300–500 cases that are recommended for stable CFA solutions [58]. Using separate samples in future studies should confirm the factor structure to provide formal cross-validation evidence. Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was conducted using Principal Axis Factoring with Varimax rotation. Although inter-factor correlations in the final solution ranged from 0.54 to 0.68, Varimax rotation was retained because it produced cleaner, more interpretable factor structure with no cross-loadings ≥ 0.30 and is consistent with prior KAP instrument validation studies. Sensitivity analyses confirmed that oblique rotation (Direct Oblimin) yielded equivalent factor assignments with loading differences < 0.05, supporting the robustness of the Varimax solution. Before extraction, the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity checked to see if the sample was good enough. We used several convergent criteria to decide whether to keep a factor: eigenvalues greater than 1.0 (the Kaiser criterion), scree plot examination, parallel analysis [59], and theoretical interpretability.

After that, Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was done on the whole sample (N = 480) using maximum likelihood estimation to check the factor structure that EFA had found. The indices mentioned above were used to see how well the model fit. We used several indices with set cutoffs to see if the model fit: χ²/df ratio (< 3.0 acceptable, < 2.0 good), Comparative Fit Index (CFI > 0.90), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI > 0.90), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA < 0.08, with 90% CI reported), and Standardised Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR < 0.08) [60, 61].

We used Cronbach’s alpha coefficients (threshold > 0.70) on the full sample and Composite Reliability (CR > 0.70) from CFA factor loadings to check for reliability. Average Variance Extracted (AVE > 0.50) was used to test convergent validity. This means that factors capture more variance from their indicators than from error. Discriminant validity was evaluated by comparing the square root of the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) with inter-factor correlations; √AVE must surpass all correlations to ensure sufficient discrimination [62]. Independent samples t-tests analysed the disparities in KAP scores between participants possessing and lacking prior VR experience, accompanied by the computation of Cohen’s d effect sizes. We used SPSS version 27.0 and Mplus version 8.0 for all of the analyses, and we set the level of statistical significance at α = 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The Human Research Ethics Committee (Jawatankuasa Etika Penyelidikan Manusia) at Universiti Sains Malaysia gave their permission for the research (JEPeM Code: USM/JEPeM/PP/25030255). Everyone who took part signed a form saying they understood what they were doing before filling out the questionnaire. Students were told that they could leave at any time without penalty or affecting their academic standing, and participation was completely voluntary. All of the data were collected without anyone knowing who they were and stored safely on university computers that only research team members could access with a password. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and the Malaysian Code of Responsible Conduct in Research.

Results

Participant characteristics

The final analysed sample included 480 participants, consisting of 299 males (62.3%) and 181 females (37.7%), with a mean age of 21.07 years (SD = 1.93, range: 18–26). There were 98 freshmen (20.4%), 132 sophomores (27.5%), 142 juniors (29.6%), and 108 seniors (22.5%) among the participants. The majority of participants (89.2%) were enrolled in Physical Education programmes, while the rest (10.8%) were in Sports Science programs. Participants indicated an average of 2.8 semesters (SD = 1.35, range: 1–8) of formal tennis instruction.

In terms of past VR experience, 163 participants (34.0%) said they had used VR technology before (coded as a binary variable: yes = 1, no = 0), while 317 participants (66.0%) said they had never used VR before. Among participants with prior VR experience, 72.6% reported primary use for gaming or entertainment, 18.5% for sport-related purposes, and 8.9% for educational purposes. This distribution reflects the current VR usage landscape in China, where consumer gaming applications predominate over specialised training contexts.

Content validity

The expert panel review showed strong evidence that the content was valid. The Scale-level Content Validity Index using the averaging method (S-CVI/Ave) was 0.85, which is higher than the acceptable level of 0.80 [55]. The Individual Item-level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) values ranged from 0.83 to 1.00. All of the items that were kept met the minimum acceptable threshold of 0.78. Expert feedback led to small changes in the wording of six items to make them clearer without changing the main content. This made sure that all participants, no matter how much VR experience they had, would be able to understand the items.

Exploratory factor analysis

Preliminary analyses validated the data’s appropriateness for factor analysis. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure of sampling adequacy was 0.948, which Kaiser [63] said was “marvellous” adequacy. Bartlett’s test of sphericity was statistically significant (χ²(435) = 5309.87, p < .001), which meant that the correlations between items were big enough for meaningful factor extraction.

EFA showed a clear three-factor solution that explained 47.4% of the total variance. This was backed up by several convergent retention criteria. The Kaiser criterion (eigenvalues > 1) showed that there were three factors with eigenvalues of 7.83, 3.45, and 2.94. The examination of the scree plot (Fig. 1) revealed a distinct elbow following the third factor, and parallel analysis validated that merely three factors surpassed chance-level eigenvalues derived from random data. Factor 1 (Knowledge) added 26.1% of the difference, Factor 2 (Attitudes) added 11.5%, and Factor 3 (Practices) added 9.8%. All 30 items had primary factor loadings between 0.513 and 0.737 on their theoretically assigned factors, which is higher than the recommended minimum of 0.40 for keeping items [51]. There were no cross-loadings higher than 0.30, which confirms simple structure. The item communalities were between 0.339 and 0.575. Table 1 shows the full factor loading matrix, which includes all cross-loadings that are greater than or equal to.30. Harman’s single-factor test [64] showed that the first unrotated factor explained only 29.8% of the total variance. This means that common method variance is not likely to have a big effect on the factor structure. This test is a necessary but not sufficient diagnostic for excluding common method bias; however, the low first-factor variance offers preliminary evidence against significant common method variance in the current data. To supplement this, a common latent factor (CLF) analysis was conducted in Mplus by adding an unmeasured latent variable with paths to all 30 indicators. The average common method variance estimate across items was 7.3%, substantially below the 25% threshold indicative of serious bias [64]], further corroborating the validity of the factor structure.

Confirmatory factor analysis

CFA on the complete sample (N = 480) validated the three-factor model established via EFA (Fig. 2). Model fit indices showed that the model fit the data very well: χ²(402) = 433.64, p = .133; χ²/df = 1.08; CFI = 0.994; TLI = 0.993; RMSEA = 0.013 (90% CI: 0.000, 0.028); SRMR = 0.051. The chi-square value of 0.133, which is not significant, shows that the model-implied covariance matrix was not significantly different from the observed structure, which is consistent with a good fit. The CFI and TLI values were all over 0.99, which is much higher than the recommended 0.90 level. The RMSEA value of 0.013 with an upper CI of 0.028 is well below the 0.05 level for close fit [65]. All standardised factor loadings were statistically significant (p < .001) and ranged from 0.515 to 0.727, which is higher than the recommended level of 0.40 [51]. The correlations between the factors were 0.62 (Knowledge-Attitudes), 0.54 (Knowledge-Practices), and 0.68 (Attitudes-Practices). This means that the three constructs are somewhat related but are not the same thing.

Reliability and validity

All of the subscales and the total scale had very good internal consistency reliability (Table 2). Cronbach’s alpha values varied from 0.862 (Practices) to 0.884 (Knowledge), yielding a total scale reliability of 0.918, all significantly surpassing the 0.70 threshold [66]. McDonald’s omega (ω) coefficients, which give a more reliable estimate of reliability than Cronbach’s alpha, ranged from ω = 0.87 to ω = 0.90 across subscales (ω_total = 0.92). This supports the alpha-based estimates. The CFA factor loadings gave us Composite Reliability values between 0.87 and 0.91. This is more proof that the method is reliable through a different way of estimating it. The Average Variance Extracted values for all subscales—Knowledge (0.52), Attitudes (0.56), and Practices (0.51)—were higher than 0.50, which shows that the factors capture more variance from their indicators than from measurement error. Using the Fornell-Larcker criterion [62], we were able to confirm discriminant validity. The square root of the AVE for each factor (Knowledge = 0.72, Attitudes = 0.75, Practices = 0.71) was higher than all of the inter-factor correlations (Knowledge-Attitudes: r = .62; Knowledge-Practices: r = .54; Attitudes-Practices: r = .68). This shows that each factor is more closely related to its own indicators than to other factors. Table 3 shows the inter-factor correlation matrix, which has √AVE on the diagonal.

Baseline KAP assessment

The baseline KAP assessment of the entire sample showed that people had mostly neutral opinions about using VR technology in sports training (Table 2; see Fig. 3 for score distributions). The average KAP score was 2.99 (SD = 0.76) on a 5-point scale, which is close to the theoretical midpoint of 3.0. The Attitudes subscale had the highest mean (M = 3.02, SD = 0.96), followed by the Practices subscale (M = 3.01, SD = 0.92) and the Knowledge subscale (M = 2.94, SD = 0.98). The score distributions were close to normal because the skewness and kurtosis values were within acceptable ranges. There were no floor or ceiling effects, and the scores covered the whole scale. Independent samples t-tests indicated significant differences between participants with and without prior VR experience across all subscales (Table 2; see Fig. 4). Participants with prior VR experience demonstrated significantly higher scores across all subscales: Knowledge (d = 0.78, large effect), Attitudes (d = 0.62, medium effect), Practices (d = 0.52, medium effect), and total KAP (d = 0.72, η² = 0.10, medium-to-large effect). All group differences were statistically significant (p < .001).

Discussion

This study developed and validated a novel sport-specific psychometric instrument, among the few available tools specifically designed to assess knowledge, attitudes, and behavioral readiness toward VR technology in sports training contexts. The VR Tennis KAP questionnaire demonstrated promising psychometric properties across multiple indicators, providing researchers and practitioners with a reliable and valid tool for baseline assessment, needs analysis, and intervention evaluation in VR sports training research. The rigorous validation process, including separate exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses with sensitivity checks confirming factor structure stability, strengthens confidence in the generalizability of the factor structure.

The three-factor structure is consistent with theoretical conceptualisations of the KAP framework and was strongly supported by both exploratory and confirmatory analyses. Sensitivity analyses confirmed equivalent factor structures across random subsamples. According to Kaiser’s [63] criteria, a KMO value of 0.948 means that the sample size is “marvellous,” which means that the correlation matrix is very good for factor analysis. The CFA model fit indices showed that the model fit very well [60, 61]. The CFI was 0.994 and the TLI was 0.993, both of which were higher than 0.99. The RMSEA was 0.013 (90% CI [0.000, 0.028]), which means that the model fit much better than the 0.05 benchmark. The chi-square value of 0.133, which is not significant, further shows that the model structure fits the data well. The evaluation of common method bias using Harman’s single-factor test [64] revealed that the initial unrotated factor represented merely 29.8% of the total variance, significantly below the 50% criterion [64]. While this test is a requisite yet insufficient diagnostic, the minimal first-factor variance indicates that common method variance is improbable to significantly distort the factor structure. The explained variance (47.4%) is a little lower than the 50% threshold that is often used, but similar levels have been found in studies that validate KAP instruments [67, 68]. This level of explained variance is normal for multi-dimensional behavioural perception scales, where each dimension captures qualitatively different aspects of the construct. The value is similar to or higher than that of other validated KAP tools in the fields of health technology and behaviour change. This suggests that the questionnaire captures a lot of meaningful variation in how people think about VR.

The internal consistency reliability was very high, with Cronbach’s alpha values between 0.862 and 0.918. These values substantially exceed the widely accepted threshold of 0.70 [66] and are consistent with recent high-quality psychometric validations in sport technology contexts [20]; Shields et al., 2025 [25]] and are similar to or higher than other validated technology acceptance measures like the Technology Acceptance Model scales [31] and UTAUT instruments [20, 21, 32]. The instrument’s construct validity is even stronger because the convergent validity (AVE > 0.50) and discriminant validity (√AVE > inter-factor correlations) show that the three subscales measure related but different aspects of VR perceptions [62].

The baseline assessment revealed that Chinese university students with tennis training experience held predominantly neutral perceptions of VR technology. The overall mean (M = 2.99) was close to the theoretical scale midpoint. This result suggests that the target group is neither very open to nor resistant to VR training. These findings indicate a potentially receptive context for educational interventions and practical demonstrations. The Attitudes subscale yielded the highest mean score among the three dimensions, suggesting that pre-existing positive affective orientations toward VR may be leveraged in implementation efforts. The Practices subscale measures behavioural readiness and intention to engage with VR, not habitual use of VR. This is because VR was still new to Chinese university tennis programs at the time of data collection. This interpretation makes sense because the participants didn’t have many chances to build up their established VR practice habits. Scores therefore reflect readiness for VR training rather than current engagement, consistent with the early stage of VR integration in Chinese university tennis programmes. Future validation studies in settings where VR programmes are already operational may benefit from including items assessing frequency and duration of actual VR use. Future research should (a) confirm the factor structure using independent samples from multiple institutions and cultural contexts; (b) examine predictive validity of KAP scores against objective VR adoption metrics and training adherence; and (c) investigate longitudinal KAP trajectories following VR training programmes. Preliminary evidence for predictive utility derives from an unpublished companion randomised controlled trial in which participants in the higher-KAP tertile at baseline demonstrated greater responsiveness to VR training [49], suggesting that the instrument may serve as a useful screening tool in future intervention research.

Notably, participants with prior VR experience demonstrated substantially higher KAP scores across all dimensions, with a medium-to-large overall effect size (d = 0.72). This finding has significant practical implications: offering immersive VR experiences, even short introductory sessions, may be an effective approach to enhance perceptions and foster technology adoption. The largest effect was observed for the Knowledge subscale (d = 0.78), indicating that direct engagement with VR technology may enhance factual comprehension and reduce misconceptions. This pattern aligns with experiential learning theory, which asserts that direct experience serves as a significant catalyst for knowledge acquisition and attitude transformation [69]. Accordingly, practitioners implementing VR-based training programmes may consider incorporating structured demonstration sessions or trial periods to facilitate familiarisation with the technology prior to the commencement of formal training.

When interpreting these findings, it is important to recognise several limitations. First, participants were drawn from a single university in southern China, potentially constraining the generalisability of the findings to other regions, competitive athlete demographics, or cultural contexts characterised by varying degrees of technological exposure and sports training traditions. Subsequent research ought to authenticate the questionnaire across varied samples, encompassing elite athletes, youth sports participants, and demographics from other nations. Second, the questionnaire evaluates self-reported perceptions instead of actual behaviours or objective performance outcomes; subsequent research should investigate the predictive validity of KAP scores for genuine VR adoption, sustained engagement, and training results. Third, although the bilingual development process adhered to stringent translation protocols, additional validation in predominantly English-speaking cohorts is necessary to confirm cross-cultural equivalence and measurement invariance. Fourth, the cross-sectional design does not allow for causal inferences; longitudinal studies could investigate how KAP evolves after VR training interventions and whether initial KAP serves as a predictor of intervention outcomes. Fifth, both EFA and CFA were conducted on the same sample; although sensitivity analyses confirmed structural consistency across random subsamples, independent-sample cross-validation would provide stronger construct validation evidence. Sixth, the study did not assess test–retest reliability; future studies should administer the questionnaire at a two- to four-week interval and compute intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) to establish temporal stability. Seventh, potential moderating variables—including gender, sports qualification level, and frequency of prior VR exposure—were not examined; future studies should investigate whether KAP factor structures and baseline scores vary systematically across these groups. Eighth, Varimax (orthogonal) rotation was retained for interpretability; although sensitivity analyses confirmed equivalent factor assignments under oblique rotation (Direct Oblimin), future studies with larger samples should formally test the assumption of factor independence.

Conclusions

The VR Tennis KAP questionnaire is a psychometrically sound 30-item instrument for assessing knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding VR technology in sports training contexts. The questionnaire exhibits strong internal consistency (total α = 0.918), robust convergent and discriminant validity, and a theoretically coherent three-factor structure aligned with the established KAP framework, collectively endorsing its application in both research and applied contexts. The neutral baseline KAP levels observed among Chinese university students with tennis training experience indicate that specific educational interventions and practical VR experiences may be essential to enhance comprehension and facilitate the adoption of VR training technologies. The validated questionnaire serves as a significant foundation for forthcoming research on VR acceptance, predictors of adoption, and the efficacy of interventions in athletic training environments. It aids practitioners in pinpointing specific knowledge deficiencies or attitudinal obstacles that must be addressed prior to the implementation of VR-based training programs.
