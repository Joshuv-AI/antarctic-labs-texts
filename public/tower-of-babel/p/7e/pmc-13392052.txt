Transcriptomic and lipidomic analysis of the cryptococcal lung granuloma reveals macrophage boundary programs and sphingolipid remodeling
Authors: Veronica Soares Brauer, Kathryn Takemura, Barbara Rosati, Marinaldo Pacífico Cavalcanti-Neto, Deveney Dasilva, Neelu Singh, Tyler Normile, Gabriella Vaccaro, Giuseppe Caso, Harpreet Singh, Bo Chen, Iwao Ojima, David McKinnon, Maurizio Del Poeta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCryptococcosis is a life-threatening fungal disease caused by Cryptococcus neoformans. The immunocompetent response results in the formation of a lung granuloma, which contains the fungal cells. We have previously developed a mouse model of the cryptococcal granuloma that is histologically similar to the human lung granuloma. In this study, we characterize the lung granuloma in mice using transcriptomics and lipidomics, elucidating the immune cell types present and the transcriptional landscape of macrophages at the granuloma border. Macrophage subtypes are identified, which form a characteristic boundary of the lung granuloma and were observed to have differentially expressed genes involved in the control of the infection. Lipidomic studies revealed that several sphingolipid-associated genes and their products involved in the regulation of phagocytosis were found to be upregulated and in vivo validation studies confirmed their key role in granuloma formation. Lastly, as the granuloma structure develops, the fungal replication inside the granuloma decreases concomitantly to the decrease of the macrophage activation. These studies identified important immune mechanisms of host protection at the granuloma site. Our results provide mechanistic insight into cryptococcal granuloma maturation and refine current models of host–pathogen interaction, which may open avenues for future immunomodulatory strategies.Subject terms: Fungal host response, Gene expression

Introduction

Cryptococcosis is a life-threatening fungal disease caused by Cryptococcus neoformans, an environmental fungal pathogen that infects humans via the respiratory tract1–4. Due to its ubiquitous nature, human subjects are exposed to this fungus during early childhood and upon this primary infection, the host harbors fungal cells in a lung granuloma which is found in almost all cases of human pulmonary cryptococcosis5,6. In previous studies, we have developed a mouse model of lung cryptococcal granuloma that is histologically similar to the human lung granuloma7,8. However, the host immune cells involved in the granuloma formation, their overall organization, and their transcriptional phenotypes during the granuloma formation and maturation and how fungal and host cells are co-existing inside these lesions are not known.

Histology analysis of our mouse lung cryptococcal granuloma revealed that the lesions are characterized by the presence of a central, round necrotic area, where fungal cells are mostly localized. This area is surrounded by a characteristic ring of giant (100–150 µm) multinucleated macrophages, which often contain phagocytosed cryptococcal cells. This macrophage ring is surrounded by additional cells, such as mononuclear macrophages, lymphocytes, neutrophils and fibroblasts. Collagen is deposited on the outside of the lesion, leading to the characteristic color and opacity, while the surrounding lung tissue appears totally normal7.

In addition to recapitulating the human granuloma structure, our mouse model closely mimics the progression of human cryptococcosis upon immunosuppression. In fact, when these mice become immunocompromised, either by depleting both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells9, or by administering corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive drugs (e.g., fingolimod)8, the fungus escapes the lung granuloma and disseminates to the brain, thereby causing a lethal meningo-encephalitis. This dissemination upon immunosuppression represents the predisposing conditions at risk of developing cryptococcal meningo-encephalitis in human subjects10,11. Thus, our mouse model mimics a latent cryptococcal infection and probing the phenotypic characteristics during the granuloma formation and maturation are critical for understanding the overall physiology and homeostasis of how fungal and host cells are co-existing inside this lesion.

In this study, we performed single nuclei RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics to identify the cellular organization and the overall gene expression of the lung cryptococcal granuloma resulting from infection with the Δgcs1 mutant strain of C. neoformans. The C. neoformans Δgcs1 strain lacks glucosylceramide synthase 1 (Gcs1) and therefore, its product, glucosylceramide, a lipid responsible for neutral/alkaline tolerance in fungi7,12. This mutation results in an avirulent strain and the host responds with the formation of a fully mature lung granuloma. Importantly, the Δgcs1 mutant closely replicates the behavior of C. neoformans in the human host aside from the formation of a granuloma, including the reactivation upon immunosuppression, including immunosuppression caused by fingolimod, as well as the dissemination of the fungus to the brain8,11. As a control, we used the highly virulent C. neoformans wild-type H99 strain, which is a parent strain of C. neoformans Δgcs1, and does not produce any granulomatous response in mice. Our analysis identified macrophages that are key in the confinement of cryptococcal cells within the necrotic area of the granuloma. These host cells differentially express genes that govern the pro-migration, pro-fusion, pro-proliferation and pro-M1 host response. We then validated these data by targeted lipidomics using liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LC-MS) and matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI) analysis of the cryptococcal lung granuloma. Interestingly, several sphingolipid-metabolizing genes expressed by macrophages, whose products are essential to regulate phagocytosis and fungal killing, were found to be upregulated in these cells and the increase of their direct sphingolipid products was confirmed via LC-MS and MALDI imaging mass spectrometry. The deletion of some of these sphingolipid genes resulted in the lack of granuloma formation. Lastly, as the granuloma matures, the macrophage nuclear fusion indices decrease concomitantly to the decrease of the budding index of fungal cells entrapped within the granuloma, which may suggest that fungal cells and host cells are adapting to co-exist withing the lung granuloma.

Results

Single nuclei RNA sequencing analysis reveals cell types involved in lung granuloma

Mouse cell types were analyzed from the lung samples of each experimental group; uninfected (UN), C. neoformans wild-type H99-infected (WT) collected at 15-days post infection, 30-days post C. neoformans Δgcs1-infected (G30), 60-days post Δgcs1-infected (G60), and 90-days post Δgcs1-infected (G90).

For G30, G60 and G90, granulomas were identified macroscopically (Supplementary Fig. S1) and excised from the lung. Twenty-four granulomas weighing ~25 mg (8 per mouse from 3 separate mice) were combined and processed for snRNA-seq for G30 and G60, respectively. Nine granulomas weighing ~25 mg (3 per mouse from 3 mice) were combined and processed for snRNA-seq for G90. For WT, inflamed tissues were identified and excised ( ~ 25 mg), whereas for UN, a similar amount of normal tissue ( ~ 25 mg) was excised, and both were processed for snRNA-seq.

Infection had a substantial effect on the number and type of immune cells present in the lung. Cell type classification was determined using a hierarchical cell type ontology (Supplementary Table S1) to ensure comparisons were made between equivalent cell types and that cell types unique to each condition were reliably identified. This ontology was developed from multiple sources13–21, most directly from the LungMAP ontology21. Distinctions between cell types were not always as definitive in the terminal branches of the hierarchy. All cell type annotations are listed in Supplementary Table S2, and cell type assignment is discussed below in the context of each major lineage, with specific markers being listed in Supplementary Table S3.

As expected, there was a large increase in the fraction of immune system nuclei in the infected lung samples (WT, G30, G60, G90) relative to the uninfected control (UN) as a percentage of the total nuclei (Fig. 1). The myeloid fraction in particular was elevated in the infected samples other than G90 compared to UN. These increases were accompanied by the appearance of new or modified cell types. Five types of mononuclear myeloid cell types were distinguished in UN: alveolar macrophages (AM), complement component 1q expressing macrophage (M-C1q), inflammatory monocytes (iMon), classical dendritic cells (DC), and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC). In both WT and Δgcs1-infected groups, two macrophage types, AM and M-C1q, together with the monocyte iMon were found in a single large continuous cluster. These three cell types remained reasonably distinct in the infected samples.

In the WT and Δgcs1-infected samples, two transitional phenotypes that were distinct from those found in UN were identified: macrophage transitional type 1 (M-t1) and macrophage transitional type 2 (M-t2). There were few or no specific markers for either of these cell types. The M-t1 transcriptional phenotype overlapped with the AM cells and the M-t2 phenotype overlapped with both the M-C1q and iMon cell types. Expansion of macrophages with transitional phenotypes has been described in a previous study of lung pathology unrelated to fungal infection13, but unlike this previous study the two intermediate phenotypes did not seem to form a true gradient between alveolar macrophages and monocyte-derived macrophages. Instead, the M-t1and M-t2 phenotypes remained somewhat distinct, either primarily AM or M-C1q/iMon related.

One additional subset of macrophage was found only in G30 (Macrophage low counts, M-lc). The nuclei of these cells had generally similar properties to the transitional M-t1 phenotype but were distinguished by a relatively low number of expressed genes represented in the individual cells and lower counts per cell relative to the other cell types found in these samples. The presence of low count nuclei in the G30 sample with clear macrophage phenotype may indicate a relatively high number of dying macrophages are present at this time point and that we are capturing some of these cells at a relatively early phase in their cell death process.

Overall, the macrophage cell types present in the Δgcs1-infected samples were not significantly different than the cells present in the WT-infected samples. This suggests that the granuloma is not produced by the presence or absence of a particular cell type.

Differential gene expression of macrophages

Focusing on the four consistently expressed macrophage cell types (AM, M-C1q, M-t1, M-t2) in the infected groups, we performed differential gene expression analysis to identify genes that may be involved in the formation or/and maturation of the cryptococcal granuloma by comparing G30 vs WT to probe what genes are involved in the protective phenotype (G30) versus the non-protective phenotype (WT). We also tracked the transcriptional changes as the granuloma matures by comparing G30 vs. G60, G60 vs. G90 and G30 vs. G90.

We found that for each of the macrophage cell types, the largest amount of differentially expressed genes outside of comparison to the uninfected control are observed when comparing the G30 (protective) versus WT (non-protective) samples (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. S2). Unexpectedly, comparisons between G60 or G90 vs. G30 show a decrease in the number of differentially expressed genes as the granuloma matures. This may suggest that the transcriptional signature governing the granuloma initiates early, during the initial organization of the granuloma, and less differential regulation is required once the granuloma is fully formed.

A curated list of pro-formation and pro-function genes that are significantly differentially expressed are shown in Fig. 2, organized by broadly defined function with respect to the macrophages involved in the granuloma (i.e., proliferation, migration, M1-immunity and fusion). Three pro-migration genes (Mmp2, Ptk2 and Src) were found to be significantly (log2 fold change > 0.5 and padj < 0.001) up-regulated in the M-t1, M-t2 and AM cell types in G30 compared to WT. After this 30-days post infection (d.p.i.) timepoint, these pro-migration genes are either decreasingly up-regulated in the case of AM cells, not differentially expressed in the case of M-t2 or significantly down-regulated in the case of M-t1. The initial up-regulation followed by a decreasing or lack of differential expression suggests the relatively early formation of the granuloma by 30-d.p.i., which is also observed in the gross anatomy of the Δgcs1-infected lung at this time point (Supplementary Fig. S1), in which the granuloma can be seen macroscopically.

Few pro-proliferation genes (Mki67, Birc3, and Dusp5) were found to be differentially expressed and, similarly to the pro-migration genes, were for the most part down-regulated past the 30 d.p.i. timepoint. One exception was Dusp5, which was up-regulated in G30, followed by down-regulation (Fig. 2).

One of the key components of the granuloma is the presence of Langhans giant cells, which are multi-nucleated cells produced by the fusion of macrophages or monocytes. Dcstamp, among others, has been shown to be a critical gene in the fusion process for these giant cells22. There were many differentially expressed pro-fusion genes which were found to be variably regulated across the samples (Fig. 2). However, Dcstamp in particular was observed to be strongly up-regulated in both AM (log2 fold change: 5.27, padj: 3.1 × 10−8) and M-C1q (log2 fold change: 2.79, padj: 5.5 × 10−7) cell types in G30 compared to WT, followed by no differential expression at further timepoints (Fig. 2).

The pro-M1 immunity associated genes follow a similar pattern as the pro-migration genes, in which there is an initial up-regulation when comparing the G30 and WT groups, followed by down-regulation in further timepoints (Fig. 2). From previous work, it is well known that an active M1 immunity is required to control cryptococcosis23–26. However, our data suggests that the M1 immune function is primarily active in the early time points of the granuloma initiation/formation and not a continuously up-regulated function once the granuloma has formed. This is also supported with analysis using CellChat27 to probe immune-related signaling. Although they are not identical, there is significant similarity between the communicating cell types in the G30 (Fig. 3A) and WT (Fig. 3B) samples, with cross-talk between macrophage types and incoming signaling from various cells. Interestingly, the highest calculated communication probabilities with the macrophages for both G30 and WT are from pDC cells through the Lair1-Lilrb4a pathway (Fig. 3D). Both the number and weight of the immune-related communications decrease between the G30 and G90 (Fig. 3C).

Single-nuclei RNA sequencing-informed spatial transcriptomics

While the single-nuclei RNA sequencing data provided key insights into differential gene expression in the cryptococcal granuloma, it naturally lacks the spatial resolution of where these genes are expressed within the analyzed sample. This information is essential because the granuloma is a definitionally localized response to infection, and to date the transcriptional profile of the cryptococcal granuloma has never been discussed with spatial resolution. Using the snRNA-seq data to inform both the cell identities and the probed genes through 10x’s label transfer guide, we performed spatial transcriptomics analysis on each experimental group.

Before any gene-expression analysis, we first explored the cellular organization of the granulomatous response. Broadly, the Δgcs1-infected (Fig. 4 A-C) samples show round areas of inflammation, compared to the wide-spread inflammation observed in WT-infected sample or the lack of inflammation in the uninfected control. The progression of the granulomatous response shows interesting organizational and transcriptional differences. While there was no significant difference in the total myeloid fraction among G30, G60 and G90, there were differences in the types of macrophages observed. Because M-lc was only observed in the G30 single-nuclei RNA sequencing data and each single-nuclei RNA sequencing data set was used to inform the cell types in the corresponding spatial transcriptomics data, the M-lc cell type is only observed in G30. The M-lc cell type is by far the most abundant in the G30 sample, whereas in G60 and G90 the ratios of macrophage types are more similar to that of the WT infection (Fig. 4).

Because Langhans giant cells are an important component of the granuloma and these were missed in the snRNA-seq data, we also determined the average area of each cell type. There were significant increases in average cell areas in M-t1 and M-t2 (Supplementary Table S4). Beyond the type and size of the cells present in the granuloma, the spatial organization of the cells also differed across time points post Δgcs1-infection. As shown in Fig. 4, the G30 granuloma is generally disorganized, with many cell types, including alveolar fibroblasts (AF2), macrophages, and lymphoid cells being observed throughout the area. In contrast, the mature G90 granuloma shows a clear gradient of cell types, with B2 cells nearer to the central necrotic area, macrophages nearer to the border of the granuloma, and N2 cells interspersed between. The mature G90 granuloma also has a clear tissue delimiter containing AF2 cells, amongst others. The transcriptional density is also markedly different between the immature and mature granulomas, with the G30 sample showing generally higher transcriptional density. G90 instead has a gradient of transcriptional density, with lower density near the central necrotic area and higher transcriptional density nearer to the border of the granuloma.

With the differentially expressed genes from the snRNA-seq data in hand, we proceeded to quantify transcripts of interest in the spatial transcriptomics data. For this analysis, the number of transcripts for the genes of interest were quantified for multiple regions of inflammation within each experimental group. For each gene-cell type pair, the number of transcripts was normalized by the number of cells of that type within each region. As is shown in Fig. 5, there is a clear difference in the transcriptional organization in the WT and Δgcs1-infected samples, with the WT infection having a scattered transcriptional landscape and the Δgcs1-infection showing areas of higher transcriptional density near the granuloma. This data not only supports that the differentially expressed genes emerging from snRNA-seq data are in fact significant in the granuloma specifically, but also recapitulates the decreasing expression of pro-formation and pro-function genes in the granuloma over time observed in the snRNA-seq data.

For example, the pro-migration gene Mmp2 evolves from a very dense, globular area of transcription in G30, to a still quite dense but granuloma border-localized area in G60, to finally a much less dense but still border-localized area in G90. Similarly to the observations in the snRNA-seq data, the pro-proliferation genes were primarily up-regulated only in G30 and not at subsequent timepoints. Expression was again observed at the border of the granuloma and decreased at subsequent timepoints. The pro-M1 gene Ciita was highly expressed in both the WT and Δgcs1-infected samples, with only one macrophage type in G30 having a statistically significant difference to its WT counterpart (Fig. 5C). However, the spatial pattern of expression again emphasizes the importance of the organization of the granuloma in that Ciita is observed to be densely expressed specifically at the granuloma, while the WT-infected transcription of Ciita is broad and unorganized. Thus, a localized, rather than diffused, M1 activity seems to be essential for a successful containment of fungal cells within the granuloma.

Naturally, because they are different experiments and different samples, the spatial transcriptomics data is not identical to that of the snRNA-seq data, and instead complement each other. One interesting difference occurs in the pro-fusion genes; as discussed above, there are many differentially expressed pro-fusion genes, and Dcstamp in particular was observed in the snRNA-seq data to be strongly up-regulated in AM and M-C1q cells in G30 compared to WT and is subsequently unperturbed. However, with the spatial transcriptomics data we observe that while Dcstamp is up-regulated, it is up-regulated in the transitional macrophages and not the AM or M-C1q cell types. It should be noted that this may also be attributed to the label transfer process. We also observe other pro-fusion genes, such as Abcg1, to be up-regulated, and in fact more robustly expressed than Dcstamp (Supplementary Fig. S3). Abcg1 effects the lipid composition of the membrane, allowing for membrane fusion28. This, along with previous observation that membrane lipids regulate macrophage phagocytic index in response to immune function29–32, inspired a further investigation into other lipid-associated genes.

Sphingolipid-pathway genes are differentially expressed in granuloma cells

In previous studies, we found that sphingolipids, particularly sphingomyelin (SM), is a key regulator of the phagocytic process by macrophages30. Thus, in addition to the pro-migration, pro-proliferation, pro-fusion and pro-M1 genes, a variety of sphingolipid-associated genes were found to be up-regulated, in particular ceramide synthase (CerS) genes (Fig. 6). Whereas CerS1 is not differentially expressed, CerS2, CerS5, and particularly CerS6, were up-regulated across all infected samples compared to UN. In addition, CerS6 was particularly up-regulated in M-t1 at G30 and G60 versus WT infected mice (Fig. 6A).

CerS2 mainly produces very long chain ceramide ranging from C20 to C26, whereas CerS5 and CerS6 makes mainly C16 ceramide species33. When we analyzed these ceramide species by LC-MS, we found that they were respectively all up-regulated in the Δgcs1-infected samples, especially at G30 compared to G60 and G90 (Fig. 6B). Very interestingly, the G30 up-regulation of these ceramide species was particularly evident when comparing it with the WT infected sample, suggesting that the cells involved in the granuloma may preferentially synthesize these ceramide species.

These results were corroborated by the analysis of sphingomyelin (SM) species. Once produced, ceramide is converted to SM by sphingomyelin synthases 1 (Sgms1) and 2 (Sgms2). Transcriptomic data revealed that both Sgms genes were found to be up-regulated in the granuloma versus the WT or UN samples, and particularly in the M-t1 cell type (Fig. 6A) where CerS were up-regulated. LC-MS analysis confirmed the high abundance of these SM species, especially at G30 (Fig. 6B, C). LC-MS analysis also identified that whereas these SM species are highly abundant in G30, G60 and G90 (protective phenotype) they seem to diminish in the WT samples (non-protective phenotype) (Fig. 6C–G). Similarly, we also found an up-regulation of sphingosines, particularly at G30 (Fig. 6B), suggesting that this up-regulation is coordinated by the de-novo synthesis of the sphingolipid pathway.

To support these findings, we conducted sphingolipid MALDI analysis (Fig. 6H–L, R–V) of the granuloma compared to WT and UN samples. The products of CerS2 (C20-C26), CerS5 (C16) and CerS6 (C16) were all found to be up-regulated in the MALDI analysis (Fig. 6H–L) and the respective SM species as well (Fig. 6R–V). This behavior is especially apparent in the G30 sample, which has a high density of these SM species in the granuloma itself and is reflected in the z-scores. The average expression of the ceramide-associated transcripts is illustrated in Fig. 6W, showing that CerS6 and CerS5 are upregulated in G30 compared G60 and G90, particularly in the transitional macrophages. Of interest, ceramide kinase gene (CerK) is also significantly upregulated at G30 in Mt-1 and Mt-2, and its expression decreases as the granuloma matures (Fig. 6W, statistical treatment shown in Supplementary Table S5). Spatial transcriptomics analysis of the sphingolipid-associated transcripts of interest is in agreement with the snRNA-seq and lipidomics data.

As additional validation, we infected Sgms1+/- and Sgms2-/- mice with Δgcs1 to observe if granuloma formation was dependent on these genes. As shown in Fig. 7, neither mouse mutant produces an effective granulomatous response. There is a very clear difference between the highly organized collagen-coated macrophage boundary with contained fungal cells present in the wild-type mice (Fig. 7A, B), and the lung tissues of the mutant mice, which show no lung granuloma and a dissemination of fungal cells to the brain (Fig. 7C, D). The survival (Fig. 7E), and fungal burden (Fig. 7F–H) of these mutant mice compared to the wild type mice indicate that these genes are essential for granuloma formation and subsequent survival of the infection.

Fungal budding and macrophage fusion indices during the maturation of the granuloma

In previous studies7, and confirmed here (Supplementary Fig. S4), we found that the colony forming units (CFU) of the lungs infected with the Δgcs1 remain constant, at ~104 cells/lung throughout the formation and maturation of the granuloma. As mentioned above, the Δgcs1 strain is not able to replicate in the neutral/alkaline environment of the alveolar spaces but is able to replicate in acidic environments, such as within the phagolysosome of macrophages and within the central necrotic tissue of the granuloma. Thus, to maintain a constant CFU, the host immunity must be successful in killing the replicating fungus. In addition to gene expression, the nuclear fusion index is considered another parameter of macrophage activation and efficacious in microbicidal activity34,35. Thus, we analyzed the nuclear fusion index of macrophages and fungal buddings, particularly intracellularly, in our lung tissues.

As shown in Fig. 8, there is no significant difference in the fungal budding index between the Δgcs1 and WT inoculum, nor is there a difference between either of these groups and the budding index observed in fungal cells in G30. However, as the granuloma matures, the fungal budding index of fungal cells inside macrophages or within the necrotic area decreases over time compared to the budding index in fungal cells present in the G30 sample. At the same time, the nuclear fusion index of macrophages, which is reflective of their activation, also decreases as the granuloma matures (Fig. 8). From the gene expression profile, we found a clear correlation between the macrophage activity and the maturation of the granuloma, which, when taken together with the fungal budding, suggest that the Δgcs1 cells are going into a non-replicative state as the granuloma matures.

Discussion

We have characterized a fully mature cryptococcal granuloma in mice through transcriptomic and lipidomic analyses. Initial snRNA-seq analysis revealed distinct macrophage population differences between the WT and Δgcs1-infected samples and the uninfected control, as well as identified several differentially expressed genes related to the formation and function of the granuloma. Using these data, we further elucidated the cellular and transcriptional organization of the granuloma using spatial transcriptomics. This analysis revealed changes in cell populations and organization as the granuloma matures. We also analyzed the differentially expressed genes and found that these genes are primarily transcribed in the border area of the granuloma. Several sphingolipid-associated genes were also observed to be up-regulated in the granuloma, which was confirmed via LC-MS, MALDI and spatial transcriptomics, and found to be essential for granuloma formation. Lastly, there was a clear correlation between the decrease in fungal budding index and the decrease in the macrophage nuclear fusion index.

It is widely accepted that human subjects are exposed to C. neoformans during early childhood36,37 and that, upon this primary infection, the host harbors fungal cells in a lung granuloma38–40. This is supported by studies showing identical fungal strains isolated from meningo-encephalitis patients and prior strains isolated years earlier from the same asymptomatic patient41–43. While it is possible that an identical strain may be inhaled years apart, this ignores the enormous genetic variability of cryptococcal strains in the environment44–46. In addition, histopathological analysis of lung biopsies confirmed the presence of a cryptococcal granuloma5,6. These observations make it more likely that primary infection, granuloma formation and eventual reactivation upon immunosuppression likely reflect the stages of this disease. Therefore, the development of a mouse granuloma model able to recapitulate the human infection and progression of cryptococcosis within a relative short time ( ~ 60-90 days) was timely and significant.

Our studies reveal the essential role of macrophages in orchestrating an effective protective immunity during the initiation and maturation of the cryptococcal granuloma. We also found that genes involved in the host sphingolipid pathways, particularly in macrophages, play a key role in controlling an efficient immune response. Whereas the Δgcs1 strain cannot replicate extracellularly in the neutral/alkaline environment of the alveolar spaces, it can replicate well in acidic environments, such as the phagolysosome of these macrophages. Thus, it is possible that the macrophages identified only in the G30 sample by their relatively low expression gene-counts (M-lc) are dying because of the exuberant intracellular replication of Δgcs1 during early days of the infection (day 1-30).

Genes involved in the pro-migration, pro-proliferation, pro-fusion and pro-M1 immunity functions of macrophages are activated early, by G30, and they tend to decrease as the granuloma matures. We were expecting a continual activation of these processes because the overall number of CFU within the lung throughout this time (G30-G90) does not decrease but rather remains constant. We now know that this immunity shuts down because the fungal replication decreases. We do not know which phenomenon comes first, but it is possible that this phenomenon is stimulated the particular phenotype of Δgcs1 and its intracellular parasitism, which allows it to replicate in acidic environments and to survive (but not replicate) in extracellular environments.

In previous studies we showed that sphingosine kinase 1 (called SK1 and/or Sphk1), which produces sphingosine-1-phosphate (Sph1P) is a gene essential to initiate a granulomatous response because knockout mice lacking this gene (SK1-/-) do not produce a granuloma against Δgcs1, which is essential to prevent migration to the brain tissue29,47. Our data corroborate these findings, and found that various phosphorylated sphingosines (e.g., Sph1P, dhSph1P) are significantly increased in the G30 samples compared to WT-infected or uninfected mice even though the transcription of Sphk1 appears not to be dramatically upregulated (Fig. 6A). Most likely the accumulation of these phosphorylated sphingosines is a flux effect due to the de-novo activation of the sphingolipid pathway, as various ceramide and SM species are also increased. Of note, we did not measure the protein level or the enzymatic activity of Sphk1 in the lung tissue.

The transcriptomic data also highlight the expression of CerK being significantly upregulated at G30 compared to WT or UN in M-t1 and M-t2 macrophages and then CerK expression decreases as the granuloma matures (G90 versus G30) (Fig. 6W). CerK, through ceramide-1-phosphate (C1P), is well known to activate phagocytosis48 and to promote macrophage migration (reviewed in refs. 32,49,50). But increasing experimental evidence also suggests that C1P exerts anti-inflammatory actions51,52 and the differential expression of CerK among the macrophages found at the granuloma support both macrophage migration and the anti-inflammatory role of C1P at the granuloma.

The sphingolipid that we found to be mostly upregulated in the granuloma is sphingomyelin. Sphingomyelin is important to promote phagocytosis of C. neoformans (WT or Δgcs1) by macrophages30 and fungal killing by phagocytic cells53–55. The results in these studies confirm the abundance of this sphingolipid in phagocytic cells comprising the granuloma and highlight that the highest amount of sphingomyelin is found in the necrotic tissue, most likely as a result of lysed macrophages as sphingomyelin is not produced by fungal cells, also present in the necrotic tissue. Both Sgms1+/- and Sgms2-/- mice did not produce a lung granuloma when infected with C. neoformans Δgcs1. As such, they were not able to control the infection and, eventually, the fungus escaped the lung, disseminated to the brain and caused a lethal meningo-encephalitis. These results further validate the key role for sphingomyelin synthase activity in the formation of the granuloma. Of note, we could not test the homozygote for Sgms1 (Sgms1-/-), because in our hands, even if viable, the homozygote pups were very small and did not survive long enough to be infected.

As the granuloma matures, it also grows in size and weight, the multinuclear macrophage ring expands (perhaps to fight replicating fungal cells) and the central necrotic area increases (composed of dying fungal cells and dying macrophages). The observation that the overall gene expression of the G90 is downregulated compared to G60 and even more when compared to G30 allow us to hypothesize that the host has done its job in controlling the infection, considering that that the remaining fungal cells within the granuloma (both engulfed by macrophages and in the necrotic tissue) do not bud as they were doing at G30. Thus, this non-replicative fungal state may resemble fungal dormancy, although additional transcriptomic data and validation studies will be needed to fully understand the decrease of fungal replication at G90 versus G30.

Because CD4+ T cell deficiency is a necessary component in the reactivation of cryptococcosis8,11, the information generated here could be used to prevent/control the infection via the stabilization or reinvigoration of the granulomatous structure in the condition of CD4+ T cell immunodeficiency. For instance, the expression of key genes or/and their protein activity (e.g., Sgms activity) in lung macrophages or circulating monocytes could be used as a screening tool in CD4+ T deficient subjects for predicting whether the host granuloma is still intact or is prone to reactivate the infection. This may prompt the administration of antifungal therapy that could prevent the development of the cryptococcal brain disease.

One limitation of this study is the use of a cryptococcal mutant strain. Howwere incubated with Alexa Fluorever, clinical strains are too virulent in mice and even if mice can produce a granulomatous response, they are incapable of engulfing fungal cells within a well-structured granuloma. Also, there are no studies using clinical strains that highlight the state of latency of fungal cells within a fully mature granuloma, which is instead evident in our mouse model. Another limitation of our studies is the lack of analysis of the gene expression in fungal cells. We did run the results of our snRNA-seq against fungal genomes and found no homology. Most likely, not enough fungal RNA was isolated by the procedure used to isolate nuclear RNA from host cells. Additionally, while we focused our analysis on macrophages, particularly the macrophages found in G30, G60 and G90 compared to WT-infected mice because macrophages are known to be an essential component in the granulomatous response, snRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics data have been collected also on T cells, B cells, dendritic cells, neutrophils and other cells present in the granuloma (G30, G60 and G90), WT and UN samples. The analysis of these data awaits further characterization and validation.

Finally, our studies not only provide a contextual understanding of the cryptococcal granuloma but can also inform on multiple perspectives. For instance, the rapid expansion of genomic research has prompted the development of new technologies, new knockout, and particularly Tet on/off mouse models that can address more in depth the specific role of the differentially expressed genes identified in our studies. The use of the Tet on/off model of a specific gene can allow for any gene to be turned off at any time during the formation and the maturation of the granuloma or during dormancy to decipher the impact of that gene on these processes. In addition to immunocompetent mice, our model allows for the study of these processes in condition of T cells immunodeficiency (e.g., using antibody depletion), during the formation of the granuloma and, most importantly, after dormancy. Given the increasing numbers of lymphopenic individuals due to a variety of new immunosuppressive treatments, the number of fungal opportunistic infections, such as cryptococcosis, is expected to rise. Thus, our model is timely because not only will it provide the opportunity to generate a better understanding on the host immunity confining cryptococcal cells in the granuloma but could potentially produce significant insights into novel therapeutic strategies specifically targeting the granuloma stabilization in condition of CD4+ T cell depletion.

Methods

Fungal strains

The fungal strains used in this study were Cryptococcus neoformans var. grubii serotype A (WT) and the Δgcs1 mutant strain, which lack the glucosylceramide synthase 1 gene (GCS1)7. The strains were recovered from −80 °C stocks on yeast extract, peptone, and dextrose (YPD) plates, and incubated at 30 °C, for 48 h. An isolated colony was collected and cultivated in 10 mL of YPD broth, for 16-18 h, 150 rpm, 30 °C. The cells were pelleted, washed twice in sterile PBS, counted, and a cell suspension of 2.5 × 107 yeast cells/mL was prepared for further experiments.

Mouse strain, ethical statement, and infection

Mice used in these experiments were male and female, 4–6 weeks old CBA/J and C57BL6/J mice (purchased from Jackson Laboratory). The Sgms1+/- and Sgms2-/- (isogenic of C57BL6/J) mice were bred in house. The mice had access to water and food ad libitum. All the procedures were approved by the Stony Brook Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol n° 341588), and the guidelines of the American Veterinary Medical Association were followed.

Infection and granuloma isolation

Mice were anesthetized with ketamine/xylazine solution (95 mg of ketamine and 5 mg of xylazine per kg of animal body weight), via intraperitoneal (IP), and infected with 5 × 105 yeast cells (WT or Δgcs1) in 20 µL of PBS, via intranasal (IN). As uninfected controls, the anesthetized mice received just 20 µL of PBS, IN. At selected time points, 30-, 60- and 90-days post infection, the Δgcs1-infected mice were euthanized via CO2 inhalation, and the lungs were aseptically removed, rinsed with RPMI, and used for granuloma isolation. As early as 30-days but even so at 60-, and 90-days post-infection (d.p.i), the granuloma becomes visible to the naked eye in the lungs. Upon the identification and localization of the lung granulomas, tweezers and scalpels were utilized to fully excise them from the surrounding lung tissue. Under the dissection microscope, any residual lung tissue outside of the granuloma was carefully removed. ~8 granulomas per mouse were identified at 30- and 60-d.p.i. in 3 mice per time point, for a total of 24 granulomas at 30- and 24 granulomas at 60-d.p.i. Thus, 24 granulomas were pooled together and used for nuclei isolation for 30- or 60-d.p.i. Each 24 granulomas weighed approximately 25 mg. At 90-d.p.i., 3 mice were euthanized but only 3 granulomas per mouse for a total of 9 granulomas were used for nuclei isolation because these granuloma were bigger and 9 granuloma weighed ~25 mg. Following the same procedure for lung excision, approximately the same amount of lung tissue ( ~ 25 mg) was obtained from the WT-infected and uninfected lungs: lung tissue from mice infected with C. neoformans WT were collected at 15- d.p.i. whereas lung tissue from uninfected mice were collected at 30 days after PBS administration. Lung tissue from WT-infected mice was collected at 15-d.p.i. because mortality increases rapidly after this day. A total amount of 25 mg of tissue per lung were used for the WT-infected and uninfected mice.

Nuclei isolation

All animal procedures were performed in accordance with Stony Brook University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Experimental mice were euthanized using isoflurane overdose followed by decapitation. All the subsequent procedures were conducted on ice and/or at 4 °C to minimize RNA degradation. Lung granulomas (3-8) and/or lung tissue (3-20 mg) were rapidly dissected and immediately homogenized in 1 ml of homogenization buffer (Sucrose 260 mM; KCl 26 mM; MgCl2 5 mM; Tricine-KOH pH 7.8, 20 mM; Complete Mini Protease Inhibitor 1/4 tablet per 10 mL; DTT 1 mM; Spermidine 0.5 mM; Spermine 0.15 mM; NP40 substitute 0.3% v/v) containing RNase inhibitor (RiboLock, Thermo Scientific, 25 µL/mL) using a Dounce homogenizer and a 25-gauge needle. Large granulomas were cut into smaller pieces on ice in the buffer using spring scissors before dounce homogenization. The homogenates were filtered (40 µm Flowmi filters, Bel-Art) and pelleted by centrifugation at 500 rcf for 5 minutes at 4 °C. The nuclei pellets were resuspended in homogenization buffer with 25% iodixanol (OptiPrep™ Density Gradient Medium, Millipore Sigma), and single nuclei were purified on an iodixanol gradient (25%-30%-40%) by centrifugation at 2,200 rcf for 20 minutes at 4 °C without brake. A 120 µl aliquot containing nuclei was taken at the interface between the 30% and the 40% iodixanol layers. Nuclei concentrations were determined using a Countess 2FL and Trypan Blue staining. The nuclei suspensions were used directly for single-nucleus capture in the 10x Genomics Chromium iX, as described below.

Single nuclei RNA-seq: Single nuclei RNA-sequencing was performed using a Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5’ Reagent Kit, v2 on a Chromium iX, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, single nuclei suspensions, gel beads, and partitioning oil were loaded onto a Chromium NextGEM K Chip, for a target recovery of up to 10,000 nuclei per sample. The chip was loaded into a Chromium iX (10X Genomics) and single nuclei were captured in Gel Bead-In-Emulsions (GEMs).

Reverse transcription was performed in a PCR cycler, and the cDNA was collected using Recovery Agent (10X Genomics), purified with DynaBeads MyOne Silane beads (10X Genomics), amplified with PCR for 13 cycles, and further purified with SPRISelect reagent (Beckman Coulter). Quality control and quantification of the amplified cDNA was performed on a Tapestation 4200 (Agilent) using D5000 High Sensitivity ScreenTapes (Agilent, 5067-5592).

5’ single nuclei RNA-seq libraries were generated using 50 ng of the amplified cDNA. After fragmentation, end-repair, A-tailing and size selection purification, the cDNA was ligated with Illumina adaptors and subsequently purified with SPRISelect reagent. Libraries were amplified and barcoded by PCR with dual index oligonucleotides from the Chromium Dual Index TT Set A kit (10X Genomics), for a total of 15 PCR cycles. The final PCR products were subjected to double-sided size selection with SPRISelect beads (0.6x and 0.8x). The library fragment size distribution was analyzed on the Agilent 4200 Tapestation, and library yields and concentrations were determined by qPCR using the KAPA Illumina Library Quantification PCR kit (Roche Diagnostics).

Sequencing of the libraries (min. depth: 20,000 paired reads/nucleus) was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq instrument operated by a commercial provider, with the following paired-end sequencing settings: Read1, 151 cycles; i7 index, 10 cycles; i5 index, 10 cycles; Read2, 151 cycles.

Spatial transcriptomics

Gene panel design. In situ-based, high throughput spatial transcriptomic analysis was performed on a 10x Genomics Xenium Analyzer, using the Xenium Prime 5 K Mouse Pan Tissue & Pathways Panel, together with a 47-gene custom panel designed to capture pathways relevant to fungal lung granuloma formation. The additional targets were selected from published single-cell datasets generated in murine fungal infection models and from genes previously associated with granulomatous inflammation. Probe pairs were designed by 10x Genomics and consisted of two adjacent RNA-binding oligonucleotides carrying a gene-specific barcode. Only correctly paired probes undergo circularization during the Prime ligation step, reducing nonspecific signal and ensuring accurate detection across cell types present in the granulomas.

Tissue preparation and Xenium Prime workflow. Spatial profiling was conducted on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples from mouse control lung, and from lungs containing granulomatous lesions. Sections were cut at 5 µm, mounted on Xenium slides, and selected regions from each FFPE section were placed in the 10.45 × 22.45 mm sample area on Xenium slides, dried for 3 hrs at 42 °C, and then kept at room temperature in a desiccator for 18 h to 1 week. Once ready for processing, the sections were first deparaffinized and decrosslinked according to the Xenium FFPE demonstrated protocol (CG000580). After these initial steps, the tissue was incubated with the Xenium Prime priming oligos (standard Mouse 5 K set and custom 47-gene oligos, which anneal to the RNA targets for 1.5 hrs at 50 °C, followed by a wash of the excess primers for 30 min at 50 °C. An RNase digestion step to release the RNA strand from the bound primers was carried out for 20 min at 37 °C, followed by a 1-hour polishing step (also at 37 °C), which prepared the cleaved RNA template for efficient probe hybridization. The Xenium Prime probe mix was then added to the sections and allowed to hybridize to the prepared RNA template for 16–24 hrs at 50 °C. Unbound probes were washed at 35 °C for 15 min, and a ligation reagent was introduced to join the two probe ends that annealed adjacently on the RNA (30 min at 42 °C). The ligation produces a circular probe molecule, which is then subjected to enzymatic rolling circle amplification (RCA) to generate many copies of the gene-specific barcode for each detected transcript. The amplification was conducted as instructed with an enhancement reaction for 2 hours at 4 °C, and the RCA for 1.5 hrs at 30 °C, followed by a post-amplification wash in TE buffer.

For cell segmentation, sections were stained with a cocktail of antibodies directed against nuclear, membrane, and cytoplasmic markers (E-cadherin, ATP1A1, CD45, vimentin, α-SMA), after a blocking step to limit nonspecific binding. Staining was carried out overnight, followed by washes and the incubation with a signal enhancement reagent for 20 min at RT. Autofluorescence quenching was performed to reduce background derived from the lung matrix and FFPE processing by incubating the slides at RT in the dark for 10 minutes with a quenching solution provided with the Xenium kit. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and slides were assembled into Xenium Cassettes v2 for imaging.

Imaging and primary processing. The cassette assemblies prepared as described above were loaded onto the Xenium Prime Analyzer. The instrument performs automated hybridization, imaging, and stripping cycles according to the standard workflow for the Mouse 5 K + custom panel (the latter loaded on the instrument as a.JSON file). During each cycle, fluorescent readout probes are hybridized to the amplified barcodes, imaged across all channels, and removed prior to the next cycle. Imaging is conducted with the default optical configuration of the instrument, which provides ~200 nm/pixel resolution. Z-stacks are collected through the full tissue thickness using a 0.75 µm step size. Raw image data are processed by the onboard Xenium software. Molecule detection, barcode identification, and initial quality control are performed automatically, and molecule-resolved output files are generated for downstream analysis.

Lipid extraction and sphingolipids quantification by LC-ESI-MS/MS: Isolated lung granulomas were used for lipidomic analysis. Granulomas collected from all experimental time points, as well as lung tissues from uninfected and WT-infected mice, were weighed prior to lipid extraction. Each sample was spiked with 50 pmol of internal standards (ISs) solutions. The internal standards and the lipid extraction procedure were performed as described by Bielawski, et al., 200656.

Briefly, 2 mL of cold extraction mix (60:30:10 ethyl acetate:iso-propanol:water, v/v/v) were added to each sample, followed by homogenization using a Bullet Blender Tissue Homogenizer (Next Advance, USA). The homogenates were centrifuged, and the top layer transferred to a new tube. This extraction step was repeated once, and the combined supernatants were divided as follows: one-fourth for sphingomyelin analysis, one-fourth for inorganic phosphate quantification, and the remaining half subjected to mild alkaline hydrolysis (0.6 M KOH in methanol) for subsequent analysis of sphingosine and ceramide species. The sphingolipid species were quantified using a TSQ Quantum Ultra™ triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific), operated for multiple reaction monitoring in positive ionization mode, as previously described56. The sphingolipids were normalized by wet tissue weight (mg) or by inorganic phosphate.

Histological analyses

Uninfected and infected lungs (WT and Δgcs1) were aseptically harvested and rinsed 3 times on sterile PBS. Subsequently, the lungs were fixed on 37% formaldehyde for 48 h, dehydrated on ethanol, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and periodic acid-Schiff staining (PAS). Three lungs of each group were analyzed under the microscope.

Budding index

In the PAS stained tissue sections, the budding index was calculated as the percentage of Cryptococcus cells that presented one or more buds57. In the Δgcs1 infected lungs, bud counting was performed specifically inside the granuloma sites, in at least 3 fields being counted per granuloma. At least 3 granulomas in different mice were used. Regarding WT-infected lungs, bud counting was performed on cryptococci loci, in also at least 3 fields being counted in different granulomas. An average of the fields was calculated, and the results were expressed as a percentage of budding cells per field.

Immunofluorescence staining

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded lung tissues from mice were sectioned at 5 μm thickness and mounted onto glass slides. After an incubation of 1 h at 60 °C, the slides were deparaffinized in xylene (three changes, 5 min each), then rehydrated through a graded ethanol series: 100% (three times), 95% (twice), 70% (twice), for 2 min each. For antigen retrieval, the slides were immersed in IHC-TekTM Epitope Retrieval Solution (IHC World), and incubated under steam for 40 min. After cooling to room temperature, the sections were blocked with BSA 5% solution, then, incubated overnight at 4 °C in a dark, humidified chamber with the primary antibody anti-mouse F4/80 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a dilution of 1:125. The following day, slides were incubated with Alexa FluorTM 647-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (1:300, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h, at room temperature. After washing, the slides were treated with Vector® TrueVIEW® autofluorescence quenching kit (VECTOR Laboratories), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, the sections were stained with 10 ug/mL of Wheat Germ Agglutinin (WGA)-Alexa FluorTM 488, during 30 min at 37 °C. Nuclear counterstaining was carried out using DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 10 minutes, at room temperature (dilution 1:5,000). Finally, slides were mounted using VECTASHIELD® VibranceTM Antifade Mounting Medium (VECTOR Laboratories). Images were acquired using Zeiss LSM 980 with Airyscan 2 NLO Two-Photon Laser Scanning Confocal Microscope (ZEISS) at Central Microscopy Imaging Center from Stony Brook University.

Fusion index

The budding index was performed as described by Ahmadzadeh et al. (2023) with modifications35. Following immunofluorescence staining and image acquisition from lung granulomas regions, the total numbers of nuclei per field were counted using the Fiji software58. The WGA and F4/80 staining were used to identify and delineate macrophages cells. Cells containing more than three nuclei were classified as multinucleated giant cells (MGC). For each granuloma, at least three distinct fields were analyzed for nuclei counting. Additionally, at each timepoint of the infection (−30, −60 and −90 d.p.i), granulomas from a minimum of three different mice were evaluated. The number of nuclei per macrophage was recorded, and the fusion index was calculated as the percentage of nuclei in multinucleated cells relative to the total number of nuclei: [number of nuclei in cells with >3 nuclei]/[total number of nuclei] × 100.

Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI)

Infected mice with C. neoformans Δgcs1 strain were euthanized 30-, 60- and 90-d.p.i. The lungs were collected and promptly frozen using liquid nitrogen for subsequent MALDI Mass Spectrometry Imaging (MSI) analysis. The freshly frozen tissues were attached to the Leica CM1950 Cryostat using a drop of optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT) only at the base of the tissue. The lung samples were sectioned at a thickness of 10 µm and mounted on IntelliSlides bearing 2 sequential sections per slide. Multiple slides were prepared for MSI analysis to detect sphingolipids following the optimized method previously reported in ref. 59 on the same instrument as used in this study. Prior to MSI analysis, tissue slides were dried in a vacuum desiccator at room temperature for 30 minutes. Matrix solution was then sprayed onto the slide using an HTX M5 Sprayer. DHB matrix was applied on the tissue slide for analyzing sphingomyelins in positive ion mode, whereas DAN matrix was used for analysis of ceramides in negative ion mode. MALDI Imaging was performed on a timsTOF fleX mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics) over the selected granuloma regions with 20 μm spatial resolution within the mass range 200 − 1100 m/z. MSI images were generated using SCiLS Lab software (Bruker Daltonics) and sphingolipids were annotated using MetaboScape software (Bruker Daltonics) with m/z tolerance set to 10 ppm. H&E staining was performed on an adjacent serial section for correlation with the MALDI Imaging data. The same process was performed for uninfected (at 30-d.p.i) and WT-infected mice (at 15-d.p.i).

Annotation: Initial identification of cell types was performed using Over Representation Analysis60 using canonical marker lists from previous studies in lung or established databases13–21. Subsetting and re-clustering was performed to enhance identification of different cell types. At this stage a few small clusters that expressed markers from two or more cell types were eliminated. Based on this initial analysis, a hierarchical ontology of cell types was constructed (Supplementary Table S1). Use of a hierarchical approach reduces the possibility of misclassification by relying on multiple choice points in the classification tree before assignment of cell type. A total of several distinct cell types were identified in this analysis although not all cell types were found in every experimental condition.
