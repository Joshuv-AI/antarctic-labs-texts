The expansion of an invasive malaria vector: Anopheles stephensi emergence in Arba Minch town in the southern Rift Valley of Ethiopia
Authors: Fekadu Massebo, Temesgen Ashine, Nigatu Negash, Nigatu Eligo, Elifaged Hailemeskel, Thomas T. Minda, Bernt Lindtjørn, Endalamaw Gadisa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Urban areas in malaria-endemic countries in East Africa are experiencing a significant increase in malaria cases, with the establishment of an “exotic” urban malaria vector, Anopheles stephensi, increasing the risk of urban malaria. To this end, the present study aimed to investigate the emergence of this species in Arba Minch, Ethiopia. Following the detection of An. stephensi in other parts of Ethiopia, 76 artificial containers (55 discarded tyres, 18 concrete water storage, and three plastic containers) were sampled in 21 locations in Arba Minch town, for immature Anopheles mosquito stages, using the standard dipping technique. Larvae were reared into adults which were morphologically identified at the species level 2–3 days after emergence. Morphological identification results were confirmed by species-specific polymerase chain reaction. Of the examined containers, 67 (88%) had at least one Anopheles larva. Thirty-two of the adults emerged were morphologically identified as An. stephensi, with 26 (81%) confirmed by molecular analysis. This is the first study to report An. stephensi from Arba Minch, one of South Ethiopia’s largest towns, highlighting the need for increased vigilance. The planned and ongoing study in and around Arba Minch will contribute to understanding the bionomics and role of An. stephensi in malaria parasite transmission, helping develop a strategy to address the impending risk of urban malaria in Ethiopia.

Background and rationale

Malaria transmission in Africa varies between urban and rural areas. In rural areas, malaria is primarily transmitted by mosquito species not well adapted to urban areas (Hay et al. 2005). The increased mobility of people accompanied by rapid unstructured urbanisation, however, leads to the “ruralisation” of urban settings, making these suitable for malaria parasite transmission and increasing the risk of malaria in cities (Doumbe-Belisse et al. 2021). The invasion of Anopheles stephensi, an efficient urban malaria vector originating from the Indian sub-continent and the Middle East (Sinka et al. 2011), could complicate the epidemiology of malaria in African cities even more.

Anopheles stephensi is a demonstrated vector of both Plasmodium falciparum and P. vivax parasites in the field and the laboratory (Tadesse et al. 2021; Emiru et al. 2023). Its adaptability to breed in artificial containers has made it a vector of malaria parasites in urban settings (Moussa et al. 2023).

Anopheles stephensi was first identified in Africa in Djibouti in 2012 (Faulde et al. 2014). The first report of this species to contribute to urban Plasmodium transmission and increase malaria prevalence in Africa was documented by Seyfarth et al. (2019). Its introduction to Djibouti was associated with a 30-fold increase in malaria cases (de Santi et al. 2021).

In Ethiopia, An. stephensi was initially identified in Kebri Dehar, a town in the eastern part of the country, in 2016 (Carter et al. 2018). Since its detection, it has been found in multiple urban settings (Balkew et al. 2020; Ashine et al. 2024). The first record of An. stephensi in southern Ethiopia was documented in Hawassa town in 2023 (Hawaria et al. 2023), highlighting the need to monitor other regions as well.

Arba Minch is a town in the Ethiopian Rift Valley, endowed with green vegetation caused by water supply from lakes, rivers, and streams. However, the relative water richness also allows for a year-round transmission of malaria parasites, with P. falciparum and P. vivax being prevalent (Getawen et al. 2018). Many species of Anopheles mosquitoes have been documented in Arba Minch town (Getawen et al. 2018; Akirso et al. 2023). The expansion of the construction areas, the unwise use of water resources in the car washing business, and poor garage tyre disposal have been creating an increasing supply with artificial potential breeding sites. In recent surveys conducted in the town, An. stephensi was not found (Akirso et al. 2023; Ashine et al. 2024). This study aimed to check for its possible occurrence with a more in-depth examination of artificial breeding sites.

Materials and methods

This survey was conducted in January and early February 2024, the months with the lowest local precipitation in Arba Minch town (Fig. 1). Arba Minch receives an annual precipitation of over 770 mm, with rainfall peaking in April and October. The survey involved purposive searching for Anopheles mosquito immature stages in artificial containers, such as concrete and plastic water storage containers. Sampling included 21 locations and focused on containers used for car washing and discarded tyres in garages, excluding household containers. A 250-ml dipper with a handle was used to collect immature Anopheles mosquitoes following a standardized dipping technique (World Health Organization 1975). The immature stages were transported to the laboratory and reared to adults for morphological identification. Emerging adults were kept in a rearing cage and fed on 10% sugar for 2–3 days before being identified morphologically using the taxonomic key by Coetzee (2020). Genetic species confirmation was done using an An. stephensi-specific polymerase chain reaction targeting the internal transcribed spacer 2 region (Singh et al. 2023).

Results

During the survey to identify container-breeding Anopheles mosquitoes, 76 artificial containers were inspected. These comprised 55 discarded tyres, 18 concrete water storage containers, and three plastic containers (Fig. 1). Of the containers examined, 24 (32%) had at least one Anopheles larva. Ten (56%) of the 18 concrete water storage containers used for car washing and 14 (25%) of the 55 discarded tyres were positive for at least one Anopheles larva. By contrast, none of the three plastic containers checked, showed immature Anopheles stages.

Two hundred thirty-one Anopheles larvae were collected, with 131 (57%) coming from discarded tyres and a concrete water storage container in one site. Of these, 32 emerged adults were morphologically identified as An. stephensi. Twenty-six (81%) of those were confirmed by molecular analysis.

Five An. stephensi were identified in two other sites: three in one and two in the second (Fig. 1). These specimens were identified morphologically but not confirmed genetically.

Discussion

Anopheles stephensi was detected for the first time in Arba Minch, one of the largest towns in south Ethiopia. It was primarily detected in discarded tyres and concrete water storage containers intended for car washing. The adaptation of An. stephensi to artificial containers allows for malaria parasite transmission year-round, potentially equalizing the seasonality of malaria occurrence. Since its report in the eastern part of the country in 2016 (Carter et al. 2018), An. stephensi has been expanding its geographic distribution and quickly adapting to new environments (Balkew et al. 2020; Ashine et al. 2024). One possible reason for its quick adaptation to the new habitat types could be common urban practices such as poor tyre disposal and the offering of water storage containers for car washing and house construction (Yared et al. 2023).

In 2022, adult mosquitoes were collected in Arba Minch town using CDC light traps and Prokopack aspirators to assess the feeding and resting behaviour of malaria vectors (Akirso et al. 2023). Additionally, immature stages were collected from artificial breeding habitats within the compounds of 60 households where the adult sampling was done. In that study, An. rhodesiensis was found in the man-made containers inspected, but not An. stephensi (Akirso et al. 2023). Another study, carried out over the past 2 years (2021–2023), did also not detect An. stephensi (Ashine et al 2024). The selected sites for the study and the methodology used, such as semi-random sampling of breeding habitats within a selected quadrant, might partially account for that failure (Ashine et al 2024). Thus, the current study covered as many putative breeding habitats as possible, for the first time providing evidence of the presence of An. stephensi in Arba Minch.

Although An. stephensi was identified in Arba Minch town, it is still unclear which intervention method should be used to effectively control the mosquitoes. Considering the selection of breeding habitats and the adaptability of the species to urban settings, larval source management would be the best option at the moment. In fact, there is a movement towards implementing larval control as a primary strategy in countries where An. stephensi has been confirmed (World Health Organization 2023). Adult control is questionable and requires further research and investigation. This species usually feeds on animals and rests outdoors (Carter et al. 2018; Ashine et al. 2024), suggesting that interventions targeting indoor biting and resting species, as currently planned, may not be effective for controlling An. stephensi. Therefore, it is crucial to develop new tools suitable for urban areas to address the problem caused by this species. A comprehensive understanding of the ecology and behaviour of this vector species in urban settings can help design appropriate interventions.

Ongoing and planned activities intended to devise mechanisms to control An. stephensi include extensive entomological and parasitological studies in Arba Minch and other regional towns to ascertain whether transportation and related services contribute to the rapid spread of the species. These studies will also investigate the relative contribution of An. stephensi to parasite transmission and evaluate the impact of larval source management on the risk posed by this mosquito in urban settings.
