Assessing respiratory motion effects after breathing training on hybrid and IMRT plans in hypofractionated breast radiotherapy
Authors: Lingtong Hou, Ge Yan, Shengyu Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the dosimetric impact of respiratory motion after breathing training on Hybrid and IMRT plans in hypofractionated breast radiotherapy, using 4D-CT and deformable dose accumulation.MethodsTwenty-two postoperative breast cancer patients treated under free breathing underwent both 3D-CT and 4D-CT scanning after abdominal breathing training. Original Hybrid (O_HY) and IMRT (O_IM) plans were generated on 3D-CT. Each plan was mapped onto the ten 4D-CT phases and recalculated. Deformable image registration was applied to accumulate phase-specific doses onto the original 3D-CT, generating motion-inclusive deformed Hybrid (D_HY) and IMRT (D_IM) plans. Respiratory motion amplitudes of the chest wall and liver were quantified. Dosimetric parameters of targets and OARs were evaluated and compared.ResultsAfter breathing training, respiratory motion amplitudes were 13.2 ± 4.6 mm for the liver and 1.54 ± 0.85 mm for the chest wall. Both techniques achieved good target coverage in the original plans, with comparable hotspot, homogeneity, and conformity. Hybrid plans provided superior OAR sparing, particularly in the low-dose regions. After deformable dose accumulation, target coverage exhibited only minor decreases: PTV_V100% decreased by 2.5% for Hybrid and 2.1% for IMRT. PTV_D2% remained largely unchanged, and HI/CI deteriorated only slightly. OAR dose variations were minimal, with no significant differences except for a lower Breast_Dmean in the IMRT plan. Respiratory motion showed no meaningful correlations with most dosimetric parameters, except for weak correlations between liver motion and PTV_V95% and a significant correlation with Breast_Dmean in the IMRT plan.ConclusionsIn this cohort, chest wall motion amplitude after breathing training remained within 3.1 mm. Under these conditions, Hybrid and IMRT plans demonstrate comparable target dose robustness, while Hybrid achieves superior OAR sparing, particularly in the low-dose region. Based on this comparison, Hybrid planning may be considered a favorable option relative to IMRT for hypofractionated whole-breast irradiation under free breathing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13014-026-02821-0.Keywords: Breast cancer, Breathing training, Deformable dose accumulation, Hybrid radiotherapy, Intensity-modulated radiotherapy, 4D-CT, Respiratory motion

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common malignancy among women [1–3] and one of the leading causes of cancer-related death [4]. Postoperative radiotherapy after breast-conserving surgery is the standard treatment for early-stage breast cancer [5, 6], including conventional fractionation (50 Gy in 25 fractions) and hypofractionation (40–42.5 Gy in 15–16 fractions). Long-term follow-up has confirmed the safety and efficacy of hypofractionated schedules [7], and due to their shorter treatment duration and lower patient burden, they have become the international standard for adjuvant local radiotherapy in early breast cancer [8]. The higher dose per fraction in these regimens, however, imposes stricter requirements on treatment planning. The limitations of three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3D-CRT), traditionally the standard technique for breast cancer radiotherapy, have become increasingly apparent, particularly its difficulty in achieving optimal dose distribution and homogeneity in complex anatomical regions.

In recent years, intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) and volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) have gradually replaced conventional 3D-CRT for breast cancer treatment [9]. However, compared with 3D-CRT, IMRT and VMAT produce substantially larger low-dose regions in surrounding normal tissues [10–12], and exposure of normal tissues to larger dose volumes may increase the risk of radiation-induced secondary malignancies [13]. Hybrid radiotherapy [14], as a compromise approach, combines the advantages of 3D-CRT and IMRT, delivering 70–80% of the prescribed dose with 3D-CRT, while the remaining dose is delivered with IMRT. This method has gained attention because it maintains high target coverage, improves dose homogeneity, and reduces normal tissue exposure, potentially lowering the risk of secondary radiation-induced effects.

Respiratory motion can further affect dose delivery by causing displacement of the breast target and nearby organs at risk. Kinoshita et al. [15] used a real-time tumor tracking system with gold markers placed on the skin near the nipple and found that breast motion during respiration is mainly in the anterior–posterior direction (2.6 ± 1.4 mm), with smaller movements in the left–right (1.0 ± 0.6 mm) and superior–inferior (1.3 ± 0.5 mm) directions. Qi et al. [16] assessed cardiac displacement in left-sided breast cancer patients using 4D-CT and reported that the heart may shift by approximately 1 cm during respiration. Although deep inspiration breath-hold (DIBH) [17] can reduce respiratory motion and lower normal tissue doses, it requires sufficient breath-hold capability, which many patients—particularly elderly individuals or those with impaired pulmonary function—cannot achieve. Consequently, free-breathing radiotherapy remains the predominant clinical practice. Physiological evidence indicates that abdominal breathing training can reduce chest wall excursion and improve respiratory reproducibility [18], offering a practical strategy to limit motion under free-breathing conditions. Previous comparisons of breast radiotherapy techniques [19–21] have relied on static 3D-CT and therefore did not reflect respiratory-induced anatomical changes. Mankinen et al. [22] reported that respiratory amplitudes below 5 mm produced no clinically significant dose deviations in free-breathing whole-breast irradiation, although steep dose gradients near planning target volume (PTV) margins may compromise the clinical target volume (CTV) coverage. However, data remain limited regarding Hybrid or IMRT plans under similar respiratory conditions.

This study was based on 4D-CT acquired after abdominal breathing training, incorporating respiratory motion and dynamically simulating dose distributions through deformable dose accumulation (DDA), in order to comprehensively evaluate differences in dose distribution between Hybrid and IMRT plans in hypofractionated post-lumpectomy breast radiotherapy, aiming to provide more precise dosimetric data to support clinical radiotherapy technique selection.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study analyzed 22 breast cancer patients who underwent breast-conserving surgery followed by radiotherapy at our institution between June 2021 and April 2024, including 11 left-sided and 11 right-sided cases. All patients underwent both 3D-CT and 4D-CT scans under free-breathing conditions to obtain detailed anatomical and respiration-related information. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shanghai General Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (No.2022-N-09). Informed consent was obtained from all the patients.

Patients were positioned supine and immobilized on vacuum cushions, with arms raised above the head to fully expose the breast. One week before scanning, patients were trained in stable abdominal breathing to minimize respiratory motion amplitude and mitigate its impact on subsequent radiotherapy (the flowchart of abdominal breathing training is detailed in Additional file 1). Both 3D-CT and 4D-CT datasets were acquired in the same patient position using the Brilliance Bores CT scanner (Philips Medical Systems, Best, Netherlands), with scanning parameters of 120 kV, 200 mA, and a slice thickness of 2.5 mm. Respiratory signals were recorded using the Varian Real-Time Position Management system (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA), with end-inhalation and end-exhalation phases automatically annotated and manually corrected by a radiation therapist when necessary. The 4D-CT images were reconstructed into ten respiratory phases using the Philips Brilliance Bores 4D software (Philips Medical Systems, Best, Netherlands) with phase-based sorting.

Organs at risk (OARs) were first contoured using the AccuContour software (version 3.1, Manteia Medical Technologies, China) and subsequently reviewed and modified by an experienced radiation oncologist to ensure accuracy. Contoured OARs included the lungs, heart, contralateral breast, and liver. The images were then imported into the Eclipse treatment planning system (Eclipse™ 13.6, Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA), where the same radiation oncologist delineated the whole-breast CTV according to ESTRO guidelines [23] to ensure standardization and consistency. The PTV was generated by expanding the CTV by 0.8 cm in the cranio-caudal direction and 0.5 cm in all other directions, with a 3 mm margin subtracted from the body contour.

Original treatment planning was performed using the free-breathing 3D-CT to simulate clinical practice. Photon energy was 6 MV, with a dose rate of 600 MU/min, and dose calculations were performed using the Anisotropic Analytical Algorithm (AAA, version 13.6). In the original hybrid plan (O_HY), the 3D-CRT component delivered 1.86 Gy × 16 fractions (70% of the total prescription dose) using a pair of tangential beams with multi-leaf collimator (MLC)-conformed PTV expansions (1.0 cm toward the skin, 0.3 cm in other directions). Field weights were adjusted after dose calculation to improve dose homogeneity and facilitate subsequent IMRT optimization, with each field contributing 40–60% of the total dose. The doses were normalized so that 90% of the PTV received the prescription dose. The IMRT component delivered 0.80 Gy × 16 fractions (30% of the total prescription dose) using three pairs of scissor beams separated by 10–15°, with small MLC angle adjustments to minimize lung exposure. Inverse optimization incorporated the 3D-CRT dose using the base dose function. Field arrangements for the hybrid plan are shown in Fig. 1. The original IMRT plan (O_IM) prescribed 2.66 Gy × 16 fractions, with field arrangements identical to the IMRT portion of the hybrid plan. Initial optimization parameters were based on those used in the IMRT portion of the hybrid plan and were fine-tuned to ensure reasonable dose distributions. Skin flash was not applied in the IMRT plans. This design choice was made to allow a conservative and methodologically consistent comparison of dose robustness under respiratory motion after breathing training. All plans used the same isocenter and a 2.5 mm dose calculation grid, with final doses normalized so that 95% of the PTV received the prescription dose.

Because the 3D-CT and 4D-CT datasets were acquired in the same sequence, the O_HY and O_IM plans were directly applied to all ten 4D-CT phases. Dose distributions were recalculated for each phase, with the dose for each phase normalized to one-tenth of the original prescription. The resulting doses were then accumulated using equal temporal weighting, assuming regular breathing and a uniform distribution of respiratory phases. This approach provides a reasonable approximation of free-breathing dose delivery [24], under which the impact of beam–phase interplay is assumed to be limited. Using the AccuContour software, each 4D-CT phase was deformably registered to the 3D-CT. The deformable image registration (DIR) results were visually inspected for gross anatomical plausibility across all phases. The resulting deformation fields were then applied to map and accumulate dose distributions from each phase onto the 3D-CT, simulating actual dose delivery under free-breathing conditions. This process generated the deformable hybrid (D_HY) and IMRT (D_IM) plans. The workflow is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Workflow representing the data processing

Respiratory motion amplitudes were quantified from the 4D-CT dataset in two directions: chest wall motion at the nipple region, perpendicular to the tangent line, and liver motion at the superior liver region along the cranio-caudal axis. Dose metrics were analyzed for the O_HY, O_IM, D_HY, and D_IM plans for both targets and OARs. Target metrics included PTV_V100%, PTV_V95%, PTV_D2%, homogeneity index (HI), and conformity index (CI), where PTV_V100% and PTV_V95% represent the fractions of PTV receiving ≥ 100% and ≥ 95% of the prescription dose, respectively; PTV_D2% denotes the dose to 2% of the PTV volume, reflecting the hotspot. HI was calculated as (D₂% − D₉₈%) / DP, reflecting dose homogeneity, where D₂% and D₉₈% are doses to 2% and 98% of the PTV on the DVH, and DP is the prescription dose. CI was calculated as (Vt, ref / Vt) × (Vt, ref / Vref), reflecting dose conformity, where Vt is the PTV volume, Vt, ref is the PTV volume covered by the reference isodose, and Vref is the total volume enclosed by the reference isodose. OAR dose metrics included Lung_V20, Lung_V5, Breast_Dmean, Heart_Dmean, and Liver_D1cc. Lung_V20 and Lung_V5 represent the fractions of the ipsilateral lung receiving at least 20 Gy and 5 Gy, respectively; Breast_Dmean and Heart_Dmean are the mean doses of breast and heart; Liver_D1cc is the dose to the hottest 1 cm³ of the liver. Considering anatomical differences in dose exposure between left- and right-sided breast cancer, heart and liver metrics were analyzed separately for left- and right-sided cases.

Data processing and statistical analyses were performed in Python (version 3.12, Python Software Foundation, DE, USA) with SciPy (version 1.2, including Shapiro–Wilk test, paired t-test, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and Kendall’s τ analysis) and Statsmodels (version 0.14, for Bonferroni correction of multiple comparisons). Metrics meeting normality assumptions were compared using paired t-tests, whereas non-normal metrics were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons, with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. For dose metrics showing significant differences after DDA, Kendall’s τ was used to assess correlations with chest wall or liver motion amplitude.

In the original plans, both the O_HY and O_IM achieved excellent target coverage. After normalizing the PTV prescription dose to cover 95% of the PTV volume, there was no significant difference in PTV_V95% between the two plans (O_HY vs. O_IM: 99.1 ± 0.3% vs. 99.2 ± 0.2%, p > 0.05). After DDA, PTV coverage decreased slightly but significantly in both plans. For the hybrid plan, O_HY vs. D_HY: PTV_V100% decreased from 95.0 ± 0.0% to 92.5 ± 1.9% (p < 0.01) and PTV_V95% from 99.1 ± 0.3% to 98.5 ± 0.8% (p < 0.01). For the IMRT plan, O_IM vs. D_IM: PTV_V100% decreased from 95.0 ± 0.0% to 92.9 ± 1.6% (p < 0.01) and PTV_V95% from 99.2 ± 0.2% to 98.4 ± 0.9% (p < 0.01).

Hotspot (PTV_D2%), HI, and CI were comparable between original plans (O_HY vs. O_IM, PTV_D2%: 106.1 ± 0.3% vs. 106.2 ± 0.4%, p > 0.05; HI: 0.08 ± 0.01 vs. 0.08 ± 0.01, p > 0.05; CI: 0.88 ± 0.02 vs. 0.88 ± 0.02, p > 0.05). After DDA, there were no significant differences in hotspots compared with the original plans for both techniques. In contrast, HI and CI were significantly affected. For the hybrid plan, O_HY vs. D_HY: HI increased from 0.08 ± 0.01 to 0.10 ± 0.02 (p < 0.01), and CI decreased from 0.88 ± 0.02 to 0.85 ± 0.03 (p < 0.01). Similarly, for the IMRT plan, O_IM vs. D_IM: HI increased from 0.08 ± 0.01 to 0.10 ± 0.02 (p < 0.01), and CI decreased from 0.88 ± 0.02 to 0.85 ± 0.03 (p < 0.01).

For OAR dose exposure, the O_HY plan delivered significantly lower doses than the O_IM plan in most organs. Specifically, O_HY vs. O_IM: Breast_Dmean was 1.0 ± 0.7 Gy vs. 1.7 ± 1.1 Gy (p < 0.01), Lung_V20 was 8.7 ± 3.6% vs. 9.2 ± 3.5% (p < 0.01), Lung_V5 was 24.2 ± 5.6% vs. 28.9 ± 6.5% (p < 0.01), Heart_L_Dmean was 3.2 ± 1.5 Gy vs. 3.7 ± 1.3 Gy (p < 0.01) and Heart_R_Dmean was 0.8 ± 0.2 Gy vs. 1.1 ± 0.4 Gy (p < 0.01). There was no significant difference in Liver_D1cc (p > 0.05). After DDA, significant differences were observed in Breast_Dmean compared to the original plans (O_HY vs. D_HY: 1.0 ± 0.7 Gy vs. 0.9 ± 0.6 Gy, p < 0.05; O_IM vs. D_IM: 1.7 ± 1.1 Gy vs. 1.6 ± 1.1 Gy, p < 0.05), while no statistically significant differences were found in other OAR dose metrics (p > 0.05).

Discussion

Traditional Hybrid and IMRT plans for post-lumpectomy breast cancer have predominantly been compared using static 3D-CT, which do not fully account for anatomical variations during the respiratory cycle. In this study, 4D-CT was acquired, and DDA was applied to incorporate respiratory motion, enabling a systematic evaluation of static and dynamic dosimetric performance. In the original planning, both techniques achieved satisfactory target coverage with comparable hotspot, dose homogeneity, and conformity. The Hybrid plan provided superior OARs sparing, particularly in low-dose regions. After DDA, slight reductions in PTV coverage were observed for both techniques relative to the original plans, while the hotspot remained similar. HI and CI showed slight deterioration, and OAR dose variations were generally stable, with no significant differences except for a statistically lower Breast_Dmean in the IMRT plan. Respiratory amplitude analysis showed generally small chest-wall motion after abdominal breathing training and no meaningful correlations with most dosimetric parameters, except for weak associations between liver motion and PTV_V95% and a significant association with Breast_Dmean in the IMRT plan.

In this study, mean liver displacement was 13.2 ± 4.6 mm, and chest wall displacement was 1.54 ± 0.85 mm, consistent with our previous analysis of 45 patients (liver: 12.5 ± 4.1 mm; chest wall: 1.4 ± 0.7 mm) and prior reports (25, 26), confirming the representativeness of the respiratory data. No chest wall displacement exceeded 3.1 mm, likely reflecting the abdominal breathing training.

The Hybrid plan combines the advantages of 3D-CRT and IMRT. In this study, a representative composition of 70% 3D-CRT and 30% IMRT was adopted, based on institutional planning practice and prior reports in whole-breast irradiation (27). This configuration was selected to balance target coverage, dose homogeneity, and conformity, while preserving the intrinsic robustness of tangential fields against respiratory motion and setup uncertainties (28). In the original plans, both O_HY and O_IM achieved good target coverage, with similar hotspot, HI, and CI, in line with previous studies. Regarding OAR sparing, the O_HY plan significantly reduced ipsilateral lung V20 and V5, contralateral breast Dmean, and heart Dmean compared with the O_IM plan. IMRT plans typically require multiple tangential and segmented fields to achieve dose conformity and homogeneity, which increases low-dose exposure to normal tissues. In contrast, the 3D-CRT component of Hybrid plans limits low-dose spread despite the IMRT component contributing additional beam angles. These findings align with observations by Racka et al. (9), supporting the potential advantage of Hybrid planning for normal tissue sparing, particularly in low-dose regions.

DDA using 4D-CT revealed slight decreases in PTV coverage. Coverage losses were mainly observed at the distal PTV edges and at lung interfaces, where steep dose gradients and interplay effects are pronounced. In lung-adjacent regions, rapid tissue density changes reduce lateral electron scatter, resulting in dose reduction under electronic disequilibrium. In this cohort, PTV_V100% decreased by 2.5% for Hybrid and 2.1% for IMRT. The slightly larger reduction in Hybrid plans is attributable to their 70% 3D-CRT dose contribution: the steep dose fall-off near the lung interface in these fields makes them more susceptible to motion-induced coverage loss. By contrast, IMRT plans benefit from multiple modulated fields with shallower gradients, resulting in slightly smaller reductions. Previous studies(22) indicate that respiratory motion below 5 mm does not produce clinically significant dose changes in free-breathing whole-breast irradiation, whereas motion exceeding 5 mm may reduce PTV edge coverage by 1–4%. These observations support our findings, as VMAT plans with more beam angles maintain more stable PTV coverage during respiration, albeit with increased low-dose exposure to normal tissues. Although the Hybrid technique is dominated by 3D-CRT, it is not equivalent to the pure 3D-CRT plans reported by Mankinen et al. (22), which relied primarily on tangential open fields with minimal contribution from subfields. This design produced more gradual dose fall-off and greater intrinsic robustness, particularly at superficial margins near the skin. The use of highly weighted tangential fields in their 3D-CRT plan is analogous to the “skin flash” commonly employed in IMRT plans. In the present study, skin flash was intentionally omitted to allow a conservative comparison between Hybrid and IMRT under limited chest wall motion after breathing training. Its inclusion would likely further enhance target robustness and should be considered in routine clinical practice. Hotspot remained largely unchanged, while HI increased and CI decreased slightly, reflecting blurring at high-dose edges due to respiratory motion. Among all OAR metrics, only the Breast_Dmean in the IMRT plan showed a statistically significant reduction relative to the original plan, whereas the Hybrid plan exhibited a numerically smaller but non-significant decrease. This minor dose reduction is likely attributable to subtle respiratory-induced changes in breast volume and position that shift parts of the tissue slightly away from the beam. Importantly, although statistically detectable, the difference is clinically negligible. No other OAR metrics demonstrated statistically significant changes compared with the original plan. It should be noted that different hybrid dose ratios may alter the balance between robustness and OAR sparing. Increasing the IMRT component generally improves dose conformity and can reduce high doses to critical structures, but this is often accompanied by a wider spread of low doses to organs at risk, such as the lung or heart, due to multiple beam segments or modulated fields. In contrast, a higher proportion of 3D‑CRT tends to preserve the intrinsic robustness of tangential dose distributions with more confined low‑dose spillage, but may limit dose modulation and conformity. A systematic evaluation of alternative hybrid compositions was beyond the scope of the present study and warrants further investigation to clarify these trade‑offs in clinical practice.

Respiratory motion data alone did not reliably predict DDA differences, likely because post-lumpectomy breast patients exhibit limited chest wall motion, primarily affecting high-dose edge blurring rather than mid-to-low dose regions. Although weak correlations were observed between PTV_V95% and liver motion, and a moderate association was found between contralateral breast mean dose and liver motion in IMRT plans, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory given the small sample size and interpatient variability.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Owing to the application of breathing training and the limited sample size, no cases of pronounced or irregular respiratory motion were observed, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Under these conditions, free-breathing dose delivery was approximated using equal-weight phase-based DDA. While reasonable for small motion amplitudes, this approach does not explicitly model beam–phase interplay and may underestimate dose variations in regions with steep dose gradients. Moreover, uncertainties associated with DIR and dose mapping may affect the accuracy of accumulated dose, particularly at high-gradient tissue interfaces such as the lung–chest wall boundary. Finally, DDA was based on a single 4D-CT acquisition per patient and therefore does not capture inter-fractional anatomical variability; repeated 4D imaging would be required for a more comprehensive assessment.

Conclusion

In this cohort, chest wall motion amplitude after breathing training remained within 3.1 mm. Under these conditions, Hybrid and IMRT plans demonstrate comparable target dose robustness, while Hybrid achieves superior OAR sparing, particularly in the low-dose region. Based on this comparison, Hybrid planning may be considered a favorable option relative to IMRT for hypofractionated whole-breast irradiation under free breathing.
