The EAR motif-containing adaptor protein ECAP enhances ABI5-mediated ABA signaling to inhibit seed germination in Arabidopsis
Authors: Xing Li, Jian Zhang, Chenming Xu, Lili Wang, Shunping Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe phytohormone abscisic acid (ABA) regulates diverse biological processes, including seed germination, in which the transcription factor abscisic acid insensitive 5 (ABI5) plays an essential role. Here, we show that the EAR motif-containing adaptor protein (ECAP) functions as a positive regulator of ABI5 to inhibit seed germination in Arabidopsis. Like ABI5, ECAP is required for the inhibition of seed germination by ABA. Transcriptome analysis reveals that ABI5 and ECAP regulate the expression of a common set of ABA-responsive genes. Biochemically, ECAP enhances the expression of ABI5, interacts with ABI5 to promote its transcriptional activity, and binds to the promoters of ABA-responsive genes in an ABI5-dependent manner. Genetically, loss of ABI5 compromises the enhanced ABA sensitivity of the ECAP overexpression line, and overexpression of ABI5 partially rescues the reduced ABA sensitivity of the ecap mutant. Together, our results suggest that ECAP and ABI5 function coordinately to fine-tune the inhibition of seed germination by ABA.Key words: seed germination, abscisic acid, transcription, ECAP, ABI5

Introduction

The precise control of seed germination and post-germination growth is essential for plant adaptation to environmental changes and for agricultural productivity (Finkelstein et al., 2002). In agriculture, the premature germination of crops (preharvest sprouting) causes substantial yield losses, posing a global challenge to food security (Xu et al., 2022). Various endogenous and exogenous signals regulate seed germination and post-germination growth (Finch-Savage and Leubner-Metzger, 2006). Among them, the phytohormone abscisic acid (ABA) plays key roles in promoting dormancy, inhibiting germination, and suppressing early seedling establishment (Gubler et al., 2005). In the core ABA signaling pathway, ABA is perceived and bound by the ABA receptors pyrabactin resistance 1 (PYR1)/PYR1-like (PYL)/regulatory component of ABA receptor (RCAR) (Ma et al., 2009; Nishimura et al., 2009; Park et al., 2009; Santiago et al., 2009). The ABA-receptor complex then inhibits the type 2C protein phosphatases (PP2Cs), thereby releasing the inhibition of SNF1-related kinase 2 (SnRK2) protein kinases (Ma et al., 2009; Miyazono et al., 2009; Park et al., 2009; Cutler et al., 2010). Subsequently, SnRK2s phosphorylate and activate a series of downstream transcription factors, including abscisic acid insensitive 5 (ABI5) and its homologs, the abscisic acid-responsive element binding factors (ABFs), thereby mediating ABA responses (Fujii et al., 2009; Nakashima and Yamaguchi-Shinozaki, 2013; Yoshida et al., 2014).

ABI5 has been reported to function as a core transcription factor in ABA-mediated inhibition of seed germination and post-germination growth (Finkelstein and Lynch, 2000; Carles et al., 2002; Yu et al., 2015). Loss of ABI5 leads to ABA insensitivity, whereas overexpression of ABI5 results in ABA hypersensitivity (Finkelstein and Lynch, 2000; Lopez-Molina and Chua, 2000). ABI5 can bind to ABA-responsive elements (ABREs), which are present in the promoters of numerous ABA-responsive genes such as late embryogenesis abundant 1 (EM1) and EM6, thereby activating their expression to inhibit seed germination (Carles et al., 2002). Previous studies have shown that ABI5 activity is tightly regulated by its interactors (Yu et al., 2015; Du et al., 2025). Phosphorylation mediated by SnRK2 enhances its transcriptional activity (Fujii et al., 2007); the E3 ubiquitin ligases KEG, ABD1, PUB8, and PUB35 ubiquitinate ABI5 to promote its degradation through the 26S proteasome (Liu and Stone, 2010; Seo et al., 2014; Li et al., 2023; Du et al., 2024); and the E3 SUMO ligase SIZ1 SUMOylates ABI5 to enhance its stability (Miura et al., 2009). In addition, many transcriptional co-regulators have been reported to control the transcriptional activity of ABI5: ICE1, BES1, JAZ, CycC1;1, and MED25 negatively regulate the transcriptional activity of ABI5 (Hu et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2019; Pan et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2021, 2022), whereas MED16 and PRR5 regulate it positively (Guo et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2021).

EAR motif-containing adaptor protein (ECAP) was initially identified as an adaptor protein that represses gene expression. Specifically, ECAP connects jasmonate (JA) zim domain 6/8 (JAZ6/8) and co-repressor topless-related 2 (TPR2) to form a transcriptional inhibitory complex that represses JA-induced anthocyanin accumulation (Li et al., 2020). In addition, ECAP also inhibits the transcriptional activity of MYB75 to negatively regulate salt-stress responses (Li et al., 2022). Recently, ECAP was found to interact with the co-repressor leunig (LUG) and the BES1/BZR1 homolog 3 (BEH3) transcription factor to form a transcriptional activator complex that regulates anther development (Shi et al., 2024). It is possible that ECAP has many other functions that remain to be studied.

Here, we report that ECAP functions as a positive regulator of ABI5, thereby inhibiting seed germination and post-germination growth. ECAP enhances the expression of ABI5 and also interacts with ABI5 to promote its transcriptional activation activity. We propose that ECAP and ABI5 function coordinately to fine-tune ABA signaling and thus seed germination and post-germination growth.

Results

ECAP is required for ABA-mediated inhibition of seed germination

To identify new regulators of ABA signaling, we tested the inhibition of seed germination by ABA using Arabidopsis mutants available in our laboratory. We found that the germination rate of the T-DNA insertion mutant ecap-2 was significantly higher than that of the wild type (WT) on medium containing ABA (Figures 1A and 1B). The post-germination growth of ecap-2 seedlings was also better than that of the WT, with a higher percentage of green cotyledons (Figure 1C). To confirm the roles of ECAP in these processes, we transformed ECAP-GFP driven by the 35S promoter into ecap-2. As shown in Figures 1A–1C, the germination rate and percentage of green cotyledons in ecap-2 were restored to WT levels in the resulting transgenic lines (35S:ECAP-GFP/ecap-2). The expression level of ECAP was confirmed through quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT–qPCR) (Supplemental Figure 1). We also examined the ABA response of ecap-4, a recently reported null mutant of ECAP (Li et al., 2020). As observed for ecap-2, the germination rate and percentage of green cotyledons of ecap-4 were significantly higher than those of the WT after ABA treatment (Figures 1A–1C). Collectively, these results suggest that ECAP is required for ABA-mediated inhibition of seed germination and post-germination growth.

ECAP and ABI5 regulate a common set of ABA-responsive genes

To further investigate the roles of ECAP in ABA-mediated inhibition of seed germination, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of WT and ecap-2 grown on medium with or without ABA. Because ABI5 plays a central role in ABA-mediated inhibition of seed germination, we also included the reported abi5-8 mutant (Xu et al., 2019; Du et al., 2024). Data analysis revealed that 5400 genes were upregulated after ABA treatment in the WT (Figure 2A and Supplemental Table 1). Compared with the WT, 4920 and 2601 genes were downregulated in abi5-8 and ecap-2 after ABA treatment, respectively (Figure 2A and Supplemental Table 1). A Venn diagram revealed that the majority of genes downregulated in abi5-8 (3780 or 76%) and ecap-2 (1692 or 65%) were upregulated after ABA treatment in the WT (Figure 2A), providing strong evidence that both ABI5 and ECAP are required for the expression of ABA-responsive genes. The majority of genes downregulated in ecap-2 (1887 or 72%) were also downregulated in abi5–8 (Figure 2A), indicating that ECAP and ABI5 have common roles in regulating the expression of ABA-responsive genes. Among these 1887 genes, 1532 (81%) were upregulated in the WT after ABA treatment (Figure 2A). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of these 1532 genes revealed significant enrichment of genes involved in seed development, response to abscisic acid, and response to seed maturation (Figure 2B). A heatmap shows the expression levels of some of these genes (Figure 2C). To confirm the RNA-seq results, we performed RT–qPCR of two marker genes involved in seed germination (EM1 and EM6) and two well-known ABA-responsive genes (RAB18 and RD29B). Consistent with previous studies (Ji et al., 2019), the expression of EM1, EM6, RAB18, and RD29B increased dramatically after ABA treatment (Figure 2D). Their expression levels were significantly lower in abi5-8 and ecap-2 than in the WT (Figure 2D). In addition, their expression was much lower in abi5-8 than in ecap-2, indicating that ABI5 plays a more important role than ECAP. Taken together, these results suggest that ECAP and ABI5 regulate a common set of ABA-responsive genes.

ECAP physically interacts with ABI5

Previous studies have shown that ECAP functions as a transcriptional regulator mainly by interacting with other transcriptional regulators or transcription factors (Li et al., 2020, 2022). The co-regulation of ABA-responsive genes by ECAP and ABI5 prompted us to test whether ECAP functions by interacting with ABI5. First, we performed yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays. ECAP was fused to the DNA-binding domain (BD), and ABI5 was fused to the activation domain (AD). Only yeast cells co-expressing BD-ECAP and AD-ABI5 grew on the selective medium (Figure 3A), indicating that ECAP and ABI5 interact specifically in yeast cells. To test whether they interact in planta, we performed split luciferase assays. ECAP was fused with N-terminal firefly luciferase (nLUC), and ABI5 was fused with C-terminal firefly luciferase (cLUC). β-glucuronidase (GUS) was used as a negative control. Consistent with the Y2H results, a strong luminescence signal was detected when nLUC-ECAP was co-expressed with ABI5-cLUC (Figure 3B). To further confirm their in planta interaction, we carried out bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays and co-immunoprecipitation (coIP) assays. In BiFC assays, ECAP was fused with C-terminal YFP (cYFP), and ABI5 was fused with N-terminal YFP (nYFP). We detected strong YFP fluorescence in the nucleus when ECAP-cYFP was co-expressed with ABI5-nYFP (Figure 3C), indicating that ECAP and ABI5 interact in the nucleus. For coIP assays, we transformed 35S:ECAP-GFP into the previously described 35S:ABI5-4Myc line (Yang et al., 2021). We found that more ABI5-4Myc protein was immunoprecipitated by the anti-GFP antibody when ECAP-GFP protein was co-expressed (Figure 3D). To examine whether the interaction is direct or indirect, we performed pull-down assays using purified recombinant proteins. As shown in Figure 3E, GST-ECAP proteins were pulled down by MBP-ABI5 proteins, indicating that ECAP directly interacts with ABI5. To map the domains that mediate the ECAP–ABI5 interaction, we performed Y2H assays using protein truncations. We found that the C terminus of ECAP, but not the N terminus of ECAP, interacts with ABI5 (Figure 3F), suggesting that the EAR motif is not required for the ECAP–ABI5 interaction. For ABI5, only the fragment containing the bZIP domain interacts with ECAP, indicating that the bZIP domain is essential for the ECAP–ABI5 interaction (Figure 3G). Taken together, these results demonstrate that ECAP physically interacts with ABI5 in an EAR domain-independent manner.

ECAP promotes the transcriptional activation activity of ABI5

The findings that ECAP and ABI5 interact and regulate a common set of ABA-responsive genes led us to test whether ECAP promotes the transcriptional activity of ABI5. To this end, we performed dual-luciferase reporter assays. The effector constructs contained ABI5 or ECAP driven by the 35S promoter, and the reporter construct contained firefly luciferase (LUC) driven by the EM1 promoter and Renilla luciferase (REN) driven by the 35S promoter (Figure 4A). The ratio of LUC to REN represented the activity of the EM1 promoter. Different combinations of effector and reporter constructs were co-transfected into Arabidopsis protoplasts. Consistent with previous studies (Yang et al., 2021; Guo et al., 2022), expression of ABI5 significantly enhanced the activity of the EM1 promoter. Importantly, co-expression of ECAP with ABI5 further enhanced the activity of the EM1 promoter (Figure 4B), suggesting that ECAP promotes the transcriptional activation activity of ABI5. Consistent with this notion, RT–qPCR analysis showed that loss of ECAP significantly reduced the transcript levels of EM1 (Figure 4C).

Given that ECAP interacts with ABI5 and enhances its transcriptional activation activity, we hypothesized that ECAP and ABI5 can bind to the same promoter regions of ABI5 target genes. To test this possibility, we crossed the 35S:ECAP-GFP/WT transgenic line with abi5-8 to generate 35S:ECAP-GFP/abi5-8 and performed chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays. RT–qPCR showed that the transcript levels of ECAP were similar in 35S:ECAP-GFP/WT and 35S:ECAP-GFP/abi5-8 (Supplemental Figure 3). Previous studies have shown that ABI5 binds to the pEM1 region (Figure 4D) in the EM1 promoter (Chen et al., 2012; Yang et al., 2021), and we also found that ECAP-GFP bound to pEM1 (Figure 4E). More importantly, this binding was significantly reduced in abi5-8 (Figure 4F). Therefore, ECAP binds to the promoters of ABI5 target genes in an ABI5-dependent manner.

ECAP promotes the transcription of ABI5

In the RNA-seq analysis, we unexpectedly found that the expression of ABI5 was reduced in the ecap-2 mutant (Figure 2C). To confirm this result, we carried out RT–qPCR analysis, which revealed that ABI5 expression was significantly lower in the ecap-2 mutant than in the WT (Figure 5A). This result suggested that ECAP might positively regulate the expression of ABI5. To test this possibility, we performed dual-luciferase reporter assays using a reporter construct expressing the LUC gene driven by the ABI5 promoter (Figure 5B). Compared with the empty vector (EV), the effector vector expressing ECAP driven by the 35S promoter significantly increased the LUC/REN ratio (Figure 5C), supporting the positive role of ECAP in regulating the expression of ABI5. To further support this notion, we tested whether ECAP binds to the ABI5 promoter by performing ChIP assays with 35S:ECAP-GFP transgenic plants. We examined the enrichment of ECAP-GFP at multiple regions (P1–P5) of the ABI5 promoter (Figure 5D) and found that ECAP-GFP bound to the ABI5 promoter at P1–P4 but not P5 (Figure 5E). In addition, the expression of ABI5 was significantly higher in 35S:ECAP-GFP/ecap-2 than in ecap-2 in both mock-treated and ABA-treated samples (Figure 5F). These results suggest that ECAP binds to the promoter of ABI5 and promotes ABI5 transcription. Because ECAP does not contain a DNA-binding domain (Li et al., 2020, 2022), we speculated that ECAP may be recruited to the ABI5 promoter by other transcription factors.

ABI5 functions downstream of ECAP

Because ECAP positively regulates the transcriptional activation activity of ABI5 and the transcription of ABI5, we reasoned that ABI5 functions downstream of ECAP. To test this possibility genetically, we examined the ABA sensitivities of WT, 35S:ECAP-GFP/WT, abi5–8, and 35S:ECAP-GFP/abi5-8. Compared with the WT, 35S:ECAP-GFP/WT was hypersensitive to ABA, whereas 35S:ECAP-GFP/abi5-8 was insensitive to ABA (Figures 6A–6C), providing evidence that ABI5 functions downstream of ECAP. We next crossed 35S:ABI5-4Myc/WT with ecap-4 to generate 35S:ABI5-4Myc/ecap-4. Consistent with previous studies (Hu et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2021), the 35S:ABI5-4Myc/WT line was hypersensitive to ABA, showing a lower germination rate and percentage of green cotyledons than the WT (Figure 6D). Notably, compared with ecap-4, 35S:ABI5-4Myc/ecap-4 showed a significantly reduced germination rate and percentage of green cotyledons (Figures 6D–6F), indicating that overexpression of ABI5 suppressed the insensitivity of ecap-4 to ABA, providing additional evidence that ABI5 functions downstream of ECAP. We generated an ecap-2 abi5-8 double mutant through a genetic cross. Under ABA treatment, the ecap-2 abi5-8 double mutant was less sensitive to ABA than either ecap-2 or abi5-8 (Supplemental Figure 3), suggesting that ECAP may also regulate ABA signaling in an ABI5-independent manner. Together, these data suggest that ECAP regulates ABA responses in both an ABI5-dependent and an ABI5-independent manner.

Discussion

On the basis of the above results, we propose a simplified working model to illustrate how ECAP regulates ABI5 to inhibit seed germination (Figure 7). First, ECAP binds to the promoter of ABI5 to promote ABI5 transcription, possibly by interacting with other transcription factors. In addition, ECAP interacts with ABI5 to promote its transcriptional activation activity. ECAP and ABI5 thus function together to inhibit seed germination by inducing the expression of ABA-responsive genes (e.g., EM1 and EM6).

A simplified working model showing how ECAP functions in seed germination.

Under control conditions, ABI5 is not activated owing to the low level of ABA, thus inhibiting the transcription of ABA-responsive genes (e.g., EM1 and EM6) and allowing seed germination. When the ABA level is high, ABI5 is activated, promoting the transcription of ABA-responsive genes and thereby inhibiting seed germination. ECAP promotes the functions of ABI5 in two ways. First, ECAP interacts with ABI5 to promote its transcriptional activation activity. In addition, ECAP binds to the promoter of ABI5 through an unidentified transcription factor (X) to promote ABI5 transcription.

Our study identifies ECAP as a novel regulator of ABA signaling and seed germination. Seed germination of the ecap mutant was insensitive to ABA-mediated inhibition (Figure 1), providing strong evidence that ECAP is a positive regulator of ABA signaling. Mechanistically, ECAP functions by promoting one of the central regulators of ABA signaling, ABI5, as supported by multiple lines of evidence. ECAP and ABI5 regulate a common set of ABA-responsive genes (Figure 2) and interact with each other (Figure 3) to promote ABI5 activity (Figure 4). Furthermore, ECAP promoted the transcription of ABI5 (Figure 5). More importantly, loss of ABI5 compromises the enhanced ABA sensitivity of the ECAP overexpression line, and overexpression of ABI5 partially rescues the reduced ABA sensitivity of the ecap mutant (Figure 6). Although many laboratories have performed genetic screens to identify ABA-insensitive mutants, the ecap mutant was not identified, indicating that these screens may not have been saturated or suitable for the identification of weak mutants. Therefore, it is expected that additional ABA signaling components remain to be discovered through more careful and dedicated study.

This study also revealed a novel function of ECAP. ECAP has only recently been characterized, and its known biological functions include JA-induced anthocyanin accumulation (Li et al., 2020), salt stress response (Li et al., 2022), and anther development (Shi et al., 2024). Here, we found that ECAP positively regulates ABA signaling to inhibit seed germination. The roles of ECAP in seed germination were independently confirmed by Fu’s group in a paper co-submitted with our manuscript. Our work and that of Fu's group also independently found that ECAP promotes flowering. Therefore, as an adaptor protein, ECAP may interact with various proteins to regulate diverse biological processes, a possibility that warrants further investigation in future work.

ECAP contains an EAR motif, which is generally believed to function by repressing gene expression. Indeed, the EAR motif of ECAP was shown to be required for its repression of gene expression (Li et al., 2020). However, ECAP was recently reported to form a transcriptional activation complex by interacting with LUG and BEH3 to promote the expression of SPL (Shi et al., 2024). In the present study, we demonstrate that ECAP promotes the expression of ABI5 and the transcriptional activation activity of ABI5 (Figures 5C and 4B), providing further evidence that ECAP can function by activating gene expression. Interestingly, we found that the EAR motif is not required for the ECAP–ABI5 interaction (Figure 3F), suggesting that the function of ECAP in promoting gene expression may rely on its C terminus, possibly by recruiting other proteins.

The finding that the ecap-2 abi5-8 double mutant was less sensitive to ABA than ecap-2 and abi5-8 (Supplemental Figure 3A) suggestes that ECAP can also regulate ABA signaling in an ABI5-independent manner. Other transcription factors, such as ABI3 and ABI4, are also known to play key roles in ABA-mediated germination inhibition (Finkelstein et al., 2002). Therefore, it will be interesting to test the relationship between ECAP and ABI3/4, which could further enhance our understanding of ECAP-mediated ABA signaling networks.

ECAP binds to multiple regions of the ABI5 promoter and regulates the transcription of ABI5 (Figure 5E). Because ECAP lacks a DNA-binding domain, it may be recruited to the ABI5 promoter through another transcription factor. ABI5 has been reported to bind to its own promoter (Xu et al., 2014), and one good candidate is therefore ABI5 itself. Because ABI3, ABI4, ABF3, HY5, AGL21, and RAV1 can also bind to the ABI5 promoter to activate ABI5 expression (Lopez-Molina et al., 2002; Chen et al., 2008; Bossi et al., 2009; Feng et al., 2014; Yu et al., 2017; Chang et al., 2019), they are also potential candidates. Further studies are needed to address this question.

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

All Arabidopsis thaliana plants used in this study were in the Col-0 background. The Col-0 ecotype served as the WT. The ecap-2 (SALK_009978) (Li et al., 2020), ecap-4 (Li et al., 2020), abi5-8 (SALK_013163) (Guan et al., 2014), and 35S:ABI5-4Myc plants (Yang et al., 2021) were described previously. Transgenic plants were generated using the floral dip method (Clough and Bent, 1998). Seeds were surface sterilized with 2% plant preservative mixture (PPM, Plant Cell Technology), stratified at 4°C in the dark for 2 days, and then plated on half-strength Murashige and Skoog (1/2 MS) medium supplemented with 1% sucrose and 0.4% Phytagel (CP8581Z, Coolaber). Plants were grown in a growth chamber at 22°C under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod.

Seed germination and cotyledon greening assays

Germination and cotyledon greening assays were performed as described previously (Yang et al., 2023). Surface-sterilized seeds were stratified at 4°C, then sown on 1/2 MS medium with or without ABA. Germination was indicated by the appearance of the embryonic radicle under a stereomicroscope. The cotyledon greening rate was calculated as the percentage of seedlings with fully expanded green cotyledons. For each replicate, at least 60 seeds per genotype were examined.

RNA extraction and RT–qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (RN0102, Aidlab) according to the manufacturer’s handbook. RNA was resuspended in RNase-free water (CW0612M, CWBIO) and reverse transcribed using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (+gDNA wiper) (R123, Vazyme). qPCR was performed with 2× Universal SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (RK21203, ABclonal) on a fluorescent quantitative PCR detection system (Q2000B, LongGene). ACTIN8 or UBQ5 was used as an internal control. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

RNA-seq and data analysis

For RNA-seq analysis, 300 mg seeds were surface sterilized and grown on 1/2 MS medium plates with or without 0.7 μM ABA for 5 days in a growth chamber at 22°C under long-day conditions. All germinated seeds were harvested for RNA isolation using a Plant Seed RNA kit (MF7327, Mei5 Biotech). RNA-seq was performed by Novogene Technology (Beijing). Paired-end sequencing with 150-bp reads (NovaSeq-PE150) was performed on the Illumina platform. Raw data were processed and aligned to the Arabidopsis genome TAIR 10 (https://www.arabidopsis.org) using HISAT2 version 2.2.1. Gene read counts were determined using FeatureCounts (v.2.0.8). Differentially expressed genes were identified with DESeq2 (|log2 (fold change)| > log21.35, p < 0.05). GO enrichment analysis was carried out using Metascape (https://metascape.org/gp) (Zhou et al., 2019).

Plasmid construction

All constructs used in this study were generated using the Lightening Cloning kit (BDIT0014, Biodragon Immunotechnology). To generate 35S:ECAP-GFP/WT transgenic plants, the coding sequence (CDS) of ECAP was fused with GFP and cloned into pFGC5941 at the NcoI and XbaI sites. For split luciferase assays, the CDS of ECAP was cloned into KpnI/SalI-digested JW772, and the CDS of ABI5 was cloned into BamHI/SalI-digested JW771. For BiFC assays, the CDS of ECAP was cloned into the BamHI/SalI-digested pSPYCE vector, and the ABI5 CDS was cloned into the BamHI/SalI-digested pSPYNE vector. For Y2H assays, the CDS of ABI5 was cloned into EcoRI/BamHI-digested pGADT7, and the ECAP CDS was cloned into EcoRI/BamHI-digested pGBKT7. For pull-down assays, the ECAP CDS was cloned into BamHI/XhoI-digested pGEX-6P-1, and the ABI5 CDS was cloned into EcoRI/SalI-digested pMAL-C2X. For dual-luciferase reporter assays, promoter fragments of EM1 and ABI5 (1.51 kb upstream of the EM1 start codon, ATG; 2.0 kb upstream of the ABI5 start codon, ATG) were cloned into the KpnI/BamHI-digested pGreenII-0800-LUC vector. The CDSs of ABI5 and ECAP were cloned into the pFGC5941 vector. Primer sequences are listed in Supplemental Table 2.

Split luciferase assay

The split luciferase assay was performed as described previously (Yu et al., 2023). In brief, different combinations of Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 were co-infiltrated into the leaves of 4-week-old Nicotiana benthamiana. After 48 h, 1 mM luciferin was applied to the leaves, and luminescence was captured using the Lumazone imaging system equipped with a 2048B charge-coupled device (CCD) camera (Roper, Sarasota, FL, USA).

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation assay

Different combinations of A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 were co-injected into the leaves of 4-week-old N. benthamiana. After 2 days, YFP fluorescence was imaged using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope.

Yeast two-hybrid assay

The Y2H assay was performed as described previously (Chen et al., 2023). In brief, the AD and BD constructs were co-transformed into yeast strain AH109 using the polyethylene glycol/lithium acetate method. Protein interactions were assessed by the growth of co-transformed yeast cells on selective medium (SD/−Leu/−Trp/−His/−Ade) after 3–4 days.

Pull-down assay

Pull-down assays were performed as described previously (Yu et al., 2023). In brief, MBP, MBP-ABI5, and GST-ECAP proteins were expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) under induction conditions (0.5 mM IPTG, 16°C, 20 h). GST-ECAP was purified using glutathione beads (SA008005, Smart-Lifesciences). MBP or MBP-ABI5 coupled with dextrin beads (SA077005, Smart-Lifesciences) was incubated with purified GST-ECAP protein in PBS buffer (10 mM Na2HPO4, 1.8 mM KH2PO4, and 140 mM NaCl [pH 7.4]) at 4°C for 2 h, and then washed three times with washing buffer (PBS buffer plus 500 mM NaCl and 0.5% Triton X-100). After washing, the beads were boiled in 2× SDS loading buffer, followed by immunoblotting analysis using anti-MBP (AE016, ABclonal) and anti-GST (AE001, ABclonal) antibodies.

Co-immunoprecipitation (coIP) assay

The coIP assay was carried out as described previously (Yu et al., 2022). Seven-day-old 35S:ECAP-GFP/35S:ABI5-4Myc and 35S:ABI5-4Myc seedlings were treated with 50 μM ABA for 6 h. The seedlings were then ground in liquid nitrogen and resuspended in IP buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 10% glycerol, 0.1% NP-40, 0.5 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, 100 μM MG132, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail) for total protein extraction. The lysates were incubated with anti-GFP magnetic beads (L-1016, Biolinkedin) for 2 h at 4°C. The beads were then washed three times with washing buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 250–500 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM PMSF, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail), and the bound proteins were immunoblotted with anti-Myc (AE009, ABclonal) and anti-GFP (11814460001, Roche) antibodies.

Dual-luciferase assay

The dual-luciferase assay was performed as described previously (Wang et al., 2018). The reporter and effector constructs were co-expressed in Arabidopsis protoplasts or N. benthamiana leaves. LUC and REN activities were measured using a dual-luciferase reporter assay system (DL101, Vazyme) on a Mithras LB 940 multifunctional microplate reader (Berthold Technologies, Bad Wildbad, Germany).

ChIP–qPCR

The ChIP assay was performed as described previously (Yu et al., 2023). In brief, 5-day-old 35S:ECAP-GFP/WT and 35S:ECAP-GFP/abi5–8 transgenic seedlings were treated with 50 μM ABA for 6 h. Then, 0.5 g of seedlings was cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature under vacuum. Glycine was added to a final concentration of 0.2 M to quench the cross-linking reaction. The seedlings were washed with ice-cold water and ground in liquid nitrogen. The fine powder was lysed for 10 min at 4°C with 300 μl of buffer S (50 mM HEPES–KOH pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate, 1% SDS, and 1 mM PMSF). The homogenate was mixed with 1.2 ml of buffer F (50 mM HEPES–KOH pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate, and 1 mM PMSF) and then sonicated using a Bioruptor Plus sonication system (Diagenode, Liege, Belgium) to shear the DNA into fragments of 250–600 bp. The lysate was centrifuged at 4°C for 10 min at 20,000 g, and the supernatant was transferred to a new tube containing protein G beads (C600022, Sangon). The precleared chromatin was then incubated overnight at 4°C with an anti-GFP (11814460001, Roche) antibody/protein G beads complex. The immunoprecipitated chromatin was washed sequentially with low-salt ChIP buffer (50 mM HEPES–KOH, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate, and 0.1% SDS), high-salt ChIP buffer (150 mM NaCl in low salt ChIP buffer replaced with 350 mM NaCl), ChIP wash buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 250 mM LiCl, 0.5% NP-40, 1 mM EDTA, and 0.1% sodium deoxycholate), and TE buffer. Protein–DNA complexes were eluted from the beads by incubating for 15 min at 65°C with freshly prepared ChIP elution buffer (1% SDS and 0.1 M NaHCO3). The protein–DNA complexes were mixed with 0.2 M NaCl and incubated at 65°C for 10 h to reverse the cross-links, then digested with proteinase K (IPA13830, Solarbio, Beijing, China) at 45°C for 2 h. The purified DNA samples were used for qPCR analysis.
