pH-triggered liposomal strategy for cisplatin in lung cancer therapy
Authors: Gulen Melike Demirbolat, Egemen Cakirli, Alkim Beste Saglam, Sevgi Sarigul-Ozbek, Burcin Irem Abas, Ozge Cevik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cisplatin (Cis) is the first-line chemotherapy for treating the non-small-cell lung cancer. However, its low solubility, low bioavailability, and potential side effects limit its use. To overcome these drawbacks, novel pH-sensitive liposomal Cis formulations have been developed using a rapid and practical ethanol injection technique. In this study, two different liposome types were prepared, one based on phosphatidylcholine and cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHEMS), the other containing 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DOPE) in combination with CHEMS. Their homogeneity, smaller particle sizes (< 200 nm) and drug integration were confirmed using the DLS method and FT-IR and SEM–EDS, respectively. The loading efficiency was changed from 35 to 50% depending on the composition. In vitro drug release studies showed a minimum release at physiological pH (7.4) and a significantly increased release at acidic pH (5.5), in particularly for DOPE liposomes, indicating the higher pH-sensitivity. Cytotoxicity analyses performed in A549 lung cancer cell line showed that both liposomal formulations exhibited stronger antitumour effects compared to free Cis. This effect was supported by increased apoptotic activity confirmed by Annexin-V/PI staining method. These findings suggest that the pH-sensitive liposomes developed in this study offer a promising and scalable approach to enhance the selective delivery and therapeutic efficacy of Cis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20144-0.

Introduction

Cancer remains a critical global health concern. Among the various cancer types, lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related illness and death worldwide, making a substantial contribution to the incidence of new cancer cases and overall cancer mortality1. It is primarily divided into two types: non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small-cell lung cancer. Because NSCLC accounts for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases, its treatment is of significant importance2. Chemotherapy, particularly cisplatin (Cis)-based regimens, has long served as a key treatment approach for NSCLC, particularly in advanced stages where surgery is not an option3. Cis, an antineoplastic agent, works by forming crosslinks in the DNA of cancer cells, which disrupts DNA replication and transcription, ultimately triggering apoptosis or programmed cell death. However, its use is often limited by challenges such as low solubility, low bioavailability, and significant side effects, including nephrotoxicity and myelosuppression4. This has prompted the development of new drug formulations aimed at enhancing treatment efficacy while minimizing adverse effects. Nanotechnology has emerged as a promising alternative. By manipulating materials at the nanoscale, nanotechnology offers innovative approaches for improving drug delivery and reducing the toxicity associated with traditional chemotherapeutics, such as Cis. Among the most promising applications of nanotechnology in cancer treatment is the development of liposomal formulations, which enable more precise drug delivery to tumor sites5. Liposomes, spherical vesicles composed of lipid bilayers surrounding an aqueous core, enhance tumor targeting through the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect. This allows for higher Cis concentrations in tumor tissues while reducing damage to healthy tissues, thereby significantly lowering the systemic toxicity of Cis, particularly its nephrotoxic and ototoxic side effects6.

pH-dependent liposomes are particularly designed to release their contents when exposed to acidic environments7. In tumors, the extracellular pH typically ranges from 6.0 to 7.0, whereas in normal tissues and blood, the extracellular pH is approximately 7.4. Notably, the acidity increased with increasing distance from the tumor microvasculature. These highly acidic regions are often inaccessible to conventional liposomes, leading to therapeutic inefficacy8,9. Furthermore, the pH of the tumor interstitium rarely drops < 6.5. This narrow pH range makes the development of liposomal formulations that can destabilize under these conditions particularly challenging.

Conventional liposomes often exhibit slow release of active substances and may fail to fuse effectively with endosomes following cellular internalization. In contrast, pH-dependent liposomes undergo destabilization when exposed to endosomal pH levels < 5, thereby preventing degradation at the lysosomal stage. This mechanism promotes the release of the encapsulated drug into the cytoplasm. Thus, the compounds encapsulated in these liposomes are intended to be delivered into the cytoplasm through mechanisms such as destabilization or fusion with the endosomal membrane10,11. Thus, pH-dependent liposomes are being developed as an alternative to conventional liposomes for effectively targeting anticancer drugs to tumors. Their ability to overcome the limitations of conventional liposomes highlights their potential as a promising strategy in cancer therapy.

This study aimed to develop two liposomal formulations based on phosphatidylcholine (PC) and cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHEMS), the other containing 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DOPE) in combination with CHEMS. Their pH dependence characteristics relied on using either cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHEMS) alone or CHEMS in combination with (DOPE) liposomes. These small unilamellar liposomes were efficiently produced using a rapid and straightforward solvent evaporation technique eliminating the need for additional size-reduction steps. Unlike the widely used thin-film hydration method followed by extrusion—which is time-consuming, energy-intensive, requires additional laboratory equipment, and poses challenges for scale-up—this approach offers a more practical and scalable alternative with high reproducibility. It was also conducted a comparative analysis in terms of their physicochemical characteristics and cellular behavior.

Methods

Materials

The materials used for the preparation of the liposomes included 1,2-dimyristoyl-sn- glycerol-3-phosphatidylcholine (DMPC) and phosphatidylcholine (PC), which were gifted by Lipoid GMBH (Germany). 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DOPE), cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHEMS), and 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[amino(polyethylene glycol)-2000] (DSPE-PEG2000) were obtained from Avanti Polar Lipids (USA). Cisplatin, sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), methanol, and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) tablets were procured from Sigma–Aldrich (USA). A549 cell line (ATCC-CCL-185) was purchased from ATCC. All other chemicals and reagents used in the study were of analytical grade. Moreover, distilled water was used in all the experimental procedures to ensure consistency and reliability.

Determination method of Cis by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)

An HPLC system (1260 Infinity Agilent Technologies, USA) equipped with diode array detection was used to determine Cis in the liposomes. The mobile phases comprised 0.5-mM sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) solution (pH 6.0) and HPLC-grade methanol (70:30)12. The flow rate was set to 0.5 mL/min. A C18 column (KP RPPX 5 μm) was used as the stationary phase. To generate a calibration curve, Cis was dissolved in a mixture of methanol and PBS at a 10:1 ratio, forming a stock solution (227 μg/mL). The stock solution was then diluted to prepare samples with concentrations ranging from 20 to 150 μg/mL. Cis was analyzed at 210 nm13. A graph plotting the area under the peak against the concentration was created, and the calibration curve was derived as the linearity equation of the resulting line.

Production of free and drug-loaded liposomes

Certain amounts of substances were accurately weighed to produce various liposome formulations with different molar ratios or ingredients. Table 1 lists the formulation ingredients. The weighed substances were mixed with 0.5 mL of absolute ethanol and placed in an ultrasonic water bath to obtain a clear solution. The lipid solution was then injected into 10 mL of 1 × PBS at pH 7.4 under a magnetic stirrer at 500 rpm. The samples were left on a magnetic stirrer equipped with a hood overnight to allow ethanol evaporation through the vacuum effect14,15. Cis-loaded liposomes were produced using the aforementioned method by adding Cis into the ethanolic lipid solution to obtain PC or DOPE coded-liposomes (see Supplementary Materials Fig. S1). Particle size, distribution, and zeta-potential

A Litesizer 500 (Anton Paar, Graz, Austria) was used to measure the particle size and distribution during the characterization of the liposomes16. A disposable cuvette was used as the measurement cell, with the beam angle set to 90°. The target temperature was maintained at room temperature (25 ± 0.5 °C). Before the particle size and distribution measurements, the liposomes were diluted 11 times with distilled water. The measurements of the liposomes were repeated using a Wyatt Technologies Dynapro Nanostar, and the particle size and distribution were then verified.

Zeta-potential measurements were performed using a LitesizerTM 500 (Anton Paar, Graz, Austria) with the Laser Doppler Velocimetry technique using the same dilution factor as that used for particle size and distribution measurements17,18. An Omega cuvette was selected as the measurement cell by applying the Smoluchowski approximation.

During the experiments, critical parameters such as the baseline and intercept were monitored. According to the instruction manual for LitesizerTM 500, the baseline provides essential information about the reliability of the results, whereas the intercept serves as an indicator of particle detection. These parameters were carefully evaluated to ensure the validity and reliability of the results19,20. The baseline value was within the range of 1.000 ± 0.010. The intercept was 0.85–0.95.

Morphological investigation of the liposomes

Morphological analysis was performed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). TEM analysis was performed using a Talos L120C (Thermo-Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA)21. Before analysis, the samples were placed on carbon-coated TEM copper grids and left to air dry. After drying, the grids were inserted into the TEM, and images were captured at different magnifications. Semiquantitative elemental analysis was performed using an SEM Quattro S (Thermo-Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA, MAPS 3 Software) coupled with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) (EDAX) to determine the elemental ratios of platin (Pt) atoms. Samples were mounted onto aluminum stubs and sputter-coated with a 30-nm layer of gold (Au) under high vacuum conditions using the ACE200 (LEICA EM, Germany). SEM images were then captured at optimum magnifications22.

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR)

FT-IR spectroscopy was used to analyze free Cis, drug-free liposomes, and Cis-loaded liposomes23,24. A drop of each sample was applied using a Pasteur pipette and scanned within the IR range of 500–4000 cm⁻1 to compare the Cis, DOPE, and PC components. The analysis was performed using a Nicolet IS10 FT-IR spectrometer (Thermo-Fisher Scientific, USA).

Entrapment efficiency (EE) and drug loading (DL)

A certain volume (500 μL) of the prepared liposomes was subjected to ultracentrifugation (Optima Max-Xp, Beckman Colter, USA) at 60,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatants and pellets were separated. The resulting pellets were redispersed with 500 μL of 1 × PBS for further studies. The supernatants were diluted 50 times with methanol to determine EE and DL through the indirect method. Drug release study

An in vitro drug release study was conducted using the dialysis bag method. After purifying the liposomes via ultracentrifugation, the redispersed liposomal formulations were diluted with 1 × PBS at pH 7.4 at the fixed Cis concentration (0.2 mg/mL) to achieve the same amount of Cis in the liposomes and Cis solution. To prepare Cis solution, a fixed amount of Cis was accurately weighed and dissolved in a mixture of methanol (MeOH) and 1 × PBS at pH 7.4 (1:19) for use as a control. Then, 1 mL of each sample (equal to 0.2 mg of Cis) was placed in dialysis bags with a pore diameter of 10–12 kDa. After sealing the dialysis bags at both edges with closures, they were immersed in PBS at pH 7.4 or 5.5. The experiment was maintained in an orbital shaker at a constant stirring rate of 100 rpm at 37 °C ± 0.5 °C. At predetermined time intervals (10, 20, and 30 min and 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, and 24 h), 1 mL of the sample was withdrawn, and 1 mL of fresh PBS at the same temperature was added to the receiver medium to maintain a constant total volume. The cumulative drug release percentage was calculated using the following equation25:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Drug \,release\, (\%)=\frac{Release \,drug\,from \,particles\, ({\text{mg}})}{Total \,drug \,in \,particles \,({\text{mg}})}\times 100$$\end{document}

The in vitro dissolution data were analyzed using kinetic models in DD Solver. The adjusted coefficient of determination (R2adjusted), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Model Selection Criterion (MSC) were evaluated as statistical criteria26.

Stability

Liposomes were stored at 4 °C for 2 months. During storage, the particle size, distribution, and zeta-potential were studied for stability analysis.

Cell viability and morphological changes

In this study, A549 human lung cancer cells were cultivated in a specialized nutrient medium known as Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), which was designed to support the growth of these cells. This medium was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, which is an essential component for cell growth and viability27. The medium also contained 1% penicillin–streptomycin, a combination of antibiotics that prevent bacterial contamination, and L-glutamine and sodium bicarbonate, nutrients that promote cellular proliferation28. The experimental studies were conducted under controlled conditions, with the cells being cultivated at a temperature of 37 °C in an environment with 5% CO2 and 75% humidity, which are the optimal conditions for cell growth.

The effects of the formulations on A549 cells were determined using the MTT method29, and IC50 values were calculated. For this, 5 × 103 A549 cells were seeded in 100-µL medium in 96-well plates. Following cell adhesion, the formulations were added to the cells in 1:1, 1:10, 1:100 and 1:1000 dilutions (equal to 40 µg/mL, 4 µg/mL, 0.4 µg/mL, and 0.04 µg/mL of cisplatin, respectively). After the ultracentrifugation process in the liposome production method, pellets were re-dispersed with the appropriate volume of DMEM to make the cisplatin concentration equal in all samples. The plates were then incubated for 24 h at the indicated concentrations. After that, 10 µL of the MTT solution was added to the cells (ODC Research and Development ODC0009D-250 Cytotoxicity assay kit), and the plates were incubated for 4 h. The medium was then aspirated, and 100 µL of the solubilization solution was added to each well to dissolve the formazan dye. The absorbances of the formed color were measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. The MTT test was performed in triplicate, and the viability rate, IC50 calculation, and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism. Cells were also monitored using an inverted microscope (Zeiss Axiovert1) for 24 and 48 h.

CV staining

To detect interactions between cells, CV staining was performed30. Initially, 5 × 104 A549 cells were seeded in 800 µL of medium in 12-well plates, and after the cells adhered, 10-µM compounds were then added to the cells and incubated with these doses for 24 h. Following this incubation, the cells were subjected to an acetic acid/methanol fixation process using a commercial kit (ODC0011D CV staining kit-100 test). Subsequently, the cells were incubated with CV stain. After the completion of this step, the plates were washed, and the cell images were evaluated under a microscope (Zeiss Axiovert1).

Annexin-V assay

A549 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well and incubated for 24 h by adding 1:10 and 1:100 dilutions of the formulations. After the completion of the incubation period, the cell medium was removed, and the cells were washed twice with PBS. A549 cells were then detached using trypsin ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid and subsequently subjected to centrifugation at 500 g for 10 min. The A549 cells were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions using an annexin-V-FITC kit (BD Pharmigen, 556570) and suspended in an annexin-binding buffer. Then, 100 µL of the cell suspension was collected, and annexin V (5 µL) and propidium iodide (5 µL) were added. The resulting mixture was subsequently incubated in the dark and at ambient temperature for 30 min. A sample of 50,000 cells was then analyzed using a flow cytometer (Novocyte, Agilent). The gates for apoptotic and live cells were established, and data were collected for further evaluation31. In Annexin-V PI staining, the quadrant descriptions are as follows: live cell (Q1-3), early apoptosis (Q1-4), late apoptosis (Q1-2), and necrosis (Q1-1). In the calculation of total apoptosis, the sum of early and late apoptosis (Q1-4 + Q1-2) was evaluated as a percentage.

Results and discussion

Lipids, the major component of lung surfactant in addition to proteins, play a vital role in lung biology. Phosphatidylcholine (PC) comprises approximately 80% of the surfactant composition. However, the surfactant main components PC were less abundant in particular, NSCLC tumor tissues32. Therefore, we designed our liposome formulation with DMPC to balance the lipid composition in lung tissue. We modified the formulation using DSPE-PEG2000 to improve the drug’s retention in the lungs33, and CHEMS to provide pH sensitivity and to stabilize membrane structure due to its natural lipophilicity34.

More and more attentions have been paid on a formulation design on cisplatin because it is a frontline chemotherapeutic agent and its application is largely impeded because of the severe dose-limiting toxicity. Among various nanocarries, liposomes were of great importance and the most-researched nanocarriers35. DOPE:CHEMS in combination was evaluated in the previous literature. However, cisplatin-loaded pH-sensitive liposomes have been the subject of limited research. In these publications, the liposomal production method mainly relied on the thin film hydration followed by extrusion and/or their therapeutic potential investigated on breast cancer or wound healing36–38. Notably, this study is the first to evaluate the effect of cisplatin loaded pH-sensitive liposomes on the lung cancer cell line. A formulation based comparison with the relevant publications is included in the supplementary material (Table S1).

Particle size, distribution, and zeta-potential measurements

The PC-80 and PC-51 liposomes consisted of DMPC, PC, cholesterol, and PEG2000 with different molar ratios (80:15 mM and 51:44 mM of DMPC:cholesterol, respectively). The particle sizes of the PC-80 liposomes were found to be two times higher than those of the PC-51 coded liposomes (Fig. 1A). The molar ratio of lipids significantly influenced the particle size. Higher molar ratios led to bigger particle sizes. Compared with cholesterol, DMPC has a double chain and a higher molecular weight. When the amount of DMPC was low, the particle size became smaller. On the other side, the miscibility and the interactions between cholesterol and DMPC varied depending on the cholesterol concentrations. DMPC and cholesterol presented the strongest attractive interaction at higher cholesterol concentrations, in particular, in the range of 0.3–0.4 M fraction of cholesterol39. The highest interaction caused the particle size of the liposomes to be smaller. Moreover, both compounds had a polydispersity index lower than 30%, although their zeta-potentials were close to zero, which, respectively, indicated high homogeneity but low colloidal stability (Fig. 1B,C). In the preparation of liposomes through the ethanol injection technique, components were dissolved in ethanol and ethanol content was kept at 5% of the total formulation volume. It has been reported that ethanol concentrations under 7.5% of the total formulation volume lead to the formation of uniform small unilamellar vesicles14,43,44. All formulations exhibited consistent results in line with it. Moreover, based on the three production and characterization results, the repeatability of the production method was higher for PC than for DOPE. Although the relative SD of the PC liposomes for particle size was < 10%, it was < 20% for the DOPE liposomes. Furthermore, the relative SD of the DOPE liposomes for PDI was < 20%, whereas no significant differences were observed in the PC liposomes. Moreover, for both the PC and DOPE liposomes, no differences in zeta-potential were observed. Therefore, it was believed that when some factors, such as evaporation duration, vacuum effect, and injection site, were maintained, this method could have more repeatability with less variation. Despite its frequent use in liposome production, the thin film hydration (TFH) method is limited by challenges in scalability and its inherently time-consuming process. This study confirmed the ethanol injection technique as a strong alternative production method, with high reproducibility, simplicity, use of a non-toxic solvent, and scalability, consistent with findings in the literature45.

FT-IR

In FT-IR spectrum of Cis, the peak, which was observed in 3300 cm−1, represented the N–H group in its the molecular structure. The peak appeared at 800 cm-1 was ascribed to the rocking motion of NH2 groups of Cis46. In addition to these, the other observed peaks at around 1650 cm-1 and 1325 cm-1 were assigned to asymmetric and symmetric amine bending, respectively, (Fig. 5) and were consistent with the literature47. In contrast, the C-H bonds in PC and DOPE were observed at 2800–3000 cm−1, as expected for the sp3-hybridized C-H bonds48–51. Figure 5 presents the FT-IR spectroscopy results. The FTIR spectra of Cis and that of drug loaded liposomes were different, in which the peaks assigned for Cis disappeared. This points to that the drug uses those IR-active bonds to make interaction with the liposomes. The peak, which was observed in 3300 cm−1, represented the N–H group in the molecular structure of Cis. Platinum as a metal group peaked at 700 cm−1. In contrast, the C-H bonds in PC and DOPE were observed at 2800–3000 cm−1. The phosphoryl group peaked at 1000–1300 cm−1. The FTIR spectra of the drug-loaded and unloaded liposomes with PC or DOPE were similar. Regardless of the liposome composition (PC or DOPE), the characteristic peak of platinum at 700 cm⁻1 was not observed in either Cis-free liposomes or Cis-loaded liposomes. This result demonstrates that NH2 groups attached to Pt are not free any more to make rocking motion. In addition, the device measures from the particle surface; therefore, it indicates that Cis was not present on the surface, suggesting that Cis was not adsorbed onto the surface but was entirely incorporated within the structure.

EE and DL

Cis was determined using the HPLC method. Figures S6 and S7 present the spectrum of Cis and the calibration curve of a set of Cis solutions.

To determine EE% and DL%, centrifugation was performed as a purification method. The method optimization for centrifugation process was carried out before the cisplatin analysis. During the experiment, the amount of Cis was detected using both direct and indirect methods. The pellets and supernatants were measured one by one. During the method optimization, centrifugation at 15,000 rpm was applied for 45 min. The pellets were sedimented well and a clear solution was obtained after the process. However, while the separation of supernatant from pellets with pipetting, the separation could not be performed properly. Some pellets were withdrawn with the supernatants because they were fluffy. To prevent this problem (vacuum effect of pipetting), ultracentrifugation was performed. Therefore, the method was optimized using 60,000 rpm for 20 min. The pellets were better sedimented and could be completely separated from the supernatants without commonition. Since the proper separation was achieved, this method used for the measurement of Cis through the direct and indirect method. In the direct method, the pellets were re-hydrated with 1 mL of PBS and disrupted with 50 mL of methanol or Triton-X %10 and vortexed for 1 min. The percentage of Cis in the liposomes was higher in the presence of methanol compared to Triton-X %10 (data not shown). According to the analysis results, a relatively low amount of drug (20%–25%) was loaded into the PC and DOPE liposomes.

Drug release study

After purifying the liposomes through ultracentrifugation, the pellets were redispersed and then used for drug release experiments at pH 7.4 and 5.5. Drug release of free Cis reached > 90% at pH 7.4 in 30 min. Cis was released earlier in PC liposomes than in DOPE liposomes. The release of Cis in PC liposomes reached > 50% in 30 min, and reached approximately 80% at pH 7.4 in 6 h showing a modified release manner. Moreover, the total Cis release from DOPE liposomes reached 50% at maximum within 6 h with 40% of relatively burst release in 30 min. Cis was slightly released from the DOPE liposomes after 30 min (Fig. 7A). Free Cis at pH 5.5 exhibited similar release behavior at pH 7.4. It reached 60% in 30 min and 80% in 1 h. Surprisingly, Cis in PC liposomes presented 60% release within 30 min, similar to that of free Cis. However, the total release could not reach higher values after that time. In contrast, Cis in DOPE exhibited 60% release within 30 min, but it reached up to 80% in 2 h (Fig. 7B). DOPE and CHEMS were synergistically prone to release in an acidic medium. DOPE liposomes released Cis as if it was a free drug at pH 5.5, which represented an acidic tumor microenvironment. Conclusion

In summary, in this study, liposomal formulations of Cis were developed using a simple solvent evaporation technique. In this way, we obtained spherical, homogenous liposomes smaller than 200 nm with a zeta-potential of − 25 mV (Fig. 1). FT-IR and SEM–EDS confirmed the loading efficiency, which subsequently measured 35% and 50% for the PC and DOPE liposomes, respectively (Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5). The toxic effect on healthy cells can be reduced using pH-sensitive liposomes with the help of passive targeting, and the bioavailability and biosafety of the drug can be increased. Therefore, two formulations were developed using the same preparation method. Thus, DOPE and PC were compared in terms of their pH sensitivity. The rate and ratio of Cis release in the dissolution study were lower at pH 7.4 in DOPE liposomes, whereas they were higher at pH 5.5 (Fig. 7). This is an indicator of the pH sensitivity of DOPE. The efficacy of pH-sensitive liposomes on A549 lung cells was confirmed using the cell viability test and the evaluation of the apoptotic cause (Figs. 9, 10) regardless of additional pH sensitivity. Although their efficacy in cancer cells appears to be similar according to cell studies, considering that the primary distinction lies in their release profiles at pH 7.4, it can be anticipated that their effects on healthy cells will not be the same. These results exhibited promising results for the simple and rapid production of pH-sensitive liposomes and the selective treatment of lung cancer. Furthermore, in vivo studies can provide valuable information about the antitumor efficacy, tissue distribution, and mechanism of selective uptake by cancer cells. The proposed pH-sensitive liposomes can be modified with a targeting moiety, formed into inhalable pharmaceuticals, or subjected to loading other therapeutic molecules for future perspective.
