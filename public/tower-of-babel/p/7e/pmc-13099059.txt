Incidence and risk factors of preoperative anxiety among surgical patients in King Abdulaziz University Hospital
Authors: Ghader Jamjoum, Alwaleed Hesham Kaneetah, Fahad Mesfer Alghamdi, Abdulelah Abdulsalam M. Albatati, Abdulaziz Othman M. Alnahdi, Fawaz Saleh B. Almuntashiri, Abdullah Mahmoud Masoud Mahmoud, Amani Hussain Alhebaishi, Ashraf Maghrabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Anxiety is a feeling of discomfort that often involves emotions such as worry and fear. This feeling tends to intensify when someone is facing surgery. Preoperative anxiety (POA) is a common psychological condition among patients preparing for surgery, which can lead to adverse postoperative outcomes, including increased pain, delayed recovery, and a higher risk of infection. Despite its significant clinical impact, research on POA in Saudi Arabia, particularly in the Western region, is limited. This study aims to assess the incidence of POA and identify its risk factors among surgical patients at King Abdulaziz University Hospital (KAUH) in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia.Methodology:This single-center cross-sectional study was conducted in 2024 at KAUH in the Department of Surgery, a tertiary care center. The study included 70 adult inpatients scheduled for surgery under anesthesia. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire covering demographic characteristics, medical history, surgical experiences, and fears of pain or complications. A validated tool, the Amsterdam Preoperative Anxiety and Information Scale (APAIS), was used to assess anxiety levels. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26, applying Chi-square tests, Spearman’s correlation, and logistic regression to determine significant predictors of preoperative anxiety (POA).Results:Among the 70 participants, 77.1% were female, with a mean age of 35–44. The incidence of preoperative anxiety (POA) was 41.4%. Female gender was significantly associated with higher anxiety levels (89.7% vs. 10.3% in males, P = 0.036). Other significant risk factors included fear of postoperative pain (62.1% vs. 31.7%, P = 0.01) and fear of surgical complications (58.6% vs. 39.3%, P = 0.041). No significant association was found between POA and factors such as chronic disease, previous surgical history, or ASA classification.Conclusion:POA affects 41.4% of patients, especially females and those worried about pain and complications. This highlights the need for psychological assessments and clear patient education before surgery. Interventions like counseling and straightforward discussions about pain management can help reduce anxiety and improve surgical outcomes. Future research should focus on long-term studies to investigate these connections further.

Introduction

Anxiety is a sensation of discomfort, and many emotions, such as worry or fear, can be involved with anxiety.[1] Surgery is an invasive procedure done to treat and manage diseases. It is often linked with bleeding, discomfort, or pain, as well as the potential for complications, morbidities, or sometimes even death.[2] The incidence of anxiety tends to rise among individuals scheduled for surgery, regardless of the specific type of surgical procedure.[3] Preoperative anxiety (POA) is a prevalent psychological condition affecting 60-80% of surgical patients, marked by feelings of unease, fear, and stress before a surgical procedure.[4] POA is defined as not only a psychological but also a physiological state of discomfort and unpleasant mood before surgery.[5] It is characterized by feelings of powerlessness, tension, and exhaustion while anticipating potential dangers. Physiologically, patients may experience hemodynamic alterations, including disturbed breathing, increased heart rate, trembling, and sweating.[67]

The fear of the surgery itself and/or the fear related to the anesthesia are the leading causes of POA.[3] Many other factors have been identified as contributing to preoperative anxiety, including the fear of pain after the surgery, changes in body shape after surgery, chances of poor health status after the surgery, early discharge, separation from family and friends, waiting in the surgery unit, nausea and vomiting, and the possibility of the surgery being canceled.[89] Many complications after surgery have been found to have a relation with POA, with the most common complaint being postoperative pain.[1011]

In a study conducted in China involving patients undergoing laparoscopic hysterectomy, a higher incidence of preoperative anxiety was found to be associated with increased postoperative anxiety.[6] Similarly, a study conducted with 103 ENT surgery patients in Turkey found a correlation between preoperative anxiety and postoperative pain, with pain measured using a visual scale.[7] Preoperative anxiety has been shown to complicate wound healing by increasing cortisol levels in the body. Elevated cortisol levels are associated with an increased risk of infection.[12] The incidence of preoperative anxiety varies across countries. For instance, a study conducted in Germany involving 3,087 patients reported that 92.6% experienced preoperative anxiety, as measured by the Amsterdam Anxiety and Information Scale.[13] Factors contributing to this included female gender, younger age, highly invasive surgery, lack of prior surgical experience, general anesthesia, and surgeries involving malignant tumors.[24] In Saudi Arabia, a cross-sectional study conducted in Jeddah with 278 patients undergoing elective surgery showed a high prevalence of preoperative anxiety.[2] Another study in Saudi Arabia showed that 51.6% of individuals were found to have dental anxiety.[14] In Nepal, a study conducted in December 2023 found that 25.85% of patients experienced preoperative anxiety. Patients from nuclear families and those without a history of surgery were 2.5 times more likely to experience preoperative anxiety compared to those with a history of surgery.[15]

There are a few studies that have measured the incidence rates or risk factors of preoperative anxiety in the Middle East and Saudi Arabia, especially. As we mentioned earlier, preoperative anxiety has the potential to complicate the postoperative period, leading to issues such as increased pain, delayed wound healing, and an increased risk of infection. We aimed to measure the incidence and identify risk factors in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA), Jeddah, King Abdulaziz University Hospital (KAUH), which will help to guide the development of effective strategies to manage and alleviate preoperative anxiety to enhance the overall surgical experience and postoperative outcomes.

Methodology

Study design, site, and time

This single-center cross-sectional study was conducted in 2024 at the Department of Surgery in a tertiary care center and a teaching hospital, KAUH, Jeddah, Saudi Arabia.

Study participants

The inclusion criteria were adult inpatients over 18 who were scheduled to undergo any surgical procedure under anesthesia. The exclusion criteria were patients with a decreased level of consciousness. Since the total population was unknown, the sample size was determined assuming an infinite population with 95% confidence and 50% estimated prevalence. The final sample size was 70 participants.

Data collection and questionnaires

The data collection process was meticulously structured using a comprehensive literature review risk assessment form divided into three sections. The first section focused on demographic information, including age, sex, social status, income, educational level, and occupational status. The second section covered patient-related factors, such as a history of chronic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, cancer), smoking and drinking history, body mass index (BMI), psychiatric drug use, and experiences of pain in the last 6 months. Surgical history was also recorded, including the number and type of prior surgeries. The third section assessed surgical-related factors, such as the patient’s experience with anesthesia, previous hospital stays, and a companion’s presence during their stay. Additional questions focused on fears related to surgery, postoperative pain, and complications, along with the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification score.

To assess anxiety levels before surgery, the study utilized the Arabic-validated version of the Amsterdam Preoperative Anxiety and Information Scale (APAIS). This six-item questionnaire, consisting of two scales, a four-item anxiety scale and a two-item need-for-information scale, provided a reliable measure of patient anxiety. Patients rated their level of agreement with each question on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“not at all”) to 5 (“extremely”). The anxiety scale scores range from 4 to 20, and the need-for-information scale scores range from 2 to 10. Higher scores on both scales indicate more significant levels of anxiety and a higher need for information. A score of 11 or higher on the anxiety scale indicates considerable anxiety.[16171819]

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of KAUH (Reference No. 136-24). This involved submitting a detailed research proposal outlining the study’s objectives, methodology, and potential risks and benefits to the participants. Patients were informed about the survey’s methodology and purpose before verbally giving informed consent. They were also allowed to withdraw from completing the questionnaire at any time. The study strictly adhered to principles of privacy and confidentiality. Patient information was anonymized and securely stored throughout the research to ensure confidentiality and data protection.

Data analysis

The collected data was entered into Microsoft Excel 2022 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) and IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for statistical analysis. The Chi-squared test (χ2) was applied to qualitative data expressed as numbers and percentages to explore the relationship between different variables. Quantitative data were expressed as mean and standard deviation (Mean ± SD), with the Mann-Whitney test applied for nonparametric variables. Correlation analysis was conducted using Spearman’s test, and multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to assess factors associated with preoperative anxiety. The odds ratio (OR) was calculated at a 95% confidence interval (CI). A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the incidence and risk factors of preoperative anxiety among surgical patients at KAUH, Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. The findings revealed that 41.4% (29 out of 70) of patients experienced preoperative anxiety based on the APAIS classification, illustrating a significant psychological burden associated with surgery. These results are consistent with previous studies reporting preoperative anxiety rates of 51.2%, 53.6%, and 59.3%, respectively.[202122] Similarly, a study in Ethiopia found that 47% of 407 patients awaiting elective surgery experienced preoperative anxiety, as measured by the STAI score.[22] In contrast to our findings, a cross-sectional study conducted in Nepal reported a significantly lower prevalence of 25.85%.[23] This variation highlights different preoperative anxiety rates across various populations and settings.

In this study, the prevalence of preoperative anxiety was significantly higher among female patients compared to males (89.7% vs. 10.3%), respectively. This also illustrates another study done at Marburg University showed that the Female gender had the most substantial impact on all three anxiety dimensions (APAIS-A-T, APAISA-An, and APAIS-SU).[4]

This study identified fear of pain and complications as significant risk factors for preoperative anxiety, with 44.3% of participants expressing concern about postoperative pain and 41.4% worried about complications. These findings align with previous research showing a positive correlation between fear of pain or surgical complications and preoperative anxiety.[21] Addressing patient concerns by education, providing reassurance about the management of complications and how common they occur, and ensuring clear communication about available pain relief options may help alleviate anxiety and improve the preoperative experience. Many participants reported concerns such as fear of postoperative pain, changes in body shape, and separation from family and friends. These results spotlight how surgery can affect a patient’s mental health as they navigate the unfamiliar experience and potential outcomes of their procedure.

Most of the participants in the study underwent general surgery, which is associated with higher levels of preoperative anxiety compared to other types of surgery. Recognizing these differences can assist healthcare providers in tailoring preoperative counseling and support based on the specific type of surgery being performed.

Strengths and limitations

Our study’s strengths make it stand out. It first focused on a population that has not been extensively researched in Saudi Arabia, especially in the Western region. This helps fill a gap in existing literature. We used a well-established scale, the APAIS, to measure anxiety, improving our results. Our findings offer valuable insights into how common preoperative anxiety is and the factors that contribute to it among patients at KAUH. The study’s cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal conclusions, and the retrospective nature of some questions may have introduced recall bias. Additionally, the relatively small sample size, due to the limited number of patients scheduled for surgery, may impact the generalizability of the results. Future research with larger sample sizes and longitudinal designs is necessary to investigate this issue further.

Conclusion

The study found that a significant number of patients, specifically 41.4%, experienced preoperative anxiety. Key risk factors included being female, having a fear of pain, and worrying about potential surgical complications. These results highlight the importance of patient education and the need for psychological evaluations to identify and support individuals who are at risk. Managing preoperative anxiety with targeted interventions like counseling and education can significantly improve patient outcomes and reduce the chances of complications after surgery. For this reason, comprehensive preoperative assessments must include mental health evaluations to ensure better overall patient care. Future research should explore additional risk factors and develop effective anxiety reduction strategies to optimize the surgical experience.
