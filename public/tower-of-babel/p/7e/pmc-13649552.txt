HIV dominates T-cell immune responses in HIV–HBV co-infection and is associated with increased HIV reservoir transcription

ABSTRACT
Due to the effectiveness of antiretroviral therapy (ART), Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection is now managed as a chronic condition. However, people with HIV (PWH) remain susceptible to co-infections, including Hepatitis B virus (HBV), with 15% of PWH in Africa being co-infected. While co-infections may accelerate disease progression and increase AIDS-related mortality, their effects on T-cell responses and HIV reservoir size are unclear. This study compared T-cell responses in people with HIV-HBV co-infection, HIV or HBV alone, and assessed the effects of HBV on the HIV reservoir size. We screened 390 archived samples from virologically suppressed (viral load < 50 copies/mL) PWH and identified 31 individuals with HIV-HBV co-infection. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were analyzed at baseline and following stimulation to assess activation and exhaustion markers (CD25, CD38, CD69, HLA-DR, and PD-1) by flow cytometry. Inflammatory cytokines were quantified using a 12-plex Luminex panel. HIV reservoir size was determined by RT-PCR measurements of proviral DNA and unspliced RNA. The prevalence of HIV-HBV co-infection was 7.9% (31/390). At baseline, unstimulated T cells showed increased CD38+HLA-DR- expression on both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in people with HIV-HBV co-infection and HIV alone compared with HBV mono-infection. PD-1 expression on CD4+ T cells was also higher in the HIV-HBV and HIV mono-infection groups than with HBV alone. After stimulation, CD69 expression on both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells was higher in the HIV-HBV and HIV-only groups than in HBV alone. CD8+ T cells from individuals with co-infection showed higher PD-1 expression, while CD4+ PD-1 expression was similar across groups. CD38⁺HLA-DR⁺ co-expression on CD4⁺ T cells was increased in the HIV–HBV and HIV mono-infection groups but was similar on CD8⁺ T cells across groups. Pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine levels were highest in the co-infected group. The usRNA reservoir transcriptional activity level was significantly higher in HIV-HBV co-infection (p = 0.022) while cell-associated DNA remained the same for both groups. Among individuals with HIV–HBV co-infection who have achieved virological suppression, while HIV appears to be the main driver of T cell responses, HBV can result in a more active reservoir.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-61567-7.

Background

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) suppresses Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) but does not provide a cure. People with HIV (PWH) must take medications for a lifetime and deal with medication side effects, unsustainable costs, and increasing drug resistance1–3. Hepatitis B (HBV) is a huge public health burden globally and exceptionally high in HIV-endemic areas in Africa, with an estimated prevalence of 7–15% or higher4,5. Due to their similar routes of transmission including sexual contact, exposure to contaminated blood products and vertical transmission, HBV-HIV co-infection is higher in sub-Saharan Africa in both children and adults6–8. In Ghana, the prevalence of HBV in the general population is estimated to be 8–12%9,10. A pooled HBV prevalence of 11.4% was estimated in a recent cross-sectional study that reviewed registers across Ghana, making it a hyper-endemic country11. HBV prevalence is disproportionately distributed across the country, with seroprevalence rates of 22.7% and 6.4% in the Savannah and Greater Accra regions, respectively12.

HIV-HBV co-infection is a major global public health concern, as people with the two conditions have worse outcomes for either disease13. Studies have shown that persons living with HIV-HBV coinfection are more likely to develop chronic HBV infections with more pronounced complications than those with mono-infections13,14. HIV persists in persons on ART because of a stable reservoir of memory CD4 + T cells that harbor transcriptionally silent latent proviruses, which are neither eliminated by ART nor cleared by the immune system. However, upon appropriate stimulation latent proviruses can reactivate and resume viral production15–18. Similarly, in the liver cells of patients with HBV, covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) persists throughout infection18,19, and HBV DNA rebounds in PWH when nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NRTI) therapy ceases, probably due to non-adherence or adverse side effects to medication. The residual HBV antigens persist, resulting in an impaired HBV-specific immune response, including a decline in CD4+ T cell responses, in individuals with HIV-HBV co-infection. This HBV persistence exposes the immune system to continual HBV surface antigen (HBsAg) stimulation, which leads to functional T cell exhaustion, and HBV-specific CD4 + T-cells impairment19. Therefore, hepatitis B co-infection is an important factor that could significantly impact T cell responses, HIV latency reactivation, and reservoir size.

During HIV infection, virus-specific CD8+ T cell responses elicit the secretion of cytokines including interferon-γ (IFNγ), Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α) etc., and other effector molecules to control the virus. Chronic hepatitis may lead to exhaustion of HBV-specific CD8+ T cells and impairment of innate immune responses13,14. In addition, these infections could result in suppressed natural killer cell (NK) function and altered CD4+ T cell responses19,20. The interaction of the immune system with these viruses in persons living with HIV-HBV co-infection may be responsible for accelerated complications, such as liver cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and higher mortality rates21,22. Given these findings in HBV infections, it is likely that co-infection with HIV will alter T cell responses even more and modify the HIV-1 reservoir size in individuals with chronic HBV infection. However, there is limited data on how HBV co-infection affects T cell responses and reservoir size, especially in people whose HIV (HBV) is virologically suppressed. This is an important question to answer to ensure that HIV cure methods being pursued will also benefit those with HBV co-infection. Therefore, in this preliminary study, we sought to determine whether HIV-HBV co-infection influences T cell activation, exhaustion, cytokine production, and HIV reservoir size.

Materials and methods

Study population and design

Archived samples (390) from PWH in our ongoing HIV Cure Research Infrastructure Study (H-CRIS) from Korle Bu Teaching Hospital (KBTH), University of Ghana Hospital, and LEKMA Hospital were used for the study. These participants were recruited in 2019, followed up quarterly, and their viral loads were monitored; details of the cohort have been published elsewhere23. Participants were screened for HBV coinfection, then grouped and matched into HIV infection alone (thirty) and HIV-HBV co-infection (thirty), post screening. Additionally, archived samples from 22 persons with HBV alone (HEPMAL project, NMIMR-IRB approval number 046/19-20) from Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital and from six healthy persons at NMIMR were used as control groups. Data on demographic characteristics, including age, sex, educational status, income level, occupation, and comorbidities, were collected from all participants using a standardized questionnaire at enrolment. Archived peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were used for T-cell stimulation assays.

All experiments were conducted in accordance with applicable guidelines and regulations and adhered to the standards of Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP).

HBV screening

Viral Hepatitis B infection was serologically screened in plasma using a one-step multi-HBV test kit (InTec Products, Inc.). Approximately 50 µl of plasma was carefully applied to the cartridge and incubated for 15 min. The results were interpreted according to the manufacturer’s instructions and based on standardized clinical practice. The profiles of Hepatitis B markers, such as hepatitis surface antigen (HBsAg), surface antibodies (HBsAb), core antibodies (HBcAb), envelope antigen (HBeAg), and envelope antibodies (HBeAb), were obtained from the test. Based on the screening kit used, a chronic or acute HBV infection was scored when HBsAg and HBcAg were positive. Individuals with a negative ABsAg profile but positive HBcAg were categorized as immune due to vaccination or natural infection, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

DNA extraction and HBV quantification

DNA was extracted from plasma using the DNEasy extraction kit (Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. HBV DNA was quantified using the Geneproof Hepatitis B Virus (HBV) PCR Kit (Geneproof, Czech Republic) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The kits consisted of a master mix and four calibrators (A = 10000 IU/µl, B = 1000 IU/µl, C = 100 IU/µl, and D = 10 IU/µl) as standards. For each sample and calibrator, 15 µl of master mix and 5 µl of the extracted DNA sample or calibrator were added to the PCR tubes. Cycler conditions were as follows: first holding stage 37 ℃ for 2 min, second holding stage 95 ℃ for 10 min, PCR stage 95 ℃ for 5 s, 60 ℃ for 40 s, 72 ℃ for 20 s for 45 cycles. HBV viral DNA was quantified, and titters were determined by extrapolation from a standard panel.

T cell activation and cytokine production

Flow cytometry was used to characterize the resting CD4+ and CD8+ T cell populations by simultaneously staining PBMCs for activation markers (CD25, HLA-DR, CD38, and CD69) and exhaustion marker (PD1). Briefly, Cryopreserved PBMCs were thawed rapidly at 37℃ for 1 min and transferred into culture media (RPMI-1640 supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 1% L-glutamine, and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS)). Cells were rested for 2 h and 5 × 105 cells/ml and were either unstimulated or stimulated with PMA/Ionomycin as a positive control, due to its strong T cell activation effect and DMSO as a negative control and media as an experimental background control. Stimulated samples were incubated for 6 h, and a Golgi plug was added after 4 h. The supernatant was transferred to sterile plates and stored at -80 °C until ready for use in the Luminex assay, while the cell pellets were stained for flow cytometric analysis. Cell surface markers were detected by staining for CD3 (PerCP-Cy5.5), CD4 (BV421), CD8 (BUV737), HLA-DR (BV605), CD25 (PE), CD38 (BUV395), CD69 (APC), and PD1 (PE-Cy7) .

After cell surface marker staining, the cells were permeabilized for intracellular staining of cytokines using the BD Cytofix/Cytoperm kit (BD Biosciences, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The expression levels of the intracellular markers IFN-γ (APC), IL-2 (PE), and TNF-α (FITC) were measured using flow cytometry (BD LSR Fortessa X-20, Germany).

Measurement of cytokine levels by multiplex: Levels of anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory cytokines (GM-CSF, IFNɣ, IL-1β, IL2, IL4, IL6, IL7, IL8, IL10, IL12 (P70), IL13, TNFα) were measured in culture supernatants after six hours of cell stimulation using 12-Luminex plex cytokine kits (Biotechne). On the assay day, 50 µl supernatant was added to 50 µl of microparticle cocktail (Bio-Techne) and incubated for 2 h at room temperature (RT) on a shaker at 800 rpm. The plate was washed three times, and 50 µl of diluted streptavidin-PE was added to each well and incubated for 30 min at RT on a shaker at 800 rpm. The plate was washed, after which 100 µl of Wash Buffer was added to the plate, incubated for 2 min and read within 90 min using MAGPIX Analyser (Luminex Corporation). The analyser converts the mean fluorescent intensities (MFIs) into concentrations in pg/ml used for the analysis.

HIV reservoir size measurement

Nucleic acid extraction: Total nucleic acids were extracted from 1 × 10⁶ thawed PBMCs using the RADI Prep DNA/RNA Kit (RADI, South Korea) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Extracts were aliquoted and frozen at -80 °C until processing for HIV-1 cell-associated unspliced RNA (usRNA) and proviral DNA (prDNA).

Quantitative Reverse Transcription PCR for unspliced RNA (usRNA) Quantification: The Cell-associated unspliced RNA (usRNA) quantification is a sensitive method, as low levels of HIV-1 free virus (1–10 copies/ml) can be detected even when patients are suppressed24. For this method, the nucleic acid extracts were DNase-treated using a DNA-free kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to remove residual DNA. Plasmid DNA templates (1247 bp) were quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Serial dilutions were prepared based on the calculated copy numbers: 10-fold dilutions from 10⁶ to 10² copies, followed by 5-fold dilutions from ~ 0.8 to 20 copies to capture a broad range of expression levels. Serially diluted standards were subjected to quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) targeting the HIV-1 L and gag regions. The primers (Malnati Primers) used were SLTR (5′-CTAGCAGTGGCGCCCGAA-3′) and SGAG (5′-CTAGCTCCCTGCTTGCCCA-3′). Reactions were performed in 12.5 µl volumes using the AgPath-ID One-Step RT-PCR Kit (Applied Biosystems), containing 6.25 µl of 2X RT-Buffer, 0.5 µl of enzyme mix, 0.5 µl of each 10 µM primer, 0.15 µl FAM-labelled probe, 2.1 µl of nuclease-free water, and 2.5 µl of template. RT-qPCR was performed using the QuantStudio 7 Pro Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) under the following thermal cycling conditions: 50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, and 60 °C for 1 min.

Quantification of prDNA: HIV-1 proviral DNA was quantified by real-time PCR targeting the HIV gag gene (Malnati primers; forward primer: TCTCGACGCAGGACTCG; reverse primer: TACTGACGCTCTCGCACC)25, with serially diluted human genomic DNA serving as an external standard. The total reaction mix (12.5 µl) included 6.25 µl 2X RT-buffer, 0.5 µl enzyme (AgPath-ID One-Step RT-PCR Kit, Applied Biosystems), 0.5 µl of 10 µM primers, 0.15 µl FAM-labelled probe, 2.1 µl water, and 2.5 µl template. All reactions were run on the Quant Studio 7 Pro platform with cycling conditions of 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Beta-actin DNA was detected in a total volume of 25 µl, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Scientific)26. Quantified HIV-1 DNA was normalized to β-actin, which was measured in parallel using the TaqMan detection assay. β-actin quantification was performed using a TaqMan assay on the QuantStudio 7 Pro Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Each 12.5 µl reaction contained 6.25 µl of 2X RT-Buffer and 0.5 µl of enzyme from the AgPath-ID One-Step RT-PCR Kit (Applied Biosystems), 1.25 µl of 10 µM forward and reverse primers, 1.25 µl of FAM-labelled probe, 0.5 µl of nuclease-free water, and 1.5 µl of template. The thermal cycling conditions were the same as those described above for the RT-PCR assay.

Standard curves and normalization: The amount of PBMC-derived HIV-1 DNA and RNA were determined using standard curve. Standard curves were generated from serial dilutions of 10 ng/µl human genomic DNA (serving as an external standard) and run in parallel with the experimental samples using RT-PCR. For proviral DNA (prDNA) quantification, the HIV-1 copy number was normalized to the total cellular input, as determined by separate real-time PCR assays targeting the β-actin gene.

Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) was used as the endogenous control for the normalizing usRNA. The RNA extracts were diluted to 1:25 prior to analysis. Reactions were performed using the TaqMan Ribosomal RNA Control Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), which included proprietary primers and a VIC-labelled probe. Each 12.5 µl reaction included 6.25 µl of 2X RT-Buffer, 0.5 µl of enzyme, 0.0625 µl of each primer, 0.0625 µl VIC-labelled probe, 3.0625 µl nuclease-free water, and 2.5 µl of template. All reactions for both usRNA and rRNA were performed in duplicate under the same thermal cycling conditions described in the RT-qPCR assay section.

Statistical analysis

The prevalence of HBV among PWH was calculated from the total number of participants in the H-CRIS cohort who were serologically tested for HBV. All disease group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis or Mann-Whitney test, for non-parametric data. Copy numbers were determined from standard curves, and normalisation was performed using the respective endogenous controls for each target. Results from PCR quantification were generated using QuantStudio 7 Pro and all flow cytometry data were analysed using FlowJo v10.8.0. All graphics were created using GraphPad Prism v9.0.0. The significance level for all tests was set at 95% confidence interval (CI), and p-values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Screening of H-CRIS participants for HBV showed a prevalence of 7.9% (n = 31/390 participants). Among both groups of people with HIV-HBV co-infection and HIV alone, the majority were females (54.8% and 57%, respectively). Most were older than 40 years (83.8% and 85.7%, respectively), and approximately 60% of both groups had used ART extensively for more than 5 years (Table 1). The majority of individuals in the HIV-HBV coinfection (77%) and HIV mono-infection (71%) groups were virologically suppressed (viral load < 50 copies/ml) consistently over the two-year period, with 80% having a CD4 count of > 350 (cells/µL).

A total of 22 individuals with HBV mono-infection and 6 uninfected controls were included. Among HBV alone participants, 12 (54.6%) were male and 10 (45.5%) were female, whereas the control group comprised 2 (33.3%) males and 4 (66.7%) females. Most people with HBV alone were aged < 40 years (16, 72.7%), with 6 (27.3%) aged ≥ 40 years. In contrast, the control group was older, with 4 (66.7%) aged ≥ 40 years and 2 (33.3%) aged < 40 years (Supplementary Table 1).

Analysis of T cell activation and exhaustion markers in unstimulated cells

To determine the activation status of individuals with HIV-HBV coinfection, comparisons were made between HIV and HBV alone infections, and uninfected controls (UC) using flow cytometry on unstimulated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. As shown in Fig. 1A, individuals with HIV-HBV co-infection showed activation and exhaustion marker profiles (CD25, CD69, HLA-DR, CD38, and PD1), comparable to HIV alone, while expression was generally lower in HBV mono-infection on CD4+ T cells. This suggests HIV largely drives T-cell activation and exhaustion phenotypes during co-infection.

In Fig. 1B, on CD8+ T cells, both individuals with HIV-HBV coinfection and HIV infection-alone showed comparably high expression of activation marker profiles (CD69, HLA-DR, and CD38), compared to HBV-alone individuals. In contrast, PD-1 expression was significantly lower in individuals with HIV-HBV coinfection and HIV-monoinfection compared to HBV-alone individuals. An indication of alterations of T-cell populations driven by HIV, which may reduce the overall number of PD-1–expressing cells in HIV-HBV coinfections.

Differential expression of T cell markers after stimulation

To assess the activation and exhaustion states of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell populations across the study groups following stimulation, we measured differential expression of immune markers on the stimulated cells and normalized to their corresponding unstimulated cells. Overall, no significant differences in the expression levels of immune markers were observed in either CD4⁺ or CD8⁺ T cells across the groups following stimulation (Fig. 2A, B). However, relatively higher CD69 expression on CD4⁺ T cells was observed in individuals with HIV-HBV coinfection compared to HIV-monoinfection, suggesting early immune activation during coinfection. Furthermore, co-expression of HLA-DR and CD38 (HLA-DR⁺CD38⁺) on CD4⁺ T cells was high in both individuals with HIV-HBV coinfection and HIV-only relative to HBV-only infection, though not statistically significant, indicative of early HIV-1-driven immune activation by CD38 and sustained immune activation by HLADR (Fig. 2A). Similarly, on CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 2B), relative expression of the activation markers CD25 and CD69, as well as the exhaustion marker PD-1, was higher in both individuals with HIV-HBV coinfected and HIV-only infection compared to HBV-only infection.

T-cell specific cytokine expression versus secretion

We measured cell-associated cytokine levels from CD4+ and CD8+ T cells by flow cytometry following intracellular staining after stimulation. Individuals with both HIV mono-infected and HIV-HBV coinfections exhibited lower levels of interferon gamma (IFNɣ), IL-2 and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα), compared with HBV only controls, indicating impaired effector cytokine production associated with HIV infection (Fig. 3).

Next we determined cytokine secretion profiles in the culture supernatants of PBMC’s from individuals with co-infection with HIV-HBV, compared with HIV and HBV mono-infections, and uninfected controls, using the Luminex multiplex assay. Levels of pro-inflammatory (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8, IL-1β, IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-12p70, GM-CSF, IL-7) and anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10, IL-4, IL-13) were measured. As shown in Fig. 4A and B, cells from individuals with HBV alone were generally unable to produce both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines after stimulation. In contrast, cells from people with HIV-HBV co-infection produced both types of cytokines at levels comparable to those with HIV alone and uninfected controls, indicating that HIV appears to override the suppressive effects of HBV on T-cell responses during co-infection. Taken together, these findings suggest that in HIV-HBV co-infection, HIV dominates the immune activation profile, leading to cytokine production. However, this elevated inflammatory state does not translate into fully preserved T-cell function, as intracellular production of IFNγ, IL-2 and TNFα remained reduced.

Finally, we performed correlation analysis between immune markers and cytokine production after stimulation (Fig. 5). In persons with HIV-HBV co-infection and HIV alone, the activation marker CD69 correlated positively with most cytokines measured. Additionally, in the co-infection group, IL-8 correlated with PD-1 (R = 0.7) and HLA-DR (R = 0.50), whereas IL-2 showed strong correlations with CD25 (R = 1.0) and CD38 (R = 0.8). Similarly, in HIV only infection, CD25 and HLA-DR expression were broadly associated with cytokine production. In contrast, HIV mono-infection exhibited weaker and less coordinated relationships. IL-4 (R = 1.0) and IL-7 (R = 0.63) correlated with CD25, while TNF-α, IL-10, IL-2, and GM-CSF correlated with HLA-DR (R = 0.74). However, most cytokines measured for this group (TNF-α, IL-10, IL-1β, IFN-γ, IL-4, IL-2, and GM-CSF) negatively correlated with CD69 expression. These findings suggest that in HIV or HIV-HBV infections, T cell activation markers are broadly linked to cytokine production, showing a coordinated immune activation. In contrast, HBV only infection shows a pattern in which early activation does not translate to cytokine responses, consistent with impaired T cell activity in chronic HBV.

Estimation of the HIV-1 reservoir and HBV quantification

We quantified the HIV-1 reservoir size using single-step real-time PCR for both unspliced RNA (usRNA) and proviral DNA (PrDNA) in the cohort of virologically suppressed individuals. We observed that the usRNA reservoir size was larger in the co-infected group than in the HIV mono-infected group (3.03, 95% CI 2.32–3.91, p value 0.022) (Fig. 6, Supplementary Table 2). In contrast, PrDNA was similar between the two groups. This indicates that the overall size of the HIV integrated reservoir is comparable between the two groups, but HBV co-infection results in increased transcriptional activity of the latent reservoir. After HBV quantification, we observed no detectable targets of the hepatitis B virus within each HBV-infected sample, indicating the viral load was below detectable limit, although HBsAg and the HBcAg were positive serologically.

Discussion

In this study, we examined T-cell activation, cytokine responses, and HIV reservoir size in HIV-HBV coinfection compared with HIV and HBV mono-infection. Overall, we found that the immune activation profile in individuals with HIV-HBV co-infection resembles that of HIV rather than HBV. While infections with HIV or HIV-HBV showed coordinated associations between T-cell activation markers and cytokine production, HBV mono-infection displayed limited cytokine responses despite activation marker expression. Additionally, the reservoir measurement among the groups revealed a significantly higher levels of unspliced HIV RNA among those with co-infection, indicating increased transcriptional activity of the latent reservoir.

The HIV-HBV co-infected prevalence of 7.9% in our cohort is similar to what is found in sub-Saharan Africa in the range of 7–15% or higher4,5,27 and that of the general population in Ghana (8–12%)9,10. These numbers contrast with those of developed countries, where co-infection rates are less than 5%13,28.

We found that participants with HIV-HBV co-infection showed patterns of T-cell activation that were largely similar to HIV mono-infection. For instance, increased expression of activation markers such as CD38 and HLA-DR on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, and elevated PD-1 on CD4+ T cells, are well-known hallmarks of chronic infection, reflecting immune activation and T-cell exhaustion13,29. It is important to note that PD-1 expression is broadly upregulated during T cell activation and thus it can be present on both activated and exhausted T cells. In contrast, we observed that HBV infection alone exhibited weaker activation and diminished cytokine responses, in line with previous findings that chronic HBV induces impaired T-cells characterised by reduced cytokine production and proliferative capacity30. Thus, in the context of co-infection, HIV drives the immune activation which dominates the overall T-cell response profile.

The above interpretation was further supported by our correlation analyses. In both HIV and HIV-HBV co-infection, we found a broad association between inflammatory cytokine production and activation markers such as CD69, CD25, and HLA-DR, indicating a coordinated immune activation response. In contrast, HBV mono-infection of several cytokines, including TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-2, was negatively correlated with CD69 expression, indicating a dissociated pattern. This discordance is consistent with previous studies showing a dysfunctional or exhausted T-cell phenotype in chronic HBV infection31,32. Interestingly, intracellular cytokine profiling showed both HIV and HIV-HBV infections produced lower levels of important effector cytokines, including IFN-γ, IL-2 and TNF-α, compared to HBV mono-infections. Taken together with the correlation analyses, the data show that despite impairing T-cell effector function, the presence of HIV promotes an activated inflammatory environment compared to HBV alone33. Thus, although HIV drives immune activation during co-infection, it does not fully restore T-cell responses but instead results in an activated but functionally compromised immune state.

Finally, reservoir measurements showed that both individuals with HIV infection alone and HIV-HBV co-infections had similar levels of proviral DNA, but the unspliced HIV RNA levels were significantly higher in the HIV-HBV coinfection group. Since cell-associated unspliced RNA measures transcriptional activity of integrated proviruses, this finding suggests that HBV co-infection may be associated with increased HIV transcription without necessarily expanding the size of the latent reservoir34,35. Given the similarity between HIV and HIV-HBV co-infection in terms of T cell activation, it is unclear why transcriptional activity seems to be going on with the reservoir in co-infected individuals. However, a heightened inflammatory milieu or other factors could result in increased transcriptional activity in individuals with HIV-HBV co-infection, as studies have shown that pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 activate NF-κB signalling pathways that enhance HIV transcription36–38. Since these measurements do not distinguish intact from defective proviruses, this data should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

This study has several strengths, including simultaneous evaluation of immune activation, cytokine responses, and HIV reservoir measures. The limitations include a cross-sectional design, use of bulk PBMC’s for stimulation rather than isolated CD4 + or CD8 + T cells, and a relatively smaller sample size, thus limiting causal inference. Future longitudinal studies are needed to determine if co-infection directly influences the HIV reservoir. In conclusion, our findings suggests that HIV-HBV co-infection is characterized by HIV-dominated immune activation and increased transcriptional activity of the viral reservoir.
