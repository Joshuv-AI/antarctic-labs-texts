Elamipretide in pediatric Barth syndrome: from heart failure to school return
Authors: Pascal Amedro, Mathieu Andrianoely, Pauline Gohier, Justine Leroux, Nathalie Aladjidi, Stephanie Lagorce, Melissa Daccord, Marion Audie, Astrid Botte, Karine Nubret, Pascale Richard, Sandrine Roche, Delphine Lamireau, Candice Tetard, Aurelien Trimouille, Arielle Desir, Samir Mesli, Jean Donadieu, Julien Gotchac
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBarth syndrome (BTHS) is a rare X-linked mitochondrial disorder characterized by cardiomyopathy, neutropenia, and skeletal myopathy. Elamipretide is a mitochondria-targeting peptide that stabilizes cardiolipin and improves mitochondrial function. While use of this treatment in infants with BTHS has been reported in the United States, no cases have been described outside the US or in older children.Case presentationWe report the first case of a preschool-aged child with genetically confirmed BTHS and advanced heart failure, who was treated with elamipretide under a compassionate use program approved by the French Drug Administration (ANSM). At treatment initiation, this 5-year-old patient presented with severe left ventricular dysfunction (LVEF = 20%) and moderate-to-severe mitral regurgitation. He was dependent on gastrostomy feeding and had been listed for heart transplantation. Subcutaneous elamipretide (10 mg/day) was initiated alongside maximal conventional heart failure therapy. Over six months, LVEF improved to 50%, mitral regurgitation decreased to mild, Ross class improved from IV to I, and substantial gains were observed in functional capacity, nutritional status, and health-related quality of life (HRQoL). The patient returned to full-time school and remained temporarily suspended from the transplant list. Treatment was generally well tolerated. One transient, non-serious episode of abdominal pain with moderate pancreatic enzyme elevation occurred, which resolved without treatment discontinuation.ConclusionsThis case suggests that elamipretide may improve cardiac function, exercise tolerance, nutrition, and HRQoL in preschool-aged pediatric BTHS patients, potentially delaying the need for transplantation. Broader pediatric evaluation is warranted. Further strategies, such as youth-tailored hybrid cardiac rehabilitation programs, could complement pharmacological therapy and further optimize outcomes in this population.

Background

Barth syndrome [MIM:302060] is a rare X-linked recessive disorder caused by pathogenic variants in the TAFAZZIN gene, which encodes an acyltransferase essential for remodeling mature cardiolipin in the inner mitochondrial membrane [1]. Abnormal cardiolipin composition leads to mitochondrial dysfunction, resulting in a characteristic phenotype that may include dilated or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, neutropenia, skeletal myopathy, growth delay, and exercise intolerance [1–3]. The estimated prevalence is approximately 1 in 1 million live male births [4]. Cardiomyopathy typically presents in infancy and carries a high risk of death or heart transplantation in early childhood [3].

No curative therapy is currently available for Barth syndrome in children. Management remains supportive, combining guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure, prophylaxis for neutropenia, and close monitoring for arrhythmias.

Elamipretide is a mitochondria-targeting tetrapeptide that binds cardiolipin, stabilizes the inner mitochondrial membrane, improves electron transport chain efficiency, enhances adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis, and reduces reactive oxygen species production [5–7]. In the phase 2/3 TAZPOWER trial and its open-label extension, elamipretide improved functional capacity and left ventricular systolic function in adolescents and young adults with Barth syndrome [8, 9]. Expanded-access use has been reported in two critically ill pediatric patients in the United States—a newborn [10] and an 11-month-old infant [11]—both of whom showed marked recovery of ventricular function after treatment initiation.

To our knowledge, no pediatric case of elamipretide therapy has been reported in older children or outside the United States. We describe the first European case of elamipretide treatment in a child with genetically confirmed Barth syndrome, initiated at 5 years of age, with comprehensive follow-up including cardiac imaging, functional capacity testing, and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) assessment.

Case presentation

A male child with genetically confirmed Barth syndrome was followed at our tertiary pediatric cardiology center from infancy for dilated cardiomyopathy with severely reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and progressive exercise intolerance. He was the first child of non-consanguineous parents. He had been evaluated at the age of 2 years in pediatric hematology for asymptomatic transient neutropenia (0.4–0.7 G/L).

His younger brother had died at 14 months of age from fulminant dilated cardiomyopathy with cardiogenic shock refractory to maximal therapy, culminating in cardiac arrest in a septic context. Despite resuscitation and mechanical circulatory support, he died of multiorgan failure. Metabolic investigations revealed significant 3-methylglutaconic aciduria. In light of this familial history, Barth syndrome was suspected in the index patient, prompting cardiologic and genetic investigations.

The present patient underwent cardiology assessment at 3 years and 8 months as part of family screening. Echocardiography revealed an LVEF of 50% with mild ventricular dilatation. The combination of cardiac involvement and neutropenia prompted targeted genetic testing, confirming Barth syndrome. Molecular analysis by NGS panel of 72 genes involved in cardiomyopathies identified a hemizygous variant in the TAFAZZIN gene (NM_000116.5: c.278del; p.(Met93Serfs*46)) located in exon 3, causing a frameshift with a premature stop codon. This variant had not been reported in public databases (including GnomAD v4.1.0), and in silico predictions supported a deleterious effect. According to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria, it was classified as Likely Pathogenic (Class 4 PVS1, PM2). An analysis of the TAFAZZIN gene was performed on the asymptomatic patient’s mother, revealing the same mutation in a heterozygous state. Both parents had normal echocardiography, and no other affected relatives were identified.

The patient developed progressive deterioration of cardiac function. At 4 years, cardiac MRI revealed an LVEF of 35% with moderate dilation, prompting initiation of standard heart failure therapy. At 5 years, his condition had progressed to Ross class IV, with nasogastric feeding due to loss of oral intake. Echocardiography showed LVEF of 20% and moderate-to-severe mitral regurgitation. Despite optimal therapy, intermittent intravenous furosemide was required during decompensation. Feeding difficulties and malnutrition led to gastrostomy placement. Neutrophil counts remained normal under antibiotic prophylaxis. He was subsequently listed for heart transplantation (Table 1).

Given the severity and lack of alternatives, elamipretide was initiated under the Expanded Access Program, with approval from the French Drug Administration (“Agence Nationale de Sécurité du Médicament et des produits de santé”, ANSM) as compassionate use within the European Rare Disease Plan, following parental consent.

Intervention and follow-up

At baseline and after 6 months of treatment, clinical status (Ross functional class, feeding mode, anthropometric data), electrocardiography, echocardiography, physical fitness, and patient-reported outcomes were assessed. Physical fitness was evaluated in terms of functional capacity (six-minute walk test) [12], maximum isometric strength of the upper-limb (handgrip test) [13] and the lower-limb (standing long jump test) [14], static balance (one-leg stance test), speed/agility (4 × 10 m shuttle run test) [15, 16], and physical activity. A triaxial accelerometer (ActiGraph GT3X, ActiGraph LLC) was used to assess physical activity levels, offering objective data on moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) [17]. Adherence was evaluated according to the latest World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines, which recommend at least 60 min per day of MVPA for children [18]. HRQoL was measured with the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) 4.0 Generic Core Scales, both self- and proxy-reported [19].

Elamipretide was administered subcutaneously at a fixed dose of 10 mg once daily (0.13 mL of an 80 mg/mL formulation), according to the manufacturer’s Expanded Access Program dosing recommendations for children weighing 10–20 kg, using an Insuflon® subcutaneous catheter. During the first week, the device was placed in the upper arm for better tolerability in a young, active child. Thereafter, in accordance with manufacturer recommendations, it was placed in the thighs, alternating sides weekly, for the remainder of the treatment period (Fig. 1). Follow-up was coordinated with the local pediatric home hospitalization (“HAD”) team, which monitored the patient and participated in weekly cardiology staff videoconferences. The first injections were performed under inhaled nitrous oxide/oxygen mixture (MEOPA) without additional analgesia. Application of an ice pack at the injection site reduced local burning sensations. At 1 month of therapy, LVEF improved from 20% to 35%, mitral regurgitation decreased from moderate-to-severe to mild, Ross class improved from IV to II, partial oral intake resumed, and part-time school attendance was possible. At 3 months of therapy, LVEF reached 40%, mitral regurgitation improved to mild, Ross class improved to I, full-time school resumed, weight gain was adequate, and gastrostomy weaning began; transplant listing was temporarily suspended. At that time, the patient presented with low-grade fever, abdominal pain, and a moderate transient increase in pancreatic enzymes (lipase 356 U/L). Symptoms regressed within 48 h, abdominal ultrasound was normal, and treatment was maintained without interruption. Serology confirmed Mycoplasma pneumoniae primary infection, supporting an intercurrent infectious origin rather than drug-induced pancreatitis.

At 6 months, Ross class was I, oral feeding was nearly complete, LVEF was 50%, left ventricle was moderately dilated (+ 2 Z-score), and mitral regurgitation became mild (Table 1). Substantial nutritional recovery was observed, with weight increasing from 14.9 kg to 18.1 kg and corresponding improvement in age-adjusted anthropometric Z-scores. Functional capacity improved across all tested domains, with substantial gains in walking distance, muscular strength, balance, and agility. Accelerometer-derived daily moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) remained below the WHO guideline recommendation of ≥ 60 min/day at both baseline and 6-month follow-up. HRQoL scores improved markedly in both self- and proxy-reports, particularly in physical functioning and psychosocial domains (Table 2). No other adverse events were reported during follow-up.

Discussion

This is the first pediatric case of elamipretide therapy reported outside the United States, and the first in a preschool-aged child with Barth syndrome. Previous US reports described a newborn and an 11-month-old infant treated in acute, life-threatening contexts [10, 11]. In contrast, our patient presented with advanced chronic cardiomyopathy and progressive functional decline despite maximal medical therapy.

Although clinically significant cardiomyopathy became apparent later than is typically reported in Barth syndrome, no clear genotype–phenotype correlation has been established for TAFAZZIN loss-of-function variants. Notably, the patient’s younger brother, who carried the same pathogenic variant, developed severe infantile cardiomyopathy leading to death at 14 months of age. Consistent with previous cohort studies, substantial phenotypic variability has been reported among patients carrying truncating TAFAZZIN variants, irrespective of variant position within the gene [3].

Elamipretide was provided through the manufacturer’s Expanded Access Program, with approval from the French Drug Administration (ANSM), and delivered via the pediatric home hospitalization (HAD) team using a subcutaneous regimen specifically adapted to pediatric needs and compatible with ambulatory care. This mode of administration, using an Insuflon® subcutaneous catheter, avoided daily needle punctures and improved treatment acceptability for the child and caregivers. It has been used successfully for other therapies in children [20, 21], but, to our knowledge, has not been previously reported for elamipretide. While the manufacturer recommends abdominal or thigh injection sites, the upper arm was initially selected during the first week for better acceptability in a young child, before transitioning to the thigh, consistent with official guidance. This illustrates the importance of tailoring administration strategies to pediatric patients to optimize tolerability and adherence in long-term subcutaneous therapy for rare diseases. Treatment was well tolerated overall, with only one transient, non-serious episode of suspected pancreatitis, most likely related to an intercurrent Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection rather than elamipretide itself. Therapy was not interrupted, and overall safety remained favorable throughout the 6-month follow-up.

Follow-up was multidimensional, combining echocardiography with mitral regurgitation grading, functional capacity testing, accelerometer-derived physical activity assessment, patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), and nutritional assessment. Improvements were consistent across domains: LVEF rose from 20% to 50%, mitral regurgitation improved from moderate-to-severe to mild, Ross functional class improved from IV to I, and the patient returned to full-time school while remaining on temporary transplant suspension. Nutritionally, he progressed from exclusive gastrostomy feeding to near-complete oral intake. Functional outcomes, including walking distance, muscular strength, balance, and agility, showed substantial gains, and both self- and proxy-reported HRQoL improved markedly, particularly in physical functioning and psychosocial domains. Interestingly, self-reported emotional functioning scores declined during follow-up, whereas proxy-reported emotional functioning improved. Given the young age of the patient and the discordance between self- and proxy-reported assessments, this isolated finding should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect normal variability in emotional self-perception rather than a true deterioration in emotional well-being.

In young adults with Barth syndrome, physical exercise has been shown to improve muscle strength [22, 23], cardiorespiratory fitness [24], and quality of life [22, 24]. No such studies exist in children, but youth-tailored hybrid programs such as QUALIREHAB, combining center-based and home-based cardiac rehabilitation, could represent a promising approach to further optimize cardiac and muscular function in pediatric patients [25].

Conclusions

Although spontaneous improvement cannot be excluded, the magnitude and timing of changes strongly suggest a therapeutic benefit of elamipretide. This observation supports further pediatric evaluation of elamipretide not only in acute neonatal presentations but also in older children with Barth syndrome and highlights the value of innovative delivery methods to enhance adherence and quality of life in long-term subcutaneous therapy for rare pediatric disorders. Youth-tailored cardiac rehabilitation could further enhance outcomes in pediatric Barth syndrome.
