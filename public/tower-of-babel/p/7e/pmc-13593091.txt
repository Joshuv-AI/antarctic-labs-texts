Unraveling the causal links and mediation effects of lipid metabolites and inflammatory factors on gallstone disease risk
Authors: Xuan Bai, Dingzi Zhou, Jing Luo, Wenqian Yu, Hongyu Li, Guoheng Jiang, Jiong Lu, Min Gao, Menglin He, Yi Jiang, Xin Wang, Yiting Xu, Linjun Xie, Zijie Chen, Hong Yang, Cuihua Zhang, Mingshuang Tang, Xin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lipid metabolism abnormalities and inflammation have been implicated in gallstone disease (GSD) development, but the causal relationships and potential mediation effects among lipid metabolites, inflammatory factors, and GSD remain unclear. The aim of this study is to explore the causal relationships among these 3 factors. This study employed 2-sample Mendelian Randomization (TSMR) and 2-step MR to investigate the causal relationships and potential mediation effects among 91 inflammatory factors, 6 lipid metabolism-related molecules (HDL-C, LDL-C, TG, total cholesterol, ApoA1, and ApoB), and GSD. We opted for 4 distinct MR analysis methods including inverse variance weighted method, weighted median method, MR-Egger regression method and MR-PRESSO analysis. Sensitivity analyses included MR-Egger intercept tests, Cochran’s Q statistic, Steiger tests, and leave-one-out analyses. Product of coefficients method was used to estimate mediation proportion. TSMR analysis revealed that every 1-unit increase in low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), total cholesterol (TC), apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1), and apolipoprotein B (ApoB), the risk of GSD decreased by 16.5%, 10.2%, 8.4%, and 13.1%, respectively. Inflammatory factors such as Natural killer cell receptor 2B4 (CD244), Macrophage colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF-1), and interleukin-18 receptor 1 (IL-18R1) were identified as risk factors for GSD, while Fibroblast growth factor 19 levels (FGF19), Interleukin-1-alpha levels (IL-1α), and Interleukin-8 levels (IL-8) were found to be protective. Mediation analysis through 2-step MR identified potential pathways involving ApoA1--IL-8--GSD (P = .084) and IL-1α--ApoB--GSD (P = .117). This study provides robust evidence of causal links between specific lipid metabolites and GSD, as well as suggestive causal associations for several inflammatory factors. However, mediation analysis did not support significant roles for lipids or inflammatory factors as mediators in GSD pathogenesis. Future research could be further pursued in areas such as drug target intervention and mechanistic studies.

1. Introduction

Gallstone disease (GSD) is a prevalent gastrointestinal disorder, affecting 10% to 15% of adults globally and imposing a significant health and economic burden.[1] The global epidemiology of GSD exhibits substantial variations across geographic regions and between sexes. The prevalence in South America is 2.2-fold higher than that in Asia, and the prevalence in females is 1.4-fold higher than that in males. Overall, the global prevalence of GSD is 6%, and this proportion is increasing year by year.[2] Cholesterol-supersaturated bile represents the central pathogenic mechanism of gallstone formation, characterized by dysregulated hepatic cholesterol hypersecretion and impaired gallbladder absorption. Among gallstone subtypes, cholesterol stones are the most prevalent pathological form.[3] Cholecystitis, cholangitis, and acute pancreatitis are complications associated with gallstones,[4] often necessitating laparoscopic cholecystectomy. While laparoscopic cholecystectomy is characterized by low cost and short hospital stay, it carries risks of surgical complications such as stone spillage and bile duct injury. Despite the high prevalence and impact of GSD, the underlying mechanisms driving its development remain unclear.

Genes of lipid metabolism, proteins, molecular substances,[5] and lipid metabolism disorders characterized by abnormal levels of cholesterol and triglycerides[6] have been confirmed to play a key role in the formation of gallstones. Moreover, elevations in low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) caused by different metabolic pathways lead to distinct outcomes in gallstone risk.[7] Researchers have investigated multiple factors and found that obesity,[8] microbiota,[1] inflammation,[9] trace elements[10] can all increase the risk of GSD development. Elevated levels of certain specific cytokines, such as monocyte chemotactic protein 3,[11] and systemic inflammatory markers[12] have been shown to promote gallstone formation. Similarly, higher levels of inflammatory cytokines are observed in patients with GSD.[13] Moreover, gallstones themselves can induce an inflammatory response, promoting the expression and secretion of mucin. This forms a positive feedback loop of “gallstones-inflammation-mucin secretion-stone enlargement.”[14]

The previous cross-sectional studies have discussed this relationship was of limited utility, for its vulnerability and several confounders, leading to inconsistent conclusions regarding the relationships among lipid metabolism abnormalities, inflammation, and GSD. More critically, the direction of the potential causal associations and the intricate interactive mechanisms among these 3 factors remain undefined. To overcome the limitations of traditional observational studies, this study aims to assess the relationship between lipid metabolism-related molecules, inflammatory factors, and risk of GSD, utilizing the causal and mediation analysis, using the Mendelian Randomization (MR) approaches. The findings of this research are expected to elucidate the underlying mechanistic pathways of GSD development, thereby providing more robust evidence-based support for clinical prevention and treatment strategies.

2. Material and methods

2.1. Study design

MR uses single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer the causal associations between phenotypes, which can effectively avoid the biases of confounding and reverse causality in observational studies.[15] This study employs a comprehensive analytical framework, utilizing 2-sample MR (TSMR) and mediation MR to elucidate the causal relationships and potential mediation effects among lipid-related metabolites, inflammatory factors, and GSD. The study design adheres to the core assumptions of MR, ensuring relevance, independence, and exclusion criteria are met to produce robust and reliable results.[16]

TSMR was used to explore the causality between 91 inflammatory factors derived from the latest GWAS, 6 kinds of lipid metabolism-related molecules including high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1), apolipoprotein B (ApoB) and GSD, respectively. Then, total effect could be estimated as αi. A 2-step MR was further utilized to investigate potential unknown pathway associations among the 3 traits mentioned above and quantify the proportion of this mediating effect when getting statistically significant results. Primarily, we hypothesized that lipids might mediate the occurrence of GSD via inflammatory mediators. In the first step, we estimated the association of lipids with inflammatory mediators to get the effect size β1i, in the second step, multivariable MR (MVMR) was performed to derive the direct effect value β2i of inflammatory factors on GSD by adjusting for the influence of lipids. Subsequently, since it is unknown whether there is a bidirectional causal relationship between lipids and inflammatory factors, we further conducted the aforementioned mediation MR analysis using lipids as the mediating factors (Fig. 1). 2.2. Data resource

Since the data were derived from large-scale, publicly available Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) in which ethical approval had already been obtained, no additional ethical review was required for this study. For lipid-related metabolites, including HDL-C, LDL-C, TG and TC, data are derived from a comprehensive analysis published in Nature, encompassing a European population data, excluding UK Biobank participants, with an extensive sample size of 1280,016 individuals.[17] The data used for ApoA1 and ApoB were sourced from the recently published genome-wide characterization analysis of circulating metabolic biomarkers.[18] Inflammatory factor data, covering 91 plasma proteins, are sourced from a study involving 14,824 participants of European ancestry across 11 cohorts.[19] GSD data are obtained from a large-scale GWAS dataset comprising 43,639 cases and 506,798 controls, further enhanced through meta-analysis of UK Biobank and FinnGen data.[20] These datasets provide a robust foundation for our analyses, ensuring a comprehensive and diverse representation of genetic variants associated with each exposure and outcome. There was no overlap among all the samples, and the human reference genome version used was GRCh37.

2.3. Screening criteria of IVs

To ensure the validity of our IVs, we implemented stringent screening criteria. For lipid-related metabolites, IVs were selected based on a significance threshold of P < 5 × 10−8 to ensure strong associations with the exposures of interest. For inflammatory factors, given the need to capture a broader range of genetic variants, the threshold was set at P < 5 × 10−6. To address potential horizontal pleiotropy, linkage disequilibrium analysis was conducted, removing SNPs within a 10,000 kb window where R2 <0.001. Additionally, only SNPs with an F-statistic >10 were retained to mitigate weak IV bias. These criteria collectively ensure that the selected IVs are robust, relevant, and independent, thereby enhancing the credibility of our causal inferences. 2.4. Statistical analysis

For the TSMR analysis, we utilized 4 distinct MR methods: inverse variance weighted,[21] Weighted Median (WM),[22] MR-Egger regression,[23] and MR-PRESSO.[24] The inverse variance weighted method, implemented in a random-effect model, served as the primary analysis approach, allowing for heterogeneity among SNPs. The WM method provided a robust estimate, requiring at least half of the IVs to be valid. MR-Egger regression not only offered an alternative analytical approach but also tested for horizontal pleiotropy through its intercept. MR-PRESSO was employed to identify and eliminate outliers, ensuring the stability of our results. Sensitivity analyses, including MR-Egger intercept tests, Cochran’s Q statistic,[25] Steiger tests, and leave-one-out analyses,[26] were conducted to validate the robustness of our findings.

In the mediation MR analysis, the same methods were applied to explore potential mediation pathways. Notably, we set the screening threshold to P < 5 × 10−6. We calculated the standard error of the mediation effect using the Delta method. Indirect effect, also known as mediation effect, was calculated using the product of coefficients method,[27] specially as β1i×β2i. To address the potential inflation of type I errors arising from multiple MR analyses, we implemented Bonferroni correction for multiple testing. Specifically, the MR threshold criterion for determining the causal association between lipids and GSD was set at 0.008 (0.05/6), and the MR threshold criterion for determining the causal association between inflammatory factors and GSD was set at 0.0005 (0.05/91). The threshold for mediating MR remained at 0.05. We will only recognize the existence of a definite causal association when the P-value is less than the corrected threshold and the directions of the effect values obtained by the 3 statistical analysis methods are consistent. In our study, R packages such as “TwoSampleMR,” “ggplot2,” and “MendelianRandomization” were used. All the above statistical analyses were completed using R version 4.4.2.

3. Results

3.1. IVs selection

After screening with P < 5 × 10⁻⁸, F-statistic >10, and removal of linkage disequilibrium, the number of IVs strongly associated with lipid metabolites ranged from 57 to 372 SNPs, with the minimum F-statistic being 97.25. For inflammatory factors, IVs were screened using the same criteria except for a relaxed threshold of P < 5 × 10⁻⁶. The minimum F-statistic of 22.97 still exceeded the critical value for weak IVs. For additional details, please refer to Supplemental Material 1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q85.

3.2. TSMR analysis for lipids on GSD

The TSMR analysis revealed significant causal associations between specific lipid-related metabolites and the risk of GSD. A 1-unit increase in LDL-C, TC, ApoA1, and ApoB levels corresponded to a respective reduction in GSD risk by 16.5% (IVW-P < .001, 95% CI: 0.79–0.89), 10.2% (IVW-P < .001, 95% CI: 0.85–0.95), 8.4% (IVW-P = .003, 95% CI: 0.86–0.97), and 13.1% (IVW-P < .001, 95% CI: 0.83–0.91). These findings were consistent across multiple MR methods, including WM, and MR-Egger regression, further validating the robustness of these causal links. The outliers removed by the MR-PRESSO method are shown in the Supplemental Material 2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q85.

For the 2 exposure factors, HDL-C and TG, the effect sizes derived from the MR-Egger method were inconsistent with those from the other methods (IVW and WM). Even though the P-values after Bonferroni correction were statistically significant, we deemed these results as negative, indicating the absence of causal associations between HDL-C, TG, and GSD. 3.3. TSMR analysis for inflammatory factors on GSD

Among the inflammatory factors examined, natural killer cell receptor 2B4 (CD244), macrophage colony-stimulating factor1 (CSF-1), and interleukin-18 receptor 1 (IL-18R1) emerged as risk factors for GSD. A 1-unit increase in these factors corresponded to a 5.4% (IVW-P = .04, 95% CI: 1.00–1.11), 8.8% (IVW-P < .01, 95% CI: 1.02–1.16), and 4.4% (IVW-P < .01, 95% CI: 1.01–1.08) increase in GSD risk, respectively. Conversely, fibroblast growth factor 19 levels (FGF19), interleukin-1-alpha levels (IL-1α), and interleukin-8 (IL-8) exhibited protective effects, with effect sizes less than zero, indicating a reduced risk of GSD associated with higher levels of these inflammatory factors. Therefore, the above results only suggest that these 6 inflammatory factors may have a causal association with GSD. 3.4. Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to ensure the robustness of the findings. The MR-Egger intercept tests indicated no significant horizontal pleiotropy (P > .05) for the lipid-related metabolites and inflammatory factors. The leave-one-out analysis confirmed that no single SNP unduly influenced the results. The Steiger test also validated the correctness of the causal direction for all exposures. However, the Cochran Q statistic of LDL-C and TC indicated the heterogeneity. The summary results of the sensitivity analysis for lipids and inflammatory factors are shown in Table 1.

3.5. Two-step MR analysis

The analysis explored potential pathways through which lipid-related metabolites might influence GSD risk via inflammatory factors. The analysis suggested potential mediation pathways involving ApoA1--IL-8--GSD and IL-1α--ApoB--GSD. However, the mediation effects did not reach statistical significance, with mediation proportions of −10.17% (P = .084) for ApoA1--IL-8--GSD and −10.21% (P = .117) for IL-1α--ApoB--GSD. This indicates that while these pathways may play a role in the relationship between lipid metabolites and GSD, their impact is not definitively proven in this study (Fig. 6).

4. Discussion

This study employed robust MR techniques to investigate the causal relationships and potential mediation effects among lipid metabolites, inflammatory factors, and GSD. Our findings revealed significant causal associations between specific lipid-related metabolites (LDL-C, TC, ApoA1, and ApoB) and GSD, as well as suggestive causal links for several inflammatory factors (CD244, FGF19, CSF-1, IL-1α, IL-18R1, and IL-8). Although mediation analysis did not identify significant mediating roles for these factors, our results provide novel insights into the pathogenesis of GSD and highlight potential targets for prevention and treatment.

Comparison with previous studies and potential mechanisms, our study identified LDL-C, TC, ApoA1, and ApoB as significant causal factors associated with a reduced risk of GSD. This finding is somewhat counterintuitive, as dyslipidemia is generally considered a risk factor for various diseases. However, previous studies have shown inconsistent results regarding the relationship between lipid levels and GSD. For instance, some studies have suggested that different pathways for lowering LDL-C can lead to either an increased or a decreased risk of GSD,[28] while others have found no association using MVMR.[29] Elevated levels of ApoA1, constituting roughly 70% of the protein component within HDL-C,[30] have also been shown to reduce GSD risk, which is consistent with its role in reverse cholesterol transport and anti-inflammatory effects. This is further supported by epidemiological studies that have reported an inverse association between HDL-C cholesterol and GSD.

The mechanisms underlying these associations may involve the interplay between lipid metabolism and gallbladder function. Cholesterol is a vital component of cell membranes, and the cholesterol carried by LDL-C plays a crucial role in maintaining the normal structure and function of gallbladder cells. Studies have revealed that cholesterol is key in activating conserved antimicrobial pathways, which are essential for survival against bacterial infection.[31] Therefore, when pathogens invade the gallbladder, cholesterol from LDL-C might offer essential material support for the tissue’s immune defense. This helps the body resist infection and reduce inflammatory responses, potentially contributing indirectly to GSD prevention. Additionally, ApoA1, as a major component of HDL-C, may facilitate the transport of cholesterol from peripheral tissues to the liver for excretion, thus reducing cholesterol saturation in bile. The effect of TC may stem from the fact that prior studies were primarily based on cross-sectional observational designs, where serum TC exhibited an inverted U-shaped association with GSD. When TC levels exceed 2.57mmol/L, the risk of disease begins to decline.[32] This highlights the complexity of lipid metabolism in GSD pathogenesis and suggests that maintaining optimal levels of these lipids could be beneficial in preventing GSD.

The identification of specific inflammatory factors as risk or protective factors for GSD provides further insights into the pathogenesis of this disease. Our study found that CD244, CSF-1, and IL-18R1 were associated with an increased risk of GSD, while FGF19, IL-1α, and IL-8 exhibited protective effects. These results are consistent with the emerging understanding of the role of inflammation in GSD. For example, IL-8, a chemokine involved in the recruitment and activation of inflammatory cells, has been shown to reduce the incidence of GSD in our study. This may be due to its role in modulating local inflammatory responses in the gallbladder, thereby preventing gallstone formation.[33] The protective effect of FGF19, a growth factor involved in bile acid metabolism,[34] further underscores the importance of metabolic regulation in GSD pathogenesis. Conversely, CSF-1, which promotes macrophage recruitment and activation, may exacerbate inflammation and increase the risk of GSD.[35]

The interplay between lipid metabolism and inflammation appears to be crucial, as evidenced by the potential mediation pathways involving ApoA1--IL-8--GSD and IL-1α--ApoB--GSD. Although these mediation effects were not statistically significant in our analysis suggesting the potential presence of unmeasured mediating pathways that may counteract or reverse the effect of mediator, they highlight the complexity of the relationship between these factors.

The mechanisms underlying these associations may involve the interplay between inflammation and lipid metabolism. Elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-18R1 and CSF-1 could promote local inflammation in the gallbladder, leading to increased cholesterol saturation and gallstone formation. Conversely, anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1α and IL-8 may modulate the inflammatory response, reducing the likelihood of gallstone formation. Cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase (CYP7A1) is the rate-limiting enzyme in bile acid synthesis. FGF19 can suppress CYP7A1 expression, thereby reducing bile acid production.[36] To maintain the balance of cholesterol, bile acids, and phospholipids in bile, the liver decreases cholesterol secretion, which lowers the cholesterol saturation in bile. Consequently, this reduces the likelihood of cholesterol crystallization and subsequent GSD formation.

In terms of public health, our study offers more possibilities for the prevention and treatment of GSD. For example, we can carry out timely drug interventions for individuals with excessively high ApoA1 levels and observe whether the IL-8 levels increase, so as to achieve the goal of reducing the risk of GSD. Among general internal medicine patients with asymptomatic gallstones, around 2% experience significant symptoms like right upper quadrant pain and jaundice each year.[37] Once symptoms surface, complications associated with gallstones are prone to occur. The most prevalent complication is bile duct obstruction caused by the migration of stones.[38] Moreover, life-threatening conditions such as acute cholecystitis, acute cholangitis, and gallstone pancreatitis[39] may also emerge. Therefore, identifying modifiable risk factors for gallstones and implementing timely interventions during the asymptomatic window period are crucial. This approach may help reduce gallstone-related complications and the need for surgery, thereby alleviating patient suffering and decreasing economic burdens.

4.1. Strengths and limitations

The strengths of our study are as follows: First, this study is pioneering in elucidating the pathway association among lipids, inflammatory factors, and GSD. Second, it offers novel insights into the pathogenesis of GSD. Moreover, through the 2-step MR analysis, we have, for the first time, uncovered that inflammatory factors may indirectly contribute to the development of GSD by modulating blood lipids (such as ApoB), a mechanism that is often obscured in observational studies. However, our study also has certain limitations. First, the genetic data and phenotypic information used in this study were exclusively derived from European populations. Although this improves internal consistency and reduces population stratification bias, it considerably limits the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups, such as East Asian or African populations, who may exhibit distinct genetic architectures and environmental exposures. Future replication of these analyses in more diverse cohorts is essential to validate and extend our results. Second, although the GWAS dataset provided robust overall associations, its relatively restricted scope precluded more detailed subgroup analyses. For instance, we were unable to perform stratified analyses by key demographic variables such as gender, age group, or comorbid conditions, which might reveal important heterogeneity in genetic effects. Future studies with finer-grained data and functional validation will be crucial to elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying the observed associations.

5. Conclusion

Our analysis revealed robust causal associations between specific lipid metabolites (LDL-C, TC, ApoA1, and ApoB) and GSD, as well as suggestive causal links for several inflammatory factors (CD244, FGF19, CSF-1, IL-1α, IL-18R1, and IL-8). Although mediation analysis did not identify significant mediating factors, our findings highlight the potential for lipid and inflammatory factor-targeted interventions in GSD prevention and treatment. These results provide valuable guidance for future clinical research and public health initiatives aimed at reducing the burden of GSD. Mechanistic studies and basic research are essential to understand how lipid metabolites and inflammatory factors interact to influence GSD development. Additionally, large-scale prospective cohort studies and clinical trials should be conducted to confirm the causal associations and mediation effects identified in our study. Future research should also focus on exploring potential therapeutic targets and interventions based on these findings.
