Efficacy and Safety Differences Between Abiraterone and Enzalutamide in Patients with Metastatic Castration-Resistant Prostate Cancer: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Real-World Studies
Authors: Anikó Katalin Valikovics, Dániel Bacsó, Nadeen Samaien, Isabel Pinto Amorim das Virgens, Boglárka Szentes, Gergely Agócs, Mahmoud Obeidat, Tamás Széll, Boris Hadaschik, Péter Hegyi, Péter Nyirády, Tibor Szarvas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAbiraterone (ABI) and enzalutamide (ENZA) are standard treatments for metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC). However, their safety and efficacy have not been directly compared in prospective clinical trials.ObjectiveTo directly compare their safety and efficacy in prospective clinical trials.Patients and MethodsA systematic search was performed in PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library databases (CRD42024608496) on 24 June 2025. Eligibility criteria included studies on patients with mCRPC treated with ABI or ENZA reporting outcomes such as overall survival (OS), prostate-specific antigen (PSA) response (PSA50/90), progression-free survival (rPFS/bPFS), and rates of grade ≥ 3 adverse events, dose reduction, treatment discontinuation, and major cardiovascular events (MACE). Both first- and second-line mCRPC treatment settings were considered. The inverse variance method was used to calculate pooled hazard ratios (HRs), using the natural logarithm of the HR and its standard error (SE) from the available data.ResultsA total of 47 real-world observation studies and data from 71,984 patients were included, with no randomized controlled trials available for direct comparison. ENZA proved more effective in terms of PSA50 (odds ratio (OR): 1.82; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.38–2.4) and OS (HR: 0.79; 95% CI 0.71–0.87) in the overall cohort as well as in the first-line (PSA50: OR: 1.69; 95% CI 1.23–2.31, OS: HR: 0.85, 95% CI 0.79-0.92) and second-line setting (PSA50 OR: 1.78; 95% CI 1.23–2.58, OS: HR: 0.69; 95% CI 0.60–0.80). However, grade ≥ 3 AEs and treatment discontinuation rates were significantly more frequent with ENZA (OR: 2.17; 95% CI 0.85–5.54 and OR: 1.81; 95% CI 1.45–2.25, respectively), while MACE rates were lower (OR: 0.53; 95% CI 0.33–0.84).ConclusionsENZA showed greater efficacy, whereas serious AEs were less frequent with ABI. However, this difference may decrease or even disappear upon switching these therapies. Therefore, individual decision-making is encouraged, balancing efficacy and safety. ENZA should be prioritized if OS is the main goal, whereas ABI may be preferred when minimizing AEs is important. ENZA is also associated with a lower risk of MACE.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11523-026-01230-3.

Key Points

No randomized controlled trials currently compare abiraterone and enzalutamide in mCRPC with overall survival as the primary endpoint.Our meta-analysis of real-world observational data indicates that enzalutamide is associated with improved overall survival and PSA response compared with abiraterone, albeit with higher rates of serious adverse events.Treatment decisions should be individualized, taking into account efficacy, safety, comorbidities, and the potential impact of treatment sequencing.

Introduction

Prostate cancer is the second most common cancer overall, with approximately 1.4 million new cases and 375,000 deaths, making it the fifth leading cause of cancer-related death in men [1]. Antiandrogen therapy is an effective first-line treatment for metastatic prostate cancer. However, the disease often progresses, becoming unresponsive to androgen deprivation. Castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) refers to progression despite castration-level testosterone, typically in the metastatic stage (mCRPC) [2].

Despite a significant reduction in sensitivity to baseline androgen therapy (ADT), CRPC remains dependent on the androgen receptor (AR) signaling pathway [3]. Therapeutic strategies aimed at further inhibiting AR signaling were initially developed for the treatment of CRPC [3]. Abiraterone (ABI) in combination with low-dose prednisone and enzalutamide (ENZA) are both second-generation oral AR pathway inhibitors (ARPIs) with largely overlapping indication for mCRPC [4]. More recently, both agents have been approved for use in metastatic hormone-sensitive setting in combination with ADT [5]. ABI is a CYP17 inhibitor that reduces androgen production in the adrenal glands, testes, and tumor cells, whereas ENZA is an AR antagonist [6]. The efficacy of ABI and ENZA in improving survival has been demonstrated in clinical trials in both the second-line setting after docetaxel (COU-AA-301 and AFFIRM) and the first-line mCRPC setting (COU-AA-302, PREVAIL) [6–10].

However, despite their proven efficacy and widespread use, the comparative benefits and risks of the two agents are still not fully understood. No head-to-head clinical trials have compared overall survival (OS) between ENZA and ABI. The only randomized controlled trial (RCT) is a multicenter phase II crossover study [11]. However, the crossover design prevented a direct OS comparison [6]. On the other hand, several real-world studies comparing the two agents have been published over the past few years, allowing meta-analyses with large patient cohorts [12–14]. Previous reviews have primarily focused on comparing OS as the only endpoint and were based on cohorts with limited sample sizes [15, 16]. Therefore, the aim of this study is to provide a comprehensive analysis of the largest available patient population, evaluating both first- and second-line treatments and assessing multiple efficacy and safety endpoints to offer a deeper understanding of these two widely used agents.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook and the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, and the study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024608496) (Supplementary Table 1) [17–19].

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

We included studies that fit the following PICO framework [20]: P (population) patients with mCRPC; I (intervention) ENZA; C (comparison) ABI; O (outcome) OS, PSA response (PSA50, PSA90), radiographic PFS (rPFS); biochemical PFS (bPFS), AEs, MACE, dose reduction, and treatment discontinuation (see Supplementary Text 1 for definitions). Both first- and second-line applications were allowed. We excluded reviews, meta-analyses, conference abstracts, and case reports, whereas prospective trials and cohort studies were included. We excluded articles included both nmCRPC and mCRPC but did not separately reported results for the two groups.

Information Sources

We conducted a systematic literature search in three electronic databases: MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, and Cochrane Library. In addition, we reviewed the reference lists using the CitationChaser tool [21].

Search Strategy

The following search key was generated from a single domain of interventions and used in all three databases: “Abiraterone AND Enzalutamide”.

Study Selection and Characteristics

First, duplicate screening was performed by A.V., followed by abstract selection performed by two independent reviewers (D.B. and N.S.). Moreover, full-text selection was performed independently by the same authors (D.B. and N.S.). After each screening step, Cohen’s kappa was calculated. Conflicting decisions were resolved by a third reviewer (A.V.).

Data Items and Extraction

The following information was extracted independently by two authors (A.V. and D.B.): name of first author, date of publication, DOI number, study type, study period, continent, country, data source, journal name, setting line, number and age of patients, OS, radiographic/biochemical progression-free survival (r/bPFS), prostate-specific antigen response ≥ 50% (PSA50), prostate-specific antigen response ≥ 90% (PSA90), major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), grade ≥ 3 adverse events (AEs), treatment discontinuation, and dose reduction. Data extraction was also checked by a third independent reviewer (N.S.). Grade ≥ 3 AEs were extracted as reported in the included studies; however, detailed breakdowns of specific adverse event types were not consistently available.

Methodological Quality and Risk of Bias Assessment

Risk of bias was assessed separately for each study by two authors (A.V. and N.S.). Two tools were used to estimate bias: Risk of Bias [22] in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I), which is intended to assess non-randomized studies, and the ROB-2 tool for randomized clinical trials (RCTs) [23].

The level of evidence for each study was determined on the basis of the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine (OCEBM) levels of evidence (version 2, 2011) [24].

The methodological quality of non-randomized studies was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) in our study (A.V. and N.S.) [25].

The overall methodological quality of the systematic review was assessed using A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews (AMSTAR 2, 2017 version*) [26]. Each domain was assessed according to the AMSTAR 2 guidelines, and the overall reliability of the review results was classified as high, moderate, low, or critically low (A.V. and N.S.).

Synthesis Methods

Both qualitative and quantitative syntheses of the data were performed. A minimum of two clinically poolable studies was required to perform a meta-analysis. The inverse variance method was used to calculate pooled hazard ratios (HRs), using the natural logarithm of the HR and its SE from the available data [27]. To estimate the heterogeneity variance measure (tau-squared), we used the restricted maximum-likelihood estimator with the Q profile method for confidence intervals (CI) [28, 29]. The Hartung–Knapp adjustment was applied to estimate the CI for pooled effect [30]. Heterogeneity was assessed using Higgins and Thompson I2 statistics [31]. This was determined on the basis of the general guidelines of Cochrane on heterogeneity. Results were considered statistically significant if the CI did not contain the null hypothesis. Subgroup analyses were performed on the basis of treatment line (first line, second line, and mixed populations where treatment line was not specified) and data adjustment status (adjusted versus unadjusted results). Studies without clear treatment line definition were categorized as “mixed” and analyzed separately. We assumed different τ2 values in the subgroups. To assess the difference between the subgroups, we used a Cochrane Q test (an omnibus test) between subgroups [28]. The null hypothesis was rejected at a significance level of 5%. In addition, we used Egger’s linear regression asymmetry test to assess publication bias for endpoints where at least ten articles could be analyzed.

An individual patient data (IPD) meta-analysis was performed for different treatment modalities separately. IPD was retrieved from published Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves using plot digitalization tools [32] and following the methodology of Liu et al. [33]. For each treatment modality, we estimated pooled survival probabilities for predefined time intervals and pooled median survival times (with 95% CI). To this end, we implemented the multivariate method described by Combescure et al. [34]. The Greenwood formula was used to estimate CIs for pooled survival probabilities. We summarized our findings in forest plots and in tables. The statistical analysis was conducted in the R statistical software [35] using the “meta” [36], “dmetar” [37], “IPDfromKM” [33], and “metaSurvival” packages.

Results

Search and Selection

Our systematic search yielded a total of 9490 articles. After duplicate removal, 7138 articles remained, and after title and abstract selection, 186 articles were eligible for full-text selection. After the latter, 45 articles were eligible for inclusion. During our re-screening between 18 November 2024 and 24 June 2025, two additional eligible articles were identified, resulting in 47 eligible [12–14, 38–81] studies for data extraction (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). The selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram in Fig. 1, and a respective checklist was filled (Supplementary Table 1).

Basic Characteristics of Included Studies

Our systematic search found 47 retrospective observational studies. We extracted data from 47 articles, which included data from 71,984 patients with CRPC treated with ABI or ENZA. Articles were published between 2020 and 2025. A total of 42,057 patients were included in the ABI arm, while 29,927 were included in the ENZA arm (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 4).

Overall Survival

A total of 34 articles provided data on OS, of which 16 [13, 14, 41, 48, 50, 51, 56–60, 64, 66, 72, 77, 79] and 9 articles [41, 42, 51, 59, 60, 63, 71, 73, 79] reported first- and second-line ABI/ENZA treatment, respectively, while 14 articles reported results that did not define treatment lines or provided results from patients with mixed treatment lines [38–40, 44, 46, 52–54, 62, 67, 69, 74, 75, 78]. We collected both unadjusted and if available also adjusted effect measures for the most important endpoint of OS, where the adjusted estimates took into account various clinical–pathological parameters (e.g. age, baseline PSA, PSA doubling time, race, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) status, etc.) (Supplementary Table 2). These data were evaluated collectively, as well as separately in adjusted and nonadjusted subgroups.

The overall (line-independent) evaluation of 51,642 patients revealed a 21% lower risk of death in patients with mCRPC treated with ENZA compared with patients treated with ABI (HR: 0.79, 95% CI 0.71–0.87, p < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 1). The difference remained significant also when considering only adjusted (HR: 0.87, 95% CI 0.80–0.94, p = 0.005), or only unadjusted articles (HR: 0.73, 95% CI 0.61–0.87, p = 0.001), with no significant difference between the subgroups (Supplementary Fig. 2). We also pooled results on the basis of patient-level data extracted from KM curves. For the line-independent evaluation, the number of patients receiving ABI was 1392 while the number receiving ENZA was 1112. The pooled median OS was 23.9 months (95% CI 16.62–31.55 months) for ABI and 30.2 months (95% CI 23.06–38.09 months) for ENZA (Fig. 2).

Pooled Kaplan–Meier survival curves illustrating overall survival in patients with mCRPC treated with abiraterone or enzalutamide, stratified by treatment line: A all treatment lines combined, B first-line therapy only, and C second-line therapy only

In the first-line mCRPC setting, we found that the OS of 24,517 patients was 15% longer in the ENZA-treated compared with the ABI-treated patients (HR: 0.85, 95% CI 0.79–0.92, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 3). The difference remained significant in the adjusted subgroup (HR: 0.86, 95% CI 0.79–0.93, p = 0.003), while it showed an insignificant trend in the unadjusted subgroup (HR: 0.85, 95% CI 0.69–1.05, p = 0.1) with no significant difference between the subgroups (p = 0.9). Heterogeneity was low in both subgroups. In second-line treatment, both adjusted (HR: 0.71, 95% CI 0.6–0.85, p = 0.014) and unadjusted (HR: 0.66, 95% CI 0.48–0.91, p = 0.021) results remained significant. There was no heterogeneity in the adjusted group, while it was high in the unadjusted group (Supplementary Fig. 4). For the pooled KM curve data analysis, 933 patients were included in the ABI arm and 908 in the ENZA arm. The median OS was 24.4 months (CI 19.49–30.16 months) for ABI and 29.1 months (CI 22.30–35.55 months) for ENZA (Fig. 2).

For the second-line mCRPC setting, OS data were available for 5053 patients, and a 31% better survival was observed in the ENZA group compared with the ABI group (HR: 0.69, 95% CI 0.6–0.8, p = 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 5). For the pooled KM analysis, data from 452 ABI-treated patients and 204 ENZA-treated patients were available. Median OS was 13.5 months (95% CI 8.67–17.14 months) in the ABI group and 17.0 months (95% CI 10.29–23.39 months) in the ENZA group (Fig. 2).

PSA Response

For the PSA50 outcome, 17 articles were included, of which 12 reported on first-line treatment and 5 on second-line treatment settings (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 4) [6–12, 12–39, 39–41, 41, 42, 42, 43, 43–45, 45, 46, 46, 47, 47–58, 61, 63, 66, 72, 74, 76]. In the overall (line-independent) evaluation, the OR was 1.82, indicating that ENZA was associated with an 82% higher likelihood of achieving a PSA50 response compared with ABI (OR: 1.82, 95% CI 1.38–2.40, p = 0.001) (Supplementary Table 6).

In both first- and second-line mCRPC treatment settings, ENZA showed similarly favorable PSA50 outcomes compared with ABI (first-line: OR:1.69, 95% CI 1.23–2.31, p = 0.004; second-line: OR:1.78, 95% CI 1.23–2.58, p = 0.012) (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Only two articles reported PSA90 response, one with an 84% higher response rate of response for ENZA in first-line treatment and one with a 93% higher response rate for ENZA (Supplementary Fig. 8).

bPFS and rPFS

The number of eligible articles for PFS was n = 7 for bPFS [41, 44, 56, 60, 62, 64, 67]. Given the limited data available for these endpoints, only a line-independent evaluation was possible.

The risk of biochemical progression tended to be lower for ENZA compared with ABI, with a 24% relative reduction (HR: 0.76, 95% CI 0.56–1.03, p = 0.073) (Supplementary Fig. 9). On the basis of the pooled KM data for bPFS, the number of patients receiving ABI was 425 while the number of patients receiving ENZA was 393. The median bPFS time was 9.5 months (95% CI 4.97–13.22 months) for ABI and 8.9 months (95% CI 5.47–12.8 months) for ENZA (Supplementary Fig. 10).

The rPFS outcome (n = 353, ABI; n = 273, ENZA) showed no significant difference between ENZA and ABI (HR: 0.93, 95% CI 0.68–1.28, p = 0.674) (Supplementary Fig. 9). Calculated from pooled KM curves, the median rPFS was 14.8 months (95% CI 8.86–22.00 months) for ABI and 17.3 months (95% CI 8.89–22.82 months) for ENZA (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Adverse Events

Four endpoints for AEs could be assessed; MACE, grade ≥ 3 AEs, treatment discontinuation, and dose reduction. The number of articles eligible for AE endpoints were as follows; MACE: n = 5 [53, 56, 69, 80, 81], grade ≥ 3 AEs: n = 7 [44–46, 61, 66, 70, 76], treatment discontinuation: n = 4 [54, 55, 58, 68], dose reduction: n = 3 [46, 58, 76], and PSA90: n = 2 [39, 65].

On the basis of five publications, the risk of MACE was 47% lower in patients treated with ENZA compared with patients treated with ABI (HR: 0.53, 95% CI 0.33–0.84, p = 0.007) (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Grade ≥ 3 AEs tended to be higher in the ENZA-treated group compared with the ABI-treated one (OR: 2.17, 95% CI 0.85–5.54, p = 0.089) (Supplementary Fig. 12).

Four articles evaluating treatment discontinuation revealed an 81% higher risk in the ENZA-treated group compared with the ABI-treated one (OR: 1.81, 95% CI 1.45–2.25, p = 0.003) (Supplementary Fig. 13).

Information on dose reduction was available from three articles with a limited number of patients, but a significant difference was found between ABI- and ENZA-treated patients (OR: 0.24, 95% CI 0.06–0.90, p = 0.044) (Supplementary Fig. 14).

Results on Quality and Risk of Bias Assessment

Of the 47 studies included, 45 were retrospective and 2 were prospective, which were assessed using the ROBINS-I tool. Of these, 2 prospective studies were rated as having low risk of bias, 30 studies were rated as having moderate risk of bias, and 14 studies were rated as having serious risk of bias. Overall, these results indicate that the majority of the included studies were not prospective and had a moderate risk of bias, while some had a serious risk of bias due to confounding factors, participant selection, and outcome measurement. Therefore, although the overall direction of the results appears to be reliable, the strength of the evidence as a whole should be considered moderate owing to limitations arising from retrospective design (Supplementary Fig. 15).

The methodological quality of individual studies was assessed using the NOS. NOS scores ranged from 4 to 8 (median 7), which corresponds to adequate and good quality. Most studies were rated as good (≥ 7 points), with adequate quality generally owing to limited adjustment for confounding factors (comparability = 1/2) or incomplete follow-up. All studies were classified as level III evidence, consistent with its observational cohort design. The NOS analysis confirms that, although most studies met acceptable quality criteria, the retrospective design inherently limits the strength of the overall evidence (Supplementary Table 5).

The methodological quality of the systematic review was assessed using the AMSTAR-2 checklist, resulting in “high overall methodological quality.” Minor limitations included the fact that funding data for some primary studies were not fully available, but this did not affect the overall assessment (Supplementary Table 6).

Publication Bias and Heterogeneity

The endpoints of OS (all treatment lines), OS (first line), PSA50 (all lines), and PSA50 (first line) had at least ten articles included in the forest plots. None of these endpoints showed significant publication bias based on Egger’s test (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17).

Our analysis revealed a high degree of heterogeneity for the endpoints OS (I2 = 87%; 95% CI 83–90%) and PSA50 (I2 = 63%, 95% CI 38–78%) when first- and second-line treatments were analyzed together in a “line-independent evaluation.” This can be explained by the significant prognostic differences between first- and second-line settings. However, when looking separately at the treatment lines, we found that OS showed low and moderate heterogeneity in the first- and second-line settings (I2 = 30%, 95% CI 0–62%, I2 = 64%, 95% CI 26–82%, respectively), and PSA50 showed moderate heterogeneity in the first-line (I2 = 51% 95% CI 6–51%) and low heterogeneity in the second-line setting (I2 = 0%, 95% CI 0–79%).

Discussion

We conducted a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis comparing the safety and efficacy of ABI and ENZA in the first- and second-line setting of mCRPC. Our data revealed significantly better efficacy for ENZA in terms of OS and PSA50 response rate compared with ABI in both settings. Safety evaluation revealed higher rates of grade ≥ 3 AEs for ENZA, while MACEs were more frequent among patients treated with ABI.

Management of mCRPC remains a major challenge, particularly when personalizing therapeutic decisions. Treatment paradigms are evolving, with ARPIs moving to hormone-sensitive disease, and poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibition and radioligand treatment being used in earlier treatment lines. However, these are not universally available. Over the past decade, two key ARPIs, ABI and ENZA, have become the most commonly used agents in the treatment of mCRPC. Although both target the AR pathway, they have distinct mechanisms of action, which may be reflected in their different safety and efficacy profiles. In the only prospective phase II clinical trial, the two agents were compared in the first-line mCRPC setting, and ENZA showed a higher PSA response rate (80%) compared with ABI (65%) and a slightly longer median bPFS (11.2 versus 10.2 months) [8]. Our PFS results were based on data from seven studies with 1111 patients and showed different outcomes. On the basis of the pooled KM curve, the median bPFS was 9.5 months for ABI and 8.9 months for ENZA. The risk of bPFS was 24% lower for ENZA compared with ABI (HR: 0.76, 95% CI 0.56–1.03, p = 0.073). The lack of significant difference in PFS may partly reflect limitations of this endpoint in real-world settings, where imaging assessments are not performed systematically. In contrast to PSA-based endpoints, radiographic progression may be underreported or inconsistently captured, reducing the reliability of PFS comparisons.

The median OS time for ABI (24.4 months) and ENZA (29.1 months) in the first-line mCRPC setting was found to be slightly shorter than in the respective clinical trials (ABI: COU-AA-302, median OS: 34.7 months/ENZA: PREVAIL, median OS: 32.4 months) [5, 6]. These differences may be attributed the fact that prospective clinical trials typically include a highly selected patient population with generally good performance status, absence of visceral metastases, and limited comorbidities, while real-world use often involves older, frailer patients with more advanced disease and greater medical complexity. These broader patient characteristics, along with less frequent monitoring, contribute to reduced OS outcomes outside of the controlled trial setting.

Notably, in a large patient population of ~ 25,000 patients who received ABI or ENZA in the first-line setting, we found a significant, 15% better OS for ENZA-treated compared with ABI-treated patients. This difference remained partially significant, with similar HR values, when unadjusted and adjusted datasets were analyzed separately, suggesting that confounding factors are unlikely to meaningfully influence this clinically relevant finding. Accordingly, the pooled median OS was ~ 5 months longer (24.4 versus 29.1 months) for ENZA compared with ABI. This observation was further corroborated by the PSA response endpoint, which revealed a significantly, 75% higher PSA50 rate for ENZA compared with ABI. The large PSA50 difference may stems from ENZA’s direct AR inhibition, which may effectively suppress AR-regulated genes such as PSA, while ABI reduces upstream androgen levels but does not fully block receptor activation.

In the second-line mCRPC setting, the median OS in our real-world dataset was slightly lower (13.5 versus 17.0 months) compared with the corresponding approval trials, 15.8 and 18.4 months for ABI and ENZA, respectively [3, 4]. Again, the stricter inclusion criteria in the prospective studies may explain the differences. Consistent with our findings in the first-line setting, we observed a significantly longer OS of 31% with ENZA compared with ABI in the second-line mCRPC setting. This effect was evident in both adjusted and unadjusted analyses across subgroups, suggesting a low risk of selection bias. The PSA50 response rate was significantly improved by 78% in the ENZA compared with the ABI treatment group, which is consistent with our observations in the first-line setting.

Although some data suggest that ABI followed by ENZA may yield higher response rates than the reverse sequence, this should be interpreted with caution [11, 82]. Substantial cross-resistance between ARPIs results in limited clinical benefit of sequential ARPI use. Accordingly, current treatment recommendations favor switching to therapies with a different mechanism of action after ARPI progression, particularly as many patients are already exposed to these agents earlier in the disease course. However, in real-world clinical practice, a substantial proportion of patients with mCRPC receive sequential ARPI treatment. In one of the largest real-world studies, ENZA was associated with improved OS compared with ABI among patients who received only a single ARPI [40]. In contrast, no significant difference in OS was observed among patients who received both ARPIs during the course of their disease, suggesting that the survival benefit associated with initial ARPI selection may diminish when both agents are ultimately administered.

Administration of ARPI to ADT significantly increases cardiovascular AEs such as hypertension, acute coronary syndrome, cardiac dysrhythmia, and cerebrovascular events as well as cardiovascular (CV)-related death [83]. Our results revealed a mixed cardiovascular side effect profile for ABI and ENZA. ENZA demonstrated a statistically significant advantage over ABI in terms of MACE [53, 56, 69, 80, 81]. One possible explanation is that ABI combined with prednisone is linked to a higher risk of cardiovascular AEs, suggesting that prednisone dosing may influence ABI’s cardiovascular risk profile. Additionally, prednisone use in diabetic patients may cause hyperglycemia in diabetic patients, predispose to osteoporosis, and contribute to hypertension [70]. Lee et al. [53] also highlighted the occurrence of hypokalemia and consequent severe arrhythmias in patients treated with ABI. Furthermore, Matsukawa et al. reported more frequent but less severe arrhythmias with ABI compared with ENZA, while no other cardiovascular AEs differed significantly between the two agents [84]. Only five studies evaluated MACE as a composite cardiovascular endpoint, and these formed the basis of our pooled MACE analysis. In contrast, other studies reported individual cardiovascular adverse events rather than composite MACE outcomes, which limited their inclusion in the pooled analysis.

From a clinical perspective, these findings are particularly important in the management of patients with pre-existing cardiovascular risk factors or other comorbidities and support the need for a multidisciplinary approach. In patients with cardiovascular disease, ENZA should be recommended, and patients may benefit from a pretreatment oncocardiology assessment [85].

In contrast, in patients at higher risk of neurological complications, ABI may be the preferred option. This is due to the higher incidence of fatigue, cognitive impairment, and other neurological side effects observed with ENZA, which is an important consideration, especially in elderly or comorbid patients [52]. However, a detailed breakdown of grade ≥ 3 AEs was not available in our pooled analysis.

Overall, ABI showed more favorable (lower) rates of treatment discontinuation rates and incidence of grade ≥ 3 AEs, which may support its use in selected populations.

In conclusion, these findings suggest that ENZA may be preferable for patients in whom the primary goal is to maximize OS, whereas ABI may be favored for its lower side-effect profile, provided that cardiovascular risk is adequately managed.

Limitations and Strengths

This study has several limitations. We were unable to include RCTs; all other studies were real-world datasets, which are known to be at risk of selection bias because of their retrospective nature. To reduce the effect of potential bias, we performed subgroup analysis including only adjusted results. These “adjusted only” comparisons showed very similar results to those of unadjusted ones, suggesting a low influence of potential selection bias. However, selection bias may still have influenced our results, as ABI and ENZA have different contraindication profiles, which could potentially lead to differences in patient baseline characteristics. When treatment lines were analyzed together, heterogeneity was high; however, subgroup analyses by treatment line resulted in more homogeneous estimates with lower I2 values. Another limitation of this study is the lack of information across all included studies regarding patients who received both ARPIs sequentially during the course of their disease and the associated overall survival outcomes. Furthermore, it is possible that differences in non-cancer-related mortality, particularly MACE, may have contributed to the observed differences in OS between treatment groups. However, while we cannot exclude the possibility that MACE-related mortality may have contributed to mortality in patients in the ABI-treated group, we do not consider that this has a major effect on the comparative results, as MACE-related mortality has been reported to be only ≤ 1–2% in clinical trials. Some of the included studies may have used overlapping data sources (e.g., large administrative databases such as the Veterans Affairs database), potentially leading to some patients being included more than once. Additionally, AEs and safety outcomes derived from real-world data may be underreported or inconsistently documented compared with RCTs, which should be considered when interpreting safety outcomes. A further limitation was that the adjusted data considered different potential confounders. In addition, the heterogeneous definition of the PFS endpoint was a further limitation, which ultimately resulted in only a few studies with comparable PFS definitions, undermining the applicability of this endpoint. The strength of our analysis is that we followed the predefined, PROSPERO-registered, and rigorous protocol. Additionally, in our statistical analysis, we not only evaluated the results using forest plots, but also extracted IPD data from a large number of KM curves, which were used to create pooled KM curves. A further strength of our study is its inclusion of a large number (n = 47) of publications, with 71,984 patients, with available OS with 51,642 patients and PSA response data. To our knowledge, therefore, this is the largest meta-analysis on this topic. In addition, we assessed several efficacy and safety endpoints in both the first- and second-line setting, allowing not only the largest but also the most detailed data analysis. The high consistency of the results across therapy lines (first versus second) and endpoint (OS, PSA50) that we observed confirms their reliability.

Implications for Research and Practice

Despite its strengths, our study cannot replace RCTs. Nevertheless, it provides a solid basis for effect size calculations to support the planning and design of such trials. Furthermore, the exploration of the role of biomarkers may contribute to personalized therapeutic decisions [86]. The results of this study have some limitations, and this research further research warranted before translation for better patient outcomes [87, 88].

From a health policy perspective, our findings suggest that drugs have a justified place in national funding protocols, with ENZA demonstrating superior efficacy. However, long-term side effects and cardiovascular risks should also be considered by policymakers when designing therapeutic support.

Conclusions

On the basis of our findings, ENZA should be considered for a broader group of patients with mCRPC, as it was associated with more favorable oncological outcomes in terms of both OS and PSA50 response. However, these differences may become less pronounced or disappear in patients who receive both ENZA and ABI as part of a sequential treatment strategy. ENZA also showed a significant advantage over ABI regarding MACE. However, individual treatment selection remains essential, as ENZA is associated with a higher incidence of neurological side effects as well as grade ≥ 3 AEs, which were associated with higher rates of dose reductions and treatment discontinuations.
