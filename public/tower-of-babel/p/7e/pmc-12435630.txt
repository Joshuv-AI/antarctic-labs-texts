Effect of Wobble Board Training in the Sitting Position on Pain and Disability Index in Women with Chronic Nonspecific Low Back Pain
Authors: Fatemeh Taghijarah, Sayed Mohsen Hosseini, Navid Taheri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Low Back Pain (LBP) is one of the most prevalent musculoskeletal disorders in the world. About 10–15% of LBP patients develop chronic symptoms. Proprioceptive deficiency is one of the possible mechanisms for LBP chronicity, and females are more susceptible than males. We can use an unstable sitting balance or Wobble Board Training (WBT) for proprioceptive training of low back. Lumbar postural control is separated from lower limb postural control in this position. So, we aimed to study the effect of proprioceptive exercises in a sitting position on LBP patients, as it wasn’t specified in this position until now.Materials and Methods:In this randomized clinical trial, 40 women with Chronic Non-Specific Low Back Pain (CNSLBP) and a mean age of 40.37 ± 4.79 were randomly allocated into the control or intervention group subsequently after checking the inclusion criteria. Participants in the control group were treated with Conventional Physical Therapy (CPT), and participants in the intervention group were treated with CPT + WBT in the sitting position. Pain and disability index were evaluated in both groups before treatment, immediately, and 4 weeks after treatment.Results:The results show that the pain and disability index improved after treatment in both groups, but the intervention is not significantly effective on either the pain (P = 0.81) or the disability index (P = 0.15).Conclusion:WBT in a sitting position is not significantly effective in treating women with CNSLBP.

INTRODUCTION

Non-specific Low Back Pain (LBP) is a disorder that has no identifiable cause and no clear association with a specific, serious underlying anatomical impairment or disease process in the lower back region.[1] LBP is one of the most prevalent causes of the musculoskeletal disorder. LBP remains the leading cause of years lived with disabilities.[2] The lifetime prevalence of LBP is 38.9% globally;[3] this is lower in Iran, though more than a fourth of Iranians (25.2%) experience LBP in their lifetime.[4] Both in Iran and the world, LBP prevalence is higher in females than males and increases with age.[24] Only about 10-15% of LBP patients develop chronic symptoms;[5] conversely, the mean expenses for chronic LBP patients are about twice as much as acute LBP.[6]

One of the risk factors for the persistence of LBP is altered lumbar proprioception.[7] Lumbar proprioception deficiency increases pain intensity and lumbar disability.[8] Evidence indicated that lumbar proprioception is impaired during active joint repositioning sense in sitting positions.[7] Additionally, it is reported that chronic LBP patients had poorer postural control in more challenging balance tasks.[9] Thus, to decrease the pain and improve the lumbar function in patients with LBP, it is necessary to improve the proprioception sense,[8] especially in sitting positions and challenging tasks.[79] Proprioceptive Training (PrT) can be effective in preventing LBP recurrence. Still, there is conflicting evidence on its effectiveness on pain and functional status and low-quality evidence that PrT benefits conventional therapy or not.[1011]

To the best of our knowledge, there is no study using an unstable sitting position for PrT in managing females with LBP. However, exercises in the sitting position cause an efficient use of the trunk muscles in LBP patients.[12] We hypothesized that combined proprioceptive training and Conventional Physical Therapy (CPT) would improve pain and disability more than CPT alone. So, we aimed to investigate whether adding proprioceptive training to the CPT program enhances treatment outcomes in patients with chronic non-specific LBP.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This was a double-blind randomized clinical trial study. The nonspecific LBP patients were selected from those referred by the physician to the Ebn-Sina physical therapy clinic in Isfahan, Iran, by a simple randomized sampling method. This study was performed from October 2020 to October 2021.

Participants

In this study, literate women aged between 30 and 50 years who had Chronic Non-Specific Low Back Pain (CNSLBP) were included. CNSLBP is defined as the pain between the last costal margin to the inferior gluteal line which doesn’t have any specific cause and lasts more than 12 weeks.[1] Among those participants, the ones with at least 4 cm pain level and at worst 8 cm pain level out of 10 cm evaluated by Visual Analog Scale (VAS) in the last week, were included.

Participants with specific causes or diseases associated with LBP such as lumbar spine surgery, spinal canal stenosis, spondylolisthesis > grade Ⅰ, large disc herniation ≥ grade Ⅲ, LBP with radicular symptoms, history of vertebral fracture, scoliosis (Cobb’s angle >40˚) and Ankylosing Spondylitis were not included. Those who had contraindications for electrotherapy or physical activities, balance disorders with any cause, either vestibular dysfunction or positional vertigo, severe overweight (Body Mass Index (BMI) >30), analgesic use, and medications impact on balance and inability to perform Wobble Board Training (WBT) were not included. Also, participants who were not willing to participate in the study were excluded. A radiologist has reviewed and confirmed all of the radiological findings mentioned above.

Randomization and procedure

In this study, 40 women with CNSLBP who were eligible for the study were selected. Written informed consent was obtained from all selected women. Finally, selected participants were randomized into two groups through simple randomization in a 1:1 allocation ratio using a random number table. The randomization sequence was concealed from the physical therapist enrolling and assessing patients. It was impossible to blind the patients, as the nature of the treatment included exercise therapy, so the clinician evaluating the patient and the expert analyzing the statistics were blinded.

Demographic characteristics were compared in the studied groups at baseline. Also, the outcome measurements were evaluated at the baseline, immediately after treatment, and 4 weeks later by VAS and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) scales. The participants were informed to visit the clinic in person for follow-up by phone calls.

Intervention

Participants were divided into control (CPT, n = 20) and intervention (CPT + WBT, n = 20) groups. CPT treated both groups for 8 sessions (3 sessions per week), including an Exercise program and 6 standardized exercises for stretching, strengthening, and stability of the lower limbs and trunk muscles. In every session, the therapist educates the new exercise to the patients except for 4th sessions and last sessions; the participants continued the exercise program until 4 weeks after treatment. All implementations were performed by an experienced physical therapist. The patients are aware that the new exercise added to the previous ones and should be done for 3 sets a day for 10 times with a 10-second hold, including Back extensors (double knee to chest) and Hamstring Stretching (wall stretch), Abdominals and Gluteal Strengthening (posterior pelvic tilt and bridging exercise) and finally Core stability exercises (dead bug and bird dog).

Patients in the intervention group also did the WBT in the sitting position. In this exercise, patients sit on the Wobble board, a circular board with a 350 mm diameter set on a hemisphere with 50 mm height and 55 mm radius of curvature diameter.[13] The patient’s feet were on foot support to limit the lower limb movements, and foot support was set in a way in which knees were in 90˚ flexion. Also, we use a chair with armrests and back support for patients’ safety. Then, patients were asked to cross their hands and move the Wobble board in all directions as much as possible without losing balance. Movement speed and direction were controlled by every individual. The physical therapist monitored the patient. Patients did this exercise for 3 minutes in the first session and progressed to 9 minutes in the last session. If they feel tired during this exercise, they should rest for a third of the time they exercise. Subsequently, both groups received a pain modulator, 20 minutes of Transcutaneous Electrical Nerve Stimulation (TENS) with a frequency of 110 Hz and duration of 100 ms in the way that two electrodes were applied on the left and right Paravertebral in the lower back region, following this the hot pack applied on the low back for 10 minutes.

Outcome measures

We assessed pain severity and disability levels in patients with CNSLBP.

Visual analog scale

To evaluate pain severity, we used a VAS, a reliable and valid tool in patients with CNSLBP.[14] VAS is a 10 cm horizontal line scored from 0 (no pain) to 10 (severe intolerable pain).

Oswestry disability index

Disability level in the patients with CNSLBP was assessed by the Iranian version of the ODI. The validity and reliability of the questionnaire were confirmed previously. The Cronbach-α of the Persian ODI was 0.75, with an Interclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) of 0.86 to 0.91.[15] ODI included 10 subscales, including Pain intensity, Personal care, Lifting, Walking, Sitting, Standing, Sleeping, Sex activity, Social Life, and Traveling in and out of the city. Every subscale contains six statements scored from 0 to 5 points. The maximum score is 50, and the Minimum score is 0. The total score is doubled and expressed as a percentage. The higher the score, the greater the disability.[16]

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed by Statistical Package for the Social Science (SPSS) version 20. Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to examine the distribution of tested variables (age, BMI, pain number, and disability index). In normally distributed variables, the mean and Standard Deviation (SD) of continuous variables within groups at baseline and during treatment and follow-up were compared by Repeated measure Analysis of Variances (ANOVA) test. An independent T-test was used for between-group comparisons.

DISCUSSION

In this trial, we examined the effect of WBT in the sitting position in addition to CPT in patients with CNSLBP compared with CPT alone. Our results indicated that the effectiveness of both treatment protocols was similar, and adding WBT to CPT may not have significant superiority to the CPT for 4 weeks after treatment. Although there was a decreasing trend of the disability index in the CPT + WBT group from baseline to 4 weeks after the intervention, there wasn’t a meaningful difference between groups.

As mentioned, we did not find a similar study in the literature review in this field. Thus, we discuss some studies that resemble our study in results. In the study of Stankovic A LM et al., the intervention group did balance exercises on the Swiss ball in addition to some stability, strengthening, and stretching exercises for 5 weeks. The control group did the same exercises except for balance exercises. Although the patient’s pain and disability improved in the intervention group, there was no meaningful difference in outcomes between the groups.[17] Moon HJ et al. discovered that 8 weeks of stability or strengthening exercise decreased the LBP and disability, whereas the two groups did not significantly differ in results.[18]

McCaskey and colleagues reported that adding Sensory Motor Training (SMT) to PT is effective in pain reduction and functional improvement but not more than PT with sub-effective low-intensity endurance training. They used a Posturomed device to challenge the proprioception in the SMT group.[19] Also, Kim DH et al. stated that both balance exercises and stability exercises training in 24 sessions could improve pain and disability in chronic LBP patients. Still, these outcomes had no meaningful differences between the two groups.[20]

The current study’s findings were in line with the studies mentioned above. Also, our study has had some different strong points. For example, we specified the sitting position for training CNSLBP balance and proprioception. We have had a control group that received routine therapy. We used a special device that is available and easy to use in the clinic.

Hlaing SS et al. found that stability exercises could reduce disability more than strengthening exercises after 4 weeks, but this difference was not significant.[21] Similarly, our results show that WBT reduces disability by about 3%, 4 weeks after treatment compared to immediately after. Also, we found that CPT with WBT could significantly improve the disability index 4 weeks after treatment compared to the baseline. However, CPT alone results in a 1% disability increase 4 weeks after treatment compared with post-treatment, but these changes were not significant in the between-group analysis. This may be because 37.5% of the participants in our study were mildly disabled (20% disability in ODI), and the results may improve if the inclusion criteria for disability exist, as it is discovered that the more the baseline disability score is, the more the opportunity to improve.[22]

Disability improvement in the CPT + WBT group was slower but more continuous. It seems that the WBT may induce its effects for a longer time by increasing the duration of intervention.

However, two studies took the unstable sitting exercise on different unstable surfaces as a part of the lumbar stabilization program, and these stability exercises significantly improved pain and disability in patients with chronic LBP.[2324] The duration and frequency of exercise sessions in these studies are about twice ours. Also, in these studies, the unstable sitting exercise was used for the progression of stability exercise, but we used it as a single balance exercise. It is suggested that we need a stabilized lumbar spine for better results in proprioceptive training.

Khokhawala and Gaurav discovered that doing exercise on the Swiss ball (including sitting exercise) in 2 weeks could improve pain and disability significantly in comparison to other groups.[25] Our findings were not similar to theirs. They may have included both male and female participants, but we only studied females. Women with CNSLBP have poorer dynamic postural control than men,[26] so maybe improving their abilities is more complicated and needs more time. Also, different exercise protocols between studies may affect the outcomes. Khokhawala and Gaurav also didn’t control the significant mean age difference between groups (the intervention group participants were significantly younger).

Generally, exercising on an unstable surface can improve both function and pain because of its effect on muscle activity, spinal stability, proprioceptive stimulation, etc.[1227] However, evidence indicated that doing the WBT with closed eyes can produce more challenges and improve the outcomes.[28] In the current study, we could not perform the exercise with closed eyes because our studied population had limited abilities to maintain balance with open eyes, and the study duration was not long enough for training them.

The limitation of our study was the short duration of the study. However, it is suggested that we could obtain more conclusive results by continuing the training period. In addition, participants in the CPT + WBT group had higher BMI than the CPT group.

It is also suggested that future studies inspect both males and females, males lonely, to see if there is a difference in the outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS

Adding WBT in a sitting position to CPT cannot improve pain and disability more than CPT alone for 3 weeks. It is recommended to design future studies with a larger sample size and longer duration of training sessions.
