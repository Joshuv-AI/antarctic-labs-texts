Early Single-Leaflet Device Attachment After TEER
Authors: Charilila-Loukia Ververeli, Argyro Kalompatsou, Ioannis Kachrimanidis, Yannis Dimitroglou, Sotirios Tsalamandris, Maria Drakopoulou, Constantina Aggeli, Konstantinos Tsioufis, Konstantinos Toutouzas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSingle-leaflet device attachment (SLDA) is an uncommon but clinically significant complication of transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER), associated with recurrent mitral regurgitation (MR).Case SummaryA 73-year-old man with heart failure and severe MR presented with progressive dyspnea despite optimal medical therapy. Early Single-Leaflet Device Attachment After TEER

Graphical overview of the case illustrating baseline complex mitral valve anatomy, anatomy-driven initial TEER strategy, development of early single-leaflet device attachment, imaging-guided bailout implantation of a second device, and final restoration of leaflet coaptation with durable reduction of mitral regurgitation. TEER = transcatheter edge-to-edge repair.

History of Presentation

A 73-year-old man presented with progressive dyspnea on minimal exertion (NYHA functional class III) and recurrent hospitalizations for decompensated heart failure despite optimal medical therapy.

Cardiac auscultation revealed a holosystorlic murmur at the apex radiating to the axilla.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction due to dilated cardiomyopathy, arterial hypertension, and permanent atrial fibrillation. He had undergone prior implantation of a cardiac resynchronization therapy defibrillator.

He was receiving comprehensive guideline-directed medical therapy at maximally tolerated doses, including sacubitril/valsartan, β-blocker (bisoprolol), mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (eplerenone), sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor (dapagliflozin), and loop diuretic (furosemide). Anticoagulation was maintained with acenocoumarol, and he was also receiving atorvastatin and allopurinol.

He was an active smoker.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for clinical deterioration and progressive dyspnea in this patient included acute decompensated heart failure, progression of underlying left ventricular dysfunction, significant valvular heart disease, tachycardia-mediated cardiomyopathy, and myocardial ischemia.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a dilated left ventricle with reduced systolic function (ejection fraction 30%-35%) and global hypokinesia. Right ventricular size and function were preserved.

Transesophageal echocardiography demonstrated complex mitral valve anatomy with a mixed degenerative-functional mechanism of mitral regurgitation (MR). Degenerative features included a cleft-like morphology involving the A2-A3 segments and multisegment leaflet involvement. In addition, significant mitral annular dilatation (47 × 33 mm) was present in the setting of permanent atrial fibrillation and left-sided chamber remodeling.

Severe MR originated predominantly from the A2-P2 region, with additional regurgitant components at A1-P1 and along the A2-A3 interface (Figure 1, Videos 1 and 2). Three-dimensional imaging provided detailed spatial characterization of leaflet anatomy and guided procedural planning.

Baseline Mitral Valve Anatomy

(A) Three-dimensional transesophageal echocardiographic en face view demonstrating complex degenerative mitral valve anatomy with leaflet thickening and cleft-like morphology involving the A2-A3 region. (B) Color Doppler imaging demonstrating severe mitral regurgitation (MR) originating predominantly from the A2-P2 region, with additional regurgitant flow along the A1-P1 and A2-A3 segments.

Pulmonary venous flow demonstrated systolic blunting, consistent with significant MR.

Right heart catheterization confirmed low cardiac output, elevated left-sided filling pressures, and mixed pulmonary hypertension with increased pulmonary vascular resistance (5 Wood units) and a prominent left atrial V-wave.

Coronary angiography excluded obstructive coronary artery disease.

Given the patient's elevated surgical risk (The European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation (EuroSCORE II) 5.13%, Society of Thoracic Surgeons score 12.4%), a transcatheter approach was considered appropriate.

Management

Given the patient's persistent symptoms despite optimal medical therapy and elevated surgical risk, the case was discussed within a multidisciplinary heart team, and a transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) approach was selected with the patient's informed consent.

Index Procedure

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia with continuous transesophageal echocardiographic and fluoroscopic guidance via the right femoral vein.

Transseptal puncture was performed at a mid-posterior position in the fossa ovalis using a BRK needle (Boston Scientific), achieving a height of approximately 4.5 cm above the mitral valve coaptation line. A 22-F steerable guide sheath was advanced into the left atrium, and the PASCAL delivery system was introduced.

Device positioning was guided by real-time 3-dimensional transesophageal echocardiography to achieve a perpendicular trajectory relative to the mitral valve plane. An 11-5 o'clock orientation was selected to avoid the deep A2-A3 cleft-like segment. Thus, device positioning was anatomy driven rather than solely jet driven, aiming to optimize leaflet capture and device stability.

A PASCAL P10 device (Edwards Lifesciences) was positioned at the A2-P2 scallops. Sequential leaflet grasping was performed, with apparent adequate leaflet insertion (approximately 11 mm for the anterior leaflet and 8 mm for the posterior leaflet), despite a relatively short posterior leaflet (Figure 2, Videos 3 and 4).

Intraprocedural Assessment After Initial PASCAL Implantation

(A) Two-dimensional transesophageal echocardiography demonstrating the mitral valve after implantation of a PASCAL P10 device (Edwards Lifesciences), with adequate anterior and posterior leaflet insertion. (B) Continuous-wave Doppler demonstrating a low transmitral mean gradient (approximately 1 mm Hg), without evidence of significant mitral stenosis. The arrows indicate the sites of anterior and posterior leaflet insertion. (D) Color Doppler imaging demonstrating mild residual mitral regurgitation (MR) after device deployment.

Final assessment demonstrated mild residual MR without evidence of mitral stenosis (mean transmitral gradient approximately 1 mm Hg), along with significant hemodynamic improvement, including reduction of left atrial pressure and elimination of the V-wave. The favorable echocardiographic and hemodynamic result did not warrant implantation of an additional device before final device release.

SLDA Detection

Early follow-up transesophageal echocardiography demonstrated loss of posterior leaflet attachment, consistent with single-leaflet device attachment (SLDA), accompanied by recurrence of severe MR (Figure 3, Videos 5 and 6).

Single-Leaflet Device Attachment

(A) Two-dimensional transesophageal echocardiography demonstrating loss of posterior leaflet attachment after implantation of the initial PASCAL P10 device, consistent with single-leaflet device attachment (SLDA). The arrow indicates the site of posterior leaflet detachment, while orthogonal imaging confirms persistent attachment to the anterior leaflet. (B) Color Doppler imaging demonstrating recurrence of severe mitral regurgitation after SLDA.

Bailout Procedure

Given clinical deterioration, repeat TEER was performed under real-time 2- and 3-dimensional transesophageal echocardiographic guidance.

A lateral and perpendicular trajectory was achieved targeting the A1-P1 scallops. A second PASCAL Ace device (Edwards Lifesciences) was implanted adjacent to the initial device. This approach was feasible due to adequate residual leaflet length and favorable anatomical conditions for secondary device implantation.

Independent leaflet grasping restored leaflet coaptation and stabilized the initial device. Final imaging demonstrated significant reduction of MR and restoration of pulmonary venous systolic flow (Figure 4; Videos 7 and 8).

(B) Final 3-dimensional view demonstrating a double-orifice mitral valve configuration with restoration of leaflet coaptation and stabilization of the initial device. Outcome and Follow-Up

Postprocedural echocardiography demonstrated mild residual MR without evidence of mitral stenosis. Pulmonary venous flow showed restoration of the systolic component (Figure 5, Video 9).

(A) Three-dimensional transesophageal echocardiographic en face view demonstrating the final double-device mitral valve configuration after implantation of the second PASCAL Ace device. (B) Color Doppler imaging demonstrating mild residual mitral regurgitation (MR) after bailout TEER. (C) Continuous-wave Doppler demonstrating a low transmitral mean gradient (approximately 1 mm Hg), without evidence of significant mitral stenosis. (D) Pulmonary venous flow demonstrating restoration of the systolic (S) wave, consistent with effective reduction of mitral regurgitation. TEER = transcatheter edge-to-edge repair.

The patient experienced significant clinical improvement, with resolution of dyspnea and no further hospitalizations during follow-up. No complications were observed.

Discussion

This case highlights a clinically relevant complication of TEER and provides mechanistic insight into procedural planning in complex mitral valve anatomy.

TEER is an established treatment for severe MR in patients at high surgical risk.1, 2, 3 SLDA is a recognized complication, reported in approximately 1% to 5% of cases and associated with recurrent regurgitation and hemodynamic deterioration.2,3

Although the index procedure achieved optimal immediate results, early SLDA occurred, underscoring that acute procedural success does not necessarily predict durability in anatomically challenging valves.

A key consideration in this case was the use of an 11- to 5-o'clock orientation, selected to avoid the cleft-like segment and facilitate leaflet capture. In this anatomical setting, the observed discrepancy between anterior (11-mm) and posterior (8-mm) leaflet insertion suggests asymmetric leaflet engagement, which may have resulted in uneven mechanical load distribution and predisposed to delayed leaflet detachment.

Importantly, no clear intraprocedural finding mandated device repositioning or implantation of an additional device before release. MR was reduced from severe to mild, the transmitral gradient remained low, and significant hemodynamic improvement was observed. Given the marked reduction in MR severity and the potential risk of leaflet distortion associated with additional device implantation in the setting of the initial orientation strategy, a second device was not considered necessary during the index procedure.

In retrospect, the combination of complex valve anatomy, relatively short posterior leaflet length, asymmetric leaflet insertion, and a mixed degenerative-functional substrate may have increased susceptibility to delayed SLDA.

These findings highlight the importance of individualized procedural planning in anatomically complex valves, where strategies designed to avoid anatomical irregularities may influence leaflet insertion and long-term device stability.

Real-world experience with TEER systems, including the PASCAL platform, has demonstrated the importance of anatomical suitability and procedural technique in achieving durable results.4,5

Current guidelines support TEER in symptomatic patients with severe MR who are at high surgical risk;1,6 however, specific recommendations for the prevention and management of SLDA remain limited.

After SLDA, repeat TEER using a perpendicular trajectory aligned with the line of coaptation under detailed 3-dimensional echocardiographic guidance enabled effective leaflet bridging, stabilization of the initially implanted device, and durable MR reduction. This case highlights the feasibility and effectiveness of imaging-guided bailout TEER as a rescue strategy in carefully selected patients.

Limitations

This report is limited by its single-case design and the inability to establish a causal relationship between device orientation and SLDA.

Conclusions

Early SLDA after TEER may occur despite optimal initial results, particularly in complex degenerative mitral valve anatomy. Conventional device orientation strategies, including 11-5 clocking, may not ensure adequate and symmetric leaflet insertion in such anatomies. An individualized, anatomy-driven approach is essential to optimize procedural durability. Imaging-guided bailout TEER represents an effective treatment strategy in appropriately selected patients.
