Evaluating the causal role of LEPR signaling in 16 cancers: A drug-target Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Jihao Chen, Xuefei Yang, Ting Huang, Heran Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The leptin receptor (LEPR) serves as a central regulator of energy balance and metabolic homeostasis, with growing interest in its pleiotropic roles in cancer. However, existing evidence on its oncogenic function remains ambiguous, with studies reporting both tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressive effects across different contexts. This duality complicates the interpretation of LEPR’s precise role in carcinogenesis. Moreover, conventional observational studies are inherently limited by residual confounding and reverse causality, leaving the causal relationship between LEPR signaling and site-specific cancer risk unresolved. To address this, we performed a drug-target Mendelian randomization analysis to assess the causal effect of genetically proxied LEPR activity on 16 such cancers. We selected cis-expression quantitative trait loci (cis-eQTLs) associated with LEPR expression in blood from the eQTLGen Consortium (n = 31,684) as instrumental variables, and harmonized them with summary-level genome-wide association study data for cancers from the FinnGen R12 release. Among the 16 evaluated malignancies, our primary finding demonstrated that genetically elevated LEPR signaling was significantly associated with a lower risk of pancreatic cancer (odds ratio = 0.91; 95% CI: 0.87–0.96; P = 3.93 × 10−4). This association withstood Bonferroni correction and was consistent across sensitivity analyses. Suggestive inverse associations were also observed for lung squamous cell carcinoma, gastric cancer, and breast cancer subtypes (P <.05), whereas no significant causal links were detected for the remaining ten cancers. No significant heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy was detected. In summary, this drug-target Mendelian randomization study provides concise genetic evidence supporting a protective role of LEPR signaling specifically in pancreatic cancer, with suggestive effects in several other malignancies. These findings inform the pathophysiological understanding of leptin in cancer and highlight the relevance of LEPR-modulating strategies for safety assessment in oncology. Limitations include the use of blood-derived eQTLs and restriction to European ancestry, which may affect generalizability.

1. Introduction

The leptin receptor (LEPR) mediates diverse biological processes – including immune regulation, angiogenesis, and cellular proliferation – through its downstream JAK/STAT signaling cascade.[1] These pleiotropic functions have positioned the leptin-LEPR axis as a compelling candidate pathway in carcinogenesis and a potential target for cancer prevention and treatment,[2] particularly given the established link between obesity and multiple cancer types.[3–5] However, epidemiological evidence regarding the association between LEPR signaling and site-specific cancer risk remains inconsistent.[6] For several common malignancies – including breast, colorectal, pancreatic, and lung cancers – prospective and case-control studies have reported positive, null, and inverse associations, with further variation across histological subtypes.[7] These discrepancies may stem from residual confounding by adiposity and related metabolic traits, selection bias, or reverse causality due to undiagnosed preclinical disease. Moreover, circulating leptin levels reflect both overall adiposity and signaling efficiency at the receptor level, complicating causal interpretation when leptin exposure is assessed biochemically rather than via target engagement.[8] Thus, it remains unclear whether therapeutic modulation of LEPR signaling influences cancer risk across organ sites – a question with direct relevance to the safety evaluation of leptin-based agents and anti-obesity pharmacotherapies.[9]

Genetic epidemiology offers a promising framework for strengthening causal inference in this context. Mendelian randomization (MR) uses germline genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to emulate life-long differences in exposure, reducing susceptibility to confounding and reverse causation.[10] Drug-target MR extends this approach by leveraging cis-acting variants within or near the gene encoding a drug target – such as LEPR – to approximate pharmacological perturbation, offering enhanced mechanistic specificity over conventional MR based on circulating biomarkers.[11] This specificity is particularly valuable for resolving the ambiguous role of LEPR in cancer, as it isolates receptor-level effects from the confounding influences of circulating leptin levels and adiposity.

Despite these advances, no systematic evaluation of LEPR signaling across multiple cancer types using this drug-target MR strategy has been conducted. Previous MR studies have largely focused on general adiposity measures or circulating leptin in relation to a limited set of cancer outcomes, leaving the tissue-specific role of receptor-level LEPR signaling incompletely resolved. Furthermore, no prior analysis has integrated large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) resources within a unified framework[12] to address a question central to translational drug development: the direction and magnitude of the on-target effect of LEPR signaling across a broad range of cancers.

To address this gap, we conducted a pan-cancer genetic evaluation of LEPR signaling using a drug-target MR framework. The 16 cancers were selected based on: availability in the FinnGen R12 release, prior epidemiological evidence linking them to obesity or leptin biology, and representation of diverse anatomical sites and histological subtypes. We hypothesized that genetically proxied LEPR signaling would exert causal effects on cancer risk, with direction and magnitude varying across tissues. By employing genetic variants that instrument LEPR itself, this study is designed to yield evidence directly interpretable for therapeutic development – specifically, to inform the on-target safety profile of LEPR-modulating strategies.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

We applied a drug-target MR framework to assess the potential causal relationship between genetically proxied LEPR signaling and the risk of 16 site-specific cancers. Genetic instruments for LEPR expression were constructed using cis-expression quantitative trait loci (cis-eQTLs) identified in whole blood from the eQTLGen Consortium (data generated between 2011 and 2018; published in 2019). Summary-level genetic association data for cancer outcomes were obtained from the FinnGen R12 release (published in 2024). To approximate the effect of long-term modulation of LEPR activity, we selected genetic variants associated with LEPR expression levels based on a cis-eQTL meta-analysis including 31,684 individuals. The primary causal estimate was derived using the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using MR-Egger regression, weighted median, weighted mode, and simple mode approaches. We further assessed the robustness of the findings by evaluating heterogeneity (Cochran Q statistic) and horizontal pleiotropy (MR-Egger intercept test and MR-PRESSO). The overall analytical pipeline of this study is systematically illustrated in Figure 1. Briefly, the workflow encompassed the selection of independent cis-eQTLs for LEPR expression as genetic instruments, data harmonization between the eQTLGen and FinnGen R12 datasets, and the implementation of the primary IVW analysis alongside 4 complementary MR estimators. The design also incorporated rigorous sensitivity analyses, including pleiotropy and heterogeneity assessments, to ensure the robustness of the causal inferences across 16 site-specific cancers.

2.2. Genetic instruments for LEPR expression (drug-target MR)

Valid MR analysis requires genetic instruments to satisfy 3 core assumptions: strong association with the exposure (LEPR expression); independence from confounders; and influence on the outcome only through the exposure, without horizontal pleiotropy.

We retrieved cis-eQTL summary data for LEPR from the eQTLGen Consortium, as provided in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R609. To construct genetic proxies for LEPR signaling, we selected all common SNPs (minor allele frequency >1%) within a ± 100 kb window of the LEPR gene that reached genome-wide significance (P < 5.0 × 10−8) for association with LEPR expression in blood. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping was performed using a European reference panel from the 1000 Genomes Project (r2 <0.3, window size = 10 Mb) to ensure variant independence. Palindromic SNPs with ambiguous strand orientation were excluded. We applied Steiger filtering to confirm that the instruments explained more variance in exposure than in the outcome. Instrument strength was assessed using F-statistics, with all retained variants exceeding the recommended threshold of 10, indicating a low risk of weak instrument bias. Finally, we used LDlink to screen for SNPs in LD with potential confounders to minimize pleiotropic bias. If a target SNP was unavailable in the outcome GWAS summary statistics, we utilized proxy variants identified through the European reference panel of the 1000 Genomes Project, provided they exhibited high LD (r2 >0.8) with the original instrument. SNPs that were neither present in the outcome dataset nor had available high-LD proxies were excluded from the analysis to maintain the integrity of the genetic proxies.

2.3. Outcome sources

To reduce population stratification bias, all analyses were restricted to individuals of European ancestry. Summary-level genetic association statistics for 16 site-specific cancers were obtained from the FinnGen consortium (R12 release). The FinnGen R12 cohort provides a high degree of genetic homogeneity, reducing the confounding effects of diverse ethnic backgrounds. Cancer types and case-control sample sizes were as follows: lung squamous cell carcinoma (1898/378,749), lung adenocarcinoma (2219/378,749), small-cell lung cancer (909/378,749), esophageal cancer (1227/378,749), gastric cancer (2296/378,749), pancreatic cancer (3139/378,749), colon cancer (7698/378,749), rectal cancer (4844/378,749), estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer (14,540/221,705), estrogen receptor-negative breast cancer (9724/221,705), ovarian cancer (303/222,078), cervical squamous cell carcinoma (222/222,078), cervical adenocarcinoma (142/222,078), thyroid cancer (2859/378,749), bladder cancer (4852/378,749), and prostate cancer (20,368/156,671). Detailed phenotype definitions, including diagnostic codes and cohort descriptions, are provided in Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R609.

2.4. MR analysis

Causal estimates for the effect of genetically proxied LEPR signaling on cancer risk were primarily determined using the IVW method. The reliability of our causal inference is supported by the substantial sample sizes of the eQTLGen and FinnGen R12 consortia, which provide the requisite statistical power to detect potential associations. All IVs demonstrated F-statistics >10, indicating a robust instrument strength and a low risk of weak instrument bias.

To ensure the consistency of results and account for potential horizontal pleiotropy, 4 complementary MR approaches were employed: MR-Egger regression, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode. The weighted median estimator provides robust estimates even when up to 50% of the weight comes from invalid instruments, while mode-based methods identify the causal effect from the largest cluster of similar estimates. Heterogeneity across IVs was quantified using Cochran Q statistic. A fixed-effect IVW model was adopted if P ≥.05 (indicating no significant heterogeneity); otherwise, a random-effects model was applied to provide more conservative estimates. Statistical significance for the primary analysis was defined at a nominal P-value <.05.

2.5. Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the robustness of causal estimates and potential pleiotropy, we conducted multiple sensitivity tests. Horizontal pleiotropy was assessed via the MR-Egger intercept test and the MR-PRESSO framework, which also identifies and corrects for outlier variants. Heterogeneity across instrumental variants was examined using Cochran Q statistic. Leave-one-out analysis was performed to determine whether results were driven by individual SNPs. Results were further visualized using scatter, funnel, and forest plots. To address potential confounding, all instruments and their LD proxies (r2 >0.8) were screened for associations with known cancer risk factors (such as obesity, smoking history, and type 2 diabetes) using LDlink.

To account for multiple testing across 16 site-specific cancers, a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold of P <3.13 × 10−3 (0.05/16) was implemented to strictly control the Type I error rate. The rationale for including both broad cancer categories and their histological subtypes (such as lung adenocarcinoma vs squamous cell carcinoma) was to capture potential tissue-specific and subtype-specific effects of LEPR signaling, which may be masked in aggregated analyses due to biological heterogeneity.

2.6. Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study used publicly available GWAS summary statistics. All original studies contributing data were approved by their respective institutional review boards, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. No additional ethical approval was required for the present analysis.

3. Results

3.1. Genetic instruments selection

A total of 95 independent cis-eQTLs associated with LEPR expression in whole blood were identified from the eQTLGen Consortium and used as genetic instruments for LEPR signaling. All variants satisfied standard quality control criteria, with F-statistics exceeding 10, indicating strong instrument strength and minimal risk of weak instrument bias. Detailed annotation of the genetic instruments is provided in Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R609.

3.2. MR analysis on leptin drug with 16 cancers

We evaluated the causal relationship between genetically proxied LEPR signaling and the risk of 16 site-specific cancers using multiple MR methods. In the primary IVW analysis, elevated LEPR signaling was significantly associated with a reduced risk of pancreatic cancer (OR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.87–0.96; P = 3.93 × 10−4). This estimate translates to a 9% relative reduction in pancreatic cancer risk per standard deviation increase in LEPR expression, representing a moderate but clinically relevant protective effect. This association remained statistically significant after Bonferroni correction for multiple testing (P <3.13 × 10−3). The direction and magnitude of the causal estimates were consistent across supplementary MR analyses, as shown in Figure 2.

MR analysis reveals the causal effects of genetically proxied LEPR signaling on site-specific cancer risk. The IVW method identified a significant association with pancreatic cancer risk. CI = confidence interval, IVW = inverse variance weighted, LEPR = leptin receptor, OR = odds ratio.

Suggestive evidence of inverse associations was observed for several other malignancies, including lung squamous cell carcinoma (OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.86–0.98; P = 7.48 × 10−3), gastric cancer (OR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.87–0.99; P = 2.83 × 10−2), estrogen receptor-negative breast cancer (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.93–0.99; P = 4.50 × 10−3), estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer (OR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.95–1.00; P = 3.19 × 10−2), and ovarian cancer (OR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.88–0.99; P = 2.08 × 10−2). Detailed results are provided in Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R609.

No statistically significant associations were detected for the remaining ten cancers, including lung adenocarcinoma (OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.95–1.07; P = .71), small-cell lung cancer (OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 0.94–1.13; P = .53), esophageal cancer (OR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.91–1.08; P = .76), colon cancer (OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.95–1.01; P = .19), rectal cancer (OR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.95–1.03; P = .61), cervical squamous cell carcinoma (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.79–1.16; P = .66), cervical adenocarcinoma (OR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.66–1.10; P = .21), thyroid cancer (OR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.06–1.18; P = .51), bladder cancer (OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 0.99–1.08; P = .13), and prostate cancer (OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.99–1.04; P = .22). Complete variant-level results are provided in Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R609.

3.3. Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the primary causal inference, particularly for pancreatic cancer. Cochran Q test indicated no significant heterogeneity across instrumental variants (P = .72). The MR-Egger intercept test (P = .97) and MR-PRESSO global test (P = .35) provided no evidence of directional horizontal pleiotropy or outlier variants. Leave-one-out analysis confirmed that the causal estimate was not driven by any single influential SNP (Fig. 3). Visual inspection of scatter plots revealed consistent effect directions across MR methods (Fig. 4), suggesting that the protective trend is not dependent on a specific statistical model. Funnel plots showed symmetry indicative of balanced pleiotropy (Fig. 5), meaning no single group of variants disproportionately skewed the results. Furthermore, forest plots demonstrated stable effect sizes across individual genetic variants (Fig. 6), confirming that the overall estimate is a cumulative effect rather than an artifact of a few isolated SNPs. Collectively, these analyses reinforce the validity and reliability of the inferred protective effect of LEPR signaling on pancreatic cancer risk.

4. Discussion

In this comprehensive drug-target MR study, we leveraged cis-eQTLs in the LEPR gene to approximate the effect of modulating LEPR signaling at the receptor level. Rather than a universal pan-cancer effect, our primary finding indicates that genetically elevated LEPR signaling is associated with a reduced risk of pancreatic cancer, an effect that remained statistically significant after correction for multiple testing and was consistent across a range of sensitivity analyses (Figs. 2–6).[13] While we also observed suggestive protective effects for lung squamous cell carcinoma, gastric cancer, and both ER-negative and ER-positive breast cancers (Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R609), the lack of clear associations across most other sites underscores a nuanced, tissue-specific role of LEPR signaling in carcinogenesis. These results synthesize current genetic evidence to demonstrate how genetic approaches may help inform the on-target safety profile of leptin-based therapeutics.[14]

A key strength of our drug-target MR design lies in its focus on receptor-level signaling activity, which helps disentangle the effects of LEPR activation from those of circulating leptin levels. Elevated systemic leptin concentrations, often observed in obesity, typically reflect a state of leptin resistance characterized by attenuated downstream signaling.[15] Leptin resistance refers to impaired downstream signaling despite elevated circulating leptin, often observed in obesity. In this context, our genetic model – which approximates enhanced LEPR signaling – likely reflects restored activation of key pathways such as JAK2/STAT3. This, in turn, may improve systemic insulin sensitivity. This may explain why some observational studies have reported positive associations between plasma leptin and pancreatic cancer risk, whereas our genetic analysis – which models enhanced receptor signaling – suggests a protective relationship (Fig. 2).[16,17] One plausible interpretation, though speculative and requiring direct clinical validation, is that restoring physiological LEPR signaling, rather than increasing ligand concentration alone, could ameliorate metabolic conditions such as insulin resistance and chronic inflammation. This, in turn, might reduce pancreatic carcinogenesis.[18,19] This hypothesis warrants further experimental investigation using robust in vivo models.

The tissue-specific patterns observed are biologically plausible given the pleiotropic nature of LEPR signaling. However, it is crucial to note that our genetic instruments were derived from whole blood eQTLs. While these serve as robust systemic proxies, the exact signaling dynamics may vary substantially within specific tumor microenvironments. Assuming systemic proxies reflect local activity, LEPR activation engages multiple intracellular pathways, including JAK2-STAT3, PI3K-AKT, and MAPK, which influence processes such as immune surveillance, angiogenesis, and stromal interactions.[20] In the pancreas, enhanced LEPR signaling may improve insulin sensitivity and mitigate glucolipotoxicity, thereby lowering diabetes-related risk factors for pancreatic cancer.[21] The association restricted to lung squamous cell carcinoma – but not adenocarcinoma – may reflect differences in the cellular origins and microenvironments of these subtypes. These subtypes are differentially influenced by smoking and inflammatory contexts.[22] Similarly, the suggestive inverse associations observed in both ER-positive and ER-negative breast cancers imply that leptin’s role may extend beyond estrogen-mediated pathways, possibly involving immune or metabolic crosstalk in the breast tissue microenvironment.[23]

From a clinical perspective, our findings provide a foundational genetic hypothesis that enhancing LEPR signaling is unlikely to increase pancreatic cancer risk and may even be protective.[24] However, direct extrapolation to pharmacologic settings is premature. Genetic instruments model modest, life-long perturbations in signaling, which fundamentally differ from and may not fully capture the effects of acute or high-dose therapeutic interventions. Moreover, the suggestive associations in other cancers require replication in independent datasets and functional validation before any clinical implications can be drawn. Methodologically, this study illustrates how drug-target MR can serve as an “in silico Phase I” tool, helping prioritize tissue-specific safety questions for further toxicological evaluation and pharmacovigilance planning.

Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the predominantly European ancestry of the GWAS data limits the generalizability of our findings to other populations, particularly in light of known ethnic differences in leptin biology and genetic architecture.[25] Second, as addressed above, although we used blood-derived eQTLs as proxies for LEPR expression, signaling dynamics may vary across tissues. Future studies incorporating tissue-specific functional genomic data – particularly from pancreas, lung, gastric, and mammary tissues – are needed to improve anatomical resolution via colocalization analyses. Third, MR estimates reflect linear, life-long effects and cannot readily capture nonlinear or compensatory responses that may arise with pharmacological modulation.[26] Fourth, only the pancreatic cancer association met the strict multiple testing threshold; other suggestive signals should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and may be susceptible to false discovery. Finally, heterogeneity in cancer phenotype definitions and subtype classifications across source GWAS may have attenuated some site-specific estimates. To advance these findings, future validation strategies must move beyond basic functional studies to include targeted transcriptomic profiling in diverse cancer cohorts and, ultimately, carefully designed observational or phase II clinical trials to assess the dose-dependent effects of LEPR modulators on cancer incidence.

5. Conclusion

In summary, by systematically evaluating 16 site-specific malignancies, this study provides preliminary genetic evidence that elevated LEPR signaling is specifically associated with a reduced risk of pancreatic cancer, with suggestive protective effects in several other malignancies (including lung squamous cell carcinoma, gastric cancer, and breast cancer subtypes). Importantly, these MR findings are inherently hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory, reflecting the effects of life-long genetic proxies rather than definitive proof of pharmacological causality. The striking contrast between these protective associations and the null results observed for the remaining ten cancers supports the tissue-specific nature of LEPR signaling in cancer etiology, suggesting that systemic metabolic regulators exert highly localized effects depending on the distinct tumor microenvironment, and underscores the potential of drug-target MR for preclinical safety profiling. However, these results should not directly influence clinical practice without further validation through targeted functional studies, prospective cohorts, and interventional trials. Specifically, future research must prioritize colocalization analyses using tissue-specific multi-omics, mechanistic in vivo models exploring localized LEPR pathway activation, and clinical pharmacovigilance studies to evaluate the real-world, dose-dependent safety of leptin-based therapeutics.
