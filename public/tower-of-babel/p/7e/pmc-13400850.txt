Chronological versus physiological age for 10-year survival estimation: a real-world healthsystem-wide cohort analysis
Authors: Amy C. Justice, Janet P. Tate, Kathleen A. McGinnis, Jessica L. Torgersen, R. Scott Braithwaite, Vincent C. Marconi, Matthew B. Goetz, Maria C. Rodriguez-Barradas, Sheldon T. Brown, Lesley Park, Kris Ann K. Oursler, Julie A. Womack, William C. Becker

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundBenefits from clinical guidelines often only exceed harms after 10 years (payoff time). Based on population norms, guidelines are often discontinued at 75 years of age, but survival likely differs for those with complex chronic disease. Research in context

Evidence before this study

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of clinical prognostic factors for older people by Veronese et al., 2025 (PMID 38777131), encompassing Medline, Embase, Cinhal, and the Cochrane Library (up to February 1, 2023) screened 15,614 articles and found only 22 independent studies conducted in the outpatient setting. Only one of these studies predicted survival for 10 or more years and the index used (the Multidimensional Prognostic Index or MPI) required data not routinely available in the electronic health record (EHR). Further, while many others have demonstrated that prognostic indices are more accurate than age and sex alone, few have demonstrated the implications of this finding in a national healthcare system.

Added value of this study

Using data from a national healthcare system, this study quantifies the degree to which 10-year survival estimates based on population data stratified by age and sex differ from a more discriminating, individualised, and automated, index based on routine clinical measures of physiological injury. This study also quantifies the degree to which using age alone for medical guideline discontinuation can result in under and over treatment of a general clinical population and among those with diabetes or HIV.

Implications of all the available evidence

Validated, automated clinical prognostic indices are feasible and should be made routinely available in EHRs to better inform medical decision making. The Veterans Ageing Cohort Study-Charlson Comorbidity Index (VACS-CCI) is one such index.

Introduction

Benefits from clinical guidelines take time to accrue, while costs and complications typically occur immediately. The payoff time is the minimum time required for a guideline's cumulative benefits to exceed its harms.1 Accordingly, many screening and treatment guidelines are now discontinued at 75 years of age.2,3 However, age alone provides only a rough indication of an individual's remaining survival and United States (US) population estimates may be particularly inaccurate for individuals living with complex chronic diseases. As a result, exclusive use of age can reduce healthcare value in two ways: 1) by denying beneficial interventions to older healthy individuals likely to realise the payoff time and 2) by screening and treating younger less healthy individuals that, despite their age, are unlikely to reach the payoff time.

Type II diabetes (diabetes) and HIV infection are prototypical examples of complex chronic disease characterised by widespread physiological injury4 and increased risk for multimorbidity.5 Diabetes typically occurs at older ages, and HIV infection often begins at younger ages, but in both conditions, associated multimorbidity simultaneously triggers myriad guideline-directed interventions and decreases expected survival. Accordingly, the American Geriatrics Society (AGS) has published principles on the care of those with multimorbidity,6 emphasising the importance of 10-year survival as a benchmark. If the individual is likely to survive 10 years, guideline adherence is likely appropriate. If 10-year survival is unlikely, the focus should be on individual priorities, not on guideline-directed care.

AGS principles encourage providers to use clinical judgement and population life tables to estimate life expectancy. However, in the era of the electronic health record (EHR), more physiologically based standardised and automated means for estimating 10-year survival are possible at the point of care. We recently developed and validated an EHR-automated, all-cause survival index, the Veterans Ageing Cohort Study-Charlson Comorbidity Index (VACS-CCI), in a sample of 6.6 million US Veterans receiving care within the national Veterans Health Administration (VHA), the largest integrated health care system in the United States (US).7

Based on conventional statistical methods, VACS-CCI combines chronological age, standardised and routine clinical and laboratory measures, diagnostic code groupings derived from the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) and a pre-specified approach for missing data.8 It is clinically sensible, reasonably transparent, and likely more generalisable than systems developed by artificial intelligence.9 VACS-CCI has been implemented as an auto-populated decision support tool within eight US health systems employing EPIC based electronic health records (EHRs). It is also available online.10 Compared to age alone, VACS-CCI improved long-term survival estimation and was well calibrated in VHA data overall and in important subgroups.7,10 Herein, we considered the implications of using chronological age versus a previously validated measure of physiological age (VACS-CCI) for survival estimation to inform guideline application within VHA.7,10

We compared age- and sex-based US population life table estimates to VACS-CCI for 10-year survival overall and for individuals with diabetes and HIV to address the following questions: 1) How do age-based US population estimates compare to individualised estimates based on clinical data for those in VHA care? 2) How much does VACS-CCI improve our ability to differentiate those likely and unlikely to survive 10 years compared to age 75 years within VHA? 3) Do answers differ for those with diabetes or HIV compared to the overall sample and what might explain observed differences?

Methods

We employed a previously characterised sample of people in VHA care7 from the Veterans Ageing Cohort Study (VACS)-National cohort. In brief, this sample randomly selected a clinic visit date between 2007 and 2017, at least 18 months after the first outpatient diagnosis in the EHR. For individuals with HIV, the visit was at least 1 year after the date of the first antiretroviral treatment prescription. Individuals, ages 30–90 years, were followed from the index date until death, 10 years, or until 12/31/2021. Female and male sex was categorised based on the Electronic Health Record.

VACS-CCI included age, body mass index (BMI), laboratory measures (listed below), hepatitis C status, and the comorbid conditions included in CCI (listed below). VACS index laboratory values for haemoglobin, platelets, white blood count, albumin, alanine and aspartate transaminases (ALT, AST), and creatinine were restricted to VHA outpatient settings, using those closest to the index visit date within 18 months before to 14 days after. Composite markers of liver (fibrosis-4 index [FIB-4]) and renal injury (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR]) were computed. Because full liver panels are not always obtained, we set values to normal when albumin (4 g/dL) or one of ALT (25 IU/L) or AST (20 IU/L) were missing resulting in complete data for 81% of the otherwise eligible sample. Comorbid conditions included in the CCI (liver disease, diabetes, pulmonary disease, kidney disease, cancer, congestive heart failure, cerebral vascular disease, peripheral vascular disease, myocardial infarction, dementia, hemi/paraplegia, peptic ulcer disease, and rheumatic disease) were derived from ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnosis codes in the 2 years prior to index date. Conditions were considered present if one or more inpatient or two or more outpatient codes were recorded and otherwise absent.

VACS-CCI was calculated for each person at their index date. Scores ranged from 0 to 100; prior work established that a score of 42 was associated with 50% probability of 10-year survival. Having previously validated calibration of observed mortality with predicted mortality in several subgroups, including people with HIV,7 we validated calibration among those with diabetes in this analysis (Appendix 1).

Statistics

Age based population estimates and VACS-CCI estimates

We calculated US population age-based survival estimates from mortality rates provided in the Human Mortality Database11 selecting the table for 2010–2019 to best match our sample. Sex specific 1-year mortality, by age (in 1-year intervals) was used to calculate 10-year survival (S). For each age, we translated mortality (Mx) to survival (Sx = 1−Mx) and then multiplied Sx for the target age and the next 9 ages to yield 10-year survival. For example, for age 50, S= S50 x S51 x S52 x S53 x S54 x S55 x S56 x S57 x S58 x S59.

Survival probability estimates for US Veterans in care were based on previously published work.7 Stratified by sex and for each chronological age, we calculated the median and the 10th and 90th percentiles of VACS-CCI scores observed in our cohort overall and among those with diabetes and HIV. Using our published approach, we then translated VACS-CCI scores to an estimated probability for males and females overall, among patients with diabetes and among patients with HIV.

To facilitate direct comparisons between age-based estimates and those from VACS-CCI, we fitted the data points for one-unit incremental increases in age (yearly) or score (single point increase) to polynomial regression equations (Appendix 2). This ensured smooth and monotonically changing estimates and provided estimates at smaller increments. The appropriate functional form for these equations was found by improvement in r-squared and by plotting the raw point estimates overlaid with the equation derived line. Visual inspection was used to verify adequate fit over the range of values. First, we generated an equation for survival as a function of age for the US population which allowed us to estimate the equivalent age in the US population from the 10-year survival estimates using VACS-CCI. Then we generated equations for VACS-CCI scores vs age using the median, 10th and 90th percentile for each age (Appendix 3). We then translated VACS-CCI scores to an estimated 10-year survival using the prespecified equation for survival vs score. Once we had generated these continuous estimates, we overlaid plots of estimated survival by age and sex based on US life tables and VACS-CCI.

We also obtained Harrel's C-statistics and Brier Scores from Cox models of up to 10-year survival using chronological age alone and VACS-CCI score alone and determined whether condition specific biomarkers for those with diabetes (haemoglobin A1c) and those with HIV (CD4 count and HIV-1 RNA) improved discrimination of VACS-CCI.

Physiological age was defined as the age at which the median VACS-CCI survival estimate matched the US population estimate based on age alone. The age gap was the difference between the physiological and chronological age. For example, the median VACS-CCI 10-year survival estimate for a 65-year-old male Veteran with diabetes was 58.2%. This corresponded to the population estimate for males who were 73.6 years old suggesting that for 65-year-old male patients in VHA care with diabetes the average age gap equals 73.6 minus 65.0 or 8.6 years.

Correct classification

Restricting to those with index visit by 12/31/2011 (allowing ten years of follow-up), we constructed 2 × 2 tables of predicted and observed survival. We compared the performance of age 75 years versus VACS-CCI score of 42 as a 10-year survival threshold (estimated 50% 10-year survival). This was done overall, by sex, for those with diabetes, and for those with HIV. We calculated overall percent correct. We repeated this analysis in the full sample using Kaplan–Meier survival estimates with similar results (data not otherwise shown).

VACS-CCI components among those with diabetes and HIV

To better understand which components of VACS-CCI most differentiated those with and without diabetes and HIV, we summarised values for each VACS-CCI component by disease status in unadjusted and adjusted (multivariable logistic regression) analysis. Separate models were fitted with diabetes and HIV as dependent variables and VACS-CCI components (weighted as in the score) as the independent variables. The chi square value for each variable quantified its overall importance (both strength of association and prevalence). We divided the individual chi square value by the sum of all chi square values generating percentage of importance (Appendix 4).

Survival estimates: overall

Compared to US population age-based estimates, VACS-CCI improved discrimination of 10-year survival in the overall sample (Fig. 1A and B: C-statistic 0.740 vs. 0.813; Brier Scores 0.262 vs. 0.239). Age-based estimates fell above the median but below the 90th percentiles of VACS-CCI estimates for males and females in VHA care (Fig. 2A and B). Within every age, we observed a wide range of individual VACS-CCI survival estimates. Survival estimates: among those with diabetes

Those with diabetes differed substantially from those without (Table 1, Appendix 4). They were older, had a greater BMI, were less often female, had a higher white blood cell count, and a lower eGFR. They were more likely to be diagnosed with kidney disease, congestive heart failure, myocardial infarction, peripheral vascular disease, and cerebral vascular disease. VACS-CCI scores were eight points higher among those with diabetes than those without (Table 1, Appendix 4) and VACS-CCI components effectively differentiated those with and without diabetes (C-statistic 0.716; 95% CI 0.715–0.716).

Compared to age alone, VACS-CCI substantially improved survival estimation among those with diabetes (Fig. 1A and B: C-statistics 0.674 vs. 0.766; Brier Scores 0.343 vs. 0.313). Adding haemoglobin A1c to the Cox regression survival model improved the C-statistic by 0.002 and the Brier score by 0.001 points (C -statistic 0.768, Brier score 0.312), with overlapping confidence intervals. When VACS-CCI estimates were compared with age-based estimates for both males and females with diabetes, differences were similar by sex (Fig. 2C and D).

Within every age, we observed a wide range of VACS-CCI estimates (Fig. 2C and D). For example, among 65-year-old individuals with diabetes, VACS-CCI estimated 10-year survival 10th to 90th percentiles ranged from 21.6% to 73.7% for males and 39.5% to 82.1% for females.

The median VACS-CCI survival estimate for 65-year-old males with diabetes was 58.2% (Table 2, Appendix 3). This corresponds to an age-based estimates for a 73.6-year-old male in the population, for a gap of 8.6 years. For 65-year-old females with diabetes the median VACS-CCI estimate was 69.8%. This matches age-based estimates for 72.8-year-old females for a gap of 7.8 years. VACS-CCI estimates suggest an even larger gap in physiological vs. chronological age for 40-year-old individuals (14.4 years for males and 17.4 years for females).

When we considered 75 years a threshold and restricted to those for whom we had 10-year mortality follow-up (Table 3), age alone correctly classified 64.3% of those with diabetes. VACS-CCI correctly classified 77.2%. VACS-CCI improved risk classification for 12.9% or 1 in 7.8 of those with diabetes.

Survival estimates: among those with HIV

Those with HIV also differed substantially from those without (Table 1, Appendix 4). Those with HIV were younger, less likely to be female, and had a lower BMI, a lower white blood cell count, a lower estimated glomerular filtration rate, and a higher FIB-4. They were more likely to be diagnosed with hepatitis C infection and liver disease. VACS-CCI components effectively differentiated those with and without HIV (C statistic 0.816; 95% CI 0.81–0.818).

Compared to age alone, VACS-CCI substantially improved discrimination of 10-year survival for those with HIV (Fig. 1A and B, C statistics 0.637 vs. 0.766; Brier Scores 0.265 vs. 0.236). Adding CD4 count and HIV-1 RNA to the Cox regression survival model improved the C-statistic by 0.013 and the Brier Score by 0.003 points, with overlapping confidence intervals. Because few females with HIV were older than 70 years of age, we were not able to estimate risk for females with HIV beyond this age. Within observed ages, when VACS-CCI estimates were compared with age-based estimates differences between age-based estimates and the VACS-CCI were consistent by sex (Fig. 2E and F). Within every observed age, VACS-CCI survival estimates were wide ranging (Fig. 2E and F). For example, for 65-year-old males in VHA with HIV, VACS-CCI 10-year 10th to 90th percentile survival estimates ranged from 5.6% to 62.9% and among 65-year-old females in VHA with HIV estimates ranged from 27.6% to 69.0%.

The median VACS-CCI estimate for 65-year-old males with HIV was 43.4% (Table 2). This corresponds to age-based estimates for males who are 78.5 years old, for a age gap of 13.5 years. The median VACS-CCI estimate for females 65 years of age with HIV was 58.9% corresponding to the population estimate for females 76.6 years old. As a group, these females are physiologically 11.6 years older than their chronological age. At 40 years of age, VACS-CCI estimates suggest an even larger gap (23.9 years for males and 25.0 years for females).

When we restricted the sample to those with HIV for whom we had complete 10-year follow-up (Table 2), age alone correctly classified 58.1%. VACS-CCI correctly classified 73.7%. VACS-CCI improved survival classification for 15.6% or 1 in 6.4 Veterans with HIV.

Discussion

For patients in VHA care overall, 10-year survival estimates based on US population life tables stratified by age and sex (age-based) exceeded physiological estimates incorporating clinical data (VACS-CCI) and fell between the median and 90th percentiles of VACS-CCI estimates. Chronological versus physiological estimates differed more substantially for those in VHA care with diabetes and HIV across a wide range of ages. When the sample was restricted to those with completed 10-year follow up, a VACS-CCI threshold of 42 improved correct classification over the threshold of 75 years of age. Finally, when those with and without diabetes or HIV were compared, VACS-CCI components effectively differentiated those with and without both diseases. This was true despite the absence of any disease-specific biomarkers, and the addition of disease-specific biomarkers did not substantially improve survival estimation. Our findings have important implications for future application of clinical guidelines among those with complex chronic disease.

Among those in VHA care with diabetes or HIV, we demonstrate that laboratory data and comorbid disease diagnoses suggest a substantial physiological versus chronological gap in expected survival. At 65 years of age females and males in VHA care were physiologically older by 3.1–4.6 years overall, 7.8–8.6 years among those with diabetes, and 11.6–13.5 years among those with HIV. In support of our findings, previous work has suggested that the epigenetic age of those with HIV prior to antiretroviral treatment is 4.8–11.2 years older than their chronological age and that differences persist after treatment.11, 12, 13, 14

This large age gap applies to individuals in whom screening guidelines are likely to be applied. These individuals were actively engaged in healthcare, and most had achieved control of their primary disease. Over half the sample with diabetes had A1c levels below 7, and over half of those with HIV had CD4 cell counts above 500 per mm3, but in both cases their expected survival based on physiology was substantially less than that estimated based on age alone.

It is generally accepted that those with diabetes have a reduced survival largely attributable to organ system injury and multimorbidity,15 and we found this to be the case. However, the same cannot be said for those ageing with HIV. A highly cited study using data from the Antiretroviral Therapy Cohort Collaboration and the UK Collaborative HIV Cohort Study estimated life expectancy among those living with HIV initiating antiretroviral therapy after 2015. This study concluded that life expectancy for males starting treatment at 40 years of age with CD4 cell counts greater than 500 cells/microliter is only 1.5 years shorter than for the general population.16 For females, the estimate was 3.8 years. The median age in this study was 47 years, only 12.4% were 60 years of age or older, 57% had CD4 counts above 500 cells/mm3. The analysis did include measures of organ system injury or of comorbid disease. In light of direct measures of organ system injury and comorbid disease burden, we reach a very different conclusion. Specifically, at 40 years of age, males and females currently in VHA care with HIV are physiologically 24–25 years older than their age suggests. These individuals are unlikely to experience similar survival to that of the US general population.

VACS-CCI components alone effectively differentiated those with diabetes and with HIV underscoring that these chronic diseases are largely characterised by their associated organ system injury and multimorbidity. Further, adding haemoglobin A1c for those with diabetes and CD4 count and viral load for those with HIV did not substantially improve the discrimination of VACS-CCI. The effect of these disease-specific measures on survival was already reflected in the measures in VACS-CCI.

Strengths of this analysis include application of VACS-CCI, a transparent, prognostic index based on standardised clinical measures, previously validated in this sample. Validation work occurred in the largest integrated health system in the US that boasts unparallelled completeness of data including social security, and inpatient mortality making this analysis possible. The laboratory components of VACS have been widely validated outside VHA among those with HIV.7,9,17, 18, 19 CCI has been widely validated in ageing populations.8 It seems reasonable to expect that VACS-CCI will generalise well.

We explored differences for those with and without diabetes or HIV as prototypical examples of complex chronic disease characterised by chronic inflammation occurring in distinct populations. Future work should include validating VACS-CCI outside VHA and measuring the age gap for other complex chronic diseases. However, this work may be constrained because many EHRs only reliably capture deaths occurring during a within network hospitalisation.

Some variables required for VACS-CCI may be missing. In this and prior analyses of VACS-CCI, missing albumin and transaminase values were assumed to be in the normal range enabling a calculation of VACS-CCI for 81% of the otherwise eligible sample.9 Of note, CCI components are assumed absent when not specified. Also, while we had large numbers of males and females over 70 years of age in the overall sample and among those with diabetes, we had more limited numbers with HIV, requiring us to terminate estimates for females with HIV above the age of 70 years.

Both age-based and VACS-CCI estimates assume that observed survival patterns will be sustained in future. This assumption may be inaccurate for those ageing with HIV as treatments have continued to improve over the past 20 years. Those currently at advanced age likely experienced less optimised management earlier in their disease course. However, the measures of physiology included in VACS-CCI will likely respond to improvements in HIV clinical management that lead to improvements in survival.20,21

Our analyses suggest that a disease agnostic, survival index incorporating routine physiological measures available in the EHR is feasible and superior to age-based estimates. Our specific results pertain to the physiological age of Veterans receiving medical care in VHA, a population likely to have more complex chronic disease than the general population. However, the fact that US population survival estimates fell between median and 90th percentile VACS-CCI estimate suggests that our finding of much more substantial age gaps for those with diabetes and HIV will generalise outside VHA.

In conclusion, this study suggests that the value of clinical guidelines could be improved by employing physiologically based standardised survival estimates. In the era of EHRs, application of clinical guidelines should no longer depend upon chronological age thresholds or clinical judgement. We have explored 10-year survival as an example, but payoff times depend on the intervention in question. Guidelines should specify the payoff time needed. Validated, and regularly updated, physiologically based survival indices should be made available as auto-populated fields in the EHR. One such measure, VACS-CCI, is ready for application within VHA and, once validated outside VHA, may improve value in other healthcare systems as well.
