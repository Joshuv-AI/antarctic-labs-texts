Prevalence and associated factors of musculoskeletal pain among university students in Saudi Arabia: an analytical cross-sectional study
Authors: Wael Alghamdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMusculoskeletal pain is common among university students and may be influenced by prolonged sitting, digital-device use, suboptimal ergonomic practices, and low physical activity. However, evidence from Saudi university settings remains limited. This study aimed to estimate the 12-month prevalence and anatomical distribution of musculoskeletal pain among university students at Al-Baha University and to examine its associations with ergonomic behaviours and physical activity levels.MethodsThis analytical cross-sectional study was conducted among university students aged ≥ 18 years at Al-Baha University. Data were collected using self-administered questionnaires capturing demographics, 12-month musculoskeletal pain and affected body regions assessed using the Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire (NMQ), and ergonomic practices. Participants reported physical-activity summaries from their smartphone health applications (Google Fit and Apple Health). Descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, Mann–Whitney U tests, and multivariable logistic regression were used to analyse the data and identify factors independently associated with musculoskeletal pain. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).ResultsAmong 675 students, the mean age was 22.26 ± 3.36 years; most participants were male (74.4%) and enrolled in health-sciences programs (85.2%). Overall, 85.0% reported musculoskeletal pain in the previous 12 months. The neck (47.6%), lower back (46.8%), and shoulders (34.2%) were the most affected regions. In multivariable analysis, internship-level students had higher odds of pain (OR = 15.05, 95% CI: 2.91–77.78, p = 0.001), and sitting 6–8 h/day was associated with higher odds of pain (OR = 13.95, 95% CI: 4.13–47.19, p < 0.001). Low daily physical activity time (< 15 min/day) was also associated with higher odds of pain (OR = 5.70, 95% CI: 2.14–15.16, p < 0.001). The model showed acceptable discrimination (AUC = 0.793, 95% CI: 0.742–0.843, p < 0.001), although specificity and calibration were limited.ConclusionsMusculoskeletal pain was common among university students at Al-Baha University, particularly in the neck, lower back, and shoulders. The findings suggest that prolonged sitting and selected physical-activity indicators are important associated factors. These findings may inform campus-based strategies promoting regular movement breaks, ergonomic study practices, and physical activity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10228-x.

Introduction

Across the world, university students are affected by musculoskeletal pain, especially in the areas of the neck, the shoulders, and the lower back [1]. Published studies indicate that musculoskeletal pain experience ranges from around 41% to as much as 90% in various academic institutions, with commonly associated factors being inactive lifestyles, poor ergonomic practices, and overexposure to computers and smartphones [1, 2]. One study reported that during the COVID-19 pandemic, 74.6% of students experienced musculoskeletal pain, compared to 68.2% before the pandemic [3]. A further study of architecture students reported that 94.3% suffered musculoskeletal pain in at least one body region in the preceding 12 months [4]. These findings indicate the need for context-specific preventive strategies targeting modifiable behavioural and ergonomic factors among university students [5]. Given university students’ high levels of physical inactivity and extensive digital device use, their musculoskeletal health requires further clinical and research attention [3, 5].

Musculoskeletal pain among university students in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) represents an important public health concern. As learning has become increasingly digital-based, screen exposure and sedentary time have increased, though physical activity levels have tended to decline [6, 7]. In 2024, a cross-sectional study at King Khalid University (Abha), revealed that 223 of 536 undergraduates, 41.6% experienced pain in the neck, shoulders, or low back [8]. In a cross-section survey of Saudi Universities students, 66% reported lower back pain [9]. In addition, a cross-sectional study of Majmaah university students (n = 257), the 12-month prevalence of musculoskeletal pain was 87.5%, with the lower back pain (46.3%) and neck (44.0%) being the most commonly affected region [2]. The overall picture emerging from these studies suggests that a substantial proportion of Saudi university students experience musculoskeletal pain, highlighting the need for further examination of behavioural and ergonomic factors and the implementation of targeted prevention initiatives. Recent Saudi and international evidence suggests that musculoskeletal pain remains a common problem among university students. However, its prevalence and the role of behavioural and ergonomic factors may vary across academic settings. This highlights the importance of conducting context-specific research in Saudi universities that examines sedentary behaviour, ergonomic practices, and physical activity together.

Musculoskeletal pain among higher education students has been associated with particular behaviours and ergonomic habits, such as prolonged sitting, low levels of physical activity, and limited attention to ergonomic practices [9]. Evidence from Saudi university cohorts indicates these associations, linking prolonged sitting and low physical activity with musculoskeletal pain among students [2, 8]. These findings have also been reported internationally; for instance, Canadian students have shown a significant association between prolonged screen time and higher rates of both neck and back pain [5]. Poor posture showed a significant association with musculoskeletal pain; Saudi students who used their phones with marked neck flexion had higher odds of reporting musculoskeletal pain (OR = 2.276; 95% CI: 1.18–4.40) [8]. These findings indicate that physical inactivity, prolonged sitting, and poor posture during digital device use are linked to a higher likelihood of musculoskeletal pain or symptoms, pointing to the need for targeted strategies to modify these habits.

Growing developments in digital health have enabled the wider assessment of posture, ergonomics, and physical activity patterns, which may support earlier identification of musculoskeletal health concerns [10, 11]. In practice, implementing these approaches in university settings remains challenging. Such evidence is needed in Saudi Arabia, given the high prevalence of lifestyle behaviours such as prolonged sitting, extensive device use, and physical inactivity. Although several recent studies have examined musculoskeletal pain among university students, many have focused mainly on prevalence or on individual lifestyle factors. Less is known about how ergonomic behaviours, sedentary time, and physical activity are related to musculoskeletal pain when considered together in Saudi university students [2, 3, 5, 8]. Understanding these modifiable factors may be useful for researchers, clinicians in student health and rehabilitation services, and students who experience musculoskeletal pain. It can help guide earlier recognition of musculoskeletal concerns, more targeted advice, and the development of future prevention studies. This study therefore aimed to: (1) assess the prevalence of musculoskeletal pain among Al-Baha University students; and (2) use a multivariable logistic regression model to identify behavioural and ergonomic factors associated with musculoskeletal pain. It was hypothesised that prolonged sitting, suboptimal ergonomic behaviours, and lower physical activity levels would be associated with higher odds of 12-month musculoskeletal pain. The findings may generate foundational evidence that may inform the development of practical, evidence-informed prevention approaches to mitigate the burden of musculoskeletal pain in university settings.

Methods

Study design

An analytical cross-sectional design was used to estimate the prevalence of musculoskeletal pain among students at Al-Baha University between April and July 2025 and to identify factors associated with musculoskeletal pain. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline for cross-sectional studies [12]. The design was chosen because it permits concurrent assessment of the burden of musculoskeletal pain and potential behavioural and ergonomic factors within a specific cohort. Data were collected using a self-administered questionnaire that incorporated validated musculoskeletal measures and self-reported ergonomic items, alongside smartphone-derived physical-activity summaries reported from Google Fit and/or Apple Health. This approach provided complementary questionnaire and app-derived summary data on activity patterns and common study postures.

For analysis, the study focused on investigating associations between musculoskeletal symptoms and behavioural, ergonomic, demographic variables, as well as levels of physical activity. To identify factors associated with musculoskeletal pain, multivariable regression modelling was applied. No artificial intelligence technology was used for data collection, analysis, or interpretation in this study.

Participants and sampling

University students at Al-Baha University from various faculties and educational levels, including both male and female students, were the target population for this survey. To enhance representation across gender, educational level, and faculty/discipline, a strata-informed recruitment approach was used. Response distribution across these strata was monitored during data collection. Students were recruited via university-wide email invitations sent to approximately 5,000 students and through digital advertisements, with participation facilitated through an online survey link; 675 students completed the questionnaire, giving an approximate response rate of 13.5% based on the email distribution list. As this was an observational cross-sectional study with no intervention groups, randomisation and allocation procedures were not applicable. The questionnaire was administered online. Data were collected between April and July 2025 while classes were in session.

Response distributions were monitored to reduce marked imbalance across the predefined strata where feasible. No fixed proportional allocation was applied. To be eligible, students had to be currently enrolled at Al-Baha University, aged ≥ 18 years, and able to provide informed consent. Students who reported a severe diagnosed musculoskeletal condition or physical disability that markedly limited daily activities were excluded, as these factors could independently influence musculoskeletal pain and physical activity levels. Some factors, such as academic discipline, academic year, BMI, sitting time, device use, study posture, and physical activity, were considered part of the study variables rather than exclusion criteria. These factors were therefore measured and included in the analysis where appropriate. Cochran’s formula for cross-sectional studies was used to estimate an adequate sample size at a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error, using a conservative expected prevalence of 50%: n₀ = Z²p(1 − p)/d². With an approximate student population of 10,000, finite population correction was applied [n = n₀ / (1 + (n₀−1)/N)], yielding a minimum required sample of approximately 370 participants. The final sample (n = 675) exceeded the minimum required size, while maintaining students’ confidentiality and providing equal opportunity to participate.

Data collection tools

A multi-source quantitative data collection approach was used, comprising a self-administered questionnaire and smartphone-derived physical-activity summaries. This combination allowed comprehensive assessment of musculoskeletal health, physical-activity habits, and ergonomic practices using self-reported questionnaire data and smartphone-derived physical-activity summaries reported by participants.

Self-administered questionnaire

The questionnaire was provided in Arabic and English and incorporated validated instruments, alongside study-developed items, to collect data across demographic, musculoskeletal, and ergonomic domains. The self-administered questionnaire comprised the Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire (NMQ) [13, 14] and study-developed demographic and ergonomic items; the ergonomic items were informed by the modified Rapid Upper Limb Assessment (mRULA) framework [15, 16]. The NMQ was used as the primary outcome measure for 12-month musculoskeletal pain and affected body regions; it is a standardised instrument with established validity, and its Arabic version has demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties [13, 14]. The mRULA was not applied as a direct observational scoring tool; rather, its ergonomic domains informed the self-reported posture and workstation items [15, 16].

Statistical analysis

The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for data analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to describe demographic characteristics, physical activity levels, ergonomic patterns, and the prevalence of musculoskeletal symptoms. Continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations, whereas categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages.

To assess associations between musculoskeletal pain and the associated factors, bivariate analyses were performed according to variable type. Categorical variables were tested using chi-square tests, and BMI was compared between pain groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. Spearman’s rank correlation was used to assess associations among physical-activity, sedentary behaviour, and ergonomic variables. The primary outcome for logistic regression was self-reported 12-month musculoskeletal pain status (yes/no), defined as pain symptoms in at least one NMQ body region during the previous 12 months, rather than clinically confirmed musculoskeletal disorders. Multivariable logistic regression was performed on 655 participants after excluding the standing-posture category, which contained no pain-free cases and could result in unstable estimates. For descriptive and bivariate analyses, valid responses were used and denominators are reported in the relevant tables. Variables entered into the multivariable model were mutually adjusted to control for potential confounding, including academic discipline, academic year, daily device hours, common study posture, daily step count, daily sitting hours, and daily active minutes. Because the main bivariate analyses used chi-square tests for categorical variables and Mann–Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed continuous variables, homogeneity of variance was not a required assumption for the reported analyses.

To examine factors independently associated with musculoskeletal pain, multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted, adjusting for pertinent demographic factors. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, and statistical significance was set at a two-sided p-value of < 0.05. Model diagnostics included multicollinearity checks using variance inflation factors (VIFs), ROC–AUC, and internal validation using an 80/20 split-sample approach.

Ethical considerations

This study met the ethical standards of the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Al-Baha University, and ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board at Al-Baha University (Approval No. 46121733/2025; 22 March 2025). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants’ safety, confidentiality, and data integrity were maintained throughout the study. An outline of study objectives, procedures, possible risks, and potential benefits was provided to students before enrolment. All participants took part voluntarily and provided informed consent digitally before participation.

Data collection was anonymous; confidentiality was maintained by substituting numeric codes for personal identifiers. The data were password-protected and encrypted, and only the lead investigator had access. Participants could withdraw at any time without consequence. The study posed minimal physical or psychological risk, as participation was limited to self-reported questionnaires and reporting of smartphone health-app physical-activity summaries. Potential benefits included improved awareness of musculoskeletal health and lifestyle-related factors, and findings that may inform preventive strategies in universities. This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for cross-sectional studies.

Results

Participant characteristics and 12-month musculoskeletal pain prevalence

A total of 675 Al-Baha University students participated. The majority were male (74.4%), and most participants were enrolled in health-sciences programs (85.2%). The mean age was 22.26 ± 3.36 years, and the mean body mass index (BMI) was 24.22 ± 6.31 kg/m². Overall, most participants were within the normal BMI range (Table 1). In total, 85.0% of the participants reported musculoskeletal pain in the preceding 12 months. This estimate reflects the broad primary outcome of self-reported pain symptoms in at least one body region during the previous 12 months. The neck was the most affected region (47.6%), followed closely by the lower back (46.8%), and the shoulders (34.2%). Most participants with pain rated it as moderate (64%), compared with 5.2% who reported severe pain. More than half (51%) reported that pain impaired their daily functionality, but only 17% had sought medical care (Table 1). In terms of study-related ergonomics and behaviours, 82.8% of participants reported using tablets, while 63% reported not using ergonomic tools when studying. Nearly half of the students (48.4%) reported sitting for 4–6 h per day, and 37.0% recorded 2,000–4,999 steps per day, indicating low physical activity levels (Table 1).

Musculoskeletal pain was also associated with ergonomics and lifestyle factors. Multivariable logistic regression model

To examine factors independently associated with musculoskeletal pain, a multivariable logistic regression model was developed (Table 3). The model was fitted on complete cases (n = 655). The overall model was statistically significant (χ²(24) = 111.35, p < 0.001), indicating that the included variables were jointly associated with musculoskeletal pain status. The model explained between 15.6% and 27.1% of the variance in pain status (Cox & Snell R² = 0.156; Nagelkerke R² = 0.271).

After adjustment for potential confounders, the following behavioural and academic factors were independently associated with musculoskeletal pain. Compared to humanities students, those in administrative sciences (OR = 4.05; 95% CI: 1.28–12.81; p = 0.017) and health sciences (OR = 3.83; 95% CI: 1.38–10.63; p = 0.010) had higher odds of reporting musculoskeletal pain. Internship-level students had higher odds of musculoskeletal pain (OR = 15.05; 95% CI: 2.91–77.78; p = 0.001), although the confidence interval was wide; graduate students also had higher odds of pain (OR = 4.18; 95% CI: 1.08–16.15; p = 0.038).

Sitting for 6–8 h per day was associated with higher odds of musculoskeletal pain (OR = 13.95; 95% CI: 4.13–47.19; p < 0.001). Some higher step-count categories were associated with lower odds of musculoskeletal pain. Compared with the reference category, recording 2,000–4,999 steps per day (OR = 0.28; 95% CI: 0.12–0.65; p = 0.003) and 8,000–9,999 steps per day (OR = 0.18; 95% CI: 0.05–0.67; p = 0.011) was associated with lower odds of musculoskeletal pain. However, reporting < 15 min of daily physical activity was associated with higher odds of musculoskeletal pain (OR = 5.70; 95% CI: 2.14–15.16; p < 0.001).

Model performance and diagnostics

The Hosmer–Lemeshow test was statistically significant (χ²(8) = 39.56, p < 0.001). However, this test can be overly sensitive in larger samples; therefore, discrimination and classification performance were evaluated using classification metrics and ROC–AUC analysis. Table 4 summarizes the classification performance of the multivariable logistic regression model. Overall accuracy was 87.3%, with high sensitivity (98.9%) but low specificity (23.8%), indicating that the model identified students with musculoskeletal pain more effectively than those without pain. The positive predictive value (PPV) was 87.7%, and the negative predictive value (NPV) was 80.0%. Discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. The AUC was 0.793 (95% CI: 0.742–0.843; p < 0.001), indicating acceptable discrimination, although this should be interpreted alongside the significant Hosmer–Lemeshow test and low specificity (Table 5 and Fig. 1). No evidence of problematic multicollinearity was observed among the variables included in the regression model, with all variance inflation factors (VIFs) < 5.

Correlation, sensitivity, and validation analyses

Spearman’s rank correlation was used to assess associations among key continuous variables. Device time was positively correlated with daily sitting time (ρ = 0.195, p < 0.001), and BMI was positively correlated with daily sitting time (ρ = 0.153, p < 0.001). In contrast, step counts were negatively correlated with daily sitting time (ρ = −0.259, p < 0.001). MVPA was strongly positively correlated with step counts (ρ = 0.520, p < 0.001).

To assess model robustness, internal validation was conducted using a random 80/20 split of the complete-case regression sample (n = 655) into development and validation subsets. The same predictor set was refitted in the development subset and evaluated in the validation subset. In the validation subset, the model showed an overall classification accuracy of 87.6%, with high sensitivity (98.9%) but low specificity (25.9%). Findings were consistent in the low back pain subgroup, with academic year, daily sitting time, and daily physical activity time remaining independently associated with pain status. Overall, these analyses suggest that the model results were broadly consistent across subsamples.

Discussion

A high prevalence of musculoskeletal pain was observed among university students in this study. Over the 12-month study period, 85% of the participants reported pain symptoms, mostly in the neck (47.6%), lower back (46.8%), and shoulders (34.2%). Approximately half reported that pain impaired daily functioning (51%); most rated pain as moderate (64%), whereas only 17% reported seeking medical care. Musculoskeletal pain was often associated with behaviour and ergonomic factors, including prolonged sitting, higher digital-device use, and lower physical activity, whereas some higher daily step-count categories were inversely associated with musculoskeletal pain. These factors, in addition to academic year and college discipline, remained independently associated with musculoskeletal pain in the multivariable model. The discussion below considers these findings in relation to previous research, possible behavioural and ergonomic explanations, clinical and preventive implications, and the main strengths and limitations of the study.

The findings of this study are broadly consistent with previous research, with international prevalence estimates of musculoskeletal pain among university students particularly in the neck and lower back [1, 3, 5, 17]. Similar patterns have been reported among university students in both South Africa and Canada [5], with frequent complaints involving the shoulders and lumbar spine. In Saudi Arabian universities, approximately two thirds of students have self-reported lower back pain in recent surveys [9], with a high overall burden also been documented among health-related programs [18, 19]. Common characteristics reported across multiple student disciplines include prolonged sitting, high digital-device use, and poor posture [3, 5, 17, 20, 21]; however, the slightly higher prevalence observed in the present study could be partly explained by recall bias, differences in sampling strategies and measurement approaches [20], and increased reliance on screen-based learning [3]. Taken together, findings from previous studies help to interpret the demographic and behavioural factors associated with musculoskeletal pain in the present sample.

The findings in this study are consistent with previous research on the demographic, behavioural, and ergonomic factors associated with musculoskeletal pain in the neck and lower back among university students. Smartphone-related neck postures have long been linked to a higher burden of neck and shoulder complaints [17, 21]; similarly, studying on handheld devices —particularly while reclining on a bed or sofa—may place additional strain on the shoulders, back, and/or neck [22, 23]. Additional associations of musculoskeletal pain include low physical activity and lower daily step counts; in the adjusted model, very low activity (< 15 min/day) was associated with higher odds of pain, whereas higher step-count categories were associated with lower odds. The available evidence indicates that many Saudi higher education students may not meet recommended physical activity guidelines [24], despite the well-established health benefits of regular walking [25]. Interestingly, enrolment in health-related programmes was associated with musculoskeletal pain, in line with findings reported among senior medical [18] and dental students [19]. These observations may be influenced by heavier academic workloads and clinical duties within health-related programmes, which may involve prolonged standing, sustained static postures, or suboptimal ergonomics. Overall, these findings point to a combination of factors underlying musculoskeletal pain, likely involving educational demands, high screen exposure, suboptimal ergonomic habits, and low physical activity.

The findings of this study have practical clinical and preventive implications for university health services and campus policies. The frequent reporting of sedentary behaviours, suboptimal ergonomic habits, and low physical activity alongside musculoskeletal pain supports a prevention-focused approach rather than reliance on individualised treatment alone. The findings may inform universities in implementing fundamental changes, such as improving the ergonomics of study spaces and lecture halls [26], educating students on posture [27], encouraging regular breaks during study, and promoting more frequent movement during prolonged study sessions [28]. In addition, universities are encouraged to promote regular walking and step-based targets through campus-wide step challenges and active-break reminders, which should be evaluated as potential strategies for addressing musculoskeletal pain [29]. Routine screening for musculoskeletal pain and related conditions could be embedded within student health services, enabling earlier identification and timely support.

No artificial intelligence technology was used in this study. Future longitudinal studies may examine whether smartphone health-app activity summaries provide additional insight into musculoskeletal pain patterns among university students. A strength of this study is the inclusion of a relatively large student sample and the use of validated musculoskeletal measures, together with smartphone-derived physical activity data where available. However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal or temporal inference between musculoskeletal pain and the examined factors. Reverse causation is possible, as students with musculoskeletal pain may reduce their physical activity rather than low physical activity necessarily preceding the onset of pain. Therefore, the regression estimates should be interpreted as associations rather than causal or temporal effects.

Second, generalisability may be limited because participants were recruited from a single university. Because recruitment relied on voluntary online participation, selection bias cannot be excluded. In addition, the predominance of male and health-sciences students may limit the generalisability of the findings, particularly to female students and students from non-health disciplines; future studies should include more balanced samples from multiple institutions to improve external validity. Third, musculoskeletal symptoms, ergonomic behaviours, posture, and device-use variables were self-reported and may be subject to reporting and recall bias. Although the NMQ is a validated instrument, the outcome was not clinically confirmed. Because the primary outcome was broadly defined as any self-reported musculoskeletal pain in at least one NMQ body region during the previous 12 months, the prevalence estimate may be higher than estimates based on clinically diagnosed or functionally limiting musculoskeletal conditions. Smartphone-derived physical activity summaries from Google Fit and Apple Health may also vary according to device type, app availability, user adherence to carrying the device, and completeness of recorded data. Other unmeasured factors, such as previous injuries, chronic conditions, psychological stress, or prior treatment, may have influenced musculoskeletal pain and physical activity levels. Finally, the multivariable logistic regression model showed high sensitivity but relatively low specificity, suggesting a tendency to overclassify musculoskeletal pain. The statistically significant Hosmer–Lemeshow test also indicates that model calibration was not optimal. In addition, some estimates, particularly for categories with smaller cell counts, had wide confidence intervals, indicating statistical uncertainty; these estimates should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Therefore, the model should be considered exploratory rather than a definitive screening or prediction tool and requires external validation in independent student cohorts.

These findings highlight several practical recommendations and priorities for future research. Addressing musculoskeletal pain should be considered as part of university health-promotion strategies. Prevention may include posture education and ergonomic workstation use, moderating digital device use, taking regular breaks during prolonged sitting, and promoting physical activity on campus. Basic strategies—such as optimizing ergonomic study environments, encouraging walking and higher daily step counts, and reducing excessive screen time—may be relevant to addressing musculoskeletal concerns and should be evaluated in future longitudinal or interventional studies. Future research should expand on these findings using longitudinal and/or interventional designs, recruiting participants from multiple universities, and incorporating more detailed biomechanical and psychosocial measures.

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional study, self-reported musculoskeletal pain symptoms were frequently reported by university students, with 85% reporting pain during the previous 12 months. The neck, lower back, and shoulders were the most commonly affected areas. Pain was associated with academic year, academic discipline, longer sitting time, and lower physical-activity indicators. These findings suggest that student health initiatives may benefit from attention to ergonomic study practices, regular movement breaks, and opportunities for physical activity. Because of the cross-sectional design, these associations should not be interpreted as causal. Further multi-centre longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to confirm these findings and evaluate targeted prevention strategies.
