Investigating the associations between AI-Based educational tool utilization, academic procrastination, academic achievement, and time management among nursing students
Authors: Zainab Albikawi, Mohammad Abuadas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe role of artificial intelligence (AI) tools in education has transformed the way students learn and manage their academic tasks. However, health professions education has not sufficiently studied the role of AI tools in procrastination, time management, and academic achievement.ObjectivesTo investigate the associations of AI-based educational tools usage, academic procrastination, academic achievement, and time management among undergraduate nursing students.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted with a sample of 167 Jordanian undergraduate nursing students. Data was collected through validated scales measuring procrastination (General Procrastination Scale), time management skills (Time Management Questionnaire), and a researcher-developed scale measuring AI-based educational tool usage. Results: Participants demonstrated moderate levels of academic procrastination (mean = 58.28 ± 13.00) and time management (mean = 56.86 ± 11.34), alongside a very good academic achievement (GPA mean = 81.25 ± 7.80). Multiple linear regression analysis indicated that AI-based educational tool usage (B = −0.22, p = 0.007) and time management (B = −0.38, p < 0.001) were significant predictors of academic procrastination. AI-based educational tool usage was also a significant predictor of time management (B = 0.24, p = 0.003). In addition, academic achievement was significantly predicted by AI-based educational tools usage (B = 0.24, p = 0.003), time management (B = 0.21, p = 0.007), and study hours (B = 2.85, p = 0.012).ConclusionThe use of AI-based educational tools was significantly associated with better academic performance among nursing students, alongside lower academic procrastination and better time management skills. The incorporation of AI into the learning process for nursing students may support the development of efficient learning habits and positive academic outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04815-5.

Introduction

Nursing education is experiencing a significant shift driven by the integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) into pedagogical practices. Traditionally reliant on clinical practicums and lecture-based instruction, nursing programs are now incorporating AI-based educational tools such as intelligent tutoring systems, chatbots, predictive feedback engines, and virtual simulations to support more adaptive, personalized, and efficient learning [1–4]. These technologies are particularly valuable in nursing, where students must develop not only theoretical knowledge but also time-sensitive decision-making, clinical judgment, and self-regulatory skills under high academic demands [4].

Among the behavioral challenges facing nursing students, academic procrastination is a conscious and irrational postponement of academic activities, such as studying, doing homework, and preparing for exams, despite knowing that there will be negative consequences [5–7]. The prevalence of procrastination in nursing programs is further complicated by the twin challenges of both mastering the theoretical aspects and carrying out clinical responsibilities, which increases the cognitive load and the stress levels in students [8]. Procrastination is prevalent in higher education in general, with as many as 40% of college students admitting to being chronic procrastinators [9]. The prevalence among nursing students varies between 51 and 70%, which may be due to the dual nature of theoretical studies and clinical work [10]. These behaviors have been linked to stress, low self-efficacy, and low self-confidence, which, in turn, negatively impact the acquisition of competencies for quality patient care [8, 11].

Emerging evidence suggests that AI tools may help mitigate procrastination among students. Features like task reminders, gamified learning, and instant feedback decrease avoidance behaviors, and adaptive learning platforms provide personalized experiences that keep learners engaged and motivated [6, 8]. Students who use AI tools such as ChatGPT report greater confidence in initiating tasks and maintaining academic engagement [11, 12]. However, some scholars caution that over-reliance on AI may erode intrinsic motivation and self-regulation, potentially worsening procrastination when tools are unavailable [13]. In fact, it is estimated that dependence on AI has been associated with anxiety and discomfort in students when they cannot access it, reinforcing the need to manage the use of AI in education in a balanced way [14].

Academic achievement in nursing, which is usually evaluated through Grade Point Average (GPA) and competency testing, reflects both theoretical mastery and practical skill acquisition. AI tools can enhance performance by delivering personalized content, providing immediate corrective feedback, and supporting writing and research skills [3, 15, 16]. Studies have shown that when personalized learning systems are designed to respond to the ability level of students, they increase motivation and participation and achieve better learning outcomes [17, 18]. In the Jordanian nursing education setting, the positive correlation between the use of AI and academic performance could be attributed to the knowledge-based nature of nursing exams, where AI tools can provide support in summarizing content and clarifying concepts, which can be especially academically beneficial. Moreover, there has been increasing awareness and acceptance of AI tools among health students in Jordan [19], indicating a favorable context for the use of AI in learning, which could further contribute to academic achievement.

Time management is equally critical in nursing education. AI-based environments offer structured support through digital scheduling tools, time-tracking applications, and algorithm-driven study planners that prioritize tasks by deadline and difficulty level [3, 16, 20]. Unlike traditional methods, these AI-powered tools allow students to better balance their academic and clinical workloads [20, 21]. The integration of AI into nursing education therefore has great potential to contribute to the self-regulation and time management skills that are crucial for future nursing practice [21, 22]. This is particularly important given that poor time management and procrastination have been consistently reported as major behavioral challenges among nursing students, negatively influencing their academic performance [23]. Despite a growing body of literature on AI in education, there are three important gaps. First, most studies have focused on either procrastination, time management, or academic achievement, but not on the simultaneous relationship between the use of AI tools and these three outcomes. [23]. Second, there are some nursing studies that focus on procrastination behaviors [23] and AI attitudes [24]; none have comprehensively examined how AI tool usage simultaneously influences all three academic outcomes within nursing education. Third, studies in the Arab or Middle Eastern nursing education context are still very scarce [24], which restricts the applicability of the studies’ results to the region. The present study addresses these gaps by simultaneously examining the associations between AI-based educational tool usage, academic procrastination, time management, and academic achievement among undergraduate nursing students in Jordan.

Materials and methods

Design

The present study adopted an analytical cross-sectional design to investigate the associations between AI-based educational tools, academic procrastination, academic performance, and time management skills among undergraduate nursing students. Data was collected at a single point from a representative sample of nursing students at different academic levels.

Sample size, population, and data collection

The sample size for the study was determined by the G*Power software version 3.1.9.7, considering a medium effect size (f2 = 0.15), alpha level = 0.05, and desired statistical power = 0.95 for a multiple regression analysis with nine predictors. The analysis revealed that a total of 166 participants were needed for the study. However, the number was adjusted by increasing it by 20% to compensate for the loss of participants, resulting in a total of 199 participants being needed for the study. The target population for the study was all undergraduate nursing students in their first, second, and third years at nursing college in northern Jordan.

Data collection was conducted between 1 May and 1 June 2025 using an online-based self-report questionnaire. Before answering the questionnaire, the students were asked to read a detailed informed consent form that described the purpose of the study, its voluntary nature, and its confidentiality. Only those students who agreed to the consent form were allowed to continue with the questionnaire. The questionnaire was divided into two sections. The first section included items on age, gender, academic year level, daily study hours, and academic course load. Participants reported their course load based on the number of credit hours they took in the previous semester. It was classified as light (less than 12 credit hours), moderate (12–15 credit hours), or heavy (more than 15 credit hours), following the classifications used in other nursing education research. Academic achievement was assessed by the students’ self-reported cumulative grade point average (GPA) of the previous semester, which is reported on a percentage scale ranging from 0 to 100 in most Jordanian universities, with a score of 90–100 being excellent, 80–89 being very good, 70–79 being good, 60–69 being acceptable, 50–59 being pass, and below 50 being failure.

The study used a convenience sampling technique to sample participants who met the study’s inclusion criteria. These included actively enrolled undergraduate nursing students who had prior experience in using at least one AI-based educational tool. This criterion was included without specifying a minimum frequency or duration of use. If the student had previously used an AI tool, this was deemed adequate for inclusion, as the study was designed to reflect as wide a range of AI engagement behaviors as possible among nursing students. Participation in this study was entirely voluntary, and all participants gave their consent electronically before the study proceeded. Students were excluded if they reported never using AI-based tools or having a physical or mental health condition that could potentially influence their time management, academic performance, or procrastination behavior, thus affecting the study’s outcomes, automatically taken to a thank-you page.

The research team did not perform clinical evaluation or diagnosis of these conditions. Instead, one self-screening question was included in the sociodemographic section of the online questionnaire that required the participants to report if they had any currently diagnosed physical or mental health condition that interferes with their academic functioning. Students who answered yes were immediately taken to a thank you page and were not included in the study. There was no request for any additional information on the nature or type of condition, so participants were not required to provide any sensitive diagnostic information other than a yes or no answer. The reason why this exclusion criterion was added was to control for confounding variables that could impact the study results, which would enhance the internal validity of the results. The choice of a brief self-screening item was made with the goal of reducing participant burden and the possible stigma around disclosure of mental health status. No one was requested to label, describe, or identify their condition. Finally, the anonymous and voluntary nature of the questionnaire further ensured that no individual could be identified based on their answer to this item, thus safeguarding participant confidentiality and minimizing the risk of stigma.

The study used a convenience sampling technique for several pragmatic and contextual reasons; convenience sampling was used. First, the study was carried out in a single nursing college where the researchers had institutional access, and this method was the most feasible for recruiting the active students in the defined time of data collection. Second, convenience sampling is well accepted and frequently used in nursing education research, especially in cross-sectional survey studies that involve student populations and behavioral and academic outcomes. Third, the inclusion criteria were specific (active enrollment and previous experience with AI tools in education), making it logistically unfeasible to use a probability sampling method within the timeframe and context of this study.

Several measures were taken to reduce potential selection bias. No restrictions were placed on who was allowed to participate; all students in all three academic year levels (first, second, and third years) were invited to participate, ensuring that students were recruited from the full target population and not just a single cohort or class. Furthermore, to reduce social desirability bias in self-reports (e.g., GPA, study habits), responses were anonymous, and participants were made aware that their answers were voluntary and confidential and would not impact their academic record. The use of an online self-report questionnaire also provided students with the opportunity to respond in their time and in a private setting, reducing response distortion. In addition, the number of participants who participated in this study was greater than the minimum sample size found through G*Power, which indicates that the sample was adequate for the statistical analyses conducted.

The Second Section in the questionnaire included the following scales. The General Procrastination Scale (GPS) developed by Lay [7] was used in the study to measure academic procrastination among the participants. The GPS consists of 20 items. It uses a Likert scale ranging from “Extremely Uncharacteristic” to “Extremely Characteristic.” The higher the score, the higher the level of procrastination. The GPS has been widely used in academic research to measure procrastination among students. The GPS is scored from 20 to 100, with higher scores reflecting higher levels of academic procrastination. As in previous studies using the GPS, scores were not interpreted based on fixed cut-off points but on the basis of the mean and standard deviation of the sample, because there are no universally agreed upon classification thresholds for this scale [7, 25]. Participants with scores higher than the sample mean were thus considered higher procrastinators, and those with scores lower than the mean were considered lower procrastinators.

The scale has been validated in many academic research studies. Most importantly, the GPS can be used freely in academic research without any special permission required. The current research demonstrated high internal consistency, as indicated by a Cronbach alpha coefficient of 0.83, which implies a high degree of reliability for the items used in the scale to measure academic procrastination in the sample.

The Time Management Questionnaire (TMQ), developed by Britton and Tesser [26], was employed to measure students’ time management behaviors. TMQ is an established measure commonly used in academic settings. The TMQ consists of 18 items across three subscales: short-range planning, long-range planning, and attitudes toward time management. The TMQ is based on a Likert scale with five categories ranging from “Never” (1) to “Always” (5), based on which participants need to express their level of agreement or disagreement. The TMQ has a total score of 18–90, with higher scores reflecting better time-management practices. The scores were categorized into three levels based on the classification criteria used in previous studies: low (39–54), moderate (55–69), and high (70–86) [27]. The boundaries between these cut scores have been used extensively with Middle Eastern university students and are in accord with the theoretical structure of the scale.

The TMQ has good internal consistency, with an alpha value of approximately 0.71 for the entire scale. The TMQ has been widely used in research involving university students. TMQ is an effective tool for measuring the ability of students to effectively manage time in academic settings. The TMQ is reproduced in an ERIC document by Trueman and Hartley (1995, ED417667), which is free for use in academic settings. The source is not under any specific license but has been used in various academic research papers.

The GPS and TMQ were developed and validated in Western contexts but are used in their original language in this study for several reasons. First, the nursing program is taught in English, and hence, both scales are linguistically suitable for the study population, as they do not need to be translated or culturally adapted. Second, procrastination as a behavioral construct (the voluntary and irrational delay of academic tasks despite awareness of negative consequences [5, 7]) and time management behaviors (daily planning, task prioritization, and scheduling [26, 28, 29]) are universally documented phenomena and are not culture-specific. Third, the TMQ has been used in the original English version with students in healthcare-related fields in Middle Eastern universities, such as in the UAE and Jordan, and has been found to be a valid instrument suitable for cross-cultural application in the Middle Eastern educational setting [27].

In addition, the GPS has been psychometrically tested in a variety of international cohorts of students and has been found to be reliable beyond the original context of the West. Fourth, the internal consistency of both scales in the current sample of Jordanian nursing students confirmed the reliability of both scales; the GPS had a Cronbach alpha of 0.83, and it was comparable to and within the range reported in the original validation study (α = 0.82) [7] and in other psychometric studies (α = 0.876) [25]; and the TMQ had acceptable internal consistency (α = 0.71), similar to values reported in the original study [26]. Overall, the results of both instruments indicate that they are suitable and valid for the current sample of Jordanian nursing students.

To assess students’ AI-based educational tool usage, a self-report scale was developed by the authors based on a comprehensive literature review of AI adoption in higher education and student learning behaviors. The self-report scale aimed to measure the frequency and usefulness of AI adoption in academic learning. An initial item pool was developed based on typical academic uses of AI, including assignment completion, exam preparation, conceptual understanding, task organization, and perceived support for learning. The final scale included 10 items that used a Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The range of the scale is from 10 to 50, with higher scores representing more use of AI in educational settings. This is a research-developed scale, and there are no pre-established cut-off points. The scores were therefore interpreted using the mean score of the overall scale relative to the scale midpoint [29], as would be done in interpreting scores from other similar mean-based Likert-type scales. The Content Validity Index (CVI) was used to determine the content validity of the AI-based educational tool usage scale. A panel of seven experts was formed, including three educational technology experts and four nursing education faculty members who have experience in the integration of AI in educational settings.

All 10 items were rated independently by each expert on a 4-point scale from 1 (not relevant) to 4 (highly relevant) in terms of relevance, clarity, and representativeness of the construct being measured. The Item-level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) was determined for each item by dividing the number of experts that rated the item as relevant (3 or 4) by the total number of experts. The Scale-level Content Validity Index (S-CVI) was 0.92, which was excellent content validity [30]. The individual item I-CVI scores for all items ranged from 0.86 to 1.00, which is higher than the recommended score of 0.78 for a panel of seven or more experts [31]. To ensure face validity, the items were subsequently pilot tested with undergraduate nursing students, who reviewed them for clarity and comprehension and for their relevance to their nursing education experience with AI tools. The scale was finalized with minor wording changes as a result of their feedback prior to administration of the final version. The scale was found to be reliable in this study, with a Cronbach alpha of 0.84; thus, it is acceptable for measuring AI-based educational tool usage (Supplementary File 1: AI-Based Educational Tool Usage Scale).

Before filling out the AI-based educational tool usage scale, the participants were shown a short introduction statement about the AI tools in relation to this research. Some of the tools that were mentioned included large language model chatbots (such as ChatGPT, Google Gemini, and Microsoft Copilot), writing assistants that leverage AI (like Grammarly), and AI-driven search platforms (e.g., Perplexity AI). This was intended to ensure a consistent and shared conceptual understanding of AI-based educational tools among all participants.

Ethical considerations

The study has been approved by the institutional ethics committee and followed the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. All the participants gave their consent digitally by accepting the detailed description of the study’s purpose, confidentiality, and voluntary nature. The data also ensured anonymity and privacy for the participants. The participants were also informed that they can withdraw at any time. For the physical or mental health exclusion criterion, this was identified by a non-intrusive question that required the participant to respond yes or no, beyond this one item no clinical diagnosis, condition type, or personal health information was collected. The method was created to ensure the privacy of the participants, to avoid stigmatization and to decrease the sensitivity of the disclosure of mental health problems, according to ethical research principles.

Data analysis

The analysis of the data was carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25. To describe the participants’ characteristics and their responses to the study scales, descriptive statistics such as means, standard deviations, and percentages were utilized. To investigate the association between the use of AI, academic procrastination, time management, and academic achievement, Pearson correlation coefficients were used. To identify the factors that predict academic procrastination, time management, and academic achievement, multiple linear regression analysis was carried out. Before the regression analysis, the assumptions of multiple linear regression analyses were tested. Independence of errors was tested using the Durbin-Watson statistic. It varied between 1.89 and 2.03. To test multicollinearity of the data set, the variance inflation factor (VIF) range varied between 1.12 and 2.48. Hence, there is no multicollinearity in the data set. To test the normality of residuals, the normal probability plot of the residuals and the histogram of residuals indicated a normal distribution of the residuals.

Discussion

This study aimed to examine the association between AI educational tool usage, procrastination, academic achievement, and time management among undergraduate nursing students. Participants reported high levels of AI tool usage in their learning, particularly in organizing their studies, understanding complex ideas, and building their confidence. It is believed that AI tools can assist with cognitive abilities and study planning. This is also in line with general studies that suggest that AI is used in a supportive role in learning [32–34].

Interestingly, age was a significant factor related to the use of AI, with students aged 22–23 having the highest level of interaction with AI tools. This finding suggests that students are more likely to use AI as they advance academically, possibly due to various related factors. The increased academic and clinical rigor of the advanced years of nursing education, such as more complex clinical rotations, can lead to increased use of AI tools as coping and organizational strategies [35].

Moreover, older students may have more advanced self-regulation abilities and digital literacy gained from their previous academic experiences, which can facilitate their more effective and meaningful use of AI tools in their learning processes [3, 36]. This is consistent with developmental theories of self-regulated learning that propose that self-regulatory capacity grows as a function of academic experiences and cognitive maturity [36, 37]. The study needs to be replicated to determine if academic maturity is a true indicator of self-regulation or is mainly due to the increase in workload requirements.

The mean value for the GPS for nursing students shows that they have a moderate level of procrastination. The results are in line with previous research that has reported similar results for nursing students. A previous study [25] has reported similar results for undergraduate students for the GPS. The results for GPS show that nursing students have a moderate level of procrastination, which is common in academic settings, but it has an adverse effect on academic success and well-being [38]. Among Jordanian nursing students, the moderate level of procrastination found in this study can be related to the intensive nature of the Jordanian nursing program, which requires students to take many theoretical courses and complete mandatory clinical rotations, leading to high cognitive load and academic pressure [36].

Similarly, TMQ also reveals that nursing students have moderate time management skills. Similar results were found in medical and nursing students [28, 29]. This is consistent with the broader Arab cultural and educational context in Jordan, where family and social obligations characteristic of collectivist Arab culture may compete with academic time allocation, making independent time structuring particularly challenging for university students [39]. Additionally, formal time management skill development is rarely incorporated as a standalone component of nursing curricula in Jordan, further contributing to moderate skill levels in this domain [39]. Moderate TMQ scores indicate the need for interventions about the development of planning and prioritization skills, which are extremely crucial in effectively managing the academic and clinical workloads of the students [40].

Conducting multiple linear regression analyses yields results that support the role of AI usage and time management skills that are associated with reducing academic procrastination. The findings indicate a relationship between higher levels of AI usage and lower levels of procrastination; this relationship does not necessarily mean that participants acquired skills or improved self-regulation [6, 13, 14]. AI tools might either complete academic tasks or make academic work easier, thereby hiding the problem of procrastination, not solving it. Students who heavily depend on AI to start, organize, or complete academic tasks may seem to be less procrastinating behaviorally, but with a lack of intrinsic motivation and independent task-initiation skills, they may not be able to sustain academic success [13, 14].

In nursing education, the difference between completing a task with the help of AI and making real behavioral changes is crucial, as it is essential for students to learn to make independent decisions in clinical settings, which is a key part of their professional development [8, 20]. Longitudinal designs and qualitative approaches are needed in future studies to identify whether there is a real change in self-regulation due to the use of AI tools or if it is simply a coping mechanism for procrastination tendencies [3, 37]. Additionally, AI tools may be associated with reduced academic procrastination through various techniques, such as providing customized support and engagement. This can help students complete their assignments and projects on time [41, 42].

It is important to note that in this study, the use of AI tools was measured as a general construct rather than separately distinguishing between the different types of AI tools (e.g., chatbots, writing assistants, and intelligent tutoring systems), and future research should explore whether there are differential relationships between individual categories of AI tools and procrastination, time management, and academic achievement outcomes in nursing students.

In the context of Jordanian nursing education, the negative correlation between AI use and procrastination could be attributed to the high cognitive load due to both theoretical and clinical demands [35, 43]. Cognitively overloaded students are more likely to avoid tasks, and AI tools that simplify content and provide summary and structure revision could help to make the task less daunting, thereby decreasing avoidance and the resulting procrastination [6, 41]. Furthermore, as many Jordanian nursing students are commuters from home and have family obligations, the flexibility and adaptability of AI tools, which can be used anytime on their smartphones, could be a valuable support system that lessens the risk of delaying nursing studies because of time or access limitations [19].

In a similar vein, the strong negative correlation between time management and academic procrastination points to the theoretical and empirical association that has been consistently demonstrated in the literature on self-regulation. Thus, effective time management was associated with students’ ability to strategically plan, organize, and prioritize their academic responsibilities, which in turn reduces the probability of academic procrastination and last-minute cramming. Past studies have demonstrated that students with effective time management skills experience a corresponding reduction in academic procrastination and enhancement in self-efficacy that translates to improved academic performance [36, 44]. Moreover, interventions aimed at improving time management skills have been effective in reducing academic procrastination by fostering greater control over academic routines and reducing environmental distractions [45].

The utilization of AI-based educational tools was significantly associated with students’ ability to effectively manage their time. This is in line with several recent studies that highlighted the positive effects of AI utilization on students’ productivity and their ability to effectively manage their time. It has been highlighted that high utilization of AI has a significant positive association with students’ productivity [46]; for instance, ChatGPT has a significant positive association with students’ productivity and their ability to effectively manage their time, notwithstanding challenges that may be associated with it.

Likewise, AI tools play a crucial role in personalized learning and improving study habits by enhancing time management and reducing study hours without compromising performance [47]. Additionally, technological interventions such as chatbot systems have been effective in assisting students in the management of their schedules and tasks. This not only promotes the development of effective time management but also the cultivation of persistence, which are significant factors in the attainment of long-term academic success [48].

Among Jordanian university students, the relationship between AI and learning may be even more relevant, as the use of AI is still largely informal and driven by students rather than institutional integration [19]. Students who actively engage in using AI in their studies are likely to be more self-regulated and motivated, which is an independent predictor of effective time management [36, 44]. On the other hand, the organizational and planning capabilities of AI tools, such as creating study plans, outlining the content of revision by topic, and prioritizing revision content, can directly support the time management behaviors that are not explicitly taught or reinforced in the formal nursing curriculum [3, 40].

The results from the multiple linear regression analysis identified three key factors that significantly predict academic achievement. These factors include hours spent studying, AI use, and time management. The additional hours spent studying positively relate to academic achievement. Extensive literature supports the idea that additional time spent studying positively correlates with knowledge acquisition. Structured study habits have been identified as strongly correlated with GPA in medical students [49], where disciplined individual time spent studying is crucial for academic achievement. Moreover, the amount of time spent attending classes, self-studying, and group study has emerged as a significant predictor of better academic performance [50].

The well-established importance of time management in academics aligns with its status as a significant predictor. The time management disposition plays a mediating role in the relationship between balanced time perspective and academic performance, especially in resilient students [51].

This is consistent with studies that have found that poor time management skills were negatively associated with academic performance, especially when combined with other factors such as excessive social media usage [52] and poor planning [53]. On the other hand, demographic factors such as gender, age, academic year level, and course load were not found to have any statistical impact. This is also in line with other studies that have found that academic success is not associated with demographic factors, especially when compared to other factors such as behavior and cognitive abilities [54]. It implies that interventions aimed at improving study habits, technology incorporation, and time management skills could offer more general benefits despite demographic variations.

The importance of AI usage as a predictor of academic performance indicates that students can benefit from academic advantages if they can harness the potential of AI in an effective manner. In a similar vein, AI has been found to promote personalized learning and testing, associated with better GPAs [47]. However, they also caution against over-reliance on AI to the exclusion of conventional learning. Moreover, access to technology does not necessarily enhance academic performance [55]. Although AI has predictive potential for students’ grades, it should not be used in isolation from other motivational and environmental factors [37].

Within nursing education in Jordan, the positive correlation between AI use and academic performance could be attributed to the content-focused nature of nursing exams, which tend to be knowledge-based and demand students to learn vast amounts of biomedical and clinical information in English [35, 56]. In this regard, AI tools that can help with content summarization, concept clarification, and exam preparation can provide specific academic benefits. This aligns with the evidence of positive attitudes towards ChatGPT as an academic support tool among health students, where they mentioned using the AI tool to improve their understanding of complex academic content [19, 57]. Additionally, health students in Jordan have shown greater familiarity and acceptance of AI tools in their academic work [35], indicating a positive attitude toward using AI in learning, which could further enhance academic achievement.

The finding that study hours were the strongest predictor of GPA in this Jordanian sample is in line with the general assessment culture in Jordanian higher education, which is mostly based on written examinations that are high-stakes and focus on recalling vast amounts of content [35]. In this context, AI seems to be a study supplement, not a substitute: It helps to understand and organize the material, but it is the time spent on traditional study that is the primary factor in achievement [47, 55]. The complementary rather than substitutive nature of AI is a key point for educators and policymakers in Jordan who might be worried about AI affecting the rigor of academic work [19, 37].

Implications

This study also demonstrates the potential of AI tools in transforming the academic behaviors and performances of nursing students. For the academic community, it is recommended that AI tools be integrated into the academic strategies and approaches used in the classroom to promote academic engagement, prevent procrastination, and facilitate academic time management. For the policy community, it is recommended that guidelines be established to promote the institutional use of AI technologies in the academic community, including funding for the development of the digital infrastructure and faculty development initiatives. National guidelines could also be established to promote the academic use of AI tools in the curriculum, optimizing academic performance and preparedness in the healthcare education landscape.

Limitations

The cross-sectional design and self-reported data may create bias and be unable to prove cause and effect. Convenience sampling from one institution in the north of Jordan is accepted due to pragmatic and contextual reasons; however, this may affect the generalizability of the results to other nursing programs, cultural context, and/or educational systems. While efforts were made to reduce selection bias (e.g., cross-year recruitment, voluntary participation, and anonymous data collection), it is not possible to rule out self-selection bias, as students with more engagement with AI would have been more likely to participate. For future research, probability-based sampling across various institutions and geographical areas is recommended to improve the representativeness and generalizability of the results.

In addition, no distinction was made in the study between types of operating systems, devices (e.g., smartphone versus laptop), or platforms (e.g., Google Edge versus Google Chrome) when using the AI tools, which could affect accessibility and user experience. These variables should be captured in future studies to enhance the contextual interpretability and replicability of findings. A further limitation of the present study is the lack of differentiation among the specific types of AI-based educational tools used by participants. The study measured the general use of AI tools, rather than specific types of tools, such as large language model chatbots (e.g., ChatGPT, Google Gemini), AI writing assistants (e.g., Grammarly), AI-powered search platforms (e.g., Perplexity AI), and intelligent tutoring systems that differ widely in their capabilities, complexity, and cognitive demands. The lack of such distinctions may have affected the generalizability of results to the specific type of tools. Future studies should explore the individual and comparative relationships between types of AI tools and academic behavioral outcomes for nursing students.

Furthermore, the AI-based educational tool usage scale was a researcher-developed scale that has been proved to have acceptable validity and reliability but has not been fully validated with construct validity and factor analysis, which should be further examined in future studies. Also, there was no minimum number of times participants had used any AI tools to possibly account for a variance in how much they had engaged with AI tools. Recommendations for future studies include the use of more precise operational definitions of AI based educational tools usage experience to promote sample homogeneities.

Conclusion

The present study aimed to examine the associations of AI-based educational tools, academic procrastination, achievement, and time management skills in nursing students. There was a positive relationship between AI tools usage and time management skills and a negative relationship between academic procrastination and academic achievement. The findings suggest that AI tools may support academic engagement and learning behaviors among nursing students. Furthermore, it is also evident that aspects such as learning habits and time management are more critical in determining academic success than demographic factors. This reiterates the necessity for academics to transcend traditional learning methods and fully leverage technology to enhance learning practices. Considering the previously mentioned aspects, incorporating AI tools into nursing education and educating students on their use may be linked with the improved learning experiences. In this context, future initiatives must also prioritize the creation of policies that guarantee students’ access to AI tools.
