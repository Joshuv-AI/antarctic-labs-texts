Contemporary prospects of youth sports and social integration among adolescents
Authors: Huang Zijian, Wei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSports events conducted in conjunction with the curriculum may serve as a supportive factor in the development of social integration among adolescents attending school. The aim of this study is to examine how adolescent participation in various sports activities, specifically football, volleyball, athletics, and tennis, affects their level of social integration and social engagement. The study involved 400 adolescents (aged 13–15) from a single school in Ukraine. Data were collected using the Social Integration Scale (SIS-A) and the Social Engagement Scale (SCE-A), which were specifically developed for this research. The study employed a pre-test/post-test design with control and intervention groups over the course of one academic year. The results indicate that sports-based interventions were associated with improvements in social integration, with a significant increase in SIS-A scores across the four sports subgroups (p = .000), whereas the control group showed no significant changes. However, no significant improvements in social engagement were found in any group, including those involved in sports. This suggests that while sport may support social integration, it does not necessarily contribute to broader civic participation, particularly in the absence of explicit civic education or community-oriented activities within the intervention. The highest observed effects were in the football subgroup. Volleyball participation led to moderate improvements in social integration, while individual sports (e.g., athletics, tennis) had comparatively smaller effects. These findings underscore the importance of distinguishing between different dimensions of social development and highlight the need for more targeted approaches to enhance civic engagement among adolescents. Introduction

Sport is an important part of modern life, serving not only to promote physical development but also to support individuals’ integration into existing social groups1,2. However, the ongoing urbanization and increasing dependence on digital technology have contributed to a noticeable decline in physical activity among children and adolescents3. This lack of physical activity can lead to a range of negative consequences, including obesity, decreased motor development, social withdrawal, and reduced psychological resilience. In response to this challenge, education authorities are faced with the task of encouraging young people to join sports clubs and extracurricular physical activity programs4 where they can build interpersonal connections, develop leadership skills, and realize their full potential5,6. Team sports such as soccer, volleyball, and basketball are often credited with teaching adolescents how to collaborate, communicate effectively, and demonstrate trust and respect toward teammates7. At the same time, individual sports like tennis and swimming also contribute to important aspects of personal growth, such as self-discipline, perseverance, and a sense of responsibility8. Rather than viewing these categories as mutually exclusive, it is important to recognize that both team and individual sports foster a range of cognitive, emotional, and social outcomes, albeit through different mechanisms. Participating in competitions and training with peers allows adolescents to build friendships, expand their social networks, and develop a sense of community9. In addition, sports activities are often linked to broader social initiatives, such as charity tournaments or campaigns for inclusivity, which provide opportunities for young people to engage more actively in civic life10. Through such experiences, adolescents may not only improve their self-confidence and emotional resilience but also be motivated to volunteer, raise awareness about social issues, or contribute to public welfare11–13. However, the promotion of youth sports requires consistent institutional support and integration into educational and national development strategies14,15. Without such support, the potential of sport as a tool for holistic development, including social integration and civic participation, may remain underutilized.

Youth sports provide opportunities for young people to improve physical health, achieve harmonious development, and adopt prosocial behaviors16. Due to the health benefits of moderate physical activity, including improved physical well-being and a reduced risk of various diseases, amateur sports have become increasingly attractive17. As a leisure activity, sport may also offer adolescents a meaningful alternative to alcohol and substance abuse, delinquency, and antisocial behavior12. Sporting teams and clubs are often integrated into the fabric of local communities, and athletes frequently take part in public life through charity events and volunteer projects18–20. These initiatives may serve as platforms for adolescents to contribute to collective goals and gain experience in civic life21. Most existing studies focus on professional or elite adult athletes22, leaving a gap in our understanding of how sport participation affects youth in school-based or amateur settings. Furthermore, a limited number of studies explore the relationship between adolescent sport participation and social integration, and those that do often address narrow case studies or use non-comparable methodologies12,19,21,23,24. As a result, more research is needed to determine how different types of sports may influence the development of social skills, a sense of belonging, and civic-mindedness among young people. Such evidence is crucial for the creation of effective youth programs that address not only physical development but also broader social outcomes.

While the role of sport in promoting physical health and overall socialization is widely recognized, there remains a significant lack of empirical research that differentiates the impact of team and individual sports on specific aspects of adolescent social development, namely social integration and social engagement. Existing studies often focus on professional or adult athletes, overlooking school-based or amateur sports contexts for youth, and tend to treat social integration and engagement as interchangeable constructs. As a result, there is insufficient evidence regarding how different forms of sports participation distinctly influence adolescents’ sense of belonging, peer interaction, civic awareness, and involvement in community life. The aim of this study is to fill this gap by providing a differentiated empirical analysis of these constructs among adolescents aged 13–15, thereby contributing to both theoretical understanding and practical application in youth development programs. The findings of this research may inform the development of educational programs that not only improve social skills and a sense of belonging but also promote active participation in civic and community life.

Theoretical basis

The concept of social integration used in this study refers to the process through which individuals are incorporated into pre-existing networks and structures of social relationships. As originally formulated by Émile Durkheim in the late nineteenth century, social integration plays a crucial role in maintaining social cohesion and preventing alienation25,26. Durkheim emphasized the importance of shared norms and collective consciousness as factors that bind individuals to society. In the context of adolescence, social integration is particularly significant as it supports the development of a sense of belonging, attachment to peers, and constructive group affiliation27,28.

Contemporary scholars expand on this perspective, arguing that integration occurs through active participation in structured group activities, including educational and extracurricular areas such as sports29,30. Team sports, in particular, are viewed as "microcosms of society," where young people can rehearse social roles and develop cooperative behaviors31,32. Participation in such an environment provides adolescents with the opportunity to learn trust, mutual support, and conflict resolution—key elements in the integration process. In contrast, social engagement is understood here as active, voluntary participation in public or civic activities that extend beyond personal or group interests33. It encompasses forms of participation such as volunteering, involvement in cultural events, and contributions to community projects or initiatives aimed at social improvement34,35. As such, social engagement is linked to the concepts of civic responsibility and the development of a sense of agency in public processes18. Although these concepts—social integration and engagement—are interconnected, they represent different dimensions of social development. Integration concerns the internalization of group belonging and interpersonal connections, while engagement reflects an outward orientation toward the broader community and the acceptance of social responsibility. Thus, the two constructs may respond differently to the same interventions. Empirical research has demonstrated that youth participation in sports can support both of these processes9,10,12,19. However, existing studies remain fragmented, with most focusing on elite athletes or adults and only a limited number exploring adolescents in school or amateur settings21,22. Moreover, much of the literature tends to conflate integration with engagement or assumes that improvements in one automatically lead to the other without empirical verification of this connection. This study addresses these theoretical and empirical gaps by evaluating the differentiated effects of team and individual sports on social integration and engagement among adolescents aged 13–15.

Problem statement

The aim of this study is to investigate the impact of participation in targeted sports disciplines, including two team sports (football and volleyball) and two individual sports (athletics and tennis), on social integration and social engagement among adolescents. Despite the widespread belief in the positive influence of sport on youth social development, there is a lack of empirical data in the scientific literature that differentiates the impact of specific disciplines while simultaneously considering social integration and engagement as distinct concepts. It is hypothesized that participation in sports events can contribute to the development of social connections and active involvement in community life among adolescents. Additionally, the study aims to identify which discipline most significantly supports these processes. The research objectives were as follows:

To examine the level of social integration and social engagement among adolescents before and after participation in a sports program;

To examine the level of social integration and social engagement among adolescents before and after participation in a sports program;

To compare the results between the control and experimental groups, as well as pre-and post-intervention;

To compare the results between the control and experimental groups, as well as pre-and post-intervention;

To determine whether the impact on social indicators differs depending on the type of sport;

To determine whether the impact on social indicators differs depending on the type of sport;

To identify which of the four disciplines has the most positive effect on the social integration and engagement of adolescents.

To identify which of the four disciplines has the most positive effect on the social integration and engagement of adolescents.

Materials and methods

The instruments of this study are the Social Integration Scale for Adolescents (SIS-A) and the Social Engagement Scale for Adolescents (SCE-A). Each instrument has been developed specifically for the current study, as no suitable method was found for measuring social integration and social engagement in adolescents. The SIS-A consists of 14 items, divided into 7-item subscales: Sense of Belonging (SOB) and Interaction with Peers (PI). Similarly, the SCE-A contains 14 items, divided into two subscales: Community Contribution (CC) and Civic Activism (CA). The complete list of items is available in Appendix 1. Both measures use a 5-point Likert scale.

The conceptual framework for designing the instruments encompasses the core concepts of social integration and social engagement. During the assessment of content validity, a panel of qualified experts assessed whether the language of the questionnaires was appropriate and clear. The underlying factor structures of SIS-A and SCE-A were confirmed through factor analysis. A pilot study with 25 adolescents yielded data for further refinement of the questionnaires. The final assessment revealed the high test–retest reliability of both instruments. The Cronbach’s alpha values for SIS-A and SCE-A were 0.89 and 0.87, respectively, indicating high internal consistency. To assess construct validity, an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted using principal axis factoring with oblique rotation for the SIS-A and SCE-A instruments. The resulting factor structures supported the hypothesized subscales, with item loadings exceeding 0.60 on their respective factors.

Participants

The study involved 400 adolescents aged 13–15 years from a general secondary school in Ukraine. All students in the specified age group were preliminarily informed about the objectives and content of the project during a specially organized meeting. Participation in the study was open to students who provided verbal consent and submitted written permission from their parents or legal guardians. The inclusion criteria were regular attendance of physical education classes, overall satisfactory health status, absence of musculoskeletal disorders, and other functional limitations that would prevent full participation in sports activities. Additionally, participants were required to have no significant psycho-emotional disorders that would interfere with group interaction. The exclusion criteria included the presence of chronic or acute illnesses that would hinder participation in physical activity; recent injuries, surgeries, or medical restrictions; regular attendance at extracurricular sports clubs or sections that could influence the outcomes of the study; and refusal of the student or parents to participate in the project. All eligible students were randomly assigned to two equal groups: the sports group and the control group. All participants provided verbal consent, and their parents provided written informed consent, which included permission for data processing in accordance with the principles of anonymity and confidentiality. The study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration. Anonymity and confidentiality were guaranteed.

Study design

The intervention design was based on a quasi-experimental approach with pre- and post-testing. The experimental intervention was conducted from September 2022 to June 2023, inclusive, excluding official school holidays (autumn, winter, and spring breaks) during which no training sessions were held. The total duration of the active intervention phase was 30 weeks. Prior to the start of the program, all participants completed the SIS-A (Social Integration Scale) and SCE-A (Social Engagement Scale) questionnaires, after which they were randomly assigned to groups. A test for sample homogeneity was conducted following the group assignment.

The control group continued to attend standard physical education classes twice a week (45 min per session) throughout the entire period, in accordance with the school timetable, and did not participate in any of the four sports disciplines included in the experimental program.

Participants in the sports (experimental) group, in addition to the mandatory school physical education lessons, attended specialized training sessions three times a week (90 min per session), organized after school hours. As a result, the weekly additional workload for the sports group was 270 min, compared to 90 min for the control group. On average, throughout the entire active intervention period, each participant in the sports group attended approximately 81 training sessions, equating to approximately 121.5 h of additional physical activity.

Participants in the sports group were evenly distributed into four subgroups of 50 individuals based on the type of sport: football, volleyball, athletics, and tennis. Qualified professionals conducted all training sessions: four professional coaches (one for each discipline), two physical education instructors who provided general methodological and disciplinary oversight, and two educational sociologists responsible for monitoring adherence to the research protocol, ensuring ethical compliance, and organizing the surveys.

The sessions took place in either the gymnasium or outdoors, depending on the discipline and weather conditions. The football and volleyball teams were integrated into the school’s sports teams and additionally participated in interschool competitions. The athletes and tennis players trained both individually and in pairs or small groups, also participating in district and school competitions.

Thus, participants in the sports group simultaneously underwent standard physical education training and took part in a structured sports program aimed at developing physical, communicative, and social skills. Upon completion of the intervention, all participants filled out the SIS-A and SCE-A questionnaires again, and the collected data were stored for further analysis.

Data analysis

The data derived from the two questionnaires was analyzed in SPSS Statistics v24. The paired t-test was used to compare the means of the same group, and the independent samples t-test was used to compare the means of the two groups. The pre-test means of the sport subgroups were compared using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Subsequently, Tukey’s post-hoc analysis was conducted to determine which sports (football, volleyball, track and field, and tennis) were most effective in enhancing social integration and social involvement. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were also calculated for data analysis to assess the practical significance of statistically significant differences.

Social integration

In the control group, changes were minimal and statistically insignificant (p = 0.386 and 0.229, respectively). Discussion

Despite the widespread belief in sport as a catalyst for both integration and civic engagement, the present findings present a more nuanced picture. While participation in sports, particularly in team activities such as football and volleyball, clearly enhanced adolescents’ sense of belonging and peer interaction, it did not have a statistically significant impact on measures of social engagement, such as community contribution and civic activity. This conclusion was further supported by independent t-tests, which revealed no significant differences between the control and sports groups in post-testing results. These findings indicate that while participation in sports may foster peer-level social connections, it does not necessarily extend to broader civic or community-oriented behavior within the given frame. The absence of significant improvement in social engagement may be attributed to the structure of the intervention itself, as the program did not incorporate explicit civic education or structured opportunities for community involvement. These components are critical for fostering civic awareness and participation, and their omission likely limited the potential for measurable changes in adolescents’ social engagement outcomes. This limitation should be carefully considered in the design of future interventions.

This outcome may be attributed to the structure and focus of the intervention itself. While sport naturally encourages communication and collaboration, the lack of explicit components aimed at developing civic awareness, social responsibility, or community involvement likely limited its impact on broader social participation. Adolescents may benefit from programs that combine sports participation with structured civic education, volunteering opportunities, or discussions on social issues, to significantly enhance their community engagement. These results are consistent with previous studies36,37, which suggest that while sport can contribute to personal growth and peer integration, it does not automatically lead to increased civic or public activity without targeted educational content. Thus, sport can serve as a valuable foundation for social development, but additional efforts are necessary to fully unlock its potential in fostering an active civic mindset.

Previous research points out a close connection between social cohesiveness and intra-team communication, with acceptance and conflict communication as key predictors10. These findings are consistent with the current study. Here, participation in sports of any form, most notably team sports, was beneficial for improving social integration in the adolescent sports context, which shows that sports are more than just a means to achieve physical development. They act as a platform for strengthening social connections and mutual understanding. Sports education was reported to be more effective in building a sense of empowerment and confidence than traditional teaching8, which further emphasizes the importance of participation in sports competitions, both in extracurricular programs and in traditional physical education programs, which also include structured physical exercises and contribute to achieving similar developmental outcomes. Some researchers discovered that through cooperative action, team sports could promote personal growth, confidence, and social adaptation in players8; these findings complement the current study. Some scholars speculated that sports can help achieve social inclusion and investigated the prospect through a series of qualitative interviews with parents living in predominantly working-class communities11. Their findings are consistent with the current study, which, in turn, highlights the need for a more differentiated approach to the selection of sports programs.

This study provides evidence that sports participation improves social integration among adolescents, highlighting the significant effects of soccer and volleyball. The physical and psychosocial benefits of participation in youth sports vary depending on the quality of the sporting experience, as described in the previous study12. The current work echoes this discovery by showing that not all sporting activities are equal in terms of impact. At the same time, sports participation is not the only factor influencing adolescents’ physical activity. The social-ecological contexts also matter13. Although sports participation alone did not enhance social engagement, this study did not explore other contributing factors, such as family and community support, which should be considered in future work13. According to some researchers, sports participation can improve prosocial behaviors, thus deepening social interactions19. This study provides similar results.

While previous studies have demonstrated that sports interventions can enhance life skills25, psychosocial well-being38, and mental health39, the present study focused specifically on social integration and engagement. Within this narrower scope, the current findings support the notion that sport can contribute to social integration among adolescents. Another study from a more critical perspective suggests that sports participation can both increase and limit social inclusion opportunities for youth29. This discovery highlights the complex and multifaceted nature of the relationship between sports and social integration, with social efficacy as a potential intermediary factor31. Using multiple indicators and multiple causes, some researchers explored the influence of school engagement, self-esteem, and depression on adolescents’ sports participation22. Their findings provide confirmatory evidence that extracurricular sports activities are crucial for social integration. Other factors reported to have a significant effect on adolescents’ sports participation are patriotism, volunteerism, and perceived empowerment19. The current study, however, failed to find any meaningful connection between sports participation and social engagement. Therefore, additional research is needed. Assumingly, the said mismatch stems from the fact that adolescents and mature adults have different perspectives on social engagement.

Limitations

The study only involved students from a single school in Ukraine, which makes the extrapolation process difficult. In addition, the study relies on self-report data, which is subjective. It should also be noted that individual variables, such as participants’ personality traits, the presence or absence of pre-existing friendships, and the socio-economic status of their families, were not controlled for in this study. These factors may influence the level of social integration and partially explain the observed differences between the groups. Additionally, while this study focused on the effects of sports participation, it did not assess other potentially important factors contributing to social engagement, such as family influence, peer support, or institutional involvement. The absence of these variables should be considered a limitation, and future research should adopt a more comprehensive approach to understanding the multifaceted nature of adolescent civic participation. Another limitation is the time frame of the study—the changes were analyzed only over one academic year. The long-term effects of participation in sports programs on adolescent social integration and engagement were not explored. Moreover, the unequal gender distribution within the sports subgroups may have influenced peer interaction dynamics, representing a potential limitation. Additionally, as the study focuses solely on short-term outcomes, future research should pursue longitudinal designs to examine the sustainability of social integration effects and the potential delayed development of civic engagement.

Conclusions

This study identified statistically significant associations between adolescent participation in sports competitions and social integration variables, specifically feelings of belonging and peer interaction (p = 0.000). In contrast, no substantial changes were observed regarding social engagement. Specifically, measures of community contribution and civic activity did not show statistically significant improvements in the comparison groups, regardless of the intervention conditions. These results suggest that while sports may play a supportive role in strengthening interpersonal relationships and enhancing peer group integration, such effects should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal. Furthermore, the analysis revealed significant differences in levels of social integration after the intervention among the four sports subgroups, as indicated by ANOVA. The most notable associations were observed in the football subgroup, followed by volleyball, while individual sports such as athletics and tennis had comparatively weaker associations. These differences may reflect the cooperative nature of team sports, which can facilitate communication, collaboration, and mutual support among participants; however, they might also be influenced by unmeasured contextual variables such as coaching style, peer group dynamics, or pre-existing social ties.

It is essential to emphasize that, despite statistical significance, the study design does not allow for causal inferences. Therefore, the findings should be viewed as indicative of relationships, not deterministic effects. This study contributes to the existing literature by providing empirical evidence of the nuanced connections between sports participation and aspects of social development among adolescents. While participation in both team and individual sports can be linked to improved social integration, the unchanged levels of social engagement highlight the need for additional educational or civic components to encourage broader participation in community life. These findings can inform educational institutions, sports clubs, community organizations, curriculum developers, and policymakers who aim to promote social integration through sports, provided they account for the contextual limitations and avoid overstating causal conclusions.
