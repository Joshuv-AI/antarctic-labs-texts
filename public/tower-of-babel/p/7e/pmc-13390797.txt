Metalloproteins in an Era of Modern Crystallography and Why the Beamline Matters
Authors: Ailiena O Maggiolo, Aina E Cohen
Published: 2026

Introduction

The Structural Molecular Biology (SMB) macromolecular crystallography (MC) group at the Stanford Synchrotron Radiation Lightsource (SSRL) have developed state-of-the-art capabilities tailored for metalloenzyme structural analysis. Metalloproteins sit at the center of biology’s most audacious chemistry. From multi-electron redox catalysis to radical rearrangements and light-driven transformations, metal sites give proteins access to reaction landscapes that would otherwise be inaccessible under ambient conditions. Yet their study presents a fundamental paradox for MC studies: the very X-rays we use to reveal atomic structure can alter the electronic states we seek to understand. As the field moves beyond static snapshots toward mechanistic insight, success increasingly depends on our ability to maintain metal centers in their native state throughout the experiment. The SSRL SMB-MC beamlines integrate a suite of capabilities specifically designed to address these challenges. By combining in situ spectroscopic verification, intelligent dose management, controlled reaction initiation, optimized anomalous diffraction, and real-time crystallographic diffraction analysis, these tools enable researchers to interrogate metalloproteins with unprecedented rigor. This article explores how these complementary approaches are reshaping our ability to capture metalloprotein chemistry, and what this means for mechanistic studies at synchrotron beamlines.

The challenge of metalloprotein crystallography

Over the last few decades, crystallography beamlines have made remarkable strides in data rate and accessibility. Detectors became faster, robotics became routine, remote access became dependable, and automated pipelines lowered the barriers to collecting high-quality datasets. For understanding metalloprotein function, however, speed and convenience are not enough.

Many metalloprotein active sites exist in delicate redox equilibria, where a single electron can mean the difference between a resting state and a reaction intermediate. When we expose these systems to the intense X-ray beams required for high-resolution diffraction, we risk photoreducing metal centers, (such as copper sites or iron-sulfur clusters), or generating hydroxyl radicals that alter the surrounding active site environment. If X-rays alter metal oxidation states during data collection, the resulting structures will represent artifacts of radiation damage rather than biology. As we push toward understanding reaction mechanisms at the atomic level, these distinctions become critical.

What’s needed is a beamline environment that treats oxidation state verification, dose tracking, and complementary spectroscopy as essential experimental parameters, to make rigorous metalloprotein crystallography not merely possible, but routine, with tools that let users see what is happening to their crystals while they collect diffraction. This article describes how the SSRL crystallography program aligns hardware, software, and experimental strategies around the needs of metalloprotein science, especially in an era where time-resolved studies, multi-modal data collection, and remote-access workflows are redefining what beam time can achieve. These include automation for metal identification, spectroscopic validation, decision-making workflows, room-temperature experiments, serial data collection, and time-resolved crystallography including photolysis and photo-triggering methods. These components form an integrated approach designed to preserve the chemical integrity of metal sites while extracting the structural information that crystallography uniquely provides.

SSRL macromolecular crystallography beamlines

The SSRL SMB-MC program has been a long-standing driver of automation and remote operation in synchrotron MC [1, 2]. Today, SSRL operates four fully remote-access crystallography beamlines (BL9–2, BL12–1, BL12–2, and BL14–1) equipped for high throughput, reproducible data collection. BL12–1 and BL12–2 (Figure 1) are high-flux undulator microfocus beamlines delivering up to 4 × 1012 p/s to adjustable focal spots as small as 5 × 35 μm2 and with smaller collimated beam sizes available. BL9–2 provides a stable beam with an integrated remote-accessible microspectrophotometer for correlated UV-visible spectroscopic and diffraction studies. BL14–1 is a bending magnet beamline, ideal for routine and high throughput data collection. Core capabilities across all beamlines include rapid robotic sample exchange via the Stanford Automated Mounter (SAM), automated loop centering, automated energy tuning, and fast low-dose raster-based crystal identification and evaluation, features that reduce experimental overhead while increasing effective throughput. Beamline control and data-collection workflows are integrated through the Blu-Ice user interface [2], enabling researchers to execute complex experiments reliably from off site. Real-time data evaluation integrated into Blu-Ice allows users to adjust collection strategies on the fly, an essential capability for metalloprotein studies where diffraction quality or radiation sensitivity can vary widely across samples.

Intelligent strategies for radiation damage mitigation

Radiation chemistry is often the dominant experimental confounder in metalloprotein crystallography studies, where site-specific photoreduction and ligand loss can occur well before global diffraction degradation is apparent. SSRL MC beamlines therefore emphasize dose-aware data collection, where strategy is informed by quantitative dose estimates and by continuous assessment of diffraction quality.

X-ray dose is estimated a priori using RADDOSE-3D [3], which is incorporated into the Blu-Ice/DCS beamline control system (Figure 1 [inset]) [2]. This system combines crystal parameters (dimensions, composition, etc.) and beamline parameters (beam size, flux, etc.) to model the absorbed dose distribution within the irradiated volume. This enables rational selection of exposure time, attenuation, oscillation width, and total rotation range before committing valuable crystals to a full dataset [4]. Dose minimization is further supported by shutterless helical and multi-position data collection strategies, which distributes exposure along the crystal volume to reduce peak dose at any single part of the crystal. During helical data collection, an in-house–developed micro-crystal goniometer (sphere of confusion ±0.5 μm) synchronizes motion across four motorized axes to translate the crystal along a user-defined trajectory. This trajectory is selected via a video-microscope view integrated into an intuitive graphical interface.

Within Blu-Ice, integrated indexing and strategy tools (e.g., MOS-FLM-based modules and related strategy engines) support optimization of rotation start angle and data-collection geometry to achieve high completeness or multiplicity early in the experiment [5], which is an approach that is particularly valuable when radiation sensitivity limits the total dose budget. For anomalous diffraction experiments, strategy calculations can be configured to prioritize anomalous multiplicity and signal-to-noise, enabling users to balance anomalous completeness against dose constraints.

During diffraction data acquisition, real-time image evaluation provides metrics such as estimated diffraction resolution, spot quality indicators, and frame-to-frame decay trends that inform tactical decisions (e.g., whether to extend a sweep, terminate early, adjust exposure, or translate to a fresh region) (Figure 1 [inset]). AI-guided algorithms estimate maximum resolution, spot count, and split-spot detection, and assign per-image quality metrics during data collection, enabling direct comparison of predicted dose with observed radiation damage effects [6]. In parallel, automated data processing (e.g., via autoPROC, incorporated into Blu-Ice) runs concurrently with data collection, returning early indicators of completeness and quality [7]. When significant anomalous signal is detected during automated data processing, an additional job is automatically launched to re-run the pipeline with parameters optimized for anomalous signal. Following data collection, radiation damage difference maps can be computed to assess local radiation effects at metal centers, providing a post hoc diagnostic of site-specific damage.

Together, dose estimation, data collection strategies that distribute dose across the crystal, and integrated real-time analysis shift radiation damage from an uncontrolled liability into a manageable experimental variable.

Metal identification and absorption edge optimization via X-ray fluorescence scans

A potential failure mode in metalloprotein crystallography that is often underappreciated is chemical ambiguity. Incomplete metal incorporation, mixed occupancy, unintended cofactors, or heavy-atom soaks that did not take can all compromise structural interpretation. These issues can be addressed by utilizing two complementary fluorescence-based X-ray scan modes that we have integrated into Blu-Ice. Excitation scans can be used to verify metals in the crystal, and energy scans provide absorption edge characterization and optimal wavelength selection for anomalous data collection [8].

In an excitation scan, the incident X-ray energy is set above the absorption edge(s) of interest, and the fluorescence spectrum is collected (using a Vortex-EX90 detector), revealing emission lines from elements present in the crystal. Unlike an energy scan that targets a single edge, excitation scans capture fluorescence from any element with an absorption edge below the chosen excitation energy, providing a fast, highly-informative screen for intrinsic metals, heavy-atom derivatives, or metal-bearing ligands. Within Blu-Ice, excitation scans can be analyzed and compared against expected emission energies for expected elements; candidate matches are annotated by overlaying predicted emission lines onto the spectrum, providing immediate guidance on whether the observed peaks are consistent with the suspected metals [8].

Once an anomalous scatterer is verified, the absorption edge scan mode measures fluorescence intensity as a function of incident energy across a selected absorption edge (K or L edges, as appropriate). This edge-resolved scan is then analyzed by automated tools incorporating software such as autochooch [9] to estimate the dispersive and anomalous scattering factors, f’ and f”, and to propose the canonical wavelength set (e.g., peak (max f”), inflection (min f’), and a remote energy). Importantly, this procedure yields energies optimized for the specific crystal and experimental geometry, rather than relying on tabulated ideal values. This distinction matters because the absorption edge position and profile of a metal can shift by several eV in a protein environment due to coordination chemistry and oxidation state, and energy selection based solely on tabulated elemental values can markedly attenuate, or in some cases effectively eliminate, the anomalous signal.

Measuring the absorption edge directly on the crystal enables sample-specific selection of optimal energies to maximize anomalous signal for phasing and site validation. The Blu-Ice control software further streamlines the workflow by exporting the selected energies directly into the data-collection run definition, enabling users to proceed seamlessly from edge measurement to wavelength-specific data acquisition [8].

Anomalous diffraction as an essential metalloprotein tool

While much of modern crystallography relies on molecular replacement for phase determination, metalloproteins often benefit from experimental phasing methods that exploit the intrinsic anomalous scattering of native metal centers. Single-wavelength and multi-wavelength anomalous diffraction (SAD and MAD) not only provide experimental phases but also definitively establish the number and positions of metal sites, which is critical for mechanistic interpretation.

The SSRL SMB-MC beamlines offer push-button tunable energy ranges from 6 to 18 keV, providing access to K-edges for biologically relevant anomalous scatterers spanning sulfur and selenium through iron, manganese, cobalt, nickel, copper, and zinc. High flux and stable beam delivery at lower energies, particularly important for iron and manganese, enables detection of weak anomalous signals that might be otherwise missed.

Accurate measurement of Bijvoet differences depends on multiplicity and careful measurement of systematics errors. The beamlines therefore support high-redundancy acquisition and inverse-beam strategies, as well as flexible wedge definition and interleaving schemes for multi-wavelength collections [8]. These capabilities are particularly valuable for weak anomalous cases such as native sulfur SAD and for dose-limited crystals where redundancy must be accumulated efficiently.

Dose-dependent absorption edge shifts measured on the crystal can also serve as a quantitative probe of photoreduction, enabling MAD scan-based photoreduction studies to monitor the radiation sensitivity of specific metal centers during data collection.

Inline single-crystal UV-visible absorption spectroscopy

Crystallographers routinely use solution-phase spectroscopy to characterize samples before crystallization, however, the state in the crystal can differ. Crystallization conditions can be reducing, cofactors can dissociate, and crystals sitting in drops for days or weeks may undergo slow chemical changes. Single-crystal spectroscopy addresses this gap by measuring the optical signature of the same crystal used for diffraction, immediately before data collection.

At SSRL BL9–2, a fully remote-access microspectrophotometer (Figure 2(a)) is integrated into Blu-Ice, enabling acquisition of UV-Vis absorbance measurements from mounted crystals with no special preparation beyond standard mounting [10,11]. The system supports several acquisition modes suited to crystallographic use cases, including single-spectrum (“snapshot”) collection, phi-rotation scans to identify the crystal orientation that yields the cleanest spectrum, time-series measurements, and an X-ray exposure mode to quantify radiation-induced spectral changes. UV-Vis spectra can also be interleaved with diffraction during a rotation wedge by enabling MicroSpec mode in the Collect tab and specifying the acquisition frequency, allowing direct correlation of spectral changes with dose and diffraction metrics [11].

This capability is particularly valuable for metalloproteins, where site-specific photoreduction can precede obvious diffraction decay. In practice, users can verify redox- or ligand-dependent band positions (for example, heme Soret shifts or charge-transfer features) before collecting on a crystal, and they can track dose-dependent spectral changes in real time, providing direct evidence for, and a means to mitigate, X-ray-induced chemical perturbation.

In addition to the inline system at BL9–2, anaerobic offline single-crystal UV-Vis spectroscopy capabilities are available for prescreening crystals prior to beam time, enabling users to evaluate the spectroscopic state of their samples independently of beamline scheduling.

In-crystallo photolysis and photo-triggering

One of the most exciting directions in crystallography is capturing dynamic structures along a reaction coordinate. Light-driven reaction initiation, using photolysis to release caged substrates, activate photo-sensitive cofactors, or directly excite chromophores, followed by controlled trapping of intermediates, provides a powerful route to obtaining mechanistic snapshots. The SMB-MC beamlines offer a suite of capabilities that bring these experiments into practice.

Photolysis of caged substrates at cryogenic temperatures

Photocaged substrates or photo-sensitive cofactors can be activated by UV illumination directly within the crystal. SSRL BL9–2 integrates UV illumination with focusing optics that deliver a controlled light spot to the crystal, combined with software-controlled illumination timing and monitoring by UV-Vis spectroscopy. The crystal rotisserie mode available in Blu-Ice rotates crystals during UV illumination while simultaneously recording absorption spectra, providing real-time feedback on photolysis progression and enabling precise exposure control. Further, specialized light sources and controls can be integrated into any of the MC beamlines upon special request.

React-and-release experiments for delayed dynamics

In this approach, photolysis is used to fully liberate all photo-active species within the crystal while frozen at cryogenic temperatures (~100 K). The temperature is then elevated above the solvent glass transition point (~160 K) to allow substrate rearrangement and reaction progression, without ice accumulation or crystal deterioration. The crystal is subsequently cooled back to 100 K for diffraction data collection. By varying the warming time, different reaction intermediates along the catalytic trajectory can be trapped for data collection.

Light-triggering at room temperature with synchronized freeze-trapping

A purpose-built robotic freeze-trapping instrument that incorporates a mechanized nozzle-switching device, coupled with a synchronized fast light shutter and timing generator is available for researchers to freeze-capture intermediates at defined time points as short as 30 ms (Figure 2(b)). The system integrates an exchangeable objective lens equipped with a variable UV-Vis light source, an Oxford Cryosystems 800-series temperature-controlled cryostream, and an Arinax HC-Lab humidity-controller. The freeze-trapping instrument enables rapid interchange between the cryostream at ~100 K and the humid air stream at room temperature, providing precise, reproducible timing for freeze-trapping structures at defined time points after photolysis. Newly developed software and a coupled timing generator synchronize the opening of the light-source shutter with the nozzle switcher, achieving high-precision UV targeting and superb reproducibility for freeze-trapping crystals at selected time points. Safe sample storage following each freeze-trapping step is accomplished using the SAM robotic system, facilitating the generation of many time-points and replicates. Flexible integration of light sources and optical filters enables wavelength-selective photoactivation using high-powered Xe arc lamps, LEDs, and wavelength-selection filters.

Future laser photolysis capabilities at SSRL beamline 12–1

We are currently commissioning a tunable laser system spanning wavelengths from 192 to 2000 nm at 120 Hz repetition rate. This system will enable infrared temperature-jump experiments on single crystals, direct photolysis of natural chromophores, and pump-probe studies with μs time resolution. Combined with an existing Celeroton rotary shutter, capable of generating X-ray pulse durations from 5 μs to 1 ms (250–1.3 Hz), the beamlines will support precisely timed pump-probe sequences providing robust photo-triggering and freeze-trapping experiments to support a wide range of mechanistic experiments, especially those where ms-to-s timescales are relevant, or where intermediate stabilization depends on controlled cryo- or near-cryo protocols.

Serial and time-resolved crystallography

For metalloproteins, serial crystallography approaches are particularly compelling because they address two central challenges. First, dose fractionation distributes radiation exposure across many crystals, helping to preserve the desired oxidation state and minimize site-specific damage. Second, when paired with defined triggering methods (e.g., light, rapid mixing, or temperature-jump), serial data collection enables time-resolved sampling, capturing structural ensembles across precisely controlled delays.

In addition to time-resolved experiments using standard rotational crystallography, beamlines 12–1 and 12–2 support serial crystallography with various sample delivery methods, including droplet shooters, liquid injectors, and large chips for fixed-target approaches with fiducial alignment. Liquid crystal injection systems, supported in collaboration with scientists at the Linac Coherent Light Source (LCLS), deliver crystalline slurries directly into the X-ray beam, which is ideal for time-resolved mixing experiments where reactions are initiated a few ms before exposure. Fixed-target approaches can use microfabricated grids where crystals are loaded at predefined positions, enabling rapid automated data collection from hundreds or thousands of crystals with minimal overhead. These capabilities complement experimental options at the LCLS MFX beamline [12].

A broad-bandpass Mo/B4C multilayer monochromator option at SSRL BL12–1, providing an energy bandwidth of ~1% ΔE/E, boosts the X-ray flux by approximately 25-fold relative to the standard Si(111) monochromator. This high-flux mode is ideal for serial crystallography experiments. For rotational data collection, complete datasets can be acquired in under 1 second, using the DECTRIS EIGER2 XE 16 M detector.

The close partnership with the macromolecular crystallography beamlines at the LCLS, SLAC’s X-ray free-electron laser (XFEL) facility, is advantageous for both SSRL and LCLS users. Experimental setups developed and optimized at SSRL can be directly transported to LCLS for XFEL experiments. To facilitate this continuity, serial sample-delivery systems, robotics, and automation platforms are intentionally designed for cross-platform compatibility between the two facilities. This integrated approach enables research programs that leverage the complementary strengths of each source, which are the accessibility and throughput of the synchrotron for method development and systematic characterization, paired with the ultrafast time resolution and damage-free diffraction capabilities afforded by the XFEL. Together this synergy effectively multiplies the impact of every user’s investment in method development.

Room-temperature and humidity-controlled experiments

Protein crystals can behave differently at room temperature than under cryogenic conditions. Some conformational states are accessible only when solvent dynamics are preserved, catalytically essential water molecules remain mobile, and the protein populates its native conformational ensemble. There is growing interest in solving structures closer to physiological temperature, and beamlines 12–1 and 9–2 support a fully remote room-temperature program to meet this need (Figure 2(c)) [13].

Central to this capability is the Arinax Humidity Controller (HC-Lab), which maintains precise relative humidity (30–99.5%) around crystals during mounting and data collection, preserving the native hydration state. Users can ship room-temperature samples in commercially available SSRL-crystallization plates compatible with this workflow, sold by vendors such as Crystal Positioning Systems and MiTeGen. For transport, thermal shippers are used to maintain plate temperatures in the range of 2–8°C, or 15–25°C for up to 7 days, ensuring sample integrity during transportation to the beamlines [14].

The HC-Lab humidity controller, which is available for use by request at all MC beamlines also enables controlled dehydration experiments [15]. For some proteins, controlled dehydration improves diffraction quality by reducing crystal mosaicity or promoting more ordered crystal packing, an optimization that can be performed empirically on the beamline with immediate feedback [13]. The offline automated nozzle switcher additionally supports flash-freezing following controlled dehydration, and samples are robotically dismounted and stored for subsequent diffraction data collection.

As the field continues to reassess the role of cryogenic conditions in mechanistic interpretation, these capabilities point toward a future where room-temperature, and even high temperature, crystallography plays a central role in studying solvent-coupled dynamics and protonation equilibria.

Because sample temperature can be controlled over a range of 95 to 370 K at the SSRL MC beamlines, experiments are not limited to cryogenic or room-temperature conditions. Elevated-temperature data collection is also feasible and is already leveraged by researchers studying thermophilic proteins, for which higher temperatures more closely reflect biologically relevant states.

Anaerobic sample preparation

Many metalloproteins contain oxygen-sensitive metal centers whose native states are destroyed by even brief air exposure. To support these systems, the SSRL SMB-MC group provides user-accessible anaerobic glove boxes for on-site crystallization and sample preparation under inert atmosphere. This capability ensures that sensitive samples can be handled, mounted, and flash-cooled without compromising the very oxidation states that the experiment is designed to capture, and it complements the in situ spectroscopic tools that verify metal-site integrity at the beamline.

Custom experiments at the beamline

A hallmark of productive beamline science is the ability to accommodate experiments that fall outside standard workflows. Currently in development is a highly modular experimental table system at BL12–1 designed to enable users to deploy specialized setups that can be prepared offline and readily introduced to the beamline. This removable table system will allow rapid installation of custom experimental hardware without impacting the beamline’s standard capabilities. Preliminary designs for acoustic injectors, specialized goniometers, and in situ crystallization plate screening methods can be prototyped and tested without extended beam time commitments. Once prepared, the table can be transferred into the front end by beamline staff and removed after the specialized experiment to rapidly restore standard operations.

This modular philosophy lowers the barrier for exploratory experiments, making capabilities that would otherwise require dedicated instrumentation accessible to students, small research groups, and collaborative teams. Specialized setups developed on the removable table can also be readily transported and deployed at the LCLS MFX beamline, further leveraging the SSRL-LCLS ecosystem.

Conclusions

The capabilities described in this article reflect a sustained effort by the SSRL SMB-MC team (Figure 3) to provide an integrated suite of tools and methods that address the specific challenges of metalloprotein science. A user studying a dinuclear iron enzyme, for example, can verify metal incorporation by X-ray fluorescence, confirm the oxidation state by single-crystal UV-Vis spectroscopy, design a dose-managed data collection strategy, collect anomalous data at optimized wavelengths, and use AI-assisted feedback during data collection to make real-time decisions, all possible on-site or completely remotely. We also support a variety of strategies for initiating reactions in-crystallo, including light activation and freeze-trapping. These multi-modal, feedback-rich capabilities, where spectroscopy, fluorescence diagnostics, and diffraction are part of the standard toolkit, make SSRL exceptionally well-suited for metalloprotein studies.

Complementing these crystallographic capabilities, the SSRL SMB X-ray Absorption Spectroscopy (XAS) beamlines [16] provides element-specific tools for probing oxidation states, metal-ligand environments, and electronic structures in non-crystalline samples. For metalloprotein research programs that require both high-resolution structural data and rigorous electronic characterization, the combination of MC and XAS within a single facility, supported by scientific staff with deep expertise in both techniques, offers an integrated path from crystal structure to mechanistic understanding. Within the broader SLAC ecosystem, the ability to develop and validate experimental protocols at SSRL, including serial delivery systems, photo-triggering hardware, and sample-handling workflows, and then deploy them at LCLS for ultrafast, radiation-damage-free measurements opens new opportunities to study acutely radiation-sensitive and short-lived intermediate states.

For the metalloprotein community, the SSRL SMB beamlines provide a wealth of tools for advancing metalloprotein science. Looking ahead, new capabilities, such as tunable lasers, modular removable tables at SSRL BL12–1, and enhanced AI-guided analysis workflows, will enable the SSRL SMB group to continue bringing cutting-edge experimental regimes to the user community. We welcome collaborations and invite researchers to explore what these integrated capabilities can bring to their metallo-science programs [17].
