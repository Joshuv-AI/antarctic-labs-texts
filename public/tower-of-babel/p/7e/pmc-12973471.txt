A narrative review of considerations when assessing potential relationships between Guillain-Barré syndrome and vaccines
Authors: Sarah Elizabeth Williams, Olympia Evdoxia Anastasiou, Maria Maddalena Lino, Caihua Liang, Joanne Wu, Cassandra Hall-Murray, Erica Chilson, Kathleen Dooling, Kyla Hayford, Elizabeth Begier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGuillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is an acute, immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy that can result in progressive muscle weakness, paralysis, and, in severe cases, respiratory failure. While the majority of GBS cases are preceded by infectious events, concerns about a potential link between vaccines and GBS have periodically arisen. This commentary examines existing information on the association between GBS and vaccines and reviews the epidemiology, pathogenesis, and infectious and noninfectious triggers of GBS. GBS incidence rates are influenced by age, seasonality, geography, and comorbidities and are increasing over time. The complexities in assessing causality between vaccination and GBS are addressed, including the importance of diagnostic certainty and standardized case definitions. The self-controlled case series study design is most commonly used to investigate potential relationships between vaccines and GBS due to its efficiency (i.e. only vaccinated persons with GBS are included) and ability to control for fixed confounders (e.g. comorbidities), but results may be biased by time-varying confounders (e.g. viral infection activity) and lack of diagnostic confirmation. Further, attributable/excess risk estimates based on such studies are influenced by background/baseline GBS risk, which may differ by studied population. This review aims to inform ongoing research and guide public health risk assessment for this rare adverse event.KEYWORDS: Guillain-Barré syndrome, vaccination, causality

Introduction

Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is a rare but serious autoimmune disorder in which the body’s immune system attacks the peripheral nervous system, leading to muscle weakness, paralysis, and in severe cases, respiratory failure. Its exact cause is unknown, but GBS is often preceded by viral or bacterial infections.1 Over the past several decades, concerns have arisen regarding the potential association between certain vaccines and the onset of GBS. This report provides an overview of the key vaccines that have been linked to GBS, including key evidence of the associations and reviews frameworks in the causal evaluation of these associations as well as public health considerations.

Background on GBS

GBS is an acute, immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy. Clinical symptoms of GBS include an acute, rapidly progressive, symmetrical weakness, with loss of deep tendon reflexes, possible tingling in the feet and hands, and muscle aches.2 Variants of classic GBS are also defined, such as Miller-Fischer Syndrome (MFS), which presents with ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, and areflexia.2

Roughly two-thirds of GBS cases occur within weeks after an infectious event, with Campylobacter jejuni being the most common preceding infection.3 The hypothesized pathologic mechanism for GBS’ association with infection is molecular mimicry. For example, components of the C. jejuni bacterium may resemble the components of peripheral nerves, which are attacked by the immune system in GBS. However, pathologic mechanisms for development of GBS remain theoretical4 and are not fully understood. Many other infectious agents have been associated with the development of GBS, including cytomegalovirus (CMV), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), Mycoplasma pneumoniae, influenza, zika, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), hepatitis E, varicella zoster virus, COVID-19, and enteroviruses.5–9 Recent analyses have also found an increased risk of GBS following respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) disease.10

Although most GBS cases are preceded by an infection, approximately one-third of cases have no identifiable preceding event or trigger.11 These idiopathic cases remain a subject of ongoing research. In addition, other medical conditions have been associated with GBS onset, including recent surgery,12 malignancy,13 pregnancy,14 drugs, and rarely vaccinations, which will be explored in this report.

Estimates of the GBS incidence vary by several factors, including age, seasonality, geographic location, and comorbidity status.11 Overall, GBS incidence increases with age, with elderly individuals being the most at risk.15 GBS incidence tends to fluctuate seasonally, with peaks during winter and spring in temperate climates, likely reflecting patterns of respiratory and gastrointestinal infections.16 Incidence rates of GBS also vary by geographical location,11 possibly due to differences in infectious agent prevalence, environmental exposures, and population characteristics. Individuals with a history of autoimmune conditions also have a higher risk of developing GBS.17 All of these factors influencing incidence rates should be considered as possible confounders or effect modifiers when evaluating potential relationships between GBS and vaccinations.

Recent studies have shown increasing GBS incidence over time. Data from the Global Burden of Disease Study reported a 6.4% (95% CI: 3.6–9.5) increase in the age-standardized prevalence of GBS per 100,000 population between 1990 and 2019.15 A recent real-world evaluation of GBS epidemiology during and after the COVID-19 pandemic through a US-based claims database reported that among all adults, yearly GBS incidence increased over time, ranging from 3.1/100,000 PY in 2017 to 6.4/100,000 PY in 2023.18 Rates were up to 3 times higher among those with higher Charleston Comorbidity Index.

Considerations for causality assessment of GBS cases after vaccination

For GBS cases occurring following vaccination, it is critical to consider several elements to assess the likelihood of causal association with the preceding vaccine.19 The first step in assessing causality, and perhaps the most critical, is ensuring the correct diagnosis of the outcome of interest. As diagnosis in the early stage of GBS may be challenging, a combination of different clinical, laboratory, and diagnostic tests may be applied to support greater diagnostic certainty. Conversely, when less thorough evaluations are conducted, the level of certainty that the case is truly GBS is lower. Cases with less diagnostic certainty occurring following vaccination can result in misclassification bias20 and impede determination of whether the vaccination posed a true risk of GBS.

To address the challenge of clarifying diagnostic certainty, the Brighton Collaboration,21 a program of the Task Force for Global Health, developed several case definitions for reported adverse events following immunization. These case definitions aim to facilitate standardized evaluation of adverse events following immunization through categorizing by levels of diagnostic certainty.

The Brighton Collaboration case definitions for GBS and Miller Fisher syndrome have five levels of diagnostic certainty, ranging from Level 1, the highest level of certainty with data from the clinical exam, laboratory, and diagnostic testing to support the diagnosis, to Level 5 (not a case) (Table 1).22 Case definitions are used only to assess the diagnostic certainty of an event, not to demonstrate causal association with a preceding exposure.

Other groups have created frameworks for causality assessments of individual cases. For example, the Institute of Medicine in the US (IOM) created an internal criterion for determining causality of adverse events following vaccines with four levels ranging from “no evidence” to “evidence favors a causal association.” This framework has been applied to case series (ie, groups of reported cases) of rare events, such as vasculitis, following vaccination.23 Other frameworks developed to help standardize assessment of causality include the Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction Probability Scale24 and the WHO-UMC system for standardized case causality assessment.25

In addition to diagnostic certainty, another important factor when considering causal association of an adverse event following vaccination is the timing of the start of symptoms. For GBS, evidence suggests that the risk window for any triggering event known to be associated with GBS tends to be within a few weeks following exposure.11 Given that the immune system must be activated to “attack” the peripheral nerves, which induces the damage that triggers GBS symptoms, it is unlikely for causally associated symptoms to occur the day of, or one day following, any potential triggering events. Likewise, it is unlikely that an event diagnosed several months after a vaccine exposure would be causally associated with a preceding vaccine. Similarly, most cases of GBS following C. jejuni infection occur within one to 3 weeks following infection, thus a case diagnosed several months after known infection would be less likely to be related to that infection.8

Another important consideration when evaluating individual GBS cases following vaccination is the presence of alternative, potentially more likely, causes of the event. The following questions are important to ask: For a diagnosis of GBS following a specific vaccine, did the patient have any clinical signs of a preceding or concurrent infection? Does the patient have comorbid conditions, such as autoimmune disorder,17,18 which make the likelihood of developing GBS more likely than the general population? Was more than one vaccine given in the typical risk window prior to diagnosis? When considering alternative etiologies, laboratory, clinical, or other diagnostic evidence can be valuable.19

Considerations in controlled studies evaluating GBS following vaccination

Evaluating individual GBS cases following vaccination has its limitations because some GBS events would be expected to be temporally associated with vaccination by chance. Randomized controlled studies to evaluate GBS following vaccination are particularly challenging because, with rare medical diagnoses, hundreds of thousands, if not millions, of patients must be vaccinated to study the potential association between a vaccine and GBS. The self-controlled case series (SCCS) study design is the most robust and efficient method available to estimate a potential relationship between GBS and a given vaccine exposure.26 The SCCS uses a study population limited to persons who are vaccinated and have experienced a GBS diagnosis, where each individual subject serves as their own control. The incidence of GBS within a period of potential excess risk (e.g., 1 to 42 d after vaccination) is compared with incidence during other time periods (i.e., control period).27 For SCCS studies, the risk period is defined by the hypothesized increased risk period due to currently understood mechanisms of action and time to event following other recognized causes.28,29 It is not unusual for a study to present analyses using more than one risk window. In case of a vaccination series, the risk window is usually applied after each administration.29 While the risk window is guided by biological plausibility, the control period represents “baseline time” when no exposure-related risk is expected. A longer control period in SCCS is particularly important for rare events like GBS. Furthermore, a control period prior to vaccination could introduce bias due to violation of the assumption that events should not influence subsequent exposure (e.g., people who have recent or active symptoms of GBS may be less likely to receive a vaccine). Since the same individuals contribute to both risk and control follow-up time, the primary advantage of SCCS analysis is that it automatically adjusts for time-invariant confounders (e.g., demographics, chronic conditions, health-seeking behaviors).10 However, SCCS studies do not eliminate the potential for residual confounding from time-dependent effects.27 For example, influenza disease can trigger GBS events and, if an increase in influenza activity occurred disproportionality during one of the two compared time periods (post-vaccination risk period or control period), these time-varying or seasonal events could bias the assessment of risk. Also, some SCCS analyses are based on administrative claims diagnoses alone without GBS diagnosis confirmation by chart review. Lack of chart review can be problematic as details supporting the certainty of diagnosis are only available in medical chart notes.30 A recent FDA study (preprint status publication) evaluating GBS through SCCS studies following RSV vaccines reported that GBS diagnoses closer to the time of vaccination were less likely to be confirmed than those in control periods upon chart review (Figure 1), strongly suggesting clinicians are more likely to make a GBS diagnosis in the immediate post-vaccination period and may inadvertently introduce bias in self-controlled case series studies.30 Because access to medical records for outcome adjudication is often limited and challenging in real-world data sources, researchers should account for potential misclassification of diagnosis code – identified cases. Applying positive predictive value (PPV) adjustments from other studies with access to chart confirmation31 or using RWE algorithms to improve case certainty from validation studies32 can help improve the robustness of study findings.

Attributable risk is an estimate of the absolute increase in the incidence of an adverse event after vaccination (e.g., 5 excess GBS events per 1 million vaccinations). More recently, the impact of the GBS background/baseline incidence rate on attributable/excess risk (AR) calculations from SCCS studies has been described. As mentioned, SCCS studies only include vaccinated persons with GBS, not the entire vaccinated population. Liang et al.33 found that when comparing SCCS studies with similar incidence rate ratios (IRR), the AR may vary greatly due to differences in the baseline rate of GBS for the study population (Figure 2).34 Liang et al. found that applying a standardized background rate, representative of the target population for whom the vaccine is indicated when calculating AR, leads to more comparable AR results among different vaccines that are less impacted by differences in the underlying characteristics of specific RWE vaccinee populations. This SCCS limitation has been recognized by the US FDA and the following text has been added to the US prescribing information for RSV subunit vaccines on this basis “The background risk of GBS in a study population influences the excess GBS case estimate and may differ between studies, precluding direct comparison to excess GBS case estimates from other vaccine studies or populations.”35,36

Another consideration when evaluating adverse events following vaccination is the risk of the event following a natural infection with the vaccine-preventable disease. When a risk of an event following natural infection is present, that risk must be weighed against any risk of the same event from vaccine exposure, as the natural infection associated risk could be potentially prevented with vaccination. There are numerous vaccine-preventable diseases where adverse events potentially associated with the vaccine are recognized as a potential occurrence following the natural disease. For GBS, this is also the case, as evidence supports a risk of GBS following infection with influenza,9 herpes zoster,37 and COVID-19.38 For RSV,10 a recent SCCS study identified a 2 to 2.5-fold overall increased risk of GBS in the 42 d following RSV disease compared to control periods not temporally adjacent to RSV disease.10

Vaccines which signaled association with GBS

Although rare, certain vaccinations have been identified as possible triggers for GBS. In almost all cases, the absolute risk associated with vaccines is very low and far outweighed by the substantial public health benefits they provide.39 The following vaccines have been studied for their potential association with GBS.

Influenza

In 2004, the Institute of Medicine in the US (IOM) reviewed all evidence to date on neurological conditions following vaccines.40 Regarding the 1976 “swine flu” national influenza vaccine campaign in the United States, the report states there was “evidence to support an association” of GBS following pandemic influenza vaccination in adults, with a statistically significant relative risk of 7.6 estimated within 6 weeks of vaccination.41 This risk is likely an overestimate; however, as the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reported over 50 “antecedent events” identified in temporal relationship to GBS cases that could have been factors in modern-day causality assessment.42,43 For non-pandemic seasonal influenza vaccines after the 1976 season, the committee concluded that the evidence was “inadequate to accept or reject a causal relationship” between GBS in adults and influenza vaccines. In 2009, another pandemic “swine flu” vaccine campaign was conducted to protect against the 2009 H1N1 influenza strain.44 Public health agencies were vigilant in monitoring for any increases in GBS diagnosis. A meta-analysis published in 201545 found that overall, there was an increased risk of GBS following seasonal influenza vaccine (incidence rate ratio (IRR): 1.22 (95% CI: 1.01–1.48)) and increased risk of GBS following pandemic influenza vaccines (IRR: 1.84 (95% CI: 1.36–2.50)).

Shingles

A study using US Medicare data reported that the recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV) was associated with an increased risk of GBS within 42 d following RZV administration compared to recipients of the zoster virus live vaccine (ZVL) in a cohort analysis, with a rate ratio (RR) of 2.34, (95% CI: 1.01–5.41) after adjusting for age and sex.46 Using the same data source, a self-controlled case study found an RR of 4.96 (95% CI: 1.43–17.27) for RZV alone, with an estimated 5 additional cases of GBS per million doses. Post-marketing data and case reports have not demonstrated a consistent or significant link between ZVL and GBS. Most studies and surveillance data indicate that any risk, if present, is very low.

COVID-19

Regulatory agencies like the CDC, US Food and Drug Association (FDA), and European Medicines Agency (EMA) have tracked cases of GBS following COVID-19 vaccination. As of 2025, the evidence suggests that adenovirus-based COVID-19 vaccines may be associated with an increased risk of GBS (relative incidence: RI = 2.19; 95% CI: 1.17–4.07), 6.5 and 5.7 excess GBS cases per million persons attributed to ChAdOx1-S or the Ad26.COV2.S vaccine, respectively).29,47 The adenoviral vaccine is no longer available in the US. For approved COVID-19 mRNA vaccines, large-scale surveillance has not found an association.29,48

RSV

Most recently, both approved RSV protein subunit vaccines have been identified as having a potential or identified rare risk of GBS following vaccination in older adults. An FDA study found an IRR of approximately 2 for chart-confirmed SCCS studies, for both bivalent RSVpreF 2.02 (95% CI: 0.93–4.40) and adjuvanted RSVpreF3 2.46 (95% CI: 1.19–5.08), which is consistent with a 2-fold increase in risk over baseline.30 Another US study found similar IRRs for the protein subunit vaccines (1.5 (95% CI: 0.9–2.2) for RSVpreF3 and 2.4 (95% CI: 1.5–4.0) for RSVpreF) but was limited by lack of chart confirmation of GBS and relied on administrative ICD code diagnosis alone.49 Studies in the UK have found higher IRRs,50,51 but these studies are focused on an older population (persons 75–79 y of age) and are limited by inability to conduct chart confirmation of the GBS diagnoses. For Scotland, the low case counts lead to unstable point estimates. A sensitivity analysis performed as part of the English study applied the positive predictive value (PPV) from FDA’s chart confirmation assessment found that results would be comparable to the US if PPV was similar in the US and UK. The US Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices has considered the potential of GBS after both RSV protein subunit vaccines as equivalent in their risk/benefit modeling efforts.52 The rarity of GBS, the higher underlying risk of GBS in older populations where RSV vaccines are used, and the sparse data on the incidence of GBS following natural RSV infection, are challenges for conducting risk/benefit assessments of RSV vaccine and GBS risk. However, even without incorporating the potential reduction of GBS events following natural RSV disease, public health authorities agree that the RSV disease reduction in the targeted populations far outweighs the risk of GBS following vaccination.39,52

Conclusion

Vaccination is a cornerstone of public health, but rare adverse events such as GBS demand careful safety oversight. All stakeholders, including manufacturers, public health authorities, regulators, and clinicians, monitor vaccine safety extensively from early clinical development and throughout widespread use. Although GBS has been causally linked to some vaccines, most carry a very low absolute risk and the overall benefit remains far greater than the risk.

Determining a direct causal relationship between vaccination and GBS in individual cases is complicated and requires structured frameworks for assessment, considering factors like accuracy of diagnosis, timing, alternative causes, and epidemiological evidence. Ongoing post-authorization surveillance is critical to detect and evaluate rare events for new vaccines, which should be translated through updated safety recommendations, in order to maintain public trust.

In summary, while GBS following vaccination requires continued scientific scrutiny and robust monitoring, the overwhelming benefits of immunization far outweigh these rare risks. Continual evaluation and transparent communication are key to ensuring vaccine safety and protecting public health.
