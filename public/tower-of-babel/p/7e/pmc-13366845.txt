Awareness and self-reported synesthesia related experiences among undergraduate medical students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Eren Ogut, Lara Genc, Emir Mustafa Buldur, Tugba Gizem Sertduser, Dila Bade Ay, Davut Demir, Merve Akkurt, Sıla Meryem Inal, Gokhan Balci, Humeyra Elifsu Ince, Aysegul Mergen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSynesthesia refers to a neurological condition in which stimuli from one sensory pathway induce perception via another sensory pathway. This study evaluated self-reported synesthesia related experiences, awareness of such experiences, and other associated demographic and clinical attributes among undergraduate medical students.MethodsThis cross-sectional online survey targeted 617 medical students aged 18–25 years during the 2024–2025 academic year. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire on demographics, awareness of synesthesia-related experiences, selected medical and psychological factors, and their possible relevance to education. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0).ResultsAmong the 617 participants, 53.5% were female, and 46.5% were male. Only 31.8% of the participants reported previous familiarity with synesthesia-like associations. Self-reported experiences compatible with synesthesia-related subtypes varied across chromesthesia-related, grapheme-color-related, lexical-gustatory-related, time-space-related, and numerical-form-related items. Differences were also established based on gender for some items. Female participants more frequently reported headaches and synesthesia-related experiences, whereas male participants more often selected responses indicating no such experiences and more frequently endorsed numerical form-related items (p < 0.001). Age did not show any significant differences. Significant correlations also existed among the synesthetic subtypes, with chromesthesia exhibiting a positive correlation with grapheme-color, lexical-gustatory, time-space, and numerical form synesthetics (p < 0.001).ConclusionAwareness of synesthesia was low among the medical students in this study. Although some participants reported experiences compatible with synesthesia-related phenomena, these results do not confirm the prevalence of synesthesia. Instead, they described patterns of self-reported synesthesia-related experiences in an undergraduate medical student sample. Future studies using validated diagnostic and consistency-based tools are needed to determine the true frequency and educational relevance of confirmed synesthesia among medical students.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12868-026-01011-8.

Introduction

Synesthesia is a condition in which the activation of a particular sense or cognitive function automatically triggers a response in another sense or function. Although much literature has been written about the genetic, neurological, and developmental aspects of synesthesia, it is more than just an abnormal perception process and can also be linked to many perceptual, neurological, and psychiatric disorders [1–3]. Many researchers have investigated the link between synesthesia and neurodevelopmental disorders, particularly autism spectrum disorder (ASD). People with synesthesia may demonstrate some characteristics of individuals with ASDs, such as increased sensory sensitivity and perceptual detail [4, 5]. Moreover, some reports suggest that synesthetes may have increased sensitivity to emotional stimuli, unique perceptual linkages, or anxious traits, although the nature of this relationship is not clearly understood [6–10]. While there has been increasing interest in understanding synesthesia, research on self-reported synesthesia-related experiences in specific student populations, including medical students, is limited. The choice of medical students in this study was not due to any anticipated prevalence of synesthesia among medical students, but rather because of the presence of a well-defined population in which the differences between individuals in terms of sensory and cognitive perception may affect their learning and educational experiences [11–14]. For these reasons, medical students may provide a useful population for exploring awareness of synesthesia and the frequency of synesthesia-related experiences in the general population.

Therefore, the current study was designed to examine synesthesia awareness and self-reported synesthesia-related experiences among undergraduate medical students, rather than to diagnose synesthesia or estimate its confirmed prevalence [15, 16]. Synesthesia is a heterogeneous phenomenon with multiple recognized forms of synesthesia. Therefore, our questionnaire explored several subtype-related experiences, including chromesthesia, grapheme-color, lexical-gustatory, time-space, and numerical-form experiences. It was also hypothesized that there may be differences between the male and female subjects as well as between different age groups in regard to synesthetic experiences and that some specific forms of synesthesia would tend to co-exist in the same individual.

Research questions

What is the prevalence of awareness of synesthesia among medical undergraduates?

How often are self-reported synesthesia-related experiences endorsed in this sample?

Are there differences in such experiences by gender or age?

Which synesthesia-related subtype items are most frequently endorsed?

Do certain self-reported synesthesia-related experiences co-occur?

What are the implications of these results for neurodiversity awareness in medical training?

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study was designed as a cross-sectional questionnaire survey to investigate the awareness of synesthesia and self-reported synesthesia-related experiences among medical students. In terms of study participants, we recruited undergraduate medical students aged 18–25 who were attending lectures in the medical faculty for the 2024–2025 academic year. The survey was administered to all medical students we were able to reach, especially those from Istanbul Medeniyet University who agreed to participate. All medical program students in years one through six were invited to participate in the study. Recruitment was conducted via university email addresses, student learning platforms, and class WhatsApp groups. As a result, 617 students completed the online questionnaire and constituted the sample of the study (Supp. 1). Approximately 900 students were potentially involved; however, only 617 completed the survey. Participation was voluntary, and all participants provided electronic consent prior to the study (Supp. 2). No personal identifiers were collected. Individuals who did not provide consent were excluded from this study. The online questionnaire was prepared according to the existing literature on synesthesia [15, 16]. The test was performed online by a group of medical students. Since there was no objective synesthesia test based on consistency in the study, the results represent subjective self-reports of synesthesia.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were being enrolled in medical school, being between 18 and 25 years old, completing the survey, and providing electronic informed consent. The exclusion criteria were not being in medical school, providing incomplete answers, and not consenting.

Sample size and study scope

The study sample was derived from the accessible student population of the medical faculty. As synesthesia-related experiences are relatively uncommon and the present study was designed primarily as an exploratory survey, the sample was intended to provide a broad descriptive overview of awareness patterns, self-reported experiences, and subgroup differences within the available population. Accordingly, comparisons according to sex, age group, and selected synesthesia-related items were interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory analyses.

Data collection tool

Data were collected using a structured online questionnaire administered via Google Forms over one month. The questionnaire primarily consisted of multiple-choice items and included the following domains: Demographic characteristics, including age, sex, and academic year; background variables, including handedness, twin status, familiarity with the concept of synesthesia, family history of similar experiences, current medication use, and self-reported neurological or psychiatric conditions; and synesthesia-related screening items, covering experiences compatible with chromesthesia, grapheme-color, lexical-gustatory, time-space, and numerical form experiences (Supp. 1). Some items explored similar aspects of cross-modal perception in different ways; therefore, an overlap between related responses was possible. These items were used for exploratory screening only and were not intended to establish a clinical or validated diagnosis of synesthesia.

Ethical approval

The study protocol was approved by the Non-Interventional Health Research Ethics Committee of Istanbul Medeniyet University Faculty of Medicine (protocol ID: 2024-GOSEK-0004; decision no: 2024/02–05). Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and all participants provided digital informed consent before completing the questionnaires.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0. Descriptive statistics are presented as frequencies and percentages. Chi-square tests of independence were used to examine relations between questionnaire responses and sex, age group, and selected synesthesia-related response patterns. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The statistical analyses, SPSS files, and corresponding results are available in Supp. 4–8.

Results

The sample consisted of 617 participants, including 287 men (46.5%) and 330 women (53.5%). Of these, 154 participants were aged 18 years or younger (25.0%), whereas 463 participants were older than 18 years (75.0%). Most participants were first-year (55.3%) and second-year (39.5%) medical students. Only 31.8% of the participants reported previous familiarity with synesthesia-like associations, and 9.9% were left-handed. Twenty-eight participants (4.5%) reported having fraternal twins, while five participants (0.8%) reported having identical twins. Most participants, 427 (69.2%), did not report any of the conditions listed in Table 1, and 12.0% reported regular medication use. Whether the answers to Questions 9–27 in the questionnaire differed according to gender was analyzed using chi-square tests of independence (Table 2). The analysis showed that male and female participants did not differ significantly in their responses to Questions 9, 13, 16, 18, 19, 20, 23, and 24 (p > 0.05).

For Question 10, males reported having been diagnosed with a high IQ or giftedness significantly more often than females (χ² = 14.132, p < 0.001). For Question 11, females reported frequent headaches significantly more often than males (χ² = 25.931, p < 0.001). For Question 12, females reported associating letters and words with colors significantly more often than males, whereas males reported that sounds reminded them of colors significantly more often than females (χ² = 36.154, p < 0.001). In addition, males were significantly more likely to report not experiencing any of the relationships listed in Question 12 than females. For Question 14, males answered “No” significantly more often than females, whereas females answered “Not really, just slight associations” significantly more often than males (χ² = 12.000, p = 0.002). For Question 15, the grapheme-color-related item, males reported seeing letters or numbers in color significantly more often than females, whereas females reported seeing them in black significantly more often than males (χ² = 8.847, p = 0.003) (Table 2).

For Question 17, females agreed with the statement “Each number has its own color” significantly more often than males, whereas males reported not agreeing with any of the statements listed in Question 17 significantly more often than females (χ² = 13.185, p = 0.004) (Fig. 1). For Question 21, females reported that Saturday had a specific color significantly more often than males, whereas males reported that Saturday was simply a day of the week significantly more often than females (χ² = 22.398, p < 0.001). For Question 22, females reported that the letter “A” had a personality or color significantly more often than males, whereas males reported that the letter “A” was simply a letter significantly more often than females (χ² = 44.045, p < 0.001). For Question 25, females reported that February evoked a color or personality significantly more often than males, whereas males reported that February was simply a month significantly more often than females (χ² = 22.446, p < 0.001). For Question 26, females agreed with the statements “Sunlight has a pleasant sound” and “Days in the calendar have personalities” significantly more often than males, whereas males reported not agreeing with any of the statements listed in Question 26 significantly more often than females (χ² = 17.864, p < 0.001). For Question 27, the numerical form-related item, males answered “Yes” significantly more often than females, whereas females answered “No” significantly more often than males (χ² = 7.166, p = 0.007) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study provides an exploratory overview of synesthesia awareness and self-reported synesthesia-related experiences among undergraduate medical students, rather than a diagnostic estimate of confirmed synesthesia. The most immediate finding was the low level of prior awareness. This suggests that unusual cross-modal experiences may be underrecognized by medical students, even when such experiences are self-reported. This finding is important because synesthesia is now generally regarded as more common than case-based estimates suggested, particularly when wide forms of atypical cross-modal experiences are considered [17, 18].

Previous studies have shown that synesthesia-related experiences may be more frequently reported in artistic fields, and some authors have linked synesthesia with creativity and memory-related advantages [19–21]. Our study was based on an online self-report questionnaire and did not include objective consistency testing; therefore, the findings are better interpreted as patterns of synesthesia-related experiences rather than confirmed prevalence [15, 16, 18]. However, a recent study suggests that self-report measures can still provide meaningful information in synesthesia research, particularly for screening, phenotyping, and examining subtype-related variation, even when they are not used as a stand-alone behavioral confirmation tool [22]. Therefore, the present data should be regarded as evidence that synesthesia-related experiences and awareness gaps coexist among medical students.

Within our sample, positive chromesthesia responses were significantly associated with grapheme-color, lexical-gustatory, time-space, and numerical form responses. Similarly, grapheme-color responses were associated with lexical-gustatory, time-space, and numerical form responses, and lexical-gustatory responses were associated with time-space responses. Taken together, these findings suggest that cross-modal traits may cluster within the same individual, rather than emerging as entirely separate phenomena. This interpretation is consistent with the literature describing synesthesia as a heterogeneous condition with overlapping manifestations rather than a single uniform experience [21]. It is also compatible with current models suggesting that synesthetic experiences may reflect distributed rather than localized differences across perceptual and associative systems [23, 24].

According to the sex-related findings, female students more frequently endorsed several associative items, including letter-color-related responses, whereas male students more often selected responses indicating no such experiences and were more likely to endorse numerical form responses. Simultaneously, there were no significant gender differences in the direct yes/no comparisons for chromesthesia, lexical-gustatory synesthesia, or time-space synesthesia. This pattern suggests that gender differences in this study may reflect variations in the reporting of specific associative experiences rather than a general difference in synesthesia-related tendencies. No significant differences were observed between the two age groups for any of the questionnaire items. This may indicate that within a relatively narrow age range, synesthesia-related experiences are more strongly shaped by individual perceptual style than by age itself. It is also possible that the limited age variability in the present cohort reduced the likelihood of detecting age-related effects in this study. The findings can also be considered in light of previous studies linking synesthesia with neurodevelopmental variation, particularly ASD conditions [4, 9, 25–27]. Several studies have extended this relationship by showing that synesthesia is associated not only with higher rates of ASD but also with increased autistic traits, atypical sensory sensitivity, enhanced attention to detail, and partly shared genetic influences [25–28]. Although our study was not designed to test this relationship directly, and only a small number of participants reported autism or related syndromes, the literature suggests that atypical sensory integration may represent one point of overlap between synesthesia and other forms of neurodivergence. Therefore, the present results may be relevant to discussions of neurodiversity in medical education, especially in relation to individual differences in sensory-cognitive processing.

From a neurobiological perspective, previous studies have proposed that synesthesia may involve atypical connectivity or altered communication across sensory and associative brain networks [24, 29–31]. This interpretation remains plausible, but the current neuroimaging literature is more complex than a simple hyperconnectivity account. Studies have emphasized the heterogeneity across synesthesia types and methodological variability across imaging studies, while newer whole-brain studies suggest widespread structural and functional differences that extend beyond a single sensory pathway model [23, 24]. Although the present study cannot directly address neural mechanisms, the observed co-occurrence among several subtype-related responses is broadly compatible with the view that synesthetic experiences may reflect interconnected rather than fully independent perceptual tendencies.

An important contribution of this study is that it shifts attention toward an understudied population in the literature. Medical students are rarely examined in synesthesia research; however, they represent a cognitively demanding educational group in which perception, memory, and attention are continuously engaged. However, our data do not support strong claims that synesthesia-like experiences confer academic advantages in this population. This question remains open and should be evaluated directly in future studies rather than inferred from the current results. Therefore, such educational implications should be framed as hypotheses rather than demonstrated benefits, although prior studies have argued that synesthesia may interact with learning processes in conceptually meaningful ways [32, 33]. Therefore, greater awareness of synesthesia-related experiences may be most valuable not as evidence of superior performance but as a reminder that students can differ meaningfully in how they perceive, process, and organize information.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is that it examined awareness and a broad range of synesthesia-related experiences in a relatively large sample of medical students. It also goes beyond simple frequency reporting by exploring sex differences, age group comparisons, and co-occurrence patterns across multiple subtype-related items.

This study had some important limitations. All findings were based on self-reports, and no objective consistency-based synesthesia battery was used. As a result, the responses may include stable synesthetic experiences response-style differences. Participation was voluntary, which created the possibility of self-selection bias. In addition, because multiple chi-square comparisons were performed across questionnaire items, the statistical findings should be interpreted as exploratory, and the possibility of Type I error due to multiple tests should be acknowledged.

Future directions

Future studies should incorporate validated consistency-based tools to distinguish between confirmed and non-confirmed synesthesia. Multicenter studies involving students from different academic disciplines may also help clarify whether the patterns observed here are specific to medical education or reflect the wide features of young adult student populations. In addition, studies that directly examine learning outcomes, memory performance, and educational preferences in relation to synesthesia-related traits would be especially valuable.

Conclusion

This study shows that awareness of synesthesia among undergraduate medical students was limited, even though a considerable number of participants reported self-described synesthesia-related experience. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence of confirmed synesthesia prevalence but rather as an indication of the frequency and clustering of self-reported synesthesia-related experiences among undergraduate medical students in this study. Although several gender-related differences were observed in specific associative responses, no significant differences were found between age groups, indicating that these experiences may be shaped more by individual perceptual style than by age within this relatively narrow age range. Another important finding was the significant co-occurrence of several subtype-related responses, especially among chromesthesia, grapheme-color, lexical-gustatory, time-space, and numerical form experiences. The current study provides useful preliminary data from an understudied population and highlights the importance of increasing awareness of neurodiversity and individual sensory-cognitive differences in medical education. Future research using validated diagnostic tools and multicenter designs may help clarify the true frequency, structure, and educational relevance of synesthesia-related experiences among medical students.
