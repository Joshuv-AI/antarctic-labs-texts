Laparoscopic robotic-assisted transhiatal thoracic duct ligation for refractory chylothorax
Authors: Adin Reisner, Subroto Paul, Paul C Lee, Matthew L Inra
Published: 2024

Image of thoracic duct and critical structures via transhiatal approach before ligation.

Central Message

We describe an alternative approach to thoracic duct ligation for management of high output chylothorax when other, more conventional, options have failed.

Chylothorax is defined by chyle in the pleural space. Nonoperative treatment includes drainage and diet modification. Octreotide and midodrine are medical adjuncts.1 Thoracic duct embolization or needle disruption of the duct are nonsurgical interventions for treatment.2 Transthoracic duct ligation is a well-described surgical option. We present a transabdominal transhiatal surgical duct ligation for refractory chylothorax. Institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required because consent is included in the surgical consent for video for educational purposes.

Patient Presentation

A 35-year-old man with AIDS presented with dyspnea and a right pleural effusion. Drainage revealed a milky transudate. Fluid triglyceride level was 272 mg/dL, consistent with chylothorax. He was managed nothing by mouth, total parenteral nutrition, and octreotide with 2 L output daily. Additional testing, including infectious cultures, immunologic titers, tumor markers, and positron-emission tomography/computed tomography imaging were nondiagnostic. Computed tomography showed persistent right pleural effusion, moderate ascites, small bowel edema, and mesenteric varicosities. Ultrasonography and magnetic resonance imaging showed left subclavian vein thrombus and dilated lymphatic channels without a distinct thoracic duct. Duct embolization and needle disruption were unsuccessful, and output remained 2 L/day.

Right thoracoscopic robotic-assisted thoracic duct ligation was pursued. More than 5 L chyle was encountered without obvious source. The parietal pleura was inflamed. The spine, aorta, azygous vein, and esophagus were difficult to identify. The aorta was eventually identified and mass ligation was attempted. Decortication and talc pleurodesis were performed. Three chest tubes were placed.

Postoperatively, chest tube output remained elevated. He returned to the operating room for laparoscopic robotic-assisted transhiatal thoracic duct ligation. Significant chylous ascites were encountered. Hiatal dissection was performed. The esophagus was mobilized. The aorta, spine, and right pleura were identified in the mediastinum. A tubular structure was identified as the suspected thoracic duct. Mass ligation was performed (Figure 1 and Video 1).

Intraoperative transhiatal view of the mediastinum. Major structures are labeled (A), and mass ligation is performed (B). Eo, Esophagus; TD, thoracic duct; Ao, aorta; RC, right arm of crus; LC, left arm of crus.

On postoperative day 6, drainage was 42 mL. The chest tubes were removed sequentially. The patient had persistent chylous ascites and developed a left chylothorax on postoperative day 20. This chylothorax was attributed to his chylous ascites from superior mesenteric vein (SMV) stenosis. Interventional radiology placed an SMV stent that decreased output from 1 L to 90 mL.3 The patient was discharged home on postoperative day 47 without drains.

Discussion

Etiologies of chylothorax include traumatic disruption of the thoracic duct, malignant and benign obstruction, infection, autoimmune disease, and abnormal anatomy.1 This patient had an atraumatic chylothorax. Maldonado and colleagues1 report that atraumatic chylothoraces resolve with nonoperative management 27% of the time and were 4 times more likely to recur after surgical intervention when compared with traumatic chylothoraces.

Surgical intervention was indicated given the failure of other treatments. Transthoracic ligation is well described and effective.4 A hostile thorax was encountered and pertinent anatomy could not be identified. Safe exposure of the thoracic duct was limited, ultimately leading to failure.

The principal of transthoracic duct ligation is to ligate just above the diaphragm. The transhiatal approach was performed to ligate the duct with the same principal. The Da Vinci Xi surgical system (Intuitive Surgical) was used. Four robotic ports 2 fingerbreadths below the costal margin were placed in a straight line. An additional 5-mm liver retractor port and 12-mm left paraumbilical bedside assistant port were placed. This setup allowed excellent exposure of the hiatus for esophageal mobilization and mediastinal access. Because of the significant chylous ascites and large liver, the assistant port was useful for suction and retraction (Video 1).

Once the esophagus was mobilized, the Penrose drain was important for retraction and mediastinal exposure. There was significant inflammation in the mediastinum. Identifying the anatomic structures that border the soft tissue containing the thoracic duct, specifically the spine and aorta, was imperative. Despite identifying a tubular structure that was the suspected thoracic duct, mass ligation was performed to ensure ligation of the duct and any accessory branches (Video 1). Indocyanine green was not used to identify the duct because the patient did not have appropriate inguinal lymph nodes to inject.5

Suture ligation with size 0 silk sutures was performed. The robotic platform allowed us to use a 1-handed suture tie to secure the second ligature because of difficult visualization. The ligatures were marked with metal clips for future indicated interventions (Video 1). The patient tolerated this operation well and his chylothorax resolved quickly.

The patient's chylothorax was managed with ligation but he ultimately needed SMV stenting to manage his ascites and secondary left chylothorax. The etiology of his right chylothorax was multifactorial, which included aberrant anatomy with multiple lymphatic channels, subclavian vein thrombosis, and SMV stenosis.

Conclusions

Although the etiology was difficult to determine, leading to a long clinical course, conservative, interventional, and surgical measures were used to treat this patient. For treatment of high-output chylothoraces, interventions are usually necessary. If surgical treatment is indicated, we demonstrated that a transabdominal, transhiatal approach can be safe and effective for thoracic duct ligation.
