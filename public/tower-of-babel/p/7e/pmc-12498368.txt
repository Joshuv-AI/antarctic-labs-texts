Biomedical dominance and the underrepresentation of primary care: An analyses of self-claimed research priorities of German medical schools
Authors: Frank Müller, Eva Hummers, Eva Maria Noack
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrimary care providers deliver the majority of medical care serving as essential first points of contact and care coordinators. Despite the significant challenges they face, primary care research seems to lag behind and stay low-funded. Since the mid-1990s, academic reforms have emphasised metrics like third-party funding and high-impact publications. Medical schools were encouraged to develop distinct research profiles and to compete both internally and externally, leading to shifts in how research areas were prioritised, potentially disadvantaging primary care research.ObjectivesTo investigate the thematic priorities in German medical schools’ research, assess the diversification of these priorities, and examine the role of primary care research.MethodsWe conducted an analysis of the research priorities of all 39 German medical schools associated with the German Association of Medical Faculties. Data was extracted from medical school websites in October 2023. Research priorities were categorised and analysed using descriptive statistics and relationship mapping.ResultsResearch profile topics per medical school ranged from 1 to 7 (mean 3.51, SD 1.41). The most common research priorities were ‘Neuroscience & Neurology’ (69.2%), ‘Immunology/Infectiology/Immunotherapy’ (59.0%), and ‘Oncology’ (51.3%). Only a few institutions, primarily those in rural areas or recently established medical schools, prioritised adjacent primary care research fields such as health services research or community medicine.ConclusionOur analysis reveals a significant concentration of research priorities in a few biomedical fields across German medical schools, with an underrepresentation of primary care research. This suggests a potential misalignment between academic focus and societal healthcare needs.Keywords: Research priorities, primary care research, Germany, academic medicine, research funding allocation

Introduction

In Germany, as in most other countries, the vast majority of medical care is provided in primary care settings [1,2]. Primary care providers serve as first points of contact for patients, managing a broad spectrum of health conditions and coordinating care across the healthcare system. The World Health Organisation (WHO) reaffirmed in the Declaration of Astana that ‘strengthening primary health care (PHC) is the most inclusive, effective and efficient approach to enhance people’s physical and mental health’ [3]. Despite these benefits, primary care in Germany faces significant challenges, including increasing patient loads, an ageing physician workforce with many physicians approaching retirement [4]. While policy reforms alone have shown only modest improvements in primary care delivery [5,6], research-informed interventions demonstrated greater impact on patient outcomes and system performance in some studies [7,8]. While these are incident findings, this underscores the importance of robust primary care research for developing effective approaches to address various healthcare challenges.

Given the fundamental importance of primary care to overall healthcare delivery, one might expect it to be given priority within the medical research landscape. However, despite primary care’s importance, research funding seems to remain disproportionately low. While data on research spending for Germany is lacking, the German Research Foundation, with an annual budget of 3.9 billion euros the largest public funding organisation, has noted a critical shortage of systematic, methodologically sound primary care and health services research [9]. Recent international analyses have highlighted underinvestment in primary care research as a global problem, calling for strengthened clinical trials research to generate the rigorous evidence needed to maximise health gains from primary health care interventions [10].

This raises questions about how primary care research is positioned in German medical schools. Since the mid-1990s, reforms in the academic landscape of many Western countries, have transformed research evaluation metrics, emphasising third-party funding, high-impact publications, and ‘lighthouse projects’ as indicators for research quality [11]. Most prominent, the ‘Excellence Initiative’, launched in 2005, aimed to create ‘world-class universities’ that could compete with top academic institutions globally [12]. This highly competitive research funding program with a total budget of 4.6 billion euros encouraged German universities to define distinct research agenda, and to concentrate resources on these priorities.

This competitive environment potentially disadvantages primary care research, which – despite addressing widespread and everyday healthcare needs – typically generates knowledge that cannot be patented or commercialised. For example, typical and effective primary care interventions such as team-based care models, community health worker programs, or social needs screening have shown to improve chronic disease outcomes [8], but lack intellectual property protections, limiting private sector investment and creating dependency on uncertain public funding [13], making such research potentially less attractive within the current research paradigm.

Given these structural challenges in the academic landscape and their potential impact on research prioritisation, it remains unclear how primary care research is positioned within German medical schools’ self-declared research priorities. Universities’ publicly stated research profiles serve as strategic positioning tools in the competitive funding environment and reflect institutional decisions about resource allocation and faculty recruitment [14,15].

Specifically, we examine: (1) which thematic research priorities are prominently featured by German medical schools, (2) the degree of diversification across these priorities, and (3) the representation and positioning of primary care research within this competitive academic landscape.

Methods

We have conducted a systematic investigation of research priorities of all 39 German universities with a medical school that are associated in the German Association of Medical Faculties (Fakultätentag). This association is an official representation of mostly state-run medical schools, all of which are required to demonstrate substantial research activity to maintain their in order to meet regulatory requirements. Within the last decade, some smaller privately owned universities have established medical schools and received approval by respective state authorities. However, these private institutions focus on teaching and are barely involved in research activities.

The investigation of research priorities was performed through a comprehensive search on each of the medical school’s websites in October 2023. Thus, we explored the self-claimed priorities, following the assumption that universities independently determine their research priorities internally to further expand these priorities and acquire corresponding funding [14,15].

Data extraction

We extracted the exact description of research priorities in an Excel Sheet (Microsoft Excel 2019, Microsoft Corp, Redmond WA, USA). Then, EMN and FM individually performed open coding of the respective profile description. This process resulted in two very similar code systems, which were subsequently merged through a discursive process, with examples, coding rules, and descriptions for each category. After this, we performed an axial re-coding of the entire dataset.

The developed groups were (1) Immunology/Infectiology/Immunotherapy, (2) Oncology, (3) Neuroscience/Neurology, (4) Cardiology, Vascular Systems, Heart & Circulation, (5) other Fundamental Biomedical Research, (6) Diabetes & Metabolic Conditions, (7) Transplantation, (8) Health Services Research & Epidemiology, (9) Medical Technology & Biomaterials, (10) Other.

If medical schools had a research profile that could be applied to more than one of our groups, e.g. the University of Tübingen with a single research profile called ‘Immunology and Oncology’ or ‘Diabetes and Vascular Medicine’, we looked more closely at the description of the respective research topics. If they would not clearly tend to one aspect, we grouped them in both categories to account for their interconnectedness. In some cases, two research areas were grouped in the same category: e.g. University of Tübingen ‘Immunology’ and ‘Infection Research’ in ‘Immunology/Infectiology/Immunotherapy’ and Augsburg University ‘Medical Informatics’ and ‘Environment and Health’ in ‘Other’. Research foci that were neither associated with an organ system nor a medical condition but could be classified as fundamental biomedical research, such as Julius-Maximilians-University Würzburg profile on ‘Cellular Heterogeneity’ were coded as ‘Other Fundamental Biomedical Research’. Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics including absolute and relative frequencies, mean with standard deviation (SD) were used to characterise the distribution of research profile topics among various medical schools. Clusters between research profile topics were depicted using relationship maps. All analyses were conducted using SPSS 29 (IBM, Armonk NY, USA).

Research ethics

This is non-human research. The analysed data are publicly available. A vote by the Institutional Review Board was not deemed necessary.

Results

All medical schools (n = 39) had dedicated websites reporting on their research profile, mainly with clear and unambiguous labelling of their research priorities. For example, the Christian-Albrechts-University Kiel lists ‘Inflammation, Neuroscience, Oncology, Biomaterials and Medical Technology’ as priority areas, which were coded as ‘Immunology/Infectiology/Immunotherapy’, ‘Neuroscience & Neurology’, ‘Oncology’, and ‘Medical Technology & Biomaterials’, respectively.

The number of research profile topics ranged from 1 to 7, with an average of 3.51 (SD 1.41). The distribution of the respective priorities is shown on the map in Figure 2.

More than half of the medical schools had a research profile in Neuroscience & Neurology (69.2%, n = 27), Immunology/Infectiology/Immunotherapy (59.0%, n = 23), or Oncology (51.3%, n = 20) (Table 1). A third (33.3%, n = 13) of the medical schools declared all three of these categories among their prioritised topics or profile, another third (33.3%, n = 13) featured two of these three. Other widely represented research profile were Other Biomedical Research (48.7%, n = 19) or Cardiology, Vascular Systems, Heart & Circulation (38.5%, n = 15). Discussion

The analysis of research priorities across 39 German medical schools revealed notable concentration on limited research domains, with Neuroscience/Neurology (69.2%), Immunology/Infectiology/Immunotherapy (59.0%), and Oncology (51.3%) emerging as predominant priorities and one-third of institutions prioritising all three areas simultaneously. Clinical fields such as General Practice/Family Medicine/Primary Health Care, Gynaecology and Obstetrics, or Paediatrics do not appear as independent research priorities despite their clinical importance, nor do common metabolic conditions (i.e. diabetes) despite their primary care relevance, though the latter likely play associated roles in other research focus areas such as Cardiology, Vascular Systems, Heart & Circulation.

Primary care research is notably absent as a dedicated research priority. Greifswald’s focus on ‘Community Medicine’ may encompass primary care aspects, and several medical schools include ‘Health Services Research’ as a priority, such as Oldenburg. These institutions are typically characterised by some combination of being smaller, more recently established, or located in rural regions. The newer institutions, often founded specifically to address regional physician shortages, frequently evolved from local hospitals into university medical centres and thus often lack established biomedical research infrastructure that supports cutting-edge specialty research.

The absence of primary care research is particularly concerning given the high proportion of healthcare delivered in primary care settings. As German epidemiologist Schwartz noted in 1983 ‘The research gap in general practice is a deficit for medicine as a whole. It is not the result of an objective impossibility of research in general practice, but the result of omitted research investments’ [16]. Despite this early recognition, little has changed in Germany.

This underinvestment is not unique to Germany, as primary care researchers globally face difficulties securing resources and recognition within the broader medical research landscape [17,18]. For example, the US National Institutes of Health (NIH) allocated merely 0.2% of its budget to family medicine research between 2017 and 2021 [17]. Some countries, however, have taken different approaches: The United Kingdom’s National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR), established in 2006, invested over 300 million euros in Clinical Research Network in 2022 alone, establishing a robust research infrastructure for studies within the ‘NIHR Portfolio’ [19]. This demonstrates how primary care research infrastructure can be implemented through dedicated governmental investment outside traditional university structures. The Netherlands followed a profession-driven path with Dutch GPs and their self-governing bodies maintaining a research-focused agenda since the 1970s and emphasising systematic data collection through practice-based research networks. The Netherlands Institute for Primary Healthcare Research (NIVEL) is an independent research infrastructure dedicated to primary care [20] and developed a distinctive research agenda derived from existing knowledge gaps [21]. These government-led investment and profession-driven development positioned both countries as international leaders in primary care research [22]. For Germany, the critical question is if and how policy changes might strengthen primary care research to better align with healthcare delivery needs. Primary care research currently faces structural disadvantages within existing academic hierarchies, and even when funding programs are available, the absence of institutional prioritisation limits sustainable infrastructure development [23].

The observed dominance of biomedical research priorities in Germany reflects structural, historical, and self-reinforcing effects [14]. First, neoliberal higher education reforms since the mid-1990s intensified competition for research funding between German universities and between departments within institutions, forcing them to define and strategically promote distinct research priorities [12,24,25]. Second, institutions conducting primary care research are relatively new academic players that entered the competitive landscape with disadvantages. For example, the first German professorship for General Practice was established in 1976, and by 2006 merely 13 medical schools had institutionalised departments [26]. While today every medical school has a dedicated Department of General Practice, many remain understaffed and under-resourced. Established academic biomedical and clinical fields, however, entered this era with existing infrastructure and academic recognition, explaining their current prominence. Third, research-driven advances in the late 1990s likely reinforced research prioritisation in established biomedical fields. These included targeted therapy in immunology and oncology [27], or the emergence of interventional cardiology [28]. Basic genetic research gained media attention at that time, e.g. through the cloned sheep ‘Dolly’ [29]. Fourth, international research trends may have reinforced certain priorities in Germany. For instance, the ‘Decade of the Brain’ initiative in 1990 increased funding and public recognition for neuroscience [30], and in a global competitive academic marketplace [31], German medical schools likely followed suit.

Once research areas gain initial advantages, they become increasingly dominant over time. In this self-reinforcing mechanism (described as Matthew Effect) successful areas receive more funding at the expense of other research areas [32]. Once established, research priorities create momentum by attracting resources, specialised faculty appointments, and the construction of dedicated facilities. This makes it increasingly difficult to shift priorities, even when new areas emerge that might be more innovative, responsive to emerging needs, or socially relevant [11]. Geuna and Martin have indicated that performance-based funding systems that concentrate resources can create centres of excellence. In practice, well-performing departments maintain competitive advantages in subsequent funding rounds, enabling them to expand research capacity creating a self-reinforcing cycle. However, this approach comes at a cost: it creates a less balanced research landscape and can weaken innovation capabilities across the board [33], widening the gap between research and the needs for evidence and innovation in everyday healthcare.

The structural rigidity may limit German universities’ ability to respond to emerging research fields, especially in times of rapid technological change. For example, machine learning, artificial intelligence applications, or climate-change related health problems lack dedicated focus despite their growing importance in healthcare innovation. A regular critical re-evaluation of research priorities could provide more checks and balances.

Here, the critical question is if direct political influence will emerge to realign research priorities with healthcare delivery needs and how these interventions can be structured, potentially proportional to patient volumes across different care settings.

The study’s strength lies in complete coverage of all 39 German medical schools, providing a complete picture of self-indicated research priorities across the country. The publicly available information reflects how universities present themselves to stakeholders and potential funders, offering insight into strategic positioning in the competitive academic landscape [34]. These self-declared research priorities can influence future research directions through faculty recruitment and funding decisions. However, it may not accurately reflect actual research output or impact. Our analysis does not account for potential discrepancies between stated priorities and realised research activities [25].

Conclusions

Our analysis reveals an underrepresentation of primary care research in the stated research priorities of German medical schools. Only a handful of institutions, primarily those in rural areas or recently established medical schools consider health services research as a priority. This marginalisation occurs despite the crucial role of primary care in healthcare delivery and its potential to address pressing health system challenges. The concentration of research priorities in a few biomedical fields, particularly neuroscience, immunology, and oncology, suggests a potential misalignment between academic focus and societal needs. To ensure a more balanced research landscape that adequately addresses the full spectrum of healthcare challenges, policymakers and university leaders should consider strategies to better balance priorities.
