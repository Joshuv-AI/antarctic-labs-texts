Increased Bacterial Load per Neutrophil Reduces Intracellular Killing Capacity
Authors: Louise V. Duebel, Simon O. Dekker, Suzanne H. Bongers, Corneli van Aalst, Eva Mulder, Falco Hietbrink, Leo Koenderman, Nienke Vrisekoop
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNeutrophils are the most abundant innate immune cells in the peripheral blood and eliminate bacteria through phagocytosis and antimicrobial mechanisms. Early in infection, they often encounter high bacterial loads before full recruitment. Individual neutrophils can ingest many bacteria, but it remains unclear how high bacterial loads per neutrophil affect intracellular killing.MethodsNeutrophils were isolated from healthy donor blood by fluorescence-activated cell sorting. Intracellular bacterial load was quantified using imaging flow cytometry to measure spot counts and green fluorescent protein (GFP) intensity after exposure to GFP-expressing Staphylococcus aureus. A single-cell killing assay assessed intracellular killing across bacterial load categories by sorting individual GFP+ neutrophils into 384-well plates and counting wells with outgrowth after 100 h. Phagolysosomal acidification was measured using dual-labeled (pH-sensitive pHrodo and pH-insensitive PromoFluor 520 LSS NHS ester [PF520]) S. aureus bioparticles.ResultsBacterial uptake by neutrophils was highly heterogeneous in vivo and in vitro. GFP spot counts strongly correlated with GFP intensity (R2 = 0.66), allowing stratification into GFP fluorescence intensity categories. In the single-cell killing assay, higher bacterial loads per neutrophil were associated with reduced intracellular killing (χ2(4) = 11.72, p = 0.0003). Higher bacterial loads per neutrophil corresponded with diminished phagolysosomal acidification capacity (χ2(4) = 24.00, p < 0.0001).ConclusionNeutrophils ingesting higher bacterial loads exhibit reduced intracellular killing, likely due to decreased phagolysosomal acidification. These findings highlight how bacterial load per neutrophil shapes antimicrobial capacity and early infection control.

Introduction

Neutrophils are the most abundant innate effector cells in human blood, comprising 50–70% of circulating leukocytes and reaching concentrations of approximately 3–6 × 106 cells/mL in healthy adults [1]. These cells are rapid first responders of the immune system, as they migrate to sites of infection where they eliminate pathogens through phagocytosis, degranulation, production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), acidification of phagolysosomes, and release of extracellular traps [2]. Beyond this frontline activity, their contributions extend to tissue repair, immune modulation, and cross talk with immune cells involved in the adaptive immune response [3–5].

During infection, bacterial components activate the innate immune cascade, which trigger the production of inflammatory mediators that recruit first neutrophils and later monocytes to the affected tissue [6]. Although this response is rapid, several hours may pass before optimal neutrophil influx is established, creating a vulnerable lag phase in which the earliest arriving neutrophils may face high bacterial burdens. This is especially relevant for superficial wounds that are contaminated with bacteria, where physical barriers challenge neutrophil access to the infection site [7]. In this context, bacteria also have the chance to replicate unimpededly due to their short generation times [8], further increasing the burden encountered by the first responding neutrophils. This has been described before in a mouse model of cutaneous wounding with colonization by Staphylococcus aureus. Bacterial load significantly increased within 6 h of inoculation, while neutrophil numbers peaked only between 6 and 24 h [9].

Under such conditions of high bacterial burden, certain neutrophils have been shown to phagocytose large numbers of bacteria, reportedly ingesting up to 40–50 bacteria per cell in vitro [10, 11]. Interestingly, not all neutrophils ingest the same number of bacteria, where some phagocytose more than others [12]. While phagocytosis has been shown to limit bacterial growth and contain pathogens intracellularly [13–15], it remains unclear how the number of bacteria ingested per neutrophil influences killing efficacy. High bacterial loads may “dilute” and overwhelm intracellular killing machinery, such as granules protease availability and ROS production, making complete bacterial eradication less efficient. This effect has been observed in a study using bulk populations of neutrophils in suspension, where higher multiplicities of infection (MOI) (cell:bacteria ratio of 1:100) led to reduced bactericidal activity [16]. However, it remains unclear how this phenomenon translates to a single-cell level and why this plateau in killing efficiency occurs.

Here, we investigate how the number of bacteria ingested per neutrophil referred to as bacterial load per neutrophil – affects intracellular killing. If neutrophils can efficiently clear high bacterial loads per neutrophil, then phagocytosing many bacteria may be advantageous for early infection control. Conversely, if high bacterial loads per neutrophil impair killing, this may weaken bacterial growth regulation during the initial stages of infection. To investigate this, we used a single-cell killing assay, providing additional novel insight into the physiological variability in neutrophil phagocytic and killing capacity.

Methods

Blood Sampling Healthy Controls

Blood was collected via venipuncture from anonymous healthy volunteers (e.g., no self-reported illness) in 9-mL VACUETTE® sodium heparin tubes (Greiner Bio-One, Austria). Females and males were sampled, their ages ranging from 18 to 65 years.

Blood Processing

Total leukocytes were isolated as follows. Erythrocytes were removed from the 9-mL blood samples by adding lysis buffer (150 mm NH4Cl, 10 mm KHCO3, and 0.1 mm Na2 EDTA dissolved in ddH2O and adjusted to a pH of 7.4) to a final volume of 50 mL. Samples were incubated for 10–15 min at 4°C and spun down, after which the lysis step was repeated for an additional 5 min. Cells were then washed, resuspended in cold PBS2+ (PBS containing 0.32% w/v trisodium citrate [prepared by the University Medical Center Utrecht pharmacy] and 4 g/L human albumin [Sanquin, The Netherlands]) and kept on ice until further processing.

Neutrophil Fluorescence-Activated Cell Sorting

An antibody mix containing several antibody-fluorochrome combinations (online suppl. Table 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000551415) was added to the total blood leukocyte suspension and incubated for 30 min at 4°C. Samples were washed and resuspended in PBS2+ before fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) using the BD FACSAria™ III Cell Sorter (Becton Dickinson, NJ, USA). Sorting was performed at 4°C with a 85-μm nozzle. The neutrophil lineage was selected and sorted from each sample based on the gating strategy shown in online supplementary Figure 1. Sorted neutrophils were collected in separate tubes containing PBS2+.

Single-Cell Killing Assay

A single-cell killing assay was performed to evaluate neutrophil intracellular killing capacity for different bacterial loads per neutrophil. Sorted neutrophils were first resuspended in HEPES3+ (20 mm 4-[2-hydroxyethyl]-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid buffer, 132 mm NaCl, 6 mm KCl, 1 mm MgSO2, 1.2 mm KH2PO4, 1.0 mm CaCl2, 5 mm glucose, 5 mg/mL human albumin [Sanquin, The Netherlands]; pH 7.4; osmolarity 280–300 mOsm) supplemented with 40% human pooled serum [Sanquin, The Netherlands]). Green fluorescent protein (GFP)-expressing S. aureus MW2 strain [17] was added to the neutrophil suspension at a 3:1 bacterium-to-cell ratio, corresponding to an MOI of 3. This S. aureus strain had been transformed with the pCM29 plasmid containing the gfp gene [18] and a chloramphenicol resistance gene, as previously described [17]. The growth and culturing of these bacteria have been described by van Grinsven et al. [19]. The neutrophil population was incubated with GFP-expressing S. aureus for 10 min in a shaking incubator (180 RPM) at 37°C. Unphagocytosed bacteria were washed away, and samples were resuspended in PBS2+ for FACS. Doublets were excluded and neutrophils were selected based on forward scatter and side scatter parameters (online suppl. Fig. 2) and subsequently gated into categories according to GFP signal intensity (GFP negative, very low, low, medium, high, very high).

The different GFP categories were then FACS sorted (1 cell/well) into a black Falcon® 384-well Optilux plate (Corning, NY, USA) containing 40 µL medium (HEPES3+ supplemented with human pooled serum [Sanquin, The Netherlands] to an end concentration of 40%) in each well. The sorted plate was incubated at 37°C for at least 100 h. Following incubation, a snapshot of each well was taken by the Incucyte® S3 Live-Cell Analysis System (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany). Bacterial killing was then visually assessed per well where the presence of GFP signal (green wells) indicated bacterial outgrowth, while the absence of the GFP signal (gray wells) indicated successful neutrophil killing. The percentage of neutrophil bacterial killing was calculated per GFP category.

Neutrophils can “contain” bacteria for prolonged periods of time without necessarily killing them [13]. Therefore, accurate assessment of bacterial killing in this assay requires that neutrophils are no longer viable. During experimental optimization, neutrophil death reached a plateau at 100 h and at 72 h following incubation with a phagocytosis-inducing particle (online suppl. Fig. 3a). In addition, the number of wells exhibiting S. aureus outgrowth in the single-cell killing assay plateaued only after 3 days; beyond this time point, no additional wells became positive, and previously positive wells remained positive (online suppl. Fig. 3b). An incubation period of 100 h was therefore selected to ensure assessment of true bacterial killing.

Imaging Flow Cytometry

After neutrophil sorting and incubation with live GFP-expressing S. aureus, unphagocytosed bacteria were washed away as described above and samples were resuspended in 1% paraformaldehyde to fixate cells for imaging using the ImageStream®X Mark II Imaging flow cytometer. The bright-field channel and channel to measure GFP were used for image acquisition and analysis. Analysis was performed using IDEAS® analysis software version 6.0 (Amnis EMD Millipore, WA, USA). Neutrophils were gated according to the strategy shown in online supplementary Figure 4. An eroded cellular mask was generated to analyze bacterial load exclusively within the neutrophils. This mask excluded bacteria located outside the cell or attached to the external membrane (online suppl. Fig. 5). The Spot Count feature was then used to quantify the number of GFP-expressing S. aureus, or “spots,” within the defined intracellular mask. This feature quantifies the number of spots by grouping connected foreground pixels into components, where the total number of components corresponds to the number of spots, or bacteria. The signal intensity of GFP was also extracted from within the defined mask for further analysis.

Fibrin 3D Gels and Live Confocal Microscopy of Human Neutrophils

For confocal microscopy of 3D fibrin gels, Ficoll-isolated neutrophils (for detailed method see [20]) were washed in 4°C PBS and then incubated for 10 min at 37°C in a 5-μm TAMRA-SE (Life Technologies) solution in PBS. Neutrophils were then washed twice at 4°C in a 2.5% human albumin/PBS solution and resuspended in HEPES3+ with a concentration of 2 × 107 cells/mL and kept in the dark on ice.

ibidi 6-channel µ-Slides VI 0.4 (ibiTreat; ibidi GmbH, Martinsried, Germany) were filled with 30 μL of fibrin gel preparation. The latter were prepared creating a solution containing a final concentration of 4 × 106 neutrophils/mL, GFP-expressing S. aureus at a final concentration of 4 × 106 CFU/mL (MOI 1), 2 mg/mL human fibrinogen (FIB3 free of Plg, vWF, and fibronectin; Stago, Asnières sur seine, France), 40% v/v human pooled serum (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA), and 1 U/mL of human thrombin (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA). The fibrin matrix was left to solidify for at least 5 min after which 40% v/v of human serum diluted in HEPES3+ was added to both sides, to prevent drying. With the same aim, PARAFILM® (Pechiney Plastic Packaging, Menasha, WI) was used to seal the well.

Neutrophils in fibrin gels were transferred to a Zeiss LSM 710 microscope at 37°C with a Plan-Apochromat 20×/0.8 M27 air objective. Imaging was carried out for 1 h with an acquisition interval of 30 s, and 29 Z-stacks spaced by 1 µm were sequentially imaged at each interval. Dimensions of the field of acquisition were x = 424.27 µm, y = 424.27 µm, z = 28 µm. A 488-nm argon laser (6%) was used for the excitation of GFP, and the emission was detected at 488–549 nm. TAMRA-SE was excited at 561 nm (1%) and detection at an emission spectrum of 562–727 nm. Image sequences were processed and analyzed using ImageJ (version 1.53d).

Bioparticles Assay

The AQUIOS CL “Load & Go” flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, CA, USA) was used for analysis of the neutrophil phagolysosomal acidification capacity. This was performed as previously described in Hesselink et al. [21]. In short, a blood tube was placed in a cassette that was inserted into the loading dock of the device. The device mixed the blood before it automatically pipetted 43μL into a 96-well plate. Duo-labeled S. aureus BioParticles™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) with pH sensitive pHrodo™ green and additionally labeled with pH insensitive PF520 (PromoCell, Germany) were added to the well (end concentration 10 × 106 particles/mL), and left to incubate for 60 min. After incubation, the erythrocytes were lysed by addition of AQUIOS Lysing Reagent A (Beckman Coulter, CA, USA). The lysis was stopped after 30 s by the addition of AQUIOS Lysing Reagent B (Beckman Coulter, CA, USA). The sample was then automatically aspirated and analyzed in the flow cell of the device.

The results of the analyses were exported as .LMD files for further analysis using FlowJo™ software (Becton Dickinson, NJ, USA). Granulocytes were first gated based on forward scatter and side scatter parameters and subsequently gated into categories according to PF520 signal intensity (very low, low, medium, high, very high) (online suppl. Fig. 6). The fluorescence intensities of PF520 and pHrodo were then extracted from each categorical gate. Because the fluorescence of pHrodo increases as pH decreases, it serves as a readout of phagolysosomal acidification, whereas the pH-insensitive PF520 fluorescence reflects the amount of ingested bioparticles. In order to correct the pHrodo (phagolysosomal acidification) fluorescence intensity for the amount of ingested particles, phagolysosomal acidification was expressed as the ratio of pHrodo median fluorescence intensity (MFI) to PF520 MFI (pHrodo/PF520).

Mice

LysM-eGFP mice, which express enhanced GFP (eGFP) in neutrophils, were originally provided by Thomas Graf [22]. The strain was backcrossed onto a C57BL/6 background at Taconic Biosciences and subsequently transferred to our institutional animal facility. There, the mice were further bred and maintained in individually ventilated cages under standard housing conditions. Mice were allowed a 1-week acclimation period under a 12-h light/dark cycle at 20–24°C and 45–65% relative humidity, with ad libitum access to food and water. Mice of the same sex were group-housed (three to six per cage), and both males and females were included in the study.

Intradermal Injection of Heat-Killed S. aureus in the Ear and Intravital Microscopy

For intravital microscopy, MW2 was heat-killed at 60° for 60 min. Heat-killed MW2 were subsequently washed in 4°C PBS, then incubated for 15 min at 37°C in 20 μm TAMRA-SE solution in PBS. The TAMRA-SE staining was quenched by adding 500 µL human albumin and washed at 4°C by adding 1 mL of PBS and centrifugation at 2348 RCF for 5 min. Bacteria were resuspended in PBS at a concentration of 1 × 107 bacteria/mL and kept in the dark on ice until further use.

Preparation of LysM-eGFP mice for intradermal ear injections and subsequent intravital imaging followed previously described protocols [23, 24]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized using 1.5–2% (v/v) isoflurane, after which the ear pinna was positioned flat and secured onto a small wooden holder. A 0.5- to 1-µL droplet TAMRA-SE-labeled heat-killed MW2 suspended in PBS at 1 × 107 bacteria/mL was placed on the epidermal surface. Using a 31-G needle, a small intradermal pocket was made immediately anterior to the droplet, after which the ear was gently angled to facilitate passive entry of the inoculum into the pocket. Next, the mouse was positioned in a custom-built imaging chamber, with its snout in an anesthesia mask and the ear mounted on a glass slide at the chamber base. The setup was transferred to the climate-controlled (35°C) environment of an inverted Zeiss LSM 880 two-photon microscope. Images were acquired using a Chameleon Ti:Sapphire laser and an Apochromat 20×/0.8 M27 objective, with the excitation wavelength set to 920 nm. Z-stacks were acquired at 1-µm increments, 512 × 512 pixels with a frame averaging of 2. Image sequences were processed and analyzed using ImageJ (version 1.53d).

Statistical Analysis

The correlation between spot counts and GFP signal intensity was analyzed using a simple linear regression. To assess overall differences in single-cell killing capacity, MFI PF520, MFI pHrodo, and acidification capacity between the different categories of GFP and PF520 signal intensities, a Friedman test was performed without post hoc testing. When multiple data points were available, data were presented as medians with their corresponding IQR. To retrospectively compare the GFP signal intensities per neutrophil between wells that exhibited neutrophil killing or bacterial outgrowth, a Mann-Whitney U test was used. All statistical analyses and data visualized were performed using GraphPad Prism software version 10 (GraphPad Software Inc., CA, USA).

Results

Bacterial Load Is Variable among Neutrophils

To visualize neutrophil recruitment and phagocytic behavior at infection sites, various imaging techniques were used. Following incubation of human neutrophils with live serum-opsonized GFP-expressing S. aureus (MOI 3, 10 min) in suspension, imaging flow cytometry demonstrated substantial variability in bacterial load per neutrophil (Fig. 1a). Intravital two-photon microscopy following intradermal injection of heat-killed S. aureus in a mouse ear showed the sequential arrival of neutrophils at the infection site (Fig. 1b, online suppl. Video 1). Arrows highlight examples of neutrophils that phagocytosed multiple bacteria per cell upon arrival to the infection site in vivo. Similarly, in in vitro 3D fibrin gels containing S. aureus, live confocal imaging revealed human neutrophils migrating toward the infection site and phagocytosing multiple bacteria per cell (Fig. 1c, online suppl. Video 2). Together, these imaging approaches show that neutrophil phagocytosis is highly variable in both a mouse in vivo model and human in vitro models.

Left panel: brightfield; right panel: GFP channel showing individual internalized bacteria (green puncta). b Intravital two-photon imaging of a LysM-eGFP mouse ear following intradermal injection of TAMRA-labeled heat-killed S. aureus (red); neutrophils (green) are visualized in a single Z-slice; and arrows indicate neutrophils containing multiple bacteria at the infection site. c Live confocal imaging of TAMRA-stained primary human neutrophils migrating within a 3D fibrin gel and interacting with live GFP-expressing S. aureus (green); neutrophils (red) arriving at the infection focus ingest multiple bacteria.

Quantification of Bacterial Load per Neutrophil

To enable downstream functional analyses, a feasible and reliable method was required to quantify the bacterial load per neutrophil. Neutrophils were incubated with serum-opsonized GFP-expressing S. aureus, and the number of ingested bacteria per cell was quantified by imaging flow cytometry. Image masking analyses were performed to specifically identify GFP-expressing S. aureus located inside each neutrophil (Fig. 2a), thereby excluding spots located outside of the neutrophil or attached to the outside of the cell membrane (online suppl. Fig. 5). Spot count analysis revealed a median spot count of 2 (IQR: 0–4), and MFI calculation showed a median GFP MFI of 5,687 (AU) (IQR: 1,919–13344) (Fig. 2b). Correlation between spot count and GFP MFI was assessed through simple linear regression (Fig. 2c). This revealed a significant positive correlation, F(1, 3352) = 6,564, p < 0.0001, and R2 = 0.6620. The best-fit equation was Y = 3,250X + 956.7. This shows that GFP signal intensity can be used as a surrogate marker for the bacterial load per neutrophil.

Spot counts of GFP-expressing S. aureus within neutrophils correlate with GFP fluorescence intensity within neutrophils. a Representative ImageStream images and masking strategies showing a neutrophil with one spot count versus eight spot counts, demonstrating the use of an eroded mask to quantify spots/fluorescence confined to the inside of the cell. b Spot count and GFP fluorescence intensity within sorted and fixated GFP+ neutrophils. Data are presented as medians with their respective IQR. c Linear regression analysis showing the relationship between the spot count per cell (x axis) and the fluorescence intensity of the GFP signal (y axis). The solid line represents the best-fit regression, with the dotted lines indicating the 95% confidence interval. The regression equation, p value for the slope, and R2 are indicated. 3,354 neutrophils from one donor are plotted in b and c.

Increased Bacterial Load per Neutrophil Is Associated with Impaired Killing

To assess the effect of increased bacterial uptake on neutrophil intracellular killing capacity, neutrophils with different bacterial loads per neutrophil were subjected to a single-cell killing assay (Fig. 3a). Neutrophils were incubated with live serum-opsonized GFP-expressing S. aureus and sorted according to categories of GFP intensity (GFP negative, very low, low, medium, high, and very high) (Fig. 3b). Using the best-fit equation shown in Figure 2c, the number of ingested bacteria per sorted GFP intensity category could be calculated: −0.28 for GFP negative, 0.05 very low, 0.63 for low, 2.57 for medium, 8.04 for high, and 21.5 for very high (Fig. 3c). Interestingly, a Friedman’s test showed significant differences in the killing capacity among these categories, χ2(4) = 11.72, p = 0.0003, with higher GFP MFIs associated with decreased killing capacity (Fig. 3d). A simple linear regression additionally revealed a significant negative correlation between GFP MFI and bacterial killing, F(1,13) = 47.66, p < 0.0001 and R2 = 0.7857 (online suppl. Fig. 7). These findings indicate that higher bacterial loads per neutrophil were associated with a decreased killing capacity.

Impaired Neutrophil Killing Can Be Retrospectively Mapped to Higher Bacterial Loads per Neutrophil

The same phenomenon was observed when applying an alternative analysis strategy to a single-cell killing assay using sorted neutrophils containing GFP-expressing S. aureus (see online suppl. Text 1 for methods). Using index sorting data, bacterial killing or bacterial outgrowth outcomes scored after at least 100 h of incubation were retrospectively integrated with FACS sorting data. FACS events corresponding to 96 sorted GFP+ neutrophils were mapped to their respective GFP fluorescence coordinates on the initial sorting plot and integrated with neutrophil killing outcomes (Fig. 4a). GFP fluorescence intensity, measured at the time of sorting, was then compared between wells that showed bacterial outgrowth and those that showed bacterial killing. In wells with bacterial outgrowth, GFP fluorescence intensities measured at the time of sorting were significantly higher than those of neutrophils exhibiting bacterial killing (p < 0.001; Fig. 4b). The same trend was observed when this analysis was repeated using five independent donors (data not shown). This further indicates that neutrophils with increased bacterial loads exhibit reduced killing capacity compared to neutrophils with lower bacterial loads.

Decreased intracellular bacterial killing capacity can be retrospectively mapped back to lower GFP fluorescence intensities by index sorting. a Representative FACS plot of GFP fluorescence intensities of individual neutrophils from a single donor in a single-cell killing assay, integrated with bacterial killing (red) or outgrowth (green) outcomes using index sorting. Neutrophils that were recorded but not sorted for the single-cell killing assay are shown in gray. b 96 GFP+ neutrophils were sorted, all containing GFP-expressing S. aureus (red and green). After 100 h of incubation, wells were scored for bacterial killing (red, n = 47 events) or bacterial outgrowth (green, n = 49). Data are presented as individual GFP fluorescence intensity per event with corresponding medians and IQRs. Statistical significance was tested using a Mann-Whitney U test and represented as ***p < 0.001.

Higher Bacterial Loads per Neutrophil Associated with Decreased Acidification Capacity

It was hypothesized that impaired bacterial killing at higher bacterial loads per neutrophil could result from decreased phagolysosomal acidification capacity. This was investigated by incubating neutrophils with bioparticles coupled to a pH-insensitive dye (PF520) and pH-sensitive dye (pHrodo). Following bioparticle phagocytosis, neutrophils become PF520+, and phagolysosomal acidification can be measured by an increase in pHrodo fluorescence in response to a lowered pH in the phagolysosome (online suppl. Fig. 6). PF520 was used, as with GFP above, to define categories of different bacterial loads per neutrophil (PF520 negative, very low, low, medium, high, and very high) (Fig. 5a). Signal intensities of PF520 and pHrodo were extracted from within these gates and the acidification ratio (pHrodo/PF520) was calculated to correct the acidification signal for the amount of ingested bioparticles (Fig. 5b–d). A Friedman’s test revealed significant differences among these categories for the PF520 signal intensity (χ2(4) = 24.00, p < 0.0001) and the pHrodo signal intensity (χ2(4) = 24.00, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 5b, c). Phagolyosomal acidification capacity was also significantly different across the categories (χ2(4) = 24.00, p < 0.0001), with higher bacterial loads per neutrophil associated with reduced acidification capacity (Fig. 5d). These findings support the hypothesis that ingestion of higher bacterial uptake per neutrophil results in an overall decrease in phagolysosomal acidification capacity leading to impaired bacterial killing.

The fluorescence intensity of PF520 (b) and pHrodo (c) for each PF520 category, as shown in a, was plotted. d The phagolysosomal acidification ratio was plotted for each category of PF520. The ratio between the pHrodo MFI and the PF520 MFI was calculated to correct the acidification signal for amount of phagocytosed bioparticles within neutrophils. Data are visualized as medians with their corresponding IQR. Overall statistical significance between categories was tested using a Friedman’s test without post hoc testing. Statistical significance is represented as ****p < 0.0001.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that higher bacterial loads per neutrophil are associated with a decreased killing capacity, which can be accurately quantified with our new single-cell killing assay. To quantify bacterial load, we validated the correlation between the fluorescence intensity of the GFP signal and the number of GFP+ S. aureus per neutrophil using imaging flow cytometry. This approach enabled us to categorize neutrophils according to intracellular bacterial load per neutrophil and assess both their killing efficiency and phagolysosomal acidification. Our findings indicate that increased bacterial loads per neutrophil were associated with a progressive decline in acidification capacity, suggesting a potential mechanistic explanation for the impaired killing at higher ingested bacterial loads.

The pH of the phagosome in neutrophils after ingestion is a much disputed topic [25]. Early studies proposed that a substantial drop in phagosomal pH is required for pathogenic killing [26, 27], while subsequent work has shown an initial alkalinization in the phagosome followed by only moderate acidification [28, 29]. Leliefeld et al. [13] showed that S. aureus proliferation is directly correlated with the pH of its surroundings, with reduced growth at lower pH levels. In addition to influencing bacterial growth directly, phagolysosomal acidification modulates key neutrophil antimicrobial mechanisms, including the activity of granule-derived enzymes (such as proteases) and MPO-dependent halogenation reactions (HOCl production) [30, 31]. Our findings support the concept that neutrophil phagolysosomal acidification occurs and that this correlates with the effectiveness of the intracellular killing of S. aureus. Acidification primarily reflects V-ATPase function, which pumps H+ produced by NADPH oxidase (NOX2) into the phagosome thereby lowering pH [32]. We hypothesize that as the number of ingested bacteria per neutrophils increases, the antimicrobial machinery, such as not only proton pumping but also ROS generation and granule enzyme activity, can become relatively “diluted,” thereby reducing killing efficiency. This dilution could occur when multiple bacteria occupy a single phagolysosome, or when several independent phagolysosomes have to share limited antimicrobial resources. The latter is supported by a study by Naish et al. [25] where they observed lysotracker colocalization with singular bacteria, suggesting the formation of multiple phagolysosomes. Further research is still required to define the precise factors that limit bacterial killing when bacterial loads per neutrophil increase.

The heterogeneity in the number of phagocytosed bacteria per neutrophil was striking in our data, with some neutrophils ingesting no bacteria and others more than ten. Interestingly, bacterial killing remained largely similar for neutrophils estimated to have ingested 0.05–2.57 bacteria, whereas the largest decreases in bacterial killing occurred in neutrophils estimated to ingest 8.04–21.5 bacteria per cell. Given that a proportion of neutrophils were capable of phagocytosing within this higher range, the question arises as to what mechanism underlies this variability. This heterogeneity could reflect either intrinsic, predetermined neutrophil phenotypes or stochastic biological variation. For phagocytosis, Hellebrekers et al. [12] proposed a spectrum for phagocytic capacity of neutrophils (“competitive phagocytosis”), in which neutrophils with different phagocytic capacities compete for available bacteria. In their study, neutrophils that failed to phagocytose GFP-expressing S. aureus during initial incubation subsequently phagocytosed at comparable percentages when reexposed to new bacteria. This argues against a fixed neutrophil phenotype, as the population ultimately displays a reproducible distribution of phagocytic capacities. It does, however, still suggest that some neutrophils may be initially more inclined to ingest more bacteria, at least in suspension where chances to phagocytose are equal between neutrophils. In infected tissue, however, neutrophils will arrive sequentially, with the first arriving neutrophils exposed to the highest bacterial burden and therefore having the opportunity to phagocytose more bacteria than those that arrive later. As a result, encounter dynamics and spatial-temporal patterns of uptake likely shape both the bacterial load per neutrophil and killing efficiency, without requiring predetermined cellular phenotypes. Nonetheless, this interpretation is based on a relatively homogeneous neutrophil population under homeostatic conditions and does not exclude the possibility that circulating neutrophil subsets in pathological states, such as cancer or inflammation, exhibit distinct and differential effector functions [33, 34].

Neutrophils may exhibit the behavior of extensive phagocytosis with compromised bacterial killing as an early means of containing infection. As the first responders to infection, neutrophils arrive at the site of infection before monocytes. Because many bacteria species divide rapidly, their ingestion by neutrophils – even without immediate killing – can slow bacterial proliferation. This has been shown by Seiß et al. [15] in vivo with S. aureus infections and by Leliefeld et al. [13] in vitro in fibrin gels. It can therefore be speculated that the earliest arriving neutrophils phagocytose as much as possible, thereby limiting further bacterial expansion. This can also be seen, for example, in Seiß et al. [15], where neutrophils gradually accumulate at an infected ear and persist at high numbers for up to 48 h postinfection. In line with this, a recent publication has interestingly shown that macrophages are capable of trapping live infected neutrophils via macrophage αMβ2 integrin receptor, suggesting the possibility of a functional “handover” where macrophages resolve and process infected neutrophils [35]. However, this neutrophil-based containment may also have drawbacks. Certain pathogens, including S. aureus, exploit phagocytosis as an immune-evasive strategy, using the intracellular compartment to shield themselves from other neutrophils or immune cells and even from antibiotics [36, 37]. Moreover, if neutrophils cannot kill the ingested bacteria and are still mobile, this could even facilitate the spread of pathogens to other areas of the body. For example, Shannon et al. [38] reported that Yersinia pestis that did not interact with neutrophils remained at the infection site, while Y. pestis that associated with neutrophils migrated away from primary infection site. Similarly, Mycobacterium bovis has been shown to be transported as viable bacilli through lymph nodes by neutrophils thereby spreading infection [39]. Future experimental approaches could help determine whether extensive phagocytosis with compromised bacterial killing is ultimately a method of protective containment or a detrimental risk of bacterial dissemination. For instance, locally injecting neutrophils that have ingested high bacteria loads into the skin and tracking subsequent macrophage responses and bacterial dissemination could reveal whether macrophages effectively clear these infected neutrophils or whether neutrophils facilitate pathogen spread.

Here, we present a novel approach to study intracellular killing at the single-cell level. Previous research has primarily examined neutrophil intracellular killing in bulk suspension assays [16, 40, 41]. A fundamental limitation of bulk assays is that they eliminate the ability to directly assess neutrophil heterogeneity, which may be physiologically important. In addition, bulk suspension assays introduce a possible compensatory effect, in which neutrophils that ingest fewer bacteria can compensate the reduced killing capacity of neutrophils with higher ingested bacterial loads. Consistent with this, Li et al. [41] showed that the rate of bacterial killing in bulk suspension depended more on the concentration of neutrophils than on the MOI, indicating that increased neutrophil abundance can buffer the effects of higher bacterial loads. More broadly, bulk assays do not represent physiological conditions, where neutrophil recruitment is gradual and sequential rather than synchronous. Single-cell analysis therefore provides a more physiologically relevant perspective on neutrophil heterogeneity and reveals functional diversity that bulk suspension assays obscure.

To conclude, we show that neutrophils carrying high bacterial loads exhibit a decreased killing capacity, which is at least partially attributable to impaired phagolysosomal acidification. It would be important to determine whether this phenomenon is enriched in specific circulating neutrophil subsets and whether it is altered in particular infectious and/or inflammatory states. Further research is needed to establish whether this represents a beneficial containment mechanism or a risk of bacterial dissemination, and whether targeted approaches, such as intracellular antibiotics, could be used to eliminate pathogens within these poorly performing neutrophils.
