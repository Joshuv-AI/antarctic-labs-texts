A case report of surgical repair of large superior vena cava saccular aneurysm with thrombosis
Authors: Akitoshi Takazawa, Chiho Tokunaga, Norihiro Okada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuperior vena cava aneurysms are very rare mediastinal masses, classified as fusiform or saccular. These lesions are difficult to diagnose, and the optimal treatment strategy remains controversial.Case presentationWe present the case of an asymptomatic 54-year-old woman with an upper mediastinal mass. Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging showed a saccular aneurysm of the superior vena cava with a relatively large amount of intraluminal thrombus. Due to its tendency to enlarge, reconstruction of the superior vena cava with a fresh autologous pericardial patch was performed. Postoperatively, the patient experienced exertional shortness of breath, and CT showed a pulmonary thromboembolism, which resolved after anticoagulant therapy.ConclusionIn this report, we provide an update on the surgical management of patients undergoing treatment for superior vena cava aneurysms. Perioperative anticoagulation is considered important for mitigating the risk of pulmonary thromboembolism.Keywords: Superior vena cava aneurysms, Large aneurysm with thrombosis, Fresh autologous pericardial patch, Postoperative pulmonary thromboembolism, Case report

Background

A superior vena cava (SVC) aneurysm (SVCAn) is a rare condition characterized by abnormal dilation or expansion of the SVC. SVCAn was first described in 1950 by Abbotto [1], with a classification system reported in 1964 [2]. SVCAn can vary in size and may be either symptomatic or asymptomatic. Although several factors, such as congenital, trauma, infection, and inflammatory disease, may contribute to the development of SVCAn, the exact cause remains unclear. According to previous reports, SVCAn is classified into two types: fusiform and saccular [3–5]. Fusiform aneurysms are more commonly observed, whereas saccular aneurysms are exceedingly rare, with no specific prevalence reported [6]. The most serious complications associated with SVCAn include pulmonary embolism and, ultimately, aneurysmal rupture [4, 6–10].

Case presentation

We presented a case of a large saccular SVCAn with thrombosis in an asymptomatic 54-year-old woman. At the age of 50, an abnormal mediastinal shadow was detected on a chest X-ray during a routine check-up. The patient was referred to a pulmonologist, and a contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan confirmed the diagnosis of SVCAn with a maximum diameter of 34 mm, without intraluminal thrombus. At that time, since the patient was asymptomatic and the aneurysm was not thrombosed, she was managed with annual chest X-ray follow-up by her local physician. However, four years later, a follow-up X-ray showed enlargement of the mediastinal shadow. A subsequent contrast-enhanced CT revealed that the SVCAn had enlarged to a maximum diameter of 64 mm (Fig. 1a) and now contained thrombus (Fig. 1b). Preoperative three-dimensional CT showed a saccular SVCAn located approximately 15 mm cranial to the confluence of the SVC, the right brachiocephalic vein, and the innominate vein (Fig. 1c). No right atrial thrombus was identified on preoperative transthoracic echocardiography and contrast-enhanced CT. Considering the risk of pulmonary thromboembolism due to the large thrombus and the potential for rupture with further enlargement, surgical resection was scheduled. Although anticoagulation was considered prior to surgery, it was not initiated because the patient was asymptomatic and the preoperative contrast-enhanced CT showed no evidence of pulmonary embolism. Additionally, the potential risk of thrombus dislodgement associated with anticoagulation was discussed. After the risks and benefits of surgery were explained, the patient opted against anticoagulant therapy.

The surgical strategy involved isolating and excising the SVCAn, followed by reconstruction of the SVC using a fresh autologous pericardial patch.

Through a median sternotomy, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) was established by cannulating the ascending aorta. A two-stage cannula was inserted from the right atrium into the inferior vena cava (IVC), and an additional cannula was placed in the innominate vein to avoid elevation of central venous pressure. The wall of the SVCAn was very thin and fragile; therefore, the surrounding tissue was carefully dissected (Fig. 2a). To prevent dislodgement of the thrombus, the junction of the SVC and the right atrium was encircled and snared with tape for vascular control. Additionally, vessel loops were placed around the distal portion of the right brachiocephalic vein and the innominate vein prior to opening the aneurysmal sac. A large amount of organized thrombus was adherent to the SVCAn wall. The azygos vein drained into the posterior aspect of the aneurysm and was preserved during surgery. The aneurysm was resected anteriorly, the thrombus was removed, leaving the posterior wall and azygos vein intact. Bleeding from the azygos vein was controlled with gentle suction during suturing, and the SVC was reconstructed with a fresh autologous pericardial patch (Fig. 2b). During the procedure under CPB support, cardiac arrest and myocardial protection were not required. The procedure was completed without complications.

The patient was weaned from the ventilator on the day of surgery and transferred to the general ward the following day, at which point aspirin and warfarin were initiated. Due to concerns about postoperative bleeding, heparin was initially withheld. Instead, coagulation parameters, including prothrombin time–international normalized ratio (PT-INR) and activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), were monitored daily to maintain adequate anticoagulation, with a target PT-INR of 1.5 to 2.0. Her hemodynamic status was stable; however, she continued to experience dyspnea during rehabilitation. The patient showed mild hypoxemia, with oxygen saturation falling below 90% when nasal oxygen (1 L/min) was discontinued. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated preserved cardiac function without signs of right ventricular strain. On postoperative day 8, a contrast-enhanced CT scan confirmed a successfully reconstructed SVC. However, pulmonary thromboembolism was detected in the right upper pulmonary artery (Fig. 3a). Anticoagulation therapy was intensified with intravenous administration of heparin, and the target PT- INR was increased to 2.0–2.5.0.5 to ensure adequate anticoagulation. The patient’s symptoms gradually improved, and follow-up CT scans confirmed resolution of the thrombus (Fig. 3b). The patient was discharged on oral anticoagulant therapy. One year after surgery, there has been no recurrence of pulmonary embolism and no evidence of thrombosis. Warfarin was switched to a novel oral anticoagulant (NOAC), and the patient remains stable under continued follow-up.

Discussion and conclusions

SVCAn is a very rare anomaly, with no established numerical prevalence reported. The origin of SVCAn may be congenital or acquired and can be associated with trauma or prior surgeries. Morales et al. reported in 2021 that only 25 cases of saccular SVCAn had been documented before their case [11]. Due to its rarity, reports on SVCAn are limited, and the indications for treatment, as well as the optimal methods and strategies, remain subject to debate. Tomasz et al. reported the use of percutaneous transcatheter thrombin injection for treating SVCAn [10], while Loic et al. described the use of endovascular stenting and coil embolization for filling the SVCAn [12]. These endovascular therapies are considered effective treatment options for small aneurysms. However, managing large saccular aneurysms with thrombus, as in the present case, remains particularly challenging.

The first point of discussion in this case is whether to use CPB or not. CPB has been used in previous reports where surgical resection was performed [6, 13–16]. In the present case, due to the large size of SVCAn and the anticipated extensive defect of the SVC wall after resection, partial clamping was considered unsuitable as it could compromise the operative field and the risk of bleeding. To ensure secure reconstruction, full clamping of the SVC was planned, necessitating CPB to maintain hemodynamic stability. Additionally, to minimize the risk of thrombus dislodgement during aneurysm manipulation, the SVC was clamped before resection. IVC drainage has been reported as the most commonly used venous drainage site, while the brachiocephalic vein has also been used, involving bilateral [13], right-sided [14], and left-sided [15] approaches. Although pulmonary embolism occurred postoperatively, this strategy was intended to reduce the risk of intraoperative embolization.

Regarding the reconstruction of the SVC, the diameter of the SVC was approximately 18 mm, and the orifice of the aneurysm measured approximately 20 mm. Resection resulted in a defect involving nearly half of the SVC circumference. If primary closure had been attempted, the residual lumen would likely have been narrowed to less than 10 mm in diameter. According to Haywood et al., primary closure is not recommended when the defect exceeds 30–50% of the SVC circumference due to the risk of significant stenosis [17]. Therefore, patch reconstruction was chosen to maintain adequate venous patency. The most commonly employed reconstruction method involves the use of fresh autologous pericardium [13, 14]. Soares et al., however, reported a case where reconstruction was performed using a bovine pericardial patch [15]. Among the available materials, we selected a fresh autologous pericardial patch because it is widely used in similar cases and offers excellent biocompatibility and flexibility [13].

The second point of discussion is how to appropriately manage anticoagulant therapy in the perioperative period. According to previous reports, no established consensus regarding anticoagulant therapy has been reached. Koga et al. reported the administration of anticoagulant therapy for preventing thrombus formation during conservative treatment of fusiform SVCAn [18], while Varma et al. administered antiplatelet therapy after resection and direct suture repair of SVCAn [6]. However, no clear strategy has been proposed for perioperative anticoagulant therapy in cases of saccular aneurysm. In our case, despite performing surgical resection under CPB to isolate the SVC and prevent thrombus migration, and the immediate initiation of both warfarin and antiplatelet therapy after surgery, the patient developed a postoperative pulmonary embolism. This sequence of events raises two important considerations. First, the embolization may have already occurred preoperatively, despite the absence of findings on imaging. Second, initiating anticoagulation with heparin immediately after surgery may have been more effective in preventing thromboembolic complications. In retrospect, for patients with large saccular aneurysms containing a thrombus, preoperative anticoagulant therapy may be warranted after careful evaluation of risks and benefits. Furthermore, concurrent administration of heparin with warfarin should also have been considered to ensure effective anticoagulation in the early postoperative period.

In conclusion, surgical resection may represent an effective strategy for patients with enlarged SVCAn, with reconstruction using autologous pericardium under CPB support. Although postoperative pulmonary thromboembolism occurred in this case, the thrombosis resolved following appropriate anticoagulant and antiplatelet therapy. This case highlights the importance of perioperative anticoagulation in managing the risk of thromboembolic complications associated with thrombosed SVC aneurysms.
