Presurgical magnetoencephalography‐derived network control metrics distinguish successful hippocampal resection vs sparing in temporal lobe epilepsy
Authors: Guhan Seshadri NP, Himanshu Kumar, Neha Sara John, Andreas V. Alexopoulos, Richard C. Burgess, Hiroatsu Murakami, Balu Krishnan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSurgical decision‐making in temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) faces a critical challenge in determining whether the hippocampus can be safely spared during anterior temporal resection, particularly when surgery involves the language‐dominant hemisphere. We investigated whether presurgical network control metrics derived from magnetoencephalography (MEG) can differentiate patients who achieved seizure freedom with hippocampal resection (HR) from those who achieved seizure freedom with hippocampal sparing (HS).MethodsWe analyzed presurgical spike‐free interictal MEG in 25 TLE patients with seizure freedom after anterior temporal resection (19 with hippocampal resection, 6 with sparing). Functional networks constructed from MEG data were partitioned into communities using a Louvain‐based consensus clustering algorithm. Within identified hippocampal‐related communities from each patient, we applied control centrality analysis to quantify the influence of each node on community synchronizability. Group differences were assessed using Fisher’s exact tests with false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied within each frequency band and hemisphere. Leave‐one‐subject‐out (LOSO) sensitivity analysis assessed the robustness of findings to individual subjects.ResultsAfter FDR correction, synchronization of the contralateral anterior superior temporal sulcus (STS) in the HS group at beta band emerged as the primary finding (p = .001, q = .02). This finding showed 100% stability in LOSO and persisted in a subgroup analysis excluding patients with hippocampal seizure onset (p = .004). At p < .05, additional region‐frequency differences formed a coherent anatomic pattern: the HR group showed higher synchronization fractions within ipsilateral limbic structures, whereas the HS group showed higher synchronization in the ipsilateral posterior hippocampus and contralateral temporal regions with 100% LOSO stability.SignificancePresurgical MEG‐based network control analysis identified contralateral anterior STS synchronization as a candidate biomarker for hippocampal sparing eligibility in TLE. This proof‐of‐concept finding warrants prospective validation as potential tools to support individualized surgical planning beyond localization‐based approaches.

INTRODUCTION

Temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) is the most common form of drug‐resistant focal epilepsy, affecting nearly one‐third of patients with epilepsy. 1 Surgical resection remains the most effective treatment, with seizure freedom rates exceeding 60%–70% in appropriately selected drug‐resistant focal epilepsy cases. 2 , 3 Among surgical options, anteromesial temporal resection has long been established as a standard treatment for mesial TLE. 2 , 4 , 5 This surgical procedure traditionally involves removing key mesial temporal structures including the hippocampus, amygdala, parahippocampal gyrus, and portions of the anterior temporal neocortex to target the dominant seizure‐generating zone. 6 The hippocampus has received particular attention among the surgical targets, as it is often involved in the seizure‐onset zone, yet it is also central to memory consolidation and higher cognitive functions. 7 , 8 Hippocampal resection may enhance seizure freedom but carries the risk of memory decline and other neurocognitive deficits, 9 , 10 especially on the language‐dominant side. Therefore, the decision to remove or preserve the hippocampus represents one of the most critical dilemmas in epilepsy surgery, and this trade‐off underscores the growing interest in hippocampus‐sparing approaches when clinically feasible, especially for cases without hippocampal sclerosis.

Evidence suggests that hippocampal involvement in epileptogenesis is not uniform across mesial temporal epilepsy (mTLE) patients without hippocampal sclerosis. Some individuals achieve long‐term seizure freedom even when the hippocampus is preserved, whereas others require hippocampal removal for successful outcomes. 11 Therefore, identifying in advance in which patients the hippocampus can be safely preserved, and which truly require its removal not only addresses a central clinical challenge but also offers a critical opportunity to minimize surgical burden and better protect neurocognitive and memory outcomes. Current tools, including structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), hippocampal volumetry, and qualitative assessment of hippocampal sclerosis, provide important but incomplete information. 12 These measures often fail to capture the broader network context in which the hippocampus is embedded. As a result, decisions regarding hippocampal resection are made with a combination of imaging, electrophysiological test results, and other clinical information such as neuropsychological testing, rather than a single biomarker that can predict seizure outcomes.

Network neuroscience provides a principled framework to address this gap by conceptualizing epilepsy as a disorder of distributed brain networks rather than a focal disorder. 13 , 14 Functional connectivity measures derived from electrophysiological recordings can characterize interactions across large‐scale brain systems and reveal network‐level abnormalities associated with epilepsy. Magnetoencephalography (MEG) is well suited for this purpose as it provides whole‐brain recordings with millisecond temporal resolution and has been used widely to characterize epileptic network abnormalities. Previous studies have employed MEG‐derived connectivity and graph‐theoretic metrics for surgical outcome prediction in epilepsy, 15 , 16 , 17 demonstrating the clinical relevance of network‐level measures in presurgical evaluation. Although hippocampal sources are located deep within the brain and produce relatively low‐amplitude magnetic fields compared to neocortical activity, empirical and simulation studies have demonstrated that MEG can detect and reconstruct hippocampal and related mesial temporal signals when appropriate source modeling and analysis techniques are employed. 18 , 19 , 20 Of note, the accuracy of MEG source localization for deep structures, including epileptiform sources, has been characterized extensively, 21 supporting the feasibility of studying mesial temporal network dynamics with appropriate methodological considerations. Resting‐state MEG connectivity studies have shown altered network organization in epilepsy and highlighted the relevance of network topology for surgical outcome. 22 , 23 , 24 However, most prior work has either examined whole‐brain network properties or focused on localized regional abnormalities, providing limited insight into the role of specific epileptogenic subnetworks in seizure freedom. In mTLE, seizure generation and propagation are increasingly understood to arise from interactions within a hippocampal‐centered network rather than from the hippocampus alone. Therefore, this study focuses specifically on the functional community in which the hippocampus is embedded. Community detection methods, such as the Louvain algorithm, identify coherent functional modules that reflect interacting subnetworks, whereas consensus clustering stabilizes these partitions across repeated runs. 25 This approach allows the hippocampus to be studied as part of a mesial temporal network rather than as an isolated structure. We additionally applied control centrality analysis to quantify how each node in the hippocampal network influences large‐scale synchronization dynamics. 26 This framework directly supports our central hypothesis: differences in hippocampal network control, rather than hippocampal involvement alone, differentiate patients who require hippocampal resection from those who achieve seizure freedom with hippocampal sparing.

In this study, we analyzed presurgical spike‐free interictal MEG data from 25 patients with drug‐resistant TLE who subsequently underwent surgery and achieved seizure freedom. Of these, 19 patients underwent hippocampal resection, whereas the hippocampus was spared in 6 patients. We hypothesized that the network organization of the hippocampus, as reflected in its community associations and control centrality, differs systematically between the two groups. By applying consensus community detection and nodal control centrality analyses, we sought to identify the most robust network signatures that distinguish patients requiring hippocampal resection from those who can achieve seizure freedom without it, and to characterize the broader pattern of network control organization associated with each surgical outcome.

METHODS AND MATERIALS

MEG acquisition and preprocessing

We selected 5 min of interictal MEG recordings based on criteria of artifact‐free, spike‐free, continuous segments during wakefulness, from two groups of patients undergoing anterior temporal lobe resection (ATLR): 19 received ATLR with hippocampal removal (HR‐ATLR) and 6 who underwent a hippocampus‐sparing ATLR (HS‐ATLR). The selected MEG recording duration was consistent across all 25 patients, and all the 25 patients achieved seizure freedom after surgical resection (International League Against Epilepsy‐I [ILAE I]). This study was approved by Cleveland Clinic Institutional Review Board, and the complete patient demographic details are given in Table 1. MEG recordings were post‐processed using the temporally extended signal space separation (tSSS) algorithm. 27 This method reduces interference from external magnetic sources and simultaneously corrects for small head movements during the recording. 27 , 28 , 29 The data were then subjected to a series of filtering steps: a band‐pass filter was applied between .5 and 150 Hz, followed by two notch filters at 60 and 120 Hz to mitigate power‐line contamination and its first harmonic. The broadband preprocessing filter was applied up to 150 Hz; however, subsequent analyses were restricted to canonical frequency bands up to 90 Hz (gamma band) and therefore not intended to isolate high‐frequency oscillation (HFO) activity but to ensure signal integrity prior to band‐specific decomposition. Overview of the magnetoencephalography (MEG) analysis pipeline. (1) Spike‐free interictal MEG (5 min) from hippocampus resected (HR) and hippocampus spared (HS) groups was denoised using temporally extended signal space separation (tSSS) and filtered (.5–150 Hz, 60/120 Hz notch). (2) Source reconstruction used subject‐specific forward models, LCMV beamforming, and VEP atlas parcellation (162 regions of interest [ROIs]) to extract ROI time series. (3) Functional connectivity was computed using phase‐locking value (PLV) from Hilbert‐transformed signals across epochs. (4) Community structure was identified via iterative Louvain clustering with consensus (threshold τ = .5). (5) A hippocampal‐centered module and contralateral counterparts were defined based on ≥80% cross‐frequency consistency. (6) Synchronizability and control centrality characterized nodes as synchronizing or desynchronizing. (7) Group differences were tested using Fisher's exact test and odds ratios with correction for multiple comparisons. (8) Robustness was assessed via leave‐one‐subject‐out and subgroup analyses.

Source reconstruction and ROI time series extraction

Source localization was performed in Brainstorm. 30 Individual cortical surfaces were reconstructed from T1‐weighted MRI scans using FreeSurfer, 31 and forward model was computed using overlapping spheres. 32 The overlapping spheres model was selected because it offers balance between computational efficiency and source localization accuracy for MEG applications. 32 For MEG, unlike electroencephalography (EEG), the difference between overlapping spheres and boundary element method (BEM) forward models is modest because magnetic fields are less sensitive to volume conduction through tissue layers. Data and noise covariance matrices were estimated from artifact‐free MEG data and empty‐room recordings, respectively. Neural sources were estimated via a linearly constrained minimum variance (LCMV) beamformer with fixed orientation. 33 Cortical parcellations were defined using the Virtual Epileptic Patient (VEP) atlas (144 regions), 34 and an additional 18 subcortical structures were included to form a hybrid atlas comprising 162 regions of interest (ROIs). ROI time series were extracted by averaging flip‐sign‐corrected vertex signals within each region. 14 FreeSurfer parcellations were visually inspected for all patients with hippocampal sclerosis (n = 3) to confirm acceptable segmentation quality.

Frequency‐specific functional connectivity analysis

ROI time series were filtered into five bands: delta (.5–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz), and gamma (30–90 Hz), using zero‐phase Finite Impulse Response (FIR) filters. Each 5‐min recording was divided into five non‐overlapping 1‐min epochs. The use of 1‐min epochs allows stable estimation of phase locking value (PLV) while assuming local quasi‐stationarity. 35 For each epoch, the instantaneous phase was extracted via the Hilbert transform and PLV was computed between all ROI pairs in source space following LCMV beamforming, which acts as a spatial filter that substantially reduces volume conduction artifacts. The five connectivity matrices were averaged to produce a single 162 × 162 adjacency matrix per subject per band.

Community detection and consensus clustering

For each subject, the resulting 162 × 162 connectivity matrix per frequency band was used to infer large‐scale network modules using a multirun Louvain consensus framework to obtain robust and reproducible community assignments. 25 The Louvain algorithm was run with the default resolution parameter γ = 1.0. The Louvain algorithm is stochastic and may yield variable partitions across repeated runs due to random initialization and modularity degeneracy. To address this, we adopted a multirun consensus strategy where the Louvain modularity maximization was iterated 100 times across multiple random seeds for each subject, generating an ensemble of subject partitions. A co‐classification matrix was subsequently constructed, in which each entry represented the fraction of runs in which a given pair of nodes was assigned to the same module. This co‐classification matrix was thresholded at 𝜏 = .5 based on majority‐vote criterion, requiring that nodes co‐occur in the same module in more than half of the runs to be considered a reliable connection. This majority‐vote criterion is widely used in consensus partitioning and balances module stability with preservation of network resolution. 25 The thresholded matrix was then re‐clustered using the Louvain algorithm to obtain a consensus partition. This iterative procedure was repeated until convergence, defined as the absence of changes in community labels across successive iterations, yielding a final consensus modular structure for each subject.

Network of interest (hippocampal community)

The VEP atlas subdivides the hippocampus into anterior and posterior portions. Given the established role of the anterior hippocampus in mTLE, we focused on communities containing the anterior hippocampus. For each subject and frequency band, community detection was performed independently, and the community containing the contralateral anterior hippocampus was identified. The contralateral hippocampus was used as a reference node to provide a stable and standardized anatomic anchor for community definition across subjects, avoiding potential confounds from pathological alterations in the ipsilateral epileptogenic hippocampus. This contralateral community was then mapped to the ipsilateral hemisphere using atlas‐defined homologous regions from the VEP parcellation. The VEP atlas defines regions as anatomically corresponding left–right pairs, enabling consistent interhemispheric mapping. This approach provides a standardized anatomic framework for extracting comparable networks across subjects, rather than assuming identical functional organization between hemispheres.

Across patients, consensus community detection within each frequency band consistently identified a community containing the contralateral anterior hippocampus. This recurring hippocampal community was observed across all frequency bands and served as the basis for a common network module. To define a frequency‐invariant core network, we selected regions that co‐occurred within this hippocampal module in at least four of the five frequency bands (≥80% consistency threshold). This threshold was defined as a priori to prioritize robustness against band‐specific variability rather than to optimize group differences. This frequency‐invariant community served as the common anatomic substrate for all subsequent synchronizability and control centrality analyses across subjects.

Synchronization and control centrality analysis

Network synchronizability and nodal control centrality was quantified from the Laplacian spectra of subject‐specific hippocampal‐centered community (network of interest). For each hemisphere, the adjacency matrix A of the hippocampal community was converted to a graph Laplacian L = D − A. Community synchronizability was defined as S=λ2/λN. Control centrality for each node j was computed as Cj=S−j−SS, where S₋ⱼ is the synchronizability after virtual removal of node j. 26 Nodes with C j < 0 (synchronizing) stabilize network coherence; nodes with C j > 0 (desynchronizing) destabilize it. This was computed separately for ipsilateral and contralateral subnetworks.

Statistical comparison between groups

Demographic and clinical variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U (continuous) and Fisher's exact (categorical) tests. For primary analyses, nodes were classified as synchronizing (C j < 0) or desynchronizing (C j > 0) per subject, band, and hemisphere. For each region, 2 × 2 contingency tables (group × classification) were constructed, with each subject contributing one observation per table. Group differences were assessed with Fisher's exact test; effect sizes were reported as odds ratios (ORs). Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction (q < .05) was applied within each band and hemisphere, reflecting independent neurophysiological bands and separately derived hemispheric network. FDR‐surviving results (q < .05) are reported as primary findings and results with p < .05 are reported as exploratory findings. Robustness was evaluated via leave‐one‐subject‐out (LOSO) analysis, and a subgroup analysis compared HS patients with HR patients whose seizure‐onset zone (SOZ) did not involve the hippocampus (n = 8). All code is available at https://github.com/spikelab‐ccf/MEG‐Network‐Control‐TLE.

RESULTS

Group demographic and clinical characteristics

The HR and HS groups did not differ significantly in age at surgery (HR: median 37, IQR 25–43; HS: median 25, IQR 20–26; U = 82.5, p = .110), sex (63% vs 33% male; p = .350), surgical side (58% vs 100% left; p = .129), pathology (FCD vs non‐FCD; p = .350), MRI findings (p = 1.000), ASM count (both median 2; U = 53.0, p = .814), or follow‐up (median 6.7 vs 8.2 years; U = 38.0, p = .239). All HS patients had left‐sided surgery; all three hippocampal sclerosis patients were in the HR group (Table 1).

Consensus‐derived mesial temporal community

Consensus clustering yielded a 17‐region mesial temporal community centered on the contralateral anterior hippocampus (Figure 2). Pairwise Jaccard similarity across consensus thresholds (τ = .3–.7) ranged from .76 to .93 (Figure S1). The number of Louvain communities per subject ranged between 3 and 7 (Table S1), with no between‐group differences in any band (all p > .10; Table S2). Contralateral temporal region (primary finding)

The contralateral anterior STS in the beta band was the sole FDR‐surviving comparison (p = .001, q = .02, OR = .2) with the HS group showing higher synchronizing control compared to the HR group (LOSO stability = 100%). The same region showed a strong theta‐band effect with similar observation of higher synchronization in the HS group (p = .005, q = .08; OR = .3; LOSO = 100%).

Ipsilateral medial and lateral temporal structures (exploratory findings)

Three additional comparisons reached p < .05: ipsilateral amygdala in beta (p = .02, q = .33; OR = ∞; LOSO = 100%), ipsilateral posterior hippocampus in gamma (p = .02, q = .33; OR = .5; LOSO = 100%), and ipsilateral T1‐lateral‐anterior in theta (p = .023, q = .38; OR = 14; LOSO = 44%).

Directional consistency

Among the five comparisons that reached p < .05, two showed higher synchronization in the HR group, both involving ipsilateral limbic regions (amygdala and T1‐lateral‐anterior). The remaining three showed higher synchronization in the HS group, involving the ipsilateral posterior hippocampus and the contralateral anterior STS, with the latter observed in two frequency bands.

Sensitivity analyses

The primary finding remained significant in all 25 LOSO iterations. Among exploratory findings, the contralateral STS (theta), ipsilateral amygdala (beta), and ipsilateral posterior hippocampus (gamma) also showed 100% LOSO stability; the ipsilateral T1‐lateral‐anterior (theta) showed 44% stability. In the subgroup analysis (8 HR patients without hippocampal SOZ vs 6 HS, Table S3), the contralateral anterior STS remained the strongest discriminator (beta: p = .004, q = .07; OR = .08; LOSO = 100%), with additional effects in the contralateral temporal pole (beta: p = .02, q = .29; OR = .03; LOSO = 100%) and ipsilateral T1‐lateral‐anterior (theta: p = .025; q = .52; OR = 35; LOSO = 100%; beta: p = .025; q = .29; OR = 35; LOSO = 100%). No comparisons survived FDR in this reduced sample (n = 14).

DISCUSSION

Presurgical network control as a marker for surgical decision‐making

This study investigated whether presurgical MEG‐derived network control metrics could inform the decision of hippocampal resection vs sparing in TLE. We identified a hippocampal‐centered functional community and quantified each node's influence on community synchronization by integrating consensus clustering with control centrality. The most robust finding was that synchronizing control of the contralateral anterior STS distinguished HS from HR patients (q = .02) with 100% LOSO stability. This was supported by a broader pattern of network control differences that formed an anatomically coherent dissociation between surgical groups. A key methodological contribution was the derivation of a frequency‐invariant hippocampal community through cross‐band consensus clustering, providing a principled and reproducible anatomic framework for control centrality analysis.

Our approach extends prior work demonstrating the prognostic value of non‐invasive network analysis in epilepsy surgery. Nissen et al. 17 identified the epileptogenic zone through hub status in resting‐state MEG networks, and Corona et al. 15 mapped epileptogenic networks through virtual sensor implantation, showing that connectivity‐derived hub metrics predicted outcome better than dipole localization. Corona et al. 16 further demonstrated that this framework could replicate intracranial EEG connectivity features non‐invasively. Although these studies focused on predicting general surgical outcome, our work addresses a distinct question: whether network control metrics can inform the specific decision of hippocampal sparing. The control centrality framework complements hub‐based approaches by quantifying not just centrality within a network, but each node's directional influence on community synchronization dynamics.

Contralateral anterior temporal synchronization as a candidate biomarker

The contralateral anterior STS emerged as the strongest discriminator between surgical groups, with complete separation in the beta band: every patient who achieved seizure freedom with hippocampal sparing exhibited synchronizing control at this node, whereas only a minority of HR patients did. This pattern extended to the theta band (100% LOSO stability), suggesting a frequency‐robust rather than band‐specific phenomenon. The anterior STS is a multimodal association area within the temporal cortex that participates in auditory, language, and social processing networks, and has established connectivity with mesial temporal structures including the hippocampus and amygdala. 36

From a network control perspective, the synchronizing influence of the contralateral anterior STS may reflect a distributed bilateral architecture in which contralateral temporal regions actively contribute to stabilizing hippocampal network dynamics. Prior work has shown that preserved contralateral temporal network involvement is associated with favorable postoperative outcomes and cognitive preservation. 37 , 38 In patients where this bilateral stabilization is intact, hippocampal resection may not be necessary for seizure control because the broader temporal network provides sufficient regulatory capacity. Conversely, the absence of contralateral synchronizing control in most HR patients suggests a network configuration in which synchronization control is concentrated ipsilaterally within limbic structures. This interpretation aligns with prior findings showing that non‐invasive MEG‐derived network measures can identify clinically meaningful network hubs whose resection is associated with seizure freedom 15 , 16 , 17 and high‐frequency oscillation mapping. 21 Although those studies identified epileptogenic hubs for resection, the present study identifies a contralateral node whose synchronizing influence may serve as a marker of network configurations in which hippocampal resection is unnecessary. Of note, this effect persisted in a subgroup analysis restricted to HR patients without hippocampal SOZ (p = .004), suggesting that contralateral STS synchronization captures aspects of network organization not fully explained by conventional seizure localization.

Ipsilateral medial and lateral temporal network control profiles: Exploratory evidence

Although no individual finding beyond the contralateral anterior STS survived FDR correction, three exploratory results showed 100% LOSO stability: ipsilateral amygdala synchronization in the HR group (beta), ipsilateral posterior hippocampal synchronization in the HS group (gamma), and contralateral STS synchronization in the HS group (theta). These consistent effects suggest the presence of real group differences that the current sample may be underpowered to detect after correction for multiple comparisons. The ipsilateral amygdala demonstrated elevated synchronizing control exclusively in HR patients, which may reflect tightly coupled limbic hypersynchrony. 36 In contrast, preserved posterior hippocampal gamma synchronization in HS patients may indicate intact local inhibitory regulation. 39 Taken together, the directional pattern across these findings, with ipsilateral limbic synchronization characterizing the resection group and posterior or contralateral temporal synchronization characterizing the sparing group, suggests a coherent distinction in network control architecture that warrants further validation in larger cohorts.

Clinical implications for surgical planning

The present findings suggest that presurgical MEG‐derived network control metrics may provide clinically relevant information beyond conventional seizure localization. The contralateral anterior STS synchronization pattern represents the most promising candidate biomarker for identifying patients in whom hippocampal sparing is feasible. Of note, the subgroup analysis demonstrated that the STS effect persisted when comparing HR patients without hippocampal SOZ (n = 8) to HS patients (p = .004), with additional effects emerging in the contralateral temporal pole and ipsilateral T1‐lateral‐anterior (100% LOSO). This provides preliminary evidence that the metric captures aspects of network organization not fully explained by SOZ localization alone. If validated prospectively, this measure could complement existing presurgical tools including structural MRI, neuropsychological testing, and invasive monitoring.

Methodological limitations and future directions

This study demonstrates the feasibility and potential clinical relevance of control centrality mapping; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. The sample size was modest, particularly in the HS group (n = 6), thereby limiting generalizability and precluding formal classification analysis. Surgical decisions were made clinically, and it remains unclear whether some HR patients could have achieved seizure freedom with hippocampal preservation. Moreover, seizure freedom following hippocampal sparing does not necessarily imply that the hippocampus was uninvolved in the epileptogenic network, and seizure freedom itself is an imperfect proxy for validating the biological rationale of the surgical approach. Hippocampal involvement in the SOZ differed between groups (present in most HR, absent in HS). The subgroup analysis restricted to HR patients without hippocampal SOZ (n = 8) showed persistence of the contralateral anterior STS effect (p = .004), suggesting that network control profiles may capture features beyond SOZ localization, although confirmation requires larger cohorts. Group differences in surgical laterality and unassessed effects of specific anti‐seizure medications may confound results. Spike‐free selection based on visual inspection does not exclude visually absent epileptiform activity, although any such residual activity would affect both groups similarly and cannot account for the observed between‐group differences. In addition, structural abnormalities such as hippocampal sclerosis may affect the accuracy of automated FreeSurfer parcellation, although only three patients had hippocampal sclerosis and all were in the HR group.

The mirroring of contralateral community labels assumes anatomic correspondence based on the bilaterally symmetric VEP atlas parcellation. 34 Although this approach provides a standardized framework for consistent interhemispheric comparison, it does not account for potential subject‐specific deviations in functional organization. Moreover, the contralateral hemisphere in TLE is less affected, rather than fully normal, 40 , 41 which may attenuate the observed differences. The biological meaningfulness of the identified communities, although supported by their anatomic coherence and cross‐frequency stability, remains to be formally validated through convergence with structural connectomes or intracranial EEG connectivity. Future studies could directly compare ipsilateral and contralateral community structures to quantify the degree of asymmetry introduced by the epileptogenic process.

MEG source localization for deep structures remains less precise than for neocortex, and PLV may retain sensitivity to residual volume conduction despite beamforming; future validation with measures insensitive to zero‐lag coupling (e.g., imaginary coherence, phase lag index) is warranted. Tamilia et al. 21 demonstrated that MEG localized the irritative zone with ~15 mm accuracy relative to intracranial EEG, outperforming conventional and high‐density EEG, and that proximity to resection predicted outcome. Although these findings support the feasibility of MEG‐based mesial temporal analysis, the spatial precision for hippocampal and amygdalar sources remains lower than for neocortical regions.

Finally, the present study did not assess the predictive accuracy of the identified network metrics at the individual patient level. Establishing clinical utility will require prospective, multicenter studies with larger and more balanced cohorts that can support robust cross‐validation of predictive models. Future work should extend this framework to larger, prospective, multicenter cohorts and integrate control centrality with complementary non‐invasive biomarkers such as MEG‐derived network hubs. 16 , 17

CONCLUSION

This proof‐of‐concept study demonstrates that presurgical MEG‐based network control analysis can distinguish patients achieving seizure freedom with hippocampal resection vs sparing. The contralateral anterior STS synchronization in the beta band emerged as the most robust biomarker, surviving FDR correction with perfect LOSO stability. This finding was embedded within a broader pattern in which limbic‐dominant synchronization characterized the resection group and posterior/contralateral temporal synchronization characterized the sparing group. These preliminary findings motivate prospective, multicenter validation of contralateral temporal synchronization as a candidate presurgical biomarker for hippocampal sparing decisions in drug‐resistant TLE.
