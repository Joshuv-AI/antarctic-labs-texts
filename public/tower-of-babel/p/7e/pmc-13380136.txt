Delayed Sciatic Neuropathy Due to Late Migration of Cerclage Wire Following Total Hip Arthroplasty
Authors: Yakup Yildirim, Anar Alakbarov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFemoral cerclage wiring is commonly used during total hip arthroplasty (THA) to prevent propagation of intraoperative femoral cracks. Neurologic complications related to cerclage wiring are extremely rare, particularly as delayed presentations. We report a 62-year-old woman who developed acute, severe sciatic pain 7 years after uncemented THA in which a prophylactic 18-gauge cerclage wire had been applied for a nondisplaced femoral crack. Lumbar imaging was normal, but pelvic radiographs showed wire breakage. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated migration of a wire fragment adjacent to the sciatic nerve distal to the lesser trochanter. Surgical exploration revealed a hook-shaped wire fragment encircling the nerve. Wire removal and neurolysis led to complete symptom resolution. Late wire breakage and migration may cause severe neurologic complications after THA.Keywords: Sciatic nerve, Late complication, Hip arthroplasty, Cerclage wiring

Introduction

Circumferential femoral cerclage wiring is commonly used in non-cemented total hip arthroplasty (THA), either prophylactically to prevent femoral crack formation or to enhance stability and prevent distal crack propagation. Vascular injuries related to cerclage wiring have been reported at rates of 2–7%; however, nerve injury is extremely rare [1].To date, only 4 cases of sciatic nerve injury associated with cerclage wiring have been reported, all of which occurred as acute intraoperative injuries except for 1 case [[2], [3], [4], [5]].

Here, we present an unusual case of delayed sciatic nerve injury developing 7 years and 4 months after uncemented THA. This case highlights a rare but important late complication of femoral cerclage wiring and emphasizes the need for long-term awareness of neurologic symptoms following THA.

Case history

Patient written informed consents and permissions were obtained. A 62-year-old woman underwent right-sided uncemented THA for severe degenerative osteoarthritis. A standard anterolateral approach was used. During femoral stem implantation, a small crack occurred that did not extend distal to the lesser trochanter. Although the femoral component was stable, a prophylactic circumferential 18-gauge monofilament cerclage wire was applied.

Postoperatively, the patient was allowed partial weight-bearing for 3 weeks, followed by full-weight bearing. The postoperative course was uneventful, and follow-up radiographs showed no abnormalities.

Fourteen months later, the patient underwent left-sided uncemented THA. No femoral crack occurred on the left side, and cerclage wiring was not required. Healing on the left side was also uneventful. The patient was followed at regular intervals for 2 years after the second operation and was subsequently lost to follow-up. During the follow-up, there was no evidence of stem subsidence or loosening (Fig. 1).

Latest follow-up X-ray 1 year prior to disintegration and wire migration.

Seven years and 4 months after the right THA, the patient contacted our clinic on phone and reporting sudden-onset severe pain involving the entire right leg. The pain began during a long-distance bus journey, progressively worsened during travel, and became unbearable upon disembarking. She was advised to seek immediate local medical attention but declined and instead returned to our institution.

At presentation, the patient had intense, intractable pain and appeared restless. Strong analgesics were administered before examination. She described radiating pain from the right buttock to the calf, accompanied by mild numbness over the posterior thigh. Motor strength was preserved, and deep tendon reflexes were intact. Straight leg raise testing was mildly painful, while passive hip motion did not exacerbate symptoms.

Lumbar magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was obtained due to suspicion of disc herniation. Although mild degenerative changes and minimal disc protrusions were observed, these findings did not explain the severity of symptoms. The largely unremarkable lumbar MRI prompted further evaluation of the hip.

Pelvic radiographs revealed a broken and fragmented cerclage wire on the right side, without evidence of implant loosening or bony pathology (Fig. 2). MRI examination demonstrated a wire fragment located adjacent to the sciatic nerve at a level distal to the lesser trochanter (Fig. 3).

Detection of disintegration and wire migration at diagnosis (white arrow).

MRI view demonstrating the wire.

Urgent surgical exploration was planned. Under general anesthesia, the patient was positioned prone. A longitudinal incision was made from the lower buttock to the proximal posterior thigh. The sciatic nerve was carefully explored proximally and distally, and vessel loops were placed for protection. A hook-shaped cerclage wire fragment encircling and anteriorly displacing the sciatic nerve was identified (Fig. 4). The wire was removed, and the nerve was released from surrounding fibrotic tissue. The postoperative course was uneventful, with complete resolution of pain. The patient returned to daily activities within 2 weeks. She was followed for 20 months at regular intervals to monitor for neurological symptoms or other complications. She did not report any complaints during the follow-up period (Fig. 5).

Discussion

This case represents the second reported instance of delayed sciatic neuropathy caused by late migration of a cerclage wire following THA. The previously reported delayed case differed substantially from the present one, as it involved a lateral transtrochanteric approach and interlocking square-knot cerclage fixation of a trochanteric osteotomy, with early postoperative wire breakage [5]. In contrast, the current case involved circumferential prophylactic cerclage wiring applied for a femoral crack without causing any symptoms for years. Despite these differences, both cases required surgical removal of the wire due to severe sciatic nerve irritation.

Most reported sciatic nerve injuries associated with cerclage wiring have occurred intraoperatively. Mallory described a case in which a cerclage wire penetrated the sciatic nerve during transtrochanteric THA, resulting in permanent motor deficit despite early intervention [4]. Nakamura et al. reported acute sciatic nerve strangulation following cerclage fixation of a periprosthetic femoral fracture, with limited neurological recovery after intervention [3]. Yue et al. described a delayed but still relatively early case of traumatic sciatic nerve transection caused by a cerclage wire, resulting in persistent motor dysfunction despite nerve reconstruction [2]. Compared with these reports, the present case is notable for its very late onset, absence of motor deficit, and complete recovery after timely surgical treatment.

Delayed cerclage wire failure is generally attributed to metal fatigue resulting from cyclic loading. Micromotion and interaction with body fluid are the other proposed factors for late metal failure. The fatigue strength of stainless steel decreases markedly in the living body and is suggested that the fatigue strength of metal is governed by the corrosive effects of environment [6]. Early failure usually occurs due to excessive mechanical strain, whereas late failure is often associated with nonunion. In the present case, fracture healing was complete, suggesting that progressive metal corrosion may have contributed to weakening of the wire. Stem subsidence and loosening may be considered potential contributing factors to wire disintegration and migration; but there was no convincing evidence of radiological or clinical evidence of stem loosening or progressive subsidence. Prolonged sitting during long-distance travel may have acted as a precipitating mechanical factor, leading to wire breakage and migration.

Summary

Although cerclage wire fracture is often considered clinically insignificant, this case demonstrates that late wire migration can result in neurological complications many years after surgery. Additionally, the use of monofilament cerclage wire may have contributed to this complication. Multifilament cable systems, polymer-based cable systems, and looped wire constructs individualized for patient may offer greater resistance to fatigue and, even if broken, are less likely to form sharp fragments capable of encircling and injuring the sciatic nerve. This consideration may be relevant when selecting cerclage materials during THA.
