Europe’s hot weather: a health emergency hiding in plain sight
Authors: Hans Henri P Kluge
Published: 2026

Europe has a preparedness problem on top of a hot weather problem. The tools to prevent most heat-related deaths exist, are well documented, and have been available for nearly two decades. What’s missing is not knowledge. It’s urgency, and a shift to treating heat as a recurring public health risk, not a one-off emergency. If the deaths and growing disease burden being counted this summer don’t produce the sustained planning, investment, and commitment needed to fix the problem, it’s hard to imagine what will.

In early July, I convened an emergency call on extreme heat, bringing together representatives and experts from WHO European Region Member States, the European Commission, and civil society. With >130 participants, the agenda was clear: what did the current heatwave teach us, and are we ready for what comes next?

Heat kills

The meeting confirmed something that the data have been telling us for years, and that political systems have been too slow to absorb: extreme heat is not only a weather event. It is a health security threat.

Heat kills quietly, indoors, mostly older people living alone, as well as outdoors, often agriculture and construction workers. Heat kills without the visible drama of a flood or a wildfire. That invisibility costs lives. The European Region has lost >200 000 people to heat in just the past 4 years [1]. This summer’s toll is still being counted, but already, just five countries have reported nearly 10 000 excess deaths due to the heat. Western Europe recorded its hottest June on record, surpassing the previous record set in June 2025 [2].

Heat is not an equal opportunity killer. Women face heat-related mortality roughly twice that of men [3]. Pregnant women exposed to extreme heat face elevated risks of premature delivery, low birth weight, and stillbirth [4]. Older adults living alone, people with chronic conditions, outdoor workers, residents of poorly insulated buildings, and those without access to cooling are all disproportionately exposed. The 2003 European heatwave killed an estimated 70 000 people in a single summer [5]. The lesson was learned slowly and incompletely, and the most vulnerable are still paying for that delay.

What works

The evidence on what works is not in question. Countries with functioning heat-health action plans responded fast, coordinated well, and protected their people effectively during the current heatwave. Examples shared by Member States are numerous and inspiring. Italy’s mortality surveillance system, active in 45 cities, gave decision-makers near real-time data throughout the crisis. Spain worked directly with media partners to improve how risk is communicated. Austria activated workplace heat-protection regulations and brought national and regional coordination structures together. North Macedonia partnered with Red Cross and Red Crescent teams to reach people without permanent housing. These are not experimental interventions. They are replicable responses, built on evidence, that save lives.

WHO published updated Heat-Health Action Plans Guidance in June 2026, setting out an eight-element framework covering governance, early warning systems, at-risk populations, communication, health system resilience, reducing heat exposure, surveillance, and evaluation [4]. Implementation of such plans has been associated with a roughly 25% reduction in excess heat deaths [6]. Europe’s 2023 heat mortality burden would have been 80% higher without the adaptation measures already in place [7]. The tools exist. The evidence base is strong.

And yet fewer than half of WHO European Region Member States have a national heat-health action plan. That is not a knowledge gap. The guidance has existed since 2008. It is a political will gap, and it is measurable in lives.

Persistent gaps

The gaps named by countries at our July meeting were specific and solvable. Many people do not recognize they are personally at risk. Cooling facilities are insufficient and poorly publicized. Long-term care residents, homeless people, and socially isolated older adults are still not being reached consistently. Some countries face procedural delays in formally declaring heatwaves, slowing the public health response at exactly the moment speed is most critical.

Heat is also inseparable from the wider climate crisis. A recent study estimates that the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation faces a 25% risk of shutdown even under a Paris-compatible emissions scenario [8]. Six of nine planetary boundaries have already been transgressed [9]. The northward expansion of the Aedes albopictus mosquito, driven by warming temperatures and other environmental factors, is placing an additional 5 million people per year at risk from dengue and chikungunya [10]. Extreme heat does not exist in isolation from these wider systemic shifts—it is just one symptom of a climate under unprecedented pressure.

The Pan-European Commission on Climate and Health, which I convened last year, and which launched its Call to Action in May 2026, named heat as a core dimension of the climate-health crisis and called on Member States to recognize that climate change poses a real threat to human health and security [11]. The 2023 Budapest Declaration on Environment and Health committed WHO European Region Member States to developing and implementing heat-health action plans [12]. The political commitments are on paper. What the current heatwave has shown, graphically and lethally, is that the gap between commitment and implementation is still too wide.

What next?

Three things need to happen now to close this gap. First, every country without a heat-health action plan needs one, using existing WHO guidance as a resource. Second, every country with a plan must test it, fund it, and connect it to the cross-sectoral coordination structures that turn a document into a system that actually protects people. Governments must confront the longer-term reality: heat-health action plans buy time, but they don’t stop the temperature from rising. And finally, the structural changes—to buildings, transport, cities, food systems, and the fossil fuel subsidies financing the climate crisis—must accelerate in parallel. Prevention is always cheaper than treatment. The summer of 2026 is the most expensive possible way to learn that lesson.

Our ambition is zero preventable heat deaths. That’s not a slogan. It is a public health target. And it is still achievable—if we decide it is.
