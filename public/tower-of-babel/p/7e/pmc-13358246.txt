Prevalence and frequency of haematological testing in patients initiating clozapine in the USA
Authors: Katsiaryna Bykov, Mufaddal Mahesri, Krista F Huybrechts, Lauren V Moran, Catherine S Hwang, Junyi Wang, Joyce Lii, Gita A Toyserkani, Esther H Zhou, Cynthia LaCivita, Gerald J Dal Pan, Aaron S Kesselheim, Ameet Sarpatwari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUntil June 2025, individuals initiating clozapine in the USA were required to undergo routine haematological testing in accordance with the Clozapine Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS) programme. Although the Clozapine REMS programme was eliminated in June 2025, clinical and prescribing recommendations on testing remained intact. Little is known, however, about adherence to testing requirements in practice.ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to assess the prevalence and frequency of haematological testing in patients on clozapine.MethodsA cohort study using three US health insurance claims databases (1 January 2000–31 March 2022) assessed (1) proportion of individuals with at least one haematological test (white blood cell count with differential) prior to clozapine initiation and (2) frequency of testing (number of tests per 30 days of follow-up) during months 0–6, 7–12 and 13–24 of clozapine therapy. Tests were identified based on billing codes; patients hospitalised during the assessment period were assumed to have received testing.FindingsA total of 62 003 clozapine initiations (mean (SD) age, 36 (15) years; 57% male) were identified, of which 71% were preceded by testing within 60 prior days. The mean (SD) testing rate while on therapy was 2.2 (1.8) tests per patient for each 30-day period during the first 6 months of treatment, 2.0 (1.8) tests per 30 days during months 7–12 and 1.5 (1.4) tests per 30 days during months 13–24.ConclusionsIn this study of individuals initiating clozapine in the USA, haematological testing was completed at a lower-than-expected rate prior to clozapine initiation and during the first 6 months of treatment but was in accordance with testing requirements thereafter.Clinical implicationsAlthough clozapine drug labelling recommends routine haematological testing, especially during the first 6 months of treatment, such testing may not consistently occur. Reasons for less-than-recommended testing frequency should be further investigated.

Background

Since its approval by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 1989, clozapine has been the only antipsychotic on the US market with an indication for treatment-resistant schizophrenia.1 2 The drug is also indicated for reducing suicidal behaviour in patients with schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder in the USA, and is recommended by multiple clinical guidelines and professional societies, including the American Psychiatric Association, as a second-line therapy for people with schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder, complicated by persistent suicidality or aggressive behaviour.2 These recommendations are based on evidence demonstrating the drug’s efficacy compared with other antipsychotics in these patient populations.35 Yet there is a concern that clozapine is underused in the USA and globally.68

Barriers to wider clozapine use may include routine haematological testing to monitor for clozapine-induced agranulocytosis, a rare but potentially fatal side effect of the drug.911 In the USA, clozapine products were initially subject to safety programmes that included restricted distribution by each manufacturer of the drug to manage the risk of severe neutropenia. From 1998 to 2005, these programmes required a baseline white blood cell (WBC) count, then weekly monitoring of WBC counts for the first 6 months, then every other week thereafter. In 2005, the testing requirement changed to a baseline WBC count and then weekly monitoring of WBC counts and absolute neutrophil counts (ANCs) for the first 6 months, every other week for the second 6 months and then every 4 weeks thereafter. Because multiple generics were available and each manufacturer had their own safety programme, patients and providers had to enrol in multiple programmes if their supply changed from one manufacturer to another.12

In 2015, the manufacturer programmes were replaced by a single Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS) programme, which required the same frequency of testing but for ANCs alone (provided ANCs remained within normal range (≥1500 /µL)).13 Prescribers had to be certified in this Clozapine REMS programme to prescribe the drug for outpatient use and follow requirements for ANC monitoring before and during treatment.13 Patients also had to be enrolled in the Clozapine REMS programme by a certified provider. In addition, outpatient pharmacies had to be certified in the Clozapine REMS programme to receive and dispense clozapine, and pharmacists had to obtain authorisation from the Clozapine REMS programme prior to dispensing clozapine or verify that patients had a current ANC within the acceptable range.13 Due to challenges with implementation, prescriber and pharmacy certification were not enforced until February 2019. Moreover, due to concerns about denying patients access to clozapine, implementing the requirement to have a current ANC documented in the REMS prior to dispensing was also delayed.12 After March 2020, around the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, prescribers could use their best medical judgement on whether the benefits and risks of continuing treatment with clozapine in the absence of laboratory testing was in the best interest of the patient.14 In July 2021, the frequency of reporting ANCs to REMS was reduced to monthly, regardless of monitoring frequency. In June 2025, the FDA officially eliminated the Clozapine REMS programme, though clinical and prescribing recommendations on ANC monitoring in the FDA-approved clozapine drug labelling remained intact.15

While haematological testing requirements for clozapine have been in place for more than 30 years, little is known about adherence to these requirements in clinical practice. The objective of this study was to assess the prevalence and frequency of haematological testing in patients starting clozapine therapy, as observed in US health insurance claims data. This evaluation was part of a broader evaluation of REMS programmes and how they affect drug access, health outcomes, and patient and physician experiences in the USA.16

Methods

Data sources

Data came from three national US healthcare insurance claims databases: (1) a Medicaid database (2000–2018), with data from the joint state-federal public health insurance programme for individuals with low income; (2) Optum’s de-identified Clinformatics Data Mart database (2004–31 March 2022), a large commercial health insurance database; and (3) the Merative Marketscan database (2003–2020), another large commercial health insurance database. All databases contain longitudinal patient-level information for billed inpatient and outpatient services, outpatient pharmacy dispensings, and demographics with enrolment information.17 The data sources were chosen for the study because, unlike electronic health records, health insurance claims data in the USA are not limited to a specific health network or a hospital and contain data on all billed healthcare services, regardless of where they were provided. Claims data also include patients across all US states, providing a representative sample of the US population. For this study we included data from both commercial and public health insurance, ensuring representativeness across groups of different socio-economic and employment status.

Study design and population

We designed a cohort study of patients who initiated clozapine therapy between 1 July 2000 and 31 March 2022 and had at least 6 months of insurance enrolment prior to clozapine initiation with no clozapine dispensing during that time. In study-eligible patients, haematological testing was assessed prior to clozapine initiation and while on therapy. To assess testing while on therapy, patients were followed from the day of clozapine initiation for 24 months (731 days) and censored on death, end of insurance enrolment, end of data, admission to a nursing home or hospice, or discontinuation of clozapine, if occurred earlier than the end of follow-up (figure 1). Clozapine discontinuation was defined as no subsequent refills of clozapine within 30 days following the end of the days’ supply for the last dispensing. Patients could contribute multiple clozapine initiation episodes, as long as eligibility criteria were satisfied (no prior use for 6 months and at least 6 months of insurance enrolment). Multiple clozapine initiation episodes were allowed and were treated as independent observations, because, as per clozapine label recommendations for ANC testing, if patients interrupt clozapine treatment for 30 days or longer, they should be monitored as if new patients.18 Additional eligibility criteria were implemented for Medicaid enrollees. Specifically, we excluded Medicaid enrollees 65 years of age and older at the time of clozapine initiation (index date) because these individuals also qualify for Medicare, and claims for these dual-eligibles may be incomplete in the Medicaid database. In addition, we required eligible Medicaid beneficiaries to be enrolled in fee-for-service plans or managed care plans with evidence of complete claims and dispensing data. We also excluded MarketScan enrollees with Medicare due to potential lack of claims for laboratory testing.

Testing requirements and study design.

Clozapine dispensings were identified using pharmacy claims that included information on dispensing dates, quantity dispensed, days’ supply, and National Drug Codes, which were linked to generic drug names. Patient demographics were assessed at the time of clozapine initiation; other patient characteristics, including the presence of schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder diagnoses, other comorbidities, prior use of antipsychotics and prior healthcare utilisation, were assessed during the baseline period, defined as 6 months before clozapine initiation (figure 1). Self-reported race and ethnicity information was available for the Medicaid population. The Optum Clinformatics database included estimated race/ethnicity; no information on race/ethnicity was available in MarketScan. Patient characteristics were assessed within each database and in the overall cohort that included data from the three databases and all available years (2000–2022).

Haematological testing

We identified haematological testing using Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes. In addition, since tests ordered in a hospital setting are usually not billed separately to insurance, we assumed that tests were done in patients who were hospitalised. Several definitions were implemented. First, we assessed ANC testing based on the presence of CPT codes for WBC count with differential, or a hospitalisation (online supplemental eTable 1). Then, we assessed testing for any WBC count, with or without differential, based on CPT codes for any WBC or complete blood cell count, revenue codes for haematology or a hospitalisation (online supplemental eTable 1). Finally, we broadened the definition of any WBC count testing to include an emergency room visit as a setting where testing could have occurred (online supplemental eTable 1).

Analysis

We evaluated testing at baseline (prior to clozapine initiation) and during treatment (following clozapine initiation). For baseline testing, we assessed the proportion of clozapine initiators who had evidence of testing (1) within 31 days prior to and including the clozapine initiation date, and (2) within 60 days prior to and including the clozapine initiation date.

We assessed testing during treatment in a subcohort of patients who had at least one clozapine refill following the initial dispensing.

Since testing requirements differed across treatment duration, we split follow-up into three assessment periods: the first 6 months of treatment (days 1–182 following clozapine initiation), months 7–12 of treatment (days 183–365 following clozapine initiation) and months 13–24 of treatment (days 366–731 following clozapine initiation). For each period, we assessed the number of tests for each individual and divided that number by the number of follow-up days to arrive at a patient-specific rate of testing, expressed as the number of tests per 30 days. 30 days were chosen to allow comparison across assessment periods and with ANC monitoring requirements in effect under the Clozapine REMS programme during the study period. Per these requirements, patients were expected to have at least four tests per 30 days during the first 6 months, at least two tests per 30 days during months 7–12 and at least one test per 30 days during months 13–24 of therapy. To describe population rates, we calculated mean values with standard deviations (SD) and median values with interquartile ranges (IQR; first quartile–third quartile) across all eligible patients. We also quantified the proportion of individuals with evidence of at least one test during the respective window (prevalence of testing). Only individuals still under follow-up on day 183 and on day 366 contributed to the analyses of testing during months 7–12 and 13–24, respectively. In addition to presenting population rates over the study period (2000–2022), we also plotted yearly rates over time.

Finally, we assessed mean (SD) and median (IQR) number of clozapine refills and mean (SD) and median (IQR) days’ supply for all clozapine refills dispensed within each assessment period.

Main analyses of the prevalence and frequency of testing, as well as of clozapine dispensings for each assessment period, were conducted within each database and in the overall cohort with data from all three databases and across all available years (2000–2022).

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted several sensitivity analyses to ensure the robustness of our findings. First, since individuals who receive medical care that is not billed to their medical insurance could be missing CPT laboratory codes in the data, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding individuals with no medical claims during the baseline period (6 months prior to clozapine initiation). Second, we conducted analyses in patients who had at least one claim (CPT or revenue code) for any WBC count, with or without differential, during the baseline period. Finally, we excluded hospitalisations from the definition of WBC test (ie, we assumed that testing was not done during a hospitalisation).

Results

We identified 62 003 clozapine initiations in 54 169 individuals eligible for inclusion in the study, with 51 738 (84%) represented by the Medicaid population. Mean age at the time of therapy initiation was 36 years (SD 15); 57% were male. Most patients (47 763; 88%) contributed one clozapine initiation episode; 12% of study individuals (n=6406) contributed more than one episode. Key patient characteristics, on a clozapine initiation episode level, are presented in table 1. Most patients (46 569; 75%) used at least one other antipsychotic during the 6 months prior to clozapine initiation. A quarter of patients (15 417; 25%) were hospitalised within 30 days before the start of therapy; 13 094 (21%) had a diagnosis code for a drug use disorder. Mean days’ supply for the initial clozapine prescription was 12.7 days (SD 9.8), with a median of 7 days (IQR: 7–14).

Testing prevalence prior to initiation

Out of 62 003 patients, 63% had claims-based evidence of a WBC count with differential test within 31 days prior to clozapine initiation and 71% had one within 60 prior days (table 2). The proportion of patients with a test for any WBC count (with or without a differential) was 67% within 31 days and 74% within 60 days prior to initiation, further increasing to 70% (within 31 days) and 78% (within 60 days) with the addition of an emergency room visit to the definition (table 2). Results were similar between Medicaid and MarketScan enrollees; Optum Clinformatics enrollees had slightly higher prevalence of testing (online supplemental eTable 3).

Testing prevalence and frequency during treatment

Out of 62 003 patients in our cohort, 52 217 (84%) had at least one clozapine refill within 30 days following the end of the days’ supply of their initial clozapine dispensing and were thus eligible for the analysis of testing frequency during treatment. During the first assessment period, covering days 1–182 of treatment, these patients stayed on treatment and under follow-up for a mean of 133 days and had a mean of 12.6 (median 10, IQR: 5–22) clozapine dispensings, most of which (at least 75%) were for 7 or fewer days (online supplemental eTable 4). The mean WBC with differential testing rate during this period was 2.2 tests (SD 1.8) per 30 days (table 3). Fewer than a quarter of patients had testing at a rate of at least 4 tests per 30 days (table 3). Any WBC count testing rates were slightly higher than WBC with differential testing rates (table 3), though still, on average, lower than the expected 4 tests per 30 days.

There were 28 428 patients still on treatment and under follow-up on day 183 postinitiation and, therefore, eligible for the analysis of testing during months 7–12 of treatment (with the required testing rate of two tests per 30 days). The mean number of clozapine dispensings during this 6-month period was 11.5 (median 11, IQR: 6–15) with a mean days’ supply of 13 days (median 14, IQR: 7–14; online supplemental eTable 4). The mean testing rate during this period was 1.7 tests (SD 1.4) per 30 days for WBC with differential and 2.0 tests (SD 1.8) for any WBC (table 3).

There were 19 718 patients still on treatment and under follow-up on day 366 postinitiation and therefore eligible for the analysis of testing during months 13–24 (with the required testing rate of one test per 30 days). During this 12-month period, patients received a mean of 16.2 dispensings (median 14, IQR: 9–23) with the mean days’ supply of 17 days (median 14, IQR: 7–28) (online supplemental eTable 4). The testing rate during this period was 1.3 tests (SD 1.1) per 30 days for WBC with differential and 1.5 tests (SD 1.4) for any WBC count (table 3).

Database-specific results were similar across the three databases (online supplemental eTables 4 and 5). Throughout follow-up, testing frequency was highest among Optum Clinformatics enrollees and lowest among MarketScan enrollees, though differences were minor (online supplemental eTable 5).

The evaluation of testing rates over time did not reveal substantial trends (online supplemental eFigure 1). While the testing rate during the first 6 months increased slightly after 2005, it remained below the required four per month throughout the study period (online supplemental eFigure 1).

Sensitivity analyses

Out of 62 003 clozapine initiations, 1555 (3%) patients had no medical claims during the 6-month baseline period prior to clozapine initiation. Sensitivity analyses of 60 448 individuals with evidence of medical claims yielded similar results (online supplemental eTables 6 and 7). The analysis of 41 825 clozapine initiations with at least one claim for a WBC test during the baseline period yielded higher prevalence of testing both before and after clozapine initiation (online supplemental eTable 8), as well as higher frequency of testing throughout clozapine therapy (online supplemental eTable 9), as compared with the main analysis. Yet, even in this population, testing during the first 6 months of clozapine therapy was below the expected four tests per 30 days (online supplemental eTable 9). Finally, the sensitivity analysis with testing definitions that assumed that no testing was done during hospitalisations yielded similar testing frequency while on clozapine, but lower prevalence of testing prior to clozapine initiation (online supplemental eTables 10 and 11), as compared with the main analysis, suggesting that at least prior to clozapine initiation some tests were done while in a hospital.

Discussion

In this study of more than 60 000 clozapine initiations covering 2000–2022, we observed that about 30% of patients had no claims-based evidence of haematological testing within 60 days prior to clozapine initiation. Most patients’ initial clozapine prescription consisted of a 7-day supply. Among those with at least one refill, the mean rate of ANC testing (WBC with differential) after clozapine initiation was 2.2 tests per patient per 30 days during the first 6 months of treatment, less than the Clozapine REMS programme requirement of one test per week (four tests per 30 days). Observed ANC testing rates were more in line with Clozapine REMS programme requirements during months 7–12 (two tests per 30 days) and 13–24 (one test per 30 days) of treatment. Our results were consistent across sensitivity analyses, including in subpopulations of patients with at least one medical claim or those with at least one claim for a WBC count, with or without differential, during the 6 months before clozapine initiation.

Clinical and prescribing recommendations on ANC monitoring and the US Clozapine REMS programme requirements during the study period specified that patients had to be tested at least weekly during the first 6 months therapy, every 2 weeks during the subsequent 6 months and monthly thereafter. In the USA, prior to 2025, pharmacies were required to obtain authorisation from the Clozapine REMS programme prior to dispensing clozapine or verify that patients had an available, current ANC that was within the acceptable range. Prior to 2021, prescribers were required to submit ANC results to REMS programme within 7 days of the phlebotomy date for weekly monitoring, 15 days for every-other week monitoring and 31 days for monthly monitoring, though some flexibilities were permitted.19 Between 2021 and 2025, prescribers were required to submit information on tests monthly and could authorise continuation of therapy if one or more ANC was missing, while pharmacies had to verify that the patient was enrolled and the patient status form had been completed in the last 37 days.19 However, our data predate this change.

Several factors could explain our results. First, it is possible that some tests were part of bundled payments with other services or covered through means other than insurance. In addition, information on clozapine use during the baseline period could be missing if patients received clozapine that was not billed to their insurance. Our definition of clozapine initiations would be misclassified in such instances. While some missingness in the data should be expected, it should not be substantially different across follow-up periods. Yet, the frequency of testing met REMS requirements during months 7–12 and exceeded them during months 13–24, suggesting that missingness alone does not completely account for less-than-expected testing frequency during the first 6 months of clozapine treatment.

In addition to observing less-than-expected testing rates during the first 6 months of treatment, we observed lower-than-expected rates of clozapine refills. In our study, most clozapine prescriptions dispensed during the first 6 months were for 7 days, necessitating weekly testing if patients were to receive the drug from the pharmacy according to the requirements of the Clozapine REMS programme. Yet, the mean number of dispensings was far below the 24 dispensings that would cover patients for 6 months. While some patients may have had fewer clozapine dispensings because they stopped the treatment or were censored for other reasons (death, loss of insurance, nursing home admission), at least 50% of patients stayed on treatment and under follow-up for the duration of this 6-month period.

Our data preclude us from assessing the reasons for treatment non-adherence and its connection to haematological testing non-adherence. Treatment non-adherence may be driven by barriers to frequent haematological testing, or testing non-adherence could be secondary to treatment non-adherence. Given that even before 2021, providers could authorise continuation of treatment without a test, testing non-adherence should not have been a major reason for medication non-adherence, though it is possible that providers were hesitant to authorise refills without a test or were not aware of that process.

The degree to which less-than-expected testing impacts the detection of neutropenia is important. Clozapine-induced agranulocytosis is a rare side effect of clozapine, but it can be fatal if not detected in a timely manner.20 21 The risk appears to be highest during the first 6 months of treatment and decreases steeply thereafter.2123 While there is an ongoing debate about the necessity of indefinite monitoring and testing,19 there is general consensus among experts and guidelines on frequent monitoring during the initial 6 months.9 22 At the same time, the fact that weekly testing may impact adherence to treatment or treatment initiation should be considered in light of clozapine’s established clinical benefits and low incidence of clozapine-induced agranulocytosis. In a recent analysis of US claims data (2000–2022), fewer than 0.1% of patients on clozapine experienced severe neutropenia while on the drug.23 An evaluation of nationwide data in Finland (1996–2017) yielded a cumulative incidence of agranulocytosis of 1.37% (95% CI 0.58% to 3.16%).22 Clozapine remains the only medication approved for treatment-resistant schizophrenia, a condition that affects up to 34% of patients with schizophrenia.2 24 Clozapine is also the only medication approved for reducing suicidal behaviour in patients with schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder.2 It is important to assess how less-than-recommended haematological testing frequency affects the detection and subsequent outcomes of neutropenia in patients who are not adherent to the weekly testing schedule. In the meantime, physicians should strive to improve testing rates during the first 6 months of clozapine therapy to detect neutropenia early.

Our findings should be interpreted considering the following data and study limitations. First, information on haematological tests could be missing from the claims data, therefore underestimating the true testing rate. Second, we did not have information on test results. Lower ANC counts would necessitate more frequent testing, in which case, the required testing would be higher than the standard we used. Third, missing data on clozapine dispensings are unlikely, although possible if patients paid for the drug out-of-pocket rather than through insurance. Fourth, all patients in our study were insured, either through private or public insurance; thus, our results might not be generalisable to patients who receive clozapine but are not insured. It is possible that patients without health insurance are more susceptible to both testing and treatment non-adherence.

Conclusion

In this study of more than 60 000 patients initiating clozapine, we found that, on average, haematological testing was done at a lower rate than expected based on Clozapine REMS programme requirements and clinical recommendations during the first 6 months of treatment. However, the rate of testing was more in line with requirements during months 7–12 and 13–24 of treatment. Understanding the reasons for less-than-expected testing during the initial 6 months of therapy would be beneficial to ensure patient safety and improve risk mitigation strategies.
