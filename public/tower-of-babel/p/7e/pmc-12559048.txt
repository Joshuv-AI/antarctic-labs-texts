Research on 3D-printed scaffolds with microstructure bio-inspired optimization for orbital bone defect repair
Authors: Xueman Lv, Yan Liu, Lina Wang, Xuanting Liu, Shuo Wang, Zihe Xu, Yunhai Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMechanical compatibility is a major challenge in designing orbital bone scaffolds, which involving material selection, structural design and fabrication processes. In this study, a novel impact model database containing essential components involved in tissue engineering repair of orbital fracture was established for finite element analysis (FEA). The mechanical compatibility between various pattern-designed scaffold and the orbital bone defect site was tested to raise the optimized square pattern filled scaffold for the subsequent study. Based on the optimized structure, 3D printed bone scaffolds with different β-TCP contents were fabricated. It was confirmed that the composite scaffold containing 30% β-TCP and 70% polycaprolactone (PCL@30TCP) demonstrated significantly enhanced hydrophilicity, mechanical strength, water absorption, and accelerated degradation relative to other groups (p < 0.05). In vitro evaluations confirmed the significant advantages in cytocompatibility and osteogenic activity of PCL@30TCP scaffold (p < 0.05). Furthermore, rabbit orbital defect repair experiments demonstrated that the 3D-printed PCL@30TCP scaffold markedly promoted osteogenesis at the defect site through three synergistic mechanisms: enhancing neo-bone formation and maturation, guiding tissue growth into the interior structure of scaffold, and obviously upregulating bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP-2) and osteocalcin (OCN) expression (p < 0.05). Importantly, comprehensive biosafety assessments validated the clinical applicability of the PCL@30TCP scaffold. These findings indicate that the square-patterned PCL@30TCP 3D-printed scaffold exhibits exceptional osteogenic performance both in vitro and in vivo, demonstrating clinical potential for orbital bone defect repair.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Finite element analysis, 3D printing, Polycaprolactone, β-TCP, Orbital Bone defect repair

Introduction

Orbital bone defects have long represented a great clinical challenge due to the highly complex anatomy of the orbital region. The treatment of orbital bone defects is currently palliative and not reparative, suggesting an urgent demand for biomaterials that regenerate orbital bones. Orbital reconstruction necessitates exceptional precision and personalized design to ensure that implant positioning, morphological features, and dimensional parameters meticulously conform to the unique fracture patterns and defect geometries of patients [1–3]. Beyond biocompatibility, mechanical stability, and biodegradation synchronized with bone regeneration process, scaffold materials must demonstrate exceptional adaptability for intraoperative contouring to fit anatomical variability. Equally essential are rationally designed porous structures featuring optimized pore interconnectivity and microstructure parameters. These characteristics synergistically serve dual purposes: promoting osteoblast adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation while enabling efficient nutrient diffusion and metabolite clearance [4–6].

To meet these requirements, a large number of orbital bone scaffolds have been studied. Zhang et al. developed mechanobiologically optimized magnesium scaffolds (Ca-P-coated Mg-Zn-Gd scaffolds, referred to as Ca-P-Mg) for the orthotopic reconstruction of large-sized orbital bone defects [7]. The scaffolds robustly enhanced bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell (BMSC) osteogenic differentiation. In addition, the increased angiogenesis can facilitate osteogenesis, revealing the efficacy of Ca-P-Mg scaffold in healing orbital bone defects and warrant further exploration of these scaffolds for clinical applications. Shi et al. developed a novel approach using computer numerical control (CNC) material reduction manufacturing technology to produce a customized polyetheretherketone (PEEK) scaffold that conforms to the specific shape of orbital bone defects. In order to enhance the bioactivity of scaffold, deferoxamine (DFO) was in situ encapsulated into polydopamine-hybridized zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (pZIF8-DFO) nanoparticles, which was subsequently adhered to the sulfonated PEEK (sPEEK) scaffold through polydopamine modification. This novel PEEK scaffold that combines enhanced angiogenesis, stem cell recruitment, immunomodulation, and osteogenic differentiation, showcasing its promising potential for orbital bone reconstruction [8]. Obviously, current research is focused on fabricating scaffolds that fit the defect geometry as well as enhancing the osteogenic activity of the scaffolds. However, distinctive microstructure of orbital bone has not yet received sufficient attention in terms of their impact on the repair effect of implants.

Emerging evidence from extensive research have demonstrated that bone tissue exhibits distinctive microstructure that play a critical role in its biological functions and physiological integrity. This scientific understanding has driven significant interest in the development of bone tissue repair scaffolds with unique microstructure. Such bio-inspired scaffolds demonstrate enhanced capacity to direct osteoblast differentiation, facilitate bone matrix deposition, and promote the formation of neo-bone tissue [9, 10]. Optimized pore structure and surface morphology enhance cell infiltration and subsequent vascular network formation, while providing mechanical support during the remodeling process [11–13].

Three-dimensional (3D) printing technology has revolutionized the field in fabricating bone tissue engineering scaffolds, leveraging its patient-specific customization capability and sophisticated structural fabrication. This additive manufacturing approach enables layer-by-layer deposition of biomaterials guided by patient-specific medical imaging data, achieving reasonable pore structure through precisely engineered scaffolds with complex structures tailored to individual defect morphology [14, 15]. Esfahani et al. engineered a novel hexagonal structure in glass-ceramic scaffolds to fabricate anisotropic, highly porous 3D scaffold [16]. Compared to conventional scaffold designs, these hexagonally configured scaffolds demonstrated superior mechanical performance, offering innovative solutions for load-bearing bone defect treatments in orthopedics, dentistry, and maxillofacial surgery. The scaffolds not only deliver adequate mechanical integrity to support bone regeneration but also enhance cell infiltration, vascularization, and osteogenesis through their optimized porous network. Although the 3D structure does not directly determine the biocompatibility of the scaffold, it affects cell adhesion, migration, and differentiation by affecting the pore structure and surface area.

Material selection is another key point of bone scaffolds. 3D printing technology not only supports the printing of a variety of biological materials, including polymers, ceramics, metals and their composites, but also can be selected and optimized according to specific needs to meet the requirements of the scaffold in terms of mechanical properties, biocompatibility, bioactivity, and degradation [17, 18]. Polycaprolactone (PCL), a semi-crystalline polymer, serves as an ideal base material for 3D printing due to its exceptional processability and controlled biodegradability [19]. However, PCL inherently lacks osteo-inductive property. β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP), an inorganic bone substitute material, demonstrates chemical composition analogous to native bone tissue and has shown osteoconductive capacity to accelerate osteogenesis [20]. The synergistic integration of these two materials through composite blends has been extensively documented in 3D-printed scaffolds for bone tissue engineering applications. These composite scaffolds leverage structural stability of PCL and bioactivity of β-TCP to achieve balanced mechanical-osteogenic performance [21–23]. Nevertheless, despite promising outcomes in general orthotopic bone repair models, translational research focusing on orbital bone regeneration remains conspicuously absent.

The achievement of biomechanical compatibility between scaffold materials and recipient bone beds is critical in implantation [24]. Bone scaffolds with internal bionic structure can attain mechanical properties that closely match those of native bone tissue. This mechanical congruence serves to effectively mitigate stress shielding effects while maintaining sufficient structural integrity to provide protection for delicate anatomical regions such as the orbital bone. Such biomechanical optimization not only prevents secondary injury but also creates favorable mechanical microenvironment for bone regeneration, ultimately leading to improved clinical outcomes [25]. It has been fully confirmed that 3D printed composite bone tissue engineering scaffolds have potential in orbital bone repair [26]. However, the verification of mechanical compatibility between the orbital bone bed and the scaffolds composed of different materials and structures still remains a challenging issue. Recently, the FEA has been widely used to study the mechanical dynamics of bone implants in bone defect sites. A study from Roatesi indicated that the loadings due to masticatory forces generate stress concentration and consequently, an adequate risk concerning the implant stability should be assessed [27]. Therefore, an accurate FEA model is used to calculate the stress and displacement in the whole implant-bone system during osseointegration. According to this research, it was confirmed that the FEA is suitable to model the osseointegration progress, offering valuable information concerning the stress concentration zones in the implant-bone system and consequently, the risk evaluation, both for pre- and post-osseointegration. However, due to the lack of model database, the FEA data of 3D printing scaffolds in orbital bone defect repair has not been widely studied.

In this study, a 3D orbital burst injury model was reconstructed based on human orbital image data. At the same time, four biomimetic scaffolds with distinct pore geometries (Triangular, Square, Diamond, and Honeycomb) were designed. FEA was used to test the mechanical compatibility between the designed bionic model and the human orbital bone defect in order to optimize the internal bionic structure shape of the 3D printing orbital bone scaffold. Guided by the optimization results, a series of PCL/β-TCP composite scaffolds with graded inorganic phase content (0–30 wt%) were fabricated via fused deposition modeling (FDM) 3D printing. The orbital bone regenerative potential of different PCL/β-TCP scaffolds was comprehensively validated in vivo and in vitro via cell experiments and rabbit orbital bone defect repair models. The possibility of applying PCL/β-TCP scaffolds with biomimetic microstructures to orbital bone repair was discussed, which provides a useful idea and method for follow-up research.

Materials and methods

Materials

PCL (molecular weight = 80,000 Da) was obtained from the Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. β-TCP was purchased from the Shanghai EPRUI biotech Co., Ltd. The reagents for the experiments were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA) if not otherwise noted.

Reconstruction of orbital bone 3D model

A healthy adult male volunteer meeting stringent inclusion criterion-specifically excluding individuals with orbital fractures, neoplasms, inflammatory conditions, osteoporosis, or other osseous pathologies-was recruited following ethical review board approval. A 16-slice spiral CT scanner (GE MEDICAL SYSTEMS) with standardized parameters (120 kV tube voltage, 88.50 mA tube current, 1.0 mm slice thickness/interval) was employed to generate high-resolution DICOM datasets of orbital anatomy. To reconstruct a 3D model of normal orbital bone structures and adjacent tissues, CT slice images were processed using Mimics 21.0 software. The software’s bone-specific threshold segmentation function (default bone CT parameters: 226–2311 HU) was utilized to segment each slice image. Pixels within this CT parameter range were annotated and differentiated from other pixels. Subsequently, the software’s image reconstruction function was applied to connect adjacent slice images, generating a preliminary 3D model. By comparing with the actual structure of the human orbital bone and adjacent tissues, the reconstructed CT model was refined using Geomagic Studio 2018 (Geomagic, Morrisville, USA) software for continuous smoothing, detailing, and diagnostic analysis. This process eliminated detrimental features such as fractures, foreign bodies, and overlapping structures that impeded the creation of a three-dimensional solid model. The resulting model more closely approximates the actual anatomical structure while facilitating the construction of a three-dimensional solid model suitable for finite element analysis.

Establishment of FEA model of orbital bone fracture

The 3D models were assembled and subjected to interference analysis in SolidWorks 2018 to establish comprehensive ocular tissue and intact orbital bone models. Using the advanced surface editing tools of Geomagic Studio 2018, the target region was surgically resected and morphologically reconstructed. The defect-containing model was subsequently reimported into SolidWorks 2018 for secondary reconstruction, generating a biomechanically validated orbital defect model that preserves anatomical continuity while enabling controlled simulation of fracture mechanics.

Establishment of FEA model of implant

Using SolidWorks, the orbital implants were computationally designed to precisely fit the contour of fracture defects. The implants were parameterized with a thickness of 0.4 mm, matching the mean cortical dimensions of the inferior and medial orbital walls. The trabecular bone properties were assigned according to Table 1 with reference to previous study [28]. The porosity of scaffold was systematically calibrated to 55%. Following geometric finalization, the models underwent FEA preprocessing in HyperMesh. The meshed assemblies were subsequently imported into ABAQUS 2020 for nonlinear FEA under physiologically representative loading conditions, enabling comprehensive evaluation of stress distribution patterns and biomechanical compatibility at bone-implant interfaces.

Establishment of FEA model of orbital bone impact test

FEA of human orbital tissue, bone, implants, and impactors were conducted in ABAQUS 2020. The 3D reconstructed orbital model was imported into ABAQUS 2020. In this study, all four walls of the orbital bone and surrounding tissues were fixed, and the loading speed of the punching force was set to 30 mm/s. The contact between orbital tissue, bone, and the impact cylinder was defined as face-to-face contact. Soft tissues were modeled as adipose tissue. C3D10M elements were used for the meshing of bone and other tissue, while C3D8R elements were used for the meshing of implants and impactors. The total simulation time was 0.3 s.

Fabrication of orbital bone scaffolds

The varying proportions of PCL particles and β-TCP powder were mixed and subsequently extruded using a twin-screw extruder (QXQM-8, TENCAN, China). The β-TCP content was set at 10 wt.%, 20 wt.%, 30 wt.%, and 40 wt.% respectively. Fibers with a diameter of 1.75 mm were fabricated under an extrusion temperature of 80 °C and an extrusion speed of 5 mm/s. The scaffolds with external dimensions of 10 mm × 10 mm × 2 mm were designed using SolidWorks 2018 software. The design file was then imported into Cura 14.12 software, where the printing temperature was configured to 80 °C and the printing speed was configured to 8 mm/min. Using the prepared fibers, bionic orbital bone scaffolds were fabricated by a FDM 3D printer (DK2, Flash Technology, China). During the printing process, a cold air nozzle was employed to cool the model position to prevent potential deformation.

Characterization of orbital bone scaffolds

The micromorphology and microstructure of scaffolds was observed by ESEM (XL30 FEG, Philips). The hydrophilicity of the scaffolds was tested using a contact angle tester (Kruss DSA 10 instrument, Germany). The tensile modulus and bending modulus of the material were obtained by tensile and bending tests using a universal mechanical testing machine (C43.104, MTS Systems, USA). The size of tensile test samples prepared by 3D printing is 10 × 40 × 20 mm, and the size of bending test samples is 10 × 25 × 2 mm, with 6 samples in each group. The constant displacement rate of the indenter was 1 mm/min. The water absorption and degradation of scaffolds were measured by quantifying the mass changes before and after water absorption and degradation. The 3D printed bone scaffold was vacuum-dried overnight. Its initial dry weight was measured (accuracy 0.1 mg) and recorded as V₀. The sample was then fully immersed in deionized water to ensure that the pore structure of the material is fully exposed to the liquid medium. Samples were taken at the time points of 1, 4, 8 and 12 weeks. The free water on the surface was gently absorbed by a sterile filter paper. A wet weight measurement was made immediately and recorded as V₁, and then the water absorption rate (%) of the scaffolds was calculated according to Formula (1). The samples for measuring scaffold degradation were immersed in SBF solution and incubated in a shaker at 37 °C and 80 rpm. The weight of the scaffolds at time points at 1, 4, 8, and 12 weeks was recorded as V2, and then the weight loss rate (%) of the scaffolds was calculated according to Formula (2).

Cell culture

MC3T3-E1 cells (Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences) was used in this research. The cells were expanded in Dulbecco’s minimum essential medium (DMEM, Corning, USA) containing 10% FBS (Gibco, USA), 100 mg/L streptomycin (Sigma) and 63 mg/L penicillin (Sigma).

Adhesion and proliferation of MC3T3-E1 cells on scaffolds

Following UV sterilization (30 min), scaffolds were immobilized in 24-well culture plates. MC3T3-E1 cells were seeded at a standardized density of 3 × 10⁴ cells/well in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Cell adhesion on scaffold surface was performed using live/dead fluorescence staining. Following a 3-day culture period after cell seeding, the culture medium was carefully aspirated. The scaffolds underwent three gentle PBS washes (5 min each) to remove residual medium. Freshly prepared working solutions containing 2 μM Calcein-AM and 4.5 μM propidium iodide (PI) in PBS were subsequently applied to completely cover the samples. After 30-min incubation in darkness at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, the stained constructs were immediately observed under a fluorescence microscope (E80i, Nikon, Japan). The cellular proliferation was assessed using CCK-8 assay (Dojindo Laboratories). Following a 1 and 3-day culture period, the culture medium was replaced with 1 mL of fresh medium. Subsequently, 100 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation in a humidified atmosphere (37 °C, 5% CO₂) for 2 h. Post-incubation, 200 μL of the reacted supernatant from each well was carefully transferred to a fresh 96-well plate. Optical density (OD) values were quantified using a microplate reader (M200, Tecan Switzerland) at a detection wavelength of 450 nm.

Osteogenic differentiation of MC3T3-E1 cells on scaffolds

The osteogenic differentiation potential of MC3T3-E1 cells cultured on scaffolds was systematically evaluated through alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and alizarin red S (ARS) staining. Following a 7-day culture period, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature for 30 min. ALP staining was initiated by applying 1 mL of freshly prepared ALP working solution, followed by a 4-h incubation at 37 °C under dark conditions. Post-staining, the reaction solution was discarded, and samples underwent three final PBS washes to eliminate unbound dye. The ALP staining was observed via microscope (E80i, Nikon, Japan) by identifying purplish-black granular precipitates within the cytoplasm. Cells on the scaffold were lysed using 200 μL of cell lysis buffer. Then, 10 μL of the lysate, 40 μL of assay buffer, and 50 μL of pNPP staining working solution (Beyotime, China) were mixed, and the mixture was reacted at 37 °C for 10 min. After that, 100 μL of reaction stop solution was added to terminate the reaction, and a microplate reader (M200, Tecan Switzerland) was used to measure the absorbance at 405 nm.

After 14 days of culture, the cells were fixed with 4% PFA at room temperature for 30 min. Then 1.5 mL of alizarin red dye (0.1% concentration) was applied to stain the calcium nodules in cells. After being incubated in a dark place for 30 min, the dye solution was discarded. The cells were then washed with PBS 3 times to remove excess alizarin red dye. The ARS staining was observed via microscope (E80i, Nikon, Japan) by identifying orange or red calcium nodule in cell. In order to quantitatively determine the mineralization in the cell, cetylpyridinium chloride (CPC) was used to dissolve alizarin red. After sufficient dissolution, 200 μL of supernatant was transferred to a now 96-well plate. The absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader a microplate reader (M200, Tecan Switzerland).

Real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCT)

After 7 and 14 days of culture, the expression of runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2) and collagen-I (COL-I) was quantified via RT-PCR. The cells were digested from different scaffolds, and the total RNA from each group was extracted with TRIzol reagent according to the reagent instructions. The total RNA concentration and purity were determined with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Tecan M200). The RNA samples were reverse transcribed into cDNA via a PrimeScript kit (Takara, China) for RT-PCR. The RT-PCR amplification procedure was performed as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C or 30 s, 58 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 1 min. The sequence of RT-PCR primers designed by Beacon Designer 8.14 is shown in Table 2. These primers were synthesized by Comate Bioscience Co., Ltd. GAPDH was used as an internal reference gene.

Animals and experimental protocol

Eight male Chinese white rabbit (3 kg) obtained from Liaoning Changsheng biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shenyang, China) were acclimatized under specific pathogen-free conditions for 7 days prior to experimentation, with all procedures approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Jilin University School of Pharmaceutical Science. A 10 mm transcutaneous incision was created along the inferior palpebral margin. Blunt dissection with ophthalmic scissors exposed the orbital rim, where a standardized 6-mm diameter full-thickness osseous defect was created using a diamond-coated drill burr (Meisinger) under continuous saline irrigation. Pre-sterilized experimental scaffolds were implanted with flanged margins secured to host bone using 6-0 polypropylene sutures, ensuring 1-mm peripheral overlap to prevent scaffold migration.

The animals were sacrificed at 8 weeks after surgery. The defect site and surrounding bone tissue were harvested. Residual soft tissues were meticulously dissected before specimens underwent 24-h fixation in 4% PFA in PBs. Fixed samples were then subjected to micro-computed tomography imaging using a high-resolution Micro-CT (ScanScan v1172, Bruker)) with the following parameters: 90 kV source voltage, 114 μA current, and 25 μm isotropic voxel size. 3D reconstruction of both neoformed bone and residual scaffold material was performed using CTvox and CTvol software (Bruker, Germany). Quantitative morphometric analysis was conducted in CTAn (Bruker, Germany) to calculate bone volume fraction (BV/TV, %), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th, mm), trabecular number (Tb.N, mm⁻¹), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp, mm).

Histological staining

The orbital bone tissue of the experimental animals was immersed in a bone decalcification solution and subsequently incubated on a shaker at 37 °C for two months to achieve complete decalcification. The organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) from each group were fixed in a 4% PFA solution. Subsequently, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson’s trichrome, and Sirius red staining were performed on tissue sections of the orbital bone defect region to assess and evaluate the repair of bone tissue in the defect area. The areas of yellow (COL-I) and green (COL-III) in Sirius red-stained photographs were measured by ImageJ 1.54 g software (NIH, USA), and then the ratio of COL-I to COL-III was calculated for graphing. Immunohistochemical staining was employed to detect the expression levels of BMP2 and osteocalcin (OCN) in the defect region. The paraffin sections were dewaxed and subjected to antigen retrieval, then incubated with primary antibodies against OCN (GeneTex, GTX13418, 1:500 dilution) and BMP2 (Bioss, bs-1012R, 1:500 dilution) at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, they were stained with CoraLite594–conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG(H + L) (Proteintech, SA00013-4, 1:1000 dilution) at 4 °C for 1 h, and finally the cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (1 μg/ml) for 1 min. Images were captured using a fluorescence upright microscope (Zeiss A1, Germany). The relative fluorescence intensities in the immunofluorescence photographs of BMP2 and OCN were measured by ImageJ software 1.54 g software (NIH, USA). H&E staining was also used to examine the tissues of the experimental animals and evaluate the biosafety of the implanted materials.

Statistical analysis

All the data are shown as the means ± standard deviations. One-way analysis of variance with post-hoc Tukey’s honestly significant difference test (alpha = 0.05) was used to determine significant differences. The data presented in this study are presented as the means ± standard deviations. *p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All the quantitative data were analyzed via Origin 8.0 software (Origin Lab Corporation, USA).

Results and discussion

Establishment of FEA model

In this study, an FEA model for scaffold mechanical compatibility in the orbital bone defect was established via geometric reconstruction and stress analysis. As shown in Fig. 1, a complete orbital bone 3D model was first constructed based on CT data. All non-manifold edges, surface discontinuities, and overlapping triangular elements were systematically rectified. Anatomically guided manual interventions were implemented to fill cortical bone porosity in regions exhibiting pathological thinning, resulting in a three-dimensional orbital model compliant with FEA requirements for geometric fidelity and mesh integrity. To optimize computational efficiency while preserving biomechanical relevance, a symmetry-exploited simplification strategy was applied, retaining only the right orbital complex and associated peri-orbital tissues for subsequent simulations.

Smoothing, refinement, and diagnostic processing of the original CT data and the reconstruction of the 3D model: A coronal plane, B transverse plane, C sagittal plane, D initial 3D reconstruction, E reduction processing of the right orbital model, and F processed 3D reconstruction of the right orbital model

Based on the complete orbital bone model, the internal orbital fracture model was further constructed. The 3D model was shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. Informed by clinical fracture epidemiology where blowout fractures predominantly occur in the inferior and medial walls with rare superior wall involvement, particular attention was given to posterior orbital wall biomechanics. The posterior medial wall and lateral orbital rim, serving as critical load-bearing structures for anterior globe projection, necessitate precise modeling-defects in these regions were computationally simulated to induce enophthalmos through posterior support loss [29, 30]. Based on these considerations, a standardized orbital floor defect model was created featuring a diameter circular defect of 20 mm at the inferomedial wall junction.

The impact model used in this study was a standard model for evaluating ocular burst fractures [31]. To investigate the interaction between implants, ocular tissue, and bone during burst fractures, an ocular impact model was developed. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S2A–C, the orbital model was meshed with 10-node tetrahedral elements using the software’s default algorithm, with mapped triangular meshing applied to applicable bounding surfaces. Due to its structural simplicity, the implant was meshed with C3D8R elements via the medial axis algorithm. To improve simulation accuracy of bone–implant contact, the orbital contact region was locally refined. The global seeding size for the orbital bone was set to 2, while the contact interface with the implant was assigned a refined seeding size of 1. For the implant, a finer seeding size of 0.5 was used to accurately resolve stress and deformation during impact. The boundary conditions for both the orbital bone and contents were defined. In this study, the orbital contents were simplified and modeled as a single substance (adipose tissue). The elastic modulus of the orbital bone was set to 3500 MPa (Poisson’s ratio 0.3), while the elastic modulus of the orbital content was set to 200 MPa (Poisson’s ratio 0.49) [32]. Boundary conditions were set as follow: under normal conditions without external impact, the average intraocular pressure and orbital pressure were assumed to be 13 mmHg [31, 33]. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S2D, all degrees of freedom of the resected orbital bone region were constrained to prevent unrealistic rigid body motion during the impact event, thereby ensuring simulation accuracy. A tie constraint was applied to the bone–implant contact interface to mimic the surgical fixation. Surface-to-surface contact interactions were defined between the orbital contents and the bone, as well as between the peri-orbital tissues and the implant. The number of elements for the bone mesh was 34,242, for the orbital tissue mesh was 26,195, for the implant mesh was 1904, and for the impactor mesh was 300. It was assumed that the punching force was 600 N, and the impactor was made of 65 manganese steel with a diameter of 60 mm. Based on typical striking injury scenarios, the striking direction was chosen as a 15° tilt from the anterior sagittal position.

In the present study, to test the mechanical compatibility of scaffolds with different internal structures and orbital bone, four biomimetic scaffolds with different filling patterns (Triangular, Square, Diamond, and Honeycomb) were designed (Fig. 2A–D). As shown in Fig. 2E, the representative points of Ap, Bp, Cp, and Dp on the scaffold, as well as Ab, Bb, Cb, and Db on the adjacent orbital bone, were set as location of the simulation analysis points. In the study, the eyeball and surrounding soft tissues serve as the medium for impact transmission. For the feasibility of modeling and simulation, their material properties are characterized by a linear elastic material model. There may be certain limitations in using the linear elastic material model for soft tissues, which needs to be evaluated by more research work.

FEA of implant orbital bone bed and scaffolds

Figure 3A presents the deformation diagrams of β-TCP/PCL bionic bone scaffolds with various filling patterns under impact simulation. Comparative analysis revealed that all four scaffolds demonstrated effective load-bearing performance. Under impact loading conditions, the scaffolds maintained structural integrity without material detachment or excessive deformation. Notably, the triangular-pattern scaffold exhibited the most significant deformation, with most regions demonstrating 1.125 mm displacement and a maximum displacement of 1.500 mm at point P. In contrast, scaffolds with honeycomb, diamond, and square filling patterns showed superior deformation resistance, with most areas displaying less than 1 mm displacement. Localized deformations in specific P points of these scaffolds only reached up to 1.125 mm. Further analysis indicated that the honeycomb and square patterns demonstrated more favorable deformation distribution characteristics compared to the diamond pattern, exhibiting smaller areas of concentrated stress in high-deformation regions.

B Quantitative detection of strains across the locations of the simulation analysis points. C von Mises stress distribution profiles of the bionic bone scaffolds under impact simulation. D Time-dependent stress distribution profiles at designated points on the bionic scaffold during dynamic loading progress. (T represents triangular, S represents square, D represents diamond, and H represents honeycomb.)

For comparative impact deformation analysis, representative measurement points (Ap-Dp) on the scaffold surfaces and corresponding reference points (Ab-Db) on adjacent orbital bone tissue were established as analysis locations. Figure 3B quantitatively illustrated the strains across these measurement points. Quantitative comparison revealed that point A sustained greater strain than points B-D, aligning with the deformation distribution characteristics observed in Fig. 3A. This concentration of mechanical stress around point A created a critical deformation zone, which significantly influenced overall scaffold behavior under load. Notably, square-pattern scaffold demonstrated superior strain stability with lower deformation values at corresponding points compared to triangular, honeycomb, and diamond scaffolds. Crucially, the consistent deformation profile of square-pattern scaffold across different loading points suggested enhanced mechanical property relative to triangular, honeycomb, and diamond scaffolds.

Figure 3C displayed the von Mises stress distribution profiles of the bionic bone scaffolds under impact simulation. Confining analysis to equivalent stress thresholds revealed distinct stress propagation characteristics among scaffolds. Scaffolds with honeycomb patterning exhibited the most extensive high-stress regions (σ ≥ 120 MPa), exceeding those observed in diamond, triangular, and square scaffolds by 18–35%. Critical stress concentration zones were identified in honeycomb (two loci) and diamond (three loci) scaffolds, mainly localized in the inferomedial regions. In contrast, square and triangular scaffolds demonstrated similar stress distribution without notable stress concentrations. This stress distribution characteristic suggested enhanced load transmission in square and triangular scaffolds, which was regard as a critical factor for reducing implant failure [34]. Quantitative analysis confirmed the superiority of square scaffold, exhibiting a maximum stress of 144 MPa, representing reductions of 10.5% (vs. honeycomb: 161 MPa), 8.9% (vs. diamond: 158 MPa), and 14.1% (vs. triangular: 168 MPa). The stress peak localization in the inferolateral scaffold region aligns with biomechanical loading patterns reported in cranial implant studies, consistent with findings by Lipa et al. [35].

Figure 3D illustrated the time-dependent stress distribution profiles at designated points (A-D) on the bionic scaffold during dynamic loading progression. It was found that with the progress of impact, the impact force was transmitted to the scaffold through the soft tissue and bone part of the orbit, resulting in a gradual rise in stress on the scaffold. Notably, square and honeycomb pattern scaffolds demonstrated superior stress dispersion, exhibiting lower peak stresses compared to triangular and diamond scaffolds, which was consistent with the spatial stress distributions in Fig. 3C. Regional stress heterogeneity was pronounced, with points A and D sustaining higher stress than points B and C. It suggested that the buckling effects within the bony interface were dominant, while the hydraulic transmission effect of soft tissue was relatively small.

Achieving biomechanical compatibility between scaffolds and recipient bone beds remains critical for orbital reconstruction [24]. Bionic scaffolds with optimized structures can replicate mechanical properties of native bone, mitigating stress shielding effects [25]. However, the verification of material-structural compatibility still persists as a key challenge. FEA has emerged as a vital tool for evaluating implant biomechanics, with Roatesi’s study confirming its efficacy in modeling osseointegration dynamics and identifying pre-/post-operative stress concentrations [27]. While FEA enables precise quantification of stress-displacement relationships in bone-implant systems, its application to 3D-printed orbital bone scaffolds remains limited by insufficient material property databases and validated computational models. In the present study, the model of implant orbital bone bed and scaffolds was established for FEA. The results of the FEA showed that there was no obvious excessive deformation or stress concentration in the square pattern filled scaffold under the simulation, which highlighted the mechanical compatibility of the square pattern filled scaffolds with the implant orbital bone bed. On the basis of this result, the subsequent study was carried out using the square pattern filled scaffold.

Preparation of composite 3D printed scaffolds

As shown in Supplementary Fig. S3, we successfully prepared PCL/β-TCP composite 3D printing filaments with different β-TCP contents by using a small mixer extruder (QXQM-8, TENCAN, China). The appearances of the filaments were light yellow, and the color gradually became lighter with the increase of β-TCP content. All filaments in each group showed uniform mixing of organic and inorganic components, with good uniformity in filaments diameter, and these filaments can be used for subsequent 3D printing of scaffolds. Due to the β-TCP in the material blocking the printhead during the printing process, the printing of scaffolds with PCL@40TCP filaments failed, while filaments in other groups successfully printed the scaffolds. The appearance and microscopic morphology of the scaffolds were shown in Fig. 4. The scaffold in each group had the same square pattern filling structure as design. The PCL scaffold appeared pale-yellow and translucent, with a smooth surface. With the increase of β-TCP content, the transparency of the scaffold gradually decreased and changed step by step from pale yellow to off-white. The microstructure of the scaffolds with different contents of β-TCP was observed by SEM. The surface of the PCL group was smooth. No granular β-TCP component was found. Granular β-TCP powder was observed on the scaffold surface in the PCL@10 TCP, PCL@20TCP, and PCL@30TCP groups, which made the scaffold surface rough. With the increase of β-TCP content, the amount of β-TCP on the surface also increases. The results indicated that the PCL scaffolds with different β-TCP contents were successfully prepared.

Characterization of composite 3D printed scaffolds

Hydrophilicity is one of the important properties affecting the cytocompatibility of materials. Appropriate hydrophilicity can promote cell adhesion and growth on the material surface. Both hydrophobic and superhydrophilic materials are unfavorable for cell growth on the material [36, 37]. The qualitative and quantitative results of water contact angle on composite scaffolds with different β-TCP contents were shown in Fig. 5A, B. It was observed that the contact angle on the PCL scaffold surface was 106.3 ± 4.2°, while those on the scaffold surfaces of the PCL@TCP, PCL@20TCP and PCL@30TCP groups were 92.6 ± 2.8°, 86.9 ± 5.1°, and 83.7 ± 2.4°, respectively, which were significantly decreased (p < 0.05). As the amount of β-TCP added increased, the contact angle between the material surface and the droplet gradually decreased, indicating that the hydrophilicity of scaffolds gradually increased. The addition of β-TCP improved the hydrophilicity of the material. According to recent report, the use of bioceramic as reinforcement materials can improve the hydrophilicity of materials [38]. It has been indicated that the adhesion ability of osteoblasts is the strongest on the surface with a contact angle near 65° [39]. In the present study, it is found that the addition of β-TCP had a positive effect on the hydrophilicity of PCL materials, making the hydrophobic surface more hydrophilic, which was more conducive to cell adhesion. Such improvements may be particularly important for biomedical applications, as hydrophilic materials generally have better biocompatibility and biological activity, contributing to cell attachment, growth, and tissue regeneration.

C the compression and D bending strength of composite scaffolds with different β-TCP contents. E Tensile strength and F bending strength stress-strain curves. G Water absorption rates of composite scaffolds with varying β-TCP contents. H Weight loss rates of scaffolds with varying β-TCP contents during degradation, n = 3 *p < 0.05

Bone repair scaffolds should have compatible mechanical properties to meet the requirements of bone repair. As a synthetic polyester material, PCL is insufficient in mechanical property to applied in the bone defect repair. A large number of studies had proved that the mechanical strength of organic-inorganic composites with uniform mixing was significantly higher than that of single-component polymer materials [40–42].

The tensile and bending mechanical properties of scaffolds in different groups were tested. As shown in Fig. 5C, D, with the increase of β-TCP content in the scaffold material, the tensile modulus and bending modulus of the scaffolds gradually increased. Among them, the tensile modulus of the three groups of scaffolds containing β-TCP was different, but the difference was not statistically significant. The tensile modulus of the three groups was significantly higher than that of the PCL group (p < 0.05). During the tensile test, all fractures of the scaffold occurred in the middle part, and no fracture due to stress concentration was found at the clamp. The bending modulus of the PCL@30β-TCP group was also significantly higher than that of the PCL group (p < 0.05). The bending modulus of the other two scaffolds containing β-TCP was also higher than that of the PCL group, but the difference was not statistically significant. As shown in Fig. 5E, PCL exhibits the highest tensile strength. As the β-TCP content in composite material increases, the tensile strength of the scaffold decreases accordingly. As shown in Fig. 5F, PCL scaffolds exhibit the highest bending strength, while those containing β-TCP scaffolds have lower bending strengths than the PCL group.

The water absorption and degradation properties of scaffolds are critical in bone repair process, as they directly influence the cell attachment, proliferation, growth, and repair outcomes of bone defects. Appropriate water absorption facilitates the recruitment of cells and nutrients during the early stages of implantation, thereby filling the damaged area effectively. Scaffolds with high water absorption can rapidly absorb bodily fluids, providing a suitable microenvironment that promotes cell adhesion and proliferation. However, excessive water absorption may result in scaffold swelling, compromising structural integrity and potentially causing fractures or dislocations, which could hinder bone repair [43, 44]. In this study, the water absorption characteristics of composite scaffolds with varying β-TCP contents were evaluated. The results were presented in Fig. 5G. During the initial week, all scaffold groups exhibited rapid water absorption. In the first week, the rapid water absorption of the scaffolds containing β-TCP was mainly caused by the hydrophilic β-TCP material embedded on the surface. β-TCP, a hydrophilic material, possesses numerous active hydroxyl groups on its surface that form hydrogen bonds with water molecules, enabling it to adsorb water within the composite scaffolds and thereby to enhance water absorption [45, 46]. The water absorption of β-TCP material reached a saturated state within 1 week, and the change in water absorption performance was not obvious after 1 week. As a hydrophobic polymer, PCL demonstrates the lowest saturated water absorption, and the main water absorption phenomenon was mainly caused by the water retained in the pores and the water adsorbed on the material surface.

The ideal degradation of scaffold should align with the growth of new bone tissue. Overfast degradation may result in load failure of scaffold, thereby failing to provide sufficient support for new bone development. Conversely, excessively slow degradation may hinder further growth of new bone tissue and impede its regeneration. Figure 5H illustrates the weight loss rates of scaffolds with varying β-TCP contents during degradation. Over time, the degradation-induced weight loss increased progressively in all groups. The fastest weight loss occurred during the first week, likely due to the initial shock shedding of β-TCP from the scaffold surface during the early stages of the degradation experiment. Subsequently, weight loss slowed across all groups. The slope of the weight loss rate curve reflected the degradation rate of the material. During the first week, an increase in β-TCP content corresponded to a steeper curve slope, indicating a higher degradation rate. Upon entering the slow degradation phase, the slopes of the curves for the PCL@30TCP and PCL@20TCP groups remained slightly higher than those for the PCL@10TCP and PCL groups. These findings clearly demonstrated that the degradation rate of composites increased with higher β-TCP content. This result was consistent with Lei et.al. research, where the degradation rate of PCL/magnesium phosphate composite scaffolds also accelerated with increasing inorganic component content [47]. Due to its solubility in aqueous solutions, the incorporated β-TCP not only modified the physicochemical properties of the scaffolds, but also enhanced their biodegradation performance.

Osteogenic activity of composite 3D printed scaffolds

When the bone scaffold is implanted into the bone defect site, osteoblast-related cells recruited from the surrounding tissues must first adhere to the scaffold surface and colonize to further play their role and complete the subsequent bone repair process [48]. Figure 6A showed the live/dead staining results for cell adhesion on the scaffold. No significant presence of red-stained dead cells was observed across all four groups of scaffolds, indicating that all scaffolds exhibited excellent cytocompatibility. Regarding the number of green-stained living cells, the composite scaffold group containing 30% β-TCP demonstrated significantly higher cell viability compared to the other three groups. The cell adhesion on the composite scaffold with 10% β-TCP content was slightly lower than that on the scaffold with 20% β-TCP content, while the PCL scaffold showed the lowest level of cell adhesion. Figure 6B shows the results of the CCK-8 assay for cells cultured on different scaffolds at 1 and 3 days. These results revealed that the proliferation capacity of cells was different among composite scaffolds with differing β-TCP contents. On day 1, the cell proliferation in PCL@30TCP and PCL@20TCP groups was significantly higher than that in the PCL group (p < 0.05). On day 3, the cell proliferation in PCL@30TCP and PCL@20TCP groups was significantly higher than that in the PCL and PCL@10TCP groups (p < 0.05). The content of β-TCP in composite materials has a certain effect on the proliferation ability of cells cultured on scaffolds. When the TCP content is ≥20%, the addition of TCP significantly promotes cell proliferation. The physical and chemical properties of the material surface influenced cell adhesion and proliferation. This study has confirmed that the hydrophilicity of scaffolds with different contents of β-TCP is different. The higher the β-TCP content, the stronger the hydrophilicity. The addition of β-TCP made the scaffold surface change from hydrophobic to hydrophilic. And the water contact angle of the PCL@30TCP scaffold (83.7 ± 2.4°) was the most favorable for cell adhesion. The surface roughness of morphology is also an important factor affecting the adhesion ability of cells to scaffold. SEM images of the scaffold surface confirm that the surface roughness increases with the increase of β-TCP content in the material (Fig. 4). The results showed that two physicochemical factors, hydrophilicity and surface morphology, affected the adhesion and proliferation ability of cells on composite scaffold.

Gene expression level of C Runx2 and D COL-1. ALP staining (E) and quantitative (F) of cells on scaffolds after 7 days of culture. G ARS staining on scaffolds after 14 days of culture. As shown in Fig. 6C, D, the qPCR results demonstrated the regulatory effect of scaffolds on osteogenic differentiation-related genes in MC-3T3-E1 cells. On day 7, the expression levels of Runx2 in PCL@20TCP and PCL@30TCP groups were significantly higher than those in the PCL group. On day 14, the mRNA expression levels of Runx2 in PCL@10TCP, PCL@20TCP, and PCL@30TCP groups were 2.10, 2.49, and 2.85 times higher than those in the PCL group, respectively. The trend of Runx2 upregulation was obviously increased at day 14. When the β-TCP content was ≥20%, its expression levels were significantly higher than those of the PCL group on both day 7 and day 14. The expression level of COL-I on day 14 was slightly higher than that on day 7. Because β-TCP has osteoinductivity, it has been widely used in the preparation of bone repair scaffolds [49, 50]. β-TCP can slowly degrade in vivo and release Ca2+ and PO₄3- ions, which both contribute to the formation of new bone [47, 51]. Runx2 is a core regulatory factor in the process of osteogenic differentiation, playing a crucial role in skeletal development and bone formation. Runx2 can bind to and activate the promoter regions of osteoblast-specific genes, such as type COL-I, OCN, and osteopontin (OPN), thereby promoting the differentiation and maturation of osteoblasts [52].

Figure 6E showed ALP staining of cells on different scaffolds after 7 days of culture. The results showed that the purplish-black staining was obvious in all groups. The staining of the composite scaffold group with 30% β-TCP content was much more obvious than that of the other three groups. Staining intensity was slightly weaker on composite stents with 10% β-TCP content than on composite scaffolds with 20% β-TCP content, while staining was lightest on PCL scaffold. The quantitative of ALP activity was shown in Fig. 6F. With the increase of β-TCP content, the ALP activity of cells on different scaffolds was gradually upregulated. Compared with the control group (PCL), the scaffolds containing β-TCP significantly enhanced the activity of ALP (p <0.05). Furthermore, the ALP activity in the PCL@30TCP group was the highest, and significantly higher than that in the other groups (p <0.05). Figure 6G showed the ARS staining on different scaffolds after 14 days of culture. The results showed that various amounts of red-stained calcium nodules were deposited in different groups. The staining depth of the composite scaffold group with 30% β-TCP content was more obvious than that of the other three groups, while the staining on the PCL scaffold was the lightest. Figure 6H shows the quantitative results of ARS. It was consistent with the staining results. The scaffolds containing β-TCP can significantly promote calcium deposition in cells. Although calcium deposition gradually increased with the increase of β-TCP content, quantitative results were not statistically significant between the three groups containing β-TCP.

β-TCP has emerged as a promising material in bone tissue engineering due to its exceptional osteogenic properties, primarily attributed to its superior biocompatibility and inherent bone conductivity [45, 53]. As a bioceramic material, β-TCP demonstrates excellent tissue compatibility, with optimal porous structure and surface properties for osteoblast adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation. While PCL demonstrates satisfactory biocompatibility and degradability, its osteoinductive potential remains absent. Our investigation addressed this limitation through development of a β-TCP/PCL composite system, creating 3D printed scaffolds. The results revealed that incorporation of β-TCP significantly enhanced the osteogenic activity of scaffold, with osteogenic potential demonstrating concentration-dependent enhancement to β-TCP content. These findings align with our initial project, confirming the effectiveness of this composite strategy for bone regeneration applications.

3D printed scaffolds promote the rabbit orbital bone defect repair

The scaffolds used in the experimental repair of orbital bone defects in rabbits were shown in Supplementary Fig. S4. The scaffold had a round sheet structure. The size was 6 mm (diameter) × 2 mm (thickness). There was a 0.5 mm thick skirt (diameter 10 mm) at the bottom of the scaffold. The interior filling pattern is square with porosity of 55%. The implantation of 3D printed scaffolds into the rabbit orbital bone defect was carried out according to the process shown in Supplementary Fig. S5. At 8 weeks post-surgery, the animals were sacrificed. The samples were taken from the injured sites. Supplementary Fig. S6 showed the appearance images of the specimens taken from the orbital bone injury site. Obvious connective tissue wrapping was observed around the scaffold in each group. Compared with the grayish white connective tissue around PCL scaffold, the connective tissue around the β-TCP containing scaffolds was red. This suggested that blood flow around the composite scaffold containing β-TCP was more robust.

Micro-CT was used to scan and 3D model reconstruction of orbital bone injury specimens. The result of 3D tissue reconstruction was shown in Fig. 7A. The coverage area of new tissue on the wound was improved in all groups. The new bone grew from the sides to the middle along the structure of the scaffolds. With the increase of β-TCP content, the coverage area of new tissue on the wound increased gradually. PCL@30TCP group almost completed the full coverage of the wound surface. Figure 7B shows the representative X-Ray images of specimens. With the increase of β-TCP content, the amount of new bone inside scaffolds gradually increased. The new bone mainly grew along the structure to the inside of the pore of the scaffold. At 8 weeks, the pores inside the PCL@30TCP scaffolds were basically filled with new bone tissue. These new bone tissues and scaffolds together completed the coverage of defect.

C 3D reconstruction of new bone in the defect area. D Merge diagrams of 3D reconstruction of new bone and scaffold. To further characterize the formation of new bone tissue around and inside the scaffold, the area of interest was redefined using CTvol. The 3D reconstruction of new bone in the defect area was performed and shown in Fig. 7C. Compared with the other groups, the new bones in the PCL@30TCP group were the most abundant, distributing in the pores of the entire scaffold. The thickness of new bone in the PCL@10TCP and PCL@20TCP groups was comparable to that in the PCL@30TCP group. However, the new bone was mainly distributed within and around the bottom region of the scaffold in these two groups. The merge photographs of 3D reconstruction of new bone and scaffold were shown in Fig. 7D. According to the density difference between the new bone and the scaffold, we marked the new bone with yellow color. Compared with other groups, the PCL@30TCP group had the most new bone, which was distributed throughout the pores of the entire scaffold. At the bottom of the scaffold, the new bone forms a more complete bone plate. The new bone mass in PCL@20TCP and PCL@10TCP group, which was mainly distributed at the bottom and around the scaffold, was less than that in PCL@30TCP group. The amount of new bone in PCL@20TCP stent was higher than that in PCL@10TCP group. The new bone in PCL scaffolds is minimal and only existed at the edges of the underside of the scaffolds.

To quantify the orbital bone repair effect of each scaffold, we used CTAn to analyze the bone mass (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular number (Tb.N), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) at the injured site. The results were shown in Fig. 7E–H. BV/TV represents the content of new bone tissue per unit volume, which is an important indicator to measure bone repair. The results showed that with the increase of β-TCP content, the BV/TV of the defect site was significantly increased (p < 0.05). Bone trabecula is the extension of bone cortex in cancellous bone, which connects bone tissue, maintains the stability and overall structure of bone, provides internal support for bone, and participates in the growth and development of bone. The thickness, number and separation of bone trabeculae are interrelated and reflect the internal structure of the bone. In the present study, the Tb.Th in PCL@30β-TCP group was significantly higher than that in the other three groups (p < 0.05), while the Tb.N was also significantly higher than that in PCL group and PCL@10TCP group (p < 0.05). The Tb.N in PCL@20TCP group was lower than that in PCL@30TCP group. However, there was no statistical difference between them. In addition, the Tb.Sp in PCL@30TCP group was significantly lower than that in the other three groups (p < 0.05). These results fully indicated the outstanding promotion of PCL@30TCP group on orbital bone repair.

Histological assay

In this study, H&E staining was used to observe the tissue morphology of orbital fracture defect area after 8 weeks of repair. The results were shown in Fig. 8A. The scaffolds of each group were wrapped with connective tissue. The tissue extension pores grew into the interior of the scaffold. The PCL scaffold was mainly surrounded and invaded by loose fibrous connective tissue. A large number of inflammatory cells could be seen in the interstitial space. The connective tissue around and inside the composite scaffold containing β-TCP was denser. Lots of new bone tissue and few inflammatory cells were obvious inside the scaffolds. And the new tissues in the PCL@30TCP group were the densest. Figure 8B showed the Masson staining results. The blue areas represented new collagen-rich new bone tissue, while the red areas represented mature bone tissue. The results showed that the PCL scaffold was mainly surrounded by loose collagen fibers. Only a few scattered red tissues could be observed in the new bone tissue in the PCL group. With the increase of β-TCP content, the density of new tissue around and inside the scaffold gradually increased. More red-stained areas could be observed inside the new bone tissue, indicating that the new bone is gradually maturing. Among them, the PCL@30TCP group had the most and largest red-stained areas, indicating that the new bone in this group had the highest maturity. The addition of β-TCP effectively promoted the formation and maturation of the new bone.

Histological evaluation of rabbit orbital bone defect repair. Photos of A H&E staining, B Masson trichrome stain, C Sirius red staining, immunofluorescence staining of D BMP2 and E OCN. F COL-I/COL-III ratio based on Sirius red staining. Relative fluorescence intensity of G BMP2 and H OCN based on the immunofluorescence images. Bars were determined in photos, n = 3 *p < 0.05

Sirius red staining was further used to evaluate the arrangement, distribution and maturity of collagen fibers in the regenerated bone tissue. The results were shown in Fig. 8C. Red and yellow fibers represented type 1 collagen, which is abundant in bone tissue. Green fibers represented type III collagen, which is immature, unstable, and mainly consists of cartilage. The results showed that the arrangement of collagen fibers around and inside PCL scaffolds was loose and disorderly, while the collagen fibers around and inside scaffolds containing β-TCP were thicker and closely arranged. With the increase of β-TCP content, the thickness and density of collagen fibers also increased. Moreover, the proportion of red and yellow type I collagen also significantly increased with the increase of β-TCP content (p < 0.05), which was confirmed by quantitative analysis of the stained pictures (Fig. 8F).

Bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP2) is a growth factor which plays a key role in osteoblast differentiation and bone regeneration [54]. OCN, which is specifically produced by osteoblasts, and is the most abundant non-collagenous protein in bone, was demonstrated to inhibit bone formation and function as a hormone, which regulates glucose metabolism in the pancreas, testosterone synthesis in the testis, and muscle mass [55]. In the present study, the contents of BMP2 and OCN in the orbital fracture defect were detected by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. D-E). The staining images showed that the BMP2 and OCN in PCL group were the least. With the increase of β-TCP content, BMP2 and OCN in tissues also increased gradually. In these groups containing β-TCP, BMP2 and OCN were mainly enriched in the tissues adjacent to the scaffolds. Quantitative results of the relative fluorescence intensity based on the immunofluorescence images were shown in Fig. 8G, H. The relative fluorescence intensity of BMP2 and OCN in PCL@30TCP group were both the highest in all groups, and significantly higher than those in PCL group (p < 0.05). These results fully indicated that β-TCP in the composite scaffolds could significantly promote the expression of BMP2 and OCN in the tissue, and further promote bone regeneration and repair. The promoting effect was positively correlated with the content of β-TCP in the scaffold.

Biological safety of 3D printed orbital bone scaffolds

Biological safety evaluation remains critical for implants as byproducts of scaffold degradation may induce biotoxicity through hematogenous spread. To assess potential organ-specific toxicity, the comprehensive histological analyses of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were performed after 8-week orbital implantation. Histological evaluation via H&E staining (Fig. 9) revealed preserved tissue integrity across all experimental groups. The tissue cells were closely arranged. There was no significant hemorrhage, inflammatory cell infiltration, or cell disintegration and death in any of the tissues. The absence of histopathological abnormalities in critical organ systems demonstrated excellent systemic biocompatibility, confirming the clinical viability of β-TCP/PCL composite scaffolds for orbital reconstruction.

Conclusion

In this study, FEA revealed that square-patterned scaffold exhibited superior mechanical compatibility for orbital defect repair compared to honeycomb, triangular, and diamond-patterned designs. Based on the optimized structure, 3D printed bone scaffolds with different β-TCP contents were fabricated. The composite scaffold containing 30% β-TCP (PCL@30TCP) demonstrated enhanced hydrophilicity, mechanical strength, water absorption, and accelerated degradation relative to other groups. In vitro evaluations confirmed the significant advantages in cytocompatibility and osteogenic activity of PCL@30TCP scaffold. Furthermore, rabbit orbital defect repair experiments demonstrated that the 3D-printed PCL@30TCP scaffold markedly promoted vascularization and osteogenesis at the defect site through three synergistic mechanisms: enhancing neo-bone formation and maturation, guiding tissue growth into the interior structure of scaffold, and upregulating BMP-2/OCN expression. Importantly, comprehensive biosafety assessments validated the clinical applicability of the PCL@30TCP scaffold. Collectively, these findings indicate that the square-patterned PCL@30TCP 3D-printed scaffold exhibits exceptional osteogenic performance both in vitro and in vivo, demonstrating clinical potential for orbital bone defect repair.
