Diabetic Platelets: Pathophysiology, Clinical Significance, and Therapeutic Perspectives
Authors: Neha Sharma, Suneet Kumar Verma, Sourabh Sharma, Nitin Kapoor, Sanjay Kalra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Platelets are crucial for haemostasis and thrombosis. They acquire a distinct prothrombotic and proinflammatory platelet phenotype in individuals with diabetes mellitus, particularly type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Such platelets in people with diabetes (diabetic platelets) contribute to the pathogenesis of micro- and macro-vascular complications in T2DM. Chronic hyperglycaemia, oxidative stress, advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) and insulin resistance converge to reprogram platelet function at the molecular level. This results in platelet hyperreactivity, enhanced aggregation and a diminished therapeutic response to standard antiplatelet medications. Platelets in people with diabetes play a central role in the development and progression of cardiovascular disease (CVD), the most common cause of mortality in such patients. This manuscript explores the structural and functional changes in platelets in people with diabetes, underlying molecular mechanisms, their role in vascular complications and therapeutic perspectives in patients with diabetes. Also, we introduce the concept of ‘haematobolomics’ to drive more research in the metabolic profile of platelets in people with diabetes, being a potential avenue for personalised therapeutics.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a global pandemic with far-reaching implications because of its profound systemic effects, particularly on the vascular system [1]. In the vascular pathology of diabetes, the platelet is one critical but often overlooked component. Platelet activation is tightly controlled in healthy individuals by maintaining a balance of pro-aggregatory and anti-aggregatory signals. Usually, antithrombotic surface is maintained by the endothelium through release of anti-aggregatory nitric oxide (NO) and prostacyclin (PGI2) [2]. However, in the diabetic milieu, this delicate balance gets disrupted because of various factors, viz hyperglycaemia, advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress and metabolic derangements [3–5]. Such platelets in people with diabetes are both hyperresponsive to classical agonists such as thrombin, collagen, adenosine diphosphate (ADP), arachidonic acid, shear stress and resistant to inhibitory signals, creating a pro-thrombotic environment which exacerbates the end organ damage via atherosclerosis and thrombosis [5]. Understanding platelets in people with diabetes is important for clinicians, as it provides a mechanistic link between metabolic dysfunction and cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. This information will help formulate future strategies for risk stratification and personalized antiplatelet therapy. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Molecular and Functional Alterations

Diabetes platelets exhibit differences in morphology, surface receptor expression, intracellular signalling and functional response to vascular injury. The earliest and most prominent change is reduced membrane fluidity, primarily because of membrane protein glycation and lipid peroxidation [6, 7]. This impairs the signal transduction, paradoxically resulting in exaggerated responses to activation signals. Furthermore, platelets in people with diabetes display increased intracellular mobilization of calcium in response to physiological agonists (e.g., ADP, thrombin, and collagen) which facilitates their activation and granule secretion (pro-aggregatory phenotype) [8, 9].

Surface proteins of platelets in people with diabetes, including glycoprotein Ib and IIb/IIIa, undergo non-enzymatic glycation and facilitate platelet adhesion and aggregation [10, 11]. Furthermore, increased oxidative stress and resultant reactive oxygen species (ROS) damage intracellular components of platelets in people with diabetes and interfere with the anti-thrombotic effect of NO and prostacyclin [12, 13]. In normal physiology, a mild inhibitory effect is exerted by insulin on platelet activation through pathways involving phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) and Akt. This inhibitory effect is lost in patients with T2DM with insulin resistance, allowing unchecked platelet activation [14]. Furthermore, in platelets in people with diabetes, the surface activation markers, such as P-selectin (CD62P), CD40 ligand (CD40L) and activated glycoprotein IIb/IIIa complex, are consistently high. These markers not only indicate hyperactivity of platelets in people with diabetes but also enable their cross-talk with leucocytes, endothelium and coagulation cascade, ultimately facilitating thrombosis [10]. Vascular Complications

Platelets in people with diabetes are the key pathogenic factor in both micro- and macro-vascular complications of diabetes. They form the basis of pathophysiological pathways of diabetic vascular disease [15]. The hyperactive platelet phenotype hastens both initiation and progression of atherosclerosis. These activated platelets adhere to the dysfunctional or damaged endothelium through interactions involving glycoprotein Ib and von Willebrand factor. Subsequently, α-granules of these activated platelets release pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, promoting leukocyte recruitment, endothelial cell activation and smooth muscle proliferation – key steps in atherogenesis [2, 16].

Platelets in people with diabetes respond vigorously compared with regular counterparts in case of plaque rupture, eventually forming rapid and extensive thrombus [5, 17, 18]. This behaviour of platelets in people with diabetes is responsible for the macro-vascular complications of diabetes, viz acute coronary syndrome, cerebrovascular accidents and peripheral vascular disease. In addition, platelets in people with diabetes play a pathogenic role in the progression of micro-vascular complications too via vasoactive substances and microparticles that enhance endothelial damage and capillary refraction [5, 15]. Antiplatelet Therapy in Diabetes

Platelets in people with diabetes are the key target for secondary prevention of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease with antiplatelet agents, as they play a key pathogenic role in vascular complications [17]. However, standard antiplatelet agents such as aspirin are less effective in individuals with diabetes, as depicted in various studies. Aspirin is usually started at dose of 75–100 mg per day for irreversible COX-1 inhibition and suppression of thromboxane A2. Various reasons have been attributed to this phenomenon, described as ‘aspirin resistance.’ In diabetes, there is increased platelet turnover, altered COX expression, and amplified platelet activation via non-COX-dependent pathways, collectively contributing to this resistance [19, 20]. Various trials have compared higher dose (325 mg) of aspirin with standard dose to study the cardiovascular benefits. The recent ADAPTABLE trial ruled out any clinically significant difference in cardiovascular event rates or major bleeding risk with higher dose [21].

In addition, ‘clopidogrel resistance’ has been described, possibly due to impaired hepatic metabolism to active metabolite and CYP2C19 gene polymorphisms, high turnover and increased platelet aggregation [22–24]. It has been demonstrated that clopidogrel at an enhanced dose of 150 mg per day exhibits significantly higher platelet inhibition compared with 75 mg daily dose. The OPTIMUS study was a landmark trial in this regard and proved that a higher dose can overcome this diabetes-associated resistance [25]. Apart from genetic polymorphism, various other factors affect the dosage of antiplatelets in individuals with diabetes, including body mass index, inflammatory state, glycaemic control and various drug interactions, including with proton pump inhibitors and statins. Individualised antiplatelet dosage in high-risk diabetic individuals can enhance the cardiovascular outcomes [20–26].

These drawbacks are often concerning while preventing or treating vascular complications in diabetes. Newer agents such as ticagrelor and prasugrel can help potentiate the therapeutic benefit. These newer agents have consistent and potent P2Y12 receptor inhibition compared with aspirin. However, these benefits must be carefully weighed against the risks of bleeding [27–29]. Future Directions

There is growing interest in monitoring platelet function in individuals with diabetes for advanced therapeutics. Many techniques are available for research purpose, viz flow cytometry for surface marker analysis, light transmission aggregometry and point-of-care tests including VerifyNow or Platelet Function Analyzer (PFA-100) [32–35]. These assays can help determine therapeutic response, but their integration into clinical practice is still limited. Furthermore, omics technologies, such as transcriptomics, proteomics and metabolomics, have identified unique signatures in platelets in people with diabetes, and would help in developing precision medicine approaches for individuals with diabetes [36, 37].

Haematobolomics

We propose ‘Haematobolomics’ for the extensive and efficient analysis of metabolites and metabolic pathways in circulating blood cells, especially platelets, utilizing advanced metabolomic techniques including nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, mass spectrometry, and liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) [38]. Haematobolomics can open new avenues in the research of the altered bioenergetic and metabolic characteristics of platelets in people with diabetes. It can also facilitate new research in response to platelets in people with diabetes to antiplatelet medications, potentially identifying biomarkers for resistance and toxicity. Recent findings indicate that the metabolic reprogramming of platelets could act as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target for vascular issues related to diabetes [39]. Consequently, haematobolomics could connect phenotypic platelet dysfunction with molecular mechanisms, opening the door for personalized treatment approaches in managing cardiovascular health in patients with diabetes.

Conclusions

Platelets in people with diabetes play an active role in the development of vascular complications rather than being mere participants in haemostasis. The diabetic milieu leads to significant changes in the structure and function of the platelets, making them hyperactive and less responsive to standard treatments. These modifications contribute to both micro- and macro-vascular complications, highlighting the importance of careful monitoring and customized treatment strategies for individuals with diabetes. Although conventional antiplatelet medications are fundamental to treatment, their varied effectiveness in patients with diabetes emphasizes the pressing need for more tailored and mechanism-based therapies.
