Identification and validation of prognostic genes associated with integrative stress response in lung adenocarcinoma and construction of the risk models
Authors: Jianjun Fu, Yunming Tao, Wu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract​Integrated stress response (ISR) genes are implicated in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) prognosis, but their clinical utility remains unexplored. This study aims to identify ISR-related prognostic genes and construct a risk model for LUAD survival prediction.​ LUAD transcriptomic and clinical data were retrieved from public databases. ISR-related genes (ISR-RGs) were screened via differential expression and regression analysis. A risk model and a nomogram integrating clinical indicators were built and validated. Functional enrichment, immune cell infiltration, and RT-qPCR in clinical samples were performed.​​​ Five prognostic genes (AGER, GPX3, CCNA2, KCNK3, and CHEK1) were identified. High-risk patients exhibited poorer survival. The nomogram was able to forecast the survival of LUAD well. Genes were functionally linked to cell cycle and DNA replication and correlated with immune cells (e.g., CCNA2 positively with CD4⁺ T cells [cor = 0.52]; CHEK1 negatively with memory B cells [cor = − 0.40]). RT-qPCR confirmed dysregulation: AGER, GPX3, and KCNK3 downregulated and CHEK1 and CCNA2 upregulated versus controls. The five prognostic genes pertinent to both LUAD and ISR were identified, and a risk model was constructed for the good prediction of LUAD survival, which offered a fresh outlook for alleviating the poor prognosis of LUAD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40547-x.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma, Integrated stress response, Prognosis, Nomogram, Immune microenvironmentSubject terms: Genomics, Assay systems, Genetics research, Cancer, Cancer genomics, Lung cancer, Oncogenes

Introduction

Lung cancer ranks among the leading causes of cancer-related deaths globally. As a major subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) accounts for approximately 40% of all diagnosed lung cancer cases1. Although significant progress has been made in targeted therapy and immunotherapy for LUAD in recent years, over 60% of NSCLC patients develop resistance within 1–2 years of treatment, leading to a sharp decline in therapeutic benefits2. Current data indicate that the five-year survival rate for LUAD patients is less than 50%, dropping to as low as 7% for those with metastatic disease3, with considerable variability in outcomes among patients (i.e., prognostic heterogeneity)4. Conventional prognostic indicators such as tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging, which are currently relied upon in clinical practice, are insufficient to fully explain this heterogeneity or to correlate tumor microenvironment status with drug sensitivity. This situation underscores the urgent need to identify novel prognostic biomarkers based on key molecular pathways, which should possess the dual value of “predicting outcomes” and “guiding treatment”.

The Integrated Stress Response (ISR), an evolutionarily conserved intracellular signaling network5, has been recognized as a central regulatory pathway in tumorigenesis and progression. It maintains proteostasis under stress by reprogramming transcription and translation processes; aberrant upregulation of ISR activity directly promotes tumor invasion, proliferation, and therapy resistance in various alignancies6. In LUAD, the phosphorylation level of eIF2α (eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2α), a core mediator of the ISR, has been demonstrated to correlate positively with accelerated tumor growth and poor patient prognosis7–9—suggesting that the ISR likely acts as a critical molecular hub regulating LUAD outcomes. However, the association between LUAD and the ISR remains incompletely understood. For instance, which specific genes within the ISR-related genes directly regulate LUAD prognosis is still unclear. Current studies have focused only on the impact of single stress types on LUAD10,11, without identifying ISR-related core genes with clinical translational value. Furthermore, there is a lack of integrated prognostic models constructed based on ISR-related genes, as existing models often analyze molecular markers or clinical indicators in isolation12. Additionally, the mechanisms linking ISR-related prognostic genes to the LUAD tumor microenvironment and drug sensitivity remain elusive, failing to provide a molecular basis for “prognostic stratification-based individualized therapy.” These gaps directly limit the practical application of ISR in LUAD prognosis assessment and treatment.

To further investigate the impact of the ISR on LUAD prognosis, this study undertakes the following innovative explorations: First, we systematically obtain LUAD transcriptomic data from public databases and identify core genes with independent prognostic value from an ISR perspective, addressing the issue of “undefined ISR-related prognostic genes.” Second, we construct a risk model based on these genes and validate its stability using both The Cancer Genome Atlas - Lung Adenocarcinoma (TCGA)-LUAD and GSE50081 datasets. Finally, through analyses of tumor mutational profiles, immune cell infiltration, and drug sensitivity assessments, we reveal the mechanisms linking ISR-related prognostic genes to the tumor microenvironment and response to chemotherapeutic agents. This work not only clarifies the molecular logic underlying their role in LUAD prognosis but also identifies potential therapeutic targets for high-risk patients, ultimately providing a new theoretical basis and technical support for precise prognostic stratification and personalized therapy in LUAD.

Materials and methods

Data sources

Gene expression matrices and mutation information for lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) were acquired from the University of California Santa Cruz (UCSC) Xena (https://xena.ucsc.edu/, August 31, 2024) as a training set (TCGA-LUAD), and included 511 LUAD tissue samples (disease group) and 59 adjacent non-tumor lung tissue samples (control group). The validation set was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Among them, GSE50081 (platform: GPL570) included a total of 127 LUAD tissue samples with survival information, and GSE14814 (platform: GPL96) incorporated 71 LUAD tissue samples with survival information. Moreover, integrated stress response-related genes (ISR-RGs) were obtained from the literature13, comprising 5 categories: 47 unfolded protein reaction (UPR) genes, 79 heat shock response (HSR) genes, 58 oxidative stress response (OSR) genes, 119 hypoxic stress response (HySR) genes, and 231 DNA damage response (DDR) genes, these genes totaled 529 ISR-RGs after deduplication (Supplementary Table 1).

Identification of candidate genes

In the disease and control groups of TCGA-LUAD, the DESeq2 package (v 1.38.0)14 was adopted to get the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (P < 0.05 and |log2fold change (FC)| > 2). DESeq2 uses a method called “size factors” to normalize the data, so as to eliminate differences in sequencing depth and other technical variations between samples. DESeq2 uses the geometric mean method to calculate the size factors. Afterwards, the log2FC values were sorted from largest to smallest, and the ggplot2 (v 3.4.1)15 and the pheatmap packages (v 2.15.1)16 were employed to draw a volcano map and a heatmap, respectively. Finally, the ggvenn package (v 0.19)17 was employed to acquire the candidate genes by taking the intersection of DEGs and ISR-RGs.

Functional and interaction characterization

To investigate the pathways related to candidate genes, based on the database, the clusterProfiler package18 (v 4.2.2) was adopted to proceed Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)19,20 analyses of candidate genes. A p-value < 0.05 indicates a statistically significant difference. The enriched results were sorted by count value from largest to smallest, and the top 5 results were visualized.

Furthermore, to study the interactions of candidate genes at the protein level, the candidate genes were imported into the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) database (https://string-db.org/), and the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was built (confidence level > 0.4). After the removal of isolated proteins, Cytoscape21 (v 3.10.2) was employed to visualize the network.

Identification of prognostic genes and construction of risk models

So as to assay the relevance among candidate genes and overall survival (OS) of LUAD. First, the survival package22 (v 0.4.9) was employed to proceed with univariate Cox regression analysis (P < 0.01) and proportional hazards (PH) assumption test (P > 0.05). Afterwards, the glmnet package23 (v 4.1.4) was employed to perform least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis, when the model error rate was minimized, genes with non-zero variable coefficients were selected as prognostic genes.

Then, the surv_cutpoint function in the survival package (v 0.4.9) was then employed to calculate risk scores for prognostic genes to construct the risk model. Risk score = Expi1 × Coefi1 + Expi2 × Coefi2 + . + Expin × Coefin, where Coefi denotes the risk coefficient pertinent to each gene, while Expi signifies the expression level of each corresponding gene. The surv_cutpoint function in the survminer package24 (v 0.4.9) calculated the optimal cut-off value for the risk score. The function utilized the maximum selected rank statistics method to iterate through all potential cutoff points of the risk score and selected the cutoff point that maximized the survival difference between high- and low-risk groups, thereby avoiding the subjectivity associated with manual threshold setting. In this study, based on the “risk out” dataset containing survival time, survival status, and risk scores, LUAD samples were classified into high- and low-risk groups. Besides, the survminer package (v 0.4.9) and log-rank test were adopted to draw a Kaplan-Meier25 curve comparing the differences in survival status of risk groups. Next, the timeROC package26 (v 0.4) was employed to plot 1, 2, and 3 years of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to appraise the foreseeable power of the model. It should be noted that to validate the results further, the above analyses were also performed on the GSE50081 and GSE14814 datasets.

Construction of the nomogram

For better application, within TCGA-LUAD, the survival package (v 0.4.9) was employed to proceed with univariate Cox regression analysis on 6 variables, namely risk score, age, gender, T/N/M stages (P < 0.05). Subsequently, PH assumption testing was performed (P > 0.05), and independent prognostic factors were acquired by retaining clinical indicators with a hazard ratio (HR) ≠ of 1 in multivariate Cox regression analysis. Based on this, the rms package (v 6.5-0) (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=rms) was employed to construct a nomogram predicting survival for LUAD. Finally, the nomogram was appraised by plotting a calibration curve; the ROC curves were adopted to appraise the precision of the model. Besides, Wilcoxon and Kruskal-Wallis tests were employed to further contrast risk scores across clinical indicators (P < 0.05).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

This study utilized the clusterProfiler package (v 4.2.2) to perform GSEA to explore pathways associated with the identified prognostic genes. To assess the relationships between the prognostic genes and other genes in the disease and control groups of the training set, the psych package (v 2.4.3) was employed to perform Spearman correlation analysis. Genes were sorted in ascending order according to their Spearman correlation coefficients, thereby generating a list of genes correlated with each prognostic gene. For GSEA, the reference gene set c2.cp.kegg.v11.0.symbols was obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, available at https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb).

Mutation analysis and drug sensitivity analysis

Founded on the somatic mutation data from the TCGA-LUAD samples, the oncoplot function in the maftools package27 (v 2.4.0) was applied to generate waterfall plots to explore mutation signatures between risk groups. Subsequently, the TCGAmutations package28 (v 1.0) was applied to load the TCGA data and intersect it with the samples of the risk group to obtain intersected samples. The maftools package27 (v 2.4.0) and Wilcoxon test were applied to compute the score of tumor mutational burden (TMB) for LUAD samples and differences among risk groups (P < 0.05), respectively. Additionally, the psych package29 (v 2.2.9) was applied to observe a relativity analysis amid risk scores and TMB scores (|correlation (cor)| > 0.3, P < 0.05).

To further explore the potential guidance of risk scores for chemotherapy. First, the oncoPredict package30 (v 0.2) was adopted to get half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of 198 chemotherapeutic drugs in the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/), and the differences in IC50 of chemotherapeutic drugs between the risk groups were acquired (P < 0.05). To further investigate the relevance among prognostic genes and chemotherapeutic agents, psych package31 (v 2.2.9) was applied to observe the correlation between prognostic genes and chemotherapeutic agents (|cor|>0.3, P < 0.05).

Immune infiltration analysis and immune checkpoint analysis

To further assess the immune infiltration of LUAD in the risk group, the Cell-type Identification By Estimating Relative Subsets of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT) algorithm32 (v 1.03) was adopted to assay the infiltration of 22 immune cell types33 in LUAD (P < 0.05). Prior to analysis, the gene expression matrix was normalized using Total Sum Scaling34. Specifically, the expression value of each gene within a sample was divided by the total expression of that sample, resulting in a sum of 1 for the gene expression values per sample. This normalization method effectively eliminated expression bias caused by differences in sequencing depth among samples, ensured the comparability of gene expression data across different samples, and established a reliable data foundation for accurately quantifying and comparing immune cell infiltration proportions in each sample. In the analysis, a threshold of P < 0.05 was set as the screening criterion. Subsequently, the Wilcoxon test was employed to identify differential immune cells between the risk groups (P < 0.05). Further analysis was conducted to examine the correlations among these differential immune cells and their associations with prognostic genes (|cor| > 0.3, P < 0.05). Additionally, to understand the expression of prognostic genes in immune checkpoints35,36, firstly, differential immune checkpoints between risk groups were acquired (P < 0.05). Subsequently, the psych package (v 2.2.9) was adopted to understand the relationship between differential immune checkpoints and prognostic genes. Finally, to evaluate the immunotherapy response in LUAD samples, the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm37 (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu) was adopted to assay the sensitivity of the risk model to immunotherapy and visualized by the TIDE score.

Chromosome, subcellular localization, and functional similarity analysis

The RCircos package38 (v 1.2.2) was employed to map the localization of prognostic genes on chromosomes, providing a basis for subsequent applications of prognostic genes. Subsequently, the amino acid sequences of the proteins encoded by prognostic genes were obtained from the Protein database (https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/), and the online database Cellular Localization of Proteins (Cell-PLoc, http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/Cell-PLoc-2/) was employed to predict the subcellular localization of the proteins encoded by prognostic genes, which helps to dig deeper into the functions of these proteins. In addition, the GOSemSim package39 (v 2.24.0) was employed to calculate similarity scores to analyze the functional profiles of different prognostic genes in LUAD.

Building molecular regulatory networks

To target and regulate the expression of prognostic genes, the NetworkAnalyst (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/) from the Encyclopedia of Nuclear DNA Elements (ENCODE) database (https://www.encodeproject.org/) was employed to predict transcription factors (TF) for prognostic genes. At the same time, the miRDB (https://mirdb.org/) and miRnada (http://mirtoolsgallery.tech/) were employed to predict miRNAs that target prognostic genes. Then, Cytoscape (v 3.10.2) was employed to visualize TF-mRNA-miRNA regulatory networks.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

So as to confirm the consistency of prognostic genes expression with bioinformatics results, the prognostic genes expression was verified by RT-qPCR. The five LUAD tissue samples and five adjacent non-tumor lung tissue samples were collected. It was important to note that all samples were collected from Gaoxin Branch of The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University, with donors giving informed consent. The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki and was conducted with approval from The Biomedical Ethics Committee of Gaoxin Branch of The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (approval number: HEC[2025]009 ). The total RNA of the samples was extracted by the Trizol method (Ambion, 15596-018CN, USA)40, and the cDNA was synthesized using the SweScript First Strand cDNA synthesis kit (Servicebio, G3333-50, China). GAPDH was employed as an internal reference gene, and the 2−ΔΔCt method41 was employed to calculate the relative expression levels of prognostic genes. Primer sequences were shown in Supplementary Table 2. The resultant data were statistically analyzed and visualized by Graphpad Prism42 (v 8.0).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (v 4.2.3). For the analysis of clinical characteristics, the Wilcoxon test was applied for comparisons between two groups, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for comparisons among multiple groups. For RT-qPCR data, inter-group comparisons were performed via a non-parametric method (Mann-Whitney U test). All other statistical analyses were carried out using the Wilcoxon test, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Possible functions involved in the 34 candidate genes and their interactions

After threshold screening, there were 2,264 DEGs within TCGA-LUAD, which contained 575 up-regulated and 1,689 down-regulated DEGs in the LUAD samples (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1A). The top 10 up- and down-regulated DEGs had different expression patterns in disease and control groups (Fig. 1B). Finally, the intersection of DEGs and ISR-RGs yielded 34 candidate genes (Fig. 1C). Then, totally 749 GO entries were enriched (P < 0.05), containing 647 biological processes (BP), 38 cellular components43, and 64 molecular functions (MF), including response to hypoxia, response to decreased oxygen levels, and cellular response to hypoxia (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Table 3). Similarly, 14 KEGG pathways were enriched (P < 0.05), with higher enrichment in pathways such as glutathione metabolism, homologous recombination, and biosynthesis of cofactors (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Table 4). Besides, after excluding 10 isolated genes, there were 24 candidate genes that had interactions with each other (Fig. 1F). Notably, BLM, CHEK1, and RAD51 interacted with various proteins.

Risk modeling and its assessment based on 5 prognostic genes

After PH assumption test (Supplementary Table 5), 10 candidate genes were retained, among these, KCNK3, GPX3, AGER (P < 0.05, HR < 1), and TOP2A, UBE2T, MCM10, CCNA2, CHEK1, RAD51, BLM (P < 0.05, HR > 1) were identified as candidate prognostic genes, notably, candidate prognostic genes were mostly risk factors for LUAD (HR > 1, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). Then, the 5 prognostic genes were distinguished when lambda.min = 0.023, namely AGER, GPX3, CCNA2, KCNK3, and CHEK1 (Fig. 2B). The prognostic genes KCNK3, CCNA2, GPX3, AGER, and CHEK1 were located on chromosomes 2, 4, 5, 6, and 11, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Subsequently, the degree of functional similarity between the prognostic genes was further observed, and the results demonstrated that AGER, CCNA2, and CHEK1 had high similarity scores and might perform similar functions in LUAD (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Subsequently, a risk model was constructed by calculating risk scores for prognostic genes (Table 1). Based on the optimal cutoff value (0.1125725), LUAD samples were divided into high-risk and low-risk groups, with 339 and 172 samples, respectively. In the risk curve, an increase in the risk score corresponded to a decrease in survival time and a simultaneous increase in the number of deaths (Fig. 2C, D). Besides, the KM survival curves revealed a marked difference amid the risk groups, and survival rates were lower in the high-risk group (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2E), and the area under the curve (AUC) values were greater than 0.6, suggested that the set up risk model could forecast the survival probability of LUAD (Fig. 2F). It should also be noted that in the GSE50081 and GSE14814 datasets, LUAD was divided into high-risk and low-risk groups using the same method. Analysis of the two datasets showed that the higher the risk score, the greater the number of deaths (Fig. 2G-H). The KM survival curve demonstrated that the difference in survival rates between the two groups of patients was statistically significant, with patients in the high-risk group having a lower survival rate (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2I-J). The AUC values of the model for predicting 1-year, 2-year, and 3-year survival rates of LUAD patients were all greater than or equal to 0.6, indicating that the risk model established based on AGER, GPX3, CCNA2, KCNK3, and CHEK possessed robustness (Fig. 2K-L).

Construction and evaluation of a nomogram

We integrated clinical indicators to formulate a predictive model for estimating OS in LUAD patients. Risk score and T/N/M stages were recognized as independent prognostic factors after analysis (P < 0.05, HR ≠ 1)(Fig. 3A, B, Supplementary Table 6). For better application, a nomogram predicting 1-, 2-, and 3- years survival for LUAD was constructed based on these independent prognostic factors (Fig. 3C). Subsequently, the calibration curves diaplayed that the nomogram almost overlapped with the reference line (Fig. 3D). The AUC values of the ROC curves were greater than 0.7 (Fig. 3E). This demonstrated the good accuracy and predictive power of the nomogram constructed based on independent prognostic factors.

Additionally, the clinical value of the risk model in LUAD was further observed. The results showed that risk scores differed markedly under different age, gender, T1 and T2, N0 and N1, N0 and N2 (P < 0.05), notably, risk scores were significantly higher in LUAD patients younger than 60 years (Fig. 3F). Overall, the inclusion of examining clinical indicators makes the risk model and nomogram more helpful in achieving individualized treatment in LUAD clinics.

GSEA results and molecular network regulation of prognostic genes

The GSEA results displayed that AGER was markedly enriched in 85 pathways (P < 0.05), including cell cycle, proteasome and spliceosome (Fig. 4A), CCNA2 was significantly enriched to 71 pathways (P < 0.05), for instance cell cycle, DNA replication, and spliceosome (Fig. 4B), the GPX3 was significantly enriched in 81 pathways (P < 0.05), including the lysosome, spliceosome, and cell cycle (Fig. 4C), the KCNK3 was significantly enriched in 78 pathways (P < 0.05), including the spliceosome, cell cycle, and neuroactive ligand receptor interaction (Fig. 4D), CHEK1 was markedly enriched in 77 pathways (P < 0.05), including cell cycle, proteasome and mismatch repair (Fig. 4E) (Supplementary Table 7). Notably, prognostic genes were co-enriched into 37 pathways, such as the arachidonic acid metabolism, cell cycle, and DNA replication (Supplementary Table 8). Overall, prognostic genes may be involved in these pathways, and targeting these pathways might provide novel insights for the treatment of LUAD.

Tumor mutation analysis and drug sensitivity analysis

A total of 495 samples were obtained after taking the intersection, including 169 samples in the low-risk group and 326 samples in the high-risk group. The somatic mutation rate were higher in the high-risk group.The mutation frequencies of TP53 and TTN was higher, with more mutation types, mainly missense mutation multi hit and nonsense mutation (Fig. 5A). The mutation frequency of KRAS and TTN was higher in high-risk group, mainly missense mutation (Fig. 5B). Subsequently, TMB score was higher in the high-risk group and markedly different from the low-risk group (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 5C). Moreover, a positive pertinence was further observed between risk scores and TMB scores (cor = 0.41, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 5D). These analyses revealed that there was a relevance between risk score and tumor mutation, understanding these differences and relationships across risk groups provides insights for subsequent related studies.

Immune infiltration of prognostic genes

The immune cell infiltration profile between risk groups was shown in Fig. 6A. The highest positive relevance was watched among CCNA2 and CHEK1 (cor = 0.80, P < 0.05) (Fig. 6C). By understanding the relevance among differential immune cell and among them and prognostic genes, it was possible to make the speculation that they might affect the occurrence and development of LUAD by interacting with each other to alter the immune microenvironment.

Discussion

Globally, LUAD continues to be among the primary causes of cancer-related deaths45, with the ISR critically implicated in driving aggressive tumor behavior, accelerated progression, and unfavorable clinical outcomes. Leveraging bulk transcriptomic data from TCGA and GEO repositories, our study systematically identified 5 ISR-associated prognostic genes through differential expression analysis and regression modeling, and subsequently established a robust prognostic signature. A clinically interpretable nomogram integrating 4 independent prognostic determinants, including risk score, T stage, N stage, and M stage, exhibited superior predictive performance for LUAD survival outcomes. Stratification by risk scores revealed significant disparities in immune microenvironment profiling and pharmacogenomic susceptibility disparities between high- and low-risk subgroups. Furthermore, functional enrichment analysis elucidated pathway signatures and molecular regulatory networks governed by the prognostic genes. These findings provide critical scientific references for optimizing prognostic stratification and advancing immunotherapeutic strategies in LUAD, while also identifying mechanistically grounded therapeutic targets.

This study identified 5 prognostic biomarkers linked to ISR in LUAD: AGER, GPX3, CCNA2, KCNK3, and CHEK1.AGER, a multi-ligand cell surface receptor, is known to initiate pro-inflammatory intracellular signaling pathways that play roles in various physiological and pathophysiological processes, and are closely associated with the development of autoimmune diseases, cancer, and other disorders46,47. Our RT-qPCR assays revealed that AGER expression is downregulated in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), which is consistent with the findings reported by Sarang et al.48. Moreover, low AGER expression was significantly correlated with histological features, clinical stage, and lymph node metastasis in LUAD. In addition, AGER hypermethylation showed a clear association with tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, suggesting that AGER may influence LUAD progression by modulating the immune microenvironment49. Together, these findings highlight the potential of AGER as a valuable prognostic biomarker for LUAD.Furthermore, upon binding to its ligands, RAGE can activate multiple pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, among which the nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) pathway is particularly critical50. The activation of NF-κB not only induces the expression of a large number of inflammatory factors (such as TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) but also directly or indirectly exacerbates intracellular oxidative stress, which is one of the important upstream signals that induce the ISR51,52. Studies have shown that under high-glucose conditions, activation of AGER promotes tumorigenesis by O-GlcNAcylating c-Jun, a process accompanied by drastic changes in cellular metabolism and redox status, thereby creating conditions for ISR activation53.

GPX3 is a selenium-dependent enzyme primarily localized in the plasma, whose core function is to exert critical extracellular antioxidant effects by scavenging reactive oxygen species54 and hydrogen peroxide, thereby protecting cells from oxidative damage55. Studies have found that GPX3 expression is downregulated due to promoter hypermethylation in various cancer tissues56. Additionally, GPX3 can regulate inflammatory responses and suppress tumor progression, with recent research suggesting its potential involvement in neutrophil-mediated pro-tumor mechanisms57,58. Our RT-qPCR results showed that GPX3 expression is downregulated in LUAD, which is highly consistent with the findings of multiple studies reporting decreased GPX3 expression in cervical, thyroid, head and neck, lung, and melanoma cancers, where its low expression is associated with poor patient prognosis55,59. Furthermore, another study discovered that Opsin3 can regulate the proliferation, migration, and apoptosis of LUAD cells via the GPX3 pathway60, underscoring the potential of GPX3 as a promising therapeutic target for LUAD.In the regulation of the ISR, GPX3 plays a critical role as a “buffer.” When reactive oxygen species54are abnormally elevated, protein folding dysfunction, lipid/nucleic acid damage, and calcium homeostasis imbalance trigger endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, activating the PERK branch of the ISR61,62. As a key sensor of ISR, PERK phosphorylates eIF2α to inhibit global protein synthesis while selectively promoting the translation of the transcription factor ATF463,64. ATF4 subsequently drives the expression of a series of adaptive or pro-apoptotic genes, thereby determining the cell’s fate. By reducing ROS levels, GPX3 can mitigate ER dysfunction caused by oxidative stress at its source, thereby negatively regulating the overactivation of ISR. This “antioxidant-ISR suppression” mechanism tightly links the loss of GPX3 to tumor cell stress adaptation and poor prognosis, providing a novel metabolic-immunological synergistic strategy for intervening in LUAD progression.

CCNA2 (cyclin-A2), a member of the cyclin A family, is a crucial cell cycle regulatory protein that plays a key role in cell cycle progression, particularly at the G1/S and G2/M transitions65,66. Relevant studies have shown that compared to normal tissues, the expression level of CCNA2 is significantly elevated in LUAD tissues, which is consistent with our research findings67. Mechanistically, research indicates that after binding to CDK2, CCNA2 phosphorylates the AXIN1 complex, thereby inducing ubiquitin-dependent degradation of β-catenin, ultimately suppressing the Wnt signaling pathway, disrupting the maintenance of AT2 cells, and consequently regulating the development and progression of LUAD68. Furthermore, Tanshinone IIA can inhibit LUAD progression by modulating the CCNA2-CDK2 complex69, and the antihyperlipidemic drug Rosuvastatin can also suppress tumor progression and enhance cancer cell sensitivity to chemotherapy by downregulating CCNA2 expression in LUAD70. These findings collectively suggest that CCNA2 is a valuable prognostic biomarker for LUAD, and targeting CCNA2 represents a promising therapeutic strategy.On the other hand, the ISR serves as a core defensive network for cells to cope with various stresses, such as endoplasmic reticulum stress, oxidative stress, and nutrient deprivation. It phosphorylates eIF2α to globally suppress protein synthesis while selectively activating transcription factors like ATF4, thereby regulating cellular outcomes toward repair, senescence, or apoptosis61,62. From a functional logic perspective, CCNA2, as a key driver of cell cycle progression, and the ISR-mediated stress response and cell fate determination mechanisms both play crucial roles in regulating cell proliferation and homeostasis. The two may interact or cross-regulate each other during tumor development.

KCNK3 encodes the two-pore potassium channel protein TASK-1, which primarily functions to maintain the resting membrane potential and potassium ion homeostasis, while also regulating cellular excitability and stress signal transduction71,72.KCNK3, as a key regulator of the background potassium current, maintains the stability of the cell membrane potential, thereby indirectly influencing cellular energy expenditure, calcium ion influx, and mitochondrial function73,74. These processes are critical upstream signals that trigger or regulate ISR. Our RT-qPCR results showed that the expression of KCNK3 is significantly downregulated in LUAD. Research by Lin et al. also found that KCNK3 is significantly downregulated in LUAD tissues, and its low expression is associated with a poor prognosis in patients. Furthermore, overexpression of KCNK3 can regulate the occurrence, development, and glucose metabolism of LUAD both in vivo and in vitro. Regarding the mechanism, KCNK3-mediated differential metabolites are primarily enriched in the AMPK signaling pathway, and it suppresses the proliferation and glucose metabolism of LUAD cells by activating the AMPK-TXNIP pathway74. Although other mechanisms underlying the relationship between KCNK3 and LUAD are not yet fully understood, as an ion channel-related gene, its functional abnormality and association with tumor stress adaptation and prognosis have been preliminarily confirmed75.

CHEK1, a member of the CHEK family, is a serine/threonine-specific protein kinase whose primary function is to sense DNA damage and activate cell cycle checkpoints, thereby regulating DNA repair and cell survival76. Studies have reported that the alteration frequency of CHEK1 is 0.80% across all cancers, while its mutation rate is 2.62% in malignant solid tumors77. In LUAD, CHEK1 is highly expressed, and this upregulation is associated with promoter methylation, gene amplification, and miRNA regulation. High CHEK1 expression predicts a poor prognosis in patients. Furthermore, CHEK1 is primarily enriched in the cell cycle pathway; tumor cell clusters expressing CHEK1 are more prone to metastasis and epithelial-mesenchymal transition. Within the ISR, CHEK1 influences LUAD prognosis by mediating DNA damage repair and cell cycle regulation78. Our RT-qPCR results also demonstrate that CHEK1 is highly expressed in lung adenocarcinoma, providing a multi-angled potential target for the prognostic assessment and targeted therapy of LUAD.Furthermore, the regulatory link between CHEK1 and the ISR pathway is primarily mediated through the common bridge of oxidative stress. On one hand, stressors such as ROS can directly cause DNA damage, thereby activating the CHEK1-mediated DNA damage response79. On the other hand, CHEK1 itself can function as a nuclear ROS sensor; oxidative modification of its specific cysteine residues alters its conformation and function, subsequently inhibiting mitochondrial protein synthesis and forming a cross-organelle regulatory circuit that promotes chemotherapy resistance80. In summary, CHEK1 is highly expressed in LUAD and associated with poor prognosis. It not only drives tumor progression through cell cycle and DNA repair pathways but also, via its interaction with the ISR pathway at the level of oxidative stress, jointly influences tumor cell stress adaptation and therapy resistance. This makes it a potential therapeutic target with multidimensional regulatory value.

GSEA revealed significant associations between AGER, GPX3, CCNA2, KCNK3, and CHEK1 and pathways such as the cell cycle, DNA replication, proteasome, and spliceosome. These findings suggest that these pathways play important functional roles in the pathogenesis and progression of LUAD. Aberrant activation of cell cycle and DNA replication pathways represents a core characteristic enabling unlimited proliferation of tumor cells78,81. The enrichment of the five prognostic genes identified in this study within these pathways indicates their potential involvement in the malignant progression of LUAD by regulating cell cycle progression and DNA replication processes.Among these genes, CCNA2, a member of the cyclin family, is a key regulator of the G1 to S phase transition. Its aberrant expression can lead to cell cycle dysregulation, thereby accelerating tumor cell proliferation66. CHEK1, a crucial kinase in the DNA damage response pathway, can halt the cell cycle upon detection of DNA replication anomalies or damage, buying time for DNA repair76. However, CHEK1 is frequently overexpressed in tumors, which paradoxically helps tumor cells cope with stress induced by therapies like chemotherapy, promoting drug resistance and survival82,83. Although AGER and GPX3 are traditionally associated with the regulation of oxidative stress, their enrichment in cell cycle and DNA replication pathways in this study hints that they may indirectly influence cell cycle progression by modulating oxidative stress levels84,85. For instance, GPX3, acting as a glutathione peroxidase, clears intracellular reactive oxygen species54. Altered ROS levels can affect the expression and activity of cell cycle-related proteins, thereby regulating cell proliferation86. AGER, on the other hand, might participate in activating cell cycle regulatory signaling pathways by mediating extracellular matrix signals86,87. The enrichment of KCNK3, a potassium channel protein, in the cell cycle and DNA replication pathways might be related to the role of potassium ion balance in regulating cell proliferation signals88.The proteasome pathway is a key route for protein degradation under cellular stress and is crucial for maintaining intracellular protein homeostasis (proteostasis)89. In LUAD, oxidative stress and DNA damage signals associated with genes like AGER and GPX3 can further activate the proteasome pathway, promoting the degradation of tumor suppressor proteins such as p5390. Upregulation of proteasome activity significantly enhances the adaptability of tumor cells to stress responses91. Regarding the spliceosome pathway, direct research linking the target genes AGER and CHEK1 to this pathway is currently relatively limited. However, studies have confirmed that spliceosome dysfunction is closely associated with platinum resistance and prognosis in LUAD. For example, SNRPA can influence the DNA damage repair efficiency of LUAD cells by regulating the alternative splicing of exon 8 of the DNA repair gene ERCC1; its high expression predicts shorter survival in patients receiving platinum-based chemotherapy92. Considering the regulatory role of the ISR on RNA processing, it is plausible that splice variants of genes like AGER and CHEK1 might be involved in the functional regulation of the spliceosome pathway.In summary, the prognostic genes identified in this study—AGER, GPX3, CCNA2, KCNK3, and CHEK1—likely participate in the ISR regulatory network in lung adenocarcinoma by coordinately regulating the cell cycle, DNA replication, proteasome, and spliceosome pathways. This provides potential targets and a theoretical basis for targeted therapy in LUAD. Future research should further explore the cross-regulatory mechanisms between these pathways to deepen the understanding of the role of the ISR in the development and progression of lung adenocarcinoma.

Analysis of tumor mutational patterns revealed elevated somatic mutation burden in high-risk subgroups compared to low-risk counterparts. Supporting evidence49 indicates that AGER downregulation correlates significantly with adverse histological grade, advanced staging, lymph node metastasis, and TP53 mutations, positioning it as a potential biomarker for unfavorable LUAD prognosis, with its expression positively associated with infiltrating immune cell abundance. Given the established link between TP53 mutations and poor outcomes in LUAD, our findings suggest that risk-stratified personalized therapeutic strategies could offer renewed hope for LUAD management, a paradigm already validated in malignancies like acute myeloid leukemia93.

In the immune infiltration analysis, we identified 10 types of immune cells that showed significant differences between the high- and low-risk groups. The infiltration patterns of these cells are not only significantly correlated with patient prognosis but also provide important clues for understanding the mechanisms of immune evasion and the heterogeneity of immunotherapy response in LUAD. The infiltration levels of M0 macrophages, M1 macrophages, and activated memory CD4⁺ T cells were significantly elevated in the high-risk group, a phenomenon that may be closely associated with poor prognosis in LUAD. M0 macrophages, as unpolarized precursor cells, can be polarized into either pro-tumor M2 phenotypes or pro-inflammatory M1 phenotypes within the tumor microenvironment94,95. The concurrent enrichment of both M0 and M1 macrophages in the high-risk group of this study suggests a potential dynamic, inflammation-driven state of the microenvironment. Although M1 macrophages possess anti-tumor potential, their sustained activation can also promote tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and angiogenesis by releasing inflammatory factors such as IL-6 and TNF-α, thereby activating signaling pathways like NF-κB96. Furthermore, high infiltration of M0 macrophages may indicate dysregulated immunomodulatory functions, leading to weakened immune surveillance97. The abnormal enrichment of activated memory CD4⁺ T cells is also noteworthy. While these cells are expected to perform immune memory and helper functions, in the tumor microenvironment, they may become exhausted due to persistent antigen stimulation or differentiate into immunosuppressive regulatory T cells (Tregs)44. This study observed a strong positive correlation between CCNA2 and activated memory CD4⁺ T cells (cor = 0.52), suggesting that aberrant cell cycle regulation and T cell dysfunction may interact, jointly shaping an immunosuppressive microenvironment. In contrast, memory B cells were more highly infiltrated in the low-risk group and showed a negative correlation with CHEK1 (cor = -0.40). Memory B cells can exert anti-tumor effects through antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) and antigen presentation. Their reduced infiltration may weaken the humoral immune response, thereby facilitating tumor immune evasion98.

Integrative immune profiling of LUAD subgroups revealed profound disparities in immune infiltration patterns, TME composition, and immune checkpoint expression between high- and low-risk cohorts, with CCNA2 and CHEK1 exhibiting the strongest correlations to these immunogenomic features. These observations suggest multi-level crosstalk among prognostic genes, between immune cell populations, and at gene-immune interfaces that may collectively reprogram immunosuppressive niches, thereby promoting LUAD progression.

The LUAD prognostic risk model based on ISR-related genes, constructed in this study, demonstrates significant advantages and innovation in theoretical foundation, predictive performance, and clinical utility compared to existing models. Current LUAD prognostic models often rely on single pathological indicators (such as TNM stage)99 single-pathway gene sets, or complex multi-omics features. While valuable, these models commonly suffer from limitations such as a narrow biological perspective, high barriers to clinical translation, or a disconnect from therapeutic decision-making100,101. In contrast, our model starts from the integrated biological core hub of ISR, systematically incorporating genes from five types of stress responses—UPR, HSR, OSR, HySR, and DDR. This provides a more comprehensive molecular characterization of tumors adapting to multiple stresses, resulting in a theoretical framework that is more systematic and biologically interpretable.In terms of predictive performance, the model demonstrated robust results in the TCGA training set and two independent validation sets (GSE50081, GSE14814), with 1- to 3-year AUC values consistently exceeding 0.7. Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed significantly lower survival rates in the high-risk group (P < 0.0001), confirming its strong generalizability. Regarding clinical utility, the model contains only 5 genes, making it suitable for detection via routine clinical techniques like qPCR. Furthermore, the constructed nomogram integrates the risk score with TNM staging, enhancing the convenience and interpretability of clinical application.In summary, this model provides a multidimensional theoretical basis and a practical framework for prognosis-oriented personalized treatment of LUAD, demonstrating significant potential for clinical translation.

Conclusion

Through systematic bioinformatics exploration, this study identified 5 prognostic genes (AGER, GPX3, CCNA2, KCNK3, and CHEK1) associated with the ISR during LUAD progression. The prognostic risk model developed from these genes demonstrated robust predictive power for LUAD survival outcomes, offering novel therapeutic possibilities to counteract its adverse clinical trajectory. Further analyses confirmed the model’s associations with the dynamics of the tumor microenvironment and drug sensitivity profiles. Additionally, the TF-mRNA-miRNA network elucidated key molecules potentially governing LUAD pathogenesis. Notably, we validated the prognostic relevance of these genes in clinical cohorts. However, this study has several limitations. Firstly, RT-qPCR failed to detect significant changes in GPX3 expression, which may be attributed to its low expression abundance, the small sample size (only 5 cases per group), and the technical sensitivity of the assay. Secondly, the research relies on bulk transcriptome data, which cannot resolve intra-tumoral heterogeneity at the single-cell level. Additionally, the analysis is confined to the mRNA level, lacking protein-level validation (e.g., Western blot/IHC) as well as cellular and animal experiments to directly elucidate the functional mechanisms of the genes. Finally, the predictive efficacy of the risk model and the clinical value of candidate drugs require further validation through prospective studies.

To address these limitations, future research will be systematically developed in the following directions: At the technical validation level, more sensitive and precise techniques such as digital PCR, single-cell RNA sequencing, and spatial transcriptomics will be employed to re-evaluate the expression of low-abundance genes like GPX3 in expanded, multi-center cohorts. This will be combined with proteomics and multiplex immunofluorescence to comprehensively verify gene expression consistency at the level of protein localization and activity. In terms of mechanism elucidation, technologies such as organoid culture, conditional gene knockout mouse models, and CRISPR screening will be used to directly validate the regulatory functions of the five core genes on phenotypes like LUAD cell proliferation, stress adaptation, and immune evasion both in vitro and in vivo. This will be integrated with single-cell multi-omics data to deeply analyze their heterogeneous expression and functional networks within different cell subsets of the tumor microenvironment. For clinical translation, prospective, multi-center observational cohort studies will be initiated to validate the independent prognostic value of the risk model. Based on predicted drug sensitivity results, personalized treatment strategies will be explored in patient-derived xenograft models or early-phase clinical trials. Ultimately, the goal is to translate the findings of this study into clinically applicable prognostic testing kits and novel combination therapeutic strategies based on the ISR pathway, thereby advancing LUAD diagnosis and treatment towards greater precision and personalization.
