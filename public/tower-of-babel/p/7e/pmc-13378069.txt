Macrophage exosome-modified Mn/Se nanoheterostructure induces post-infarction cardiomyocyte regeneration and immunometabolic microenvironment repair
Authors: Linghuan Guo, Zhi Xiong, Kai Sheng, Qiang Zhang, Jiahui Cheng, Lingling Xu, Wen Wang, Guoyang Zhang, Tingting Yao, Dan Wang, Xiaoer Wei, Mingkang Wang, Yang Lyu, Xinxin Zhao, Lixian Jiang, Xiaojun Cai, Yuanyi Zheng, Lidan Liu, Yan Zhou, Bo Li, Yuehua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Myocardial infarction (MI) is the most lethal cardiovascular disease and poses a critical threat to global health. While current MI therapies partially improve cardiac function, advanced precision therapies with translational potential capable of interrupting the vicious pathological cycles remain a pivotal challenge to be addressed. Here, based on a Mn/Se nanoheterostructure that meets human elemental requirements, we constructed a PMS@ME nanotherapeutic system for achieving efficient post-MI repair. Polydopamine was incorporated to synergistically enhance biocompatibility and therapeutic efficacy. The surface is modified with exosomes derived from macrophages that highly express adhesion receptors, enabling precise targeting to the myocardial injury area. PMS@ME administration promotes cardiomyocyte survival, extracellular repair, and mitochondrial homeostasis, while suppressing oxidative damage and immune-inflammatory disorders, achieving preserved cardiac function. Importantly, PMS@ME can induce cardiomyocyte to restart the cell cycle and promotes post-MI cardiomyocyte regeneration by inhibiting the Hippo signaling pathway. These findings demonstrate the potential of the PMS@ME system in MI treatment, offering a blueprint for designing multifunctional nanotherapies tailored to human elemental essentials.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04572-2.

Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI) has arisen significant attention due to its high incidence, mortality rate, and poor prognosis [1]. Ischemia and hypoxia-induced mitochondrial damage-derived reactive oxygen species (ROS) promote the secretion of inflammatory cytokines and inflammatory responses, which in turn stimulate further oxidative damage [2, 3]. Excessive activation of inflammatory responses leads to the imbalance of immune homeostasis, driving a series of pathological processes, including tissue and cell injury or apoptosis, and persistent mitochondrial and cardiac dysfunction. Without effective intervention, the damage will be aggravated, leading to increased cardiomyocyte loss and a vicious cycle that ultimately results in heart failure or even death [4, 5]. Current therapeutic strategies for MI still face limitations in achieving precise specific modulation and efficient repair [6, 7]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop strategies that can target the restoration of microenvironment homeostasis and break the pathological vicious cycle.

In the natural physiological state, the regenerative capacity of mammalian cardiomyocytes declines progressively from birth, with minimal self-renewal in adults [8]. However, MI can reactivate the regenerative potential of cardiomyocytes, but the intrinsic proliferation rate of cardiomyocyte is too low to fulfill the requirements for tissue repair [9, 10]. Moreover, regenerative process requires coordination of inflammatory microenvironment and extracellular regulation [11]. Existing researches have been exploring regenerative therapies, although they still face critical unmet challenges including single therapeutic efficacy, weak regenerative effect with potential teratogenic risks, biological barriers and expensive operational costs [12–14].

Selenium (Se), one of the essential trace elements for humans, is able to directly scavenge ROS, inhibit ferroptosis, and alleviate cell damage induced by oxidative stress [15, 16]. However, Se exerts its biological functions exclusively in specific forms, and has a narrow therapeutic safety window-excessive Se accumulation will induce toxicity [15]. Notably, nanoscale Se-based drugs have addressed the aforementioned challenges while achieving modulation of the inflammatory microenvironment, promotion of tissue repair, and angiogenesis in certain disease models [17–19]. Manganese (Mn) is another essential trace element for humans with excellent biocompatibility. It forms complexes with superoxide dismutase (SOD) to enhance antioxidant activity, and mitigates mitochondrial damage caused by mitochondrial ROS [20, 21]. Meanwhile, studies have demonstrated that polydopamine (PDA) not only exhibits modest antioxidant capacity and anti-inflammatory effects, but also improves the intrinsic properties of inorganic and organic compounds, such as their oxidizing properties [22–24]. Leveraging these potential advantages and the foundation of nanomedicine development, we integrated Se and Mn to form a nanoheterostructure and used PDA as its surface coating to achieve synergistic effects of dual trace elements therapeutic applications.

In this work, we developed an engineered PMS@ME nanomedicine system for efficient targeted therapy of MI (Fig. 1). The nanomedicine was obtained by encapsulating PDA-MnSe through exosomes derived from bone marrow-derived macrophage (BMDM)-differentiated inflammatory macrophages, with a view to achieving highly biocompatible and precise delivery by taking advantage of the latter’s unique inflammatory microenvironment sensing ability and innate targeting merit [25, 26]. In vivo and in vitro results showed that PMS@ME can be specifically recruited to the site of myocardial injury, blocking the vicious cycle between immune-inflammatory response, mitochondrial damage, and abnormal metabolism. Notably, it activates cardiomyocytes to re-enter the cell cycle by inhibiting the Hippo signaling pathway, thereby promoting cardiomyocyte proliferation. Moreover, it promotes extracellular matrix repair and stimulates angiogenesis. Consequently, these attributes enable PMS@ME to mitigate adverse ventricular remodeling and provide comprehensive cardiac protection. The system offers a promising alternative for MI therapy, broadening the application prospects of multifunctional nanotherapeutic drugs based on trace elements essential to the humans.

Results and discussion

Preparation, characterizations, and enzyme-mimicking activities of PMS@ME

We first synthesized MnSe nanoheterostructures via a thermal reaction method, then prepared PDA-MnSe (PMS) by adding PDA to the MnSe dispersion. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images showed that PMS exhibited relatively uniform dispersion, with particle sizes primarily distributed between 50 and 100 nm (Fig. 2A), and energy dispersive spectroscopy mapping indicated the coexistence of C, N, O, Mn, and Se elements in the composite (Fig. 2B and Fig. S1). For MI therapy, to further endow PMS with potent potential targeting capabilities, we employed a surface coating strategy using exosomes derived from inflammatory-stimulated M1 macrophages (IM). IM was obtained from bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) induced with LPS and IFN-γ, and as expected, the membranes of IM expressed significantly more adhesion receptors CD44 and CD11b than BMDM membranes (Fig. 2C). Afterwards, IM-derived exosomes (ME) were isolated, as confirmed by TEM, nanoparticle tracking analysis, and canonical exosome-related markers CD9, CD63, CD81, and Calnexin (Fig. 2D and Fig. S2, 3). Finally, PMS@ME was constructed via an optimized ultrasonic fusion method [27]. The TEM image stained with uranium acetate and fluorescence colocalization experiments confirmed that PMS was successfully encapsulated within the ME membranes (Fig. 2E and F). CD44 and CD11b are important surface receptors on monocytes/macrophages [28]. During inflammation, these receptors can recognize adhesion-related molecules/cytokines including VCAM-1 and integrin-β1, thereby mediating the recruitment of monocytes/macrophages to the inflammatory site [29, 30]. Western blot analysis showed that both PMS@ME and ME exhibited nearly equal amounts of abundant CD44 and CD11b expression (Fig. 2G), neither of which was weaker than IM membranes. Meanwhile, VCAM-1 and integrin-β1 expression levels were dramatically increased in myocardial tissues after MI (Fig. 2H), which further enhanced the recognition efficiency of PMS@ME, facilitating its recruitment to the injured region.

The various physicochemical properties of PMS were further verified. Dynamic light scattering measurements indicated that the average particle sizes of MnSe, PMS, and PMS@ME were 125.6 nm, 146.1 nm, and 197.6 nm, respectively (Fig. 2I). Furthermore, MnSe, PMS, and PMS@ME can keep the particle size stable within 7 days (Fig. S4). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns revealed that the as-prepared MnSe exhibited good crystallinity; compared with pure MnSe, the XRD characteristic peaks of PMS showed no obvious shift (Fig. 2J). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis indicated that the FTIR spectra of PMS and MnSe were highly similar; the stretching vibration peaks of Mn-Se in both MnSe and PMS appeared in the range of 500–600 cm⁻¹ (Fig. 2K), which was close to the previously reported characteristic peaks of pure MnSe [31]. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy results showed that among the spectra of PMS, an N 1s peak was observed, which can be attributed to the nitrogen content introduced by the PDA coating (Fig. 2L). Furthermore, the measured spectra of PMS and MnSe revealed the presence of Mn 2p and Se 3d peaks. In the Mn 2p spectrum, MnSe nanoparticles (NPs) exhibited binding energies at 653.9 eV and 640.7 eV, corresponding to Mn 2p₁/₂ and Mn 2p₃/₂, respectively. For PMS, the two corresponding peaks appeared at 654 eV, and 640.3 eV. The Se 3d spectrum of PMS displayed a pair of peaks at 55.0 eV and 54 eV, while the Se 3d spectrum of MnSe showed two peaks at 55.9 eV and 54.1 eV (Fig. 2M and Table S1). These were consistent with previously reported literature, which also proved the successful synthesis of MnSe and PMS [32, 33]. The Zeta potentials of MnSe, PMS, and PMS@ME were − 33 mV, -41 mV, and − 51 mV, respectively (Fig. S5). Thermogravimetric analysis and Differential scanning calorimetry curves analysis indicated that the contents of MnSe and PDA in PMS were 58.03% and 42.97%, respectively (Fig. S6).

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) assay kits, Electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy, and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) scavenging assay kits were used to evaluate the ROS-scavenging capacities of PMS NPs. Generation of superoxide anion (O₂⁻) was achieved via electron transfer from UV-excited riboflavin, while ·OH was generated using the ferrous ion (Fe2+)/H2O2 reaction system. The results revealed that PMS exhibits significantly superior scavenging activity against O₂⁻, ·OH and H2O2 compared to PDA and MnSe (Fig. 3A-C). Dissolved oxygen content assays and H2O2 scavenging experiments further demonstrated that PMS possessed remarkable ability to mimic catalase (Fig. 3D and E). Moreover, the scavenging efficiency of PMS toward O₂⁻, ·OH, H2O2, as well as the dissolved oxygen content, exhibited a positive correlation with concentrations (Fig. 3F-I and Fig. S7). Collectively, these results illustrated the construction of the PMS@ME system and depicted its targeting potential and multiple biomimetic enzyme activities. Notably, the above experimental results indicate that compared with MnSe and PDA, PMS exhibits a significant enhancement in enzymatic activities, which may be attributed to the reduced activation energy required for the reaction by PDA coating [34].

PMS@ME alleviates MI injury and promotes cardiac function recovery

The in vivo biosafety was first evaluated before investigating the biological functions of PMS@ME. Histopathological analysis conducted at days 7 and 14 post-injection of PMS@ME revealed no major organ damage (Fig. S8), and both blood routine and biochemical parameters remained within normal ranges (Fig. S9 and Table S2). Additionally, neither PDA, MnSe, PMS, nor PMS@ME induced procoagulant effects or hemolysis (Fig. S10), demonstrating the excellent biocompatibility of PMS@ME nanomedicine system. As designed, upon tissue injury, the PMS@ME delivery system specifically recognizes and actively accumulates at target lesion sites, thereby achieving a low-immunogenic and high-safety targeted therapy. To investigate whether PMS@ME possesses targeted behavior at the cardiac pathological sites and its distribution profile in major organs, Cy5.5 fluorescent dye-labeled PMS or PMS@ME was intravenously injected into MI mice, followed by ex vivo organ fluorescence imaging at 12 and 24 h. The control group was set up by injecting Cy5.5-labeled PMS into Sham mice. The fluorescence images and quantitative analysis showed that compared with the PMS and control groups, PMS@ME could efficiently aggregate and reside at the cardiac injury site, demonstrating excellent targeting ability (Fig. 4A and B). It is noteworthy that, unlike normal hearts, PMS itself presented certain functions to accumulate within the damaged hearts of MI mouse models (Fig. 4A and B). Figures S11 and S12 illustrated the biodistribution of PMS and PMS@ME within the liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and brain, revealing no significant differences among the three groups.

Next, we conducted follow-up monitoring to evaluate the protective efficacy of PMS@ME against myocardial infarction in mice. Echocardiographic results revealed that PMS@ME treatment progressively increased left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction (LVEF) and fractional shortening (FS), while reducing left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV) and end-diastolic volume (LVEDV), suggesting significant improvements in cardiac function (Fig. 4C and D). Late Gadolinium Enhancement (LGE) is a Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) sequence utilizing gadolinium-based contrast agents and specific scanning parameters, commonly employed for diagnostic assessment of myocardial infarction extent. Specifically, when myocardial necrosis, fibrosis, inflammation, or scarring occurs, the integrity of cardiomyocyte membranes is broken and unable to clean the gadolinium contrast agents, which ultimately ends up leading to their prolonged retention in injury areas. Gadolinium-based contrast agents exhibit hyperintense signals in MRI. Thus, when MI mice are scanned during the delayed phase (specific time interval post-contrast injection), lesional areas display bright “enhanced signals” in stark contrast to normal myocardium, enabling precise localization of pathological regions [35]. Multislice LGE sequence analysis at postoperative days 4 and 14 showed that PMS@ME treatment markedly reduced infarct sizes and abnormal LV enlargement in MI mice (Fig. 4F). Combining cine MRI (a dynamic imaging sequence that captures ventricular wall thickness changes and contractile motion), we observed that MI mice exhibited pronounced regional wall motion abnormalities and a markedly elevated left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVESD)/end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD) ratio compared to the sham group (Fig. 4G and Fig. S13, 14). Notably, PMS@ME distinctly improved left ventricular contractility and normalized the LVESD/LVEDD ratio (Fig. 4G and Fig. S13, 14). These imaging data intuitively demonstrated that PMS@ME enhances ventricular wall systolic and diastolic function to maintain long-term cardiac function improvement. After 14 days of follow-up, Masson’s trichrome staining and hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining showed that PMS@ME markedly reduced myocardial fibrosis, attenuated infarct expansion, and ameliorated MI-induced adverse ventricular remodeling (Fig. 4H and Fig. S15). Moreover, Kaplan–Meier analysis showed a significantly improved 14 day-survival of PMS@ME treated mice compared to MI group (Fig. S16). Besides, all of these findings demonstrated superior overall cardioprotective efficacy of PMS@ME compared to PMS, which was attributed to the targeted modification.

PMS@ME induces extracellular repair and inhibits cardiac cell death

Myocardial tissue, a highly organized structure comprising cardiomyocytes, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, as well as vascular and neuronal networks, is filled with stroma that constitutes a complex extracellular matrix (ECM). The ECM contains structural, adhesive, matricellular proteins, and integrins, with collagens predominating as the major component [36]. Ischemic-hypoxic injury to the myocardium disrupts the dynamic balance between ECM synthesis and degradation, leading to ECM remodeling. Collagen deposition in the infarct border zone contributes to injured tissue repair and functional adaptation, which is critical for preserving cardiomyocyte viability and preventing infarct expansion and rupture after MI [37, 38]. On postoperative day 14, immunofluorescence showed that the expression levels of collagen types I and III and α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) in the infarct border zone were upregulated in the PMS@ME group than in the MI group (Fig. 5A), suggesting accelerated ECM repair. In addition to cardiomyocyte damage and disrupted extracellular homeostasis, MI also induces severe or even irreversible injury to coronary microvascular cells, while facilitating blood flow maintenance helps reduce metabolic and immune disorders in the affected area [39, 40]. The results showed that PMS@ME greatly increased the amount of CD31+ vessels in the infarct border zone (Fig. 5B and C), predicting an increase in myocardial capillary density and perfusion. Correspondingly, cardiomyocyte hypertrophy in the peri-infarct zone was alleviated (Fig. 5D and E).

Subsequently, we investigated the manifestations of cardiomyocyte death in the acute phase. In the injured myocardial tissues, western blot and immunofluorescence co-staining of TUNEL with the cardiomyocyte-specific marker cTnT confirmed that PMS@ME significantly inhibited cardiomyocyte apoptosis, as evidenced by decreased pro-apoptotic protein Bax and TUNEL+ cells, and an increase in anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 (Fig. 5F-H and Fig. S17). Increasing evidences have indicated that oxidative damage-induced cellular metabolic disorders lead to iron overload in cardiomyocytes, thereby triggering cardiomyocyte ferroptosis [41, 42]. Prussian blue staining revealed that iron deposition was significantly diminished in PMS@ME-intervened mice compared to those MI controls, indicating that PMS@ME also inhibits post-MI cardiomyocyte ferroptosis (Fig. 5I). In a H2O2-stimulated HL-1 cell model, we further validated the in vitro therapeutic capacity of the core component PMS. Before that, the time-dependent phagocytosis-enhancing properties and cellular safety of PMS were demonstrated using CCK-8, Annexin-V/Propidium Iodide (PI) staining, and Calcein-AM/PI staining assays (Fig. S18-22). Afterwards, in these therapeutic evaluative protocols, PMS exhibited superior anti-apoptotic ability to PDA and MnSe, and progressively strengthened the therapeutic effect with increasing administrated doses (Fig. 6A-F), suggesting a synergistic therapeutic function. In addition, to confirm whether the therapeutic efficacy of PMS was influenced by modification with exosome membranes derived from M1-type macrophages, verification was performed using the H₂O₂-stimulated HL-1 model. The results revealed no significant difference in the therapeutic effects between the PMS and PMS@ME groups, indicating that the modification of exosome membranes from M1-type macrophages mainly serves a targeting role and does not affect the therapeutic efficacy of PMS (Fig. S23). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that PMS@ME facilitates long-term repair of infarcted hearts, neovascularization, and cardiomyocyte survival, which contributes to the optimization of cardiac remodeling.

PMS@ME inhibits the Hippo signaling pathway to promote cardiomyocyte proliferation

To further investigate the underlying mechanisms of PMS@ME in influencing MI, HL-1 cardiomyocytes harvested from the Control, H2O2-stimulated, and PMS@ME-treated groups were subjected to RNA sequencing analysis. Principal component analysis revealed significant differences in transcriptomic profiles between the three groups (Fig. S24A). Volcano plot and heat map showed that PMS@ME led to significant upregulation of 177 genes and downregulation of 496 genes compared to pre-treatment (Fig. 7A and B). Compared to the Control group, H2O2 stimulation resulted in the downregulation of 729 genes and the upregulation of 525 genes (Fig. S24B). Venn diagram analysis quantified the overlap of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the Control vs. H2O2 and PMS@ME vs. H2O2 comparisons, with 213 genes shared between the comparisons (Fig. S24C). Gene Ontology analysis revealed that PMS@ME extensively regulated biological processes associated with the cell cycle (Fig. 7C). Further Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis showed that these upregulated DEGs were mainly enriched in the cell cycle and TGF-β signaling pathway, while the downregulated DEGs were mainly enriched in apoptosis and metabolism- and inflammation-related pathways, including p53 signaling pathway, toll-like receptor signaling pathway, mTOR signaling pathway, Hippo signaling pathway, NF-κB signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, HIF-1 signaling pathway, and TNF signaling pathway (Fig. 7D). Correspondingly, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis confirmed that PMS@ME accelerated DNA replication (Fig. 7E). These results suggested that PMS@ME may not only reconfigure the inflammatory and metabolic states of cardiomyocytes, but also induce cardiomyocyte proliferation.

Immediately afterwards, we conducted molecular experiments for validation. Immunofluorescence staining results showed that PMS@ME effectively enhanced the number of cardiomyocytes positively expressing cytokinesis marker Aurora B, proliferation marker Ki67, and mitotic marker phosphorylated histone H3 in the infarct border zone (Fig. 7F-I), suggesting that PMS@ME can promote cardiomyocyte regeneration post-MI. In addition, immunofluorescence detection combined with EdU incorporation assay and Ki67 staining was conducted on H2 O2-stimulated HL-1 cells. The results demonstrated that both PMS and PMS@ME significantly enhanced the proliferation of HL-1 cardiomyocytes. (Fig. S25, 26).

Combined with the sequencing results, we further analyzed the potential upstream regulatory signals. Among the significant enrichment of KEGG signaling pathways described above, the Hippo signaling pathway plays a critical role in biological processes such as cardiac development, regeneration, and mechanical signal transduction [43]. It is a highly conserved signaling pathway and features a kinase cascade as the core of its signal transduction. In mammals, the Hippo signaling pathway consists of upstream components including the complex of mammalian Sterile 20-like kinases 1/2 (MST1/2) and Salvador Family WW Domain Containing Protein 1 (SAV1), large tumor suppressors 1/2 (LATS1/2), along with two downstream transcriptional coactivators: yes-associated protein (YAP) and transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ) [43, 44]. When the Hippo signaling is activated, downstream effectors YAP/TAZ are phosphorylated by Hippo core kinases LATS/2. Phosphorylated YAP/TAZ remain in the cytoplasm and are eventually combined with or without 14-3-3 Protein Family (14-3-3), and then degraded. Conversely, when the Hippo signaling is inactivated, YAP/TAZ can translocate to the nucleus and interact with other transcription factors, regulating gene expression by engaging in biological processes such as cell cycle and cell adhesion [45]. Previous studies have shown that inhibition of the Hippo signaling pathway allows YAP to translocate to the nucleus, forming a functionally complementary transcriptional complex with TEAD, thereby promoting cardiomyocyte proliferation in adult mice and pigs after myocardial infarction [46, 47]. Here, western blot results showed that the expression of the upstream factors phosphorylated MST1, LATS1, and downstream phosphorylated YAP effectors was significantly downregulated after PMS@ME treatment in MI mice (Fig. 7J and Fig. S27). Furthermore, in vitro experiments also demonstrated that PMS@ME markedly inhibited the Hippo signaling pathway in H₂O₂-stimulated HL-1 cells (Fig. S28). These results confirm that PMS@ME may promote cardiomyocyte proliferation after MI by inhibiting the Hippo signaling pathway. Additionally, beyond activating anti-apoptotic and anti-inflammatory signaling, it has been proposed that the promotion of the PI3K/AKT pathway is also associated with cardiomyocyte proliferation [48]. We found that PMS@ME was able to increase phosphorylated PI3K/AKT levels (Fig. 7J and Fig. S27), demonstrating the existence of this synergistic signaling pathway.

PMS@ME remodels immune homeostasis and modulates inflammatory microenvironment

Excessive immunoinflammatory response in MI triggers extensive inflammatory cell infiltration, provoking a cascade of pathological reactions that result in tissue and cellular damage, ventricular remodeling, and ultimately progress to heart failure [49]. Following ischemic injury to the heart, resident macrophages and cardiomyocytes initiate the production of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, which subsequently induce the generation and recruitment of massive neutrophils and monocytes [50]. Monocytes that accumulate in ischemic zones primarily differentiate into two subtypes of macrophages: pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages and anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages. Subsequently, we further investigated the immunomodulatory performance of PMS@ME in MI. The obtained findings demonstrated that PMS@ME markedly reduced the levels of F4/80+CD86+ M1 macrophages and MPO+ neutrophils in the infarct margin zone, while increasing the number of CD206+ M2 macrophages (Fig. 8A-D). Previous studies have shown that regulatory T (Treg) cells, a subset of CD4+ T cells expressing transcription factor Foxp3, maintain immune homeostasis, regulate macrophage differentiation, and alleviate inflammatory injury, making them a key immune cell subpopulation for cardioprotection [51, 52]. Immunofluorescence staining showed that the quantity of Foxp3+CD4+ Tregs in the infarct border zone was significantly higher in PMS@ME-treated mice than in MI controls (Fig. 8E-G). We also examined the protein levels of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory factors in the infarcted tissues as well as upstream associated signals suggested by KEGG analysis. WB results demonstrated evident downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and iNOS) and inflammatory immune response activation signaling (TLR4, phosphorylated NF-κB p65, phosphorylated p38MAPK, and HIF-1α), and upregulation of anti-inflammatory cytokines (Arg1, IL-10, CD206, and TGF-β) after PMS@ME treatment (Fig. 8H-K). Excessive activation of HIF-1α can induce iNOS expression and synergize with NF-κB to promote inflammation [53]. Toll-like receptors (TLRs) are a class of transmembrane receptors capable of activating downstream pro-inflammatory cascades; increased TLR4 leads to activation of NF-κB, mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), and other pro-inflammatory factors, exacerbating inflammatory responses [54]. Additionally, PMS significantly suppressed iNOS expression in the RAW264.7 cell inflammatory model (Fig. 8L and M). Together, these findings indicate that PMS@ME inhibits the recruitment and infiltration of M1 macrophages and neutrophils following MI, and promotes M2 macrophage differentiation and Tregs, thereby optimizing the immune inflammatory state and the cellular survival microenvironment.

PMS@ME improves mitochondrial function and energy production

As the primary intracellular source of ROS, mitochondria-derived excessive ROS production post-MI not only impairs the stability of mitochondrial membrane potential and compromises ATP synthesis capacity but also directly damages cardiomyocytes, ultimately leading to cell death [55, 56]. In vitro experiments, we investigated whether the core nanodrug PMS affects mitochondrial function and metabolism. Fluorescence data indicated that PMS entering the cardiomyocytes could be partially colocalized with mitochondria (Fig. S29). DCFH-DA, MitoSOX, and JC-1 staining demonstrated that PMS reversed H2O2-induced mitochondrial ROS bursts and loss of membrane potential in HL-1 cardiomyocytes (Fig. 9A-F). Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) measurements and ATP detection assay revealed that PMS attenuated the decline in basal respiration, maximal respiration, and ATP production in damaged HL-1 cells (Fig. 9G and H). Meanwhile, PMS exhibited superior performance compared to MnSe or PDA and dose-dependent advantages (Fig. S30-32). In the MI mouse model, PMS@ME efficiently reduced ROS accumulation in damaged myocardium (Fig. 9I and J). Moreover, PMS@ME increased the protein levels of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ co-activator 1α (PGC-1α) and cytochrome c oxidase (COX4), both of which are factors tightly associated with mitochondrial biogenesis and ATP generation [57], suggesting that PMS@ME promotes mitochondrial regeneration (Fig. 9K and Fig. S33). Meanwhile, abnormally enhanced glycolysis and glucose oxidation after MI accelerates oxidative substrate depletion, leading to a reduction in glycogen reserves, which further impairs energy metabolism and exacerbates post-infarction injury [58]. Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining showed a significant increase in glycogen content in the infarct border zone in the PMS@ME-treated group (Fig. S34), suggesting that glycogen reserve depletion was alleviated. This facilitates rapid energy production in cardiomyocytes, maintains cellular function, and thereby alleviates ischemia-hypoxia-induced injury or even necrosis. Overall, PMS@ME reduces oxidative and mitochondrial damage while promoting mitochondrial regeneration and energy production.

Conclusion

In summary, this study constructed a PMS@ME nanodrug system for targeted therapy of MI. The surface of PMS@ME is modified using M1 macrophage-derived exosomes that retain the inflammatory chemotactic capacity of M1 macrophages, enabling efficient recruitment to the injury site. PMS@ME inhibits phosphorylated MST1, LATS1, and YAP of the Hippo signaling pathway, and increases the dephosphorylated YAP level, which translocates to the nucleus, participates in processes such as the cell cycle, thus promoting cardiomyocyte regeneration after MI. Moreover, PMS@ME promotes extracellular matrix repair and neovascularization to optimize the extracellular environment. PMS@ME also reduces M1 macrophage and neutrophil infiltration and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, while increasing the levels of M2 macrophages, Tregs, and anti-inflammatory cytokines, creating a more available microenvironment for damaged tissue repair. Additionally, PMS@ME possesses multiple enzyme-like activities for efficiently scavenging ROS, promotes mitochondrial regeneration and energy production, breaking the vicious cycle between inflammation and oxidative damage. This nanomedicine strategy shows great promise for comprehensive clinical treatment of MI as well as other ischemic-hypoxic disorders.

Several potential limitations that warrant further investigation. First, the number of biological replicates in the in vitro experiments may be slightly lower. Second, this study mainly focused on cardiomyocytes, and the protective effects of PMS@ME on other important cardiac cell types need to be further elucidated in the future. Besides, the therapeutic efficacy of PMS@ME needs to be further validated in larger mammalian models in the future.

Experimental section

Some methods are included in the Supporting Information.

Animals

Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, 20–25 g) were purchased from SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All mice were given ad libitum access to food and water and subjected to 1-week acclimatization before being included in this study. The animal procedures gained the approval of The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine affiliated Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital (Approval No: DWLL2025-1048) and performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

BMDM isolation and M1 macrophage induction

Mice aged 6–8 weeks were executed via cervical dislocation, then the tibia and femur were isolated under aseptic conditions, and transferred to a biosafety cabinet after alcohol sterilization for a further isolation of the remaining surrounding muscles and connective tissues. Bone marrow cells were rinsed with Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS) until bone marrow turned into white, then filtered through a 200mesh filter. After being filtered, bone marrow cells were lysed with erythrocyte lysis solution. After centrifugating with PBS, then removing supernatant, bone marrow cells were gained and resuspended in DMEM with 10% FBS and 10 ng/mL macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF). The medium was replaced at one-day intervals to induce the differentiation of myeloid cells into M0 macrophages. After being induced for 7 days, the medium was replaced by 100 ng/mL LPS (ST1470-10 mg) and 100 ng/mL IFN-γ (485-MI, R&D Systems) through 24 h to realize M1 polarization [59].

Isolation and identification of M1 macrophage-derived exosomes

M1 Macrophage-derived exosomes were isolated following a previous article [60]. M1 macrophages were cultured, and then the culture supernatant was centrifuged at 300 g for 10 min to eliminate free cells, and 2000 g for 10 min to remove cellular fragments. The resultant supernatant was further centrifuged at 10,000 g for 30 min at 4 °C to deplete cellular debris. Subsequently, the supernatant was subjected to sequential ultracentrifugation twice at 100,000 g for 70 min to harvest a clear precipitate containing exosomes. The isolated exosomes were resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and stored at -80 °C for subsequent experiments. For exosome characterization, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) samples were prepared following a standard protocol and visualized with a transmission electron microscope (FEI Talos F200X G2, USA). Particle size distribution was determined using a nanoparticle tracking analysis (Particle Metrix ZetaView Nano, Germany). The signature markers of the extracted exosomes were verified via Western blotting.

The synthesis of MnSe nanoheterostructures

First, 594 mg of MnCl2·4 H2 O and 576 mg of selenium powder were added to 90 mL of oleylamine solution, followed by the addition of 30 mL of 1-dodecanethiol. The mixture was then stirred for 2 h to achieve complete dissolution. After that, the solution was transferred into a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE)-lined autoclave and reacted at 180 °C for 12 h. Once cooled to room temperature, the product was first washed three times with n-hexane and then twice with ethanol. Centrifugation was performed at 8000 rpm for 5 min to collect the gray-green sample, which was finally dispersed in ethanol for subsequent use.

Preparation of PDA-MnSe

To prepare the PDA-MnSe nanocomposite, 100 mg of MnSe was dispersed in 100 mL of ethanol, and 40 mg of dopamine was then added and dispersed in the above solution. The mixed solution was stirred at 25 °C for 2 h. Afterward, the product was collected by centrifugation at 7500 rpm for 5 min, washed three times with ethanol to remove unreacted impurities. Finally, the product was dispersed in ethanol and stored at -20 °C.

Preparation of PMS@ME

PMS@ME was fabricated via an optimized ultrasonic fusion method [27]. Firstly, a 1 mL aliquot of PMS solution with a concentration of 1 mg/mL was thoroughly mixed with 2 mg of isolated M1 macrophage-derived exosome membrane (quantified based on protein content). The resulting mixture was subjected to ice bath sonication at 75 W for 5 min using an ultrasonic processor (SB-5200DT, SCIENTZ, China) to promote effective membrane coating. To achieve uniform size distribution and nanoscale dimensions, the mixture was subsequently processed through a sequential extrusion protocol utilizing Nuclepore™ hydrophilic membranes with nominal pore sizes of 800 nm and 400 nm, yielding final size of PMS@ME with a hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 197 nm.

Myocardial infarction mouse model

A myocardial infarction (MI) model was established according to a previous article [61]. The mice were anesthetized after inhaling 2% isoflurane through an anesthesia machine delivery system. The heart was exposed to perform a left-sided thoracotomy, then a 6 − 0 silk thread was used to ligate the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery. A mosquito clamp was used to gently return the heart to the chest cavity. The area supplied by LAD (anterior and apical parts of the left ventricle) turned pale indicating successful ligation. Sham-operated mice were set as the control group, and the 6 − 0 silk thread around the LAD coronary artery was not tied.

MRI measurements

All MRI experiments were performed using a horizontal bore 9.4 T/30 cm uMR (uMR 9.4 T, United Imaging Life Science Instrument, Wuhan, China) An 86-mm diameter transmit volume coil and a three-loop surface coil were used. A combination of an 86-mm inner diameter (ID) volume coil for radio frequency (RF) transmission and a custom-designed dedicated RF coil were used for mice cardiac imaging. This coil was positioned on the mouse’s abdomen to encompass the thoracic region containing the heart. To synchronize data acquisition with the ECG signs, an ECG triggering gating (SA Instruments) was applied. Typical heart rates were between 375 and 500 bpm (cycle length 120 to 160 msec) with a fluctuation of 39 bpm (10 msec) per cardiac cycle [62].

Mice were initially induced to anesthesia by 4% isoflurane. Then mice were subsequently transferred to the MRI bed with heating systems and fixed by bite and ear bars. Isoflurane level was then gradually reduced and maintained at 1.2%. During this experiment, animals were maintained at a light and stable level of anesthesia and the rectal temperature was monitored and kept at 37 °C (SA Instruments) [63].

A cardiac gated GRE sequence was used to acquire cardiac CINE images. Imaging parameters for mice were as follows: TR/TE = 2.90/1.12 ms, FOV = 16 × 32 mm2, matrix size = 104 × 208, with 6 slices which thickness was 1.0 mm. A single short-axis slice was obtained in approximately 1.5 min, leading to a total scan time for cine data of around 10 min.

For delayed contrast enhancement, 0.6 mmol/kg Gd-DOTA was injected, via an intra-peritoneal route for mice, following initial baseline scanning [64]. Following contrast agent administration and short time delay of ~ 20 min to allow for late enhancement to establish a high SNR, multi-slice IR GRE sequence with single TI point was used to acquire late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) images. Additional parameters for mice: TR/TE = 3.01/1.17 ms, FOV = 16 × 32 mm2, matrix size = 136 × 272, slice thickness = 1.0 mm, flip angle = 30°, number of signal average = 4, TI = 250 ms, total acquisition time ~ 3.5 min/slice [64].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed via GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) and SPSS software 27.0 (SPSS Inc., USA). Results in this work were expressed by mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used to statistically analyze the data. RNA seq statistics were used in the Wilcoxon rank sum test with Bonferroni correction. All statistical analyses were performed at a 95% confidence level. P < 0.05 was considered to show significant differences for all studies.
