Postoperative Outcome of Prelaminated Radial Forearm Free Flap for Soft Palate Reconstruction
Authors: Zhaojun Zhu, Felix Johnson, Amir Bolooki, Luca Engelmann, Naglaa Mansour, Andreas Knopf, Elias Scherer, Anna Stenzl, Benedikt Hofauer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesOne method for the reconstruction of the entire soft palate is to use prelaminated radial forearm flaps (PRFFF). In a two‐step procedure the back of the flap is first epithelialized with split‐skin and subsequently used for soft palate reconstruction. Limited data exist on the perioperative and postoperative course of this technique. In this study, we investigated the perioperative course and postsurgical outcomes of patients who received a PRFF for soft palate reconstruction.MethodsAll patients, who received a PRFFF at our clinics since 2012 were identified retrospectively. The operation was carried out in two stages. The perioperative course, postoperative complications and the functional outcome were recorded by analyzing patient files and operation protocols.ResultsA total of 11 patients were enrolled (sex: 9 male, 2 female; age: 58 ± 14, 10/11 squamous cell carcinoma of the soft palate, 1/11 adenoid cystic carcinoma of the soft palate). Postoperative complications occurred in 6/11 patients. Three patients underwent revision surgery. One patient developed postoperatively a central pulmonary embolism leading to a prolonged intensive care unit stay. Flap viability was 100% across all cases and there was no high donor site morbidity in postoperative follow‐ups.ConclusionThe prelaminated radial forearm free flap allows a better physiological reconstruction of the soft palate while showing similar perioperative complication rates compared to the standard radial forearm free flap. In this case series no flap loss occurred, and functional outcomes were satisfactory.

Introduction

Malignant tumors of the soft palate are relatively rare and account for 5%–12% of oropharyngeal cancers [1]. Treatment is often associated with significant functional and aesthetic deficits. The oropharynx serves both the respiratory and the digestive systems and plays a crucial role in swallowing. The resection of critical structures within the oropharynx during tumor resection can result in oropharyngeal dysphagia. Oropharyngeal dysphagia can lead to serious medical consequences, such as malnutrition, dehydration and aspiration pneumonia [2]. The soft palate, as part of the oropharynx, separates the oral from the nasopharynx. Resection of the soft palate results in velopharyngeal insufficiency leading to hypernasal speech, speech misarticulation and nasal regurgitation [3]. Non‐surgical treatment of velopharyngeal insufficiency includes speech therapy and prosthetic rehabilitation with obturators. In some cases, the obturator disturbs the movement of the residual velopharyngeal muscles. This leads to blockage or free space between the tissues and the obturator [4]. To achieve a mobile neo‐soft palate, surgical reconstruction is necessary. The radial forearm free flap has become the most popular choice for head and neck reconstruction since it shows high flap survival rates and low donor site morbidity [5]. For reconstruction of the oropharynx the radial forearm free flap (RFFF) is used. But there are disadvantages to overcome. The RFFF does not preserve natural velopharyngeal function as the flap tends to shrink due to scarring. Folding of the RFFF would lead to bulkiness of the flap affecting functional outcomes. In the reconstruction of the soft palate, it is especially important that both sides of the flap are epithelialized, as this prevents shrinkage and thus helps preserve the mobility of the neo‐soft palate, avoiding a loss of function or failure to restore it. Therefore, the prelaminated radial forearm free flap (PRFFF) was developed, which is gained by a two‐step surgical procedure. The flap is prelaminated by incorporating either a thin layer of buccal mucosa or a split‐thickness skin graft to achieve epithelialization on both sides [6]. The PRFFF has been described in several case reports and case studies in oral and maxillofacial surgery and nasal reconstruction [7, 8]. Previous studies have shown that free flaps such as RFFF are a favorable option for pharynx reconstruction with moderate complication rates. However, patients with recurrent disease and functional deficits after sufficient therapy showed dramatically decreased survival rates after flap surgery [9]. However, there are still investigations needed to fully understand the benefits and limitations of PRFFFs for oropharyngeal reconstruction. Therefore, we analyzed the perioperative course and functional outcome of patients with a PRFFF in our case series.

Materials and Methods

Patient Selection

To identify all patients who underwent a PRFFF surgery in our clinics between 2012 and 2025, we conducted a search for patient cases with a specific procedure coding number during that period. Because no specific code exists for the PRFFF, we used the general procedure code for a free radial forearm flap. Once we identified cases with the procedure coding number, we further examined the surgical protocols to confirm the patients who indeed received a PRFFF. If the tumor primarily infiltrated the soft palate and resection of the whole soft palate was necessary, a PRFFF was indicated. The perioperative course, specifically the surgery durations, time to decannulation and length of hospital stay were investigated. Postoperative complications and the functional outcome were recorded by analyzing patient files and operation protocols.

The study protocol was in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The institutional ethics board of the TUM School of Medicine and Health, Department of Clinical Medicine (Ethics committee file number: 2023‐109‐S‐SR; 21.02.2023) reviewed and approved the protocol. Patient consent was waived due to the fact that no patients were identified.

Surgical Procedure

The operation was conducted in two stages. During the first surgical procedure, the following steps were carried out: transoral resection of the oropharyngeal tumor, temporary tracheostomy, and prelamination of the radial forearm flap. Whenever possible, the non‐dominant arm was chosen for flap harvesting. Before surgery, an Allen test was performed to assess blood flow in the ulnar and radial arteries.

The radial forearm flap was partially raised beginning from the ulnar side. The incision was extended distally and radially. The radial artery, radial venae comitantes, and cephalic vein were distally ligated. The vascular pedicle was carefully dissected between the tendons of the brachioradialis and flexor carpi radialis muscles up to the proximal border of the flap, but no incision was made proximally (Figure 1A). A piece of split‐thickness skin graft, matching the size of the arm flap, was harvested from the thigh using an electric‐powered dermatome with a thickness of 0.4 mm (Figure 1A). The split‐thickness skin was sutured to the back of the partially raised forearm flap using absorbable single button sutures (Figure 1B). Finally, the entire flap was repositioned back into the wound bed, and the three open sides were closed using single button sutures (Figures 1B and 2). A radiofrequency needle was utilized for tumor resection and a temporary tracheostomy was performed.

The interval between the two stages typically spanned approximately 8–14 days, allowing for the successful engraftment of the split‐thickness skin onto the partially raised RFFF.

In the second step, the following procedures were carried out: neck dissection, total raising of the prelaminated radial forearm flap (Figure 1C), reconstruction of the soft palate, microvascular anastomosis, and closure of the donor site with a full‐thickness skin graft of the groin. An advantage of the two‐step approach is that in case of R1 resection or close resection margins, a re‐resection can be performed in the second surgical step. The pedicle of the radial artery was dissected up to its origin from the brachial artery. After removing the prelaminated flap, the donor site was closed with a full‐thickness skin graft from the groin. For reconstruction, the wound edges in the soft palate area were freshened. The prelaminated radial flap was prepared by partially deepithelializing the posterior surface in an arch shape. Subsequently, the anterior surface of the radial flap was sutured to reconstruct the soft palate. The arterial anastomosis was performed between a branch of the external carotid artery (most commonly the superior thyroid or the facial artery) and the radial artery. We performed two venous anastomoses (when harvesting the venous confluence) and three venous anastomoses (when the two radial venae comitantes were harvested separately) using a coupler system (TapMed, Germany).

Perioperative Management

After the first surgical step, most of the patients stayed at the hospital until the second step. Three patients were discharged and readmitted for the second surgery. After tumor resection nutrition was administered through a nasogastric feeding tube. Enteral feeding was continued after the second step for another 10 days. Subsequently, an oral diet was initiated with the assistance of our speech‐language pathologists.

Perioperative thrombosis prophylaxis was carried out in two ways. First, through the subcutaneous administration of 5000 IU unfractionated heparin intraoperatively following the completion of arterial and venous anastomoses. Second, through the postoperative administration of a weight‐adapted dose of low‐molecular‐weight heparin (enoxaparin).

Results

A total of 11 patients were enrolled (Table 1). In 10 out of 11 cases histology showed squamous cell carcinoma; one case showed an adenoid cystic carcinoma. In only five out of ten cases was p16 status determined since the examination of p16 status was not standard practice for oropharyngeal carcinomas back then. Just one of five cases showed p16 positivity. In two patients, the final pathology after tumor resection in the first step showed microscopic residual tumor (R1 resection). In these cases, a further resection of the soft palate during the second operation was performed to achieve an R0 resection. Table 2 shows the surgical characteristics. The mean surgery duration of the first operation was 173.2 ± 39.0 min; the mean surgery duration of the second operation including the microvascular anastomosis was 451.9 ± 116.0 min. The mean duration of the interval between the two surgeries was 13.0 ± 4.6 days. Three patients were discharged home and subsequently readmitted for the second step, while the remaining cases remained in the hospital during this interval. The mean time to decannulation after tracheostomy was 26.5 ± 12.1 days. One patient developed a non‐ST‐elevation myocardial infarction and a central pulmonary embolism 2 days after the second surgery. The patient underwent cardiac catheterization with stenting. Afterwards a triple antithrombotic therapy was initiated. Here it should be mentioned that this patient was already preoperatively cleared by anesthesia with increased cardiovascular risk due to known atrial fibrillation, arterial hypertension, type II diabetes mellitus, and coronary heart disease. In one case postoperative arterial hemorrhage occurred 11 days after the first operation. The bleeding was managed by bipolar coagulation of a small artery through a transcervical approach. Another patient was readmitted to the hospital due to a wound bed infection of the arm. The infection was treated with intravenous antibiotic therapy and subsequently improved. In two cases, there was a wound dehiscence of the reconstructed soft palate. In one case, healing occurred through granulation, while in the other case, the prelaminated flap had to be readjusted under full anesthesia. There was one case of prolonged wound healing at the donor site, but no case of total flap loss.

In the examination of velopharyngeal insufficiency, data were available for only seven patients. Among them, four out of seven patients still exhibited nasal speech. Fortunately, none of the patients reported nasal regurgitation of food. In four out of seven cases, occasional nasal regurgitation of liquid occurred in rare instances during rapid drinking (Table 3).

Discussion

Malignant tumors of the soft palate represent a subset of oropharyngeal cancers with relatively low incidence. However, they present a notable challenge for head and neck surgeons due to the essential role of the soft palate in phonation and swallowing. The primary curative approach is surgical therapy, yet this choice can be challenging due to functional and aesthetic compromise after tumor resection. While there are many studies about the RFFF, little is known about the PRFFF. The RFFF has proved to be a reliable flap, because it shows a predictable vascular anatomy, good pedicle length and almost hairless and pliable skin for reconstruction. Due to its bulkiness and the associated donor site morbidity, alternative techniques were developed. The PRFFF was first described by Costa et al. in 1993, where it was referred to as the “prefabricated flap” and used for reconstruction of the nose, lip, and ear [10]. Subsequently, the term “prelaminated flap” was introduced by Pribaz et al. in 1994, describing a process in which tissues are implanted into a vascular flap prior to its transfer [11]. For prelamination, buccal mucosa or split‐thickness skin graft can be used. Notably, fasciomucosal flaps are thinner than fasciocutaneous flaps and have the advantage of preserving the physiology of the oral mucosa, allowing saliva production [12]. The buccal mucosa is usually harvested from the cheek contralateral to the tumor's location [13]. However, the mucosa may not spread evenly, potentially leading to flap shrinkage. There also remains the concern of using mucosa with premalignant potential from the oral cavity for reconstruction, although no such cases have been documented in the literature. Poeschl et al. compared the RFFF with the PRFFF in the reconstruction of the oral cavity. The authors reported a shrinkage rate of 6% versus 87.5% in the fasciocutaneous versus the fasciomucosal group [14]. In another study, the patients who received a PRFFF showed slightly better functional outcomes, accompanied by more favorable cosmetic results at the donor site compared to the RFFF group [15]. Both groups observed a lower donor site morbidity because the primary closure of the donor site was possible for the fasciomucosal flap in most cases. Nevertheless, our study's PRFFF distinguishes itself from the prelaminated fasciomucosal flaps examined in those prior investigations. Unlike prior investigations focusing on prelaminated fasciomucosal flaps, our PRFFF involves a bilaterally epithelialized fasciocutaneous flap with a posterior split‐thickness skin graft. We believe that this method represents the best option for soft palate reconstruction. Our prelaminated flap is bilaterally epithelialized, mimicking the structure of the physiological soft palate. A big advantage of the PRFFF is its ability to resist shrinking, which helps support normal physiological functions. The double‐epithelialization prevents the back of the flap from adhering to the posterior pharyngeal wall, while allowing the flap to move freely, similar to the native soft palate. Because the skin of the forearm was also used for the flap, primary closure was not possible and donor site closure was achieved using a full‐thickness skin graft from the groin. Except for a single case of postsurgical wound infection of the forearm, there was no early donor site morbidity in our case series. The functional outcome of swallowing after reconstruction with an RFFF showed mixed results in the literature. Lahtinen et al. reported, that more than 60% of patients showed significantly impaired swallowing‐related quality of life 2 years after free flap surgery [16]. But this investigation also included other free flaps, such as anterolateral thigh flaps (ALT) and fibula free flaps, with the RFFF being the most used free flap. In a small case series of eight patients the swallowing following radial forearm flap reconstruction of major soft palate defects was examined [17]. Similar to our results, two of the patients required supplementation with percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) feeding and the general swallowing outcome was poor after reconstruction of the soft palate with RFFF. In our study, seven out of ten patients were able to return to their normal diet during the follow‐up period. Furthermore, the postoperative complications, flap survival rate and donor site morbidity were comparable to the outcomes observed with RFFF. There are also other flaps for reconstructing the soft palate. Hamahata et al. compared different surgical techniques using the ALT flap in the reconstruction of large soft palate defects. Among the 22 ALT flaps, no flap loss was observed. It appeared that, for a favorable functional outcome, the pharyngeal isthmus should be reconstructed as a tunnel structure [18]. The appearance of nasal regurgitation was comparable to our study. Another method is reconstruction via a jejunal flap. In a case series of 12 patients undergoing free jejunum flap reconstruction of the soft palate, only a third reported nasal regurgitation. Similar to our results, this did not impede the consumption of a solid meal [19]. However, the use of a jejunal flap requires a two‐cavity procedure, involving both the head‐and‐neck and abdominal regions, which increases operative time, surgical trauma, and the risk of donor‐site morbidity.

A two‐step surgical approach presents both advantages and disadvantages. Tumor resection followed by microvascular free tissue transfer for head and neck reconstruction can result in prolonged operative duration, which is associated with an increased risk of perioperative complications [20]. Research has indicated that two‐stage surgeries entail longer overall operative times and extended hospital stays in comparison to single‐stage procedures [21, 22]. However, each individual surgical step within the two‐stage process was significantly shorter than the overall surgical time of a single‐stage approach, allowing regular working hours. This facilitates a reduction in surgeon fatigue, ultimately contributing to better job satisfaction [21]. Furthermore, the two‐stage approach allows additional resection if the definitive histopathology reveals positive resection margins. Shorter surgery time also reduces perioperative complications which is reflected in the outcome of our 10 patients. Of note, one patient had perioperative myocardial infarction and pulmonary embolism; however this patient already had a medical history for cardiac risk. In all other patients, the two‐step approach could have a positive impact on cardiovascular complications; however due to the small sample size larger cohorts would be needed to confirm this.

This study has several limitations. As a retrospective analysis, it may be subject to selection and information biases. Additionally, due to its small sample size, it is challenging to generalize our findings. Nevertheless, it is worth noting that the sample size is small because oropharynx carcinomas limited to the soft palate represent a rare tumor localization, as seen in other studies as well.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this is, so far the only case series that has investigated the postoperative outcome of a bilateral epithelialized PRFFF. It demonstrates that this unique method is a promising alternative method for soft palate reconstruction after tumor resection. This configuration may better approximate the physiological structure of the soft palate compared to a unilaterally epithelialized RFFF. For further studies, the postoperative swallowing function should include additional measurements, such as fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing or barium swallow tests. Understanding the mobility of the neo‐soft palate can help improve the quality of life for patients undergoing complex head and neck reconstructions.
