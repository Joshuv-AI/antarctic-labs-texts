Synergistic anticancer effects of peanut hairy root culture crude extract in combination with gemcitabine against cholangiocarcinoma
Authors: Warissara Wongkham, Piyathida Wongkham, Apinun Limmongkon, Worasak Kaewkong, Sarawut Kumphune, Suchada Phimsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) is a highly aggressive malignancy with limited treatment options and frequent chemoresistance. Peanut hairy root culture crude extract (PCE) has previously demonstrated selective cytotoxicity toward CCA cells. Here, we investigated the combinatorial effects of PCE with standard chemotherapeutic agents on CCA cell lines. In KKU-213 A cells, PCE exhibited antagonistic and additive interactions with gemcitabine (Gem) and 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), respectively. In contrast, both combinations showed synergistic effects in KKU-100 cells, with the strongest synergy observed for PCE and Gem. Further investigation in KKU-100 cells revealed that the PCE-Gem combination significantly induced apoptosis, as evidenced by increased levels of cleaved caspase-8, caspase-3, and PARP, without affecting cell cycle progression. Notably, no cytotoxic effect was observed in normal human fibroblasts (HSF-hTERT). In a nude mouse xenograft model, co-treatment with PCE and Gem significantly inhibited tumor growth and reduced Ki-67 expression compared to monotherapies. These findings suggest that PCE enhances the anticancer efficacy of Gem in CCA and may serve as a supportive combinatorial strategy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48728-4.

Introduction

Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) is an aggressive malignancy originating from the bile duct epithelium, with rising global incidence and a particularly high prevalence in northeastern Thailand, where the rate exceeds 85 cases per 100,000 individuals1. This regional burden is strongly associated with chronic infection with the liver fluke Opisthorchis viverrini, transmitted via the consumption of raw or undercooked freshwater fish. The parasite’s metacercaria stage induces chronic inflammation of the bile ducts, a condition strongly linked to CCA development2. Additionally, demographic studies reveal that the incidence in males is significantly higher than in females (87.7 vs. 36.3 per 100,000, respectively)3. Due to the asymptomatic nature of early-stage CCA, most patients are diagnosed when the disease has progressed to the advanced stages, thereby limiting treatment options. While curative resection is feasible in only approximately 35% of cases4, chemotherapy remains a principal approach for the majority of patients, particularly when surgical is not possible. Common chemotherapeutic agents such as gemcitabine (Gem), cisplatin (CIS), and 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) are widely used; however, their clinical efficiency is hampered by systemic toxicity and the development of chemoresistance5. This underscores an urgent need for novel therapies that can effectively enhance treatment outcomes with reduced adverse effects.

Stilbenoids, a class of plant-derived polyphenols, exhibit wide range of biological activities such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer effects. Their derivatives include trans-resveratrol, trans-arachidin-1, trans-arachidin-3, pterostilbene, piceatannol, and oxyresveratrol. Among these, trans-resveratrol is the most extensively investigated and has been shown to induce apoptosis and inhibit metastasis across various cancer types by upregulating pro-apoptotic proteins (e.g., PUMA, BAX, NOXA, caspases, and cleaved PARP) and downregulating anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-26–10. Interestingly, several studies have demonstrated that trans-resveratrol can enhance the efficacy of chemotherapy by reducing cancer cell viability, inducing cell cycle arrest, decreasing colony formation, promoting apoptosis through the upregulation of BAX, caspase-9, and PARP, and inhibiting cell migration11–13. However, its clinical application of trans-resveratrol is limited by its low bioavailability due to rapid phase II metabolism14. In contrast, arachidin, a group of prenylated stilbenoids predominantly present in peanuts, demonstrates enhanced lipophilicity and bioavailability. Notably, trans-arachidins such as trans-arachidin-1 exhibit superior anticancer properties, including lower effective concentrations in cancer cell models and synergistic efficacy when combined with chemotherapeutic agents14–18.

Peanut hairy root cultures generated via Agrobacterium rhizogenes transformation, provide a sustainable system for the production of stilbenoids including trans-resveratrol, trans-arachidin-1, and trans-arachidin-319. The application of elicitors such as chitosan (CHT), methyl jasmonate (MeJA), and cyclodextrin (CD) has been reported to further enhance the accumulation of these bioactive compounds20. The crude extract obtained from peanut hairy root culture (PCE) has shown promising antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer activities, notably by inducing apoptosis via upregulating cleaved-PARP expression, inhibiting cell migration and invasion, and reducing cell viability in CCA models19–21.

Despite these promising preclinical findings, the potential of PCE in combination with standard chemotherapeutic agents remain unexplored. In this study, we investigated the synergistic effects of PCE combined with conventional chemotherapy in CCA cell lines and in a xenograft mouse model. We further elucidated the underlying mechanisms of apoptotic induction and evaluated the selectivity of this combination treatment towards cancer cells. Our results aim to offer novel insights into the potential therapeutic application of PCE as an adjuvant to enhance chemotherapy efficacy for the treatment of CCA.

Results

Determination of peanut hairy root culture crude extract (PCE)

The PCE was obtained from a single production batch to ensure consistency across all experiments. The chemical profiles of the major prenylated stilbenoids, trans-arachidin-1 and trans-arachidin-3, were characterized by HPLC at a detection wavelength of 340 nm. Representative chromatograms and full datasets from three independent experiments are provided in Supplementary Figure S1, demonstrating consistent peak profiles across replicates. Quantitative analysis revealed that trans-arachidin-1 and trans-arachidin-3 were present at 15.29 ± 3.50 and 16.76 ± 5.20 µmol/g crude extract, respectively.

Antiproliferative effects of PCE, Gem, and 5-FU in CCA cell lines and normal fibroblasts

The effects of PCE, Gem, and 5-FU at various concentrations were evaluated in KKU-213 A, KKU-100, and HSF-hTERT cells following 48 h of treatment. PCE significantly reduced the viability of both CCA cell lines in a dose-dependent manner. In contrast, HSF-hTERT cells exhibited no marked reduction in viability at concentrations ranging from 0 to 300 µg/mL, with only a slight decrease observed at higher concentrations (400–600 µg/mL) (Fig. 1a). These findings suggest that PCE exhibits greater cytotoxic effects in CCA cells compared to fibroblasts under the tested experimental conditions.

Antiproliferative effects of the combination treatments of PCE with Gem or 5-FU in CCA cell lines

The combination effects of PCE with Gem or 5-FU were evaluated in CCA cell lines using concentrations ranging from non-toxic levels to approximately 50% inhibitory concentrations for each compound. In KKU-213 A cells, co-treatment with PCE and Gem for 48 h did not enhance antiproliferative effects compared to either agent alone (Fig. 2a). However, the combination of PCE with 5-FU significantly increased growth inhibition compared to single agent treatment (Fig. 2b). In KKU-100 cells, both PCE + Gem and PCE + 5-FU combinations significantly enhanced the inhibition of cell proliferation compared to either PCE or chemotherapeutic agent alone (Figs. 2c, d).

Combination index analysis of PCE with Gem and 5-FU in CCA cell lines

Following the evaluation of antiproliferative effects, combination interactions were assessed using SynergyFinder (version 3.0) based on the ZIP (Zero Interaction Potency) model. Synergy scores were interpreted as follows: values > 10 indicated synergistic interactions, − 10 to 10 indicated additive effects, and < − 10 indicated antagonism. In KKU-213 A cells, the combination of PCE with Gem yielded a synergy scores of −11.112, indicating an antagonistic effect, whereas the combination with 5-FU produced a score 1.413, suggesting an additive effect respectively (Fig. 3a, b). In contrast, KKU-100 cells demonstrated strong synergistic interactions for both combinations, with synergy scores of 23.171 for PCE + Gem and 15.281 for PCE + 5-FU (Fig. 3c, d), with the highest synergy observed for the PCE + Gem combination.

To further confirm synergistic interactions and to identify optimal concentration pairs for downstream mechanistic experiments, Combination Index (CI) values were calculated using SynergyFinder Plus based on the Chou–Talalay framework, where CI < 1 indicates synergy and CI > 1 indicates antagonism. In KKU-100 cells, combinations of PCE at 200, 250, and 300 µg/mL with gemcitabine at 0.19 µM yielded the lowest CI values, indicating the strongest synergistic interactions (Table 2). These concentration pairs were therefore selected for subsequent in vitro experiments.

Effect of PCE in combination with Gem on cell cycle distribution and apoptosis induction

To investigate the mechanism underlying the synergistic antiproliferative effect of PCE and Gem, we examined cell cycle distribution and apoptosis induction in KKU-100 cells after 48 h of treatment. This combination was selected based on its strongest synergistic interaction observed in earlier experiments.

As shown in Fig. 4a–d, treatment with PCE, Gem, or their combination did not cause significant changes in the distribution of cells in the G0/G1, S, or G2/M phases compared to the untreated control. These results suggest that the observed reduction in cell viability is not mediated through cell cycle arrest.

To further explore the mode of cell death, apoptosis was evaluated in both KKU-100 and HSF-hTERT cells using annexin V-FITC and 7-AAD staining. In KKU-100 cells, the combination of PCE (200 µg/mL) with Gem did not significantly increase early apoptosis, late apoptosis, or total cell death compared to single-agent treatments. However, at a concentration of 250 µg/mL, the combination showed a trend toward increased late apoptosis and significantly elevated total cell death. At 300 µg/mL of PCE, the combination significantly increased both late apoptosis and total cell death, while early apoptosis remained unchanged (Fig. 4e–h). These results indicate that PCE enhances Gem-induced apoptosis in a dose-dependent manner in KKU-100 cells.

In contrast, the combination treatment did not increase apoptosis in HSF-hTERT cells. Interestingly, co-treatment with PCE and Gem resulting in lower apoptosis compared to Gem alone, indicating differential cellular responses under the tested experimental conditions (Fig. 4i–l).

Effect of PCE combined with Gem on apoptosis-related protein expression

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying apoptosis induced by the combination treatment of PCE and Gem, the expression levels of key apoptosis-related proteins were analyzed by western blotting in KKU-100 cells. Proteins examined included cleaved caspase-8, cleaved caspase-9, cleaved caspase-3, and cleaved PARP (Fig. 5a).

The results showed that neither single nor combination treatments induced activation of cleaved caspase-9. However, the relative expression levels of pro-caspase-9 was significantly reduced in cells treated with PCE at 300 µg/mL and in the combination group, compared to Gem treatment alone (Fig. 5b). Expression of cleaved caspase-8 was significantly increased in cells treated with PCE at 250 and 300 µg/mL combined with Gem, relative to both control and single-agent treatments (Fig. 5c). Furthermore, the expression levels of cleaved caspase-3 and cleaved PARP were markedly elevated in cells treated with PCE at 300 µg/mL and were significantly higher in the combination group than in single-agent groups (Fig. 5d–e).

These findings suggest that the combination treatment of PCE and Gem is associated with activation of apoptotic signaling, consistent with involvement of extrinsic apoptotic markers, as reflected by increased levels of cleaved caspase-8 and downstream apoptotic proteins in KKU-100 cells.

Antitumor effects of PCE and Gem in a CCA xenograft mouse model

Sixteen days after KKU-100 cell implantation, when the tumor volumes reached approximately 100 mm³, mice were randomly assigned to receive intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections every other day for 7 days with either vehicle control (DMSO: PBS, 1:9), Gem (100 mg/kg), PCE (625 mg/kg), or a combination of both agents (Fig. 6a). Tumor length and width were measured at each time point using a digital vernier caliper (Supplementary Table S1).

At the end of the treatment period, mice were sacrificed, and tumors were excised and photographed (Fig. 6b). As shown in Fig. 6c, treatment with Gem, PCE, and their combination significantly reduced tumor volume after 6–7 days compared to the vehicle control group. Notably, the combination treatment resulted in a greater reduction in tumor volume than PCE alone, although the difference compared to Gem alone was not statistically significant. Treatment with either Gem or PCE led to a reduction in tumor weight, with tumor growth inhibition (TGI) calculated at 59.94 ± 13.42% for Gem and 35.86 ± 19.71% for PCE. Importantly, combination treatment with Gem and PCE resulted in a markedly enhanced TGI of 88.79 ± 4.91% (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Table S2). Although this did not reach statistical significance in terms of tumor volume compared to Gem alone, these findings suggest that PCE potentiates the antitumor activity of Gem in vivo, providing an improved inhibitory response when used in combination.

To further investigate the mechanism of tumor suppression, both histological and immunohistochemical analyses were conducted. Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining revealed distinct patterns of tumor cell damage among treatment groups. In Gem-treated and combination groups, tumors exhibited extensive necrotic areas, characterized by loss of cellular architecture and cytoplasmic eosinophilia, indicative of non-specific drug-induced cell death. In contrast, tumors from the PCE-treated group showed evidence of nuclear condensation and fragmentation, hallmarks of apoptosis, without widespread necrosis (Fig. 7a).

Immunohistochemical staining for the proliferation marker Ki-67 demonstrated a marked reduction in proliferative activity across all treatment groups when compared to the control. The most pronounced decrease in Ki-67-positive cells was observed in the combination group, suggesting strong antiproliferative effects (Fig. 7b, d). Furthermore, cleaved caspase-3, a key marker of apoptosis, was most abundantly expressed in tumors from the PCE group, with fewer positive cells seen in the Gem and combination groups (Fig. 7c, e). This suggests that while Gem and the combination may suppress tumors through necrotic pathways or reduced proliferation, PCE promotes tumor suppression predominantly through apoptotic mechanisms.

Discussion

Current chemotherapy treatments for CCA are limited by adverse side effects and the frequent emergence of chemoresistance. Consequently, the use of plant-derived bioactive compounds in combination with conventional chemotherapeutics has gained increasing attention as a strategy to improve therapeutic efficacy and reduce toxicity. Previous studies have demonstrated that PCE exerts cytotoxic effects against CCA cells by inducing apoptosis and suppressing cell migration and invasion21. In the present study, we investigated the combinatorial effects of PCE and gemcitabine in human cholangiocarcinoma cell lines and an in vivo xenograft model. Although both CCA cell lines exhibited antiproliferative responses, mechanistic investigations were focused on KKU-100 cells because this model demonstrated consistent synergistic interactions with the PCE–gemcitabine combination. Notably, the combination of PCE and gemcitabine exhibited antagonistic interactions in KKU-213 A cells, highlighting the cell line–dependent nature of the combination response. In contrast, strong synergistic effects were consistently observed in KKU-100 cells, which therefore served as the primary model for subsequent mechanistic investigations. Based on these findings, mechanistic and in vivo studies were focused on the KKU-100 model to elucidate the molecular basis underlying the enhanced anticancer effects of the combination treatment.

Our results showed that PCE significantly reduced cell viability in a dose-dependent manner in both KKU-213 A and KKU-100 cells, while exhibiting minimal cytotoxicity in HSF-hTERT cells. These findings indicate differential cellular responses between CCA cells and fibroblasts under the tested conditions, consistent with previous reports showing that purified trans-arachidin-1, a major stilbenoid in PCE, selectively reduced viability in breast cancer cells without affecting normal breast epithelial cells17. The use of fibroblasts as a general representative of normal cells aligns with previous studies in oral cancer model22. Additionally, we found that KKU-100 cells were more sensitive to Gem, while KKU-213 A cells were more responded more effectively to 5-FU, corroborating previous studies comparing sensitivity among different CCA cell lines23,24.

Combination analysis revealed that PCE + Gem exhibited an antagonistic effect in KKU-213 A cells but a strong synergistic effect in KKU-100 cells. The combination of PCE with 5-FU resulted in an additive effect in KKU-213 A and a synergistic effect in KKU-100. These data indicate that both the chemotherapeutic agent and the intrinsic characteristics of the cell line contribute to the efficacy of combination therapy. Differences in drug accumulation mechanisms between cell types may explain these variations in sensitivity25. Thus, PCE appears to be more effective at overcoming drug resistance in KKU-100 cells than in KKU-213 A cells. These findings provided the rationale for focusing subsequent mechanistic analyses on the KKU-100 cell line.

Interestingly, previous studies have shown that trans-arachidin-1 and trans-arachidin-3, major constituents in peanut hairy root extract, can bind to cannabinoid receptors (CB1 and CB2), which are often overexpressed in cancer cells and have been proposed as therapeutic targets in non-small cell lung cancer14,26. It is plausible that higher expression of these receptors in KKU-100 cells may underlie the enhanced sensitivity to PCE, though further investigation is needed to confirm this hypothesis.

To further explore the underlying mechanisms, we evaluated cell cycle distribution and apoptosis. No significant changes were observed in the proportions of cells in the G0/G1, S, or G2/M phases following treatment, indicating that growth inhibition was not associated with cell cycle arrest. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that gemcitabine, in combination with plant-derived compounds, does not markedly alter cell cycle phase distribution in KKU-100 cells but increases sub-G1 populations, indicative of apoptotic cell death23. Flow cytometric analysis further demonstrated that the combination of PCE and gemcitabine significantly increased late-stage apoptosis in KKU-100 cells compared to either monotherapy. In contrast, no significant apoptotic induction was observed in HSF-hTERT cells under the tested experimental conditions, indicating differential cellular responses. Such differences may reflect variations in basal cellular stress levels and apoptotic thresholds between malignant and non-malignant cells.

In the present study, fibroblasts were used as a non-cancer control cell type. While they provide preliminary insight into differential cytotoxic responses, they do not fully represent normal biliary epithelial cells. Therefore, conclusions regarding tumor selectivity should be interpreted within the context of the tested experimental conditions, and further validation using appropriate normal cholangiocyte models will be necessary to confirm cell-type specificity. Collectively, these findings align with previous studies reporting that resveratrol enhances the efficacy of chemotherapeutic agents across various cancer types, including pancreatic, colorectal, breast, and gastric cancers11–13,27–29. Similarly, arachidin-1 has been shown to enhance paclitaxel-induced apoptosis in breast cancer cells without affecting normal breast epithelial cells18, supporting the concept that stilbenoid-derived compounds may differentially modulate chemotherapeutic responses depending on cellular context.

Western blot analysis showed increased levels of cleaved caspase-8, caspase-3, and PARP, while cleaved caspase-9 was not markedly elevated under the tested conditions. These findings are consistent with activation of apoptotic signaling involving extrinsic apoptotic markers. Biochemical activation of caspase-3 does not necessarily correlate with the proportion of early apoptotic cells at a single time point, as annexin V positivity represents a transient stage of apoptosis. At the 48-hour time point examined in this study, apoptotic cells may have already progressed toward late apoptosis or secondary necrosis, thereby limiting the detectable early apoptotic population. Similar synergistic mechanisms have been reported for other stilbenoids. For instance, piceatannol has been shown to enhance the efficacy of Gem by upregulating pro-apoptotic proteins such as Bak30. Other stilbenes, including trans-resveratrol and trans-arachidin-1, have been reported to synergize with chemotherapeutic agents like doxorubicin, cisplatin, 5-FU, and paclitaxel via modulation of pro- and anti-apoptotic proteins11,13,18,28. In the present study, the observed increase in cleaved caspase-8 suggests involvement of extrinsic apoptotic signaling in the combinatorial response. However, definitive pathway confirmation would require functional inhibition or gene silencing approaches to directly delineate pathway dependency.

In vitro experiments: a low concentration of Gem (0.19 µM) was used to limit chemotherapy-induced toxicity to approximately 10%. The combination with PCE was applied at non-toxic concentrations, corresponding to approximately 50% inhibitory concentrations, with the intention of enhancing the apoptotic effect of chemotherapy. However, the in vivo results in the KKU-100 xenograft model revealed a different outcome, likely due to the higher dose of Gem (100 mg/kg) used, which may have masked the effects of PCE.

To further examine the in vivo relevance of our in vitro observations, we employed a KKU-100 xenograft model in nude mice. Combination treatment resulted in a greater reduction in tumor volume and higher tumor growth inhibition (TGI) than either agent alone. H&E staining revealed both apoptosis and necrosis features in tumors from the Gem-treated group, with more pronounced necrosis observed in the combination group, whereas PCE-treated tumors showed classical apoptotic features, such as nuclear condensation.

Interestingly, tumors treated with PCE alone exhibited elevated cleaved caspase-3 alongside relatively high Ki-67 expression, suggesting a high-turnover tumor state. This phenomenon may be explained by caspase-3–dependent compensatory proliferation, also referred to as the “Phoenix Rising” effect, in which apoptotic cells stimulate proliferation of neighboring viable cells as a survival response31,32. In contrast, the combination treatment markedly suppressed Ki-67 expression, indicating that gemcitabine may attenuate this compensatory proliferative signaling, thereby shifting the tumor response toward sustained growth inhibition.

Although cleaved caspase-3 expression was highest in the PCE-treated tumors, the combination treatment resulted in greater tumor growth inhibition and more pronounced suppression of Ki-67 expression, indicating that enhanced in vivo antitumor efficacy is not solely dependent on apoptotic signaling.

The discrepancy between in vitro and in vivo apoptotic markers may reflect differences in temporal dynamics and tissue heterogeneity. Immunohistochemical detection of cleaved caspase-3 represents a single time-point snapshot and may underestimate earlier apoptotic events, particularly in tumors with extensive necrosis. Collectively, these findings suggest that the enhanced antitumor effect observed in vivo is likely mediated by a combination of apoptosis, proliferation suppression, and non-apoptotic cell death mechanisms.

Both apoptosis and necroptosis can be triggered by overlapping stress signals such as oxidative stress, calcium dysregulation, and ATP depletion, and the type and intensity of these stimuli determines the mode of cell death33. Under the current experimental conditions, the relatively high dose of Gem (100 mg/kg) may have contributed to extensive tumor necrosis and limited the detection of additional apoptotic signaling in the combination group, as observed by H&E staining of both tumors and vital organs (Supplementary Figure S3).

In particular, Gem-treated mice exhibited pathological changes in the liver and spleen. Although the PCE-treated group did not show signs of organ toxicity, the combination treatment did not significantly alleviate gemcitabine-associated toxicity at the administered dose. This finding highlights the non-toxic nature of PCE but also indicate that optimization of Gem dosing and treatment schedule may be required to fully elucidate the mechanistic contribution and therapeutic window of the combination strategy. Previous studies have shown that Gem at 100 mg/kg administered every three days can suppress tumor growth with limited systemic toxicity23.

A limitation of this study is that mechanistic and in vivo analyses were conducted using a single CCA cell line model. Given the observed antagonistic interaction in KKUA-213 A cells, future studies incorporating additional CCA models or patient-derived systems will be necessary to determine the broader applicability of this combination strategy.

In summary, the present study provides three key insights. First, the combination of PCE and gemcitabine demonstrated enhanced anticancer effects in CCA models compared to monotherapy under the tested experimental conditions. Second, the observed apoptotic responses were associated with activation of caspase-related signaling, consistent with involvement of extrinsic apoptotic markers. Third, differential cytotoxic responses between CCA cells and fibroblasts indicate context-dependent cellular effects rather than generalized tumor specificity.

Collectively, these findings suggest that PCE may enhance the therapeutic efficacy of gemcitabine in certain CCA models. The PCE–gemcitabine combination demonstrated synergistic interactions in vitro and reduced tumor burden in vivo. Although apoptotic marker patterns in vivo varied depending on treatment context, the overall antitumor effect indicates that multiple mechanisms—including modulation of apoptosis and suppression of proliferation—may contribute to the observed efficacy. Further studies focusing on mechanistic validation, dose optimization, and evaluation in additional preclinical models will be necessary to determine the broader translational potential of this combinatorial strategy.

Methods

Cell lines, culture conditions, and reagents

KKU-213 A (well-differentiated CCA) and KKU-100 (poorly differentiated CCA) cell lines were obtained from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources (JCRB) Cell Bank (Osaka, Japan). The human fibroblast cell lines HSF-hTERT were kindly provided by Assoc. Prof. Parinya Noisa (Suranaree University of Technology, Thailand). KKU-213 A and HSF-hTERT cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) and KKU-100 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA), both supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/mL and 100 µg/mL, respectively). All cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Gem and 5-FU were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and Merck KGaA (Germany), respectively.

Preparation of PCE

Peanut hairy root cultures were elicited with a combination of CHT, MeJA, and CD (CHT+MeJA + CD) for 72 h (h), as previously described20. The culture medium was extracted with ethyl acetate, and concentrated using a rotary evaporator at 40 °C. The resulting peanut hairy root culture crude extract (PCE) was derived from a single production batch to ensure experimental consistency throughout the study. The chemical profile and relative abundance of major prenylated stilbenoids, including trans-arachidin-1 and trans-arachidin-3, have been previously characterized using identical cultivation, elicitation, extraction, and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analytical procedures20,34. In the present study, HPLC analysis was performed at a detection wavelength of 340 nm to confirm the identity and consistency of these major constituents. The analysis was conducted in three independent runs, and consistent chromatographic profiles were obtained across all replicates. The extract was stored at − 20 °C until use to preserve the chemical integrity and bioactivity of the stilbenoid-rich constituents, as described in our previous report35. For in vitro experiments, PCE was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a stock solution at a concentration of 200 mg/mL and further diluted in culture medium to the indicated working concentrations.

Cell viability assay

The antiproliferative effect of PCE and chemotherapeutic agents were evaluated using 3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-Diphenyltetrazolium Bromide (MTT) assay. Cells (5 × 10³ cells/well) were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated for 24 h before treatment with vehicle control or increasing concentrations of PCE (0–600 µg/mL), Gem (0–12.5 µM), and 5-FU (0–400 µM) for 48 h. Combination treatments were also assessed in KKU-213 A and KKU-100 cells. Following treatment, MTT reagent (0.5 mg/mL) was added and incubated for 4 h. Formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO, and absorbance was measured at 540 nm. Cell viability was expressed as a percentage of control. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) values were calculated using GraphPad Prism (v9.0).

Synergy analysis

Drug interaction effects were evaluated using SynergyFinder (version 3.0) based on the ZIP (Zero Interaction Potency) model, where synergy scores > 10 indicate synergistic interactions, − 10 to 10 indicate additive effects, and < − 10 indicate antagonism. To further confirm synergistic interactions and identify optimal concentration pairs for downstream experiments, Combination Index (CI) values were calculated using SynergyFinder Plus according to the Chou–Talalay method, with CI < 1 indicating synergy.

Cell cycle analysis

KKU-100 cells (1.5 × 10⁵ cells/well) were seeded in 6-well plates for 24 h. Subsequently, cells were treated with PCE, chemotherapeutic agents, or their combination at synergistic concentrations for 48 h. After treatment, cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and fixed in 70% ethanol at −20 °C overnight. The following day, cells were washed again with PBS and stained using the Muse Cell Cycle Kit (Luminex, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA content was then analyzed by flow cytometry using Muse Cell Analyzer (Luminex, USA).

Apoptosis assay

Apoptosis was quantified using the Muse Annexin V & Dead Cell Kit (Luminex, USA). After 48 h treatment, cells were harvested, counted, and adjusted to a concentration of 6 × 10⁵ cells/mL. For staining, 6 × 10⁴ cells were resuspended in 100 µL of cell culture medium and mixed with 100 µL of Annexin V-7/AAD reagent for 20 min in the dark. Apoptotic populations were analyzed by flow cytometry using Muse Cell Analyzer (Luminex, USA).

Western blot analysis

Protein expression was evaluated by Western blotting. KKU-100 cells (5 × 10⁵ cells/dish) were seeded and incubated for 24 h. Cells were treated with PCE, chemotherapeutic agents or their combination at the synergistic concentration for 48 h. Cells were harvested and lysed with RIPA buffer (HiMedia, India) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (Cell Signaling Technology, USA). Protein concentrations were quantified by the Bradford assay and denatured using sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) mixed with beta-mercaptoethanol. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in TBS-T (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20) at room temperature for 1 h. The whole membrane was cut into small pieces according to their molecular weight of each protein and then probed with primary antibodies against the pro and cleaved forms of caspase-3, −8, −9, PARP and β-actin (all at 1:3,000; Cell Signaling Technology, USA) overnight at 4 °C. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies including anti-mouse and anti-rabbit (all 1:5000, Merck Millipore, USA) were then probed at room temperature for 1 h. After washing, protein band were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) and relative band intensity was measured using ImageJ by comparing the loading control was β-actin.

Xenograft tumor model

Male nude mice (BALB/cAJcl-nu), aged 4–6 weeks, were purchased from Nomura Siam International, Thailand. All animal procedures were approved by the Naresuan University Animal Care and Use Committee (NUACUC), under approval number NU-AE671012 (dated 20 September 2024).

All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. This study is reported in accordance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines.

Mice were housed and monitored under standard laboratory conditions at the Laboratory Animal Facility for Research, Naresuan University, Thailand. After acclimatization, 32 mice were injected subcutaneously into the right and left back with 1 × 106 KKU-100 cells by injecting suspended in serum-free medium mixed with Matrigel (1:1) (Corning, USA). Once tumors reached ~ 100 mm³, mice were randomized into four groups (n = 8): vehicle control, Gem (100 mg/kg), PCE (625 mg/kg), and Gem + PCE (100 + 625 mg/kg). Treatments were administered intraperitoneally every other day for 7 days. Tumor volumes were measured using digital calipers and calculated as (length × width²)/2. At the experimental endpoint, tumors were excised and weighed. The relative tumor volume (RTV) was calculated using the formula: RTV = (tumor volume on the measurement day/tumor volume on day 0). Tumor inhibition (%) = [(average tumor weight of control group - average tumor weight of treated group)/average tumor weight of control group] × 100.

Histological analysis

To evaluate histopathological alterations in tumors and vital organs, including the liver, kidney, and spleen, tissue specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature. After fixation, samples were processed and embedded in paraffin to generate tissue blocks. These blocks were sectioned into 5 μm-thick slices using a microtome and mounted onto glass slides. The sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a descending ethanol gradient (100% to 95%), followed by rinsing in distilled water. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining were then performed. After staining, the slides were dehydrated through an ascending ethanol gradient (95% to 100%), cleared in xylene (two changes, 5 min each), and mounted with a coverslip. Histological examination was conducted under a light microscope at 40x magnification to assess tissue morphology and identify any pathological changes.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tumor tissues from all treatment groups were processed for IHC to evaluate cell proliferation and apoptosis. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor Sect. (5 μm thick) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase activity and non-specific binding were blocked prior to incubation with primary antibodies against Ki-67 and cleaved caspase-3 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) overnight at 4 °C. Detection was performed using HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAB substrate, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Slides were examined under a light microscope at 40x magnification. Ki-67 positivity was defined by brown nuclear staining, while cleaved caspase-3 positivity was identified by brown staining in the nucleus and/or cytoplasm. Quantification of positive cells was carried out using ImageJ software, and the percentage of positive cells was calculated as: (Number of positive cells/Total number of counted cells) × 100%.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed independently in triplicate, and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. A p-value of less than 0.05 (p < 0.05) was considered statistically significant. GraphPad Prism (v9.0) was used for all analyses.
