A new hybrid block collocation method for solving elliptic PDEs
Authors: Mufutau Ajani Rufai, Salvatore Filippone, Higinio Ramos, Giovanni Modanese
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this article, we introduce a new hybrid block collocation method (NHBCM) using polynomial approximation for solving two-dimensional elliptic partial differential equations (PDEs). The theoretical analysis demonstrates that the NHBCM achieves fifth-order accuracy, highlighting its robustness in stability and convergence. We validate the effectiveness of the NHBCM through a series of test problems, showcasing its practical applications. The comparison results indicate that the NHBCM provides significantly more efficient solutions for both linear and nonlinear elliptic model PDEs than other numerical methods used for comparison, thereby validating the superior performance of the NHBCM.Keywords: Block colocation method, Power series, Numerical methods, Elliptic partial differential equations, Boundary value problemsSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In the fields of engineering and science, partial differential equations (PDEs) have extensive applications and significant potential for modeling natural and physical phenomena. These equations enable researchers to analyze changes that depend on multiple variables and their interrelationships. There are several types of PDEs; however, elliptic partial differential equations (EPDEs) are particularly significant due to their wide range of applications in fields such as fluid dynamics, mechanics, elasticity, electrostatics, and electromagnetism1,2. The EPDEs are powerful equations for modeling various physical phenomena. For instance, they are employed to describe steady-state heat conduction, as well as linear and nonlinear convection processes that characterize diverse transport mechanisms3. The Poisson equation, a cornerstone of potential theory, is particularly notable for its application in modeling electric and gravitational potentials, emphasizing the essential role of EPDEs in this domain4. Additionally, the fully nonlinear Navier–Stokes equations, which govern the dynamics of fluid motion, provide a comprehensive framework for analyzing the conservation of mass, momentum, and energy. These equations, which include elliptic components, further illustrate the significance of EPDEs in fluid dynamics and other fields5.

In particular, EPDEs play a crucial role in both engineering and physics as they model systems that reach a steady state, as well as a variety of real-world problems across many practical applications. For instance, in heat conduction, EPDEs can be used to analyze the temperature distribution in solid bodies at equilibrium, capturing systems where temperature gradients stabilize over time and space. In electrostatics, these equations are essential for calculating electric potential within specific domains defined by charge distributions, enabling a comprehensive analysis of electrostatic fields and forces. Additionally, EPDEs play a crucial role in fluid dynamics by determining pressure distributions in incompressible fluid flow systems, improving the understanding of liquid behavior in various engineering applications. Furthermore, in solid mechanics, elliptic PDEs are used to compute displacement fields in materials subjected to static loads, offering critical understanding into the mechanical response and deformability of structures under applied stress. This versatility demonstrates the use of EPDEs for modelling real-life problems in both engineering and applied sciences.

Numerical methods are commonly used to solve partial differential equations, including EPDE as in (1), due to their inherent complexity and the challenges of finding analytical solutions. As briefly discussed above, these equations frequently arise in real-world applications across various fields. Analytical solutions can be challenging or even impossible for many of these problems. Furthermore, numerical techniques provide flexibility in managing varying boundary conditions and can be used to solve high-dimensional problems, making them essential in modern scientific computing. For these reasons, many researchers frequently employ numerical methods to provide approximate solutions to differential problems efficiently.

The wavelet collocation method (WCM) is one of the numerical methods for solving PDEs, particularly elliptic PDEs. It uses wavelet bases to generate approximate solutions. Wavelets are localized functions that can represent data with various degrees, making them particularly suitable for numerical approximations. Two common types of wavelets in collocation methods are Haar wavelets, which are piecewise constant functions that are easy to implement, and Legendre wavelets, derived from Legendre polynomials and offering improved approximation characteristics. The ability of wavelets to operate at different scales enables them to capture essential solution features, facilitating good approximations for EPDEs.

The implementation of wavelet collocation methods involves several key steps. The problem domain is initially discretized into specific collocation points, where the solution is approximated as a linear combination of wavelet basis functions. Subsequently, the EPDE is transformed into a system of algebraic equations that incorporates the approximate solution at the collocation points, ensuring that appropriate boundary conditions are considered. Once formulated, this system can be solved using various numerical techniques such as matrix operations and iterative solvers. This results in a good solution highlighting the merits of wavelet collocation methods, such as good accuracy, flexibility in handling boundary conditions, adaptive resolution, and ease of implementation. Nonetheless, WCM has limitations, including high computational cost, difficulty in handling higher-dimensional problems, and sensitivity to the choice of wavelet basis. Further details can be found in6–12.

The Finite Difference Method (FDM) is another numerical approach to finding solutions to differential equations, which approximates derivatives using finite differences on a discretized domain. Its simplicity and straightforward implementation make it a popular choice for solving PDEs, especially on regular domains with uniform grids. It also offers flexibility, allowing enhancements to higher-order approximations that can improve solution accuracy.

However, FDM does have limitations. Accuracy is grid-dependent, often requiring finer grids, which increases computational cost. Moreover, implementing complex boundary conditions can be challenging, and time-dependent problems may suffer from stability issues if appropriate time-stepping methods are not used. Additional insights into FDM can be found in13–18.

The literature also describes several other numerical techniques and software libraries for solving different kinds of differential equations, including EPDEs. Some of the additional numerical methods include the finite element method, the Sinc-Galerkin method, regularization methods, matrix decomposition methods, multigrid methods, implicit meshless schemes, composite spectral collocation methods, optimized parametric methods, hybrid block methods, Nyström techniques, Galerkin methods, spline-based techniques, and other collocation techniques. These methods, some libraries, and their applications to EPDEs and related differential problems are discussed in detail in19–41. Most existing methods for solving elliptic PDEs often fail to converge, particularly with complex or nonlinear systems. They may also struggle with boundary conditions, have high computational costs, and encounter stability issues, which can limit their effectiveness on large or complex systems.

This research article introduces a new hybrid block collocation method (NHBCM) as an efficient numerical method for solving the elliptic PDEs, presented in (1). The NHBCM distinguishes itself from traditional numerical methods through its integration of block-based methods with the precision of collocation techniques. By leveraging the core strengths of block methods, such as the simultaneous generation of solutions at multiple nodes, enhanced numerical stability, and rapid convergence, the NHBCM achieves superior performance in both accuracy and computational time efficiency.

The key contributions of the NHBCM lie in its demonstrated ability to consistently yield accurate and reliable numerical solutions across all four test problems. The method’s convergence behavior has been analyzed, and numerical experiments confirm its robustness and accuracy when compared to several existing techniques. Unlike classical finite difference or finite element methods, which often require denser grids or more iterations for comparable accuracy, NHBCM achieves high-order convergence with fewer function evaluations, resulting in lower computational cost. This balance between accuracy and time efficiency highlights its significance and novelty, making it an efficient numerical method for researchers seeking to solve a class of elliptic PDEs in various scientific and engineering applications.

Conclusion

This article proposes a new hybrid block collocation method (NHBCM) to get an approximate solution to the elliptic partial differential equation (EPDE) in (1). The proposed NHBCM demonstrates better accuracy compared to all four existing methods used for comparison, as evidenced in the results presented in Tables 1, 2, 3, 4. For example in Table 1, for , the maximum of the NHBCM is , which is considerably lower than the maximum of for the same value of of the existing Block Unification Method (BUM) in46. Also, in Table 2, NHBCM achieves maximum absolute errors of for and continually improves to at , surpassing the errors of both the Wavelets Collocation Method (WCM) and Optimal Quadratic Spline Collocation Method (OQSCM) by multiple orders of magnitude. Similarly, Table 3 reveals that NHBCM outperforms WCM across various T values, achieving significantly smaller error metrics. The performance advantages continue in Table 4, where NHBCM consistently provides lower errors than the wavelet-based full-approximation method (WBFAM). These consistent results confirm that NHBCM is a reliable and efficient method for obtaining significantly lower maximum absolute errors, validating its potential as a new numerical method for solving EPDE in (1). In our future work, we plan to apply the modified block method to solve elliptic PDEs and other types of differential problems represented by time-dependent partial differential equations and Lane-Emden-Fowler-type equations. A key focus of our future research will be to address the computational cost and scalability of both the proposed and existing methods.
