Screening for atrial fibrillation according to serum thyroid-stimulating hormone: an opportunity for refinement?
Authors: Akash Kamalakshan Mavilakandy, Gregory Y. H. Lip
Published: 2026

Atrial fibrillation (AF) and atrial flutter (AFL) are increasingly prevalent cardiac arrhythmias that contribute to high mortality and morbidity. It is a dominant, yet preventable, cause of a cardioembolic stroke, which is associated with higher rates of mortality and disability in comparison to other ischaemic stroke causes if unaddressed (1). Indeed, stroke prevention with oral anticoagulation (OAC) is a major pillar of management in AF care pathways, as recommended in guidelines globally (2,3). Hence, the pursuit of early diagnosis and detection of AF at a population level has been of considerate interest for expeditious anticoagulation initiation and prevention of AF-related complications (4,5).

This approach is a challenging endeavour made all the more evident by the appreciable number of AF cases that remain undetected as a result of the asymptomatic and paroxysmal tendencies of the disease, that therefore results in a large proportion remaining undetected, untreated, and at a higher risk of stroke events (6).

Nevertheless, technological expansion and the development of effective anticoagulation has contributed to the interest and development of effective screening of AF (7-10). This interest has led to the conceptualisation of various AF screening initiatives, including SAFE (11), STROKESTOP (12), and AF-CATCH (13), which have all reported efficacy in identifying patients with undiagnosed AF albeit, in variable diagnostic yield.

Systematic reviews have similarly conveyed a mixed conclusion on the optimal screening approach with Elbadawi et al. reporting a higher rate of AF detection with systematic AF screening (14) while Bonander et al. could not determine an optimal screening method (15). The mixed consensus is further apparent with the differing recommendations conveyed by professional guidelines, for example, with some advocating for opportunistic screening for AF amongst individuals ≥65 years of age, and systematic screening considered for individuals ≥75 years or with an elevated risk of stroke (16). In comparison, the US Preventative Services Task Force concluded that the current evidence base is inadequate for the purposes of weighing the positives and negatives of screening in this population and thus, decided against putting forward recommendations (17). Overall, the Task Force reasoned that the possible advantages of an early diagnosis and management should be weighed against risks and uncertainty due to the potential erroneous diagnosis and associated medication overuse with significant bleeding in the context of OAC initiation following short AF episodes with indeterminate clinical significance. Large ongoing studies, such as SAFER, should overcome these issues (18).

As mentioned, a significant challenge in AF management is the large constituent of asymptomatic patients that only increases across the age group demographics (19). The limitations of the traditional diagnostic methods have led to a variety of detection strategies ranging from Holter monitoring to insertable cardiac monitors (ICM). For example, Sanna et al. reported a significantly increased rate of AF detection in patients with cryptogenic stroke that underwent subsequent monitoring with ICM (20). Furthermore, studies of patients with cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIED) have reported that patients experiencing short and subclinical AF episodes can be associated with elevated stroke risk (21).

The LOOP study was a multicentre, unblinded, randomised controlled trial that was conducted with the objective to investigate the effectiveness of systematic, intensive screening to identify patients with AF for prompt OAC initiation (22). Participants without a known diagnosis of AF with at least 1 additional risk factor were randomised to either receive an implantable loop recorder (ILR) with continuous rhythm monitoring or usual care. Following conclusion of follow-up, the detection of AF and subsequent initiation of OAC was significantly greater in the ILR group in comparison to the control group, however there was no significant differences in the risk of stroke or systemic arterial embolism.

The definition of euthyroid is derived from a 95% confidence interval (CI) of the thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) distribution in a young adult population with thyroid disease, while individuals with TSH concentrations above these intervals in conjunction with a normal free T4 level are defined as exhibiting subclinical hypothyroidism (23). Spona et al. specified 0.3–4.0 mIU/L as their reference normal range for TSH and distributed values into tertiles (T1, T2, and T3) at TSH <1.20, 1.21–1.92, and >1.92 mIU/L (24). A proportion of 94% of participants had TSH levels within the normal range while 2% and 4.2% had levels below and above the normal range, respectively. AF has long been established as a sequela of hyperthyroidism with this association described in both clinical and subclinical hyperthyroidism (25). Indeed, there is a five-fold increase in the development of AF with overt hyperthyroidism (26), while individuals with subclinical hyperthyroidism or even high-normal thyroid function have an approximate three-fold increase in risk (25). Also, the Thyroid Study Collaboration group explored the risk of AF in patients with subclinical hyperthyroidism and presented stratified analysis according to TSH levels where a TSH <0.10 mIU/L was significantly associated with AF following age- and sex-adjusted evaluation (27). Of note, other studies have suggested a U-shaped relationship between TSH levels and cardiovascular risk (28).

While numerous studies have described an increased incidence of AF in patients with hyperthyroidism, there is considerably less literature that have evaluated the incidence of thyroid disease in individuals with AF. Barbisan et al. reported abnormal TSH levels in approximately 16.6% of patients with AF (29), although Krahn et al. reported an estimated incidence of 2% for continuous clinically significant thyroid disease (30). Furthermore, Baumgartner et al. evaluated longitudinal outcomes of 11 cohorts with 30,085 patients where the authors analysed continuous TSH levels and noted an increased risk of AF with low-normal TSH levels (31). In the same study, patients with an elevated free T4 level at baseline with a euthyroid range of TSH also had an increased risk of AF.

As a result, the European Thyroid Association guidelines on endogenous subclinical hyperthyroidism advocate for treatment with antithyroid medication and β-blockers as the first line recommendation in the rate-control strategy of AF in hyperthyroid patients aged ≥65 years with a TSH level of 0.10–0.39 mIU/L (32). However, international guidelines do not objectively classify hyperthyroidism as a risk factor for systemic embolization (SE) in patients with AF or recommend whether the threshold for anticoagulation should be modified in the presence of hyperthyroidism (16).

In a post hoc analysis of the Randomized LOOP Study, Spona et al. conducted a non-prespecified post hoc analysis of the LOOP study where they endeavoured to explore the application of TSH levels in enhancing the effectiveness of AF screening (24). The primary outcome was the incidence of stroke or SE events while the secondary outcomes were composed of major bleeding events, all-cause mortality, and a composite outcome of stroke, SE, and cardiovascular mortality. A total of 6,003 patients had TSH values available and were followed up for a median duration of over 5 years, and participants within the ILR group with TSH levels within the first tertile, were significantly less likely to experience stroke or SE in comparison to the control cohort [adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) 0.52, 95% CI: 0.30–0.90; P=0.02]. Additionally, this subgroup also experienced significantly fewer composite outcomes of stroke, SE, or cardiovascular death (aHR 0.54, 95% CI: 0.35–0.84; P=0.01). Overall, a significant interaction between the TSH tertile and randomisation arm was observed for the primary and secondary outcomes with no effect of ILR screening observed in individuals in higher TSH tertiles.

Hence, TSH could have application as a determinant to distinguish among which individuals at a higher risk of stroke who may benefit from early AF detection and treatment with anticoagulation in comparison to those who would be diagnosed without derivable clinical benefit. This is particularly interesting given the LOOP study’s reported outcomes where continuous monitoring was associated with greater detection rates without a significant absence of a reduction in stroke/SE risk. Therefore, TSH could be utilised as an indicator for high stroke risk for the purposes of optimizing screening. This observation was in spite of similar detection and prevalence of newly diagnosed AF cross the tertiles of TSH which is in stark contrast to prior to literature that conveyed an association of low-normal TSH result with an increased risk of AF (25). However, low TSH levels did not demonstrate the capability for application in identifying the risk of AF among study participants with no AF at baseline.

One plausible hypothesis is an increased propensity for stroke events with the arrhythmias detected in the first tertile of TSH. With only 6% of the population demonstrating TSH levels outside of euthyroid range, the observation also cannot be justified by overt hyperthyroidism. Furthermore, the tertiles demonstrated comparable baseline demographics and risk factors although there was an absence of regression analysis to further explore the potential confounding effects of cardiovascular risk factors specific to low-TSH individuals. The increased stroke risk in this subgroup was consistent with findings from other studies (33) as well as a Mendelian randomisation (MR) study where a 1 standard deviation increase in TSH was associated a 5% decrease in the risk of stroke with AF reported as a key mediator according to multivariable MR analysis (34). Various pathophysiological mechanism for an increased predisposition to major adverse cardiovascular events in individuals with low TSH levels have been proposed with hypothesis ranging from increased fibrinogen levels and associated prothrombotic states (35) to abnormal vasculature, as reflected by carotid intima-media thickness in population-based studies (36).

The study by Spona et al. (24) alongside the LOOP study exhibits several limitations. First, post hoc analyses of a randomised control trial (RCT) should be approached with caution as the outcomes should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, being inherently underpowered in comparison to the analysis of the full study cohort. Second, the generalisability of findings is uncertain as the derived population is primarily composed of individuals from a Northern European Caucasian demographic, given the racial differences in AF-related clinical epidemiology and stroke (37,38). Lastly, given the absence of blinding in the methodology, participants in either groups could undergo changes in health behaviour in relation to AF detection during follow-up and thus influence their respective stroke risk profile.

In conclusion, these exploratory findings suggest potential application of lower TSH levels for identifying populations with a more clinically relevant phenotype that could derive benefit from the screening process as opposed to increasing the yield of screening through greater AF detection. These findings bring forth practical implications including consideration of routine measurement of TSH levels prior to enrolment for refinement of AF screening although further longitudinal studies with robust methodologies are indicated to explore this association.
