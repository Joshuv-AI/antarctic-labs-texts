Echocardiography guided management of atrial fibrillation
Authors: M Balik, P Vignon, M S Chew, G Tavazzi, P Mayo, G Doufle, J Aron, J Hastings, B Cholley, K Jiroutkova, M Slama, A Herpain, A McLean
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe growing burden of atrial fibrillation (AF) experienced by the general population translates into an increased incidence in the intensive care setting, further aggravated by illness severity. New onset AF has been established as an independent mortality predictor. Cardiology management guidelines are based on major trials that included ambulatory patients with varying degrees of ventricular systolic and diastolic dysfunction, and with variable dependences of left ventricular filling on atrial systole. Emphasis is placed on rate control combined with anticoagulation therapy, along with careful consideration of limiting any myocardial depression by antiarrhythmic medication in patients who often already have some form of structural heart disease.DesignNarrative review Objectives: Critical care echocardiography (CCE) is well established as a widely available diagnostic and monitoring tool in haemodynamically unstable patients. It assists in identifying risk factors associated with arrhythmias, reveals parameters associated with arrhythmia chronicity, and guides therapy to facilitate a return to sinus rhythm. CCE helps guide the crucial management decision to seek either rhythm or rate control and, with rhythm control, monitors return of mechanical sinus rhythm with left atrial recovery post cardioversion. Echocardiography can also help when conflicting management goals are present, such as guideline-driven therapeutic anticoagulation in the intensive care patient that is at significant risk of bleeding.ResultsThis review seeks to assist intensive care practitioners managing patients with AF, with a focus on the many benefits CCE offers, blending specific intensive care medicine data to current cardiology guidelines on arrhythmia management in these severely ill patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00134-025-08112-8.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Supraventricular arrhythmia, Echocardiography, Cardioversion, Rhythm control, Rate control

Take-home message

Critical care echocardiography is an invaluable tool for management of atrial fibrillation and other supraventricular arrhythmias in intensive care. Left atrial two-dimensional static and functional parameters together with mitral pulsed wave Doppler can help decide between a rhythm versus rate control strategy for arrhythmia management.

Introduction

The rising prevalence of arrhythmias places an increasing burden on critical care systems worldwide. Supraventricular arrhythmias, particularly atrial fibrillation (AF), are the most commonly encountered arrhythmias, affecting up to 25% of individuals over 40 years of age [1]. The incidence increases with age, illness severity, and surgical invasiveness, with perioperative AF rates reaching 15–40% after cardiac surgery and up to 46% in intensive care unit (ICU) patients with septic shock [2–6].

AF is an independent risk factor for mortality in the critically ill [4]. New-onset atrial fibrillation (NOAF) raises a logical question as to whether its deleterious effect could be reversed by cardioversion and return to sinus rhythm (SR). There is limited data on the acute benefits of a rhythm control vs. rate control strategy [7–10], although similar outcomes have been documented over long-term follow-up [11]. Non-sustained arrhythmias may spontaneously terminate and require observation only whilst the decision for electrical (DC) or pharmacological cardioversion of NOAF remains debatable [12].

Major trials from the cardiology setting have failed to demonstrate a benefit of a rhythm control over a rate control strategy [13–15]. However, most cardiology randomised controlled trials fail to consider the degree of pre-existing left ventricular (LV) systolic and diastolic dysfunction, which can be of paramount importance in the acutely decompensated patient [13, 16–21]. Indeed, there may be relevant differences in responses to antiarrhythmic therapy in subgroups stratified by pre-existing cardiac function. In the presence of clinical equipoise regarding the best strategy, there is a physiological rationale for the use of echocardiography to help determine whether rhythm control is more favourable in certain populations [22].

Critical care echocardiography (CCE) has become the imaging modality of choice for investigating haemodynamically unstable patients in the ICU. In addition, CCE has a unique and important role in the assessment of cardiac structure, function, risk stratification, and in guiding the management of an arrhythmia [1, 8, 15, 23–25].

In this review, we illustrate the role of CCE in guiding the therapeutic management of the ICU patient presenting with NOAF or an AF of unknown duration in determining whether there is a preference for rhythm versus rate control, antiarrhythmic drug selection, guidance of DC cardioversion, anticoagulation, and the role in immediate follow-up. We also address the limitations of CCE and its parameters in the presence of AF. For literature search details, see Table 1 ESM.

Associated heart disease

Left ventricular systolic function

A reduction in LV systolic function increases the incidences of both atrial and ventricular arrhythmias. While the prevalence of AF ranges between 10 and 15% in patients with moderately reduced LV ejection fraction (LVEF), it reaches 49% in those with severe LV dysfunction [14, 19]. In a cohort of non-cardiac critically ill patients, NOAF was associated with a LVEF < 35% [26].

Right ventricular function, pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale

Right ventricular (RV) dilatation is associated with new-onset arrhythmias [27]. Across all forms of pulmonary hypertension (PH), the prevalence of AF ranges from 26 to 31% [28, 29]. Arrhythmias are often a harbinger of clinical deterioration with a worsening of RV failure symptoms in the majority of PH patients [30]. Right atrial (RA) dilatation with spherical remodelling and elevated RA pressure (RAP) are associated with a high prevalence of AF. In mechanically ventilated patients with septic shock, the presence of PH following cardioversion, defined as systolic pulmonary artery pressure (SPAP) ≥ 51 mmHg obtained by echocardiography, was associated with multiple recurrences of AF [25, 30]. Cardiology guidelines suggest the echocardiographic assessment of PH by the peak tricuspid pressure gradient without considering the RAP estimated from the lower vena cava diameter and its respiratory variation [31, 32]. However, most ICU patients have a central venous catheter allowing for direct measurement of the RAP and more precise SPAP estimates.

Acute cor pulmonale (RV dilatation associated with paradoxical septal motion) may trigger AF contributing to haemodynamic instability. In chronic PH, the RV typically develops adaptive wall hypertrophy, allowing it to generate increased systolic pressure to counteract an elevated afterload. In the setting of acutely reduced RV contractility, NOAF or high-rate tachycardia can rapidly result in RV failure and cardiogenic shock [33].

Diastolic function and left atrium

LV diastolic dysfunction is common in ICU patients. It is associated with a median age of 55–65 years with inherent physiological alteration of LV relaxation, prevalence of hypertension, various forms of heart failure, ischaemic heart disease, and sepsis [34]. In patients with AF, the mitral A wave is missing; however, with intermittent cardioversion, the spectral mitral Doppler profile may provide valuable insights on prognosis [35] and on the dependence of LV filling on LA contraction [25, 36, 37]. For example, patients with abnormal (prolonged) relaxation and “normalised” (type II) mitral Doppler profile [22] experience greater benefit from maintenance of SR when exposed to critical illness than patients with advanced LV diastolic dysfunction reflected by a restrictive (type III) mitral Doppler pattern [22], where rate control may be more appropriate. Interestingly, no cardiology trial has yet attempted to stratify patients according to the severity of LV diastolic dysfunction (hence to the degree of contribution of atrial contraction to LV filling) when evaluating the management strategies (rhythm versus rate control) in the non-critically ill. Surprisingly, there is a noticeable knowledge gap on predictors of successful cardioversion in critically ill patients with AF, despite its high incidence and significant relationship with survival [25, 36–38]. An understanding of the underlying pathophysiology may be a guide to more effective treatment.

The LA dilates progressively in patients with LV diastolic dysfunction, reflecting the increased contribution of LA contraction to filling of the poorly relaxing (stage I) and progressively less compliant (higher stages) LV. Restrictive LV filling with associated elevated atrial pressure promotes AF; conversely, the LA dilatation may be a consequence of prolonged AF. Enlarged LA size (area or volume) and LV diastolic dysfunction were repeatedly identified as independent predictors of NOAF in ICU patients [38–40].

AF is typically associated with an increased LA size, as reflected by the elevated end-systolic LA diameter (LA_ESD) and biplanar end-systolic LA volume indexed to body surface area (LAVI, Fig. 1 ESM; Fig. 1). The LA_ESD should not be larger than 45 mm [41, 42]. However, a value ≥ 46 mm measured with TTE poorly predicts recurrent AF in patients with septic shock, and the LAVI should be preferred (Fig. 1) [25]. Irrespective of the parameter used to assess LA, TOE has not been validated for this measurement since the size of the anatomical structure exceeds the width of the two-dimensional field [43, 44].

Normal LAVI should be less than 35 ml/m2 in SR and less than 40 ml/m2 in patients with AF. The 35 ml/m2 cut-off reflects elevated LV end-diastolic pressure and corresponds to a diagnostic criterion of LV diastolic dysfunction [22, 31]. A large LAVI may predict a long-standing or recurrent AF, and potentially guide clinicians towards rate rather than rhythm control [39, 45–49].

Valve disorders

Valvulopathies are independently associated with arrhythmias [1, 50]. About one-third of patients with AF have associated valvular disease, irrespective of its severity as assessed by echocardiography [1, 51]. Arrhythmia is a mortality predictor in valvular disease signalling cardiac decompensation due to pressure and/or volume overload. Accordingly, the development of arrhythmias in a patient with severe valvular disease may trigger valve repair or replacement. Alternatively, the presence of valvular disease may indicate the chronicity of a recently identified arrhythmia [14]. Current terminology dedicates the term “valvular aetiology” of arrhythmia to either rheumatic heart disease, typically mitral stenosis, or to an arrhythmia occurring in patients with mechanical prosthetic valves. A diagnosis and grading of valvular disease may help estimate the risk of intracardiac thrombus formation. An arrhythmia with a stenotic valve, especially mitral stenosis, increases such risk. In contrast, the presence of a moderate-to-severe MR reduces the risk of LA clotting due to the washout effect associated with volume recirculation between atrium and ventricle [50, 52].

Echocardiographic assessment during AF

The ventricular systolic function parameters show an acceptable intra-observer and inter-observer variability if 3–5 cardiac cycles are averaged in the presence of AF, or when measuring an index beat where the two preceding RR intervals are equal [23]. The validity and reproducibility of measurements depend on the HR and regularity of the ventricular response. Unfortunately, in addition to excluding patients with significant valvular disease, most of the published comparisons between echocardiographic and invasive measurements have been based on patients with a HR below 100/min [23]. The LVEF (e.g., biplanar Simpson´s method), eyeballing contractility estimate, mitral annular plane systolic excursion (MAPSE), or mitral tissue velocity S´ may help estimate the probability of a recurrent arrhythmia and decide on the use of an antiarrhythmic agent (Fig. 2).

Loss of the atrial kick and irregular ventricular contraction can dramatically reduce RV stroke volume, decrease SPAP, impair LV filling already compromised by the paradoxical septal motion, and decrease cardiac index [28, 30]. PH may not be detected. Altered coupling between the RV and pulmonary artery is reflected by a ratio of TAPSE to SPAP lower than 0.3–0.4, which is usually accompanied by an elevation of RAP and associated systemic venous congestion [33, 53, 54].

LAVI is one of the diagnostic criteria for LV diastolic dysfunction and is integrated in the assessment of LV preload. In addition, recent guidelines recommend the use of multiple Doppler parameters for diagnosis of altered diastolic function under AF [31, 55]. An elevated ratio (E/e´ > 11) of mitral inflow and annulus displacement at the septal aspect associated with a LAVI > 34 ml/m2 predicts the occurrence of AF [39, 46]. Every 1 ml/m2 increment in LAVI increases the risk of recurrent AF by 21% [46].

Both congestion and hypovolemia of the left heart chambers result in arrhythmias in ICU patients. This arrhythmogenic tendency may be further exacerbated by catecholamine infusion, which may precipitate NOAF [26, 56]. The irregular LV stroke volume observed during AF results from the variable diastolic filling time and thus constitutes a limitation in the use of Doppler analysis, including following a fluid challenge or a passive leg raising test to evaluate fluid responsiveness (Table 1 ESM) [57]. Stroke volume or its surrogate, the left ventricular outflow tract velocity–time-integral, remains meaningful endpoints to assess the response to volume expansion, but averaging several consecutive cardiac cycles is mandatory to minimise beat-by-beat variability. For the same reasons, respiratory variations of maximal aortic Doppler velocity in ventilated patients cannot be used as an index of fluid responsiveness [58]. In contrast, the collapsibility index of the superior vena cava remains valid in ventilated patients with AF, since the loss of SR does not interfere with interactions between these great vessels and the lungs during the respiratory cycle. The position of the interatrial septum during the respiratory and cardiac cycles can outperform the Doppler indices of preload in an unstable patient with AF [59].

Recent cardiology guidelines suggest that the following Doppler parameters indicate elevated LAP: E > 1 m/s, E wave deceleration time (DtE) < 160 ms, septal E/e' > 11, tricuspid regurgitation velocity > 2.8 m/s. Therefore, they can be used to contraindicate fluid therapy, especially if bilateral pulmonary B-lines are simultaneously recorded by lung ultrasound [31, 55]. Unfortunately, usual Doppler indicators are influenced by tachycardia and rhythm irregularity during AF, afterload, and mechanical ventilation, all of which complicate their interpretation for preload assessment in the critically ill. When estimating the LV filling pressure, the correlation coefficients between the invasive measurements used as reference and mitral Doppler indices (E, DtE, E/e´) range between 0.4 and 0.7 across observational studies [23]. The pulmonary vein Doppler velocities (systolic wave and diastolic wave with its deceleration time, Dtd) show promise with correlation to SR reaching r = 0.80–0.91 [23, 60]. The LAP estimates obtained by deducting the peak pressure gradient of the mitral regurgitant jet from LV systolic pressure may perform well in patients with significant mitral regurgitation and AF, assuming more cardiac cycles are averaged [23].

Echocardiography guided AF treatment

Since DC cardioversion of AF is associated with a high rate of recurrence (> 70%), concomitant administration of an antiarrhythmic drug is warranted in most patients [20]. However, although the success rate of the rhythm control strategy reaches 70–90%, the use of antiarrhythmic drugs is fraught with side effects, including a depressant impact on myocardial contractility [1, 20].

The cardiac depressant effect of sotalol, flecainide, and beta-blockers raises concerns when used in patients with AF [61]. Amiodarone, the least cardio-depressant antiarrhythmic agent, has been historically preferred by cardiologists treating patients with “structural heart disease” [61]. This may not be the best choice for critically ill patients with septic shock, normal-to-moderately reduced LVEF ≥ 35% and a non-dilated LA, where treatment with propafenone resulted in more frequent SR at 24 h (+ 16%) and less recurrence of arrhythmia (-24%) than in the amiodarone group [8, 24]. The effects of short-acting beta-blockers in AF patients with septic shock included in major trials alluded to similar benefits [62]. Current algorithms for AF management recommend against cardiac depressant medications such as vernakalant, calcium channel blockers, propafenone, flecainide, and beta-blockers in patients with severe LV dysfunction (LVEF < 35%), for which the treatment with amiodarone and/or digoxin is reserved (Fig. 2) [1, 15].

NOAF in patients with PH is associated with a 2.1–4.7-fold increase in mortality if SR cannot be restored [63, 64]. Current guidelines advocate for a proactive rhythm-control strategy for PH patients with AF together with appropriate PH therapies [27, 32]. The priority is determining the aetiology of PH and assessment of the potential contribution of elevated LAP. The primary treatment aims at relieving RV strain by fluid unloading and adjusting mechanical ventilation to reduce RV outflow impedance. In patients with acute respiratory distress, prone positioning may also be used to unload the RV [65]. Since drugs with minimal negative inotropic activity are preferred, amiodarone is frequently used as a first-line therapy. Patients with severe RV dysfunction and preserved LV systolic function seemed to gain a larger benefit from the 1C class propafenone for rhythm control compared with amiodarone [24]. Alternative drugs which allow limiting the use of amiodarone with its potential pulmonary side effects include cautiously titrated short-acting beta-blockers (esmolol or landiolol). In case of poor tolerance due to their negative inotropic and chronotropic effects in patients with compromised RV function, infusion discontinuation will quickly restore the previous haemodynamic status. A rate control strategy (target around 100/min) is implemented if rhythm control fails. This would involve the use of amiodarone (which helps both rhythm and rate), beta-blockers (if tolerated), or digoxin.

A relationship between LA size and success of cardioversion with maintenance of SR could not be confirmed in ICU patients, unlike cardiology patients [25]. Accordingly, in haemodynamically unstable patients with low cardiac index and NOAF, a moderately dilated LA should not preclude attempts at cardioversion. Regarding specific rhythm control approaches, cardioversion with the beta-blocker derivative propafenone (class 1C antiarrhythmic) in patients with septic shock, NOAF and a non-dilated LA (LAVI < 40mls/m2) was associated with a 12-month survival benefit compared to amiodarone (OR 0.6,p = 0.01) [8, 62]. Thus, it seems that echocardiographic assessment of LAVI may detect patients with a potential survival benefit when undergoing assessment for rhythm control and cardioversion. In AF patients, 3D TTE assessment of LA volume may also predict successful cardioversion or radiofrequency catheter ablation [66].

Cardioversion to SR may not immediately translate into measurable changes in LA function using echocardiography, especially in the presence of markedly elevated LV filling pressure and failing LA. Although LA contraction may sometimes be restored immediately after cardioversion, mechanical recovery may often be more gradual and not be seen until 4–6 h post cardioversion (Figs. 1, 3) [25, 36–38]. Patients with SR on ECG without a substantial mechanical contraction (no A wave on the mitral inflow pattern) are at high risk of arrhythmia recurrences. In such patients, a rate control strategy should be preferred for the long term rather than vain iterative attempts at restoring SR. In patients with chronic persistent arrhythmia, LA functional recovery may take up to one month or longer after cardioversion [67, 68]. The LA emptying fraction (LAEF(%): [end-systolic LAV- end-diastolic LAV] / end-systolic LAV, Fig. 1) is typically between 30–60% in a healthy person in SR. LAEF is less dependent on preload when compared to LAVI or LA diameter [41, 69]. LAEF may therefore enable better decision making in the clinical setting of abrupt changes in loading conditions seen in the critically ill. In patients with septic shock, LAEF greater than 44% was associated with a sustained SR after cardioversion, and a cut-off value of 38% at 4 h after cardioversion was predictive of SR maintenance (AUC 0.69). Lower levels (Fig. 1), however, showcased almost linear inverse relationships to the number of arrhythmia recurrences [25].

During SR, LA contraction can be semi-quantitatively assessed by the measurement of both the A wave maximal velocity and velocity–time integral (Avti, Fig. 3). In a trial focussing on rhythm control in patients with septic shock, an Avti greater than 8.6 cm at 4 h after cardioversion predicted sustainability of SR, and its value was lowered proportionally to the number of AF recurrences [25]. The main limitation of this parameter is tachycardia, which leads to the fusion of both early (E) and late (A) mitral Doppler waves. To circumvent this limitation, these measurements can be performed using the trapezoid method (Fig. 3; Fig. 2 ESM). Unfortunately, this approach is not always reliable, as demonstrated in exercising healthy volunteers when a correction for HR was suggested [70]. A HR ≥ 85/min after cardioversion could potentially increase Avti due to the fusion of the A wave with the E wave, and potentially falsely predict rhythm control. Nonetheless, this relationship of Avti to HR was not proven clinically relevant in critically ill patients with septic shock and low afterload, suggesting a possible usefulness of Avti to predict AF relapse [25].

Myocardial deformation imaging can improve the diagnostic and prognostic role of echocardiography in the management of patients with AF. LA strain determined by two-dimensional speckle tracking echocardiography is a powerful predictor of AF recurrence after catheter ablation [71]. A growing body of evidence supports the estimation of LA strain for the assessment of LA function and, potentially, also recovery after cardioversion (Fig. 3 ESM) [41, 48]. The major limitations of atrial strain are the absence of ICU and pathology-specific reference values and current ICU TTE imaging quality [72].

Echocardiography-guided rhythm versus rate control approach

DC cardioversion is indicated in the management of severe haemodynamic instability and impending cardiogenic shock with low cardiac output due to arrhythmia [1, 15]. Its success, however, increases to > 80% with the addition of an antiarrhythmic agent [20].

Echocardiographic assessment (Fig. 2) starts with an estimate of LV systolic function to ensure avoidance of cardiac depressant anti-arrhythmics in patients with compromised LV systolic function. The next step is the correction of hypovolaemia in patients on vasopressors or elevated LV filling pressures that both reduce the likelihood of successful cardioversion (Table 1 ESM).

The decision regarding rhythm or rate control should follow this initial assessment (Fig. 2; Table 1). In patients with NOAF, a non-excessively dilated LA, absence of significant valvular disease, and high catecholamine requirements, the rhythm control strategy is preferred. The main reason is the immediate improvement of stroke volume and vascular resistance associated with the restoration of SR, which decreases the need for vasopressors (Fig. 4). The ensuing withdrawal of catecholamines reduces cellular stress, tachycardia, and the risk of arrhythmia recurrence [56].

Figure 4 depicts the impact of cardioversion on haemodynamic variables recorded in 187 patients with septic shock [25]. Within 4 h following the restoration of SR, both HR and cardiac output decreased despite an increase in LV stroke volume. The systemic vascular resistance rose significantly after cardioversion, restoring mean arterial pressure despite the decrease in cardiac output and allowing for a reduction in the dose of vasopressors (noradrenaline and arginine-vasopressin, Fig. 0.4). If cardiac oxygen requirements are predominantly linked to heart rate and afterload, then cardioversion is a cardiac protective step under conditions of haemodynamic stress and septic shock. The prognostic benefit is demonstrated in patients with a non-dilated LA and persistent SR after cardioversion with a mortality benefit at 1 year [8, 62]. Monitoring the effects of cardioversion is warranted especially in borderline cases, e.g. with mild-to-moderate LA dilatation (i.e. < 48 ml/m2, Table 1) [41]. In those patients, adequate LAEF (≥ 38%) and Avti (≥ 7.0 cm) at 4 h suggest an opportunity for permanent rhythm control, whereas lower values may indicate a risk of arrhythmia recurrences [25].

In patients with significant LA dilatation, moderate-to-severe valvular disease and/or high dose of catecholamines, a rate control strategy is indicated. Due to overlap between the rhythm and rate control approaches, cardioversion may be applied in borderline cases (Table 1) [11]. The benefit of rate control lies in the extension of the diastolic filling time, allowing for improved LV filling, extended coronary artery myocardial perfusion and a reduction in myocardial oxygen consumption. To date, no major trial has demonstrated superiority of the rhythm control over the rate control strategy during the long-term observation of cardiac patients [13]. This absence of evidence cannot exclude benefits when bridging haemodynamically unstable and closely monitored patients throughout their critical illness [7, 9, 10, 62].

Echocardiography-led anticoagulation strategy

A patient with AF of unknown duration or known to exist for more than 24 h should undergo a TOE prior to DC cardioversion to rule out an underlying intracardiac thrombus and to reduce the risk of systemic embolization during the procedure [14, 15]. This cardiology recommendation may apply to critically ill patients who often exhibit high plasma levels of multiple pro-coagulation factors but are also prone to bleeding complications. Thus, the benefit/risk balance should be carefully assessed in each individual prior to making a decision on anticoagulation. TOE is easily performed in critically ill patients who are often intubated and sedated. This exam has a sensitivity and specificity above 90% for the detection of intracardiac sludge or thrombus, especially in the LA or in the left atrial appendage (LAA) which are closest to the transducer. Spontaneous echo contrast is caused by low blood flow velocities promoting red blood cell aggregation. The swirling motion differentiates spontaneous echo contrast from an excessive gain setting [41]. Spontaneous echo contrast was observed in 20% of patients with a stroke, versus 5% in patients without stroke [73]. A thrombus may present as a mobile/sessile mass in the LAA. In addition, a LAA Doppler flow velocity < 0.4 m/s indicates a risk of thrombus formation, whilst very low velocities (≤ 0.3 m/s) aggravate the clotting risk (Fig. 4 ESM) [15, 41].

In ICU patients, the risk of intracardiac thrombus formation can be stratified according to the size of the LA, the presence of valvular disease, and LV systolic function. For example, a patient in AF with normal echocardiographic findings has a 1.5% risk of intracardiac thrombus formation. Therefore, anticoagulation for an arrhythmia should be weighed against the side effects in critically ill patients in whom conditions such as recent surgery, coagulopathy, or thrombopenia are not uncommon. Moreover, the suggestion from the cardiology guidelines that the benefit of anticoagulation outweighs the risk when the estimated stroke risk is higher than 2%, calculated with an aid of available risk scores (CHA2DS2-VASc ≥ 2), can expose intensive care patients to an unacceptable risk of bleeding [14, 40, 74]. Nonetheless, echocardiography-driven risk assessment demonstrates a 20% chance of developing an intracardiac thrombus in a patient with AF, dilated LA, moderately-to-severely reduced LV systolic function, and an absence of MR [45, 52]. In addition, the risk of thrombus will further increase in the presence of valvular stenosis or a mechanical valve prosthesis. The restoration of mechanical SR should reduce requirements for anticoagulation, especially if adequate post-cardioversion LAEF and Avti are present. Thus, rhythm control may logically reduce the risk of bleeding, although it should be noted that ICU data are lacking.

Conclusion and limitations

This review adds ICU-specific data to the current cardiology guidelines on arrhythmia management to assist in the management of critically ill patients with AF. Actual major limitations include scarcity of the intensive care data, and the small sample size of the single centre studies. Echocardiography has become the modality of choice for investigating haemodynamically unstable patients in the ICU [75]. With regards to the growing burden of AF, the authors believe that measurement of the end-systolic LAVI prior to treatment and the LAEF after cardioversion, respectively predictors of the probability of success or of AF recurrence, should be implemented as part of the 2D echocardiography assessment (Fig. 1). An echocardiography-driven approach to guide AF management is outlined including considerations underpinning a rhythm or rate control strategy in the critically ill. Available data paves the way for further prospective research evaluating patient-centred outcomes with echocardiography-guided arrhythmia management vs. standard of care.
