Outcomes of patients with or without DNA repair pathway alterations: the MD Anderson IMPACT2 study
Authors: Jacopo Venturini, Mehmet A Baysal, Abhijit Chakraborty, Timothy A Yap, Siqing Fu, David S Hong, Sarina A Piha-Paul, Aung Naing, Jordi Rodon Ahnert, Ecaterina Elena Dumbrava, Jennifer Beck, Yang Liu, Michael Kahle, David J Vining, Funda Meric-Bernstam, Apostolia Maria Tsimberidou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDNA repair deficiencies result in genomic instability. Data suggest that genomic instability is associated with response to immuno-oncology (IO) therapies. We analyzed outcomes of patients in the IMPACT2 study by the presence of DNA damage response (DDR) alterations and treatment type. Of 662 patients with ≥1 alterations: 109 (16.5%) had DDR alterations (treated, N = 85) and 553 (83.5%) were DDR wild-type (treated, N = 426). In patients with DDR alterations, absence of liver metastases (p = 0.029) and IO therapy (vs. chemotherapy, p = 0.020; vs. anti-DDR agents, p = 0.048) were independent factors predicting longer overall survival (OS). In the DDR-wild-type cohort, independent factors predicting longer OS were IO therapy (compared with each other treatment group: vs. IO+non-IO combinations, p = 0.003; vs. chemotherapy, p < 0.001; vs. anti-DDR agents, p < 0.001; vs. other targeted therapies, p = 0.006), absence of liver metastases (p < 0.001), and normal albumin (p < 0.001) and lactate dehydrogenase (p = 0.001) levels. Prospective studies are warranted to refine the role of DDR alterations as biomarkers of IO response.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Oncology

Introduction

Alterations in DNA repair pathways are a hallmark of carcinogenesis. They result in genomic instability, promote the accumulation of mutations and tumorigenesis, and influence treatment response1. The DNA damage response (DDR) network safeguards genome integrity by activating distinct repair systems depending on the type of damage, including homologous recombination repair (HRR), non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), mismatch repair (MMR), base and nucleotide excision repair (BER and NER), and the Fanconi anemia pathway2. The MMR and HRR systems have therapeutic implications for targeted therapies and immunotherapy3. The MMR system is critical for correcting base mismatches during DNA replication. MMR deficiency leads to error accumulation and microsatellite instability (MSI), a hypermutable phenotype characterized by high tumor mutational burden (TMB) and the generation of neoantigens that enhance tumor immunogenicity4,5. The HRR pathway ensures highly accurate repair of DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs). Germline and somatic mutations in HRR-related genes (e.g., BRCA1 and BRCA2), disrupt this process, forcing tumor cells to rely on alternative, error-prone DDR pathways such as NHEJ repair6.

Currently, several poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi) are approved by the Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of BRCA-mutated and homologous recombination-deficient (HRD) solid tumors7. PARPi inhibit single-strand break repair mechanisms, leading to the accumulation of lethal DSBs in HRR-deficient cells6,8. Olaparib is approved for the treatment of BRCA-mutated advanced ovarian9–13 and pancreatic cancers14, advanced and localized breast cancer10,15, and metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC), where the indication includes all HRR gene alterations16,17. Talazoparib is approved for BRCA-mutated HER2-negative breast cancer18 and HRR-mutated mCRPC19; niraparib for advanced ovarian cancer20,21 and HRR-mutated mCRPC22; and rucaparib for BRCA-mutated ovarian cancer23 and BRCA-mutated mCRPC24. Platinum compounds also have antitumor activity in DDR-mutated tumors25–27. Combination strategies of PARPi with chemotherapy and radiation therapy have also been investigated28,29, although their use was challenging owing to overlapping toxicities and difficulty optimizing dosing30,31. Importantly, HRR-deficient tumors have been reported to have immunogenic potential32. Following DNA damage, cytosolic DNA accumulation can activate innate immune-sensing pathways, such as the cGAS-STING axis, leading to type I interferon–mediated adaptive immune responses33. Chronic cGAS-STING activation can also promote cancer cell survival through the IL-6–STAT3 signaling axis34. Although emerging evidence suggests that genomic instability may enhance sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibitors, no systematic study across tumor types has been reported.

The aim of this study was to retrospectively analyze the clinical outcomes of patients with advanced metastatic cancers, who participated in the IMPACT2 study (NCT02152254) and whose tumors harbored a DNA-damaging alteration, by treatment type: immuno-oncology (IO) therapies, IO + non-IO combinations, anti-DDR agents, other targeted therapies, or chemotherapy-based regimens. We also analyzed the outcomes of patients who participated in IMPACT2 whose tumors did not have DDR alterations.

Results

Of 829 patients with advanced metastatic cancer enrolled in the IMPACT2 study, 692 completed molecular testing. Overall, 662 patients had tumors that harbored at least one gene alteration, and 30 patients had no alterations. Of these 662 patients, 109 (16.5%) had DDR alterations and 553 (83.5%) did not have DDR alterations (Fig. S1). We initially explored the molecular landscape through gene enrichment and interaction analyzes and subsequently evaluated the clinical outcomes by treatment type in patients with and without DDR mutations.

Molecular analysis

Gene enrichment patterns

Gene enrichment analysis revealed statistically significant associations between specific DDR gene alterations and tumor types (Fig. 1A). In the group with DDR alterations, significant enrichments were found for PALB2 (p = 0.019) in breast cancer; CDK12 (p < 0.001) in prostate cancer; BRCA1 in ovarian cancer (p = 0.001); MSH6 (p = 0.003) and STAG2 (p = 0.008) in sarcomas; PMS2 (p = 0.005) in non-ovarian gynecologic tumors; RAD51B (p = 0.006) in head and neck cancers; and NBN (p = 0.046) in endocrine tumors (Table S1).

In the cohort without DDR alterations (Fig. 1B), enriched genes in breast cancer included PIK3CA (p < 0.001), ESR1 (p < 0.001), CDKN1B (p = 0.002), CDH1 (p < 0.001), TBX3 (p = 0.002), GATA3 (p < 0.001), EMSY (p = 0.005), and PIK3R1 (p = 0.002). In prostate cancer, enrichment was noted for AR (p < 0.001), ERG (p < 0.001), PTEN (p < 0.001), FOXA1 (p < 0.001), TMPRSS2 (p < 0.001), and PPP2R2A (p = 0.002). Lung tumors showed enrichment of EGFR (p < 0.001), KEAP1 (p = 0.001), CTNNB1 (p = 0.010), STK11 (p = 0.030), and SMARCA4 (p = 0.046). In colorectal cancer, significant enrichment was observed for KRAS, APC, SMAD4, TP53, and FLT3 (each, p < 0.001) and TCF7L2 (p = 0.004). In melanoma, the frequent mutations were GNAQ (p < 0.001), BAP1 (p < 0.001), and BRAF (p = 0.049), while KRAS (p < 0.001), CDKN2A (p = 0.025), and SMAD4 (p = 0.020) were commonly altered in pancreatic cancer (Table S2).

Patients with DDR alterations

Baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of 109 patients with DDR alterations are summarized in Table 1. Their median age was 59.6 years (range, 18–84.3); 56.0% were men and 44.0% were women. Overall, 83.5% had an ECOG performance status of 1, and 65.1% had >2 lines of prior therapy (median number of prior therapies, 3; range: 0–8). The median number of metastatic sites was 2, and 37.6% of patients had liver metastases. The most common tumor types were colorectal (15.6%), breast (11.9%), and gastrointestinal (9.2%) cancers. Overall, 15.8% of patients had MSI-high tumors; 57.6% had programmed death-ligand (PD-L)1 expression ≥1%; and 34.9% of the evaluable cases had high tumor mutational burden (TMB-H, ≥10 mutations/Mb).

Treatment

Of the 109 patients with DDR alterations, 85 (78%) received treatment and were included in the outcome analyzes (Table S5). Of these 85 treated patients, 34 (40%) received IO (20 patients were treated with IO alone and 14 patients were treated with IO+non-IO combination therapy), 12 (14.1%) received anti-DDR targeted therapies, 22 (25.9%) received other targeted therapies (e.g., anti-human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 [HER2], anti-vascular endothelial growth factor [VEGF], and other tyrosine kinase inhibitors [TKIs]), and 17 (20%) received chemotherapy-based regimens, including 6 patients who received platinum-based therapy (Table S6). Given the small number of patients treated with IO alone and IO+non-IO combination therapy, these two groups were analyzed together. The baseline characteristics, treatment, and clinical outcomes of patients in each treatment group are listed in Tables S7, S8, S9, and S10, respectively.

Response to treatment

Of the 85 DDR-positive treated patients, all were evaluable for response. The objective response rate (ORR) was 20.6% in the IO group, 11.8% in the chemotherapy-based group, 8.3% in the anti-DDR group, and 0% in the other targeted therapy group. The respective clinical benefit rates (CBRs) were 47.0%, 52.9%, 25.0%, and 22.7%. No significant differences were noted among the treatment groups, except for the ORR of the IO group compared with the other targeted therapy group (p = 0.035) (Tables S11 and S12).

Progression-free survival (PFS)

Of the 85 patients, 61 (71.8%) experienced disease progression (Fig. 3A); their median PFS was 4.1 months (95% CI, 2.8–6.3). The median PFS was 5.9 months (95% CI, 3.6-NA) in patients treated with chemotherapy; 4.4 months (95% CI, 2.3–9.8) in patients treated with IO; 3.1 months (95% CI, 2.2-NA) in patients treated with other targeted therapies; and 3.0 months (95% CI, 1.7-NA) in those who received anti-DDR targeted agents (Table S11). No statistically significant difference in PFS was observed across treatment groups (log-rank p = 0.45). A trend towards improved PFS was noted for patients treated with chemotherapy compared with anti–DDR agents (p = 0.094).

Overall survival (OS)

Of the 85 treated patients with DDR mutations, 70 (82.35%) have died. The median follow-up of surviving patients was 25.67 months (interquartile range, 14.28–30.85 months). Their median OS was 11.6 months (95% CI, 8.4–15.1) (Table S11). No significant difference in OS was noted among the treatment groups (log-rank p = 0.056) (Fig. 3B). OS was significantly longer in patients treated with IO compared with those receiving chemotherapy-based regimens (p = 0.024). There was a trend towards longer OS in patients treated with IO vs. anti-DDR inhibitors (p = 0.055) and in patients treated with other targeted therapies vs. chemotherapy-based treatments (p = 0.081).

Univariate and multivariate analysis

In a univariate analysis for PFS, a trend towards longer PFS was observed in patients aged <60 years (p = 0.086) and in those with ≤2 metastatic sites (p = 0.089). In a univariate analysis for OS, longer OS was noted in patients without liver metastases (p = 0.029); a trend towards longer OS was observed in patients <60 years old (p = 0.076). When treatment was added to the model, IO was associated with longer OS compared with chemotherapy-based regimens (p = 0.028) and with a trend towards longer OS compared with anti-DDR agents (p = 0.052) (Table 2). In a multivariate analysis for PFS, the presence of ≤2 metastatic sites was an independent factor associated with a longer PFS (p = 0.044). In a multivariate analysis for OS, a trend towards longer OS was observed in patients <60 years old (p = 0.075) and without PI3K pathway mutations (p = 0.067). When treatment was included in the model, IO was an independent factor predicting longer OS compared with chemotherapy-based regimens (p = 0.021) and compared with anti-DDR therapies (p = 0.048) (Table 2).

Patients without DDR alterations

Baseline characteristics

Overall, 553 patients who had molecular profiling did not have DDR alterations. Their baseline characteristics are listed in Table S13. The median age was 60.3 years (range, 21–82 years). Most patients were women (53.7%), Caucasians (79.7%), had an ECOG performance status of 1 (87.5%), and had >2 prior lines of therapy (57.3%). Overall, 43.8% of patients had liver metastases, 10.3% had hypo-albuminemia, and 36.5% had high lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels. PD-L1 expression had a ≥1% tumor proportion score (TPS) in 38.5% of patients with PD-L1 testing, and 6.4% displayed a high TMB (≥10 mutations/Mb). The most common cancer types were colorectal (16.1%), head and neck (15.0%), gastrointestinal (11.4%), and breast (7.8%).

Treatment

Of the 553 patients without DDR alterations, 426 received treatment and were evaluable for response (Table S14). Treatment included IO (n = 76), IO combined with non-IO agents (n = 41), anti–DDR targeted therapies (n = 15), other targeted therapies (n = 187), and chemotherapy-based regimens (n = 107) (Table S5). Clinical outcomes are shown in Table S15. Table S16 compares ORR and CBR between the treatment groups.

Response to treatment

The ORR was higher in the IO group compared with the chemotherapy-based group (p = 0.004). There was a trend towards higher ORR associated with chemotherapy-based combinations compared with other targeted therapies (p = 0.061). IO was associated with higher CBR compared with anti-DDR agents (p = 0.007) and compared with chemotherapy-based combinations (p = 0.017). Additionally, IO+non-IO combinations showed higher CBR compared with anti-DDR agents (p = 0.043). Other targeted therapies had higher CBR compared with anti-DDR agents (p = 0.022) (Table S16).

Progression-free survival

Of the 426 patients, 298 (69.9%) experienced disease progression. The median PFS was 3.7 months (95% CI, 3.2–4.5). Significant differences were noted across treatment types (log-rank p = 0.0012) (Fig. 4A). Patients treated with IO had longer PFS compared with those treated with anti–DDR therapies (p = 0.006), IO + non-IO agents (p = 0.011), and chemotherapy-based regimens (p = 0.026). Patients treated with anti-DDR agents had a trend towards shorter PFS compared with those treated with IO+non-IO (p = 0.071) and chemotherapy-based regimens (p = 0.063). A trend towards shorter PFS was noted with IO+non-IO combination therapy compared with other targeted therapy (p = 0.054) (Table S15).

Clinical outcomes by treatment type in patients without DNA damage response (DDR) molecular alterations (N = 426).

A Progression-free survival (PFS). B Overall survival (OS).

Overall survival

Of the 426 patients without DDR alterations, 360 (84.5%) have died. The median follow-up of surviving patients was 22.15 months (interquartile range, 15.17–35.34 months). The median OS was 9.8 months (95% CI, 8.1–11.2). OS differed significantly across treatment groups (log-rank p = 0.0023) (Fig. 4B). OS was significantly longer in the IO group compared with the chemotherapy-based regimen group (p < 0.001), the anti-DDR therapy group (p < 0.001), the IO+non-IO treatment group (p = 0.002), and the other targeted therapy group (p = 0.003). The IO+non-IO regimen group also had longer OS than the anti-DDR therapy group (p = 0.04). A significant OS benefit was observed for the groups treated with other targeted therapy (p < 0.001) or chemotherapy-based regimens (p = 0.017) compared with the anti-DDR therapy group (Table S15).

Univariate and multivariate analysis

Univariate and multivariate analyzes are shown in Table 3. In the multivariate analysis for PFS, independent factors predicting longer PFS included the absence of liver metastases (p = 0.002), ≤2 metastatic sites (p = 0.005), LDH level < upper limit of normal (ULN) (p = 0.011), and albumin level > lower limit of normal (LLN) (p = 0.002). When treatment was included in the model, treatment with IO had longer PFS compared with chemotherapy-based regimens (p = 0.019), IO+non-IO combinations (p = 0.008), and anti–DDR therapies (p < 0.001).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study investigating the treatment outcomes of patients with advanced cancer with or without DNA repair pathway alterations treated with IO, IO + non-IO combinations, anti-DDR agents, other targeted therapies, or chemotherapy-based regimens across different tumor types. The significant tumor-specific gene enrichment patterns that were noted in the DDR-altered cohort, including PALB2 in breast cancer, CDK12 in prostate cancer, BRCA1 in ovarian cancer, and MSH6/STAG2 in sarcomas, are consistent with previous reports36–38. Gene co-occurrence patterns identified on the same (e.g., CCND1-FGF19, CDKN2A-MTAP, CDK4-MDM2) or on different chromosomes (e.g., PMS2-ATR, KRAS-APC, AR-PTEN) may indicate functional cooperation in complementary biological processes, as reported by other investigators39–42. The co-occurrence of HRR (i.e., MRE11/ATR) with MMR (i.e., PMS2) genes may amplify genomic instability, indicating tumor vulnerabilities leveraged by IO-based combination therapies, as previously suggested43. The mutual exclusivity patterns between TP53 and key HRR genes (e.g., BRCA2, ATM, and CHEK2) should be further investigated, as such patterns have been reported to reflect functional redundancy and biological dependency39–42.

An interesting finding was that in the DDR-positive group, IO was associated with longer OS compared with chemotherapy (p = 0.024) and with a trend towards longer OS compared with anti-DDR agents (p = 0.055). When treatment was included in the model, IO was an independent factor predicting longer OS compared with chemotherapy (p = 0.021) or anti-DDR agents (p = 0.048). Notably, of the 34 patients treated with IO, 2 patients had TMB-high status and 1 had MSI-high status (Table S8). The association of the use of IO with improved OS may be attributed to the presence of an immunogenic tumor microenvironment in genomically unstable tumors38,44–46. In preclinical studies, DNA damage has been linked with immune activation47,48. Cytosolic accumulation of damaged DNA activates the cGAS sensor, which in turn triggers the STING-TBK1-IRF3 cascade, promoting type I interferon and chemokine (CXCL9, CXCL10, CCL5) expression and enhancing T-cell infiltration and PD-L1 upregulation47,48. Other investigators have demonstrated that in BRCA-deficient models of triple-negative breast cancer, the efficacy of PARPi was dependent on CD8+ T-cell recruitment via intra-tumoral STING pathway activation49.

Overall, the poorer PFS and OS in patients treated with anti-DDR therapies compared with other treatments may be, at least partially, attributed to cytotoxicity associated with anti-DDR agents, which limits their prolonged administration. These cytotoxic effects may be enhanced by a reduced bone marrow reserve in this heavily pretreated population, as previously reported for PARPi-treated patients50,51.

Additionally, the antitumor activity of anti-DDR agents alone may be inadequate, at least compared with IO, to overcome the mechanisms of carcinogenesis caused by DDR alterations. Notably, only 12 patients were treated with anti-DDR blockade, including 7 patients who had tumor types with FDA-approved indications for PARPis: breast (n = 1), ovarian (n = 3), prostate (n = 2), and pancreatic (n = 1) cancer. Recent data indicate a differential sensitivity to PARP inhibition according to the HRR gene alteration52,53. In our analysis, the heterogeneity of tumor types and the complexity associated with the relative impact of specific DDR mutations may have negatively influenced the clinical outcomes observed with anti-DDR agents.

Our observations are consistent with previously published data from retrospective studies demonstrating that the use of immune checkpoint inhibition in specific tumor types and DDR-altered subgroups is associated with favorable clinical outcomes54–56. In non-small cell lung cancer patients harboring DDR mutations, treatment with PD-L1 inhibitors with or without CTLA-4 blockade was associated with a higher ORR (30.3% vs 17.2%; p = 0.01), longer PFS (hazard ratio [HR] 0.58, p < 0.001), and longer OS (HR 0.57, p < 0.001) compared with DDR-negative disease57. In metastatic DDR-positive urothelial cancers, anti–PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy achieved a higher ORR (80% vs 18.8%, p < 0.001) and longer PFS and OS (PFS HR 0.20, p < 0.001; OS HR 0.27, p = 0.010) compared with the DDR-negative subgroup55. A meta-analysis of eight trials in mCRPC patients treated with PD-L1 inhibitors with or without CTLA-4 blockade demonstrated that DDR-positive status correlated with improved PFS (OR 0.70; Z = 2.48, p = 0.01) and showed a trend toward longer OS (OR 0.71, Z = 1.38; p = 0.17)58. By pooling data from three independent cohorts of patients with colorectal and gastroesophageal cancers treated with anti–PD-L1 agents with or without anti–CTLA-4 therapy, investigators demonstrated significantly longer median OS in tumors harboring ≥2 DDR mutations compared with <2 DDR mutations56.

The combination of anti-DDR agents with IO therapy is being evaluated in clinical trials59–65. In non-small cell lung cancer, durvalumab plus ceralasertib was associated with an ORR of 26.1%, median PFS of 8.4 months, and median OS of 22.8 months in ATM-mutated tumors64. In ovarian cancer, the addition of dostarlimab to first-line chemotherapy followed by niraparib maintenance resulted in a modest PFS improvement (HR 0.85; 95% CI 0.73–0.99), with no significant benefit for BRCA-mutated patients (HR 0.91; 95% CI 0.59–1.40)59. In gBRCA-mutated breast cancer, the single-arm MEDIOLA study evaluating olaparib plus durvalumab reported encouraging antitumor activity (ORR 63.3%, median PFS 8.2 months, and median OS 21.5 months)60. In another phase 1b trial, after a 21-day rucaparib run-in, the addition of atezolizumab achieved an ORR of 60% in BRCA-mutated ovarian cancer and 40% in the triple-negative breast cancer cohort. Interestingly, post–run-in biopsy analyses from responding patients showed increased PD-L1 expression, enhanced CD8+ T-cell infiltration, and marked activation of the cGAS-STING pathway65. In prostate cancer, pembrolizumab plus olaparib was associated with improved outcomes in the BRCA-mutated subgroup compared with androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (radiologic PFS HR 0.40, 95% CI 0.21–0.79; OS HR 0.52, 95% CI 0.27–0.99)61. Finally, pembrolizumab plus olaparib as maintenance therapy in DDR-altered patients with pancreatic cancer exhibited the greatest activity in the presence of BRCA1/2 or PALB2 mutations (ORR 35%, median PFS 8.3 months, and median OS 28 months)63. Other combinations, including Wee1 or ATR inhibitors plus IO, are under investigation66,67. Furthermore, PARPi have demonstrated in vitro synergistic cytotoxicity with histone deacetylase inhibitors and decitabine in pancreatic, breast, and ovarian cancer cell lines68,69.

An intriguing finding in patients without DDR alterations (N = 426) was the longer OS observed with IO compared with other therapies. Independent factors predicting longer OS were absence of liver metastases and normal albumin and LDH levels. We have previously published that liver metastases are an independent factor predicting shorter OS in patients with advanced cancer35. When treatment was included in the model, IO was an independent factor predicting longer OS compared with all other treatment groups (vs IO+non-IO combinations, p = 0.003; vs chemotherapy-based agents, p < 0.001; vs anti-DDR agents, p < 0.001; and vs other targeted therapies, p = 0.005). The poor outcomes in the DDR-negative group indicate that anti-DDR targeted therapy should not be used in patients whose tumors lack DDR alterations, even in dose-escalation trials of novel anti-DDR agents, as they were used in the current study. Notably, of the 76 patients treated with IO, 51% were PD-L1 positive and 5.3% had TMB-high status. This novel finding of the OS benefit associated with IO therapy in patients without detectable DDR pathway alterations suggests its potential benefit beyond conventional predictors of response, such as PD-L1 positivity and TMB-high status (34.54% and 6.4% of patients, respectively, in our cohort). Mechanisms that include neoantigen burden, inflammatory cytokine signaling, or tumor microenvironmental context have been reported to modulate tumor response to IO70,71, and new biomarkers, including the emerging pro- or anti-immunogenic roles of specific P53 variants72, are being investigated.

Our study’s strengths center on the study population, with consistent inclusion criteria on a formal protocol with informed consent for evaluating relationships between tumor genomics and treatment responses/outcomes. Limitations include the ad-hoc analyses and multiple comparisons (p-values, not adjusted). Given the nature of the study, which involves various tumor types and a plethora of molecular alterations and treatments, caution is needed in the interpretation of the results. The distinct biology associated with individual alterations in the DDR pathways, which continue to be elucidated, cannot be addressed in the current study, which was initiated in 2014. As DDR alterations can confer diverse phenotypes, e.g., CDK12 loss, ATM loss, and ATR loss differ from BRCA1, BRCA2, and PALB2 mutations, they may result in variable drug sensitivities and differential response rates to PARPi. Treatment heterogeneity in the DDR-negative group and subsequent lines of therapy may have affected the OS results, considering that the study included patients treated since 2014. In addition, the risk of false-negative results should be considered, given that different and less-comprehensive next-generation sequencing platforms used across the cohort might have resulted in undetected DDR alterations. Moreover, it cannot be excluded that the benefit observed in the DDR-negative cohort may, at least in part, derive from parallel or interacting pathways not captured within the predefined DDR classification. Notably, the use of 315-, 596-, or 648-gene sequencing panels precludes systematic determination of whether the detected mutations were monoallelic or biallelic (genome-wide copy number data unavailable).

In conclusion, the key novel finding of our study was the differential benefit in OS in patients without a DNA repair pathway alteration treated with some type of immunotherapy. The gene mutation analyses revealed distinct enrichment, co-occurrence, and mutual exclusivity interactions among DDR and non-DDR genes. In addition to anti-DDR agents and chemotherapy, IO may offer clinically meaningful benefits in DDR-altered, heavily pretreated advanced cancers. Keeping in mind that the sample size of the DDR-altered population was small and heterogeneous, these results warrant further investigation in prospective trials with comprehensive molecular characterization to define which specific alterations or pathways hold the greatest clinical relevance. Multi-omic analyses and the use of artificial intelligence/machine learning tools may accelerate the identification of specific biomarkers in patients with and without DDR alterations who will benefit from immunotherapy, optimizing treatment selection.

Methods

Patients

Patients with advanced metastatic cancer across various tumor types who participated in the IMPACT2 study (NCT02152254; registration date, 5/13/2014) were analyzed. Eligible patients had previously treated metastatic cancer, an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0–1, measurable disease, and normal organ function, as previously described73,74. Patients had undergone a tumor biopsy, followed by molecular profiling performed in a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA)-certified laboratory. All patients had signed an informed consent document stating that they were aware of the investigational nature of the study. Patients in the IMPACT2 study were considered for treatment in early-phase clinical trials. Some patients were treated in the dose-escalation parts of phase 1 clinical trials, and therefore, doses of investigational drugs were subtherapeutic. Notably, some clinical trials with DDR-targeted agents did not require DDR alterations for patient enrollment in their dose-escalation part. Patients ineligible for trials received standard-of-care treatment when possible. The treatment was selected by the treating physician, taking into consideration the recommendations of the Molecular Tumor Board, clinical trial availability, patient eligibility for clinical trials, and insurance clearance.

Next-generation sequencing analysis

In the first part of the IMPACT2 study (June 2014 to April 2017), molecular analysis was performed by Foundation Medicine and included a 315-gene panel. In the second part of the study (April 2019 to September 2023), genomic testing was performed by Tempus, using a 596-gene targeted sequencing panel (April 2019 to October 2019) and a 648-gene targeted sequencing panel (November 2019 to September 2023); circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) analysis was also performed by Tempus using a 105-gene or 523-gene panel. Patients underwent immune marker testing (MSI status, TMB status, PD-L1 TPS status) when these tests were available and if tumor tissue was adequate for testing after completion of next-generation sequencing. These tests were unavailable from the beginning of the study.

Annotation of molecular alterations and analysis

Tumor molecular profiling of individual patients was discussed at the Molecular Tumor Board. Briefly, starting in 2015, tumor alterations were annotated by scientists from the MD Anderson Precision Oncology Decision Support team to determine the actionability of DDR alterations. Publicly available databases were used, including Oncology Clinical Trials Network at MD Anderson (OCTANE), Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer (COSMIC), The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), and Oncology Knowledge Base (OncoKB). A total of 27 DDR genes (ATM, ATR, BARD1, BRCA1, BRCA2, BRIP1, CDK12, CHEK1, CHEK2, FANCA, FANCC, FANCD2, FANCE, FANCF, FANCG, FANCL, FANCM, MRE11A, NBN, PALB2, RAD50, RAD51, RAD51B, RAD51C, RAD51D, RAD54L, STAG2) were selected based on their functional roles in DNA damage repair and their reported clinical relevance in prior studies, according to the FDA indications/NCCN guidelines for treatment of DDR biomarkers, trials selecting DDR biomarkers, and/or DDR genes that were designated as therapeutically actionable (PARPi)75–93. Given the biological relevance of MSI in the DDR pathways, patients with MSI-high status were classified within the DDR-positive group. In both the DDR-positive and DDR-negative cohorts, established immunotherapy-related biomarkers, e.g., PD-L1 expression (TPS ≥ 1%) and high TMB (≥10 mutations/Mb), were evaluated when available. Patients with molecular alterations in non-DDR genes were included in the group without DDR alterations. Analyzes of DDR-positive and DDR-negative cohorts were conducted separately in each “treatment” group.

Statistical endpoints

Best response was evaluated through imaging studies conducted every 2 cycles (1 cycle = 3–4 weeks, depending on protocol) by an MD Anderson radiologist, using RECIST 1.194. ORR was the sum of the CR and PR rates. CBR was defined as the sum of the ORR and the rate of SD lasting ≥4 months. PFS was measured from the time of treatment initiation until disease progression or last follow-up. OS was measured from treatment initiation until death from any cause or last follow-up. Patients without events (progression or death) were censored at the time of last follow-up for PFS and OS, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Patients’ characteristics were analyzed using descriptive statistics. Categorical data were described using contingency tables, including counts and percentages. Continuously scaled measures were summarized by median and range. The association between 2 categorical variables was examined using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were <5. Survival functions were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences in survival across treatment groups were assessed using the 2-sided log-rank test. For pairwise survival comparisons in Kaplan–Meier plots, Cox proportional hazards regression models were fit for each 2-treatment comparison, with analyses restricted to patients receiving the 2 treatments being compared. These pairwise (2-arm) Cox models were used to estimate HRs and 95% CIs.

Univariate Cox regression analyzes were initially conducted using the coxph function from the survival package to evaluate associations between PFS or OS and patients’ baseline clinical and demographic characteristics. Covariates analyzed included age (≥60 years vs. <60 years), presence versus absence of liver metastases, low versus normal serum albumin levels, elevated versus normal LDH levels, number of metastatic sites (>2 vs. ≤2), treatment type (with IO as the reference), and presence versus absence of specific gene alterations (e.g., mutations in the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, as follows: PI3K pathway mutations: PIK3CA, PIK3CB, PIK3CD, PIK3CG, PIK3R1, PIK3R2, PIK3R3, PTEN, INPP4B; AKT pathway mutations: AKT1, AKT2, AKT3, PDPK1, TSC1, TSC2; mTOR pathway mutations: MTOR, RPTOR, RICTOR, MLST8, DEPTOR). HR greater than 1 indicated an association with a worse prognosis, whereas an HR less than 1 indicated a better prognosis.

Covariates with a p-value ≤ 0.2 in the univariate analyzes were subsequently selected for inclusion in the multivariate Cox regression analysis. Before constructing the multivariate models, multicollinearity among covariates was evaluated using the vif function from the car package, while the strength of association between covariates was quantified using Cramér’s V, calculated via the assocstats function in the vcd R package. Variables without strong collinearity or substantial strength of association were included in the final multivariate Cox regression models, which were then fitted using backward stepwise selection based on likelihood ratio tests. For tumor-type-specific gene enrichment analysis, the frequency of each gene alteration in a specific tumor type was compared to the frequency in all other tumor types using Fisher’s exact test. The clinical Enrichment function from the maftools package was employed for the comparisons between the patient groups. Additionally, the somatic Interactions function from the same package was utilized to identify mutually exclusive or co-occurring gene alterations, also using pairwise Fisher’s exact tests.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Conference on Harmonization of Good Clinical Practice guidelines. All IMPACT2 study participants had provided written informed consent before enrollment stating that they were aware of the investigational nature of the study. The applicable study protocol with assigned number PA12-1161 was approved by the Institutional Review Board at The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center on May 13, 2014 (Registration Number: IRB 1 IRB00000121) and was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02152254).
