Enhancing craniofacial bone tissue engineering strategy: integrating rapid wet chemically synthesised bioactive glass with photopolymerized resins
Authors: Syed Saad Bin Qasim, Asma Tufail Shah, Umer Daood, Maha Matalqah, Salma Habib, Khaled M Saoud
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCraniofacial bone regeneration represents a dynamic area within tissue engineering and regenerative medicine. Central to this field, is the continual exploration of new methodologies for template fabrication, leveraging established bio ceramic materials, with the objective of restoring bone integrity and facilitating successful implant placements.MethodsPhotopolymerized templates were prepared using three distinct bio ceramic materials, specifically a wet chemically synthesized bioactive glass and two commercially sourced hydroxyapatite variants. These templates underwent comprehensive characterization to assess their physicochemical and mechanical attributes, employing techniques including Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, scanning electron microscopy, and nano-computed tomography. Evaluation of their biocompatibility was conducted through interaction with primary human osteoblasts (hOB) and subsequent examination using scanning electron microscopy.ResultsThe results demonstrated that composite showed intramolecular hydrogen bonding interactions with the photopolymer, while computerized tomography unveiled the porous morphology and distribution within the templates. A relatively higher porosity percentage (31.55 ± 8.70%) and compressive strength (1.53 ± 0.11 MPa) was noted for bioactive glass templates. Human osteoblast cultured on bioactive glass showed higher viability compared to other specimens. Scanning micrographs of human osteoblast on templated showed cellular adhesion and the presence of filopodia and lamellipodia.ConclusionIn summary these templates have the potential to be used for alveolar bone regeneration in critical size defect. Photopolymerization of bioceramics may be an interesting technique for scaffolds fabrication for bone tissue engineering application but needs more optimization to overcome existing issues like the ideal ratio of the photopolymer to bioceramics.Keywords: Bioactive glass, Hydroxyapatite, Photopolymer, Characterization, Bone regeneration

Introduction

The field of bone tissue engineering (BTE) was initiated almost three decades ago. The field requires a multidisciplinary approach where by researchers of different scientific background combine their mutual interests with the ultimate goal of creating bone grafts that enhance bone repair and regeneration [1]. At present, bone is thought to be the one of the most frequent types of transplanted tissue after blood transfusion [2]. Over 2.2 million bone grafting procedures are conducted every year [3]. Although metallic implants are used in several clinical scenarios there are certain drawbacks associated with them, such as lack of biometric recognition on the surface of the implant, variations in the mechanical properties in between the metal biomaterial and natural bone triggering stress shielding and eventually loosening of the implant [4]. Therefore, alternative approaches and biomimetic biomaterials are being explored that can be upscaled for BTE applications.

In order to overcome these hurdles and devise a scalable strategy for fabrication of templates for BTE, novel synthesis routes are being investigated by researchers across the globe [5]. Well investigated bioceramics like, hydroxyapatite (HA), beta Tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP) and bioactive glass (BAG) along with their composite formulations with natural and synthetic polymers have shown promising results [6]. HA is the main component of bone and is widely accepted as an artificial alternative to natural bone due to its excellent biocompatibility and osteogenic potential [7]. BAG is the third generation of biomaterials developed by Larry Hench in late 1960 with the tendency to activate genes that stimulate regeneration of living tissues. The first commercially available BAG consisted of 46.1 mol% Silicon dioxide, (SiO2), 24.4 mol % Sodium oxide (Na2O), 26.9 mol % Calcium oxide (CaO) and 2.6 mol% Phosphorus pentaoxide (P2O5) and was called 45S5 or Bioglass® [8]. They have the ability to chemically bond with existing bone [9]. Although these bioceramics have excellent compatibility a drawback which is commonly found in such materials is their poor mechanical properties [7]. Strategies to improve the mechanical properties whilst keeping the biocompatibility is an area of interest for researchers working on tissue engineering and regenerative medicine. Amongst the number of different synthesis techniques available for BAG fabrication sol-gel method had been reported extensively in the literature [10]. It was adapted to produce BAG with less crystallinity, greater porosity and surface area with enhanced bioactivity [11]. However, the preparation process is tedious, and the production cost is high [12]. Another interesting strategy for BAG synthesis that has gained traction more recently is the use of ultrasonication [13]. Ultrasound has been adapted for synthesis of different nanomaterials with unique properties. Sonochemistry has been reported to be an efficient method for rapid and facile synthesis of a variety of novel nanomaterials. Reports on ultrasound coupled with sol-gel method for synthesis of BAG are also emerging [14]. The application of ultrasound to the aqueous sol gel reaction medium leads to the formation of ultra hot spots. These spots rapidly initiate primary reactions involving production of H and -OH free radicals from H2O molecules in the reaction medium [15].

Whilst investigators explore novel methodologies to synthesize BAG, another area of interest has been the use of different methodologies for template fabrication using newly developed biomaterials. More specifically photopolymerization technology has shown promising results. HA combined with photocurable liquid polymer, such as polypropylene fumarate has been printed in the presence of low power ultraviolet (UV) light source at a wavelength of 405 nm [16]. The use of such photocurable resin’s materials has been exploited to create complex 3D structures. Amongst the liquid resin, acrylate have gained traction due to its high transparency and accuracy as well as low cost [17]. Spectroscopic studies of these components have revealed that there are chemical bond formation in between the bioceramics and photopolymer. The possible mechanisms reported from the literature are intra molecular hydrogen bonding due to the presence of hydrogen atoms in both substrates [18].The potential of methacrylate polymers to bond with HA is due to either electrostatic interactions or covalent bonding has also been reported previously [19].

Therefore, the aim of the current study was to synthesize bioceramics templates using wet chemically synthesized BAG, commercially available micro and nano-HA using photopolymerization. And to characterize the physical, chemical, and biological properties of the fabricated templates using primary human osteoblast. We hypothesize that bioceramics templates fabricated using ultrasonically synthesized BAG, commercially available micro and nano-HA via photopolymerization will exhibit enhanced physical, chemical and biological properties for bone tissue engineering compared to traditional scaffolds fabricated using the conventional techniques.

Materials and methods

Materials

Two commercially available hydroxyapatite were used. Microcrystalline HA powder (uHA) (Clarkson Chromatography products, Australia) HA 3 wt%. (1:2 PVA)). Hydroxyapatite (nanoXIM-Care Paste, FLUIDNOVA, SA, Maisa Portugal) (nHA)was used at 3wt %. It is made up of 15.5 wt% HA nanoparticles dispersed in water with an average particle size of < 50 nm and potassium bromide content of 4.5 wt %. The HA paste was heated in an oven at 500 ° C for 24 h to remove any impurities and the powder was then used in combination with the photopolymer. Bioactive glass (BAG) synthesized and characterized previously by Shah et al. (Shah et al., 2023) was used in the current investigation. Briefly, Silica based BAG with composition 70SiO2:25CaO:5P2O5 was prepared by using ultrasonication irradiated base catalysed sol-gel method. Precursors used to synthesize BAG were, tetraethyl orthosilicate, di-ammonium hydrogen phosphate and calcium nitrate tetrahydrate. The prepared BAG was homogeneous with narrow particle size distribution ~ 250 microns. BAG was calcined at 650 °C to get glassy structure with BET surface area 200 m2.g− [20]. All together three groups were prepared, µHA, BG and nHA.

Template fabrication

The BG and HA polyacrylate composites were synthesized using a photopolymerization procedure. In the atypical procedure, 0.7 g PVA was dissolved in 10 mL of 70% ethanol/water mixture, subjected to heat in a water bath, and stirred at 60 °C for 1 h. The solution was covered with aluminium foil. Then 0.35 g of BAG or material and solution was kept under continuous stirring. The solution was set to cool and then subjected to ultrasonication for 30 min to ensure full distribution of BAG, HA and uHA in the PVA solution. Finally, 3 mL (11 g) of FLGPCL04 photopolymer (density = 3.877 g/cm3) (Formlabs™ Somerville, Massachusetts, United States) was stirred for 1 h at room temperature until the mixture became transparent. The final solution was poured into 5 and 20-ml pre-cut syringes and exposed to 405 nm UV light for 1 h for cross-linking. The ratio between the particles and the polymer was determined based on the weight%. Our ratio was 3 wt% to achieve 3% loading of the particles. To study the effect of the sample treatment, we prepared two different samples; the first sample (Sample A) was left to dry at room temperature for 24 h, while the second (Sample B) was subjected to solvent exchange by immersing the sample in ultra-pure water (18 Mohm) for 24 h. We find that sample B had fewer impurities and was less dense. Small disks were made after taking out the samples and cutting them into small circles. Drying at room temperature and solvent exchange were applied throughout the experiment. The density of the samples was calculated using the formula of a cylinder volume (v) = and, where (g/cm 3), m = mass (g), v = volume (cm 3).

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was performed in attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode (GladiATR™) accessory from Pike technologies (Fitchburg, Wisconsin, USA) with a Bruker Tensor 27 FTIR spectrometer (Bruker Optics Ltd, Ettlingen, Germany). A blank background was acquired prior to analysing the specimens. The powder and lyophilized BAG, uHA and nHA samples were placed in close contact with the monolithic diamond ATR crystal. Spectra data were recorded in mid infrared range of 4000–400 cm− 1. Total 16 scans were collected at a resolution of 4 cm− 1 which were then analyzed using OPUS software (Version 6.5, Bruker Optik Germany). Data was exported as .csv and OMNIC software (Version8.2, Thermo Scientific Waltham, MA, USA) was used for peak analysis.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy

The surface chemical composition of the photopolymerized templated were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). XPS data were collected from ESCA Lab 250Xi (Thermo scientific, UK) instrument equipped with a monochromator excitation source of Al Kα (1486.5 eV and spot size 850 mm), Argon ion gun and flood gun neuralization sources. To avoid and reduce impurities contamination a small piece of each specimen was placed in customized metal sample holders. The produced spectra were processed and deconvoluted with Thermo Avantage software (v5.956). The survey spectra were collected in the wide range of 0-1300 eV at 150 eV pass energy, 50 ms dwell time and 1 eV step size, while the high-resolution narrow scans were performed at 20 eV pass energy, 50 ms dwell time and 0.1 eV step size for a different number of scans depending on the intensity of the peaks.

Scanning electron microscopy

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed under high vacuum to image ultra structure morphology of the powders. All specimens were sputtered with gold (Structure Probe Inc, West Chester PA) and mounted on aluminium stub with a carbon sticker for the surface and bulk characterization through scanning electron microscope (JEOL, JSM-5410LV, Tokyo, Japan). Images were acquired at an accelerating voltage of 15.0 kV and working distance of 11.1 mm. Multiple magnifications were used to capture the SEM images.

Nano-computerized tomography

Scanning of samples comprising of uHA, BAG and nHA was conducted with the help of Phoenix Nanotom® (GE Sensing & Inspection Technologies Ltd, Germany). Aluminium Filter of 0.5 mm was used before the lens of camera. Isotropic resolution of 4.44 μm per voxel at 100 kV accelerating voltage was set to collect the scanned images. Images were collected through 360° rotation of samples along with vertical plane. 2000 images were obtained in a duration of 2 h time. Data for reconstruction of images and porosity measurements was performed through VGS Studio max (Version 3.3). GraphPad Prism (version 8) was used to analyze diameter size of pores (µm) and graphs for frequency distribution were obtained.

Mechanical characterization

Uniaxial compressive strength of the prepared templates was conducted in dry conditions using an ElectroPuls System (E300 Instron, Norwood, MA, SA) for all three groups. The machine was equipped with a 1 kN load cell at a crosshead loading speed of 0.5 mm/ min. The samples were positioned on a flat steel base and loaded by a hemispherical stainless-steel piston, positioned in the center of the occlusal surface of the circular specimens. The test was stopped when the specimen fractured, which was detected through sound and visual perception. The specimen was retrieved from the machine and the failure was examined under illumination. From the obtained stress-strain curves the point at which the samples fractured was used to calculate the failure load which was tabulated in megapascals (MPa). Three specimens of each group were examined (15 mm in diameter and 4 mm thickness). Similar scaffolds were used to perform diametral tensile strength (S) according to ASTM standard (D3967-08). In the course of the test although the specimens were subjected to compressive load, the maximum tensile force would be developed across perpendicular surface diameters to the force’s direction. The formula used to calculate the tensile strength was, S = 2 F/ 𝜋Dt, where F is failure load (N), D and t (mm) are the diameter and thickness of the cylindrical scaffold respectively [21].

Vickers hardness

To perform Vickers hardness, the synthesized templates were grinded and polished to 1000 grit size. Microhardness was conducted using a Vickers Microhardness Indenter (Shimadzu, Japan). Testing was performed using a load of 100 g and a holding time of 15 s, after the load was removed the indentation diagonals were measures and hardness (HV) was calculated using the previously reported formula [22].

Primary human osteoblast culture

Human Osteoblasts (hOB; InSCREENeX Ltd, Braunschweig, Germany) at passage 8 were cultured in 75 cm3 (T75) cell culture flasks using hOB cell basal medium (INS-ME01006, InSCREENeX Ltd, Braunschweig, Germany). Fresh culture media was changed every 2 to 3 days and splitting of the hOB was done at 70–90% confluency. The split ratio of the hOB after the recovery from cryopreservation did not exceed 1:2. HOB were kept in a humified atmosphere inside an incubator (Cell Xpert®, Eppendorf SE, Hamburg, Germany) maintained at a temperature of 37 °C with 5% CO2. During passaging hOB were detached from the 75 cm3 cell culture flask using 2.5 mL of 0.05% trypsin–EDTA (Gibco® Life technologies, Waltham, MA, USA). The flask was stored for few minutes in the incubator until the hOB got detached and then equal amount of fresh culture media was added. Consequently, the hOB was centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 3 minutes (min). The cell pellet was resuspended in fresh media and hOB were then transferred to a new T75 flask [23].

To study the viability of hOB on HA scaffolds, hOB was seeded at a density of 15,000 per sample using an autoclaved marine grade stainless steel seeding ring (internal Ø 10 mm). All specimens were sterilized before cell seeding in 70% ethanol for 1 h followed by two phosphate buffered saline (PBS: Dulbecco’s phosphate buffered saline; Gibco™ Waltham, MA, USA) washes for 15 min each. An amount of 500 µl of fresh media were poured outside the metal rings and after that 700 µl were poured inside the steel rings to fully submerge the specimens. With this procedure, an equilibrium balance was achieved between the liquids inside each wall. The hOB was then seeded centrally in the steel rings. Culture media was changed every 3 days. Cells seeded in tissue culture plate served as positive control and wells with no cells served as a negative control.

Alamar blue assay

To quantify cell attachment and viability a fluorescent measurement of resazurin sodium salt (RSS, Stem cell technologies™, Vancouver, BC, Canada) was performed on day 4th, 7th and 10th using a concentration of 0.1 mM in CBM. For each time point hOB seeded specimens were carefully washed with PBS and 1 ml of RSS diluted 1:10 with PBS, was added followed by incubation at 37° C for 2 h (h). 200 µl of pink resorufin byproduct was taken to a 96-well plate, and measured spectrofluorometrically (CLARIOstar®, BMG Labtech, Ortenberg, Germany) at excitation 540/35, and emission 6of 30/32nm. Fluorescence was measured at 450 nm using a fluorescent plate reader (VANTAstar, BMG Labtech Ltd, Ortenberg, Germany).

Based on the metabolic activity, the system incorporated an oxidation-reduction indicator (REDOX) that both fluoresces and changes colour due to the chemical reduction of the culture media caused from cell growth. The reduction related to growth caused the REDOX indicator to change from oxidized (blue) to reduced (red) form. Once the measurements were taken, specimens were washed with PBS and fresh media was added. Consequently, the hOB was cultured further until the next time point of day 7 and 10. The results were expressed as fluorescence unit (a.u.). Consequently, after the 10th day readings specimens were fixed using 2.5% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 15 min. They were washed with PBS twice for 15 min and then dehydrated using increasing concentration of ethanol solutions, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90% and 100%. Subsequently critical point drying (CPD) was conducted, and the specimens were sputter coated with gold (Structure Probe, West Chester, PA, USA) and viewed under the Scanning Electron microscope (JEOL, JSM-IT200 InTouchScope™ Tokyo, Japan) at different magnifications. The images were captured at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV. The raw images were then transferred to ImageJ software (National Institute of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA) for further analysis.

Collagen analysis

The collagen deposited by hOB seeded on the synthesized templates was examined by Picro-Sirius Red staining. hOBs were cultured for till 21 days and collagen deposition was quantified on day 14 and 21. The culture media was removed, and the specimens were rinsed with PBS. They were then fixed with 3.7% formaldehyde for 30 min and washed again with PBS for 10 min. A Picro-Sirius Red solution (50% Direct Red dye 81 (1 mg/mL) in saturated picric acid (1.2% w/v), Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was poured on the templates, fully submerging the specimens, and the solution was left for 18 h under mild rocking (20 rpm). Then, excess dye was washed off with distilled water and the templates were destained with 1 mL 0.2 M NaOH and methanol 1:1; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 15 min for quantitative analysis. Absorbance at 490 nm was read in a 96-well plate reader. Well plates with culture media served as negative controls and hOBs seeded on tissue culture plastic served as positive controls [24].

Calcium analysis

The calcium deposited by hOBs seeded on synthesized templates was examined by 1% Alizarin Red (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) staining. The specimens were cultured for 14 and 21 days, the culture media was ret, and retrieved, the samples were rinsed with PBS. The cultures were then fixed with 3.7% formaldehyde for 30 min and washed again with PBS for 10 min. Alizarin Red solution (1 mL) was poured onto the templates, fully submerging the specimens, and the solution was left for 20 min. The unbound dye was removed by washing with distilled water. For quantification, the stain was extracted using 1 mL 5% perchloric acid (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MS, USA) for 30 min. Absorbance at 405 nm was read. Well plates with culture media alone served as negative controls whilst hOBs seeded on tissue culture plastic served as positive controls [24].

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise states all experiments were conducted in triplicated. Cell Culture data was analysed in GraphPad Prism® software version 8.0.2 (GraphPad software Ltd, San Diego, California, USA). The assumption of homogeneity of variance and normal distribution was checked using Levene’s and Shapiro Wilks test. One way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey’s multiple comparison were used if the variances between materials were equal.

Results

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

The chemical characterization of HA and BAG powders along with the photopolymerized templates was performed by FTIR spectroscopy. Characteristic peaks of HA in the fingerprint region were observed. The phosphate (PO43−) vibrations of ʋ1, ʋ3 and ʋ4 were observed at 961, 1028 and 627 cm− 1 respectively. Hydroxyl stretch on the lattice site of the HA crystal was noted at 3569 cm− 1 respectively (Fig. 1A). The BAG spectra showed a broad band in the range of 3600 to 3000 cm− 1 which corresponds to -OH group of adsorbed water. The absorption spectral bands at 2973 cm− 1 were attributed to asymmetrical and symmetrical stretching modes of -CH3. The characteristic peak of Si-O-Si was noted at 947 cm− 1 and 1070 cm− 1. Other peak at 812 cm− 1 is correlated to silicate bonds for Si-O-Si bending mode. The band at 1467 cm− 1 is related to residual carbonate (Fig. 1A). The uHA spectra showed micro carbonate substitution of B-Type (CO32− for PO43−) which was observed due to the presence of peaks corresponding to bending and stretching modes at 871 cm− 1 and 1639 –1300 cm− 1. The spectral data of the photopolymerized specimens is shown in Fig. 1B. Observations from the fingerprint region reveal that a sharp intensity peak at 1707 cm− 1 was noted and attributed to C = O stretch. At C-O-C (methacrylate) asymmetric stretch was noted at 1247 cm− 1. The oscillation of the ester group is noted at 1145 cm− 1, the signal at 779 cm− 1 corresponding to H-C = R has a decreased intensity in the spectral data of nHA. These signals along with the overtone from C = O stretch at 3360 cm− 1 in the composite templates confirmed that the polymer is a methacrylic acid ester.

Three-dimensional (3D) nano computerized tomography (Nano-CT) was conducted to characterize the ultrastructure morphology of the photopolymerized templates. Representative two-dimensional (2D) Nano-CT images are shown in Fig. 4 which have been reconstructed after scanning the specimens with the parameters mentioned earlier. Quantification of the porosity parameters like defect volume (µm3), defect volume ratio (%) and material volume (µm3) was also analyzed (Table 2) along with porosity distribution analysis (Fig. 5). The representative images displayed in different planes reveal interesting findings of the photopolymerized templates. BAG showed homogenous pore distribution observed throughout the specimen. These pores were evenly distributed with larger quantity of pores seen at the periphery of the templates. The centre part of the template exhibited lesser pores (Fig. 4A-C). The uHA displayed lesser pore as compared to BG and a solid ring of material was observed which had a clear boundary. This was a consistent finding in all three specimens as a ring was observed and a somewhat preferential pore distribution was noted around the periphery of each material. nHA also showed solid core with pores limited to the border of the polymerized template. (Fig. 4G, H, and I). Most of the pores were rounded in shape and displayed a micro porosity gradient. The porosity parameters analysed showed a higher defect volume ratio (%) in BAG (31.55 ± 8.70%) compared to nHA of. The frequency distribution analysis showed that BAG had more macro pores. A larger quantity observed in this were less than 50 μm in size (Fig. 5). The porosity histograms and defect volume analysis also revealed that the pore shape was deviated from a perfect sphere. The distribution analysis showed that there were more small diameter pores and less large diameter pores of diameter over 100 μm.

Discussion

Bone graft substitutes are commonly used in craniofacial surgery and autografts are the most viable option due to the high biological acceptance post implantation. However, certain drawbacks associated with them are limited availability, donor site morbidity and increased procedural costs. This has led to the reservations over the use of autografts and investigators are now looking other synthetic viable alternatives [25]. The aim of this study was to synthesize photopolymerized craniofacial bone graft substitutes using bioactive glass produced by a wet synthesis and two different commercially available hydroxyapatite and to assess their physico-chemical, mechanical and biological properties using primary human osteoblasts. Photopolymerization of bioactive bone scaffolds have garnered significant interest from interdisciplinary researchers in recent years [26]. In this study, we chose to compare microcrystalline hydroxyapatite (HA) powder and standard HA powder with BAG. The selection of two forms of HA was driven by the need to understand how variations in physiochemical properties affect their biological and material properties. HA is renowned for its biocompatibility and osteoconductivity, making it a focal point for optimizing bone regeneration material. BAG was included as a comparative material due to its unique bioactivity and ion release properties, providing a distinct contrast to HA. Four main three-dimensional printing technique fall under photopolymerization technique, these are namely continuous liquid interface production (CLIP), daylight polymer printing (DPP) stereolithography (SLA) and digital light processing (DLP). Furthermore this additive manufacturing technique can be harnessed to tailor make templates for load bearing applications [27].

To understand the chemical bonding and functional group interactions of bioceramics powders and photopolymerized templates FTIR was performed in an ATR mode. In ATR mode, width, intensity and area of bands in the mineral was reported to have an explicit dependence on the particle size. It was noted that the intensity and area of IR bands decline and the width of bands increased with the increment of particle size [28]. Furthermore, the same study mentioned that although the intensity enhances with decline of particle size, very minute particle sizes will lead to decreased intensity due to the wavelength and penetration depth of the IR light [29]. This was not observed in the current study since nHA had a higher intensity of the band which could be due to high crystallinity. The spectral data that was also in agreement to the studies conducted previously as well [9, 30]. Composite spectra also revealed the peaks denoted to the commercially available polymer.

The nano-CT analysis revealed interesting findings. The technique is based on its potential to provide enlarged sections of the specimens as set in the equipment software. Hence, what can virtually be seen as “voids” cannot be considered pores as they actually build the bioceramics matrix [31]. Since the bone regeneration is based on the scaffold’s intricate geometries and the aim of the study was to assess the feasibility of using a photopolymer for template fabrication. High resolution nano-CT scans revealed that after polymerization pores or voids were accumulating around the periphery of the specimens with highest number of pores in BAG specimen. These pores which were interconnected could serve as a better substrate for cellular attachment, proliferation, and mineralized matrix deposition. The heterogenous pores after curing the templates could be due to the removal of the mixture of water and ethanol used with the polymer for dispersing the bioceramics. It is interesting to find that such emulsions of bioceramics (BAG, nHA and uHA) with PVA and photopolymer can be utilized to synthesize ceramic templates by simple dilution and rapid curing via 405 nm UV light. There is morphology, orientation, interconnectivity and heterogeneity all play a vital role in bone regeneration. Moreover, the porosity gradient in biomaterials is able to mimic the properties of natural bone whereby smaller pores as able to transfer nutrients and wastes to the highly porous region [32].Whilst porosities are often desired in a scaffold, lower porosity observed in the current study indicate a denser structure which might contribute to improved mechanical stability more specifically in load bearing applications. When assessing the mechanical properties of bone scaffolds, it is essential to consider different factors. Although various tests have been established to asses such properties, the most frequently used methods for scaffold assessment are tensile and compressive strength [33]. Due to the differences in the mechanical properties and ultrastructure architecture of cortical and cancellous bone it is challenging to fabricate an “ideal bone scaffold” [34]. The higher BAG values could be due to the BAG scaffolds being more conducive to load bearing and stress distribution compared to uHA and nHA specimens. Moreover, the effect of porosity on the mechanical performance had an indirect proportionality. Where BAG with higher porosity showed higher compressive strength. It also suggests the BAG had a stronger bond with the photopolymer used leading to enhanced densification. The weaker mechanical results from tensile strength analysis of uHA and nHA may also suggest that porosities might not be the cause of the mechanical failure alone. The presence of microcracks along with pores could lead to the results observed in the current study. These are denoted as weak points and possibilities of a crack initiation or fracture. Scaffolds fabricated by lithography using beta-tricalcium phosphate showed a range of variations in the mechanical output (1.4 to 59 MPa) [35]. Moreover, the porosity distribution is non-homogenous. The porosity distribution serves as a driving factor for hardness. With respect to BAG specimens, higher values were noted. This could be due to an increment in the number of glassy pockets or a stable bond between the photopolymer and BAG thereby providing that hinder the penetration of Vickers indenter. Even with higher porosity a strong, interconnected network can affect hardness. Although the pores within uHA and nHA were comparatively less, their hardness, this could be due to the inherent brittleness and lower fracture toughness. Several studies have reported different mechanical characterization.

Therefore, a gradient pore may mimic the light spongy cancellous bone. The BAG specimen showed higher porosity percentage however nHa showed a gradient porosity with more small pores. The Alamar blue results showed an increment in viability for all templates over the course of the culture period for 10 days. The osteogenic potential of BAG, uHA and nHA templates have been assessed previously. The deviations in the observed results from Alamar blue assay may be due to biomaterials specific interactions. The size of osteoblasts is on the order of 10–50 μm, however they preferentially adhere to larger pores (100–200 μm) in order to regenerate mineralized bone tissue after implantation [36]. Although the porosity percentage was from 11 to 15% in the current study, reports in the past have shown that microporous HA with 12% porosity adsorbed 10 fold more proteins, especially fibronectin, and albumin [37]. The typical bone is composed of HA crystals deposited within an organic matrix. The morphology is composed of trabecular bone which creates porous environment of 50–90% porosity, with cortical bone surrounding it. Whereas, cortical bone has an inherent solid structure with a series of voids, for example haversian canals that result in 3–12% porosity [38]. Cortical bone constitutes upto 80% of the weight of the human skeleton and is stronger than cancellous bone [39]. While native tissue is naturally porous, these pores are not homogenously distributed. Bone tissue have four different levels of pore sizes that differ in function from topological cures to facilitating bone ingrowth [40]. The templates produced in the current study have variations in the porous and non-porous segments these can be attributed to several reason. The curing dynamics of photopolymer resin, its dept of curing, material homogeneity, variations in the temperature gradient and shrinkage stress distribution as the photopolymer cures. Cellular studies have also reported that a significant role is played by the matrix or substrate stiffness in affecting cellular decisions and tissue development through tissue engineering approached [41]. Therefore, a porous segment may be more conducive to osteogenic differentiation and vascularization.

In has been observed that an increment in bone formation depends on an increase in extracellular matrix synthesis. Bone type-I collagen synthesis is known to be up-regulated at the proliferation stage when the osteoblasts are not confluent and down regulated at subsequent stages [42]. Collagen and calcium deposition analysis results observed in the current investigation suggest that the osteoblast was in active phase of proliferation and triggering bone extracellular matrix deposition. Specifically on BG samples followed by uHA and nHA. Similarly quantitative analysis of calcium mineral content showed that hOB was able to deposit higher amount on BG specimens. This behaviour of BG templates can be correlated to the release of ionic dissolution products, whereby additional soluble silica (released as silicic acid) and phosphate released from BG can further enhance osteogenesis. Silica has been reported to play a pivotal physiological role in osteoblast proliferation, osteogenesis and bone calcification [43]. The osteoblasts showed adherence to the templates fabricated in the current study. In general, cellular attachment is mediated by specific molecules such as integrins at specific binding sites. On initial attachment to a substrate cell explores its microenvironment and migrates using nanometre scale processes such as filopodia and lamellipodia. Which were observed in the electron micrographs of the current study as well. Collectively cells showed a rounded morphology and attachment was noted on all templates. While the current study provides a preliminary evaluation of the potential of our templates for promoting bone regeneration, we recognize the importance of validating these findings using a relevant critical-sized alveolar bone defect model. We plan to conduct pore structure modification studies to achieve a more homogenous distribution, in-vitro cell distribution studies, in-vivo studies to assess the biological performance in a more complex physiological environment and to provide empirical data supporting the effectiveness of the synthesized templates. Additionally, a comprehensive degradation study of the templates will supplement this data. Degradation studies on bioceramics scaffolds suggest that β-TCP has a faster degradation which cannot match the growth rate of new bone tissue [44]. Whereas HA and BAG exhibit slower degradation rates. BAG undergoes resorbability and this depends on the silica contents and network connectivity. It is characterized to undergo leaching, dissolution and precipitation of ions. The BAG used in this work was prepared using a modified sol-gel method mainly by rapid wet chemical synthesis. In vivo studies have shown that BAG showed around 30 wt% weight loss in 10 days supplemented with new bone formation in ≥ 21 days [45]. This future research will be crucial in verifying the regenerative potential of our biomaterials in clinical settings. Some of the other limitations of the current study that need further investigation are the use of other forms of bioceramics, parameters related to sedimentation of bio ceramic fillers and blockage of the UV light during photopolymerization. The ideal ratio of the polymer and ceramic fillers need further optimization to achieve characteristics that may serve as in ideal scaffolds for bone tissue engineering for craniofacial applications.

Conclusion

In this study, porous bio ceramic scaffolds were prepared using bioactive glass synthesized using rapid wet chemical synthesis, micro, and nano hydroxyapatite via photopolymerization. The templates exhibited porosity, high compressive strength and hardness. Encouraging positive cellular response supplemented by collagen and calcium deposition assay, indicating their potential to deposit mineralized matrix. These templates show promise for bone regeneration; however, further optimization is necessary to enhance their porosity for broader applications in bone engineering. Therefore, despite its convenience, it requires further exploration and tailoring to develop customized templates for personalized medicine.
