Synthesis of Two-Dimensional (Cu–S)n Metal–Organic Framework Nanosheets Applied as Peroxidase Mimics for Detection of Glutathione
Authors: Yuan-Hsiang Yu, Xiao-Yuan Lin, Kun-Ling Teng, Wei-Fan Lai, Chia-Chi Hu, Chia-Hsuan Tsai, Ching-Ping Liu, Hui-Ling Lee, Cing-Huei Su, Yen-Hsiang Liu, Kuang-Lieh Lu, Su-Ying Chien
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractFacilely synthesized peroxidase-like nanozymes with high catalytic activity and stability may serve as effective biocatalysts. The present study synthesizes peroxidase-like nanozymes with multinuclear active sites using two-dimensional (2D) metal–organic framework (MOF) nanosheets and evaluates them for their practical applications. A simple method involving a one-pot bottom-up reflux reaction is developed for the mass synthesis of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets, significantly increasing production quantity and reducing reaction time compared to traditional autoclave methods. The (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets with the unique coordination of Cu(I) stabilized in Cu-based MOFs demonstrate impressive activity in mimicking natural peroxidase. The active sites of the peroxidase-like activity of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets were predominantly verified as Cu(I) rather than Cu(II) of other Cu-based MOFs. The cost-effective and long-term stability of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets make them suitable for practical applications. Furthermore, the inhibition of the peroxidase-like activity of (Cu–S)n MOF nanosheets by glutathione (GSH) could provide a simple strategy for colorimetric detection of GSH against other amino acids. This work remarkably extends the utilization of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets in biosensing, revealing the potential for 2D (Cu–S)n MOFs.

Introduction

Nanozymes serve as biocatalysts by mimicking natural enzymes (e.g., horseradish peroxidase (HRP)).1 Because of their nanoscale effects, surface effects, and high activity, nanozymes have been widely used in the chemical (biofuels, catalysts, and sensors) and medical (medicine) industries.2−9 Several nanozymes, such as Fe3O4,10 CeO2,11 Au nanoparticles,12 porous carbon nanospheres,13 organic–inorganic nanomaterials,14−16 and metal–organic frameworks (MOFs),17 function as peroxidase nanozymes. However, some peroxidase mimics exhibit poor catalytic activity. Moreover, the process of peroxidase-like nanozyme synthesis is generally complicated. Therefore, peroxidase-like nanozymes with high catalytic activity and stability, which can be synthesized through facile synthesis, are urgently required.

MOFs are ordered polymer networks of crystalline organic–inorganic hybrid materials comprising inorganic metallic ions coordinated by diverse organic ligands. Because of their large specific surface areas, structural tunability, and multitudinous functionalities, MOFs are widely used in gas separation and storage, chemical sensing, biosensing, and catalysis.18−22 However, for MOFs to be effectively applied, some problems must be addressed, such as their long reaction times with high temperature and pressure requirements, scale-up-related challenges, and low product yields.23 By incorporating transition metals and coordination ligands into the structure and topology of 2D MOFs, various 2D nanosheet properties may be generated. The 2D MOF nanosheets are comparable to other 2D materials, such as graphene, graphitic carbon nitride, boron nitride, transition metal phosphides, and pnictogen materials.24−30 In industrial applications, the essential properties of 2D nanomaterials, such as their high aspect ratios, large specific surface areas, nanosheet morphologies, and multiple properties along the nanosheet planes, can yield devices with superior electrical, optical, mechanical, thermal, and barrier properties compared with those made from bulk materials.31,32 Additionally, 2D MOF nanosheets have multiple metallic centers and more accessible active sites on the surface, leading to more possibilities for interactions between substrate molecules and active sites.33,34 Since their inception by Kobayashi and co-workers,35 charge is delocalized, and the 2D MOF develops conductive properties when efficient π-conjugation occurs in the organic linkers and the coordination nodes of 2D MOF; conductive MOFs have been receiving considerable attention and used in electronics, optoelectronic devices, sensors, and energy conversion and storage devices.36−42 In addition, 2D MOF semiconductors may be used to develop photodetectors, light-emitting diodes, nonlinear optical devices, field-effect transistors, photovoltaic devices, supercapacitors, fuel cells, lithium-ion batteries, and thermoelectric transformation systems.43−49

Top-down and bottom-up approaches can be used to synthesize 2D MOF nanosheets. The top-down approach involves exfoliation (ultrasonication, chemical exfoliation, or electrochemical exfoliation) of MOF crystals into monolayer or multilayer MOF nanosheets.50−52 For example, to synthesize [Cu2Br(IN)2]2, corresponding MOFs are exfoliated following the top-down approach because of the relatively weak interaction between the layered MOFs.53 The bottom-up approach facilitates the molecular-level synthesis of the MOF nanosheets under suitable conditions. For instance, a 2D MOF, Cu3(C6O6), was prepared by the bottom-up approach and exhibited a semiconductor band structure with a direct band gap of 1.5 eV. The O–Cu–O bonds offer efficient charge delocalization, which gives rise to a highly dispersive conduction band.54 This approach is advantageous because it is more direct, effective, and controllable than the top-down approach. The large specific surface area and high aspect ratio of 2D MOF nanosheets substantially increase their surface electrocatalytic activity, surface contact area, and surface activity compared to those of bulk crystals. The mass production of 2D MOF nanosheets is crucial for the adaptive and cost-effective development of 2D MOF semiconductors.

A MOF {[Cu2(6-Hmna)(6-mn)]·NH4}n (1, 6-Hmna = 6-mercaptonicotinic acid; 6-mn = 6-mercaptonicotinate; NH4+ = ammonium ion) as a 2D (Cu–S)n plane structure achieved conductivity with a small band gap (1.34 eV); its electrical conductivity (10.96 S cm–1) was the highest among those of all analyzed MOF single crystals.55 This indicates that this highly conductive MOF has potential for semiconductor applications. Because of the properties of (Cu–S)n MOFs, 2D conductive MOFs offer considerable advantages. However, due to the quantity (several tens of milligrams), yield (approximately 50%), and crystal form of the product synthesized using a batched hydrothermal autoclave method, it can be limited for application. In the present study, we successfully developed a facile synthesis method for the mass production of 2D (Cu–S)n MOF nanosheets. A bottom-up approach was adopted using a simple one-pot solvothermal reflux reaction. This method increased the production quantity and yield, forming the product into 2D nanosheets.

To date, several studies have reported that Cu-based MOFs could serve as peroxidase mimics56−60 because of their abundant active sites, large surface areas, tunable surface functionalities, and cost-effectiveness.61 To the best of our knowledge, however, catalytic sites of the peroxidase-like activity were mainly limited by the coordination of Cu(II) in the Cu-based MOFs.56−60 Inspired by CuS nanoparticles with intrinsic peroxidase-like activity,62,63 the as-prepared (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets were considered to develop as peroxidase mimics. In the presence of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), the nanosheets could catalyze a colorless substrate of 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) to a blue color of oxidized TMB (oxTMB). In this catalytic reaction, H2O2 was decomposed to generate hydroxyl radicals (•OH) responsible for converting TMB into oxTMB. Accordingly, the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets were developed as a simple and selective colorimetric sensor for detecting glutathione (GSH) against other amino acids.

Taken together, the present study offers a significant breakthrough in the synthetic approach of (Cu–S)n MOFs 2D nanosheets. The one-pot bottom-up reflux reaction substantially improved the production quantity and reduced the reaction time compared with that of the traditional autoclave method. In addition, (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets with the coordination of Cu(I) were discovered to possess an impressive peroxidase-like activity for the first time and applied for the colorimetric detection of GSH (as shown in Figure 1).

Experimental Section

Materials

Cu(NO3)2·3H2O and CuSO4 were purchased from Showa Chemical Industry, and 6,6′-dithiodinicotinic acid and pyrazine were obtained from Tokyo Chemical Industry (TCI). Dimethylformamide (DMF) was purchased from Echo Chemical, and 2,2-azinobis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline)-6-sulfonic acid (ABTS) was purchased from Alfa Chemistry. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2; 30%), 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB), and sodium acetate trihydrate were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Glutathione (GSH) and terephthalic acid (TA) were purchased from Acros Organics. An acetate buffer solution (pH = 4) was obtained from a commercial source.

Characterization

Element analysis was performed using a vario EL Cube Elemental Analyzer (Elementar; for nitrogen, carbon, sulfur, hydrogen, and oxygen). The size and morphology of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets were determined through field-emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a JSM-7600F (JEOL). In addition, the nanosheets’ composition and element distribution were analyzed through SEM with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy and element mapping. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy images were obtained using JEM-2100F (JEOL). For chemical analysis, high-resolution X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used with a scanning XPS microprobe (PHI Quantera II; ULVAC-PHI). The structure of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D crystal was determined through single-crystal X-ray crystallography. It was performed using a Bruker Kappa 4CCD diffractometer with monochromatic Mo Kα radiation (λ = 0.71073 Å). Cryostream Cooler (Oxford Cryosystems) was used to maintain the operating temperature at 150 K. HKL ScaLAPACK was used to adopt the multiscan approach for data reduction. The crystallographic data can be obtained free of charge from the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre as supplementary publication no. CCDC-2158967. Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) was performed using a D2 PHASER (Bruker, Zhubei, Taiwan) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.1542 nm); the operation conditions were as follows: accelerating voltage, 25 kV; working current, 10 mA; and diffraction angles for scanning, 2–40°. The functional groups of compounds were determined through Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy using a Spectrum 100 FTIR (PerkinElmer). Fluorescence spectra were measured using an LS 55 Luminescence Spectrometer (PerkinElmer). Ultraviolet–visible absorption spectra were obtained using a V-670 UV–vis–NIR Spectrophotometer (JASCO). Emission spectra were obtained using an FP-8300 (JASCO). Zeta potential was evaluated using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Panalytical).

Preparation of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D Nanosheets and (Cu–S)n MOF 2D Crystal

Two-dimensional (2D) metal–organic framework (MOF) nanosheets [(Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets] were synthesized using a simple solvothermal reflux method, which is a modified version of the traditional high-pressure autoclave method.55 The mass synthesis of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets depended on the molar ratios of the incorporated reactants. In brief, 6,6′-dithiodinicotinic acid (25 mmol; 7.70 g) and CuSO4 (50 mmol; 7.98 g) were dissolved in DMF. The solution was bubbled and refluxed under N2 for 24 h. During the reaction process, the color changed from pale blue to blue–green, green, red–brown, and finally red. After cooling, the red product was collected and washed with DMF and then with ethanol. Afterward, the product was dried under a vacuum to obtain (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets; the yield was approximately 90.2% (weight, 8.26 g). An appropriate reaction temperature and N2 gas are imperative for a well-controlled reaction and S–S bond cleavage. For the synthesis of the aforementioned nanosheets, CuSO4 is recommended instead of Cu(NO3)2·3H2O, which may emit a red-brown nitrogen oxide gas during the reaction. The solvent was prepared by using DMF and H2O (5:1 v/v, similar to the autoclave method) to monitor the changes in color during the reaction. XRD and FTIR spectroscopies were used to assess the samples at the trace levels (Figure S1). The temperature for the reflux reaction performed using the mixed solvent was 118 °C, which was difficult to ensure for every batch precisely. We optimized the conditions to develop a single-solvent (DMF) reaction under reflux. This reflux method was used to prepare the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets. The structure of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets appears to be [Cu(6-Hmna)2]n (1, 6-Hmna = 6-mercaptonicotinic acid). Anal. Calcd for C12H8Cu2N2O4S2: C, 33.17; H, 1.86; N, 6.45; S, 14.76. Found: C, 32.94; H, 1.89; N, 6.46; S, 14.55 (Table S1). We successfully crystallized the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets to form a (Cu–S)n MOF 2D crystal. It was synthesized using 6,6′-dithiodinicotinic acid (0.10 mmol; 30.8 mg) and copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate (0.20 mmol; 46.1 mg) in a DMF solution at 140 °C for 72 h by using the autoclave hydrothermal method. The structure of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D crystal was determined through single-crystal X-ray crystallography (Table S2).

Examination of the Peroxidase-like Activity of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D Nanosheets

The peroxidase-like activity of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets (2.6 μg mL–1) was evaluated at pH 4 (acetate buffer, 10 mM) with TMB (0.2 mM) as peroxidase substrates in the presence of 0.3 M H2O2. For the inhibition of peroxidase-like activity, various concentrations of KSCN (0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 mM) were mixed with the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets for 5 min. Next, acetic buffer, H2O2, and TMB were added to the mixture of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets and KSCN. After the solution was incubated for 20 min, the color change of the resulting solution was obtained with a spectrophotometer (JASCO V-670).

Detection of Hydroxyl Radicals during the Peroxidase Reaction

TA was used as a fluorescent probe (λex = 315 nm/λem = 425 nm) to detect •OH. Initially, (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets (2.6 μg mL–1) were added to acetate buffer with TA (0.5 mM) and H2O2 (0.3 M). The resulting solution was incubated for 20 min, and then the solution was centrifuged. After that, the supernatant was measured with a fluorescence spectrophotometer (JASCO FP-8300).

Colorimetric Sensing of GSH

Typically, different concentrations of GSH were added to the solution of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets (2 μg mL–1) with 0.2 mM TMB and 0.3 M H2O2. After the solution was incubated for 20 min, the color change of the resulting solution was obtained with the spectrophotometer (JASCO V-670). For the selectivity test, amino acids such as l-histidine (His), l-arginine (Arg), l-cysteine (Cys), l-serine (Ser), l-methionine (Meth), l-phenylalanine (Phen), l-threonine (Thr), l-tryptophan (Try), l-aspartic acid (Asp), and GSH with the equivalent concentration of 100 μM were added to a mixture of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets, H2O2, and TMB, respectively. After the reaction time of 20 min, the absorption value at 652 nm of the resulting solution was recorded by the UV–vis spectrophotometer.

Results and Discussion

Synthetic Breakthrough of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D Nanosheets

The (Cu–S)n MOF was first reported by literature.55 Typically, {[Cu2(6-Hmna)(6-mn)]·NH4}n MOF was prepared through crystal engineering; in situ hydrothermal processes were initiated through a reaction of 6,6′-dithiodinicotinic acid, Cu(NO3)2·3H2O, and pyrazine in a solution of dimethylformamide (DMF) and water (5:1 v/v) at 140 °C for 6 days. The method developed in this study for synthesizing (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets was straightforward and direct. The reaction was simplified to a one-pot reflux of 6,6′-dithiodinicotinic acid and CuSO4 reflux in DMF solution under an N2 atmosphere. As a result, the yield was improved by >90% compared to the autoclave method.55 The increased production quantity indicates the potential of this method for mass production of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets. Using this method, we produced several grams of nanosheets per batch with 50 mmol of CuSO4 and 25 mmol of 6,6′-dithiodinicotinic acid, obtaining a yield of approximately 90.2% (weight, 8.26 g). Furthermore, the reaction time was reduced from 6 days to 1 day. The (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets were synthesized through a reflux reaction involving the cleavage of the S–S bond in 6,6′-dithiodinicotinic acid with CuSO4. The method did not involve pyrazine, because it may be chemically transformed into NH4+. Pyrazine is used in the autoclave method, in which the (Cu–S)n MOF layers are self-assembled through the ionic bonding of the carboxylate groups of Cu2(6-Hmna)(6-mn) and the NH4+ cations to form the crystal structure {[Cu2(6-Hmna)(6-mn)]·NH4}n.55 In the absence of NH4+, which is essential for layer-by-layer packing, the structure of (Cu–S)n MOF nanosheets was expected to extend in the planar direction of the nanosheet rather than perpendicularly because of the free-off from the interlayer ionic bonding force.

Morphology and Crystal Structure of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D Nanosheets

Figure 2a–c depicts the SEM images (top and cross-sectional views) of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets. The nanosheets had dimension areas of several micrometers and thicknesses of <70 nm. The nanosheets were irregular in shape, with some visible nanoparticles or defects on their surfaces. In addition to SEM being used for morphological analyses, SEM-EDS and element mapping were performed to explore the distribution of the elements on the surface of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets. As a result, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, sulfur, and copper were distributed uniformly on the nanosheet surfaces (Figure S2).

Scanning electron microscopy images of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets—top views (a and b) and cross-sectional view (c)—and (Cu–S)n MOF 2D crystal (d). Structure of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D crystal along the b-axis (e), c-axis (f), and a-axis (g).

To determine the structure of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets, a single (Cu–S)n MOF 2D crystal was formed using a hydrothermal method. Its structure was investigated through X-ray crystallography to determine the crystal’s exact composition (Table S2). The crystal formed a needlelike shape (Figures 2d and S3a,b). The structure of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D crystal was as follows (Figures 2e–g, S4, and S5): {[Cu2(6-Hmna)2]·H2O}n, orthorhombic, space group Pna21, a = 11.3723(6), b = 3.8127(2), c = 31.2171(15) Å, Z = 4, R1 = 0.0517, wR2 = 0.1234, based on reflections I > 2σ(I). Table S2 lists the crystal parameters. In this crystal, a copper atom served as the center and was bonded to three sulfur atoms and one nitrogen atom from the 6-Hmna ligands. A consecutive (Cu–S)n plane was shown in the crystal structure (Figure 2e–g) illustrated using the Oak Ridge Thermal-Ellipsoid Plot (ORTEP). Furthermore, water molecules were packed into the framework through H-bonding and interacted with nearby carboxylic acid groups, thus stabilizing the framework. The (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets were evaluated through FE-TEM. The planar shape of the nanosheets validated their 2D structure (Figure S3c,d).

XRD, FTIR, and XPS of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D Nanosheets

The (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets, (Cu–S)n MOF 2D crystal, and simulated pattern of the (Cu–S)n MOF crystal were analyzed through powder XRD; the obtained data were compared with those of {[Cu2(6-Hmna)(6-mn)]·NH4}n [denoted as (Cu–S)n MOF].55 As shown in Figure 3a, the diffraction pattern of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets (red line) was similar to that of the (Cu–S)n MOF (green line). For the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets, the diffraction angles were 6.4, 12.7, and 19.0°, which corresponded to the 2D crystallographic planes of (002), (004), and (006), respectively. By contrast, for the (Cu–S)n MOF, the diffraction angles were 5.7, 11.3, and 17.0° for the 2D crystallographic planes of (002), (004), and (006), respectively. The resultant layer structure of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets differed from that of the (Cu–S)n MOF. Using Bragg’s law, we calculated the d-spacing value of 1.38 nm for the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets and 1.55 nm for the (Cu–S)n MOF. The difference between the d-spacing values of the (Cu–S)n MOF and (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets was 1.7 Å, which is almost equivalent to the size of NH4+. This finding indicates that the layers of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets lack NH4+ intercalation between the (Cu–S)n layers. Therefore, the structure of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets may be as follows: [Cu(6-Hmna)2]n. This structure does not have packing ions between its layers. The diffraction pattern of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D crystal (blue line) was consistent with the simulated pattern (purple line), which validated the purity of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D crystal and its agreement with the crystal structure of {[Cu2(6-Hmna)2]·H2O}n, in which water molecules are packed between the layers.

The structure of the (Cu–S)n MOF is {[Cu2(6-Hmna)(6-mn)]·NH4}n. The proposed structure of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets is [Cu(6-Hmna)2]n. The structure of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D crystal is {[Cu2(6- Hmna)2]·H2O}n.

The (Cu–S)n MOF, (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets and crystal, and 6,6′-dithiodinicotinic acid (H2dtdn; organic ligand) were compared in terms of their FTIR spectroscopy spectra (Figure 3b). The FTIR spectroscopy spectra of the (Cu–S)n MOF, (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets, and crystal, respectively, exhibited characteristic peaks at 1692, 1676, and 1718 cm–1 corresponding to the C=O stretching of an uncoordinated carboxylic acid; peaks at 1589 and 1431 cm–1 corresponding to aromatic C=C stretching; and peaks at 529, 534, and 524 cm–1 corresponding to the Cu–S bending mode. The vibrational modes were similar among the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D crystal and nanosheets, H2dtdn, and (Cu–S)n MOF. The H2dtdn exhibited characteristic S–S stretching vibration at 549 cm–1, which disappeared after forming the Cu–S bond.

The coordinated elements were qualitatively and quantitatively assessed through XPS. The XPS spectra of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets (Figure S6) were compared with those of the (Cu–S)n MOF and (Cu–S)n MOF 2D crystals (Figure 3c,d). The results indicate that the coordination geometries of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets, (Cu–S)n MOF 2D crystal, and (Cu–S)n MOF are similar and further reveal that the characteristic bond energy of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets was consistent with that of the proposed structure [Cu(6-Hmna)2]n. Figure S6a shows the XPS spectrum of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets for C 1s at 283.4 eV (C–C), 284.3 eV (C=C/C=N), and 287.7 eV (C=O) corresponded to the aromatic ring and the carboxyl group. Figure S6b shows the XPS spectrum of Cu 2p with two prominent peaks at 931.1 and 951.0 eV corresponding to Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2 from Cu(I), respectively.64,65 In addition, the two weak peaks at 933.3 and 952.8 eV are associated with Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2 from Cu(II), respectively,64,65 which were consistent with those of the (Cu–S)n MOF.55 Furthermore, through the computation of bond valence via Cu–S and Cu–N bond lengths using X-ray single-crystal data, we establish the oxidation state of copper, with data results confirming copper as a monovalent state (sum of the bond valences (∑S) for copper is approximately 0.973, which is close to 1, indicating its oxidation state of +1), as shown in Table S3. The (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets, (Cu–S)n MOF 2D crystal, and (Cu–S)n MOF were compared in terms of the XPS spectra for N 1s (Figure 3c). In the case of the (Cu–S)n MOF, the bond energies for N 1s were 398.5, 399.5, and 401.1 eV for = N–, C–N, and NH4+, respectively. However, the peak corresponding to NH4+ was not visible in the spectra of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets and crystal. Thus, the structures of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets and crystal are free from NH4+ intercalation between the layers. This result is consistent with XRD and corroborates the proposed structure of [Cu(6-Hmna)2]n. The (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets, (Cu–S)n MOF 2D crystal, and (Cu–S)n MOF were compared in terms of the XPS spectra for O 1s (Figure 3d). The C–O bond energies of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D crystal and (Cu–S)n MOF were 530.3 and 530.2 eV, respectively, and the O–H bond energies were 531.5 and 531.7 eV, respectively. However, the proposed structure of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets included 6-Hmna instead of 6-mn, and the structure of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D crystal was {[Cu(6-Hmna)2]·H2O}n; therefore, the O–H bond energies had a higher proportion than that of the (Cu–S)n MOF, with the structure of {[Cu2(6-Hmna)(6-mn)]·NH4}n. These characterizations and comparisons verified that the structure of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets was very similar to that of the (Cu–S)n MOF single crystal.55 Since the newly developed synthetic method of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets significantly increased the production quantity and reduced the reaction time, it was worth developing further applications of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets.

Peroxidase-like Activity of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D Nanosheets

Previously, CuS particles and nanoparticles have been demonstrated to be good peroxidase mimics,62,63 and the Cu atoms all have the oxidation state of +1 in CuS quantum dots.66 The XPS analysis of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets (Figure S6b) confirmed that Cu(I) was predominantly involved in the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets as stated above. Accordingly, the peroxidase-like activity of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets was investigated through an oxidation reaction with the peroxidase substrate TMB and H2O2 in an acetate buffer solution (pH 4.0). As shown in Figure 4a, no distinctive change in the corresponding absorption feature was noted among the reaction systems containing TMB and H2O2 as the control group. However, a prominent absorption peak was observed at 652 nm when (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets were added to the TMB–H2O2 system. The change in color from colorless to blue in photographs 1–3 could be observed with the naked eye. These results identified the peroxidase-like activity of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets. To verify active sites of the peroxidase-like activity of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets, KSCN was used to interact with Cu.67 In Figure 4b, the introduction of KSCN significantly decreased the absorbance at 652 nm, indicating the successful inhibition of the peroxidase-like activity of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets. Accordingly, the coordination of Cu(I) in (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets was confirmed to act as active sites for peroxidase-like reactions. It should be noted that catalytic active sites of the peroxidase-like activity were mainly limited by the coordination of Cu(II) in most Cu-based MOFs.56−60 This is the first example of the unique coordination of Cu(I) stabilized in Cu-based MOFs, which exhibited remarkable activity to mimic natural peroxidase.

In the following, (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets can catalyze the other peroxidase substrate 2,2-azinobis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline)-6-sulfonic acid (ABTS) in the presence of H2O2, which examined an absorption band around 415 nm. Of note, (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets exhibited much higher peroxidase-like activity for TMB than that for ABTS (Figure S7a). It was speculated that the surface charge characteristics between (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets and peroxidase substrates might affect the peroxidase-like activity.68 Since (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets (the ζ potential −50 mV) are negatively charged nanozymes, it is likely to have a strong affinity to positively charged peroxidase substrate TMB rather than negatively charged one ABTS, leading to a significant difference in the catalytic activity (Figure S7b). Therefore, it is reasonable that the ζ potential intensity of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets had negative values due to several carboxylate groups on their surface. This result revealed that the surface charge of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets is negatively charged, which is more suitable for combining with TMB rather than ABTS in the following experiments.

On the basis of kinetic analysis, the catalytic activity of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets followed a typical Michaelis–Menten curve that was evident within a certain range of either H2O2 or TMB concentrations (Figure 4c,d). With the Lineweaver–Burk equation, the Michaelis–Menten constant (Km) and catalytic constant (Vmax) were obtained, as shown in the inset of Figure 4c,d, respectively. Kinetic parameters for other Cu-based MOFs used as peroxidase mimics were also listed in Table S4 for comparison.56−60 The apparent Km and Vmax values of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets were similar to those of some reported Cu-based MOFs,56,59,60 but the catalytic performance was not superior to those of Cu/Fe-based MOFs.57,58 Impressively, the peroxidase-like activity of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets in aqueous solution could sustain for more than 1 year (Figure S8). In addition, the peroxidase-like activity of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets was significantly higher than that of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D crystal based on the equivalent Cu content (Figure S9). Therefore, the facile synthesis and long-term stability of the peroxidase-like activity for (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets exhibited the superiority of cost-effectiveness, which would be beneficial for further practical applications.

To study the catalytic mechanism of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets, terephthalic acid (TA) was used in the presence of H2O2 and (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets. TA is a sensitive fluorescent probe, which can directly react with •OH and then transfer to a highly fluorescent product 2-hydroxyterephthalic acid (HTA).69,70Figure 5a showed the remarkable emission peak of 425 nm in the mixture of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets, TA, and H2O2 as compared to control experiments. This result demonstrated that the peroxidase-like activity of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets originated from the catalytic decomposition of H2O2 to produce •OH. Since GSH has been known as an antioxidant, it was commonly used to reduce the oxidized TMB.67,71 After the addition of GSH, an apparent decrease in absorbance at 652 nm was observed in the mixture of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets, H2O2, and TMB (Figure 5b). It revealed that the peroxidase-like activity of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets was sensitive to concentrations of GSH. In addition, different amino acids were used to replace GSH to determine the selectivity. As shown in Figure 5c, only GSH could significantly regulate the peroxidase-like activity of the (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets. Accordingly, selective detection of GSH was achieved based on the inhibition of the peroxidase-like activity of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets. The feasibility of (Cu–S)n MOF nanosheets for sensing GSH was validated as shown in Figure 5d. The linear calibration curve for determining GSH was plotting the value of ΔA vs the concentrations of GSH in the range of 5–60 μM. The corresponding limit of detection (LOD) was 1.64 μM, which was listed in Table S5 for comparison. These results indicated that the inhibition of the peroxidase-like activity of (Cu–S)n MOF nanosheets by GSH could provide a simple strategy for the colorimetric detection of GSH.

Conclusions

We developed a simple method for effective mass production of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets. This facile synthesis method involves a one-pot bottom-up reflux reaction. The structure of a crystal formed using a (Cu–S)n MOF 2D crystal was determined to be {[Cu2(6-Hmna)2]·H2O}n; as evident, the structure is packed with water molecules between the layers of (Cu–S)n networks. The proposed structure of the synthesized (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets is [Cu(6-Hmna)2]n; this structure is free from the intercalation of packing ions between its layers. The nanosheets have a 2D planar shape with a thickness of <70 nm. The mass synthesis of these nanosheets may advance 2D MOF technology. Furthermore, (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets exhibited peroxidase-like activity to catalyze the oxidation of TMB in the presence of H2O2. Importantly, the coordination of Cu(I) in (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets was confirmed to act as active sites for peroxidase-like reactions. This is the first example of the unique coordination of Cu(I) stabilized in Cu-based MOFs, which exhibited remarkable activity to mimic natural peroxidase. The kinetic studies revealed that the apparent Km and Vmax values of (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets were similar to those of some reported Cu-based MOFs. Taking advantage of the peroxidase-like activity, (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets are applicable for selectively sensing GSH against other amino acids. In addition, (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets exhibit sensitivity for detecting glutathione (GSH) in the range of 5–60 μM with a limit of detection (LOD) of 1.64 μM. Therefore, the facile synthesis and long-term stability of the peroxidase-like activity for (Cu–S)n MOF 2D nanosheets exhibited the superiority of cost-effectiveness, which would be beneficial for other practical applications.
