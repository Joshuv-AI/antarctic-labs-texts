Comparative Evaluation of Three File Systems for Shaping Curved Canals: An In Vitro Approach
Authors: Karuna R. Siraparapu, X. Nagamaheshwari, Veerendra S. Patil, Masuna Rukmini Amulya, Sainath Adsare, Karanam Apoorva Prakash
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:To compare and evaluate the shaping ability of ProTaper Next, TruNatomy, and Flexer heat activation rotary files in simulated curved canals.Materials and Methods:Forty-five severely curved simulated canals in resin blocks were prepared to an apical size of 25 using the following systems (n = 15 per group): ProTaper Next, TruNatomy, and Flexer heat activation. Composite images were made from the superimposition of pre- and post-instrumentation images. Parameters evaluated were total resin removal, centering ability, canal transportation (at the apical, curved, and coronal part), and preparation time.Results:Canals prepared with TruNatomy were better centered in the apical part than those prepared with PTN and Flexer heat activation. Flexer heat activation removed significantly more resin at the outer aspect of the maximum point of curvature. PTN and TruNatomy caused similar canal transportations. Instrumentation with Flexer heat activation required more time than with the two other instruments.Conclusion:Within the limitations of the study, all instruments were safe to use and preserved the original canal shape well. In the apical part of the canals, TruNatomy obtained the best results with regard to canal transportation.Clinical Significance:These findings underscore the importance of considering file selection based on specific canal characteristics and treatment objectives to optimize clinical outcomes in endodontic practice.

INTRODUCTION

Achieving success in root canal procedures demands accurate diagnosis, effective treatment planning, and thorough cleaning, shaping, and sealing of the entire root canal system. Key components include removing inflammatory or necrotic tissue and bacteria, shaping the canal to a tapering funnel shape while maintaining its original design and overcoming challenges like curved or restricted canals.[1] Advanced instrument designs, particularly rotating nickel-titanium (NiTi) instruments, offer improved safety, faster preparation, and better preservation of the original canal shape. However, current literature often compares only two instrument types, limiting comprehensive insights.[2345] Therefore, this study aims to compare and evaluate the shaping ability of ProTaper Next, TruNatomy, and Flexer heat activation rotary files in simulated curved canals.

METHODOLOGY

The in vitro study, conducted at the Department of Conservative Dentistry and Endodontics, HKES S. Nijalingappa Institute of Dental Sciences and Research in Kalaburagi, obtained institutional ethical clearance (HKES/SNIDSR/IEC/11/21) before determining a sample size of 45 (n = 15) through one-way ANOVA.

Preparation of simulated root canals

Forty-five simulated curved canals (Endo Training Block-L, Dentsply Maillefer) were used in this study: taper = 0.02, apical diameter = 0.15 mm, length = 16.5 mm, curvature = 60° with a 4.5 mm radius (measured using Pruett’s technique). Canal patency was confirmed by passing a size 10 K-file just beyond the apex. The resin blocks were randomly divided into three groups (n = 15 per group): Group 1 shaped by ProTaper Next, Group 2 by TruNatomy, and Group 3 by Flexer heat activation.

Study protocol

Each canal received a new instrument, and Glyde-Prep was used before each use. Distilled water was used for irrigation with a 5ml syringe, and 1 mm short of the working length. Glide path preparation was skipped due to standardized resin block sizes. A size 10 K-file maintained canal patency after each file stroke. The instrumentation sequence followed the manufacturer’s instructions, with canals irrigated between instruments.

Group 1 used ProTaper Next instruments at 300 rpm and 2–5.2 Ncm torque with an Xsmart plus endomotor. The TRN prime file (26/ 0.4) was set at 500 rpm and 1.5 Ncm torque. Group 3 used Flexer heat activation files at 250–400 rpm and 2.0–2.5 Ncm torque. These instruments have a non-cutting tip, a positive rake angle, and a triangular cross-section.

Image analysis and assessment of canal preparation

All canals were injected with blue ink for preoperative imaging and photographed with a fixed digital camera. Distilled water rinses were performed before and after instrumentation. The canals were then filled with red India ink and re-photographed under the same conditions. Pre- and post-instrumentation images were combined using Adobe Photoshop Elements 7.0, and resin removal was measured at 20 points along the canal using Image J 1.46r software. A blinded examiner evaluated shaping quality and preparation time, assessing total resin removal, centering ability, canal transportation (apical, curved, and coronal parts), and preparation time.

Total resin removal

Total resin removal was measured by summing the resin removed from both the inner and outer sides of the simulated curved canals.

Centering ability

Centering ability was assessed by comparing the resin removed from the inner and outer sides at each measuring point using a paired t-test (P < 0.05). Good centering ability was indicated by no significant difference between the two sides.

Canal transportation

Apical part

Transportation was measured at points 0 to 3 by subtracting the inner side resin removal from the outer side. Positive values indicate inner side transportation, and negative values indicate outer side transportation.

Middle part (curvature)

Transportation at points 4 to 7 was measured similarly. Positive values indicate straightening of curvature.

Coronal part

Transportation at points 8 to 10 was measured similarly. Positive values indicate more resin removal on the inner side of the canal curvature and negative values on the outer side.

Preparation time

Preparation time included active instrumentation, cleaning instrument flutes, patency checks with a size 10 K-File, and irrigation, recorded with a chronograph.

Instrument separation and errors

Two experienced operators examined the prepared resin blocks under loupes for instrument separations or shaping errors. Data was entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet and checked for discrepancies.

RESULTS

The data was analyzed by SPSS (21.0 version). Shapiro–Wilk test was used to check which all variables were following normal distribution. Data was normally distributed; therefore, bivariate analyses were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by post hoc Tukey’s test. Level of statistical significance was set at P value less than 0.05.

Comparing resin removal effectiveness on the inner canal wall revealed no significant difference among points 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, and 9 across the three study groups (P > 0.05) [Graph 1]. However, at points 1, 2, 5, and 10, Flexer heat activation exhibited significantly higher resin removal efficacy compared to ProTapers and TruNatomy files, in that order. There was no significant distinction observed in resin removal efficacy on the outer wall at points 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, and 10 across the three study groups (P > 0.05) [Graph 2]. However, at points 1 and 5, Flexer heat activation demonstrated significantly higher resin removal efficacy compared to PTN and TruNatomy files. No significant difference was seen in the centering ability of three file systems used in the present study at apical, coronal, and middle level as P > 0.05 [Table 1]. Preparation time was found to be significantly more when Flexer heat activation file as compared to ProTaper Next and TruNatomy file as P > 0.05 [Table 2].

DISCUSSION

Multi-rooted teeth pose a challenge in endodontics due to their complex anatomy. Effective treatment requires flexible instruments to prevent canal transportation and ensure proper preparation, especially in curved canals. Stainless steel files, once common, were too rigid for larger sizes. The introduction of flexible Ni-Ti instruments transformed endodontics by easily navigating curved canals with minimal stress. This study evaluated three techniques—Flexer heat activation, TruNatomy, and ProTaper Next (PTN)—in severely curved simulated resin block canals. Despite manufacturer recommendations for a size 15 glide path, no prior glide path was created, as the canals matched ISO-size 15 with a 0.02 taper. Resin block canals, widely used in research, offer excellent standardization and serve as validated substitutes for extracted human teeth.[6]

This study found that all three instruments caused some canal transportation in the apical region, straightening of the curvature, and narrowing of the coronal portion. Consistent with Sanfelice et al.’s research, both reciprocating and rotating instruments tended to straighten the canal. TruNatomy (TN) showed the least apical transportation, followed by PTN, and then Flexer heat activation.[7] Schäfer and Florek[8] demonstrated PTN’s superior centering ability and less resin removal compared to Flexer heat activation, aligning with our findings. Flexer heat activation caused more straightening by removing more resin from the inner curvature, similar to previous research. While Wu et al.[9] noted PTN’s better shaping ability in curved sections, our study found no differences between TruNatomy and PTN in shaping the curvature of simulated canals.

In this study, the apical preparation size was standardized to size 25 for all groups, despite the varying tapers of the instruments. The PTN system includes five instruments with tip tapers of 4% to 7.5% and diameters of 17 to 50. TruNatomy (TN) files come in small, prime, and medium sizes with diameters of 20, 26, and 36, all with a 4% taper. Flexer heat activation files, with a Conservative 6 design, come in lengths of 21mm, 25mm, and 31mm. Their sequence includes Mx, M1, M2, C1, C2, and selective C3, with tip diameters of 0.19 to 0.25 and tapers of 7% to 9%. TN files showed better performance in the apical region. PTN’s superior apical shaping may be due to its off-centered rectangular cross-section.[91011] No significant differences were found between PTN and TN in overall shaping effectiveness. However, Flexer heat activation caused more straightening in the canal’s curved portion. No instruments were fractured during the study.[12]

Preparation time in our study included active instrumentation, instrument changes, flute cleaning, patency checks with size 10 stainless steel K-Files, and irrigation.[13] While PTN and TruNatomy showed similar preparation times, Flexer heat activation required more time due to the use of additional instruments. Comparable results were found in studies using PTN on natural teeth. All instruments maintained the original canal shape safely. TruNatomy showed the best outcomes for apical canal transportation. One study limitation is the use of resin blocks, which do not precisely mimic natural teeth. Dentin, found in natural teeth, differs in hardness from resin, affecting debris generation during instrumentation. Consequently, outcomes in natural teeth may not mirror those in resin blocks. Additionally, natural teeth often have more complex anatomy than the single patent canal represented in resin blocks, posing a challenge in real clinical scenarios.

CONCLUSION

Within the scope of this study, it can be concluded as all instruments were deemed safe and effectively preserved the original canal shape. TruNatomy displayed superior centering ability compared to other file systems examined. Additionally, Flexer heat activation resulted in greater resin removal, albeit with an increased preparation time. Importantly, no instances of instrument separation were observed across all three file systems.
