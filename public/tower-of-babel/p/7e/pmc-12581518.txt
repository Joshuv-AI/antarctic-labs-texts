Background

Aortic coarctation

Native coarctation of the aorta (CoA) is a congenital heart defect accounting for 6–8% of all congenital heart diseases, with a prevalence ranging from 0.4 to 9 cases per 1000 live births [1]. Given the annual number of births worldwide, it is estimated that approximately 1.3 million children are born with congenital heart defects, with CoA being one of the most significant malformations in terms of morbidity and mortality [1]. CoA is characterized by a mechanical obstruction located in the aortic isthmus, which, without appropriate treatment, can lead to severe complications such as persistent hypertension, heart failure, aneurysm formation, bacterial endocarditis, and aortic dissection [2].

Two main approaches have been established for treating CoA: surgical repair and percutaneous interventions, each offering distinct advantages and posing unique challenges in pediatric patients [2]. However, there is no clear consensus on which of the two approaches is superior. Particularly for the pediatric population, a 1996 randomized clinical trial by Mccrindle et al. of 21 pediatric patients showed that medium-term resting blood pressure and physical activity performance were similar between patients undergoing balloon angioplasty (BA) and surgical repair [3]. However, multiple studies have since shown that, in the long term, balloon angioplasty patients require more frequent reinterventions for re-coarctation or aneurysms [3–5]. Similarly, the 2015 Multicenter Coarctation of the Aorta Stent Trial intermediate report supports using Cheatham Platinum stents in children and adults after a 2-year follow-up; nevertheless, a longer follow-up is advised due to early and late aortic wall injury and the need for re-expansion of small-diameter stents [4].

The limited number of primary studies has hindered efforts to propose evidence-based recommendations for managing aortic coarctation. In 2012, Siqueira Pádua et al. conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis comparing stent placement to surgery but found no studies that met their inclusion criteria [6]. Similarly, in 2014, Hu et al. compared balloon angioplasty with surgery and found, unsurprisingly, that balloon angioplasty offered short-term benefits due to its minimally invasive nature; however, there was a trend favoring surgery due to lower complications and recoarctation rates over the mid- and long-term. Nevertheless, including pediatric and adult patients introduced significant heterogeneity, limiting the generalization of the results [7]. 2019 Wu et al. systematic review addressed this limitation by including only pediatric patients. Compared to BA, surgery was significantly associated with a lower incidence of recoarctation, repeat interventions, and residual trans-coarctation gradient during mid- to long-term follow-up with no differences in aneurysm formation rate, immediate residual gradient, complications, or perioperative mortality. Despite these findings, the overall level of evidence was low, emphasizing the need for new, well-designed randomized controlled trials [8].

Given the lack of consensus, inconsistent outcomes, and methodological limitations in previous studies, there is a need for an updated and comprehensive review to address these gaps and provide clearer guidance for clinical decision-making in the pediatric population.

This review is therefore planned as an update of two previous systematic reviews [6, 8]. Unlike prior reviews, this protocol introduces several methodological improvements, including (i) the application of a pediatric-specific population filter to all database searches, (ii) the comparison across all three relevant interventions (surgery, balloon angioplasty, and stenting), and (iii) the pre-specification of subgroup and sensitivity analyses using contemporary meta-analytical techniques. These enhancements aim to increase the methodological rigor, clinical applicability, and transparency of the review process.

Objective of the study

Through this systematic review and meta-analysis update, we aim to provide evidence-based guidance for treating aortic coarctation in children. Our primary objective is to compare the short-term and long-term safety and effectiveness of surgical repairs versus percutaneous management (balloon angioplasty and stent implantation) for aortic coarctation in the pediatric population.

Safety will be evaluated in terms of operative mortality within the first 30 postoperative days, long-term survival over at least 5 years of follow-up, and the incidence of postoperative complications such as hypertension and complications of the posterior aortic wall, categorized as early (within 30 days post-procedure) and late (beyond 5 years of follow-up). Effectiveness will be assessed based on the incidence of recoarctation and reintervention, narrowing and pressure gradient reduction, and the blood flow restoration rate.

Methods/design

This systematic review and meta-analysis update protocol follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines for reporting systematic reviews evaluating healthcare interventions [9] and will be conducted considering the Cochrane Collaboration remarks on updates [10]. It is registered in the International Prospective Registry of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) database (CRD42024549170).

The final report will follow the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) checklist, and the AMSTAR 2 (A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews) will be used for supplementary quality assessment control [11, 12].

Criteria for considering studies in this systematic review

Types of studies

We will consider both published and unpublished randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that evaluate surgical or percutaneous repair for our specific condition and population. Additionally, observational studies will be incorporated due to limited comparative research availability.

Unpublished RCTs will be identified through searches in trial registries, conference proceedings, and by contacting investigators when possible. When available, data will be extracted and assessed for risk of bias using the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool. Sensitivity analyses excluding unpublished data will be conducted, and such studies will be clearly distinguished in the synthesis and GRADE rating to account for their lower certainty due to the absence of peer review.

Eligible observational studies will include prospective and retrospective cohort studies, as well as case–control studies, provided they compare at least two of the following interventions: surgical repair, balloon angioplasty, or stenting. Case reports, case series, and cross-sectional studies will be excluded.

Types of participants

We will include studies involving pediatric patients (under 18 years old) with aortic coarctation, either primary or associated with other congenital heart defects, who underwent surgical or percutaneous repair.

The following subgroup analyses will be considered:

Neonates: 1 day to 30 days

Neonates: 1 day to 30 days

Infants (younger and older): 1 month to 24 months

Infants (younger and older): 1 month to 24 months

Preschool children: 2 years to 6 years

Preschool children: 2 years to 6 years

School-age children: 7 years to 12 years

School-age children: 7 years to 12 years

Preadolescents and adolescents: 12 years to 18 years

Preadolescents and adolescents: 12 years to 18 years

Types of interventions

We will cover all effective surgical and percutaneous treatments for aortic coarctation, such as open surgery, minimally invasive procedures, stent placement, and intra-aortic balloon dilation. There will be no differentiation based on the type of stents used (bare metal, covered, balloon-expandable, or self-expanding). Studies comparing only two interventions will also be included.

Types of outcome measures

The primary outcomes include effectiveness, measured by a residual pressure gradient ≤ 20 mmHg post-procedure and absence of recoarctation (residual pressure gradient > 20 mmHg after hospital discharge) and reintervention (surgery or percutaneous management for aortic recoarctation); and safety outcomes such as surgical complications (aortic wall injury, aortic dissection, aneurysm, balloon rupture, new-onset atrial fibrillation, hemorrhage, and respiratory failure), operative mortality within the first 30 postoperative days, and long-term survival over at least 5 years of follow-up.

Secondary outcomes encompass all-cause perioperative mortality (defined as mortality before hospital discharge), post-procedure arterial hypertension (blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg following corrective intervention), survival, and hospitalization time. Exclusion criteria include non-original research publications (e.g., literature reviews, commentaries, editorials), studies lacking data on outcomes of interest (e.g., post-intervention pressure gradient, complications, recurrence), investigations involving combined interventions, studies including adult populations without separate pediatric analysis, and duplicate studies, in which case the study with the larger sample size will be included.

Search methods for identification of studies

The following databases will be searched: Scopus, LILACS, MEDLINE, CENTRAL, EMBASE, IBECS, OpenGrey, ClinicalTrials.gov, and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP). The search strategy will include comparative studies between intravascular stenting and surgical repair of aortic coarctation without time restriction. The search strategies were based on prior systematic reviews on this topic and subsequently refined to enhance methodological rigor. For the comparison between balloon angioplasty and surgical repair, we adopted the core search terms reported by Wu et al. (2019 [8]), incorporating a pediatric population filter (0–18 years) across all databases to ensure alignment with our target population. In the case of stent versus surgery, we expanded upon the strategy proposed by Pádua et al. (2012) [6] by including additional terms such as “transcatheter” and “interventional therapy” to increase search sensitivity and improve the identification of relevant contemporary studies. In both search strategies, the pediatric age filter was applied consistently. These modifications were implemented to maximize both the comprehensiveness and specificity of the evidence retrieval process in accordance with our research objectives.

Database-specific search strategies, MeSH terms, Boolean operators, truncation, and filters will be adapted to the syntax and structure of each electronic database. The full search strategies for each database are provided in Supplementary Material 1 (Search strategies).

Electronic searches

The following terms will be used for the first search: (surgical procedures, operative) OR (Surgery) OR (surgical treatment) OR (conventional surgery) AND (Angioplasty, Balloon) OR (interventional therapy) OR (intervention) AND (aortic coarctation) OR (coarctation of the aorta). For the second search, the following terms will be used: (surgical procedures, operative) OR (Surgery) OR (surgical treatment) OR (conventional surgery) AND (Stents) OR (Stent) OR (transcatheter) AND (aortic coarctation) OR (coarctation of the aorta). In addition to the time limit, the “child” filter corresponding to each database comprising populations aged 0–18 years will be used for these searches. In addition, references of all included studies and previously performed meta-analyses will be manually reviewed to identify other eligible studies. All database search results will be exported to Zotero.

Data collection and analysis

Selection of studies

In the first instance, the search results will be merged using Rayyan reference management software, where duplicate records will be eliminated. Two investigators will independently carry out the title and abstract screening. This approach ensures objectivity and minimizes bias in study selection. Subsequently, a second full-text screening will be performed independently by two investigators. Any disagreement on the eligibility of studies will be resolved by consensus with a third investigator. We will document the reasons for exclusion in a PRISMA flow diagram [9].

Data extraction and management

Patient demographics, including age, weight, and sex, will be recorded as baseline clinical information. Posttreatment pressure gradient refers to the residual pressure gradient across the coarctation site assessed via catheterization or echocardiography within 1 h after intervention, with a value ≤ 20 mmHg considered effective treatment. Severe complications included aortic wall injury, dissection, aneurysm formation, balloon rupture, new-onset atrial fibrillation, bleeding, and respiratory failure.

Recoarctation was defined as a peak systolic pressure gradient > 20 mmHg following hospital discharge or the need for reintervention. Reintervention was defined as any surgical or percutaneous procedure performed exclusively to address recoarctation. Perioperative mortality encompassed all-cause deaths occurring before hospital discharge. Postoperative hypertension was defined as blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg after intervention. Hospital length of stay was measured as the total number of days from the procedure to discharge.

Short-, mid-, and long-term follow-up periods will be classified as < 1 year, 1–3 years, and > 3 years, respectively.

Assessment of risk of bias in included studies

A heterogeneity analysis will be performed using a forest plot to analyze the variation between the results of the studies and their methodologies. A funnel plot will be performed to graphically assess the publication bias that may exist in the meta-analysis.

Quality assessment will be performed independently by both reviewers, and any disagreement on the assessed quality will be resolved by consensus with a third reviewer. The quality of the clinical trials will be assessed using the Cochrane RoB collaborative tool, with which the risk of bias assessment will be performed. This tool determines the risk of bias based on the following six dimensions: details of the randomization method; allocation masking; masking of participants, personnel, and outcome assessment; incomplete outcome data; selective reporting; and other sources of bias [13].

The quality of the observational studies will be assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS). The maximum NOS score is 9; each study will be rated low (0–5) or high quality [14].

If there are more than ten studies available, we will use funnel plot symmetry to analyze publication bias [10]. The level of evidence will be evaluated using the Grades of Recommendation, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system [15]. The GRADE system categorizes evidence into four levels: (i) high quality, where additional research is very unlikely to change confidence in the estimate of the effect; (ii) moderate quality, where additional research is likely to have an important impact on confidence in the estimate of the effect and may change the estimate; (iii) low quality, where additional research is very likely to have an important impact on confidence in the estimate of the effect and is likely to change the estimate; and (iv) very low quality, where any estimate of the effect is highly uncertain.

Measures of treatment effect

Dichotomous data

For dichotomous outcomes, such as the presence of complications or the occurrence of recoarctation, ORs or RRs will serve as the primary effect measures. ORs will reflect the odds of an event occurring in one group compared to another, while RRs will show the probability of the event in relation to the total population at risk. The choice between OR and RR will depend on the primary studies’ reporting; however, RRs are preferred for outcomes where baseline risk is crucial [16–19]. Confidence intervals will be calculated to assess the precision of estimates. Meta-analytic pooling will be performed using a DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model, ensuring robust estimates in the presence of study-level variability [10]. We will use the DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model to estimate pooled treatment effects, given the anticipated heterogeneity in patient populations, interventions, and outcome definitions across studies. This model accounts for both within-study and between-study variance and is widely used in meta-analyses of clinical interventions. When data availability is sufficient, subgroup analyses will examine potential modifiers, such as surgical approach or patient demographics, to better understand treatment effects in specific subpopulations.

Continuous data

The weighted mean difference will be used for continuous outcomes when all studies report outcomes on the same scale, ensuring straightforward interpretation. When scales differ, the SMD will standardize results by calculating the difference in means divided by the pooled standard deviation. Both measures will include 95% CIs. In cases where studies report only medians and ranges instead of means and standard deviations, the formulas proposed by Hozo et al. will be applied to estimate the missing statistics [20]. Continuous data pooling will use random-effects models to accommodate variability between studies. Sensitivity analyses will assess whether including estimated data influences the overall findings. Additionally, subgroup analyses may explore outcome variations based on follow-up duration, patient age, or intervention type.

Dealing with missing data

We will contact the study authors to request additional information or clarifications to address missing data. When these efforts are unsuccessful, analyses will rely on the available data, discussing the potential bias introduced by missing values. Imputation methods will be used for missing standard deviations, such as estimating them from confidence intervals, p-values, or interquartile ranges when sufficient data are available. Sensitivity analyses will assess the robustness of the results to missing data by comparing outcomes with and without imputation. Missing outcome data will be carefully noted, and their potential impact on the review’s conclusions will be discussed to ensure transparency and reliability.

Assessment of heterogeneity

Heterogeneity will be assessed using both qualitative and quantitative methods. The chi-squared (χ2) test will identify the presence of heterogeneity with a significance threshold of p < 0.10. Quantitative assessment will be conducted using the I2 statistic, which measures the proportion of variation in study outcomes due to heterogeneity rather than chance [21]. I2 values will be interpreted as follows: < 25% indicates low heterogeneity, 25–75% moderate heterogeneity, and > 75% high heterogeneity. Forest plots will visually present study results, highlighting variability in effect sizes [19].

Where heterogeneity is significant or high, subgroup analyses will explore potential sources, such as patient characteristics, intervention methods, or study design differences. If heterogeneity persists despite subgroup analyses, narrative synthesis will supplement the meta-analytical findings.

Assessment of reporting biases

Reporting biases, such as publication bias, will be evaluated using funnel plots for outcomes with at least 10 studies. Asymmetry in the funnel plot may indicate the presence of bias, which will be further tested using Egger’s regression test or Begg’s rank correlation test. A significant p-value (< 0.10) will suggest potential reporting bias. When bias is detected, trim-and-fill methods will be applied to adjust for missing studies and estimate the potential impact on pooled results. These adjustments will be explicitly described, and their influence on the overall findings will be evaluated to ensure the reliability of the conclusions.

Data synthesis

Data synthesis will involve both quantitative and qualitative approaches. Where studies report sufficiently homogeneous data on populations, interventions, and outcomes, a meta-analysis will be performed using random-effects models to account for between-study variability. The meta-analysis will generate pooled effect estimates for dichotomous and continuous outcomes and their 95% CIs. A narrative synthesis will be provided to describe patterns and relationships across studies for outcomes with substantial heterogeneity (I2 > 75%) that cannot be resolved through subgroup or sensitivity analyses. All analyses will be performed using R Studio software, ensuring rigorous statistical evaluation and reproducibility.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses will evaluate the robustness of the meta-analysis findings. The leave-one-out method will systematically exclude each study in turn, allowing us to assess the influence of individual studies on the overall results. If the direction or magnitude of effect estimates changes markedly with the exclusion of a specific study, it will indicate that the findings are sensitive to that study’s inclusion. Further sensitivity analyses will examine the impact of study quality, data imputation methods, and statistical model assumptions. These analyses will ensure that the conclusions are reliable and not disproportionately influenced by methodological or data-related factors.

Summary of findings table

A summary of findings table will be developed to present the key results of the review in a concise, user-friendly format. This table will include the primary and secondary outcomes, such as perioperative mortality, recoarctation, reintervention, complications, and arterial hypertension, alongside the corresponding effect sizes and confidence intervals. The GRADE approach will assess the quality of evidence for each outcome, considering factors such as risk of bias, inconsistency, imprecision, and publication bias. This table will also include explicit statements about the certainty of the evidence and its clinical implications, enabling readers to quickly assess the reliability and relevance of the findings.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis will address a critical gap in the current evidence regarding the optimal management approach for native CoA in pediatric patients. Despite significant advances in surgical and percutaneous techniques, no consensus remains on the best strategy for achieving long-term safety and effectiveness in this population [22]. This study’s comprehensive comparative outcomes analysis will help bridge these gaps and inform clinical decision-making.

Previous reviews and meta-analyses have been limited by the inability to adequately compare percutaneous treatment with surgical repair due to the limited number of high-quality studies [6–8]. This review will address existing gaps by incorporating the most recent evidence, focusing exclusively on the pediatric population, and conducting a comprehensive analysis to compare the safety and effectiveness of surgical repair, balloon angioplasty, and intravascular stenting for aortic coarctation.
