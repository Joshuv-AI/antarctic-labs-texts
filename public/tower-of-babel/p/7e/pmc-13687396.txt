Comparison of neuromelanin-sensitive magnetic resonance imaging using different magnetic resonance protocols on the same patients with Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Rinka Hirose, Keita Matsuura, Yuichiro Ii, Hiroyuki Kajikawa, Masayuki Maeda, Maki Umino, Ken-ichi Tabei, Akisato Nishigaki, Shotaro Horie, Hidehiro Ishikawa, Ken Kagawa, Akihiro Shindo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In Parkinson’s disease (PD), neuronal loss occurs in the substantia nigra (SN) and locus coeruleus (LC). Previous studies using neuromelanin-sensitive magnetic resonance imaging (NMI) at 3T MRI have described the measurement methods and diagnostic significance of the contrast ratio (SN-CR) and area (SN-A), as well as their association with clinical symptoms. However, no reports have evaluated the consistency of NMIs acquired using different MR protocols in the same patients over a short period. Therefore, this study evaluated the similarity of two-dimensional (2D) NMIs obtained simultaneously under different imaging parameters. The SN-CR, LC-CR, and SN-A were compared in 54 of 55 patients with PD who underwent 2D NMIs within 3 months under different imaging conditions, with one patient excluded due to artifacts. The relationships of these variables with motor and cognitive function were then examined. The mean age of the cohort was 62.3 ± 8.0 years. The mean disease duration was 11.4 ± 5.2 years, and the male-to-female ratio was 25:29. No significant correlation was found for SN-CR (r = 0.233, p = 0.091) and LC-CR (ρ = 0.180, p = 0.192). However, a significant, albeit weak, positive correlation was observed for SN-A (ρ = 0.403, p = 0.003). Standardization of imaging protocols is essential to minimize technical variability.

Introduction

In Parkinson’s disease (PD), neuronal loss in the substantia nigra (SN) and locus coeruleus (LC) can be detected using neuromelanin-sensitive magnetic resonance imaging (NMI) at 3T magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). NMI can be performed in 2D and 3D. Previous studies on 2D-NMI have characterized the analytical methods and diagnostic significance of contrast ratio (CR) and area, as well as their association with clinical symptoms (Matsuura et al. 2013, 2016, 2021; Sasaki et al. 2006; Tanaka et al. 2011; Kusama et al. 2025; Gaurav et al. 2021). We previously reported on the diagnostic utility and symptom associations of different imaging conditions across various patient groups (Matsuura et al. 2013, 2016, 2021). However, no studies have evaluated the consistency of 2D-NMIs acquired with different MR parameters in the same patients over a short period. Theoretically, if the same cases are examined within a short timeframe, a high correlation between images is expected, and the clinical significance should remain equivalent. Confirming this consistency is essential to determine the utility of NMI data acquired across multiple centers or using different scanner protocols. Therefore, this study evaluated the similarity of 2D-NMIs obtained within 3 months under two different imaging parameters previously reported to be useful in patients with PD.

Materials and methods

This study enrolled patients with PD who had undergone 2D-NMI with two different MR parameters within 3 months at our facility. In total, 55 cases were identified between August 2017 and November 2022. Patients were clinically diagnosed on the basis of respective MDS clinical diagnostic criteria (Postuma et al. 2015). Of these, 54 were included in the analysis, with one case excluded due to artifacts. This study was approved by the Ethics Committees of Mie University Hospital and Suzuka Kaisei Hospital (H2019-134, 2019-11).

MRI protocols for NMI

Protocol 1: All images were obtained using a 3-Tesla MR unit (Verio; Siemens AG, Erlangen, Germany) with a 32-channel head coil. The pulse sequence used for NMI was a T1-weighted turbo spin-echo (TSE) technique with the following parameters: repetition time, 550 ms; echo time, 11 ms; echo train length, 4; field of view, 200 mm; matrix size, 448 × 311 (pixel size: 0.45 × 0.64 mm); slice thickness, 2.5 mm; gapless, 6-averaged; acquisition time, 9 min; slices, 12 (Matsuura et al. 2013, 2016).

Protocol 2: All MRI images were obtained using a 3-Tesla MR unit (Ingenia; Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) with a 32-channel head coil. The pulse sequence used for NMI was a T1-weighted (T1W) TSE technique with the following parameters: repetition time, 700 ms; echo time, 13.3 ms; TSE factor, 3; field of view, 180 mm; matrix size, 384 × 307 (pixel size, 0.47 × 0.59 mm); slice thickness, 3 mm; slice gap, 0.3 mm; 9 slices; and acquisition time, 9 min 53 s (Matsuura et al. 2021).

NMIs were acquired in the oblique axial orientation, perpendicular to the floor of the fourth ventricle. The imaging range extended from the posterior commissure to the inferior boundary of the pons. At the level of the lower edge of the inferior colliculus, we calculated the area (SN-A) and contrast ratio (SN-CR) of the SN pars compacta (SNc). SN-A measurements were conducted using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD), following previously described protocols. Furthermore, the signal intensity and contrast ratio (LC-CR) of the LC were assessed at a plane 7 mm caudal to the inferior colliculus, as per established methodology (Matsuura et al. 2013; Matsuura et al. 2016; Matsuura et al. 2021). All NMI measurements were performed by the expert rater (K.M.) who was blinded to the patient data and who established the high reliability of this method in our previous reports (Matsuura et al. 2016, 2021). In these studies, this rater demonstrated excellent inter- and intra-rater reliability (intraclass correlation coefficients) for SN-A (0.94 and 0.86, respectively), SN-CR (0.86 and 0.65, respectively), and LC-CR (0.88 and 0.79, respectively).

Clinical assessments

We evaluated the clinical scores of the Japanese version of the Movement Disorder Society Revision of the Unified PD Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS), Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Japanese version of Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA-J), Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS), Raven’s colored progressive matrices (RCPM), the Trail-Making Test (TMT), Pareidolia test, Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D), Freezing of Gait Questionnaire (FOG-Q), Timed Up and Go test (TUG) and the Parkinson’s disease Sleep Scale-2 (PDSS-2) (Kashihara et al. 2014; Folstein et al. 1975; Fujiwara et al. 2010; Johns 1991; Raven 1941; Partington JE 1949; Uchiyama et al. 2012; Lewinsohn et al. 1997; Giladi et al. 2000; Bloem et al. 2016; Suzuki et al. 2012).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), while power calculations were conducted using G*Power version 3.1.9.7 (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Germany). Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The statistical methods for each evaluation were as follows:

Inter-Protocol Correlation (Table 2): Correlation analyses were performed to assess consistency between Protocols 1 and 2. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) was used for SN-CR, while Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was used to assess LC-CR and SN-A, based on the distribution of the data. Agreement and Conversion Analyses (Figs. 2 and 3): To evaluate the agreement between the two imaging protocols, Bland-Altman analysis was performed for all NMI metrics to assess systematic bias and 95% limits of agreement. Additionally, linear regression analysis was conducted to explore the feasibility of cross-protocol conversion formulas. Correlation with Clinical Assessments (Table 3): The relationships between NMI metrics (SN-CR, LC-CR, and SN-A) and clinical scores (e.g., MDS-UPDRS, cognitive tests, and sleep scales) were analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) for both protocols. Furthermore, because this was a retrospective study, an a priori sample size calculation was not feasible. Instead, a post hoc sensitivity analysis was conducted to determine the minimum detectable correlation coefficient given our sample size (N = 54), assuming an alpha level of 0.05 and 80% power.

Results

The mean age of the 54 patients was 62.3 ± 8.0 years, with a mean disease duration of 11.4 ± 5.2 years (M: F = 25:29). The demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients are shown in Table 1. No significant correlation was observed between the two MRI protocols for SN-CR (r = 0.233, p = 0.091) or LC-CR (ρ = 0.180, p = 0.192). For SN-A, a significant but weak positive correlation was observed (ρ = 0.403, p = 0.003) (Table 2). In our post hoc sensitivity analysis, the minimum detectable correlation coefficient to achieve 80% power at a 5% significance level with 54 patients was 0.400 for SN-CR and 0.257 for LC-CR, respectively. The observed correlation coefficients for SN-CR (r = 0.233) and LC-CR ( ρ = 0.180) fell below this threshold. To evaluate the agreement between the two imaging protocols, Bland-Altman analysis was performed for all NMI metrics. Significant systematic biases were observed across all parameters: Protocol 1 yielded significantly higher values for SN-A (mean bias: 26.81 mm2, p < 0.001) and SN-CR (mean bias: 5.67, p < 0.001), while Protocol 2 showed higher values for LC-CR (mean bias: -1.95, p < 0.001). The 95% limits of agreement were notably wide for all measures (SN-A: -8.32 to 61.94 mm2; SN-CR: -0.12 to 11.47; LC-CR: -8.31 to 4.42), indicating poor interchangeability between the two platforms. Furthermore, a significant proportional error was detected for SN-A (r = 0.56, p < 0.001), suggesting that the discrepancy between protocols increases in patients with larger SN volumes (Fig. 2). Linear regression analysis was also conducted to explore the feasibility of cross-protocol conversion formulas. However, the resulting models demonstrated extremely low coefficients of determination (R2 = 0.13 for SN-A, R2 = 0.05 for SN-CR, and R2 = 0.06 for LC-CR), confirming that simple mathematical correction is insufficient to harmonize data across these different imaging conditions (Fig. 3). Correlations between NMI measures and clinical assessments were inconsistent (Table 3). In Protocol 1, a significant negative correlation was found between MDS-UPDRS Part IV and SN-CR (ρ = −0.306, p = 0.024), while a positive correlation was observed between the ESS and LC-CR (ρ = 0.305, p = 0.025). However, these correlations were not replicated in Protocol 2. In Protocol 2, a significant negative correlation was observed between the apathy scale and SN-CR (ρ = −0.301, p = 0.027). No significant correlations were found for MMSE or MoCA-J in either protocol.

Discussion

Both Protocols 1 and 2 have been previously validated in our prior studies as effective tools for the clinical diagnosis and longitudinal assessment of PD. We previously established the utility of these 2D-NMI sequences for identifying neuronal loss in the SN and LC and for correlating these findings with clinical symptom progression (Matsuura et al. 2013, 2016, 2021). Thus, since their clinical utility has been confirmed, correlation coefficients would theoretically approach 1.0 when the same patients were assessed by NMI within a short time frame.

Contrary to this expectation, we observed only a weak correlation for SN-A and no significant correlation for CR values. The relatively higher correlation observed for SN-A (ρ = 0.403) compared to the contrast ratios (SN-CR and LC-CR) may be attributed to the fundamental differences in the nature of these metrics. SN-A is a spatial and morphometric measure. While its absolute values are undoubtedly altered by slice thickness and interslice gaps, the relative anatomical size differences among patients are somewhat preserved. In contrast, CR is a signal-intensity-based metric. Signal intensity is highly vulnerable to variations in TR/TE, vendor-specific background signal suppression, and radiofrequency coil characteristics (Ji et al. 2022; Trujillo et al. 2024). Therefore, CR metrics are more likely to lose cross-protocol correlation entirely. Furthermore, the larger anatomical volume of the SN makes it relatively more robust against severe partial volume effects compared to the microscopic LC, which easily loses its contrast-to-noise ratio under suboptimal conditions.

The likely primary cause of this discrepancy is the differences in imaging parameters. Specifically, Protocol 1 used 2.5-mm slices with no interslice gap, whereas Protocol 2 employed 3.0-mm slices with a 0.3-mm gap. Variations in slice thickness directly influence the signal-to-noise ratio and the spatial resolution required to capture small structures such as the LC. Indeed, several previous reports have suggested that differences in acquisition parameters can yield inconsistent results (Wengler et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2017; Keren et al. 2009). Furthermore, the use of an interslice gap in Protocol 2 introduces a risk of sampling bias, where the target structure may be missed entirely or fall between acquired slices (Fig. 1). The increased slice thickness in Protocol 2 also likely exacerbates partial volume effects, thereby reducing the contrast-to-noise ratio of the small LC region (Liu et al. 2017; Keren et al. 2009). In addition to differences in slice thickness and interslice gap, other factors such as vendor-specific sequence implementation, radiofrequency coil characteristics, and image reconstruction algorithms may have contributed to the observed discrepancies. Therefore, the variability observed in this study is likely multifactorial rather than attributable to a single parameter. These multifactorial differences clearly manifest in our Bland-Altman and linear regression analyses. The significant systematic bias, where Protocol 1 yielded higher SN-A and SN-CR, may be attributed to thinner slices reducing partial volume effects, thereby preserving the high signal of the SNc. Conversely, the higher LC-CR in Protocol 2 might result from differences in TR/TE or vendor-specific background signal suppression. Furthermore, the significant proportional error observed for SN-A (r = 0.56) is highly suggestive of the impact of the interslice gap in Protocol 2; larger SN volumes span more slices, leading to a cumulatively larger unmeasured area within the 0.3-mm gaps. Crucially, the extremely low coefficients of determination (R2 ≤ 0.13) in our regression models demonstrate that these discrepancies are non-linear. This indicates that simple post-hoc mathematical corrections cannot reliably harmonize data acquired across different platforms or parameters.

Although the relationship between the LC complex and sleep disorders has been established previously, our results were inconsistent between the two platforms. A similar discrepancy was observed in the relationship between apathy scale scores and SN-CR values. To our knowledge, such inconsistencies have not been previously reported. These findings indicate that 2D-NMI metrics are highly sensitive to technical variations, which may limit their utility in multicenter trials or longitudinal studies unless imaging protocols are strictly harmonized. While automated analyses have been proposed to address such variability, establishing a standard automated method remains crucial (Beach et al. 2025; Dunnwald et al. 2021). Integrating such methods in the future is desirable to minimize subjective bias and reduce arbitrariness in NMI assessment.

Study limitations

This study is limited by a small sample size, the lack of a healthy control group, and potential manual measurement errors. Although a blinded expert rater conducted the assessments, manual delineation of the ROIs inevitably introduces some subjectivity. Implementing automated analytical methods could reduce this arbitrariness and potentially improve measurement consistency. Furthermore, we focused strictly on 2D-NMI and did not evaluate 3D-NMI protocols. Another limitation is the lack of test-retest reliability assessment within the same imaging protocol. Because this study utilized retrospectively collected clinical data, it was not feasible to perform multiple scans on the same patients under identical conditions to evaluate intra-protocol scan stability. While the high intra- and inter-rater reliabilities of our measurement method have been established (Matsuura et al. 2016, 2021), future prospective studies incorporating test-retest evaluations are needed to fully distinguish scanner-induced variability from true biological differences. Finally, as indicated by our sensitivity analysis, our sample size of 54 was only powered to detect a correlation coefficient of 0.400 for SN-CR and 0.257 for LC-CR. Therefore, we cannot definitively rule out the possibility of a Type II error regarding the null findings for SN-CR (r = 0.233) and LC-CR (ρ = 0.180). However, in the context of validating protocol interchangeability, a high degree of correlation (e.g., r > 0.8) is required. Even if statistical significance had been achieved for these specific variables with a substantially larger sample size, such weak correlations (r < 0.38) would correspond to extremely low coefficients of determination (R2 < 0.06). Such a weak relationship would not alter our primary conclusion that these protocols lack clinical interchangeability, and simple mathematical correction is insufficient. Furthermore, the diagnosis was based on clinical criteria without neuropathological confirmation. The potential inclusion of other parkinsonian syndromes, such as multiple system atrophy or progressive supranuclear palsy, may have introduced heterogeneity into the cohort and confounded the NMI findings.

Conclusion

2D-NMI measurements of the SN and LC exhibited considerable variability across different imaging conditions. To improve reliability, future efforts should prioritize standardized imaging protocols, 3D-NMI volume analysis, or automated segmentation approaches to minimize human error and technical variability.
