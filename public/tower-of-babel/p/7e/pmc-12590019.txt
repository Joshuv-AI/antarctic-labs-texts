Knockdown of LRP11-AS1 inhibits papillary thyroid tumor growth by modulating miR-615-3p/AKT2 axis
Authors: Peng Li, Zhongguang Wu, Lanlan Li, Yudan Chen, Chi Zhang, Weidong Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Patients with papillary thyroid cancer (PTC) who experience local recurrence and distant metastases have a poor clinical prognosis and low survival rate.•Significant overexpression of LRP11-AS1 was observed in both PTC tissues and PTC cell lines.•Inhibitory effects of LRP11-AS1 knockdown on the growth and migration of PTC cells were verified.•LRP11-AS1 exhibited potential oncogenic effects in PTC by regulating the miR-615-3p/AKT2 axis.

Introduction

Thyroid cancer (TC) is the most commonly occurring form of endocrine cancer, with an estimation of 586,000 new cases in 2020, ranking it as the 9th most prevalent cancers worldwide [1]. Approximately 85% of TC case are attributed to papillary thyroid cancer (PTC), making it the most prevalent subtype [2]. Despite the relatively low mortality rate of PTC, patients with local recurrence and distant metastases experience a poor clinical prognosis and low survival rate [3]. BRAF mutation, occurring in 45% of PTC cases, is the most prevalent genetic alteration in PTC [4]. The BRAFV600E mutation has been found to be associated with an increased risk of recurrence and cancer-related mortality in PTC case [5,6].

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are a class of non-coding RNA molecules, greater than 200 nucleotides in length, that play a regulatory role in gene expression by interacting with DNA, RNA or proteins at various levels of transcription, post-transcription, and translation [7]. Dysregulation of lncRNAs is prevalent and they play crucial roles in various processes related to TC, including tumorigenicity, angiogenesis, proliferation, migration, apoptosis and differentiation. One mechanism by which lncRNAs function is acting as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) that interact with microRNAs (miRNAs), thereby impeding miRNA interaction with target genes [8]. A novel transcript, ENSG00000273132, located on chromosome 6, is a lncRNA that acts in an antisense manner towards low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 11 (LRP11), and we name it as LRP11-AS1. This study aimed to investigate the expression and malignant behavior of LRP11-AS1 in PTC.

In this study, LRP11-AS1 was found to be overexpressed in papillary thyroid cancer, which might promote cell growth and migration by regulating cell cycle and inducing epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Additionally, silencing of LRP11-AS1 was found to suppress the growth and migration of PTC cells by modulating the miR-615-3p/AKT2 axis. These findings suggest that the newly identified long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) LRP11-AS1 could have potential applications as a biomarker and therapeutic target for PTC.

Material and methods

Cell culture, transient and stable transfection of LRP11-AS1

BCPAP, TPC1 and Nthy-ori 3-1 cell lines were obtained from the cell bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences. BCPAP cells and TPC1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, USA) and Nthy-ori 3-1 cells was cultured in F12 medium (Gibco, USA). Both media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Bovogen, Australia) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cells were incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 environment. Smart silencer (RIBOBIO, China) and Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) were used to perform transient knockdown of PTC cells following the manufacturer’s instructions. The LncRNA LRP11-AS1 smart silencer used in this study is a mixture of three small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) and 3 antisense oligonucleotides. The targeted sequences of siRNAs were as follows: 5’-CCAGCAGACCTAGCTGCCA-3’, 5’-CTAGCTGCCAGCAGTGACA-3’, and 5’-CATCCTTTTCTGAGAAAGA-3’. The targeted sequences of antisense oligonucleotides were as follows: 5’-GACAACATGCGTTACCTTCA-3’, 5’-TGCAGCAGTGCCCACTCATA-3’, and 5’-TGGTCATCTGTGCTCTCGCG-3’. Lentivirus was used to achieve the overexpression of LRP11-AS1. The virus was packed using packaging plasmid psPAX2, pMD2.G and PHBLVTM, and recombinant plasmid pHBLV-CMV-MCS-EF1-ZsGreen-T2A-Puro with the sequence of LRP11-AS1 (ENSG00000273132, Hanbio Biotechnology, China). Lentivirus and Polybrene were used to transform PTC cells, which were then selected with 3μg/mL of puromycin. The overexpression of miR-615-3p was achieved by transfecting PTC cells with miR-615-3p mimics and mimics control (RIBOBIO, China) using lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blot

Cells were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with 1 × protease inhibitor (70090050, Biosharp, China) and phosphatase inhibitor (70080020, Biosharp, China) at 4°C. Western blot was conducted following the standard protocol. The antibodies used in the study included: anti-p-mTOR S2448 (2971S, Cell Signaling Technology, CST), anti-mTOR (2972S, CST), anti-p-AKT S473 (4060S, CST), anti-AKT (4691, CST), anti-GAPDH (D16H11, CST), anti-AKT2 (D6G4, CST), anti-cyclin B1 (4138T, CST), anti-N-cadherin (D4R1H, CST), anti-vimentin (D21H3, CST) and corresponding secondary antibodies from CST.

Clinical study and samples from patients

The clinical study protocol and ethics aspects of this research were approved by the Ethics Committee of Shenzhen University General Hospital. Tumor tissues and corresponding adjacent normal tissues were obtained and embedded in paraffin for subsequent analysis.

Extraction of RNA, reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-RT-qPCR)

RNA and miRNA from cells were extracted using an extraction kit (Tiangen, China) and reversed transcribed into cDNA using either the reverse transcription kit (RR037A, Takara) or the miR-X miRNA First-Strand Synthesis Kit (638313, Takara) following the respective manufacturer’s protocols. RNA and miRNA from paraffin-embedded patient samples were extracted using either the RNA extraction kit (DP439, Tiangen, China) or miRNA extraction kit (DP502, Tiangen, China). Quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using the LightCycler 480 instrument (Roche) using a SYBR green RT-qPCR kit (AG11701, Accurate biology, China). The RT-qPCR assays utilized the following primers: The RT-qPCR assays utilized the following primers: LRP11-AS1 forward: 5’-CTAGCTGCCAGCAGTGACAA-3’, LRP11-AS1 reverse: 5’-GCGAGAGCACAGATGACCAC-3’, β-actin forward: 5’-TGGCATCCACGAAACTACCT-3’, β-actin reverse: 5’-ACGGAGTACTTGCGCTCAG-3’, miR-615-3p forward: 5’-TATATATCCGAGCCTGGGTCTCCCT-3’, U6 forward: 5’-AACGCTTCACGAATTTGCGT-3’, U6 reverse: 5’- CTCGCTTCGGCAGCACA-3’. The reverse primer for quantitation of miR-615-3p was provided by Takara. The determination of BRAF mutation in the clinical samples was performed using RT-qPCR, following the protocol described by Schnittger et al [9].

Proliferation and colony formation assays

Cells were seeded at a density of 3000/well in a 96-well plate and transfected with 50 nM of LRP11-AS1 or negative control smart silencers. Cells proliferation was assessed determined using the CCK-8 reagent (Hanbio Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the absorbance at 450 nm (OD450) was measured at different time points. Stably transformed cells overexpressing LRP11-AS1 were seeded at a density of 3000/well in a 96-well plate, followed by CCK-8 assays. A colony formation assay was conducted by seeding 1000 cells/well in a 6-well plate, followed by incubation at 37°C for 14 days. The resulting colonies were fixed with methanol and then stained with crystal violet.

Migration and invasion assays

Cells were transiently transfected with LRP11-AS1 smart silencer or miR-615-3p mimics, along with their respective controls, and incubated for 48 hours prior to conducting the migration and invasion assays. In the transwell migration assay, 2 × 104 cells/well were seeded in the upper chamber of the transwell insert (NEST, 725301) using RPMI medium without FBS. RPMI medium with 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber of a 24-well plate. After allowing migration for 24 hours, the cells were fixed with methanol and stained with crystal violet. The transwell invasion assay was performed by coating the membrane of the transwell insert with Matrigel (Corning, 354234), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The upper chamber of the transwell insert was seeded with a total of 5 × 104 cells/well, which were allowed to invade for 32 hours before fixation and staining.

Flow cytometry

BCPAP cells (6 × 104) were seeded in a 12-well plate and incubated for 24h prior to transfection with LRP11-AS1 smart silencer for 72 hours. After detachment, the cells were stained with a cell cycle analysis kit (550825, BD) and subsequently analyzed using flow cytometry and the BD FACSCanto II instrument.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Fragments of LRP11-AS1 or 3’UTR of AKT2 gene, which includes the binding site of miR-615-3p and the mutant sequence, were generated and subsequently sub-cloned into the pmirGLO Dual-Luciferase reporter plasmid (GENEWIZ, China), respectively. TPC1 and BCPAP cells were seeded in a 24 well plate at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well and incubated for 24 hours. Subsequently, these cells were co-transfection with either miR-615-3p mimics or mimics NC, along with the dual-luciferase reporter plasmids for the wild type (LRP11-AS1 wild type and AKT2 wild type) or the mutant (LRP11-AS1 mutant and AKT2 mutant) luciferase, respectively. Luciferase activity was measured 48 hours after transfection using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (E1910, Promega).

Xenograft nude mice model

Female BALB/c nude mice (4∼6 weeks old) were purchased from Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center (Guangzhou, China) and acclimatized for 7 days in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) chamber. BCPAP cells (1 × 10⁶ cells/mouse) with LRP11-AS1 knockdown or control were subcutaneously inoculated into the axilla of mice. Both tumor volumes and weights were calculated. After four weeks, mice were sacrificed and the tumors were removed, fixed and paraffin-embedded. All procedures complied with the ethical guidelines of Animal Ethics Committee of Shenzhen University Medical School (Protocol No. SYXK-2022-0302).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Immunohistochemical staining for Ki67(Servicebio, Cat.GB111499) and AKT2 (CST, Cat.D6G4), were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions (Servicebio company). The slides were examined to count the Ki67 and AKT2 positive cell area in 4 randomly selected × 400 high-power fields under microscopy. ImageJ software was used to analyze the results of IHC.

Statistical analysis

The quantitative results were reported as the mean ± SD of at least 3 independent experiments. Statistical significance between two groups was assessed using the Student’s t-test or using one-way ANOVA using a Tukey for multiple comparisons where there were >2 groups.

Results

LRP11-AS1 is overexpressed in PTC tissues and cell lines

The expression of LRP11-AS1 was evaluated in clinical samples of PTC and cell lines of PTC. As shown in Fig. 1A, there was a significant up-regulation of LRP11-AS1 in TC tumor tissue specimens compared to the adjacent normal tissues. Cases with the BRAFV600E mutation exhibited higher expression levels of LRP11-AS1 than the wild type group (Fig. 1B). To determine the clinical relevance of LRP11-AS1, its prognostic value was assessed. As shown in Fig. 1C, Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that patients with high expression of LRP11-AS1 had a significantly lower Survival probability compared to those with low expression (p=0.006). Overexpression of LRP11-AS1 was observed in tumor tissue samples of PTC compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1D). LRP11-AS1 exhibited high expression in the PTC cell line TPC1 and BCPAP compared to the normal human thyroid epithelial cell Nthy ori 3-1 (Fig. 1E). Knockdown of LRP11-AS1 inhibits the migration, invasion and EMT of PTC cells

The function of LRP11-AS1 in the migration and invasion of PTC cells was investigated. Knockdown of LRP11-AS1 suppresses the migration of TPC1 and BCPAP cells (Fig. 4A-C). It also inhibits the invasion of TPC1 and BCPAP cells (Fig. 4D-F). LRP11-AS1 knockdown leads to down-regulation of the mesenchymal marker N-cadherin and vimentin (Fig. 4G-J). Additionally, the phosphorylated levels of AKT and mTOR are significantly reduced in LRP11-AS1-silenced TPC1 and BCPAP cells, while the total protein levels remain unchanged. LRP11-AS1 modulates the growth and migration of PTC cells by interacting with miR-615-3p

Potential miRNAs regulated by LRP11-AS1 were predicted by the online lncRNA target predictor, lncRNASNP2 (Figure S1). Among them, miR-615-3p, a tumor suppressive miRNA in various cancers, was selected for further investigation. The expression of miR-615-3p was significantly lower in PTC cells compared to the normal thyroid cell line Nthy-ori 3-1 (Fig. 5A). RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated that silencing LRP11-AS1 significantly unregulated miR-615-3p expression, while overexpression of LRP11-AS1 significantly down-regulated miR-615-3p expression (Fig. 5B and 5C). In addition, overexpression of miR-615-3p significantly down-regulated LRP11-AS1 expression (Fig. 5D). A dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed to investigate the interaction between miR-615-3p and LRP11-AS1. The interacting site between LRP11-AS1 and miR-615-3p is shown in Fig. 5E and Fig. 5F demonstrates that the luciferase activity of the LRP11-AS1-wild type reporter plasmid was significantly suppressed by co-transfection with miR-615-3p mimics compared to the LRP11-AS1-mutant reporter plasmid group. Overexpression of miR-615-3p leads to down-regulation of LRP11-AS1 and inhibition the growth of PTC cells (Fig. 5G-I). These results suggest that knockdown of LRP11-AS1 enhances miR-615-3p expression of in PTC cells and that LRP11-AS1 may interact with miR-615-3p at the identified interacting site. The overexpression of miR-615-3p in PTC cells reversed the enhanced proliferation induced by LRP11-AS1 overexpression (Fig. 5I). Similarly, the overexpression of miR-615-3p reversed the enhanced migration (Fig. 5J and 5L) and invasion (Fig. 5K and 5M) induced by LRP11-AS1 overexpression in PTC cells. These findings indicate that miR-615-3p can counteract the promoting effects of LRP11-AS1 on proliferation, migration and invasion in PTC cells. AKT2 was a target of miR-615-3p and LRP11-AS1 regulated AKT2 through miR‑615‑3p

We performed mechanistic investigation to elucidate the underlying mechanisms by which LRP11-AS1 enhances the growth, migration and invasion of PTC cells. Previous studies have reported that miR-615-3p inhibits the expression of AKT2 by interacting with the 3’-untranslated regions (3’-UTR) of AKT2. In our study, we examined the relative expression of AKT2 in different cell lines and found that the PTC cell line BCPAP and TPC1 exhibited higher expression of AKT2 compared to the normal thyroid cell line Nthy-ori 3-1 (Fig. 6A). We identified a potential binding site on the 3’-UTR of AKT2 for miR-615-3p and confirmed the binding using a dual-luciferase reporter assay (Fig. 6E and 6F). Moreover, overexpression of miR-615-3p downregulated AKT2 expression in PTC cells (Fig. 6B, 6G and 6H). Additionally, knockdown of LRP11-AS1 downregulated AKT2 expression and overexpression of LRP11-AS1 upregulated AKT2 expression in PTC cells (Fig. 6C, 6D, 6I and 6J). These results indicate that LRP11-AS1 targets the miR-615-3p/AKT2 axis in PTC cells. In rescue experiments, it was observed that inhibition of miR-615-3p restored the suppression of AKT2 caused by LRP11-AS1 knockdown at both the transcriptional (Fig. 6K) and translational (Fig. 6L and 6M) levels. Overexpression of AKT2 reversed the anticancer effect of LRP11‑AS1 knockdown in PTC cells

To further investigate the impact of AKT2 in reversing the anticancer effect caused by LRP11-AS1 knockdown, we conducted rescue experiments by overexpressing AKT2 in PTC cells. Discussion

Although most PTC patients generally have a favorable prognosis, there are about 10% of PTC cases that will dedifferentiate into the aggressive type and have poor prognosis [[10], [11], [12]]. LRP11-AS1 was previously reported as a key modulator in promoting the progression of triple-negative breast cancer and colorectal cancer [13,14]. In this study, we found that LRP11-AS1 is significantly upregulated in both PTC tissues and PTC cell lines. We also observed that LRP11-AS1 knockdown inhibited PTC proliferation, migration, and tumor growth in in vitro and in vivo experiments.

We investigated the mechanisms through which LRP11-AS1 promotes the growth, migration and invasion of PTC cells. Bioinformatics analysis revealed a potential interaction between LRP11-AS1 and miR-615-3p. Several studies have reported miR-615-3p to be a tumor suppressive miRNA in various cancers [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. In this study, we observed down-regulation of miR-615-3p in PTC cell lines compared to the normal control cells. Silencing LRP11-AS1 resulted in an up-regulation of miR-615-3p expression, suggesting that LRP11-AS1 targets miR-615-3p. The dual-luciferase reporter assay confirmed the binding interaction between LRP11-AS1 and miR-615-3p. The enhanced proliferation and migration of PTC cells induced by LRP11-AS1 overexpression were reversed by the overexpression of miR-615-3p, indicating that LRP11-AS1 promotes the growth and migration of PTC cells by targeting miR-615-3p.

AKT2, a serine/threonine protein kinase, has been demonstrated to facilitate tumor growth, invasion and metastasis, alongside its various functions in cell proliferation and survival, cellular senescence, and metabolism [21]. Overexpression of AKT2 has been reported in human lung, breast and colon cancer tissues [[22], [23], [24]]. In particular, overexpression of AKT2 promoted proliferation, migration, and invasion of non-small cell lung cancer [25]. AKT2 impacted the proliferation, migration and invasion of lung adenocarcinoma cells by regulating the cell cycle, promoting EMT and enhancing the expression of matrix metalloproteinase [26,27]. Overexpression of AKT2 promoted the growth and invasion of colorectal cancer cells [28]. Up-regulation of AKT2 enhanced viability, migration and invasion, but inhibited apoptosis in hepatocellular cancer cell [29]. The dual-luciferase reporter assay conducted in this study unveiled the interaction between miR-615-3p and the 3′-UTR of AKT2. The use of miR-615-3p mimics resulted in the inhibition of AKT2 expression in PTC cells. Furthermore, silencing of LRP11-AS1 was able to inhibit the expression of AKT2 in PTC cells. Rescue experiments represented that inhibition of miR-615-3p could restore the expression of AKT2 that was inhibited by LRP11-AS1 knockdown, indicating that LRP11-AS1 regulates AKT2 expression through miR-615-3p. Functionally, the over-expression of AKT2 partially counteracted the inhibitory effect of LRP11-AS1 knockdown on the proliferation, migration and invasion of PTC cells. Knockdown of LRP11-AS1 in PTC cells resulted in the upregulation of miR-615-3p, the down-regulation of AKT2, and the inhibition of growth of PTC tumor in nude mice model. LRP11-AS1 exerted a tumorigenic effect in PTC by acting as a sponge for miR-615-3p and targeting the miR-615-3p/AKT2 axis. LRP11-AS1 has the potential to serve as a biomarker for the diagnosis and therapies of PTC.

Building on our finding that LRP11-AS1 functions as an oncogenic driver in PTC, it is plausible that it cooperates with other pro-tumorigenic lncRNAs through overlapping mechanisms such as ceRNA competition, EMT promotion, and activation of PI3K/AKT–MAPK signaling. Many PTC-associated lncRNAs promote malignancy by sponging tumor-suppressive miRNAs, creating a ceRNA “tug-of-war.” In this context, LRP11-AS1 may synergize with lncRNAs like MALAT1 (EMT/proliferation) [30], HOTAIR (chromatin remodeling; higher expression linked to PTC risk) [31], LINC00887 (upregulated and pro-proliferative) [32], ABHD11-AS1 (oncogenic in PTC) [33], thereby amplifying pro-growth signaling and invasive phenotypes within shared regulatory circuits. Conversely, LRP11-AS1 may act antagonistically toward tumor-suppressive lncRNAs frequently downregulated in PTC—such as PTCSC3 [34] and PTCSC2 [35] (classical suppressors), as well as GAS5 [36], and ACTA2-AS1 [37]—whose functions include restraining proliferation, preserving differentiation, and inhibiting EMT. Within a ceRNA framework, these tumor-suppressive lncRNAs could also compete for common miRNA pools, thereby buffering the oncogenic effects of LRP11-AS1. Taken together, current evidence supports a model in which LRP11-AS1 integrates into a broader lncRNA regulatory network, with synergistic and competitive interactions contributing to the complexity of PTC pathogenesis, consistent with recent overviews highlighting extensive lncRNA crosstalk in PTC [38].

There were some limitations in our study. First, while the subcutaneous xenograft model effectively demonstrated LRP11-AS1’s role in driving tumor growth (as evidenced by increased tumor volume/weight and corroborated by Ki67 staining in Fig. 8F), it does not fully capture the metastatic cascade—a critical feature of BRAFV600E-driven malignancies. To address this gap, future investigations should employ orthotopic models to evaluate organ-specific metastasis, or utilize tail vein injection to quantify lung/liver colonization. Second, although our correlative data strongly suggest MAPK/ERK pathway-mediated regulation of LRP11-AS1 (as BRAFV600E tumors exhibit hyperactivation of this pathway), direct mechanistic evidence—such as ERK-dependent transcriptional control remains to be validated. This limitation is partially mitigated by our discussion of plausible regulatory mechanisms, but definitive proof requires targeted experiments like MAPK inhibitor treatments combined with ChIP-seq for transcriptional factor binding.

Conclusion

LRP11-AS1 is frequently overexpressed in PTC, particularly in cases with BRAFV600E mutation, and which could promote proliferation of PTC cells by regulating the cell cycle and enhance migration of PTC cells by regulating EMT. Silencing of LRP11-AS1 inhibits growth and migration of papillary thyroid cancer by modulating miR-615-3p/AKT2 axis. LRP11-AS1 has the potential to serve as a biomarker and therapeutic target for PTC, especially in case with BRAFV600E mutation.
