Pharmacological Characterization of a Novel Complement C3 Inhibitor Antibody Fragment
Authors: Stephanie Jungmichel, Stefan Kreideweiß, Fabian Scheifele, Anna Sobieraj, Philipp Richle, Pankaj Gupta, Daniel Lenherr-Frey, Remko A. Bakker, Leonardo Borras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a leading cause of visual impairment in developed countries, characterized by a slow, progressive deterioration of the macula [1–3]. In advanced cases, AMD is categorized as neovascular (“wet”), which is caused by the development of abnormal blood vessels that leak fluid into the retina, or dry, which involves degeneration of photoreceptors and the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) [1, 2, 4]. Geographic atrophy (GA) represents the late stage of dry AMD [2]. In individuals with GA, macular deterioration leads to progressive, irreversible vision loss [1, 2, 4]. GA affects more than 5 million people worldwide, and will grow to a projected 10 million by 2040, representing a significant healthcare burden [5]. Unlike neovascular AMD, treatment options for GA are limited to pharmacotherapies that provide only a modest reduction in its progression [2, 5, 6].

The complement cascade is a key driver of disease development and progression in AMD and GA, as evidenced by the accumulation of complement proteins, the membrane attack complex (MAC), proinflammatory cytokines, and chemokines in the retina [4, 7]. Several polymorphisms in complement cascade proteins have been linked to dry AMD, implicating complement dysfunction in drusen formation and GA [4]. Ultimately, complement-mediated damage in GA causes the destruction of photoreceptors, RPE, and choriocapillaris, leading to the characteristic atrophic lesions and vision loss [2].

Activation of the complement cascade occurs via three distinct pathways: the classical pathway (CP), the alternative pathway (AP), and the lectin pathway (LP) [4]. All three complement pathways converge prior to the cleavage of complement component C3 into C3a and C3b. C3b then combines with C3 convertase to form C5, which triggers the terminal phase of the complement cascade, culminating in the formation of the MAC [4]. C3b also contributes to a proinflammatory amplification loop, whereby C3b interacts with complement factors B and D to form the convertase C3bBb, which then cleaves further C3 into C3a and C3b [8]. In GA, clinical and preclinical data point toward CP and AP as the main driving pathways [9–12]. As C3 and C5 are central to the complement pathway, these components have become therapeutic targets in GA.

In 2023, the first two treatments were approved by the Food and Drug Administration for GA; both target the complement system [13]. Pegcetacoplan, a C3-inhibiting polyethylene glycol (PEG)ylated peptide, and avacincaptad pegol, a complement component C5-inhibiting PEGylated ribonucleic acid aptamer, were approved based on their effectiveness in reducing the rate of GA progression [13]. Since then, pegcetacoplan and avacincaptad pegol have also received approval in Australia [14, 15], and avacincaptad pegol was granted conditional approval in Japan [16]. Targeting C3 may have a greater impact than targeting C5 due to its earlier position in the complement cascade, thereby providing additional effects against potentially harmful downstream signaling [4, 6]. These treatment options have shown modest reductions in the rate of GA progression, and there remains a significant unmet need for therapies with an improved benefit-to-risk profile.

Bruch’s membrane (BrM) is a barrier between the retina and choroid, limiting protein diffusion between compartments and playing a key role in controlling complement activation within the eye [17]. Molecular transport is primarily limited by the BrM and the residual extracellular matrix (ECM). Transport across the BrM, which is an ECM structure, is predominantly governed by passive diffusion and depends on the physicochemical properties of the molecule, including hydrodynamic size, surface charge, and hydrophobic interactions [17, 18]. With increasing age, the BrM is known to undergo several changes, including thickening due to the formation of basal laminar deposits (BLamDs) and basal liner deposits (BLinDs), which contribute to the pathogenesis of several retinal diseases, including AMD. Both advanced forms of AMD are preceded by the accumulation of extracellular material in the BrM [17].

In GA, most complement activation occurs deep in the retina, on both sides of the BrM [12, 19]; thus, effectively targeting the complement system requires molecules that sufficiently cross the BrM [17, 20]. The molecule discussed is a humanized C3-binding antibody fragment that has been optimized for high solubility and stability and was designed with a small size, which is expected to adequately cross the BrM. The antibody fragment is currently being evaluated for the treatment of GA. In this preclinical analysis, we pharmacologically characterized the antibody fragment and assessed its affinity for different C3 variants, its potency in inhibiting MAC formation, and its ability to traverse the BrM compared with a pegcetacoplan-similar (PEG-s) molecule and the compstatin derivative APL-1.

Methods

Antibody Fragment

The compound is a humanized antibody fragment engineered for enhanced stability, solubility, and production yield that binds with high affinity to C3 and efficiently inhibits complement pathway activation. The antibody fragment is expected to bind to C3 and its breakdown product C3b; no binding to C3a is anticipated.

In Vitro Affinity

The kinetic parameters of the antibody fragment binding to C3 and C3b were assessed using surface plasmon resonance. For the in vitro affinity and in vitro potency assessments (described below), pegcetacoplan could not be sourced at the time of study; therefore, a surrogate non-PEGylated C3-binding synthetic cyclic peptide, resembling APL-1, was used as comparator molecule in the potency assays. APL-1 is a non-PEGylated short-acting C3-binding synthetic cyclic peptide that is a second‑generation compstatin derivative, an early C3 inhibitor in the same family as pegcetacoplan [21, 22]. Pegcetacoplan comprises two APL-1 moieties bridged by a 40-kDa PEG linker, resulting in an extended half-life and 50% higher activity than APL-1 [21]. As such, APL-1 exhibits the same binding behavior to C3 as pegcetacoplan, but without the large PEG linker.

Using a Biacore T100 instrument (T200 sensitivity enhanced), six different C3 variants, including C3 single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) variants associated with GA, were immobilized as ligands on a HC30M SPR sensor chip (XanTec Bioanalytics, Germany) using sulfo-NHS/EDC coupling chemistry. The variants were serum-purified human C3 and C3b (Complement Technology Inc., TX, USA), C3 from cynomolgus macaques, and recombinant human C3, C3 SNP variant P314L, and C3 SNP variant R102G. Ligand capture levels ranged from 4000 to 6000 RU. Five increasing concentrations of the antibody fragment (0.63–10 nM [four 1:2 dilution steps]) and APL-1 (1.2–100 nM [C3, C3b; four 1:3 dilution steps] or 3.1–50 nM [recombinant human C3, C3 SNP P314L; four 1:2 dilution steps]) were injected consecutively using the single-cycle kinetics mode to determine the kinetic parameters. Dilutions and kinetic experiments were performed in HBS-EP + buffer (Teknova Inc., CA, USA). For APL-1, the affinity to the C3 SNP R102G ligand was not determined because this mutation is far away from the APL-1 binding region on the target surface [23]. Additionally, the affinity of APL-1 to cynomolgus C3 was not measured. The data were fitted to a 1:1 Langmuir binding model, and kinetic parameters were determined.

Purification of Human C3 Variants

Recombinant human C3, C3 SNP P314L, and C3 SNP R102G were expressed in Chinese hamster ovary cells (ExpiFectamine CHO Expression Kit; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) and purified from cell culture supernatant using histidine capture chromatography followed by preparative size exclusion chromatography.

Purification of Cynomolgus C3

C3 from cynomolgus serum (Silabe) was purified according to Ruseva et al. [24]. In brief, cynomolgus serum was incubated with sodium sulfate (10%; Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) to remove high-molecular-weight compounds by fractionated precipitation. The dialyzed soluble fraction was further purified by two consecutive anion- and cation-exchange chromatography steps. A final preparative size-exclusion chromatography was performed to remove remaining high-molecular-weight aggregates and impurities from the preparation.

In Vitro Potency

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) and hemolytic assays were used to quantify the extent of complement inhibition with the antibody fragment in human serum preparations. APL-1 was used as the reference to evaluate the potency of complement inhibition. The ability of the antibody fragment and APL-1 to inhibit MAC formation after CP, AP, and LP activation was determined in human serum. In these respective assays, microtiter strips coated with human immunoglobulins, lipopolysaccharides, or mannan specifically activated the CP, AP, and LP pathways, respectively. Formation of the terminal MAC was detected by a specific alkaline phosphatase–labeled antibody to the C5b-9 neoantigen. The amount of C5b-9 generated is proportional to the functional activity of the CP, AP, and LP, respectively.

In addition to the MAC formation assays, the ability of the antibody fragment to inhibit the CP and AP was determined in in vitro hemolytic assays that more closely simulate the in vivo functions of complement activity. Specifically, the antibody fragment and APL-1 were evaluated for their ability to inhibit hemolysis of antibody‑sensitized sheep erythrocytes mediated by CP activation in human serum and hemolysis of rabbit erythrocytes mediated by AP activation in human serum. Further details of the MAC formation and hemolytic assays are provided in the Supplementary Material. Optical density values were converted and expressed as percentage of activation. Half-maximal inhibitory concentration values were determined by applying a four-parameter curve fit using GraphPad Prism. Mean half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were compared numerically. IC50 values were calculated from three independent experiments. No formal statistical analysis was done to compare data sets.

Diffusion Through the BrM

Diffusion through the BrM was investigated for the antibody fragment and compared against a PEG-s molecule (MW 42 kDa; PEG conjugated to two cyclic APL-1 peptides) for up to 72 h using enriched porcine BrM (a suitable model for diffusion experiments due to the similar pentalaminar structure of BrM in humans and pigs [25]) mounted in Ussing chamber devices. Replicates were performed using different membranes in separate Ussing chambers. The antibody fragment and the PEG-s molecule were added together at 100 μg/ml in assay buffer (phosphate-buffered saline containing sodium azide and protease inhibitors) to the sample chamber of each Ussing system, while the diffusate chamber was filled with an equal volume of assay buffer. The Ussing device was incubated for up to 72 h at room temperature with gentle agitation to facilitate diffusion. Samples were removed from both the sample and diffusate chamber at multiple time points and subsequently analyzed using sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis with Coomassie staining for protein detection (for the antibody fragment) or barium iodide solution for PEG detection (for PEG-s molecules). Samples were loaded on NuPAGE Novex 4–12% Bis Tris gels, and electrophoresis was performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific), applying 125 V for 90 min. Bands were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA) [26] and are represented as percentage of the test compound in the diffusate chamber, where 100% refers to the summed band intensities of sample and diffusate chambers in the same Ussing chamber.

Ethical Statement

No ethical approval was required for this study as it did not involve human participants or live animals.

Results

Affinity

The antibody fragment showed high affinity in the picomolar range for all tested C3 ligands, including the GA‑associated C3 SNPs P314L and R102G, and an approximately twofold lower affinity for cynomolgus C3 (Table 1). APL-1 showed a lower affinity compared with the antibody fragment in the single‑digit nM range, comparable with published values [27]. In Vitro Potency

Both the antibody fragment and APL-1 showed dose-dependent inhibition of MAC formation upon activation of the CP, AP, and LP in human serum (Fig. 2). The potency of the antibody fragment was superior to APL-1 in the CP and LP assays and comparable to APL-1 in the AP assay (IC50 values: 79 vs. 402 nM for CP; 344 vs. 333 nM for AP; 67 vs. 222 nM for LP). Evaluation of dose-dependent inhibition in the hemolytic assays after activation of CP and AP in human serum revealed lower mean IC50 values with the antibody fragment compared with APL-1 (74 vs. 709 nM for CP, 705 vs. 2535 nM for AP). Discussion

This preclinical analysis showed that the anti-C3 antibody fragment exhibits high affinity for the evaluated C3 variants and high potency in inhibiting MAC formation; both were substantially greater than those of the second-generation compstatin derivative, APL-1. The antibody fragment was able to cross the BrM more effectively than the PEG-s molecule.

Furthermore, the antibody fragment exhibited superior potency for inhibiting CP- and LP-mediated complement activation compared with APL-1, although potency differences were less apparent for AP-mediated complement activation.

Because pegcetacoplan could not be sourced at the time of the study, and the synthesis of pegcetacoplan surrogates did not result in the predicted potency levels for complement inhibition, the predecessor molecule APL-1, a synthetic cyclic peptide lacking the conjugation to PEG as present in pegcetacoplan, was used as the reference molecule in our analyses (Boehringer Ingelheim, Data on file, 2024). Pegcetacoplan was reported to exhibit a 50% higher activity on a molar basis than APL-1 [21], supporting the superior potency of the antibody fragment for complement inhibition in all CP- and LP-related assays, as well as in the hemolytic assay for AP.

Previous studies have shown that protein diffusion across the human BrM depends on molecular weight, hydrodynamic radius, molecular shape, post-translational modifications, and propensity to interact with BrM components [18]. In diffusion experiments using enriched porcine BrM mounted in Ussing chamber devices, the antibody fragment showed a better penetration profile than the PEG-s molecule. Even though pegcetacoplan has a MW of 42 kDa, which is in the range of freely diffusible proteins [18], its large hydrodynamic radius, attributed to the linear 40 kDa PEG chain, which has a hydrodynamic radius of 9.58 nm [28], likely impedes diffusion across the BrM. In contrast, the antibody fragment has a lower molecular weight and a hydrodynamic radius of 2.5 nm, as corroborated by dynamic light-scattering measurements (Boehringer Ingelheim, data on file, 2024).

In GA, the RPE is largely lost and is often functionally compromised in the junctional zone surrounding GA lesions. The movement of molecules is limited by the BrM and the residual ECM by passive diffusion. Transport across the BrM becomes increasingly restricted with age because of membrane thickening, lipid accumulation, and structural remodeling. These changes can also be seen in the development of advanced stages of AMD with the accumulation of BLamDs and BLinDs and the formation of drusen in the BrM [17]. Consequently, demonstrating efficient diffusion across the BrM in an in vitro model and showing a clear advantage of the smaller antibody fragment over alternative therapeutic formats are important. Although the limited availability of human donor eyes precluded direct assessment of human BrM, the porcine BrM shares key structural and compositional features with the human membrane. Therefore, we consider the porcine model a relevant and appropriate system to demonstrate that the antibody fragment permeates the BrM more efficiently than larger molecules with higher hydrodynamic radii.

Therefore, the antibody fragment is particularly well suited to block greater amounts of C3 on both sides of the BrM, potentially reducing lesion growth in GA more efficiently than pegcetacoplan. In addition, inhibiting complement activity in the choriocapillaris may more effectively prevent the subsequent formation of the proinflammatory anaphylatoxin C5a, which can freely diffuse across the BrM and increase the risk of wet AMD [29]. Several complement inhibitors targeting different components of the complement cascade and/or specific complement pathways have been evaluated in the clinic for the treatment of GA; however, up to now, pegcetacoplan, which binds both C3 and C3b, and avacincaptad pegol, which targets C5, are the only complement inhibitors with moderate positive clinical results for the treatment of GA. By combining effective complement inhibition with the ability to permeate tissues and cross the BrM, the antibody fragment has the potential to become best-in-class therapy for patients living with GA.

While this study provides important insights into the pharmacological properties of the antibody fragment, several limitations should be considered. First, all experiments were conducted in vitro; therefore, the findings may not fully reflect the complexity of complement activation and regulation in vivo within the human retina. Second, although porcine BrM is structurally comparable to human BrM, it may not fully replicate age-related changes or pathological alterations observed in patients with GA, which may influence molecular diffusion. Longer-term safety and tolerability will require evaluation in clinical studies. Taken together, these findings support further investigation of the antibody fragment to better understand its therapeutic potential and long-term impact in patients with GA.

Conclusion

The antibody fragment is a potent and specific C3 inhibitor that displays dose-dependent inhibition of MAC formation across all three complement pathways and effectively crosses the BrM. In line with these promising preclinical results, the antibody fragment is now being investigated in clinical trials in humans.
