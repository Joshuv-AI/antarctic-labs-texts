Learning to navigate interprofessional boundaries in health care
Authors: Julia Paxino, Christy Noble, Walter Eppich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Health professionals must navigate complex systems—and that means negotiating role boundaries. Making this “boundary work” explicit in HPE can strengthen collaboration, clarity, and patient care. #HealthProfessionsEd #InterprofessionalEducation

In health care, professional roles are becoming increasingly challenging to navigate. More clinical complexity demands specialisation, while workforce shortages require role diversification. 1 These pressures are blurring the previously clear demarcations between health care professions and disciplines. 1 Professional boundaries can clarify responsibilities and protect scope of practice, but when they become rigid rather than negotiable, they can constrain collaboration and jeopardise safe patient care. 1 For example, Cruess and Sternszus 1 present a vignette in which a general surgery resident preparing to discharge a patient after an uncomplicated surgery discovers the patient is experiencing homelessness. With no social worker available, the resident is told by their supervisor that addressing housing is out of their scope, an approach that risks patient safety and leaves the trainee unclear about their responsibilities. This vignette highlights that boundaries, while formally sanctioned through legal or regulatory frameworks, are enacted informally through everyday practices influenced by hierarchy and power. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Rather than reinforcing such rigidity, Cruess and Sternszus call for education to prepare health care professionals to recognise and negotiate boundaries that structure health care practice. 1 These negotiations require boundary work. Boundary work is defined as the ongoing effort to negotiate professional boundaries in practice 2 , 6 and is enacted through everyday communication and interactions as teams collaborate to achieve safe patient care. 2 , 3

In this commentary, we examine how boundaries are negotiated through discourse (patterned communication and interactions that shape shared meaning) and argue that interprofessional curricula must develop practical, discursive skills for managing increasingly blurred professional boundaries. Cultural‐historical activity theory (CHAT) provides a useful lens for examining boundary work as collective, negotiated activity and highlights the central role of discourse alongside other mediating elements. 7 Within this framing, boundaries emerge through the interaction of rules (formal and informal expectations), division of labour (role distributions and accountability) and mediating artefacts such as language (the spoken, written and nonverbal ways team members communicate) and material tools (material and digital artefacts used to mediate communication and action, e.g., documentation or clinical protocols). 7

From a CHAT perspective, teams engage in the activity of boundary work to negotiate responsibility and meaning in pursuit of a shared objective (e.g., safe discharge). Language and tools are not separate. Together, they shape how teams perform interprofessional boundary work. Language frames how professionals talk about, negotiate and make sense of work; tools structure interactions, embed assumptions about roles and responsibilities and reinforce particular ways of working across boundaries. In interprofessional practice, meaning is co‐produced through language and mediated by tools across professional boundaries; these discursive and material elements can enable alignment and shared understanding or, if misaligned, produce fragmentation. 8 For example, standardised communication tools, such as the SBAR (Situation–Background–Assessment–Recommendation) handover form, use common terminology and structured prompts that can make expectations explicit and support coordinated action. 4 , 6 , 8 , 9 Conversely, profession‐specific language, such as jargon or acronyms, or poorly designed tools can obscure meaning and create gaps in responsibility. 4 , 6 , 8 , 9

Structured language and tools can strengthen collaboration across professional boundaries by prompting shared reflection on goals, strategies and outcomes and identifying process improvements. 8 , 10 Practices such as team huddles and debriefings help align goals and make tacit assumptions explicit. 11 However, we must recognise that the capacity of language and tools to support interprofessional boundary work depends heavily on context. Discursive elements that enable coordination at one time may lose effectiveness as work practices, teams and contexts evolve. 6 Therefore, ongoing evaluation and reflexivity play an essential role in ensuring that language and tools facilitate, rather than hinder, boundary work and the negotiation of safe patient care.

To engage in this essential evaluation and reflexivity, health professionals require a clear understanding of the discursive practices that underpin interprofessional boundary work. As such, these skills must be incorporated into health professions education. To reduce rigid professional boundaries, interprofessional curricula must move beyond ‘bringing professions together’ and move towards illuminating how discursive practices shape collaboration.

This curricular shift includes the integration of practical, discursive skills (how to use language) and the deliberate use and critique of mediating artefacts (how to use and adapt communication tools), so learners can perform boundary work effectively as part of everyday clinical practice. For example, interprofessional education curricula might include team critical reflection on profession‐specific tools to identify instances of shared language and profession‐specific jargon. Simulation of interprofessional scenarios with intentionally ambiguous or overlapping roles (e.g., a discharge scenario where medical stability, functional safety, caregiver capacity and environmental risk overlap, requiring the team to negotiate meaning and responsibility rather than relying on profession‐specific criteria alone). The scenarios are then followed by structured debriefings that foreground how language and tools shaped shared sense‐making and decisions. Further, facilitators can then guide learners to question assumptions about shared understanding and actively solicit alternate perspectives from different professions. Deliberate team reflection offers a mechanism for this process which creates more inclusive, collaborative interprofessional teams. 10

Teaching the deliberate use and critique of language, communicative practices and mediating tools offers a concrete way to equip future health care professionals to engage proactively when professional boundaries blur. By focusing on how to use language and tools in everyday interactions, health professions education can better prepare clinicians to negotiate boundaries, sustain safe interprofessional practice and ultimately improve patient care.
