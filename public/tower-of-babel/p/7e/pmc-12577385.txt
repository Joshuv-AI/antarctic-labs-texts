Investigating the role of homocysteine and B vitamins in gestational diabetes mellitus: Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Xiaojing Zhan, Huilian Chen, Yifei Lin, Yiming Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a significant pregnancy complication, and its relationship with serum levels of B vitamins and homocysteine (Hcy) remains unclear. This study aims to investigate the causal associations between genetically predicted serum levels of B vitamins (vitamin B6, B12, and folate) and Hcy with GDM risk.MethodsIndependent single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with Hcy (n = 14), Hcy (independent of BMI) (n = 11), vitamin B6 (n = 16), vitamin B12 (n = 8), folate (n = 14), and folate (independent of BMI) (n = 13) were selected as instrumental variables (IVs) based on genome-wide significance. Summary-level data for GDM were sourced from the FinnGen consortium.ResultsThe MR analysis demonstrated a significant association between serum Hcy levels and GDM risk [OR (95% CI) = 1.28 (1.09, 1.51), p = 0.003] using the inverse variance weighting (IVW) method. Following the exclusion of BMI-related IVs, a suggestive association was observed between serum Hcy levels and GDM risk [OR (95% CI) = 1.26 (1.04, 1.54), p = 0.019].ConclusionsOur study indicates a suggestive association between Hcy levels and GDM, emphasizing the necessity for additional research to explore the role of Hcy in GDM management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-025-01234-1.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is characterized by any form of glucose intolerance that develops or is first identified during pregnancy [1, 2] and can be classified as A1GDM and A2GDM [3]. GDM is associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes [4] and poses a significant risk to maternal and infant health [5]. Over recent years, the global prevalence of GDM has been on the rise due to the increasing obese population [6], and this trend is expected to continue in the future. The public health implications of GDM are increasingly apparent, leading to a substantial health and economic burden [7]. Given the high prevalence and significant burden of GDM, it is crucial to identify associated risk factors and develop effective prevention strategies.

B vitamins, such as folate (vitamin B9) and vitamins B6 and B12, serve as essential cofactors in single-carbon transfer reactions, playing key roles in the synthesis of monoamine neurotransmitters, phospholipids, and nucleotides [8, 9]. Insufficiency in any of these B vitamins can result in elevated levels of homocysteine (Hcy) in the blood [10]. Homocysteine, an intermediate metabolite of methionine [11], is a non-proteinogenic sulfhydryl-containing amino acid [12]. Methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) gene mutations have been linked to increased expression of homocysteine, particularly in women with low folate intake [13]. Previous research has demonstrated that elevated Hcy levels independently increase the risk of GDM [14–16]. In the metabolism of Hcy, B vitamins play a crucial role, including folate, vitamin B6, and vitamin B12 (Fig. 1). Hcy is either remethylated to methionine, involving the transfer of a methyl group from 5-methyltetrahydrofolate (the active form of folate) or betaine, or it is irreversibly removed via the transsulphuration pathway. In the latter process, Hcy combines with serine to form cystathionine. High levels of folate [17] and vitamin B12 [18] have been significantly associated with the risk of GDM. Conversely, Radzicka S et al. found no disparity in serum Hcy levels between the GDM and control groups [19]. There is conflicting evidence concerning the involvement of folate and B12 in the observational epidemiology of GDM [20, 21], and the precise pathway linking them remains unclear. Traditional observational studies may carry biases such as confounders or reverse causality [22], which could hinder the accurate determination of the causal relationship between B vitamins, Hcy, and GDM, leaving the causal role of Hcy and B vitamins in the development of GDM uncertain.Fig. 1Overview of the Roles of Folate, Vitamin B6, and Vitamin B12 in Homocysteine Metabolism

Mendelian randomization (MR) serves as a crucial epidemiological statistical approach utilizing genetic instrumental variables (IVs) from large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) to investigate genetic causality between exposures and outcomes [23, 24]. Previous studies have employed MR methods to investigate the causal relationships between serum Hcy, vitamin B levels, and conditions such as obesity [25], mental disorders [26], and other diseases. To our knowledge, there have been no studies analyzing MR for B vitamins, Hcy, and GDM. Hence, it is necessary to delve deeper into the causal link between GDM and these nutrients.

In the current study, we adopted a two-sample MR methodology using summary statistics from a vast GWAS to evaluate the causal relationship between serum B vitamins (folate, vitamin B6, B12) and Hcy levels and GDM.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was conducted according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology using Mendelian Randomization (STROBE-MR) guidelines (Table S1) [27]. The schematic diagram of this study design is shown in Fig. 2. Briefly, we used a two-sample MR approach to investigate the causal relationship between exposure (serum levels of vitamin B6, vitamin B12, folate, and Hcy) and the outcome (gestational diabetes mellitus). When selecting single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables (IVs), the following three assumptions must be met [23]: (1) the relevance assumption, where the IVs should be strongly associated with the exposure; (2) the independence assumption, where the IVs should not be associated with confounders; (3) the exclusivity assumption, where the IVs should affect the outcome only through the exposure (Fig. 2). All data used in this study were obtained from publicly available research from studies with relevant participant consent and ethical approval. Therefore, this study did not require additional institutional review board approval.Fig. 2The schematic diagram of this study design

Data on the genetically predicted serum levels of homocysteine and B vitamins

We conducted a search for published GWAS studies assessing individuals of European ancestry in the GWAS Catalog and PubMed (the latest search accessed in July 2024). GWAS summary-level data related to genetically predicted serum Hcy levels were sourced from a meta-analysis encompassing 10 studies with a total sample size of 44,147 individuals of European ancestry, which measured Hcy concentrations in the blood (Table 1) [28]. Additionally, GWAS summary-level data on the genetically predicted serum levels of B vitamins (vitamin B6, vitamin B12, and folate) were obtained from the UK Biobank Consortium, which includes 64,979 individuals of European descent (Table 1). Information of data sources

Data on the genetically predicted risk of gestational diabetes mellitus

GWAS summary-level data for the genetically predicted GDM were obtained from the FinnGen consortium, a project that has been ongoing in Finland since 1970 (Table 1). This study utilized data from the FinnGen consortium R10 release data, which includes a total of 123,579 individuals of European descent, comprising 5,687 GDM patients and 117,892 controls, reflecting a prevalence of 4.60%. According to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10), both primary and secondary diagnostic codes for GDM were O24, indicating gestational diabetes. This dataset provided information on the associations between instrument SNPs and the risk of GDM.

Selection of instrumental variables

During the IVs extraction phase, we applied the following criteria to define the SNPs as IVs. First, SNPs strongly associated with Hcy and B vitamins were identified at the genome-wide significance threshold (p < 5 × 10−8). However, we selected SNPs with a more relaxed threshold (p < 5 × 10−6) to identify a sufficient number of candidate instruments related to folate, vitamin B6, and vitamin B12, due to the limited number of such SNPs. Subsequently, SNPs exhibiting linkage disequilibrium were filtered out, employing a correlation threshold parameter set at r2 ≤ 0.001 and kb ≤ 10,000. SNPs with minor allele frequencies (MAFs) ≤ 0.01 were excluded. During the harmonization of exposure and outcome datasets, ambiguous SNPs—those with non-concordant alleles or palindromic with intermediate allele frequencies—were excluded [29]. Weak instrumental variable bias in MR analysis was assessed using F-statistics [30], as detailed in Supplementary Table S2. All IVs had F-statistics exceeding 20, demonstrating that the genetic variants used were strong instruments. We applied the NHGRI-EBI Catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/) and the LDtrait tool (https://ldlink.nih.gov/?tab=ldtrait/) [31] (the latest search accessed on July 2024) to ensure that each SNP selected was independent and free from potential confounders. And SNPs associated with the potential confounder (BMI) were removed. Finally, we selected 14 SNPs for Hcy, 11 SNPs for Hcy (independent of BMI), 16 SNPs for vitamin B6, 8 SNPs for vitamin B12, 14 SNPs for folate and 13 SNPs for folate (independent of BMI) for MR analyses. Summary statistics for these selected SNPs are presented in Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Two-sample univariate MR analyses were performed independently to evaluate the causal effects of genetically predicted serum levels of Hcy and B vitamins (vitamin B6, vitamin B12, and folate) on the risk of GDM. We employed several MR methods, including inverse variance weighted (IVW), weighted median (WM), simple mode, weighted mode, and MR-Egger, to obtain MR estimates. These methods were chosen due to their different assumptions about horizontal pleiotropy. The IVW method, based on a multiplicative random-effects model, was the primary statistical approach and assumes that the instruments affect the outcome only through the exposure of interest, without engaging in alternative pathways [32]. To address pleiotropy, we employed four additional MR approaches that relax IVs assumptions: MR-Egger, WM, simple mode, and weighted mode methods [33–35]. Sensitivity analyses were conducted with different methods to verify the robustness and validity of the results. Cochran’s Q test was used to assess heterogeneity [36], while the MR-Egger intercept test and MR-PRESSO global test were applied to detect horizontal pleiotropy [33, 37]. Scatter, forest, and funnel plots were used to display the results and stability of the MR analysis. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the result’s robustness, removing each unique SNP iteratively. To minimize the risk of Type I errors due to multiple statistical tests, we applied a Bonferroni correction, adjusting the significance level to p < 0.0125 (0.05/4) [38]. p-values above this threshold but below the conventional level (p < 0.05) were categorized as suggestive of causal associations [39]. All statistical tests were two-sided, and R software (version 4.3.3), along with the TwoSampleMR and MR-PRESSO packages, were used for analysis.

Results

Causal relationship from serum homocysteine levels to GDM

For serum Hcy levels, Fig. 3 presents the results of MR analyses for its causal relationship with GDM. The IVW method reveals a significant association [IVW, OR (95% CI) = 1.28 (1.09, 1.51), p = 0.003], while the WM method shows a suggestive association [WM, OR (95% CI) = 1.24 (1.01, 1.54), p = 0.047]. Results from other MR methods were similar but not statistically significant (all p > 0.05). Scatter plots were generated to assess the impact of each MR method, revealing a positive trend between Hcy and GDM (Fig. 4A). Cochran’s Q test and leave-one-out analysis indicate no significant heterogeneity in MR results (MR-Egger, Q = 11.131, p = 0.518; IVW, Q = 12.595, p = 0.480) (Table S3 and Figure S1A). MR-Egger intercept test (Intercept = 0.016, SE = 0.013, p = 0.250) and MR-PRESSO global test results display a symmetric funnel plot (Table S4 and Figure S2A), indicating no significant horizontal pleiotropy (Global p > 0.05). Additionally, the forest plot also indicates balanced distribution and effect of each SNP (Figure S3A). To eliminate BMI as a confounding factor in the causal relationship between serum Hcy levels and GDM, first, we excluded the BMI-associated SNPs rs154657, rs7130284, and rs548987 using the NHGRI-EBI Catalog and LDtrait tool. We then reassessed the causal relationship between Hcy and GDM, independent of BMI. The IVW method reveals a suggestive association [IVW, OR (95% CI) = 1.26 (1.04, 1.54), p = 0.019]. Results from other MR methods were not statistically significant (all p > 0.05; Fig. 3). But scatter plots illustrate a positive trend between Hcy and GDM (Fig. 4B). Cochran’s Q test (MR-Egger, Q = 4.626, p = 0.866; IVW, Q = 5.383, p = 0.864) and leave-one-out analysis showed no heterogeneity (Table S3 and Figure S1B). MR-Egger intercept test (Intercept = 0.012, SE = 0.014, p = 0.407) and MR-PRESSO global test indicated no pleiotropy (Table S4). The funnel plots and forest plots are shown in Figures S2B and S3B.

Causal relationship from serum B vitamins levels and GDM

For serum levels of vitamin B6, vitamin B12, and folate and GDM, Fig. 3 shows no significant association between these B vitamins and GDM (IVW: all p > 0.05), with corresponding scatter plots shown in Figs. 4C-E. In the sensitivity analysis, including Cochran’s Q test and leave-one-out analysis, reveal no significant heterogeneity in the MR results (all p > 0.05) (Table S3 and Figures S1C-E). Additionally, MR-Egger intercept test (all intercept p > 0.05) and MR-PRESSO global test (Table S4) did not indicate any evidence of horizontal pleiotropy. Lastly, funnel and forest plots demonstrate balanced distribution and effects of each SNP (Figures S2C-E and S3C-E).

Using the NHGRI-EBI Catalog and LDtrait tools, we found no BMI-related SNPs in the IVs for vitamin B6 and vitamin B12, but identified one BMI-related SNP (rs2449166) in the IVs for folate. After excluding this SNP, we re-evaluated the causal relationship between serum folate levels and GDM, independent of BMI. Figure 3 shows no significant association between serum folate levels and GDM (all p > 0.05), with corresponding scatter plots in Fig. 4F. Cochran’s Q test and leave-one-out analysis indicate no significant heterogeneity in the MR results (all p > 0.05) (Table S3 and Figure S1F). MR-Egger intercept test (all intercept p > 0.05) and MR-PRESSO global test (Table S4) revealed no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy. Funnel and forest plots are shown in Figures S2F and S3F.

Discussion

This two-sample Mendelian randomization study examined the potential causal roles of B vitamins and Hcy in GDM. The analysis identified the suggestively potential causal association between elevated Hcy levels and an increased risk of GDM, suggesting that higher Hcy levels may contribute to the development of GDM. Even after eliminating BMI-related instrumental variables, it remains plausible that elevated Hcy levels have a suggestive genetic association with GDM. However, no evidence was found to support a causal association between serum levels of B vitamins and the risk of GDM.

Numerous observational studies [18, 40, 41] and meta-analyses [42, 43] have established the important role of B vitamin levels (folate and vitamin B12) in GDM. Weelden et al. [44] did not find evidence supporting an association between folate and vitamin B12 levels and GDM, which is consistent with our findings. We propose two possible reasons for the lack of association. First, most markers of vitamin B12 status are physiologically influenced during pregnancy, which complicates the accurate assessment of vitamin B12 levels [45]. Second, the relationship between B vitamins and GDM may vary across different populations and stages of pregnancy. Research has shown that low vitamin B12 levels during the mid-to-late stages of pregnancy may be linked to an increased risk of GDM, particularly in Asian populations [46]. In contrast, no significant association was observed in European populations or during the early stages of pregnancy [46]. However, previous research indicates that higher folate levels [18, 40, 47] are significantly associated with an increased risk of GDM. Additionally, while some studies suggest that higher vitamin B12 levels [18] are significantly linked to GDM risk, others [40, 43] have reported that vitamin B12 deficiency is associated with an increased risk of GDM. The contradictory findings regarding the relationship between vitamin B12 and GDM risk in the available literature highlight the complexity of this issue. Some results differ from our study, and several factors may explain these discrepancies. First, observational studies often struggle with bias due to confounding risk factors and may not effectively control for reverse causality. Second, our study focused on serum vitamin B levels, which may not always accurately reflect dietary intake and plasma concentrations of B vitamins. To address these limitations, we employed MR approach, which enhances the credibility of our findings. Further large-scale studies from diverse regions and populations are needed to clarify the precise relationship between B vitamins and GDM.

In addition, previous research on serum Hcy and GDM [48–50] has demonstrated that serum Hcy levels are significantly elevated in GDM patients, independent of other confounding factors. Interestingly, some studies [51, 52] have identified elevated Hcy as an independent predictor of GDM, particularly during mid-pregnancy. Liu et al. [53] also showed that Hcy-related dietary patterns are positively associated with GDM. These findings support our conclusions and offer a practical approach to the early prevention and management of GDM.

Recent evidence [47] suggests that elevated Hcy levels may contribute to metabolic disorders, including insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, liver damage, and oxidative stress, ultimately leading to GDM. As a pro-inflammatory factor, Hcy influences GDM development through three main mechanisms: (1) Insulin resistance: Elevated Hcy induces oxidative stress in adipose tissue, reducing insulin sensitivity and promoting resistance. (2) Immune response: Increased insulin secretion due to insulin resistance leads to oxidative stress in β-cells, which promotes inflammation, macrophage infiltration, and worsens insulin resistance. (3) Circulatory disturbance: Hcy-induced inflammation causes vascular constriction and microthrombosis, leading to tissue hypoxia and worsening GDM complications. Additionally, excess Hcy excretion in GDM patients creates a cycle that worsens both Hcy levels and GDM progression [54]. Therefore, elevated Hcy levels, through oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, and vascular damage, play a significant role in GDM’s onset and progression, affecting the health of pregnant women. Hcy should be considered a key clinical marker in both pre-pregnancy and prenatal assessments.

This study has several notable strengths. First, employing the MR design allowed us to simulate a randomized controlled trial within the framework of an observational study. While clinical randomized controlled trials provide the highest level of evidence for establishing causality, they are often prohibitively expensive and challenging to conduct. MR studies, on the other hand, can effectively mitigate confounding and reverse causality due to the random assignment of alleles during gamete formation. Consequently, our MR study offers more robust statistical validity compared to previous findings, although some weak associations may still be missed. Second, given the increasing prevalence of GDM among women, our findings hold significant implications for the prevention, management, and treatment of GDM, particularly concerning the role of Hcy. Our results suggest that serum Hcy levels may be able to serve as an early potential predictor of GDM onset.

However, there are some limitations to consider. First, changes in serum micronutrients may affect GDM patients differently across trimesters. The lack of individual-level data in the summary statistics restricted our ability to stratify the GDM population by gestational period. Second, while vitamin supplementation can influence serum vitamin B levels, our study did not account for whether participants received such supplementation, potentially affecting the vitamin-outcome relationship. Third, the GWAS data utilized in this study are derived from a European population. Although efforts were made to reduce population structure bias, this limitation may affect the generalizability of our findings to other populations. Therefore, caution should be exercised when interpreting these findings in relation to different ethnic groups and even global populations. Future research should aim to use larger GWAS samples from diverse populations to validate our results further. Fourth, we chose an SNP inclusion threshold of 5× 10−6, which may be a potential limitation of our study. Although it makes the analysis more inclusive, it may introduce weaker tools into the MR analysis. Therefore, this relatively loose threshold and its potential bias should be taken into account when interpreting the results. Finally, the MR method has inherent limitations. Although we employed a range of strategies to mitigate the impact of multi-effectivity, we acknowledge that, even with negative test results, the potential bias introduced by unknown multi-effectivity effects on the outcomes cannot be eliminated.

Conclusion

In summary, this study found a suggestive association between Hcy and GDM. Specifically, genetically determined higher circulating levels of Hcy were suggestively linked to an increased risk of GDM according to MR methods. Our study indicates that reducing Hcy levels could be a potential strategy for preventing GDM, providing a rationale for targeting Hcy reduction as a preventive measure. Further research is required to elucidate the impact of Hcy on the onset and progression of GDM, and to confirm this association through large-scale randomized controlled trials and rigorous scientific animal experiments.
