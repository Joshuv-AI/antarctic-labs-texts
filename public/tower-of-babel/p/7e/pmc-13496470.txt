Renal Function Equations and Medication Dosing: The Daily Clinical Dilemma
Authors: Witt Durden, Amna Anees, Joel Topf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn 1976, Donald Cockcroft and M. Henry Gualt published a formula that estimates creatinine clearance. This study involved only Caucasian males and was done prior to the development and standardization of the modern creatinine assays. With the major advances in medicine and nephrology that have occurred over the past half century, it seems almost inconceivable that we would still be using this formula as the standard for medication dosing, yet this is the clinical reality. We explore the tension between guidelines that suggest using eGFR for medication dosing and the practical reality of our current system and the knowledge gaps that have led to continued reliance on this formula.Keywords: Renal function estimate, Medication dosing, GFR, Creatinine clearance

Introduction

Our knowledge of renal physiology is remarkable with ever-growing advances in diagnostics and treatments related to kidney diseases. Despite this, we still struggle answering a simple question in our daily practice: What estimate of renal function should we use to base medication dosing? We illustrate this point by a case. A 62-year-old paraplegic male with history of a chronic indwelling foley catheter, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, and a chronic stage IV sacral decubitus ulcer presented to the emergency room with concern for a urinary tract infection and sepsis. He was empirically started on Intravenous vancomycin and meropenem while the appropriate evaluation and management for suspected sepsis was initiated. The metabolic panel at our institution reports a creatinine clearance (CrCl) value based on the Cockcroft-Gault (CG) formula and an estimated GFR (eGFR) using the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) non-race-based equation (Table 1). His CrCl was reported as 116 mL/min and the eGFR was 104 mL/min/1.73 m2 (serum creatinine of 0.7 mg/dL). Medications, specifically antibiotics, were dosed based on these values. However, due to concern that his low muscle mass may overestimate his true GFR, a cystatin C based eGFR (based on the 2012 CKD-EPI equation) was ordered that showed an eGFR of 26 mL/min/1.73 m2 (cystatin C of 2.24 mg/L). The eGFR based on the 2021 combined CKD-EPI creatinine-cystatin C equation was 49 mL/min/1.73 m2. Finally, a 24-h CrCl was performed that reported a CrCl of 70 mL/min (urine volume 1800 mL and urine creatinine of 39.4 mg/dL). With renal function estimates ranging from 26 to 116 mL/min, we were left trying to determine which value to use to anchor our treatment plan on. The two extremes of kidney function estimates can either lead to increased toxicity risk of medications or subtherapeutic treatment of a critically ill patient.

Guideline review

Both the KDIGO 2024 CKD guidelines and the National Kidney Foundation's KDOQI US commentary on these guidelines address medication dosing. They recommend that in most clinical settings, validated eGFR equations using serum creatinine are appropriate for drug dosing. When more accuracy is required, they recommend using an equation combining creatinine and cystatin C or obtaining measured GFR.1,2 Notably, neither group recommends using CrCl to base medication dosing. Until recently, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) required pharmaceutical companies to establish dosing ranges in CKD using the Cockcroft-Gault (CG) equation. The FDA now recognizes eGFR as an accurate method to use in drug pharmacokinetic studies in patients with kidney disease. However, in the most recent 2024 guidelines, they still include measured CrCl as “reasonable to assess renal function in pharmacokinetic studies”.3

Exploring the problem

Guidelines suggest using eGFR or even measured true GFR (mGFR) for medication dosing. However, KDIGO commentary notes that nearly all pharmacokinetic studies have used CrCl estimated by the CG formula, and very few studies have been conducted to validate using eGFR for medication dosing.1 Practicing providers who look up renally adjusted medication dosing using any common reference guide, will find most of the medication dosing to be based on CrCl. Many providers erroneously equate the values of eGFR and CrCl and risk making major dosing errors. The KDOQI work group commentary suggests multiplying the CrCl by BSA/1.73 m2 to align units with eGFR (mL/min),2 however, few providers are aware of this suggestion and there is limited evidence to support this practice. Thus, we have a major concern that seems under addressed: what are frontline providers supposed to do? Without strong practical or evidence-based guidance, most providers continue to use CrCl to base their medication dosing decisions. The question becomes, is it the right approach and could it be causing harm?

A recently presented abstract at the National Kidney Foundation 2025 Clinical Meeting by Yuan Khor et al. examined carboplatin dosing, a chemotherapeutic agent with a narrow therapeutic index typically dosed based on CrCl. The researchers hypothesized that CrCl may overestimate GFR, leading to excessive dosing and increased toxicity compared to using the eGFR-CR-CYS equation. Their findings showed that 26 % of patients required significant dose adjustments. The group who received the excess doses had higher rates of anemia, thrombocytopenia, acute kidney injury, and death at 90 days.4 This data highlights that the CG formula is not only less accurate but may lead to adverse events.

While it is true that for many medications with a wide therapeutic index, the risks are lower, and dosing thresholds may not differ significantly regardless of the method used. There is, however, risk for harm in using the CrCl formula with medications that have a narrow therapeutic index, in elderly patients and in those at extremes of body weight.5

Call to action

In 1976, Donald Cockcroft and M. Henry Gualt developed a formula that estimated CrCl (Table 1). This study involved only Caucasian males and was done prior to the development and standardization of modern creatinine assays.1 It is almost inconceivable that nearly 50 years later we would still be using this formula as the standard for dosing medications, yet this is the reality.

Clinical practice paradigm shifts are often slow. Ideally, we should transition from the CG formula as soon as possible, but the CG formula is embedded in our current system, and this will be a challenge to quickly rectify. Hospital based providers often rely on clinical pharmacists for dosing recommendations, many of whom use the CG formula and advocate for its use.6 Additionally, many electronic medical record (EMR) systems still use CG-based alerts. As highlighted previously, current guidelines clearly endorse the use of eGFR equations for medication dosing, and these equations are undeniably superior to the CG formula. However, almost all pharmacokinetic studies were performed using CG as the standard and very few studies have been conducted to validate using eGFR for medication dosing. Practicing clinicians receive information from clinical trials in the form of product labels. Translating this information into daily decisions, however, requires considerable nuance. For instance, many pharmacists in the hospital setting use clinical judgment and deviate from standard dosing protocols. Despite its limitations, CG equation can still provide a reasonable approximation for quick assessments or when a precise GFR is not essential. However, when dosing medications with a narrow therapeutic index and those with potential for toxicity, additional caution is required as certain populations are prone to inaccurate CrCL estimates. These include patients at the extremes of weight, females, and cancer patients as they were not included in the CG study group.1 We need further studies to address this knowledge gap.

While we cannot abruptly and completely abandon the CG formula, we certainly need a sense of urgency to develop and implement evidence-based alternatives. This needs to be a high priority for future research studies. Additionally, strong advocacy is needed to urge the FDA to remove the CG equation from the design of all future pharmacokinetic studies.

Conclusion

Circling back to our case, not surprisingly, standard vancomycin dosing based on CrCl was used. Based on this dosing standard, the usual dose would have been 2 g every 12 h. However, using clinical judgement, the team started conservative dosing at 1500 mg every 12 h. In a scenario familiar to many hospitalists, the patient subsequently lost IV access twice and, as a result, received only 1500 mg once daily for the next 2 days. Appropriately timed Vancomycin levels were collected with a trough of 10.3 mcg/mL and a peak of 12.7 mcg/mL. This correlated to a therapeutic area under the curve (AUC) of 496. So, the serendipitous issue of losing IV access and massive underdosing from the standard protocol led to therapeutic dosing in our patient!

Despite advancements in estimating renal function, the CG formula is inextricably embedded in our system. While more accurate equations exist, they require further validation before they can be integrated into routine medication dosing decisions. Real time mGFR is on the horizon and may soon become a clinical reality,7 but even then, we must know how to translate this data to medication dosing. In the meantime, effective clinical decision making requires a nuanced approach while knowing the strengths and limitations of our current data and of each test and formula.
