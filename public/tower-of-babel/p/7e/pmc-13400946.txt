Reverse Remodeling in Pediatric Myocarditis With DCM Phenotype Impact of rASD and PAB
Authors: Thushiha Logeswaran, Hakan Akintürk, Dietmar Schranz, Karin Klingel, Matthias Müller, Alina Schnabl, Kerstin Gummel, Lucie Rüblinger, Bettina Reich, Martin Farr, Christian Jux, Blanka Steinbrenner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyocarditis progressing to a dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) phenotype carries a high risk of end-stage heart failure in early childhood.ObjectivesGiven the age-dependent regenerative capacity of the immature myocardium, we evaluated a staged, pathophysiology-guided strategy combining left atrial decompression and pulmonary artery banding (PAB) to promote myocardial recovery in selected infants and young children.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 31 children <3 years (55% female; median age 312 days) with biopsy-proven myocarditis and a DCM phenotype treated between 2013 and 2024. All underwent standardized multimodal assessment, including echocardiography, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, invasive hemodynamic assessment, and endomyocardial biopsy.ResultsAt presentation, all patients were in Ross functional class > III; median BNP was 2,258 pg/mL (IQR: 770-4331), and 90% required inotropic support. The mean echocardiography-derived left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction at admission was 24% ± 6.2% with marked LV dilatation (mean z-score +5). The median LV end-diastolic pressure (was 20 mm Hg (IQR: 15-23), indicating elevated filling pressures. A restrictive atrial communication (restrictive atrial septal defect or patent foramen ovale dilatation) was created in 17 patients for atrial decompression. Seventeen patients met the predefined criteria for surgical PAB, including severely reduced LV ejection fraction (mean 19% ± 6%), marked LV dilatation (z-score +6 ± 1.6), and preserved right ventricular function. During a median follow-up of 2 years (IQR: 1-7) overall survival was 90%. Heart transplantation was required in 19%; mechanical circulatory support in 6%. Among patients selected for PAB, 76% achieved sustained functional recovery.ConclusionsA staged, pathophysiology-guided strategy incorporating left atrial decompression and PAB may promote myocardial recovery in selected young children with myocarditis-associated DCM.Key words: dilated cardiomyopathy, mechanical circulatory support, myocarditis, pulmonary artery banding, recovery, restrictive ASD

Viral infections represent the leading cause of myocarditis in children, and a major contributor to severe heart failure in this population.1 Viral injury and the subsequent inflammatory response activate signaling pathways that result in cardiomyocyte necrosis or apoptosis.2,3 Persistent inflammation accelerates adverse left ventricular (LV) remodeling characterized by interstitial fibrosis, ultimately leading to a dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) phenotype.4

This substantial risk of progression to end-stage heart failure necessitates mechanical circulatory support (MCS) and heart transplantation (HTx) as rescue therapies. Contemporary registry data indicate that children with myocarditis are listed for HTx with higher clinical acuity and a greater need for MCS compared with other cardiomyopathy etiologies.5

Outcomes for infants supported with ventricular assist devices have improved considerably, with mortality rates below 10% in patients weighing <5 kg.6 However, Pedimacs data highlight the high-risk profile of this population: although children with cardiomyopathy supported by ventricular assist device demonstrate survival rates exceeding 90%, approximately 65% undergo transplantation within 6 months of support, whereas durable myocardial recovery remains rare (<5%).7

These findings suggest that once myocarditis progresses to persistent inflammation with an established DCM phenotype, spontaneous recovery is unlikely. Nevertheless, given the intrinsic regenerative and adaptive potential of the immature myocardium—particularly in infants and younger children8—therapeutic approaches to promote myocardial recovery warrant further investigation.

In myocarditis, impaired relaxation and increased ventricular stiffness may occur early in the disease course, driven by inflammation, myocardial edema, and microvascular dysfunction, leading to elevated LV filling pressures.

Clinically, this pathophysiology contributes to pulmonary venous congestion and may culminate in acute respiratory failure.9 Sustained elevation of pulmonary afterload can further compromise right ventricular (RV) performance, increasing the risk of RV-pulmonary artery (PA) uncoupling.10

PA banding (PAB) modulates ventricular-ventricular interaction (VVI) by inducing a leftward shift of the interventricular septum, thereby reducing LV cavity size and improving ventricular geometry.11 This geometric change restores a more favorable preload-contractility relationship and has been associated with improved LV ejection fraction (LVEF).12 In addition, RV systolic performance may improve through an Anrep-effect, whereas diastolic filling may benefit from septal repositioning and improved ventricular synchrony.

With increasing clinical experience using PAB to promote cardiac recovery in children with DCM and end-stage heart failure, it has become evident that patient age and myocardial developmental stage critically influence recovery potential. The immature myocardium in infants and young children appears to retain greater capacity for functional recovery.8

Given the poor prognosis in young children presenting with subacute or chronic myocarditis and a DCM phenotype, we report our single-center experience with a pathophysiology-guided diagnostic and interventional strategy. Patients underwent comprehensive multimodal assessment and were treated according to the predefined criteria, including optimized guideline-directed heart failure therapy, transcatheter creation of a restrictive atrial septal defect (rASD) in patients with diastolic LV dysfunction, and surgical PAB in selected patients with advanced LV remodeling and preserved RV function (Central Illustration).

Reverse Remodeling in Pediatric Myocarditis With DCM Phenotype: Impact of Restrictive Atrial Septal Defect and PAB

A comprehensive multimodal assessment identified children with myocarditis with a DCM phenotype. In patients with elevated left ventricular (LV) filling pressures, a rASD creation provided atrial decompression reducing pulmonary venous congestion, improving left atrial-ventricular coupling, and consequently right ventricular (RV) filling. In biopsy-proven myocarditis, progression to a remodeling DCM phenotype is characterized by severe left ventricular dilatation and interstitial fibrosis, both indicative of adverse remodeling. In selected patients with preserved RV function, pulmonary artery banding (PAB) promoted reverse remodeling and sustained myocardial recovery. Methods

Study design and patient population

This retrospective, single-center study included pediatric patients younger than 3 years of age who were diagnosed with myocarditis-associated DCM and required inpatient treatment between 2013 and 2024.

All patients were managed according to a standardized institutional diagnostic and therapeutic protocol, with individualized adjustments based on clinical status and disease severity.

Medical therapy

Intravenous immunoglobulin was administered to all patients at the time of diagnosis. Standardized heart failure therapy was initiated in all children and followed a triple neurohumoral blockade strategy to promote reverse remodeling. Core components included selective β1-adrenergic blockade, long-acting angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin II type 1 receptor antagonists, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists.

Hemodynamic assessment and interventional strategy

In patients with echocardiographic evidence of diastolic dysfunction and elevated LV end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP) (>15 mm Hg), or with signs of postcapillary pulmonary hypertension, the creation of a rASD was considered to facilitate left atrial decompression. In patients with advanced LV remodeling and preserved RV function under optimized heart failure therapy, PAB was considered. In cases of progressive hemodynamic deterioration despite optimized medical therapy, MCS was implemented as rescue therapy.

Ethical approval

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from legal guardians for interventional procedures, including creation of a restrictive atrial communication and/or surgical PAB. Institutional ethics committee approval was granted under PAB protocol (JLU-Giessen 191/13).

Diagnostic work-up

Clinical data collection included age, sex, weight, height, admission date, and severity of heart failure, as assessed by the modified Ross Classification. The need for mechanical ventilation, inotropic support, and prior cardiopulmonary resuscitation were recorded. Heart failure biomarkers, including brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) and cardiac troponin I, were documented at presentation and follow-up.

Detection of viral genomes followed a virus-adapted sampling strategy, including (reverse transcription [RT-]) polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis of endomyocardial biopsy (EMB), EDTA blood, urine, and stool.

Echocardiography

Cardiac function was assessed using transthoracic echocardiography in accordance with current pediatric imaging standards. LVEF was calculated using the Simpson biplane method. LV end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD) was assessed and calculated using CardioZ (Pediatric Reference Database Evelina Children's Hospital). LV dilatation was defined as an LVEDD z-score >+2,13 with severe dilatation characterized by an LVEDD z-score >+4. Severe LV systolic dysfunction was defined as LVEF <35% despite optimized medical therapy, confirmed by echocardiography and/or cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Mitral valve regurgitation was categorized in 3 grades, structural mitral valve disease excluded.

VVI was assessed in apical four-chamber and parasternal short-axis views, with particular attention to asymmetric septal motion and altered LV contraction geometry. RV systolic function was evaluated using tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion. The presence of an atrial shunt—rASD or patent foramen ovale (PFO)—and its flow characteristics, including Doppler-derived pressure gradients, were documented.

Cardiac MRI

Cardiac MRI was performed using a 3-T MAGNETOM Verio scanner (Siemens Medical Systems). The protocol included cine imaging for ventricular function and volumetry, T1-and T2-weighted sequences, and late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) imaging following intravenous gadolinium administration. The following parameters were recorded: LVEF and RV ejection fraction (RVEF); LV and RV end-diastolic and end-systolic volumes; and the presence and distribution of LGE. All parameters were entered into the study database.

Cardiac catheterization and EMB

All patients underwent diagnostic cardiac catheterization to assess hemodynamics and exclude structural abnormalities, including coronary anomalies. LVEDP was measured when clinically feasible. In patients with LVEDP >15 mm Hg and evidence of diastolic dysfunction, a rASD was created using the standard Brockenbrough transseptal technique. After transseptal puncture, gradual balloon dilatation was performed to establish a restrictive interatrial communication allowing controlled left-to-right shunting and left atrial decompression.14, 15, 16

Three to 4 EMB samples were obtained from the right interventricular septum for histological and immunobiological analysis. PCR (RT-PCR) was performed to detect cardiotropic viral genomes in myocardial tissue and peripheral blood samples.

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry

Myocardial tissue samples were analyzed by histology, immunohistology, and molecular diagnostic techniques. Acute lymphocytic myocarditis was defined as the presence of >25 lymphocytes per mm2 with increased CD68+ macrophages.

According to the modified Dallas criteria, acute myocarditis required evidence of active myocyte necrosis with inflammatory infiltrates, whereas chronic myocarditis was characterized by persistent inflammatory infiltrates with focal or diffuse myocardial fibrosis. Ongoing myocarditis was defined as >14 inflammatory cells/mm2 (lymphocytes and macrophages) in combination with myocyte necrosis and fibrosis.1,17

Genetic testing

When feasible, next-generation sequencing was performed to identify pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants associated with cardiomyopathy. A custom gene panel (Twist Bioscience) targeting established DCM-associated genes was applied. Variant analysis was conducted using JSI Sequence Pilot software and incorporated multiple databases (HGMD, ClinVar, gnomAD, Ensembl) as well as in silico prediction tools (e.g., REVEL). Variants were classified according to the current American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines.

Outcome measures

The primary endpoint was overall survival, including survival after HTx. The secondary endpoint was functional myocardial recovery, defined as LVEF >55% and normalization of age-adjusted LVEDD, defined as a z-score <2, on serial echocardiographic/MRI assessments, and clinical improvement to Ross Class I during follow-up. Partial recovery was defined as improvement of LVEF to 40% to 45% with reduction of LVEDD z-score without full normalization.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.0) and R (version 4.5.1, R Core Team). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR), as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For paired analyses, baseline and follow-up measurements obtained from the same patients were compared using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed rank tests, as appropriate. Effect sizes for paired comparisons were calculated using Cohen’s d. Longitudinal changes in LVEF and LVEDD z-scores over follow-up were assessed using linear mixed-effects models.

Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to estimate overall survival and freedom from death or HTx. All tests were 2-sided. Given the exploratory nature of the study and the limited sample size, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed. Estimated effects, corresponding 95% CIs and P values should be interpreted cautiously.

Results

Patient cohort and clinical severity

Thirty-one infants and young children with biopsy-confirmed myocarditis and a DCM phenotype were included. The median age at admission was 312 days (IQR: 120-593 days), the median body weight 7.9 kg (5.9-10.5 kg), and 17 patients (55%) were females. All patients were referred for consideration of MCS or high urgency listing due to advanced heart failure.

At admission, all patients were in modified Ross functional class (FC) > III, including 17 (55%) in Ross FC IV. Twenty-five (81%) required intensive care; mechanical ventilation was required in 12 (39%). Five patients had a history of resuscitation. Inotropic support was administered to 29 patients (93%), at minimum as continuous milrinone infusion. Two patients required immediate extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), 1 for refractory ventricular tachycardia (VT) and 1 for rapidly progressive biventricular failure. Intravenous immunoglobulins were administered in all cases.

Heart failure severity was reflected by markedly elevated BNP levels, with a median BNP of 2,258 pg/mL (IQR: 770-4331 pg/mL).

The clinical course was predominantly subacute or chronic, with symptom duration exceeding 4 weeks in 25 patients (81%). Guideline-directed medical therapy with angiotensin-converting enzyme-inhibitor, beta1-blocker, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist was initiated in those cases and was well tolerated.

Cardiac imaging

Cardiac MRI was performed in 28 of 31 patients. MRI was not feasible in 2 patients on ECMO; both had echocardiographic LVEF <20%. One additional patient was excluded due to arrhythmia. Among patients, who underwent MRI, the mean LVEF was 23.6% ± 10% and mean RVEF was 53.5% ± 12.5%. LVEDD was 43 ± 8.5 mm, corresponding to a z-score of +5 ± 2. LGE was present in 9 of 27 (33%), including transmural patterns in 2.

Genetic testing

Genetic testing was performed in 15 patients (48%), 3 had variants of uncertain significance; no pathogenic or likely pathogenic mutations were detected.

Clinical phenotype and results of endomyocardial biopsy

EMBs were obtained in all patients. Histopathological analysis revealed acute lymphocytic myocarditis in 8 patients, a subacute inflammatory pattern in 4, chronic lymphocytic myocarditis in 16, and healed myocarditis with a DCM phenotype in 3. Viral genomes were detected in 26 cases (84%); viral detection was based on a multispecimen diagnostic approach by (RT-) PCR, including blood, urine, stool, and EMB. Parvovirus B19 DNA was detected most frequently with concordant myocardial and systemic detection (n = 14, 45%). Interstitial fibrosis was present in 27 patients (84%) with a focal pattern in 10 and a diffuse pattern in 17. Evidence of ongoing myocarditis, reflected by myocyte necrosis, was observed in 11 patients (35%).

Hemodynamics and interventional strategy

Cardiac catheterization was performed after a median of 8 days (IQR: 3-20) following admission. LVEDP was measured in 29 patients and was elevated, with a median of 20 mm Hg (IQR: 15-23). All children showed clinical and radiographic signs of pulmonary venous congestion. Atrial decompression was performed in 17 patients (55%). Five patients had a pre-existing PFO with a median LVEDP of 14 mm Hg and did not require enlargement, whereas balloon dilation was performed in 2 patients with a pre-existing PFO and a median LVEDP of 20 mm Hg.

A rASD was created in 15 patients. In the rASD-group, median LVEDP was 22 mm Hg (IQR: 18-24 mm Hg). In 2 patients, progressive septal restriction with increased pulmonary congestion necessitated reintervention. In both cases, an interatrial septal stent was implanted to maintain sustained atrial decompression.

Following atrial decompression, a controlled interatrial gradient of approximately 5 to 10 mm Hg was achieved.

Nine patients did not undergo atrial septal intervention (median LVEDP 18 mm Hg), including 2 with elevated LVEDP and high right atrial pressure, which limited the expected benefit.

Notably, in 3 patients with reduced RV function at admission, rASD creation was associated with improved RV function.

The median interval between catheterization and PAB was 14 days (IQR: 6-29 days).

PAB cohort

Seventeen patients met the predefined criteria for PAB. Pre-PAB MRI demonstrated severe LV dysfunction (mean LVEF 19% ± 6%) with preserved RV function in most patients (mean RVEF 58% ± 10%). Three patients exhibited moderately reduced RV function (median RVEF 40%), which improved after atrial decompression. The mean LVEDD was 45±8 mm, corresponding to a mean z-score of +6 ± 1.6, indicating significant LV dilatation. EMBs revealed interstitial fibrosis in all cases, with a focal pattern in 5 and a diffuse pattern in 12. Figure 1 summarizes the inclusion criteria for surgical PAB.

Overall survival in the PAB group was 94%. Thirteen of 17 patients (76%) achieved full functional recovery, with normalized LVEF and LVEDD, and sustained improvement to Ross FC I. Four patients were classified as nonresponders. Two with transmural LGE—consistent with infarction secondary to Coxsackievirus B3 myocarditis—underwent successful HTx. The remaining 2 patients showed initial improvement after PAB but proceeded to HTx before the potential effect of PAB could be evaluated; 1 of these patients died 6 months after Tx due to severe rejection. No patient in the PAB group required MCS.

The median follow-up was 3 years (range: 1-9 years). Responders (n = 13) were discharged after a median hospital stay of 49 days. Functional recovery was observed within 6 months (median 113 days) after PAB. The median PAB gradient was 57 mm Hg (Figure 2).

Overall cohort outcome

During a median follow-up of 2 years (IQR: 1-7 years), overall survival in the entire cohort was 90%. Six patients underwent HTx during the course of the disease. Full functional recovery was achieved in 21 patients (68%), with a mean LVEF of 56.4% ± 8.4% and a LVEDD z-score of 1.1 ± 1.3 at follow-up. Three additional patients showed partial recovery with improved systolic function (mean LVEF 47%) but with persistent ventricular dilatation. Three deaths occurred: One patient due to refractory VT despite 35 days of ECMO. A second patient, who had achieved partial recovery (LVEF 45%, LVEDD z-score+4), died suddenly from VT 12 months after discharge despite resuscitation attempts. The third death occurred after HTx due to severe, treatment-refractory rejection. A summary of clinical outcomes and follow-up is provided in Table 3.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of biopsy-confirmed myocarditis with a DCM phenotype, we systematically evaluated a pathophysiology-guided strategy combining staged left atrial decompression and PAB.

To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first structured analysis of PAB within a predefined mechanistic treatment algorithm in this population.

Our findings suggest that personalized, age- and disease-specific drug therapy, combined with mechanistic interventional-surgical hybrid procedures, promotes immediate hemodynamic improvement and enables cardiac remodeling and functional recovery.

A young age also appears to facilitate recovery in cases of advanced LV dilatation with severe systolic dysfunction, despite histological evidence of interstitial fibrosis, provided that RV function is preserved or restored.

Step 1: left atrial decompression

In patients with elevated LVEDP and diastolic dysfunction, creation of a restrictive atrial communication served as an unloading strategy independent of subsequent PAB. This approach targets a key early pathophysiological mechanism in myocarditis-associated DCM, namely left-sided filling pressures with pulmonary venous congestion.18 In contrast to the maladaptive RV response in postcapillary pulmonary hypertension, the RV response to a centrally placed PAB reflects a controlled increase in afterload without impairing the compliance of the pulmonary vascular bed. This maintains RV-PA coupling through adaptive hypertrophy and enhanced contractility.10,18,19 The distinction between passive pulmonary congestion and adaptive RV conditioning is central to the staged strategy applied in this study and has broader implications for the interpretation of elevated right-sided pressures in pediatric heart failure.

Step 2: pulmonary artery banding as reverse-remodeling therapy

PAB was reserved for patients who met strict criteria under optimized medical therapy,20 including marked LV dilation (z-score ≥+4) and LVEF <35% with preserved RV function. Systematic EMB revealed that patients selected for PAB predominantly exhibited a chronic inflammatory pattern with fibrosis and minimal necrosis, consistent with an unfavorable remodeling phenotype rather than active inflammatory damage. The chronic inflammatory pattern suggests persistent structural remodeling as a possible mechanism for ventricular dilation13,21 despite the recognized regenerative potential of the immature myocardium.8,22

Progressive LV dilatation increases wall stress according to Laplace law, thereby perpetuating maladaptive remodeling and ultimately heart failure.23 PAB modifies VVI by shifting the septum to the left, thereby promoting structural and functional restoration, including synchronized cardiac contraction.24

In our cohort, 76% of PAB-treated patients achieved full functional recovery. Longitudinal mixed-effects modeling demonstrated that most functional recovery and reverse remodeling occurred within the first 6 to 12 months after PAB, followed by sustained stabilization during follow-up. Notably, none of the PAB-treated patients required MCS, and HTx was avoided in most cases. These findings suggest that even in advanced remodeling and fibrosis, unloading the LV through geometric reconfiguration can restore contractile function, provided RV function is preserved.

Patients who did not undergo PAB exhibited a distinct phenotype, often characterized by a less dilated left ventricle, more pronounced diastolic dysfunction, and often accompanied by RV dysfunction, arrhythmias, or hemodynamic instability.

Study limitations

This study is limited by its retrospective, single-center design and small sample size, which restricts statistical power and generalizability. Treatment allocation was not randomized and reflects institutional expertise and evolving practice patterns and therefore selection bias cannot be excluded. Patients undergoing PAB represented a clinically selected subgroup with preserved or recoverable RV function.

In addition, follow-up was not fully standardized across the cohort. Patients undergoing PAB were consistently followed at our center, whereas some patients managed conservatively were followed in external outpatient settings.

Finally, the molecular mechanisms underlying interventricular interaction and reverse remodeling after PAB remain incompletely understood. Further prospective multicenter studies incorporating advanced and biomarker profiling are needed to refine patient selection and clarify the mechanistic pathway associated with recovery.25

Conclusions

A staged, pathophysiology-guided strategy combining transcatheter rASD creation and surgical PAB was associated with substantial reverse remodeling and high rates of functional recovery in infants and young children with advanced myocarditis-associated DCM.

In subacute myocarditis with elevated filling pressures and moderate LV dilation, atrial decompression effectively reduces pulmonary congestion and stabilizes hemodynamics, thereby improving both LV filling and RV performance.

In advanced LV dilatation, however, atrial unloading alone appears insufficient to reduce LV wall stress. Under these conditions, PAB may serve as a targeted reverse-remodeling intervention by restoring LV geometry, reducing wall stress, and improving systolic performance. Prospective multicenter studies are warranted to validate selection criteria, optimize timing, and define the roles of rASD creation and PAB within contemporary pediatric heart failure algorithms.
