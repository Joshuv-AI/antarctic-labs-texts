Evolving aspects of oral care in modern nursing: a systematic review
Authors: Elham Rasoulian Barzoki, Somayeh Fallah, Samaneh Marofi, Mahla Talebi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOral care is a critical yet often neglected aspect of nursing, essential for preventing complications like ventilator-associated pneumonia and systemic inflammation. Despite its importance, nurses face challenges due to limited training, time constraints, and inconsistent protocols. The evolving role of nurses in oral care reflects broader healthcare shifts toward interprofessional and patient-centered models. This systematic review examines these dynamics to inform evidence-based improvements.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, this systematic review synthesized qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods studies from 2015 to 2025, sourced from PubMed, CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and grey literature. Search terms targeted nurses’ roles, perceptions, barriers, and interventions in oral care. Eligible studies involved registered nurses or students in healthcare settings, with data extracted on study characteristics, outcomes, and interventions. Quality was assessed using JBI and MMAT tools, and a narrative synthesis integrated findings via thematic analysis using NVivo.ResultsFrom 2,347 records, 32 studies across 12 countries were included, covering ICUs (n = 12), general wards (n = 8), and nursing homes (n = 5), with sample sizes ranging from 7 to 1,576 participants. Key themes emerged: inadequate knowledge due to limited education, variable attitudes influenced by setting, inconsistent practices lacking standardized tools, and barriers like workload and resource shortages. Enablers included enhanced training, with over 80% of nurses in multiple studies seeking further education, patient empowerment, interdisciplinary collaboration, and policy support. Training programs, such as virtual reality simulations, improved knowledge and confidence, while resource availability enhanced practice quality.ConclusionNurses’ effectiveness in oral care is hindered by educational gaps, systemic barriers, and inconsistent prioritization, but bolstered by targeted training and resources. These findings can guide nursing curricula and hospital policy reforms to prioritize oral care globally. Comprehensive nursing curricula, evidence-based protocols, and interdisciplinary collaboration are essential to elevate oral care as a core responsibility. Future research should focus on scalable interventions in diverse settings to enhance patient outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07000-3.Keywords: Systematic review, Nursing education, Interdisciplinary collaboration, Oral health, Oral care, Oral hygiene, Nursing

Introduction

Oral care is a vital yet frequently undervalued component of comprehensive nursing care, particularly in hospital settings where patients are acutely ill, elderly, or dependent on others for personal hygiene. The oral cavity plays a critical role in overall health and well-being, serving as a gateway to the respiratory and gastrointestinal systems [1]. Consequently, inadequate oral hygiene is not merely a discomfort or aesthetic issue but a serious health concern that has been linked to preventable complications such as ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), endocarditis, poor glycemic control in diabetic patients, and increased systemic inflammation [2]. Oral health plays a crucial role in maintaining systemic well-being, especially among hospitalized and critically ill patients, where poor oral hygiene can precipitate respiratory infections, sepsis, and delayed recovery. The neglect of oral care was particularly evident during the COVID-19 pandemic. Many ICU-bound patients experienced inadequate oral hygiene management, contributing to complications such as ventilator-associated pneumonia and secondary infections [3]. Several reports noted that these issues were largely due to the lack of structured oral care protocols and insufficient training among frontline nursing staff, who were already overwhelmed with respiratory management. This recent example underscores the consequences of neglecting oral care in vulnerable patients and reinforces the need to integrate comprehensive oral hygiene practices into nursing curricula and hospital protocols. Despite this, oral care training in many settings remains inconsistent and underemphasized [4–6]. Among critically ill or ventilated patients, lack of oral care can lead to colonization of the oral flora with pathogenic microorganisms, increasing the risk of aspiration and respiratory infections. Despite the evidence supporting its importance, oral care remains one of the most neglected aspects of nursing care worldwide [7].

The problem is multifaceted. Studies have consistently shown that nurses often deprioritize oral care in favor of more urgent or visible medical needs, viewing it as a low-status or auxiliary task. This perception is reinforced by systemic barriers such as time constraints, high patient-to-nurse ratios, lack of standardized protocols, insufficient training, and the absence of dedicated oral care tools [8]. In many cases, nurses report feeling unprepared or unsupported to deliver oral hygiene effectively, particularly in high-acuity units such as ICUs, oncology wards, and long-term care facilities. The invisibility of oral care in most clinical auditing systems further exacerbates the problem, as it is rarely monitored or evaluated against performance indicators [9].

Another critical issue lies in the educational system. Nursing curricula in many countries provide minimal coverage of oral health beyond basic hygiene principles [10]. As a result, new graduates often enter the workforce with limited knowledge about the connection between oral health and systemic disease, the importance of individualized oral care assessments, and the use of evidence-based interventions [11]. This gap in education contributes to inconsistent practice patterns and reduces the visibility of oral health as a nursing responsibility [12].

Moreover, the role of nurses in oral care is evolving in response to broader shifts in healthcare delivery, such as the emphasis on interprofessional care models, preventive health strategies, and patient-centered approaches [13]. Nurses are increasingly expected not only to perform routine oral care but also to identify oral health problems early, educate patients and families, collaborate with dental professionals, and advocate for resources that support hygiene maintenance [14]. These expanded roles require both theoretical knowledge and clinical competency—resources that are often not sufficiently supported in current practice environments.

Furthermore, as healthcare systems adapt to aging populations, rising chronic disease burdens, and increasing emphasis on preventive care, the role of nurses in maintaining and promoting oral health has become even more critical [15]. Emerging research highlights the need for updated protocols, competency-based training programs, and organizational support to empower nurses in this domain [16]. In addition modern nursing refers to contemporary nursing practices post-2015, characterized by interprofessional collaboration, patient-centered care, and the integration of evidence-based education and protocols [17, 18].

While several studies have explored specific aspects of oral care in nursing, such as technique effectiveness, nurses’ attitudes, and infection prevention outcomes [19, 20], there is a lack of reviews that synthesize these diverse strands of evidence to provide a comprehensive picture of the challenges and advancements in this field. Previous reviews have often been limited to narrow contexts (e.g., ICU settings only) or lacked a systematic approach, making it difficult to translate findings into broader clinical recommendations or policy reforms [21, 22].

Therefore, this systematic review aims to examine the evolving aspects of oral care within the scope of modern nursing practice. Specifically, it explores how nurses perceive and enact their roles in oral care, what barriers they encounter, what educational or systemic gaps exist, and how various interventions have attempted to improve practice. By synthesizing findings across qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-method studies, this review provides a multidimensional understanding of current practices and identifies key areas for improvement. Ultimately, the goal is to support the development of more effective, evidence-informed strategies to enhance oral care delivery and position it as a core component of professional nursing responsibilities.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines to ensure a comprehensive and transparent synthesis of evidence on the evolving role of nurses in oral care within modern nursing practice [23]. The review integrated findings from qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods studies to explore nurses’ perceptions, practices, barriers, and educational needs related to oral care, as well as interventions aimed at improving oral care delivery. Also, the study has been registered in PROSPERO (ID: CRD420251116467).

Search strategy

The databases searched included PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, CINAHL (Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature), Embase, and Cochrane Library. Additional searches were performed in Google Scholar for grey literature and non-indexed sources. The time frame for the search was set from January 2015 to May 2025 to capture contemporary and evolving practices in oral care within nursing. No language restrictions were applied during the initial search; however, only studies with an English abstract or full text were considered eligible for data extraction.

The search strategy was developed using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords related to oral care and nursing roles. The search employed the following specific terms combined with Boolean operators: (“Oral Care” OR “Mouth Care” OR “Dental Hygiene” OR “Oral Hygiene”) AND (“Nurses” OR “Nursing Staff” OR “Registered Nurse” OR “Clinical Nursing”) AND (“Practice” OR “Role” OR “Responsibility” OR “Attitude” OR “Knowledge” OR “Intervention” OR “Protocol”) AND (“Modern Nursing” OR “Current Practice” OR “Contemporary Nursing”). For example, a PubMed search string was: ((“Oral Care“[MeSH Terms] OR “Mouth Care” OR “Dental Hygiene” OR “Oral Hygiene”) AND (“Nurses“[MeSH Terms] OR “Nursing Staff” OR “Registered Nurse” OR “Clinical Nursing”) AND (“Practice” OR “Role” OR “Responsibility” OR “Attitude” OR “Knowledge” OR “Intervention” OR “Protocol”) AND (“Modern Nursing” OR “Current Practice” OR “Contemporary Nursing”)). Similar search strings were adapted for other databases, adjusting for syntax as needed. More Details for search strategy of each database is provided in supplementary file.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) published in English between January 2015 and May 2025; (2) focused on nurses’ roles, perceptions, practices, barriers, or educational needs related to oral care in healthcare settings (e.g., hospitals, ICUs, nursing homes, community health centers); (3) included registered nurses, licensed practical nurses, or nursing students as the primary population; (4) employed qualitative, quantitative, or mixed-methods designs; and (5) reported outcomes related to oral care knowledge, attitudes, practices, barriers, or interventions. Studies were excluded if they: (1) focused exclusively on dental professionals or non-nursing staff; (2) addressed oral care in non-healthcare settings; (3) were narrative reviews, editorials, or opinion pieces without primary data; or (4) were not available in full text.

Study selection

The study selection process followed a two-stage screening approach. First, titles and abstracts of retrieved records were independently screened by two reviewers using Covidence software to assess eligibility based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. In other words, disagreements during the full-text review were similarly resolved through discussion, and if consensus was not achieved, a third reviewer was involved to make a final decision. Second, full-text articles of potentially eligible studies were retrieved and independently evaluated by the same two reviewers for final inclusion. Data extraction

Data were extracted using a standardized form developed for this review. Extracted data included: (1) study characteristics (author, year, country, setting, design); (2) sample characteristics (size, population, demographics); (3) nurses’ roles in oral care (e.g., assessment, hygiene provision, documentation); (4) outcome measurements (e.g., knowledge, attitudes, practices, barriers); (5)interventions or focus of the study; and (6) key findings. Two reviewers independently extracted data, with discrepancies resolved through consensus or consultation with a third reviewer to ensure accuracy.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using appropriate tools based on study design. Quantitative studies were evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Analytical Cross-Sectional Studies or Randomized Controlled Trials, as applicable. Qualitative studies were assessed using the JBI Checklist for Qualitative Research [24]. Mixed-methods studies were evaluated using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) [25]. Two reviewers independently appraised each study, and scores were compared to ensure consistency. Studies were not excluded based on quality scores but were considered in the interpretation of findings to account for potential biases or limitations. The results of quality assessment is presented as supplementary file.

Data synthesis

A narrative synthesis approach was employed to integrate findings across studies, as described by Popay et al. (2006) [26], due to the heterogeneity in study designs (qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods), settings (e.g., ICUs, general wards, nursing homes), and outcome measures (e.g., knowledge, attitudes, practices, barriers). This method facilitated a structured yet flexible synthesis of diverse evidence without requiring meta-analysis. The synthesis process followed four key stages: (1) developing a preliminary synthesis by organizing data into descriptive summaries; (2) exploring relationships within and between studies through thematic coding using NVivo software; (3) assessing the robustness of the synthesis by cross-checking findings against study quality appraisals; and (4) synthesizing findings into a coherent framework. Quantitative data, such as knowledge scores or practice rates, were summarized descriptively using frequencies, means, or ranges, as appropriate. Qualitative data, including themes from interviews or focus groups, were analyzed inductively to identify common patterns, divergences, and contextual influences. Associations between variables (e.g., training and practice outcomes, demographic factors and attitudes) were examined where reported, using narrative descriptions to highlight consistencies or contradictions across studies. The synthesis was guided by an iterative thematic framework, ensuring findings were systematically categorized while remaining responsive to emerging patterns in the data.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The systematic literature search identified 2,347 records across PubMed, CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase, with an additional 53 records retrieved from grey literature and hand-searching reference lists. After removing duplicates (n = 612), 1,788 records underwent title and abstract screening, resulting in 204 articles for full-text review. Of these, 32 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the narrative synthesis. The process for identifying, screening, and including grey literature was as follows: Grey literature was searched primarily through Google Scholar, targeting non-indexed sources such as theses, dissertations, conference proceedings, and reports related to nurses’ roles in oral care. A simplified search string was used due to Google Scholar’s 256-character limit (e.g., “oral care nurses practice “). The first 200–400 records from Google Scholar were manually screened to ensure relevance, as recommended for systematic reviews. Additional grey literature sources included reference lists of included studies and relevant organizational websites (e.g., World Health Organization, International Council of Nurses). Figure 1 shows the study selection process.

These studies, conducted between 2015 and 2025, spanned diverse settings, including intensive care units (ICUs) (n = 12), general hospital wards (n = 8), nursing homes (n = 5), community health centers (n = 3), and other settings such as pediatric hospitals and schools (n = 5). The studies originated from 12 countries, with the majority from China (n = 9), Iran (n = 6), and Australia (n = 3). Study designs included cross-sectional surveys (n = 15), qualitative studies (n = 7), randomized controlled trials (n = 3), mixed-methods studies (n = 3), descriptive-analytical studies (n = 3), and one quasi-experimental study. Study designs included cross-sectional surveys (n = 15, 46.9%), qualitative studies (n = 7, 21.9%), randomized controlled trials (n = 3, 9.4%), mixed-methods studies (n = 3, 9.4%), descriptive-analytical studies (n = 3, 9.4%), and one quasi-experimental study (n = 1, 3.1%). Sample sizes ranged from 7 to 1,576 participants, primarily registered nurses, with some studies including licensed practical nurses, nursing students, or supervisor nurses. The narrative synthesis of 32 studies revealed five key themes that encapsulate the evolving role of nurses in oral care: (1)knowledge and educational preparedness, (2) attitudes and prioritization of oral care, (3) variability in oral care practices, (4) barriers to effective oral care delivery, and (5) enablers for enhancing oral care. These themes were derived through an iterative thematic analysis, integrating quantitative data (e.g., knowledge scores, practice rates) and qualitative findings (e.g., nurses’ perceptions, barriers) to provide a multidimensional understanding of current practices. The synthesis highlighted significant gaps in nurses’ training and resources, inconsistent prioritization of oral care across settings, and the impact of systemic barriers like workload and lack of interdisciplinary collaboration, while identifying education, policy support, and patient engagement as critical enablers for improvement. These themes provide a comprehensive picture of how nurses contribute to oral health and highlight opportunities to strengthen their role in delivering consistent, high-quality care. The following is a detailed description of data synthesis:

Knowledge and educational preparedness

The foundation of nurses’ ability to provide effective oral care lies in their knowledge, which is heavily influenced by the quality and continuity of their education. Many nurses receive basic oral health training during their initial nursing programs, but this is frequently described as limited, superficial, or not revisited in later years, particularly for those working in general wards or community settings [27, 30, 35]. For instance, community nurses have reported that their early training covered basic oral care techniques, such as brushing and mouth rinsing, but lacked depth in areas like recognizing oral diseases or managing complex cases [27]. This gap in knowledge is evident in nurses’ limited ability to identify signs of oral cancer, such as changes in oral tissue, or to understand the infectious nature of dental caries, which affects their ability to educate patients effectively [35]. Additionally, nurses often lack familiarity with standardized oral assessment tools, such as the Oral Health Assessment Tool (OHAT) or Oral Assessment Guide (OAG), which are rarely integrated into their practice [56].

Efforts to address these gaps through targeted education have shown promise. For example, pediatric nurse practitioners who participated in a structured program combining classroom learning with practical exercises gained a deeper understanding of oral health needs, enabling them to better assess and manage children’s oral care [51]. Similarly, nursing students trained through virtual reality simulations developed greater confidence in handling geriatric oral care, particularly for patients with mobility or cognitive challenges [32]. School nurses who proactively sought out additional resources, such as online courses or workshops, also demonstrated improved competence in addressing dental trauma and other oral health issues [55]. These examples underscore the need for ongoing, specialized education that goes beyond initial training, incorporating innovative methods and regular updates to keep nurses informed about best practices in oral care.

Attitudes and prioritization of oral care

Nurses generally recognize oral care as an essential component of patient care, but their willingness to prioritize it varies depending on their work environment, patient population, and professional experience. In critical care settings like ICUs, nurses often view oral care as a high-priority task, valuing its role in preventing serious complications such as ventilator-associated pneumonia, which can significantly impact patient outcomes [20, 21, 27, 33]. In these settings, nurses see oral care as a core part of their responsibility, often integrating it into daily routines for ventilated or unconscious patients. Conversely, in general wards, nurses may consider oral care less urgent, particularly for patients who are physically and cognitively capable of managing their own hygiene [45]. This perception is sometimes reinforced by a belief that oral care is a personal responsibility rather than a clinical priority, leading to inconsistent attention [52].

Some nurses express reluctance to perform oral care, describing it as unpleasant or challenging, especially when dealing with resistant patients or complex oral conditions [53]. For example, nurses in nursing homes have noted discomfort when addressing oral care for older adults with poor dentition or cognitive impairments [34]. However, experience and context play a significant role in shaping attitudes. More experienced ICU nurses tend to value oral care more highly, likely due to their exposure to its clinical importance in preventing infections [33]. In contrast, newer nurses in some settings may display more positive attitudes, possibly because their recent training emphasized oral health as part of holistic care [31]. These variations highlight the need to foster consistent, positive attitudes across all settings, ensuring nurses view oral care as a vital nursing responsibility regardless of the clinical context.

Variability in oral care practices

The delivery of oral care by nurses varies significantly, with differences in frequency, thoroughness, and adherence to best practices. In some hospitals, nurses provide adequate oral care, particularly for vulnerable patients like those in ICUs, but routine oral health assessments are often neglected, especially in general wards [20, 22, 28]. For instance, nurses may assess the oral health of unconscious patients but rely on patients or caregivers to manage care for those who appear capable [20, 35, 42]. This reliance is particularly common in settings where nurses assume patients can handle their own hygiene, leading to inconsistent care delivery [52]. The frequency of oral care also differs, with some nurses incorporating it into regular shift routines, while others provide it sporadically, depending on time availability or patient needs [53].

The use of evidence-based tools and protocols is limited, with many nurses lacking access to standardized oral care guidelines [20]. Instead, they often rely on basic supplies like gauze and saline, which are less effective than specialized tools like toothbrushes or medicated mouthwashes [49]. Certain practices, such as cleaning the tongue, gums, or moisturizing lips, are more commonly performed, particularly in settings where nurses are trained to focus on these tasks [20, 53]. However, overall adherence to structured protocols remains low, with some nurses struggling to integrate new guidelines into their practice [36]. Training and gender also influence practices, with trained nurses and female nurses more likely to engage in oral care, possibly due to greater exposure to education or cultural expectations around caregiving [28]. This variability highlights the need for standardized protocols and training to ensure consistent, high-quality oral care across all settings.

Barriers to effective oral care delivery

Nurses face multiple challenges that hinder their ability to deliver effective oral care, which can be grouped into four key areas.

Workload and time constraints

Heavy workloads and staffing shortages are significant obstacles, particularly in general wards where nurses juggle multiple responsibilities [27, 29, 35]. Nurses often prioritize tasks like medication administration or wound care over oral care, especially during understaffed shifts. For example, in busy hospital settings, nurses have reported that oral care is frequently deferred due to the time required for other urgent clinical duties [27]. This challenge is less pronounced in ICUs, where oral care is seen as critical, but even there, time constraints can limit thoroughness [29].

Resource limitations

The lack of appropriate oral care tools is a recurring issue. Nurses frequently rely on inadequate supplies, such as gauze and saline, due to the unavailability of toothbrushes, mouthwashes, or specialized kits [20, 27]. In some cases, the preparation of oral care kits is time-consuming, and certain mouthwashes, like chlorhexidine, are unpalatable to patients, reducing compliance [49]. The absence of tools like suction toothbrushes for intubated patients further complicates care, particularly in critical care settings where such equipment is essential [35, 41].

Patient-related factors

Patients themselves can present challenges, particularly those with cognitive impairments, such as dementia, or physical limitations, like lockjaw or muscle rigidity, which make oral care difficult [27, 49]. Resistant or agitated patients, common in ICUs or nursing homes, often refuse care, requiring nurses to spend additional time and effort [34, 40, 49]. Even patients with intact capacities may prioritize other health concerns over oral hygiene, further complicating nurses’ efforts [35].

Lack of interdisciplinary collaboration

Limited collaboration with dental professionals restricts nurses’ ability to provide comprehensive oral care. Nurses often face unclear referral processes and minimal feedback from dental services, leaving them unsupported in managing complex cases [54]. In some regions, the absence of dental hygienists places additional responsibilities on nurses, who may lack the expertise to handle advanced oral health needs [38, 49]. Additionally, doctors in acute care settings may focus on life-threatening conditions, relegating oral care to a lower priority, which undermines its importance [49].

Enablers for enhancing oral care

Several factors can support nurses in delivering effective oral care, grouped into four areas.

Enhanced education and training to address workload and time constraints

Ongoing education is crucial for improving nurses’ skills and confidence. Structured training programs that combine theoretical learning with practical exercises have helped nurses better understand and manage oral health needs [36, 37, 43, 51]. For example, hands-on training has proven effective in preparing nurses to care for pediatric patients, while virtual reality simulations have enhanced skills for geriatric care [32, 51]. In ICU settings, repeated practice has enabled nurses to master oral hygiene tasks, such as cleaning ventilated patients’ mouths [43]. Specific interventions with measurable outcomes include: virtual reality-based training for nursing students, which significantly improved knowledge (p < 0.001), self-efficacy (p = 0.003), attitudes (p = 0.020), and intention to assist (p = 0.030) in geriatric oral care [32]; a structured training program for ICU nurses, which increased competency scores to ≥ 95 after six practice repetitions (p < 0.0001) [43]; and an evidence-based oral care protocol with education and reminders, which improved knowledge (68.8 to 72.3, p < 0.001) and attitude scores (3.5 to 4.1, p < 0.05) but showed reduced adherence for some practices [36]. Continuous post-registration training is also essential to address gaps in initial education and keep nurses updated on best practices [30].

However, Heavy workloads and time constraints often lead nurses to deprioritize oral care, particularly in busy general wards [27, 35]. Ongoing education and training programs are critical enablers that improve efficiency and confidence, allowing nurses to integrate oral care into their routines more effectively. Structured training, combining theoretical learning with practical exercises, has been shown to enhance nurses’ skills in managing oral health needs [43, 51]. For example, virtual reality simulations improved nursing students’ competence in geriatric oral care, enabling quicker and more confident care delivery despite time pressures [32]. In ICU settings, repeated practice sessions helped nurses master oral hygiene tasks for ventilated patients, reducing the time required for effective care [43]. Continuous post-registration training, incorporating innovative methods like scenario-based learning, ensures nurses remain updated on best practices, mitigating the impact of workload pressures [33, 42].

Patient and family empowerment to mitigate patient-related challenges

Educating patients and their families about the importance of oral care can significantly increase their engagement. For instance, explaining how oral hygiene prevents serious conditions like pneumonia motivates patients to participate in their care [27]. Internet-based interventions, including WeChat education and QR code-based resources, reduced periodontal probing depth (p < 0.05), bleeding on probing (p < 0.05), and improved self-efficacy for self-care (p < 0.05) in patients with periodontitis [37]. Innovative approaches, such as using hospital TV prompts to remind patients about oral care, providing oral care packs with QR codes linking to instructional videos, or offering verbal encouragement, have been suggested to improve compliance [50, 57]. These strategies are particularly effective in settings where patients have some independence but need guidance to prioritize oral health [52].

Patient-related barriers, such as resistance or cognitive impairments, complicate oral care delivery, particularly for patients with dementia or physical limitations [27, 34, 40, 49]. For example, explaining how oral care prevents serious conditions like pneumonia motivates patients to participate actively [27]. These approaches are particularly effective in settings where patients have some independence but require guidance to prioritize oral health, directly addressing challenges posed by patient resistance or cognitive limitations [52].

Interdisciplinary collaboration to overcome lack of interdisciplinary support

Collaboration with dental professionals and other healthcare providers is vital for enhancing oral care. Involving dental hygienists in preventive tasks, such as fluoride application or scaling, can reduce the burden on nurses and improve care quality [38]. Clearer referral pathways and regular feedback from dental services can help nurses manage complex cases more effectively [46, 54]. Another included study validated a competency tool for dental hygienists in ICU settings, achieving consensus on 38 indicators across five domains, improving interdisciplinary cooperation [48]. Multidisciplinary teams that include dentists, speech-language pathologists, and nurses have been proposed to deliver comprehensive oral care, particularly in settings with high-risk patients, such as ICUs or nursing homes [44, 48].

Limited collaboration with dental professionals leaves nurses unsupported in managing complex oral health cases, compounded by unclear referral processes [38, 49, 54]. Structured interdisciplinary teamwork is a key enabler that addresses this barrier by integrating dental expertise into nursing practice. Resource and policy support to counter resource limitations

Access to adequate resources and standardized guidelines is essential for effective oral care. Providing tools like suction toothbrushes or combined oral care kits improves the safety and efficiency of care, especially for intubated or dependent patients [35, 49]. Strong clinical leadership, such as nurse unit managers advocating for oral care, helps elevate its priority in busy settings [27, 47]. Evidence-based guidelines and familiarity with tools like the Oral Health Assessment Tool can support nurses in conducting thorough assessments and delivering consistent care [39, 56]. Hospitals implementing oral care protocols with specialized tools achieved higher compliance rates and better patient oral health outcomes, such as improved Beck Oral Assessment Scale scores (p < 0.001) [30]. Hospitals that invest in these resources and policies create an environment where oral care is more feasible and effective [52].

The lack of appropriate oral care tools, such as toothbrushes or medicated mouthwashes, is a significant barrier, particularly in non-ICU settings where nurses often rely on inadequate supplies like gauze and saline [20, 27, 47]. The conceptual model

Several factors can support nurses in delivering effective oral care, grouped into four areas, which are integrated into a conceptual model based on the COM-B framework (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation–Behaviour) to illustrate how these enablers drive effective oral care practices [58]. In this model, capability is enhanced through targeted education and training, equipping nurses with the knowledge and skills to perform oral assessments and interventions confidently [32, 43, 51]. Opportunity is facilitated by providing access to resources like suction toothbrushes and evidence-based guidelines, as well as fostering interdisciplinary collaboration with dental professionals to support complex cases [35, 38, 49]. Motivation is strengthened through patient and family empowerment, positive attitudes cultivated by leadership, and institutional prioritization of oral care, which collectively encourage nurses to prioritize and sustain oral care practices [27, 47, 52]. These components interact dynamically: enhanced capability increases motivation by boosting confidence, while improved opportunity reinforces capability by providing practical tools and support. This model provides a theoretical basis for understanding how interventions targeting capability, opportunity, and motivation can drive behavior change in nurses’ oral care practices. Quality assessment summary

The quality of the 32 included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklists for quantitative and qualitative studies and the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) for mixed-methods studies. Of the 32 studies, 25% (n = 8) were rated as high quality (low risk of bias), 69% (n = 22) as moderate quality (moderate risk of bias), and 6% (n = 2) as low quality (high risk of bias) (Fig. 3). High-quality studies typically employed robust methodologies, such as validated tools, clear randomization, or rigorous qualitative analysis, but were often limited by small sample sizes or specific settings. Moderate-quality studies commonly relied on self-reported data or single-site designs, increasing potential bias. The two low-quality studies had significant limitations, including unclear sampling methods and lack of objective outcome measures. These quality ratings were considered during the narrative synthesis to weigh the robustness of findings, with detailed results provided in the supplementary file. Discussion

This systematic review synthesized evidence from 32 studies and revealed five key themes characterizing the evolving role of nurses in oral care: (1) knowledge and educational preparedness, (2) attitudes and prioritization of oral care, (3) variability in oral care practices, (4) barriers to effective oral care delivery, and (5) enablers for enhancing oral care. Collectively, these findings illustrate a dynamic yet fragmented landscape, where nurses’ oral care contributions are shaped by educational background, clinical context, resource availability, and interdisciplinary collaboration.

A comparative analysis across settings reveals distinct patterns. In ICUs, nurses demonstrate higher knowledge and prioritization of oral care due to its link to preventing ventilator-associated pneumonia, supported by targeted training and access to tools like suction toothbrushes [59]. General wards show lower prioritization, with oral care often viewed as a personal hygiene task, compounded by limited training and resources [60]. Community care settings, including nursing homes and community health centers, face unique challenges, such as patient resistance and minimal dental professional support, but benefit from patient empowerment strategies like educational videos [1]. ICU settings report fewer resource barriers compared to general wards, where gauze and saline are common, and community settings often lack standardized protocols entirely [61] Training interventions, such as virtual reality simulations, are more common in ICU and academic settings, while community nurses rely on self-directed learning [55]. These differences underscore the need for setting-specific interventions to address unique barriers and leverage enablers like interdisciplinary collaboration in ICUs and patient education in community care.

A recurring theme across the included studies was the inadequacy of oral health education within nursing curricula and continuing education programs. This is consistent with prior reviews that found oral health to be underrepresented in nursing education globally [12]. Many nurses reported receiving only superficial training during their undergraduate programs, insufficient for recognizing signs of oral disease or conducting standardized assessments. These findings align with a multicenter survey, which found that less than 30% of nurses in primary care settings could confidently identify early symptoms of oral pathologies [62].

Encouragingly, the review identified evidence that targeted training interventions can address these knowledge deficits. Studies that employed immersive strategies—such as virtual reality simulations or interprofessional workshops—demonstrated improvements in nurses’ confidence and competency, particularly in complex contexts like pediatric or geriatric care. This supports educational theories advocating for experiential and continuous learning in clinical skill development [63]. To address these gaps, nursing curricula should integrate longitudinal oral health modules that emphasize disease recognition, systemic health linkages, and standardized assessment tools like the Oral Health Assessment Tool (OHAT). Continuing professional development (CPD) programs should include regular, evidence-based workshops focusing on oral-systemic health connections and practical skills, such as managing oral care for ventilated or cognitively impaired patients. Such educational reforms are critical to equipping nurses with the capability to deliver high-quality oral care across diverse settings.

The attitudes of nurses toward oral care varied significantly depending on their clinical setting and professional experience. In intensive care units (ICUs), oral care was more likely to be perceived as a high-priority task, particularly in relation to its role in preventing ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP). This is corroborated by previous findings, which reported higher compliance with oral care protocols in critical care settings where its impact on morbidity is well documented [64]. This aligns with other researches, which reviewed oral care interventions in critical care settings and found that nurses’ prioritization of oral care is higher in ICUs due to established links with VAP prevention [15, 64]. Our review extends this by highlighting that positive attitudes in ICUs do not consistently translate to general wards, where oral care is often deprioritized, suggesting a need for broader cultural shifts across all healthcare settings. Institutional policies should foster a cultural shift by embedding oral care as a clinical priority through leadership modeling, clear guidelines, and inclusion of oral health indicators in hospital quality audits. This can help standardize attitudes and ensure oral care is valued as a critical component of patient care, regardless of setting.

Conversely, in general wards or community settings, oral care often took a backseat to other nursing responsibilities. Nurses in these environments frequently viewed oral care as a matter of personal hygiene rather than a clinical responsibility, echoing findings from studies in the UK and Australia [65]. Furthermore, negative perceptions—such as considering oral care to be unpleasant or time-consuming—were more common among nurses without recent training or exposure to vulnerable populations. These variations suggest that experience and context influence the perceived importance of oral care. Nurses with greater clinical exposure to oral health complications or recent training were more likely to prioritize and appreciate its clinical significance. Clinical protocols should be developed to ensure consistency in oral care delivery, particularly for vulnerable populations such as ICU patients, geriatric patients, and those with chronic illnesses. These protocols should include checklists, such as those based on the Oral Assessment Guide (OAG), and mandate the use of specialized tools like suction toothbrushes to enhance practice quality and reduce variability.

Institutional efforts to standardize attitudes—through both education and leadership modeling—are essential for cultivating a uniformly proactive approach to oral care [66]. In high-resource settings, such as the UK or Canada, variability is partly mitigated by access to evidence-based guidelines and specialized tools, though adherence remains inconsistent [21, 35]. In low-resource settings, such as Eritrea or Nigeria, the lack of standardized protocols is exacerbated by reliance on inadequate supplies, like gauze and saline, leading to poorer oral health outcomes [19, 20]. These disparities highlight the need for globally adaptable protocols that account for resource availability while maintaining a minimum standard of care.

One of the most striking findings of this review is the high variability in oral care practices across clinical settings. This inconsistency reflects a lack of universally accepted protocols and limited integration of oral health assessment tools, such as the Oral Health Assessment Tool (OHAT) or the Oral Assessment Guide (OAG), in routine nursing care. This is consistent with global nursing research, which highlights the absence of standardized oral care practices as a critical barrier to quality care delivery [67]. Similarly, it was noted that oral care is one of the most frequently neglected tasks due to inconsistent protocols and resource limitations, particularly in non-ICU settings [68]. Our review builds on this by identifying specific enablers, such as standardized tools and policy support, that can reduce variability and improve care quality across diverse settings, including low-resource environments not extensively covered in prior reviews. Hospitals should adopt evidence-based oral care protocols, ensuring access to specialized tools and integrating oral health assessments into routine nursing workflows. These protocols should be adaptable to resource availability, particularly in low-resource settings, to ensure a minimum standard of care globally.

Studies also showed a reliance on improvised tools (e.g., gauze and saline), with infrequent use of specialized supplies such as suction toothbrushes or antiseptic rinses. These discrepancies may stem from both systemic and contextual factors—including supply availability, institutional priorities, and time constraints. Research found that hospitals with oral care protocols in place achieved higher compliance rates and better oral health outcomes among patients, suggesting that institutional commitment plays a vital role in standardizing practices [69].

Furthermore, nurse-specific variables such as gender and prior training were found to influence oral care frequency and technique, consistent with previous research, which linked higher oral care adherence to professional development exposure [70]. Policy support should prioritize resource allocation, ensuring the availability of adequate staffing, time, and oral hygiene supplies, such as suction toothbrushes and medicated mouthwashes, to facilitate consistent care delivery. Including oral health indicators in hospital quality assurance and patient safety monitoring can further reinforce institutional commitment.

The review identified four key categories of barriers impeding effective oral care: workload and time constraints, resource limitations, patient-related challenges, and lack of interdisciplinary collaboration. These findings echo those of previous studies that have consistently reported oral care to be deprioritized during busy shifts or staff shortages [71].

Resource limitations were especially prominent, with many nurses reporting insufficient access to basic tools like toothbrushes or mouthwash, particularly in non-ICU settings. This is concerning, as studies have shown that the availability of proper equipment is directly associated with the quality of oral care delivered [19].

Patient-related barriers, such as resistance or cognitive impairment, were also notable. Nurses described difficulty performing oral care for patients with dementia, psychiatric disorders, or reduced consciousness—issues widely recognized in geriatric and critical care literature [72]. To address patient-related barriers, healthcare institutions should implement patient and family education initiatives, using tools like QR-coded instructional videos or hospital TV prompts, to increase engagement and compliance with oral hygiene practices.

Additionally, the review highlighted a troubling lack of collaboration with dental professionals. In settings with unclear referral pathways and minimal interdisciplinary communication, nurses felt unsupported in managing complex oral cases. This aligns with international research calling for integrated care models that embed dental expertise within multidisciplinary hospital teams [73]. To overcome this barrier, healthcare systems should foster interprofessional collaboration through joint training sessions with dental professionals and establish clear referral pathways to support nurses in managing complex oral health needs. Integrating oral health into multidisciplinary care teams, particularly in critical care and long-term care settings, can enhance care quality and reduce the burden on nurses.

Despite these barriers, the review also identified key enablers that can support high-quality oral care. The most consistent enabler was enhanced education—both initial and ongoing. Studies included in the review illustrated that well-structured, context-specific training improved nurses’ self-efficacy and practice. This reinforces the call from WHO and other global bodies to integrate oral health into broader health promotion efforts [74].

Patient and family empowerment was another promising strategy. Educating patients and caregivers about the health implications of oral hygiene was shown to increase engagement and compliance, particularly when using creative educational tools like QR-coded videos or visual reminders. This aligns with the health belief model, which posits that behavior change is more likely when individuals perceive personal benefit [75].

The predominance of studies from China (n = 9) and Iran (n = 6) raises considerations for the generalizability of findings to Western and low-resource settings. In China and Iran, healthcare systems often operate under centralized models with varying resource availability, which may amplify barriers like limited access to specialized tools or interdisciplinary collaboration compared to Western settings with more robust dental infrastructure [76, 77]. For instance, Western hospitals often have better access to evidence-based guidelines and tools like the Oral Health Assessment Tool (OHAT), leading to higher compliance rates, though adherence remains inconsistent [78]. Conversely, in low-resource settings, the reliance on improvised supplies (e.g., gauze and saline) and lack of standardized protocols mirror challenges in some Chinese and Iranian studies, suggesting partial applicability [49]. However, cultural differences in nursing roles and patient expectations in Western settings may limit direct translation of findings, necessitating tailored interventions. For Western contexts, integrating oral care into existing quality assurance frameworks and leveraging dental hygienist support can enhance implementation [79]. In low-resource settings, scalable solutions like low-cost oral care kits and community-based training programs could address resource constraints while aligning with local needs [80]. These adaptations ensure that the review’s findings remain relevant across diverse global healthcare systems, supporting the development of context-specific strategies to improve oral care delivery.

To guide the integration of oral care into nursing practice, we propose a conceptual framework based on the COM-B model (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation–Behaviour) [58]. This multi-level framework addresses education, clinical practice, institutional support, and interprofessional collaboration to drive behavior change in nurses’ oral care practices. This framework integrates the review’s findings into a cohesive strategy, where enhanced capability (through education) increases motivation by boosting confidence, while improved opportunity (via resources and collaboration) reinforces capability by providing practical support. By addressing these components, healthcare systems can drive sustained improvements in nurses’ oral care practices, ultimately enhancing patient outcomes across diverse settings.

Interdisciplinary collaboration also emerged as a critical facilitator. Inclusion of dental professionals in hospital settings—whether through direct services or consultative roles—has been shown to reduce oral health-related complications [81]. Finally, the review underscores the importance of clinical leadership and policy support. Nurses are more likely to prioritize oral care when it is reinforced by unit managers and when institutional policies provide the necessary time, tools, and training. To address global disparities, policies should include oral care indicators in hospital quality audits to ensure accountability and prioritize resource allocation, particularly in low-resource settings where oral care is often neglected. This could involve mandating regular audits of oral care practices as part of hospital accreditation processes, ensuring that oral health is recognized as a critical component of patient care worldwide.

Limitations

This systematic review provides a comprehensive synthesis of the evolving role of nurses in oral care; however, certain aspects offer opportunities for further exploration. The studies were heterogeneous in design, setting, and outcome measures, precluding meta-analysis and limiting quantitative synthesis. The diversity in study designs, ranging from qualitative to quantitative and mixed-methods approaches, enriched the narrative synthesis but limited the feasibility of meta-analysis, which could have provided quantitative precision for specific outcomes. The geographic concentration of included studies, with a majority originating from China and Iran, may limit the generalizability of findings to other healthcare systems with different cultural, educational, or resource contexts, suggesting an opportunity to include more diverse global perspectives in future research. Additionally, the reliance on self-reported data in some studies presents an opportunity to complement these findings with objective measures, such as observational assessments, in future research. These considerations highlight the potential for expanding the scope and depth of evidence to further strengthen the understanding of nurses’ roles in oral care.

Conclusion

Nurses are pivotal in delivering oral care, but their effectiveness hinges on addressing educational gaps, inconsistent practices, and systemic barriers. For nursing educators, integrating comprehensive oral health modules into undergraduate and postgraduate curricula is essential. These should include virtual reality-based training for hands-on skill development in managing complex cases, such as geriatric or ventilated patients, and interprofessional workshops with dental professionals to teach disease recognition and systemic health linkages. Continuous professional development (CPD) programs, incorporating scenario-based learning and annual refreshers, can ensure nurses remain adept at using standardized tools like the Oral Health Assessment Tool (OHAT), fostering confidence and competence across settings.

For hospital administrators, implementing evidence-based oral care protocols is critical to reduce practice variability, particularly for vulnerable populations like ICU and geriatric patients. These protocols should mandate checklists, such as those based on the Oral Assessment Guide (OAG), and ensure access to specialized tools like suction toothbrushes and medicated mouthwashes. Patient and family education, using tools like QR-coded instructional videos or hospital TV prompts, can enhance engagement and compliance, addressing patient-related barriers such as resistance or cognitive impairment. Administrators should also prioritize staffing and time allocation to prevent oral care from being deprioritized during busy shifts.

Policymakers must advocate for systemic changes to elevate oral care as a core nursing responsibility. Nursing accreditation bodies, such as the American Nurses Credentialing Center or the International Council of Nurses, should incorporate oral health competencies into certification standards to ensure nurses are equipped with the necessary skills across diverse healthcare settings. This includes mandating oral health indicators in hospital quality audits to ensure accountability and allocating resources for oral hygiene supplies, particularly in low-resource settings. Fostering interdisciplinary collaboration through clear referral pathways with dental professionals and integrating oral health into multidisciplinary care teams can reduce nurses’ burden and improve care quality. Future research should explore longitudinal interventions and focus on understudied settings, such as community care, to identify scalable solutions for improving oral care delivery. Specifically, studies should evaluate the cost-effectiveness of virtual reality-based oral care training in nursing curricula to determine its feasibility for widespread adoption. Additionally, qualitative studies examining barriers to oral care in home-health or community settings could provide insights into unique challenges outside hospital environments. Future work should explore the cost-effectiveness and global scalability of training interventions to ensure sustainable improvements in oral care delivery across diverse healthcare systems. Finally, assessing the long-term sustainability and scalability of oral care interventions across diverse healthcare systems will be critical to ensuring lasting improvements in practice and patient outcomes.
