Lymphedema After Salvage Surgery for Thymic Carcinoma
Authors: Yuuki Kou, Nobuhisa Yamazaki, Yasuto Sakaguchi, Hirokazu Tanaka, Makoto Sonobe
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
A 58-year-old woman underwent an extended thymectomy and left upper lobectomy subsequent to induction chemoradiotherapy for advanced thymic carcinoma. The pathologic stage of the tumor was ypT0 N0 M0 stage 0. Six months after surgery, she presented with edema in her left arm and left breast. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the chest revealed no signs of cancer recurrence or arterial or vascular occlusion. Consequently, she was diagnosed with lymphedema.

Lymphedema is a chronic and progressive consequence of cancer treatment. Most cases are manifested as secondary lymphedema, with diverse underlying causes. Nevertheless, no instances of secondary lymphedema resulting from chemoradiotherapy and surgical intervention for thymic carcinoma or lung cancer have been documented. In this report, we present a distinctive case of secondary lymphedema after salvage surgery for advanced thymic carcinoma.

A 59-year-old woman was incidentally found to have a substantial 9.0-cm-diameter tumor in the left upper lobe of the lung during a routine medical checkup. She was subsequently referred to our hospital for further assessment and management. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the chest revealed a sizable tumor located in the left mediastinum, invading the left upper lung lobe, accompanied by mediastinal lymph node enlargement (Figure 1). Transbronchial lung biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of c-kit–positive thymic carcinoma. Her clinical staging was classified as cT3 N1 M0 stage IVa. After initial chemoradiotherapy, which consisted of 5 cycles of concurrent chemotherapy with carboplatin and paclitaxel along with a total radiation dose of 60 Gy delivered in 2-Gy fractions, the tumor regressed, rendering it amenable to surgical resection. Subsequently, she underwent an extended thymectomy and left upper lobectomy employing a hemi-clamshell approach. The postoperative course was uneventful, and she was discharged from our facility on postoperative day 11. However, 6 months after surgery, she presented with swelling in her left arm and left breast (Figure 2). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography did not reveal any arterial or vascular occlusion or cancer recurrence. Based on clinical examination and imaging findings, a diagnosis of lymphedema was established. Despite manual lymphatic drainage, her symptoms showed only partial improvement. Comment

There are various causes of secondary lymphedema. Breast cancer, head and neck cancer, and melanoma are the most prevalent causes of cancer-associated lymphedema in the upper extremities. For instance, the reported incidence of secondary lymphedema subsequent to breast cancer treatment ranges from 6% to 63%.1 Moreover, the risk of lymphedema has been reported to increase by up to 45% after adjuvant radiation therapy to the axillary or supraclavicular lymph nodes.2 McDuff and colleagues3 found that patients undergoing axillary lymph node dissection without regional lymph node radiation experienced the most pronounced lymphedema risk in the initial 6 to 12 months. In contrast, axillary lymph node dissection with regional lymph node radiation peaked in hazard rate between 18 and 24 months and showed a significant decline after 30 months. Similarly, patients who had sentinel lymph node biopsy with regional lymph node radiation saw the hazard rate peak between 36 and 48 months and decline thereafter. Hence, surgery-related lymphedema tends to be manifested earlier compared with radiation-related lymphedema.

No instances of lymphedema after systemic treatment of thymic or lung cancer have been reported to date. In our case, the swelling was observed from the left breast to the left upper limb, thus indicating that the lymphatic drainage in these areas is directed toward the left axilla. Manual lymphatic drainage is indeed performed in the direction of the left axilla and has proved to be effective. Although radiotherapy has been administered, it has not been targeted directly at the axillary or supraclavicular lymph nodes (Figure 3); hence, we believe that the impact would be comparable to that of radiotherapy to the chest wall. Furthermore, radiotherapy to the internal mammary lymph nodes is unlikely to contribute to arm lymphedema.4 Warren and coworkers,5 in their review of the effect of radiotherapy on the risk of lymphedema after breast cancer treatment, reported that axillary lymph node dissection and supraclavicular irradiation increased the risk of lymphedema, whereas whole breast radiotherapy or chest wall radiotherapy alone did not significantly elevate the risk of lymphedema.Figure 3The patient’s radiation therapy was planned as depicted.

Although salvage surgery after induction chemoradiotherapy for superior sulcus tumors is considered 1 of the optimal treatments of Pancoast tumors,6 similar to our case, no reports of secondary lymphedema have been documented. Unfortunately, the precise cause of lymphedema in this case cannot be ascertained with certainty, but we think that a slight influence of the radiotherapy might lead to lymphedema. Although exceedingly rare, the possibility of secondary lymphedema should be considered in patients undergoing similar treatment.
