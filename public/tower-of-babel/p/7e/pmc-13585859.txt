Metabolic Syndrome and Its Components Are Associated with Closed-Type Endoscopic Atrophic Gastritis in a Large Screening Cohort
Authors: Sun Young Yang, Huiyeon Kim, Jung Kim, Ji Hyun Song, Jooyoung Lee, Soie Chung, Eun Young Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeIn recent studies, metabolic syndrome (MetS) has been identified as a risk factor for gastric cancer. To enhance risk stratification protocols and prevention for gastric cancer, we sought to determine whether MetS was associated with atrophic gastritis (AG), the premalignant lesion for gastric cancer.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study enrolled 12,353 participants who underwent endoscopy during health check-ups between January 2015 and December 2023. Gastric mucosal atrophy was graded according to the Kimura–Takemoto classification. To minimize confounding, propensity score matching was performed using age, sex, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and H. pylori IgG serostatus. Additional analyses were restricted to participants without a history of H. pylori eradication.ResultsAmong participants without prior H. pylori eradication, MetS was significantly associated with closed-type gastric atrophy (C2–C3) (OR 1.153, 95% CI 1.006–1.321, P = 0.041) after propensity score matching. Among the individual MetS components, abdominal obesity, hypertriglyceridemia, low HDL cholesterol, and elevated blood pressure were significantly associated with closed-type atrophy, whereas elevated fasting glucose was not. Similar findings were observed across alternative definitions of gastric atrophy (C2–O3), whereas no significant association was observed for open-type atrophy (O1–O3).ConclusionMetS and several of its components were associated with closed-type gastric atrophy (C2–C3), particularly among individuals without prior H. pylori eradication. These findings suggest that metabolic dysfunction may be associated with moderate atrophic gastric changes and support a potential role of metabolic health in gastric mucosal integrity beyond established H. pylori-related risk factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10620-026-10114-5.Keywords: Atrophic gastritis, Kimura-Takemoto classification, Metabolic syndrome, Helicobacter pylori

Introduction

Gastric atrophy constitutes a pivotal precursor lesion in the multistep cascade leading from chronic gastritis to intestinal metaplasia and, ultimately, gastric carcinoma [1]. Helicobacter pylori (HP) infection remains the predominant etiologic factor driving mucosal inflammation and glandular loss, with prospective data demonstrating that HP-positive individuals have substantially higher odds of developing atrophic changes and subsequent gastric cancer compared to uninfected controls [2]. In Korea, the prevalence of HP infection remains among the highest worldwide. The 2021 Korean evidence-based clinical practice guidelines estimated the national prevalence at 54% (95% CI 50.1–57.8%), underscoring the continuing high infection burden in the Korean population [3].

Concurrently, metabolic syndrome (MetS), defined by the clustering of central obesity, dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and hypertension [4], has emerged as a contributor to systemic inflammatory and neoplastic processes [5]. MetS as a chronic inflammatory disease might create a pro-cancer environment and increase cancer incidence. Several epidemiological studies suggest that MetS and its components may independently or in combination increase the risk of several types of cancer, such as pancreatic cancer [6], colorectal cancer [7], and postmenopausal breast cancer [8].

In a large-scale prospective cohort of over 100,000 Korean adults, those meeting MetS criteria exhibited a 26% higher hazard of developing gastric cancer compared to metabolically healthy individuals [9]. Specific MetS components, particularly hypertriglyceridemia, low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and hyperglycemia, were independently linked to elevated gastric cancer risk, suggesting that metabolic derangements may create a pro‐oncogenic milieu in the gastric mucosa [9]. However, a limitation of this study is that it did not adjust for HP infection, the predominant risk factor for gastric cancer.

Although MetS has been linked to a higher risk of gastric cancer, it is still unclear how these metabolic changes translate into early mucosal alterations. AG is an important intermediate step in this pathway [1], yet the relationship between MetS and the development of AG itself has not been well examined. Since AG represents a critical intermediate stage in gastric carcinogenesis, identifying metabolic determinants of AG may refine early risk stratification. Because AG usually results from long-standing inflammation and progressive mucosal injury, it is reasonable to hypothesize that the chronic metabolic and inflammatory burden associated with MetS may be linked to atrophic changes in the stomach [5]. Still, evidence directly evaluating this connection remains limited, especially in populations such as Korea where HP infection is highly prevalent [3].

Therefore, this study aimed to examine whether MetS was associated with the prevalence of AG in Korea, where HP infection remains highly common. By comparing endoscopic findings in individuals with and without MetS and adjusting for HP infection and other relevant factors, we sought to clarify whether metabolic abnormalities were associated with gastric atrophy.

Methods

Study Population

The inclusion criteria for this retrospective single-center study were participants who underwent comprehensive health examinations, including esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2023. The EGD results were retrospectively reviewed. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No. H-2401–095-1503), with a waiver of informed consent granted for this retrospective study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. A total of 23,822 screening examinations were initially identified. For participants who underwent repeated examinations, only the most recent examination was retained, resulting in 21,309 unique individuals. Participants with missing data on blood pressure, waist circumference, fasting glucose, HP IgG, HDL cholesterol, alcohol consumption, or smoking variables were excluded. Individuals with a history of gastric cancer or suspected gastric cancer on biopsy were also excluded. After applying these exclusion criteria, 12,353 participants were included in the final analysis (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Demographic Characteristics, Anthropometric Data, and Laboratory Findings

Demographic characteristics, anthropometric measurements, health examinations, and medical questionnaire data were obtained using standardized protocols as described previously [10]. Participants were asked to report their weekly alcohol consumption in terms of the number of drinks and categorized as abstainer (0 drink/week), light (1–6 drinks/week), moderate (7–13 drinks/week), and heavy (≥ 14 drinks / week). This classification was adapted from previous epidemiological studies on cardiovascular and metabolic outcomes [11, 12]. Smoking status was classified according to participants’ responses to a self-reported questionnaire, following the definitions used in previous epidemiologic studies [13]. Participants were categorized as follows: never smokers, individuals who had never smoked or had smoked fewer than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime; current smokers, individuals who had smoked at least 100 cigarettes and were smoking at the time of the survey; and former smokers, individuals who had smoked at least 100 cigarettes but had quit smoking at the time of the survey. In addition, heavy cumulative smoking was defined as a lifetime exposure of ≥ 30 pack-years, consistent with prior epidemiologic literatures [14]. Physical inactivity was defined as engaging in less than 150 min per week of moderate-intensity physical activity or less than 75 min per week of vigorous-intensity activity, or an equivalent combination of moderate- and vigorous-intensity activity, according to the World Health Organization guidelines [15].

Blood samples were collected after a 12-h fast. Serum samples were collected in tubes containing a clot activator and serum gel separator. Centrifugation was performed at 3000 rpm for 10 min within 30 min of blood withdrawal to prevent glycolysis. All analyses were performed immediately after centrifugation. Fasting glucose, triglyceride (TG), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) were measured using the Architect C16000 (Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, IL, USA). HP IgG levels were analyzed using the Immulite 2000 (Siemens AG, Erlangen, Germany). The laboratory reference ranges used at our institution were as follows: fasting glucose, 70–110 mg/dL; TG, 0–200 mg/dL; HDL-C, 35–55 mg/dL. H. pylori IgG was measured in IU/mL and interpreted as negative (< 1.0 IU/mL) or positive (≥ 1.0 IU/mL).

Definition and Endoscopic Grading for Gastric Mucosal Atrophy

The grade of gastric mucosal atrophy was evaluated based on the location of the endoscopic atrophic border (EAB), which was identified by characteristic differences in the capillary pattern, mucosal color, and surface elevation of the gastric mucosa in a non-overdistended stomach, as described by Kimura and Takemoto [16]. The extent of atrophic gastritis (AG) was classified into six grades (C1 to O3) according to the Kimura–Takemoto (K–T) classification. In C1, the EAB is limited to the antrum; in C2, the EAB is at the angle or lower corpus; in C3, the EAB is at the upper corpus. In O1, the EAB is parallel to the vertical axis of the stomach and is on the lesser curvature; in O2, the EAB involving the anterior and posterior walls; in O3, the EAB reaching the greater curvature. To evaluate the association between metabolic syndrome and the presence of clinically meaningful gastric atrophy, AG was primarily defined as Kimura–Takemoto grades C2–O3. Participants without atrophy or with C1 atrophy were classified as the reference group. Additional analyses were performed using alternative definitions of atrophy, including closed-type atrophy (C2–C3) and open-type atrophy (O1–O3). For sensitivity analyses, atrophy severity was additionally categorized according to the K–T classification. Closed-type atrophy was defined as grades C2–C3 and open-type atrophy as grades O1–O3. In analyses of closed-type atrophy, participants without atrophy or with C1 atrophy served as the reference group. In analyses of open-type atrophy, participants without open-type atrophy (no atrophy or C1–C3 atrophy) served as the reference group.

All esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) procedures were performed by senior endoscopists with at least five years of clinical experience at Seoul National University Hospital Gangnam Center. All endoscopists underwent standardized training to ensure objective and consistent evaluation of endoscopic AG. To minimize inter-observer variability, substantial efforts were made, resulting in inter-observer agreement with kappa values of 0.76–0.78 for AG diagnosis [17]. All EGDs were performed using conventional white-light video endoscopes (GIF-H260/H290 series; Olympus Optical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) as part of a comprehensive health-screening program, with AG grading based on the K–T classification.

Definition of Metabolic Syndrome

We defined MetS and components according to the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP-ATP III) and modified the waist circumference based on Korean population criteria (4, 18, 19). Individuals with at least 3 of the 5 following components were defined as MetS: (1) abdominal obesity (men ≥ 90 cm; women ≥ 85 cm); (2) hypertriglyceridemia (≥ 150 mg/dL); (3) low HDL-C (men < 40 mg/dL; women < 50 mg/dL); (4) elevated blood pressure (≥ 130/85 mm Hg); and (5) hyperglycemia (fasting plasma glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL). This MetS definition criterion was commonly used in health examination centers in Korea (9).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS Statistics 27.0 for Windows (IBM SPSS Statistics, Armonk, NY, USA) and Python version 3.11.13. All statistical outcomes were based on two-sided tests, and P-values < 0.05 were considered significant. For continuous variables, data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and were compared between groups using a t-test. Categorical variables were reported as numbers and percentages, and chi-squared test was used to compare categorical variables. Propensity score matching was performed separately for MetS and each of its individual components. Participants with missing data on history of HP eradication were excluded from propensity score-matched analyses. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression models including age, sex, smoking status, alcohol consumption, HP IgG serostatus, and prior HP eradication history. Nearest-neighbor 1:2 matching with replacement was applied using a caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score. Generalized estimating equation (GEE) models were subsequently applied to account for the clustered structure of the matched data, and covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences. To examine the potential impact of unmeasured confounding, E-values for the point estimate and the lower bound of the 95% confidence interval were calculated to estimate the minimum strength of association required to explain away the observed findings. Additional sensitivity analyses were performed using alternative definitions of gastric atrophy (C2–O3, C2–C3, and O1–O3). Furthermore, analyses were repeated after restricting the study population to participants without a history of HP eradication to evaluate the potential influence of eradication history on the observed associations.

Results

Table 1 shows the characteristics of the participants with and without MetS. A total of 12,353 participants were included, classified into the MetS group (n = 2,736) and the non-MetS group (n = 9,617). The MetS group was older (54.9 ± 9.7 vs. 53.6 ± 9.2 years, P < 0.001) and had a higher proportion of men (77.4% vs. 55.5%, P < 0.001). Regarding lifestyle factors, heavy drinker (drinking ≥ 14 drinks per week) was more common in the MetS group (33.4 vs. 21.1%). The prevalence of current smoking (25.8% vs. 15.0%) and heavy cumulative smoking (≥ 30 pack-years: 14.6% vs. 6.5%) was also higher (all P < 0.001). Physical inactivity was more frequent in the MetS group (43.4% vs. 36.4%, P < 0.001). Clinical profiles were consistently less favorable in the MetS group: body mass index (26.5 ± 3.1 vs. 22.9 ± 2.9 kg/m2), waist circumference (93.8 ± 7.7 vs. 83.4 ± 8.1 cm), systolic/diastolic blood pressure (128 ± 13/84 ± 9 vs. 116 ± 13/76 ± 9 mmHg), triglycerides (186 ± 122 vs. 94 ± 49 mg/dL), and fasting glucose (114 ± 25 vs. 98 ± 17 mg/dL) were all higher, whereas HDL-C was lower (46 ± 11 vs. 57 ± 13 mg/dL) (all P < 0.001). HP IgG seropositivity did not differ between groups (57.1% vs. 58.7%, P = 0.141). In contrast, AG was more prevalent in the MetS group (57.2% vs. 52.6%, P < 0.001). The distribution of K–T grades differed significantly (P < 0.001), with a lower proportion of non-atrophic findings in the MetS group (42.8 vs. 47.4%).

The observed odds ratios corresponded to E-values ranging from 1.57 to 1.84. The standardized mean differences (SMDs) for major covariates before and after propensity score matching are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2. Prior to matching, notable imbalance was observed in variables such as age, sex, HP IgG, smoking, and alcohol intake. After matching, SMDs for most covariates were below 0.1, indicating good balance between groups. Age showed a small residual imbalance in the MetS analyses of both populations; however, the magnitude of imbalance was modest and unlikely to materially affect the results.

Additional sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of the findings according to different definitions of AG and HP eradication history. In the total propensity score-matched population, the association between MetS and atrophy remained significant when atrophy was defined as C2–O3 and C2–C3, whereas no significant association was observed for open-type atrophy (O1–O3). Similar findings were observed among participants without a history of HP eradication. Detailed results are presented in Supplementary Tables S1–S5 and Supplementary Figures S3–S7. As an additional analysis to further minimize the potential influence of H. pylori-related factors, we performed a multivariable logistic regression analysis restricted to participants without a history of HP eradication who were also seronegative for HP IgG. In this subgroup, MetS remained independently associated with closed-type atrophy (C2–C3) after adjustment for age and sex (OR 1.231, 95% CI 1.021–1.484, P = 0.029; Supplementary Table S6), providing further evidence that the observed association was unlikely to be entirely explained by H. pylori-related factors.

Discussion

In this large endoscopy-based health-screening cohort, we observed that MetS was modestly but consistently associated with closed-type atrophy (C2–C3), even after adjustment for major established risk factors for gastric cancer. Importantly, the association remained significant when atrophy was defined using a stricter threshold (C2–O3) and among participants without a history of HP eradication, supporting the robustness of the findings. Additional analyses according to the severity and extent of atrophy provided further insight into the relationship between metabolic dysfunction and gastric mucosal changes. MetS remained significantly associated with closed-type atrophy (C2–C3), whereas no significant association was observed for open-type atrophy (O1–O3). These findings may suggest a stronger association between metabolic factors and closed-type atrophy; however, the relatively small number of participants with open-type atrophy warrants cautious interpretation.

Intestinal-type gastric adenocarcinoma is known to develop through a multistep cascade from chronic gastritis to AG and intestinal metaplasia [1], in which HP infection has long been considered the most important risk factor [18]. However, accumulating evidence suggests that systemic inflammation and metabolic abnormalities may also influence gastric mucosal alterations, thereby increasing interest in the potential role of the metabolic milieu beyond infectious factors [19, 20]. Although previous studies have reported associations between metabolic disorders and advanced gastric mucosal changes [20], large-scale endoscopy-based health-screening studies evaluating the relationship between metabolic syndrome and AG remain limited.

Given the strong contribution of HP infection to the development of AG, it has been challenging to assess the independent effect of MetS. To address this issue, we applied propensity score matching to balance major confounders, including age, sex, HP IgG serostatus, smoking status, and alcohol consumption. Furthermore, to minimize the potential misclassification of HP status resulting from previous eradication therapy, we conducted additional subgroup analyses restricted to participants without prior history of HP eradication. This approach is well recognized for improving covariate balance between groups in observational studies [21, 22]. The observed odds ratios corresponded to E-values ranging from 1.57 to 1.84, suggesting that an unmeasured confounder would need to have a moderately strong association with both MetS and closed-type AG (C2-C3) to fully explain away the observed associations. The persistence of the association between MetS and closed-type AG after matching suggests that metabolic factors may contribute, at least in part, to gastric mucosal vulnerability. Although the effect sizes were modest, they were consistent across multiple metabolic components. Moreover, the association remained significant in participants without prior HP eradication who were also seronegative for HP IgG, further supporting that the observed relationship cannot be fully explained by measured HP-related factors (Supplementary Table S6). Nevertheless, due to the cross-sectional nature of this study, causal relationships cannot be directly established, and the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Among the individual components of MetS, central obesity, hypertriglyceridemia, low HDL-C, and high BP were consistently associated with closed-type atrophy, whereas impaired fasting glucose was not. This pattern suggests that the observed association may be driven primarily by obesity-, dyslipidemia-, and blood pressure-related metabolic abnormalities rather than impaired glucose metabolism alone. Previous studies have suggested that chronic low-grade inflammation, adipokine dysregulation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction may contribute to gastric mucosal injury in individuals with metabolic syndrome [19, 20, 23–31]. Our findings are generally consistent with these proposed mechanisms.

A Swedish study reported that the association between BMI and atrophic corpus gastritis was most pronounced in younger adults aged 35–44 years, among whom overweight and obesity were associated with a 2.8-fold and 4.7-fold higher odds of AG, respectively, even after adjustment for HP infection. [23]. Moreover, visceral fat accumulation has been shown to be more closely related to upper gastrointestinal diseases than body mass index alone, with metabolically active visceral adiposity potentially contributing to systemic inflammatory responses [24]. Histological evidence of HP infection and mononuclear cell infiltration, commonly seen in AG, has been associated with higher serum TG and very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) levels, with lower HDL-C [25]. In line with these findings, the associations observed in our study between central obesity, hypertriglyceridemia, low HDL-C, and AG suggest that visceral adiposity-related metabolic stress or lipid metabolic alterations may be linked to increased gastric mucosal susceptibility.

With respect to elevated blood pressure, direct epidemiological evidence linking hypertension to endoscopic atrophic gastritis remains scarce. However, previous studies have demonstrated associations between HP infection and hypertension [26–29], suggesting that chronic low-grade systemic inflammation may represent a shared biological pathway. In addition, hypertension is characterized by endothelial dysfunction and microvascular alterations, which could potentially impair gastric mucosal perfusion and increase susceptibility to atrophic changes. Nevertheless, given the cross-sectional nature of this study, the observed association may reflect shared inflammatory or metabolic backgrounds rather than a direct causal effect. Further longitudinal investigations are warranted to clarify this relationship.

In contrast, elevated fasting glucose did not show a significant association after multivariable adjustment. The attenuation of the association after adjustment suggests that the crude association between hyperglycemia and AG may largely reflect confounding by age, sex, or lifestyle factors rather than a direct metabolic effect. Conversely, triglyceride levels did not show a significant association in the univariate analysis but became statistically significant after multivariable adjustment. This pattern suggests a suppression effect, whereby strong correlations between TG and male sex or smoking [30, 31]—both positively associated with AG—may have masked the underlying association in crude analysis.

MetS is closely associated with a state of chronic low-grade inflammation, and pro-inflammatory mediators derived from adipose tissue may contribute to a systemic inflammatory environment [19]. Such a metabolic inflammatory state may interact with HP-induced mucosal injury and influence the progression of atrophic changes. Previous reports have suggested that diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and fatty liver disease are associated with more advanced endoscopic atrophy and gastric neoplasia [20]. However, given the cross-sectional design of the present study, these mechanistic pathways cannot be directly confirmed. Longitudinal studies are warranted to clarify the temporal and causal relationships.

The weaker association observed in open-type AG may be explained by differences in the pathogenic mechanisms involved in the initiation and progression of gastric atrophy. While MetS may contribute to the development of early atrophic changes through systemic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation, progression to severe atrophy might be influenced more strongly by established risk factors such as persistent HP infection, aging, smoking, and dietary salt intake. Therefore, it is possible that the relative contribution of MetS becomes less apparent in advanced stages of gastric atrophy, where the cumulative effects of these factors may predominate.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes determination of temporal or causal relationships between metabolic syndrome and atrophic gastritis. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted cautiously, and longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal sequence and potential causal pathways. Second, atrophic gastritis in this study was assessed endoscopically using the K–T classification rather than histologic biopsy. Although endoscopic diagnosis of atrophic gastritis may be considered a limitation in research settings, it is widely used in clinical practice because of its practicality. Importantly, previous studies have demonstrated substantial agreement between the Kimura–Takemoto classification and histologic atrophy [32, 33]. In addition, inter-observer agreement among experienced endoscopists in our center was high (kappa 0.76–0.78)[17], supporting the reliability of the endoscopic assessment. Third, HP infection status was determined using serology, which cannot reliably distinguish active infection from past exposure. Because this was a retrospective analysis, confirmatory tests such as the urea breath test or histologic examination were not available. Therefore, HP seropositivity in our study should be interpreted as reflecting either current or previous exposure. Finally, although propensity score matching was used to balance major confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as dietary habits, socioeconomic status, medication use, and physical activity cannot be excluded. Future studies incorporating comprehensive confounders would help clarify the potential role of diet in the relationship between metabolic syndrome and gastric mucosal alterations.

Despite these limitations, this study has several notable strengths. We evaluated the association between metabolic syndrome and endoscopic gastric atrophy in a large health-screening population and applied propensity score matching to minimize the influence of major confounders. We also performed additional analyses according to the severity of atrophy and among participants without a history of HP eradication, which demonstrated consistent findings. Although the observed odds ratios were modest, the potential public health impact may still be substantial in populations with a high prevalence of gastric atrophy.

Notably, the association was most evident for closed-type atrophy (C2–C3), whereas no significant association was observed for open-type atrophy. These findings suggest that metabolic abnormalities may be more closely related to moderate atrophic changes than to advanced atrophy. In addition to HP-related factors that have traditionally been emphasized in gastric cancer risk assessment, metabolic health status may represent another important factor influencing gastric mucosal integrity.

Because HP infection remains the strongest established risk factor for gastric cancer, preventive strategies have primarily focused on eradication therapy and dietary modification. However, our findings raise the possibility that metabolic dysfunction may also contribute to gastric mucosal alterations. If future longitudinal studies clarify the temporal relationship between metabolic abnormalities and gastric atrophic progression, integrated risk assessment strategies incorporating both HP-related and metabolic risk factors may improve the prevention and early detection of gastric cancer.
