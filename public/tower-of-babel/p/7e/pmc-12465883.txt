Tip-flexible suctioning ureteral access sheath combined with flexible ureteroscope: a single-institution experience including in vitro experiments
Authors: Tao Wang, Jielong Zhou, Xinyu Zhai, Xiaocheng Sun, Yan Wang, Cheng Ma, Dexin Song, Dongliang Xu, Chuanmin Chu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo evaluate the clinical application value of tip-flexible suctioning ureteral access sheath (TFS-UAS), this study compares the efficacy and safety of TFS-UAS versus traditional ureteral access sheath (T-UAS) in treating unilateral renal or proximal ureteral calculi, and further summarizes the usage strategy of TFS-UAS by synthesizing vitro experiment and surgical best practices.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 196 patients with renal or proximal ureteral calculi treated with FURS and holmium laser lithotripsy at Shuguang Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. Patients were divided into TFS-UAS and T-UAS groups. Primary data included operative time, immediate SFR (iSFR), and 2-week SFR. Secondary data encompassed changes in blood parameters (leukocytes, hemoglobin, urea nitrogen, creatinine), complications, and hospital stay.ResultsThis retrospective study found that urologists preferred to use TFS-UAS for larger (17.73 ± 7.79 mm vs. 12.56 ± 5.14 mm, P < 0.001), harder (1156.52 ± 420.15 vs. 778.39 ± 513.46 Hounsfield units, P < 0.001), and more complex (modified STONE score: 10.86 ± 2.51 vs. 7.37 ± 2.28, P < 0.001) upper urinary tract calculi. No significant differences in operative time or SFR were found between the TFS-UAS and T-UAS groups overall. However, for stones ≥ 15 mm, those that were larger(22.57 ± 6.35 mm vs. 18.70 ± 3.82 mm, P = 0.001), harder(1160.76 ± 382.84 vs. 896.56 ± 465.33, P = 0.007), and more complex(modified STONE score: 11.43 ± 2.40 vs. 8.63 ± 2.31, P < 0.001), TFS-UAS significantly reduced operative time (53.92 ± 21.77 vs. 65.76 ± 22.43 min, P < 0.001) and improved iSFR(91.89% vs. 73.17%, P = 0.063), 2-week SFR(81.08% vs. 56.10%, P = 0.018).ConclusionsTFS-UAS combined with FURS significantly improves operative efficiency and SFRs for upper urinary tract calculi ≥ 15 mm, demonstrating clinical advantages.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12894-025-01922-4.

Introduction

As a common urological disorder, urolithiasis affects approximately 8.1% of the population in China [1–3]. Ureteral obstruction caused by urolithiasis may lead to flank pain, urinary tract infection(UTI), and renal impairment, thereby significantly impacting patient health [3, 4]. Current urological guidelines in China, the US, and Europe recommend retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) as the first-line treatment for upper urinary tract calculi < 20 mm [5], while percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) is preferred for stones > 20 mm [3, 4]. With advancements in flexible ureteroscope (FURS) technology, upgraded ureteral access sheaths (UAS), high-power laser systems, and accumulated clinical expertise, RIRS indications have expanded [3, 6]. Multiple studies have reported comparable stone-free rates (SFR) between RIRS and minimally invasive PCNL (mPCNL) for stones > 20 mm [7–9]. Consequently, guidelines now permit experienced operators to treat renal stones up to 30 mm with RIRS [4].

Traditional UAS (T-UAS) requires basket extraction for stone fragments, leaving residual dust for spontaneous passage. In addition, its rigid tip design presents several drawbacks, including uncontrolled intrarenal pressure (IRP); difficulty in managing small lower calyceal stones with narrow infundibulopelvic angles (IPA) or stones with a large burden; compromised visualization due to stone dust and blood clots; and an increased risk of postoperative infectious complications [3, 10, 11]. In recent years, the introduction of FURS has significantly reduced the operational and maintenance costs associated with reusable flexible ureteroscopes in clinical practice [3]. Tip-flexible suctioning UAS (TFS-UAS), featuring an 8 cm flexible distal segment capable of navigating the ureteropelvic junction (UPJ) into target calyces, enhances stone clearance, lowers IRP, and reduces complications by combining vortex flow and suction [5, 9, 12–20]. Thus, clinicians have increasingly adopted FURS with TFS-UAS for stones > 20 mm. Wang et al. demonstrated that comparable SFR between TFS-UAS + FURS and mPCNL for 20–30 mm upper urinary tract calculi, with fewer bleeding/pain complications and shorter hospitalization [9]. This retrospective study evaluated the efficacy of TFS-UAS versus T-UAS in upper urinary tract calculi management at our hospital from March 2023 to August 2024, supplemented by in vitro experiments to elucidate clinical applications.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study included 196 cases of renal calculi or upper ureteral calculi meeting inclusion criteria at Shuguang Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine from March 2023 to August 2024. Retrospective data collection covered: demographic parameters (age, gender, BMI), maximum stone diameter, maximum computed tomography (CT) value of stone, modified STONE score, preoperative urine culture, perioperative indicators (blood leukocytes, hemoglobin, BUN, serum creatinine, operative duration, immediate stone-free rate (iSFR), postoperative complications, etc.), SFR postoperatively at 2 weeks.

Stone burden was determined by measuring maximum stone diameter on CT urography. For multiple stones, the sum of maximum diameters was calculated. All patients underwent preoperative urine culture and CT urography. If preoperative urinalysis indicated UTI, culture-directed antibiotics were administered to treat the infection. For all other patients, routine prophylactic antibiotics were given one day prior to surgery to prevent infection. No significant hematuria or urosepsis occurred postoperatively.

Description of the procedure

Operations were conducted in lithotomy position under general anesthesia. Primary lithotripsy: first, a rigid F6/7.5 ureteroscope was used to explore the ureter, followed by placement of a safety guidewire. For upper ureteral stones, holmium laser was initially applied to rapidly fragment the stones and push them into the renal pelvis. Secondary lithotripsy: First, a rigid F8/9.8 ureteroscope was employed to remove the ureteral stent. Subsequent exploration was performed, and a safety guidewire was reinserted.

TFS-UAS group: Based on intraoperative exploration, TFS-UAS (Specifications: F10-12/F11-13/F12-14; Manufacturer: Shenzhen Cooper Medical Co., Ltd., Approval No.: YXZZ20162020589) was advanced over the guidewire to UPJ or below. The guidewire was then replaced with an F8.6 disposable FURS (Manufacturer: Anhui Happiness Works Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., Approval No.: WXZZ20222060243). An irrigation pump and negative-pressure suction system were connected. Irrigation flow rate was adjusted to 100–200 ml/min. Negative pressure was set at 150–200 mmHg (approximately 0.02–0.027 MPa; 1 mmHg = 0.133 kPa). A 200-µm holmium laser fiber was inserted with the following settings: fragmentation mode 1.2 W, 30 Hz; dusting mode 0.8 W, 30 Hz.

Basket extraction was avoided for stone removal. For lower calyceal stones, basket relocation was selectively used. The sheath position was continuously adjusted to optimize stone clearance. The cloverleaf-shaped negative-pressure port on the TFS-UAS was intermittently occluded when approaching stone fragments to facilitate dust aspiration.For larger fragments entering the sheath, the scope was slowly withdrawn under continuous suction. If aspiration failed, the TFS-UAS was retracted to the renal pelvis outlet. The FURS was then inserted into the lower calyx to relocate residual stones, dusting lithotripsy was performed, and the TFS-UAS was re-guided into the calyx to assist with irrigation-mediated fragment aspiration.

T-UAS group: Based on intraoperative exploration, T-UAS (Specifications: F10-12/F11-13/F12-14; Supplier: Boston Scientific Medical Trading (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., Approval No.: GXZJ20192022305) was advanced over the guidewire to the UPJ or below. The guidewire was then replaced with the same FURS as in the TFS-UAS group, and identical holmium laser power settings were applied for lithotripsy. For stone fragments > 2 mm, basket extraction was preferentially attempted.

Postoperative Protocol: Both groups routinely received double-J stent placement after successful lithotripsy. Follow-up CT urography was performed at 2 weeks postoperatively. Ureteral stents were removed for patients with no residual stones.

Observation indicators

iSFR: The presence of residual stone fragments ≥ 3 mm identified during intraoperative endoscopic inspection of the collecting system is defined as clinically significant residual stone [21].

Residual stone rate: At 2 weeks postoperatively, stone clearance was evaluated via entire urinary tract CT. Residual fragments ≥ 3 mm in maximum diameter were defined as clinically significant residual stones [21].

Operation parameters: Duration (defined as the interval spanning from initial insertion of the rigid ureteroscope into the ureter to final placement of the indwelling urinary catheter upon procedure completion), hematologic changes (leukocytes, Hb, BUN, Cr).

Occurrence of complications: Fever, pain, urinary leakage, perinephric fluid, etc [21].

Hospital stay (Discharge criteria): Patients without postoperative complications are maintained under observation until resolution of gross hematuria, followed by catheter removal and discharge.

Patients with complications are discharged only after complete resolution of the managed complications.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 23.0 software. Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using Student’s t-test. Categorical variables were presented as frequency (n, %) and analyzed via Chi-square test. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The incidence of urolithiasis is increasing annually. According to urological guidelines in China, the US, and Europe, PCNL remains the preferred treatment for upper urinary tract stones > 20 mm. However, PCNL requires establishing a renal parenchymal access tract, especially for non-dilated renal calculi, which demands extensive surgical experience and advanced technical skills from the operator. Additionally, intraoperative/postoperative complications like massive hemorrhage and severe infections may occur, with a longer recovery period [22]. Traditional flexible ureteroscopy for large stones faces bottlenecks including prolonged operative time, “snowstorm” effect impairing visibility, accumulated stone fragments affecting lithotripsy assessment, and postoperative stone fragments potentially forming ureteral steinstrasse. During lithotripsy, renal pelvic/calyceal mucosal and capillary damage causes blood to enter irrigation fluid, blurring the field of view. Blood clots may also obscure larger stone fragments, increasing residual stone risk. Continuous irrigation to maintain visibility, if poorly drained, can elevate IRP. IRP > 30 mmHg sustained over 45 s may lead to systemic absorption of bacterial/toxic irrigation fluid, causing postoperative fever, urosepsis, or septic shock [10]. Prolonged RIRS in a relatively closed renal pelvic environment can also elevate irrigation fluid temperature, causing thermal injury to urothelium [23]. Studies show thermal injury risk inversely correlates with IRP and irrigation flow rate [23]. Thus, reducing IRP or increasing flow rate can mitigate thermal injury.

Negative pressure ureteral access sheath (NP-UAS) is widely used clinically [8, 24–26]. Studies show combining RIRS with NP-UAS allows simultaneous lithotripsy and aspiration. Irrigation fluid refluxes through gaps between the FURS and sheath, with active negative pressure increasing reflux velocity. This maintains visibility, reduces lithotripsy temperature and IRP, improves SFR, and decreases infection/fever risks [8, 24–26]. However, traditional NP-UAS often struggles to pass the UPJ into the renal pelvis/calyces, leading to long turbulent flow paths and reduced aspiration efficiency. RIRS generates great amount powdered/granular fragments, with larger pieces requiring postoperative basket extraction, which is cumbersome and time-consuming. Most fragments rely on spontaneous passage, a lengthy process prone to steinstrasse or re-aggregation [11].

TFS-UAS largly overcomes these limitations [5, 9, 12–20]. In addition to all the feature of T-UAS, TFS-UAS is more advanced during RIRS, for: ① Continuous low renal pressure: The negative pressure handle mimics nephroscope sheath principles. F8.6 disposable FURS with F11/13 sheath achieves ideal scope-sheath ratio (0.75). Combined with central suction (150-200mmHg) and continuous pump irrigation (150–200 mL/min), high flow rates dissipate holmium laser heat, reducing thermal injury [23]. ② Flexible tip design enabling close access to renal pelvis/calyces, especially lower pole stones. Simultaneous water flow and negative pressure aspirate powder through scope-sheath gaps, while larger fragments are extracted during scope withdrawal. Prompt removal of blood/pus improves visibility, reduces clot formation and urosepsis risk, shortens operative time, and enhances stone clearance. ③ Cost reduction: Similar pricing to conventional sheaths avoids extra costs. Reduced basket usage (only for unreachable calyces) lowers hospitalization expenses. Limitations include potential mucosal injury from repeated sheath manipulation requiring relaxed ureters, and temporary pressure reduction/pause during hemorrhage to maintain visibility.

This retrospective study found urologists prefer TFS-UAS + FURS for older patients, females, larger ureteral diameter and double-J stent recipients. Previous studies showed reduced ureteral insertion forces in these demographics, indicating preference for better ureteral conditions [27]. Although the preoperative ureteral stent was not significantly related to improved SFRs [28, 29], a more rigorous study design would control for baseline interventions such as prestenting. Although overall TFS-UAS and T-UAS groups showed no significant differences in operative time or SFRs, TFS-UAS demonstrated superior outcomes for ≥ 15 mm stones: shorter operative time and higher SFRs despite larger, harder, more complex stones. For smaller stones, setup time for TFS-UAS equipment and experienced operators’ ability to compensate T-UAS limitations with high-power laser + basket negated significant differences. In conclusion, TFS-UAS + FURS offers higher SFRs and shorter operative times for ≥ 15 mm upper urinary tract stones.

Operation Best-practice with TFS-UAS + FURS

TFS-UAS differs technically from T-UAS. We concluded the following operation best-practice based on in vitro experiments, our experience, and previous studies:

① TFS-UAS requires deeper placement into the renal pelvis compared to T-UAS (typically at/below UPJ). The 5–10 cm longer sheath and protruding inner core allow depth assessment via guidewire manipulation (Video). Passive bending at the sheath tip combined with active FURS bending provides triple articulation for complex anatomies.④ For fragile stones, use TFS-UAS to enclose and aspirate fragments during lithotripsy. Prioritize pelvic/upper calyx fragments first to avoid lower calyx re-accumulation.⑤ Optimal application of a motorized irrigation system is recommended to maintain continuous irrigation. This approach ensures stable flow dynamics and controlled negative pressure, thereby enhancing procedural safety and efficiency. Inadequate irrigation combined with excessive negative pressure can induce renal pelvic collapse and calyceal neck constriction, significantly compromising procedural maneuverability. The optimal intraoperative state is characterized by a mildly corrugated appearance of the renal pelvic wall under endoscopic visualization. This can be achieved by modulating irrigation flow rate and intermittent suction activation to maintain low IRP, thereby ensuring procedural safety while minimizing risks of sepsis and perinephric hematoma. In cases with acute IPA or elongated narrow calyceal necks where sheath-endoscope co-entry is unachievable, and the sheath’s presence restricts endoscope deflection, stone relocation using retrieval baskets becomes necessary. If basket extraction fails, position the TFS-UAS at the lower calyx entrance, insert the FURS to pulverize stones into dust, and utilize irrigation flow to flush particles into the TFS-UAS for aspiration. For deeply inaccessible lower calyces, combine novel actively deflectable negative-pressure sheaths with the passive bending capability of FURS to achieve calyceal access for lithotripsy.⑧ For medullary sponge stones, TFS-UAS + FURS efficiently aspirates intramucosal fragments. Limitations

Retrospective design with potential selection bias, small sample size, lack of standardized outcome criteria and semi-quantitative evaluation of procedural parameters such as surgeon maneuverability, visual clarity, and instrumental stability, short 2-week follow-up without long-term quality of life/complication/recurrence data.

Conclusion

In this study, TFS-UAS + FURS offered shorter operative times and higher immediate/short-term SFR for ≥ 15 mm upper urinary tract stones. While requiring good ureteral conditions and experienced operators to manage sheath positioning and pressure balance, it provides clinical advantages over conventional approaches.
