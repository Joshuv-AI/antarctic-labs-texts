Persistence and booster response of rabies antibodies among health care workers with multiple vaccinations
Authors: Mark Joseph Castellano, Johanna Beulah Sornillo, Nobuo Saito, Akira Nishizono, Catalino Demetria, Leilanie Nacion, Beatriz Quiambao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rabies is a fatal but vaccine-preventable disease. Health care workers (HCWs) in endemic areas may receive repeated rabies vaccination because of occupational exposure, yet data on long-term antibody persistence and booster response after multiple prior vaccine regimens remain limited. This study aimed to determine the rabies antibody profile of HCWs previously vaccinated with rabies vaccine. We analyzed 126 HCWs from the Research Institute for Tropical Medicine (RITM) and Muntinlupa Animal Bite Treatment Centers (ABTCs) in the Philippines. Vaccination records were reviewed, and booster doses consisting of 0.1 mL purified Vero cell rabies vaccine (PVRV) were administered intradermally on days 0 and 3. Pre- and post-booster rabies antibody levels were measured using the rapid fluorescent focus inhibition test (RFFIT). All HCWs vaccinated within the previous year retained pre-booster antibody titers ≥ 0.5 IU/mL. Participants who had received three or more prior rabies vaccine regimens also maintained protective pre-booster antibody levels within 3–5 years after the last vaccination. After booster administration, all participants achieved antibody titers above the protective threshold ≥ 0.5 IU/mL, regardless of prior vaccination history or time since last vaccination. Repeated rabies vaccination was linked to sustained antibody persistence, while previous vaccination history was associated with preserved booster responsiveness among HCWs. These findings suggest that, in addition to time since last vaccination, the number of prior rabies vaccine regimens may help inform about the persistence of protective antibody levels in previously immunized individuals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41182-026-00980-0.

Introduction

Rabies is a deadly, but preventable zoonotic disease of the central nervous system [1]. Globally, rabies deaths number around 59,000 annually from over 150 countries, with 95% of cases from Africa and Asia [2]. According to the Philippines Department of Health (DOH), a total of 426 human rabies deaths were recorded in 2024 [3]. Once symptoms appear, the disease is almost invariably fatal, emphasizing the importance of prevention through vaccination. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) for all individuals with any category II or III exposure, regardless of previous vaccination history. For those previously vaccinated, two booster doses on days 0 and 3 are recommended, without the need for rabies immunoglobulin [4]. Despite the widespread use of rabies vaccines, limited data exist on the persistence and booster response of rabies antibodies among individuals who have received multiple vaccination regimens. Understanding how long protective antibody levels are maintained and how effectively booster doses elicit immune responses is essential to guide evidence-based booster recommendations. This knowledge gap is particularly important among health care workers (HCWs), who face occupational risks of repeated exposure through patient care, animal bite management, or laboratory handling of rabies specimens. Evaluating antibody persistence and booster response among HCWs can inform occupational safety policies and ensure continuous protection in high-risk settings. This study aimed to characterize the rabies antibody profile of HCWs with multiple prior vaccine regimens and to assess their antibody response according to the time interval since the last vaccination.

Materials and methods

This longitudinal observational study among HCWs from the Research Institute for Tropical Medicine (RITM) and the Muntinlupa Animal Bite Treatment Centers (ABTC) in the Philippines from October 2021 to September 2022, who had previously received either pre- or post-exposure rabies vaccination, was approved by the RITM Institutional Review Board (IRB) (Approval No. 2021-14; July 19, 2021). The list of HCWs who previously received at least one rabies vaccine regimen (i.e., receipt of at least one dose of rabies vaccine either as pre-exposure prophylaxis [PrEP] or PEP, regardless of the completion status) was obtained from the RITM Employees Health Service (EHS) clinic and from ABTC in Muntinlupa City. A complete regimen was defined as receiving 3 doses of PrEP, 4–5 doses of PEP, or 2 booster doses, and regimens with missing doses were categorized as incomplete.

After obtaining their informed consent, HCWs were interviewed, vaccination records were reviewed, and demographic data were obtained. Pre-booster rabies virus neutralizing antibody (RVNA) level was obtained prior to giving of booster doses. Two intradermal booster doses were given, 0.1 ml each on day 0 and day 3, using a commercially available purified Vero cell rabies vaccine (Speeda, Liaoning Chengda Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) with potency of ≥ 2.5 IU. To determine post-booster antibody levels, sera were collected seven days after the receipt of the day 3 booster dose. RVNA levels for both pre- and post-booster were determined using the Rapid Fluorescent Focus Inhibition Test (RFFIT) at RITM Special Pathogens Laboratory [5, 6]. Serum samples underwent heat inactivation at 56 °C for 30 min. The inactivated specimens were serially diluted twofold and incubated with a standardized dose of rabies Challenge Virus Standard (CVS-11). The mixture was maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 90 min. A BHK-21 cell suspension (4 × 105 cells/mL) was added to all wells and incubated for 22–24 h. Cell monolayers were fixed using 80% cold acetone and treated with FITC-labeled anti-rabies conjugate (1:50 dilution). Eight fields per well were examined using immunofluorescence microscopy to identify fluorescent foci. The 50% neutralization endpoints were determined using the Spearman-Kärber method, and values were converted to International Units per milliliter (IU/mL) by calibrating against WHO International Standard Rabies Immunoglobulin. Based on international standards, an adequate immunologic response, defined as rabies VNA levels ≥ 0.5 IU/mL, is considered indicative of protective immunity [7, 8].

We used descriptive statistics, frequencies and proportions, mean and standard deviation [SD], to summarize the characteristics of the HCWs in terms of their demographics, rabies exposure, and RFFIT results. We performed a log transformation on both the pre-booster and post-booster antibody titers from RFFIT. Using the means command of Stata, we estimated the geometric mean titers (GMT) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

To determine predictors of the pre-booster immune response, multivariate logistic regression was performed, with RFFIT results categorized as protective or non-protective. Multivariate linear regression was used to evaluate the association between the number of prior rabies regimens and the time since the last regimen, with log-transformed post-booster antibody titers as the dependent variable. For ease of interpretation, regression coefficients and their corresponding 95% CIs were back-transformed and reported as geometric mean ratios (GMRs). Other covariates in these models included sex, age group (19–39 years, 40–59 years, and ≥ 60 years), nature of work, and regimen completion. In all regression analyses, covariates with a global p-value < 0.20 in univariate analysis were included in the initial multivariate model and retained in the final model only if their p-value was < 0.05 following backward stepwise selection. The adjusted odds ratio (aOR) and adjusted GMRs were reported with their 95% CIs. Data management and statistical analyses were performed using Stata/SE 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA).

Results

A total of 169 HCWs were initially identified from RITM and Muntinlupa ABTCs. After excluding 31 individuals (18 without vaccination records and 13 for other various reasons), 138 met the inclusion criteria. Of these, 129 consented and were enrolled, with three later withdrawing, resulting in 126 HCWs included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Pre-booster antibody levels

The pre-booster GMT of females (1.87 IU/mL) was higher than that of males (1.37 IU/mL), although the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.392). The differences in the pre-booster GMT among age groups 19–39, 40–59, and ≥ 60 years were likewise not significant (p = 0.331). Most HCWs (42.9%) received one regimen of rabies vaccine, while 5 HCWs (4%) got more than four regimens (Table 2). Thirteen of the 126 HCWs (10.3%) had pre-booster RVNA levels < 0.5 IU/mL, with 12 (92.3%) of them having previously received only one vaccine regimen. All these 13 HCWs mounted antibody levels above the protective threshold of 0.5 IU/mL after booster doses. Among HCWs who previously received one rabies vaccine regimen, 22.2% had pre-booster antibody titers below the protective threshold of 0.5 IU/mL, whereas only 2.9% of those with two prior regimens were below the threshold (Table 2). All HCWs with three or more prior regimens had pre-booster antibody titers ≥ 0.5 IU/mL. Pre-booster geometric mean titers (GMTs) generally increased with the number of prior regimens, from 1.18 IU/mL (95% CI 0.88–1.58) in those with one regimen to 2.86 IU/mL (95% CI 2.28–3.59) among those with three regimens.

When grouped according to the interval since the last rabies vaccination regimen, all HCWs who had received vaccination within the previous year demonstrated pre-booster antibody titers ≥ 0.5 IU/mL, with a pre-booster GMT of 2.81 IU/mL (95% CI 2.48–3.18) (Table 2). The highest proportion of participants with antibody titers below 0.5 IU/mL was observed in those vaccinated 6–10 years previously (38.9%).

Among HCWs who received less than three prior PrEP or PEP regimens (n = 89), all participants vaccinated within one year maintained pre-booster antibody titers ≥ 0.5 IU/mL (Table 3). The proportion with antibody titers below 0.5 IU/mL was noted at 20.0% at 1–2 years, 4.4% at 3–5 years, 43.8% at 6–10 years, and 16.7% for those vaccinated more than 10 years. On the other hand, among HCWs who had received three or more prior regimens (n = 37), all maintained pre-booster antibody titers ≥ 0.5 IU/mL across all observed intervals up to 10 years after the last vaccination. However, only two participants in the 6- to 10-year subgroup retained antibody titers above the protective threshold. In univariate analysis, female participants were less likely than males to have pre-booster antibody titers below the protective threshold of 0.5 IU/mL, however this association did not reach statistical significance (OR 0.33, 95% CI 0.10–1.08; p = 0.066) (Table 4). Age group, nature of work, and completeness of previous regimens were also not significantly associated with antibody levels below the protective threshold. Among vaccination-related factors, receiving two prior rabies vaccine regimens was significantly associated with lower odds of having antibody titers below 0.5 IU/mL compared with receiving only one regimen (OR 0.10, 95% CI 0.01–0.83; p = 0.033). In multivariable logistic regression, this association remained significant after adjustment for potential confounders (aOR 0.11, 95% CI 0.01–0.99; p = 0.049). With respect to time interval since the last rabies vaccination, participants vaccinated 3–5 years previously were less likely to have antibody titers below the protective threshold compared with those vaccinated more than 5 years earlier (OR 0.07, 95% CI 0.01–0.60; p = 0.016). This association remained significant after adjustment (aOR 0.08, 95% CI 0.01–0.77; p = 0.029). No participant vaccinated within one year had antibody titers below 0.5 IU/mL, and no participant who had received three or more prior regimens had antibody titers below the protective threshold. Post-booster antibody levels

The post-booster GMTs ranged from 3.35 to 4.00 IU/mL across the number of regimen groups (Table 2). Participants with one prior regimen had the highest post-booster GMT at 4.00 IU/mL (95% CI 3.71–4.31), while among those with multiple prior regimens, GMT ranged from 3.35 to 3.82 IU/mL, without a consistent trend according to increasing number of previous regimens. Furthermore, across all time intervals, the post-booster GMT remained above 3.6 IU/mL, with the highest value observed in participants vaccinated 3–5 years earlier (4.25 IU/mL, 95% CI 3.94–4.59).

All HCWs including those who initially had pre-booster antibody levels of < 0.5 IU/mL developed antibody levels of ≥ 0.5 IU/mL after the booster doses, generally consistent regardless of the number of regimens or time interval from the last vaccination (Table 2). The longest interval between the most recent vaccination and booster administration was 22 years, observed in one participant who no longer demonstrated protective antibody titers prior to boosting.

In univariate linear regression, female participants had significantly higher post-booster GMT than males (GMR 1.48, 95% CI 1.03–2.10; p = 0.033) (Table 5). Participants who had received four or more prior rabies vaccine regimens had significantly lower post-booster GMT compared with those who had received one regimen (GMR 0.54, 95% CI 0.33–0.88; p = 0.014). After multivariable adjustment for sex, number of prior regimens, and years since the last rabies vaccination, female sex remained independently associated with higher post-booster GMT (adjusted GMR 1.70, 95% CI 1.19–2.39; p = 0.004). Compared with one prior regimen, receiving two regimens (adjusted GMR 0.66, 95% CI 0.46–0.96; p = 0.029) and four or more regimens (adjusted GMR 0.51, 95% CI 0.31–0.82; p = 0.006) remained significantly associated with lower post-booster GMT. Among time interval categories, participants vaccinated 3–5 years prior had significantly higher post-booster GMT than those vaccinated more than 5 years earlier (adjusted GMR 1.65, 95% CI 1.05–2.59; p = 0.030). Discussion

The main finding of our study was that repeated rabies vaccination was associated with sustained pre-booster antibody persistence among health care workers, wherein all participants who received three or more rabies vaccines maintained antibody titers above the protective threshold of 0.5 IU/ml within the observed follow-up intervals. On the other hand, sub-protective pre-booster antibody levels were observed primarily in participants with less than three regimens, particularly among those with longer intervals since last vaccination. This finding suggests that repeated antigen exposure enhances long-term immunologic memory and sustains detectable rabies virus-neutralizing antibodies. In the study of Morris et al. among bat workers (N = 185), all 40 individuals in the secondary group (i.e., completed a primary PEP course + booster) maintained titers ≥ 0.5 IU/mL two years after their last booster, whereas 11.1% of those in the primary group (i.e., received only a primary PEP course) had titers < 0.5 IU/mL immediately prior to their first booster, with all subsequently achieving ≥ 0.5 IU/mL after boosting [9]. Although our estimates should be interpreted with caution due to small subgroup sizes, these findings suggest that the number of prior vaccine regimens can be associated with sustained antibody levels.

Participants were categorized according to the number of rabies vaccine regimens previously received. However, the number of doses per regimen may vary depending on whether the individual received PrEP or PEP, and whether the regimen was completed or not. Consequently, individuals within the same number of regimens may have received different cumulative numbers of vaccine doses. To explore this further, a supplementary analysis was performed evaluating the relationship between the cumulative number of vaccine doses received and pre-booster RVNA titers (Suppl Table 1). No significant correlation was observed, suggesting that cumulative dose alone may not fully determine long-term antibody persistence. This is consistent with the study of Briggs and Schwenke, in which no significant difference in the longevity of circulating antibodies was observed between patients (N = 875) who received a three-dose pre-exposure regimen, and those who received a five-dose post-exposure vaccination regimen with human diploid cell rabies vaccine (HDCV) [10]. In contrast, Fukushima et al. reported that individuals (N = 97) receiving three or more doses had significantly higher GMT and seroprotection rates compared to those who received only two doses, even after a long follow-up [11], suggesting that a minimum threshold of antigen exposure may be required to establish durable immunity.

The time since the last vaccination also influenced antibody persistence among participants. Among those with less than three prior rabies vaccine regimens, the proportion with antibody titers below the protective threshold increased, particularly in those last vaccinated 6–10 years prior. In contrast, all participants who had received ≥ 3 prior regimens within 3–5 years after their most recent vaccination maintained pre-booster RVNA titers at or above the WHO-defined protective threshold. A similar pattern was observed among those vaccinated 6–10 years previously, but this finding should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited sample size. Even though point estimates of pre- and post-booster GMT varied across time categories, the overlapping confidence intervals signify that these differences are not statistically significant. This finding is comparable to the reports of Brown et al., who, among 89 travelers previously vaccinated with PrEP, observed an increasing trend in GMT over time without a clear explanation [12]. Notably, all participants vaccinated within one year prior to antibody testing retained pre-booster antibody levels above 0.5 IU/mL, indicating persistence of serologic protection during this interval, despite evidence from other studies showing early antibody decline within months after vaccination [13–19]. In relation to this, the current WHO recommendations state that when repeated rabies exposure occurs within three months after completion of a full PEP regimen, only wound care is required and no further rabies vaccine or rabies immunoglobulin (RIG) should be administered. For exposures occurring more than three months after the last PEP, the booster vaccination schedule for previously immunized individuals should be followed, and RIG is not indicated [20]. In our study, the persistence of protective antibody levels in all participants vaccinated within one year suggests that detectable serologic protection may extend beyond the currently recommended 3-month interval in previously immunized individuals. However, since this study assessed antibody persistence rather than clinical protection, and because the sample size remains limited, these findings should be interpreted as supportive evidence of sustained short-term immunity, rather than as a basis for modifying current post-exposure recommendations.

All participants mounted adequate protective antibody levels after booster vaccination, regardless of the number of prior regimens received or the time from the last vaccine dose, signifying a robust anamnestic response even after prolonged intervals. Even if variations in post-booster GMTs were seen across groups, these differences are not likely to be clinically significant, since all participants still mounted protective antibody levels (≥ 0.5 IU/ml) after receiving the booster doses. A systematic review by Langedijk et al. showed that rabies vaccine booster doses were effective, independent of route of administration or time interval [14]. Several determinants can influence the immune response to vaccination, including host-related factors such as age, sex, and genetic background; vaccine-specific factors such as formulation, type, and dosage; and administration-related factors such as injection site, route, and timing of vaccination [21]. In our study, after adjustment, sex, age, work type, and regimen completeness were not independently associated with antibody persistence, suggesting that repeated vaccination history may exert a stronger influence than demographic characteristics alone. Our study has several limitations. It could not fully distinguish between pre-exposure and post-exposure regimens, vaccine type, administration route, or the completeness of previously received regimens, which may influence cumulative antigen exposure. Furthermore, small subgroup sizes, particularly in participants with longer vaccination intervals and higher numbers of prior regimens, also limit the precision of some estimates. Despite these limitations, the findings suggest that the number of prior rabies vaccine regimens and the interval since the last vaccination may help inform interpretation of serologic persistence among previously immunized health care workers. Larger longitudinal studies are needed to better explain how prior repeated vaccinations may be incorporated into booster decision-making strategies for occupationally exposed populations.

Conclusion

We characterized the rabies antibody profile of health care workers, particularly those who had previously received multiple rabies vaccine regimens. Health care workers with recent rabies vaccination within the past year, as well as those with multiple prior regimens, generally maintained protective rabies antibody levels before booster administration. All participants also demonstrated adequate post-booster antibody responses, indicating preserved anamnestic response, despite varying vaccination histories. The findings of the study also suggest that protective antibody levels may persist for up to one year after prior PrEP or PEP in previously immunized individuals, supporting the evidence of sustained short-term immunity. Among health care workers who had received three or more prior rabies vaccine regimens, antibody titers remained protective within 3–5 years after the last vaccination, suggesting that both time since last dose and number of prior regimens may aid interpretation of antibody persistence and guide booster decisions within current guidelines.
