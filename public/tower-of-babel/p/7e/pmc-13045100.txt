Integrating epidemiological modeling and time-series forecasting to optimize pandemic patient allocation
Authors: Seyedreza Abazari, Onur Alisan, Omer Arda Vanli, Eren Erman Ozguven
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis paper addresses the challenge of allocating patients to healthcare facilities with limited capacities during infectious disease outbreaks. The method is based on a hierarchical time-series model to forecast hospital bed demand and a mixed-integer nonlinear mathematical model for allocating patients among a set of regions to minimize disease spread.MethodsOur contributions include (1) a new hierarchical time-series model for forecasting hospital bed demand to enhance regional predictive accuracy, (2) a mathematical model that integrates the forecasting model with a Susceptible, Infected, Recovered (SIR) epidemic model to capture metapopulation dynamics and the impact of patient allocation on disease spread across regions, and (3) a sensitivity analysis to assess the importance of the optimization and forecasting parameters on allocation performance.ResultsThe proposed approach is illustrated with a real-data case study from the COVID-19 pandemic in Florida which demonstrates that forecasting performance depends strongly on regional hospital capacity. The hierarchical model performs better in high-capacity regions, while the univariate model is more effective in regions with sparse bed availability. At the state level, both models yield comparable objective function values, but they lead to markedly different spatial distributions of unmet demand. The sensitivity analysis enables us to study the contributions of individual factors and shows that decision-making frequency plays a more critical role. Based on these findings, monthly decision intervals are recommended and forecasting model selection should be tailored to regional capacity.ConclusionThe results highlight the practical effectiveness of the proposed approach and its ability to capture trade-offs in patient allocation strategies. By explicitly modeling the additional disease transmissions resulting from patient reallocations across counties, the proposed framework offers actionable insights to support healthcare preparedness and operational decision-making during pandemics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12942-026-00455-9.

Background

The pandemic (COVID-19) has profoundly impacted public health and caused significant economic losses worldwide. Unlike previous health crises, COVID-19 spread rapidly on a global scale, exposing vulnerabilities in both healthcare infrastructure and economic systems across countries [1]. By June 2023, the number of confirmed COVID-19 cases had reached approximately 690 million, with an estimated 7 million reported deaths [2]. This unprecedented scale triggered widespread government interventions, including lockdowns and travel restrictions, which further strained economic activities worldwide [3]. The pandemic placed immense strain on healthcare systems globally, challenging their capacities to respond effectively. Hospitals faced unprecedented surges in patient volume, creating bottlenecks in resource allocation and delivery of care [4]. In epidemic outbreaks, the operations of the healthcare system are directly influenced by the growth of infections, underscoring the need for robust preparedness and flexible resource management strategies [5]. Effective epidemic response depends heavily on dynamic allocation of patients or resources that can adjust to fluctuating infection rates, supported by predictive models and real-time data analytics. For instance, studies conducted in China revealed low levels of satisfaction among healthcare workers regarding the availability of essential protective equipment (PE), such as N95 face masks, protective clothing, and goggles, with satisfaction rates ranging from 15% to 53% [6]. This shortage not only jeopardized the safety of frontline workers but also contributed to increased infection risks among healthcare staff, undermining overall response capacity. Similarly, reports from Spain and Italy highlighted the overwhelming demand for intensive care units (ICUs), which exceeded the available capacity in many hospitals [7], while in the United States, emergency department (ED) overcrowding has become a national healthcare crisis [8]. Beyond critical care capacity, medicine shortages and improper use of pharmaceuticals emerged as pressing issues during the pandemic, leading not only to financial losses but also to significant risks for patient outcomes [9]. Equally important was the challenge of effectively allocating patients across healthcare facilities to optimize resource use and prevent overwhelming any single hospital [10]. Developing dynamic patient allocation and transfer strategies became essential to balance demand with capacity, ensuring timely care delivery and reducing unmet demand.

This paper considers the patient allocation problem for pandemic response. Patient allocation is concerned with making decisions about redistributing patients from overburdened to underutilized facilities while utilizing healthcare infrastructure [11]. This dynamic approach helps prevent bottlenecks, ensures that no hospital is overwhelmed while others operate below capacity, and maintains equitable access to care. Recent studies have also demonstrated the use of patient reallocation models in disaster scenarios, such as dialysis patients during infrastructure disruptions, to improve equitable access and system resilience [12]. As an example, in Spain in early April 2020, to alleviate the pressure on Madrid’s healthcare system during a significant surge in COVID-19 cases, patients were transferred to hospitals in neighboring regions with comparatively lower case burdens [13]. Furthermore, research on patient allocation models has shown that when hospitals are overwhelmed, collaboration between facilities, as well as an efficient allocation of patients, is necessary to distribute the medical burden effectively [14]. This approach not only helps alleviate pressure on overburdened hospitals but also ensures that the available medical resources are effectively triaged to the patients who need them most [15]. In fact, optimization methods have been widely applied to solve patient allocation problems, including operating room scheduling, emergency patient allocation, and healthcare facility allocation, to ensure that critical resources are deployed efficiently during crises [16–18]. Recent work has also applied patient allocation optimization during the COVID-19 crisis specifically, integrating compartmental models and real-world hospital data to develop efficient cross-regional allocation strategies under capacity constraints [19].

Resource allocation methods during pandemics typically focus on optimizing the distribution of critical supplies such as PPE, ICU beds, medical equipment, and test kits to hospitals, pharmacies, and other demand points to ensure timely and equitable access for vulnerable populations [20–22]. While these strategies are essential for long-term capacity planning, they often fall short in addressing the immediate pressures hospitals face during a pandemic. Procuring and distributing resources is time-intensive and may not match the speed or geographic variability of infection surges. In contrast, patient allocation provides a unique operational advantage because it does not depend on assumptions about a hospital’s ability to absorb new resources. By focusing on balancing demand across facilities rather than expanding supply, patient allocation ensures that every available bed and healthcare worker is utilized efficiently. This makes it not just a necessary alternative in data-constrained settings, but a vital complement to resource-based strategies in any real-time pandemic response.

The selection of timing and the location of transferred patients during a pandemic requires formulating a dynamic decision-making model. In this context, patient transfers refer to the movement of individual patients between healthcare facilities based on changing demand and capacity conditions. In practice, such transfers can be implemented when the first hospital that the patient visits has reached full capacity and the patient is then redirected by the hospital administration through coordination among hospitals within regional healthcare networks to the nearest facility with available beds. Similar redirection mechanisms have been documented in recent studies, including a case study from Kentucky in which patients were guided to alternative facilities through coordinated communication and established referral policies [14]. The demonstrated effectiveness of such strategies has led to increasing interest in data-driven approaches for patient reallocation that dynamically track demand in real time, enhancing healthcare systems’ ability to respond to sudden changes in demand and capacity. Such interregional patient allocation models help optimize healthcare resources by enhancing healthcare systems’ responsiveness in emergencies [10]. Mathematical models form the foundation of most patient redistribution approaches. Aydin & Cetinkale [23] developed a model to optimize patient distribution and evacuation rates over a medium-term planning horizon, demonstrating its effectiveness in Istanbul. Similarly, Dijkstra et al. [24] used queuing network theory to balance COVID-19 patient loads within and across regions, while other studies incorporated extensions of the Susceptible–Infected–Recovered (SIR) model to account for vaccination impacts, aiming to minimize mortality and location inequity under healthcare capacity constraints [25]. Other recent studies were tailored to specific resource allocation challenges, such as vaccines and staff workload, as reviewed in [26–28].

An accurate epidemic model of infectious disease is needed to control the disease spread to new areas through human mobility. Prior research establishes a significant relationship between mobility and disease dynamics, especially for highly contagious diseases like COVID-19 [29]. Compartmental epidemiological models have been extensively employed to capture these dynamics and assess the effects of interventions such as lockdowns and social distancing [30–32]. Recent studies have demonstrated that integrating real-world surveillance data into SEIR-based models can significantly improve short-term outbreak forecasting, even in data-scarce settings such as rural communities [33]. Models like SIR and its variations, which are used extensively for analyzing disease propagation, are shown to be promising for patient reallocation applications. However, recent reviews highlight that extending these compartmental models to include endogenous behavioral feedback such as changes in perceived risk and adherence to non-pharmaceutical interventions can significantly improve realism and predictive performance, particularly over longer horizons [34]. For instance, Cooper et al. [35] used an SIR model with an exposed compartment to incorporate the disease incubation period, while Calvetti et al. [36] developed an SEIR model to differentiate transmission in rural and urban areas. To account for movements within networks of geographically isolated populations, metapopulation-based epidemic models have been extensively investigated. This modeling approach, originated from the work by Keeling & Rohani [37], who considered a metapopulation consisting of two patches, provides valuable insights into the dynamics of disease transmission across interconnected populations. Goel et al. [38] further extended this approach and investigated the effectiveness of lockdown measures in pandemic management by developing two variations of a mobility-based SIR model. Other approaches incorporated the demographic distribution of various locations, including Mo et al. [29] who explored the role of regional disparities and the movement of individuals between regions in driving the transmission of epidemics using metapopulation models. Literature review summary

Patient satisfaction and experience are key indicators of healthcare service quality, influenced by various experiential factors and care settings [43]. Despite advancements, existing patient allocation approaches face challenges, particularly in accurately forecasting demand and incorporating this information into mathematical models. Effective demand forecasting is critical not only for the sustainability and efficiency of healthcare systems but also for maintaining patient satisfaction [44]. Moreover, traditional models often overlook patient mobility, even though inter-regional movement can accelerate the spread of highly contagious viruses like COVID-19, further complicating patient allocation and resource management. These limitations highlight the need for a patient allocation framework that (i) incorporates accurate demand forecasting based on real-world data and (ii) accounts for the dynamic effects of patient transfers on disease transmission. To address these challenges, this paper proposes a data-driven mathematical model for patient allocation within healthcare systems during a pandemic. Methods

Study area and time period

The study focuses on the State of Florida during a pivotal 250-day period of the COVID-19 pandemic, from January 14 to September 20, 2021. Florida comprises 67 counties, with each county characterized by distinct demographic compositions and healthcare capacities. This timeframe is of particular interest as it began shortly after COVID-19 vaccines were made publicly available in December 2020. For the same time frame, data on healthcare system capacities were sourced from the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences (NIEHS) on GitHub [45]. In this data set, the utilization of hospital beds (PctBeds), number of staffed beds available (Staffed.All.Beds), ICU bed availability (Staffed.ICU.Beds), hospitalization rates, testing counts, and vaccination counts during the study period are provided for all 67 counties of Florida.

Methodology

The proposed data-driven nonlinear mathematical model for optimal allocation of COVID-19 patients to healthcare facilities aims to simultaneously minimize the unmet demand for hospital resources, the number of transferred patients per county, and the new infections that arise due to transfers. Specifically, the mathematical model utilizes a discrete-time SIR metapopulation model to predict the infection counts under counterfactual scenarios in which patients are assigned to healthcare facilities located in different health system regions (different from those for which the data were collected) and time-series forecasting models to predict hospital bed demand corresponding to the infection counts. The math model formulation uses the SIR equations and the time-series forecasting model estimated based on the actual hospital capacities and the number of beds per new infection to account for how the patient allocations and resulting movements would impact the spread of the disease.

Time-series prediction of health-system demand during pandemics

In this paper, we propose a hierarchical time-series model to estimate hospital bed demand per infection count in the patient allocation approach. Specifically, we adopt a hierarchical autoregressive-integrated moving average (ARIMA) time-series modeling framework for modeling the time-series data of hospitalized patients and the COVID-19 infection counts [48, 49].

Before presenting the implementation details, it is important to clarify the conceptual differences between the univariate and hierarchical time-series models used in this study. The univariate model forecasts hospital bed demand for a single time-series, using only its own past observations and treating each region independently. In contrast, the hierarchical model jointly forecasts hospitalization demand across multiple spatial levels (county, region, and state) while ensuring consistency between the disaggregated and aggregated forecasts [51]. The latter approach allows regional forecasts to incorporate information from higher levels of aggregation, improving predictive stability and accuracy, particularly in regions with limited or noisy data.

The best ARIMA model was selected by fitting the model combinations of (p, d,q) in which p = 0,1,2; d = 0,1,2; and q = 0,1,2 and the best model was chosen based on the AIC [49]. The model that best fits the data was the ARIMA (0,1,2) model, which provided the smallest AIC value of 488.56. Residual diagnostic plots of the best model (residuals over time, autocorrelation function of residuals, and the histogram of residuals overlayed with a normal density curve) did not result in any concerns about whether the assumptions of independence and normality for residuals are satisfied, and the model can be used for prediction.

In our analysis, we considered 14 days (bi-weekly), 20 days, and 28 days as possible values for the decision-making frequency, which determines the length of the forecasting horizon. In all cases, the first 40 days of the study period are used to construct the training data to fit the time-series model. For example, for bi-weekly decision-making, the model was trained with data from days 1 to 40, and total hospitalizations were predicted for the next 14 days (days 41 to 54). The model was then updated with actual values from days 41 to 54, and predictions for the next 14-day period (days 54 to 68) were made. This process was repeated iteratively, with the training set expanding each time by adding the newly available data, continuing until day 250. The first 40 days of the study period were used as the training data for both the SIR and time-series forecasting models. Because time-series forecasting relies on sequential data, the training set must consist of the earliest part of the series to maintain correct temporal ordering and prevent leakage of future information [52]. While extending the training data window could slightly improve ARIMA accuracy, it would reduce the untrained portion of the data over which the optimization model operates. Preliminary experiments confirmed that a 40-day sequential window provided a reasonable balance between reliable model estimation and sufficient horizon for optimization. In order to use all data available at a given time point and improve the accuracy of the model over time, we use an expanding window approach. This aligns with the real-world scenario in which the data is relatively stationary and as more data becomes available with time, the past data remains relevant for predicting future hospitalizations. It also better captures long-term trends in hospital bed demand, where past trends are crucial for making future predictions.

SIR modeling and nonlinear program for optimal patient allocation

The SIR model was selected to represent the spread of COVID-19 due to its proven effectiveness [53] using only quantities that were consistently available in our dataset. Although reported cases do not equal true infections, surveillance literature shows that routinely reported case counts form a scaled but dynamically coherent representation of the underlying infection process when reporting rates are relatively stable [54], and thus can serve as a valid proxy for the “Infected’’ compartment in mechanistic models. This justification is particularly important here because more detailed compartments, such as “Exposed’’, “Vaccinated”, and “Hospitalized” compartments in the SEIR, SIRV and SIRH models, respectively, were not observable in our data, and no reliable estimates of latent infections or incubation dynamics were available at the county level. The SIR framework, therefore, provides a transparent and computationally efficient structure to illustrate our main contributions of embedding demand forecasting and epidemic dynamics directly into a large-scale optimization model. Nonetheless, the framework we present is fully generalizable, and if reliable data on exposed or vaccinated compartments are available, it can be easily expanded to include SEIR, SIRV, or multi-compartment variants with minimal structural modification.

We next explain how the nonlinear programming model integrates the SIR epidemic framework to capture the epidemic progression dynamics and the effects of patient movement on disease transmission. The objective function minimizes two competing outcomes: the unmet hospital demand (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:UD$$\end{document}), representing patients who need care but cannot be hospitalized, and the transferred patients (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:TP$$\end{document}), representing those moved between counties to balance capacity. Because patient transfers can reduce unmet demand but may increase disease transmission risk, the model seeks an optimal balance between accessibility of care and containment of infection spread under evolving epidemic conditions. The SIR equations, included as constraints, track how the numbers of susceptible, infected, and recovered individuals evolve over time in each county, ensuring that patient transfers are reflected in the infection dynamics. While the SIR equations do not appear explicitly in the objective function, they influence it indirectly through their impact on patient allocation and the number of infected individuals.

(14) and (15), merely to improve readability and to clearly illustrate the normalization process applied to each term (i.e., we would have an identical model if we removed these two constraints and instead represented the objective function as the summation of the expressions of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:TP$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:UD$$\end{document}). According to Eqs. (14) and (15), each term is normalized to ensure balanced contributions to the objective. Since the magnitude of unmet demand is typically much larger than that of patient transfers, dividing by the number of regions (n) and possible transfer arcs (n(n − 1)) adjusts for scale differences between the two measures. This normalization prevents either term from dominating the optimization results and improves numerical stability without imposing arbitrary weights. Consequently, the objective yields a more balanced trade-off between minimizing unmet healthcare demand and minimizing the logistical cost of inter-county transfers.

Constraint (16) balances satisfied demand, unmet demand, patient transfers and new demand due to infected people. The left-hand side represents the satisfied and unmet demand in county \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:i$$\end{document} at time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{t}^{{\prime\:}}$$\end{document}, while the right-hand side combines local hospitalization demand (derived from the forecasted infection portion using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{\alpha\:}}_{i,{t}^{{\prime\:}}}{I}_{i,t}$$\end{document}) and the net flow of patients transferred in and out of the county. Note that using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{i,t}$$\end{document} in the constraint rather than a direct demand forecast \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{W}}_{i,t}$$\end{document} ensures that hospitalization needs remain dynamically linked to the infection trajectory generated by the SIR model. This connection allows the optimization to reflect how changes in infection levels influence capacity usage and unmet demand, maintaining a consistent feedback loop between epidemic progression and allocation decisions. Constraint (17) limits the number of admitted patients (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{i,t{\prime\:}}$$\end{document}) by the county’s effective bed capacity, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{i}\times\:{\gamma\:}_{i}$$\end{document}. Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{i}$$\end{document} is the total number of beds and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\gamma\:}_{i}$$\end{document} represents the recovery rate, which captures the turnover of available beds due to patient discharges. This ensures that admissions do not exceed realistic daily capacity. Constraint (18) updates the number of susceptible individuals (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{i,t}$$\end{document}) by subtracting newly infected individuals from the previous day. The infection rate depends on both the contact rate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\beta\:}_{i}$$\end{document}) and the current number of susceptible and infected individuals, scaled by the county population \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{N}_{i}$$\end{document}.

Constraints (19) and (20) represent the infection dynamics in two different time periods, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:t^\prime$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:t$$\end{document} respectively. Constraint (19) defines the evolution of infected individuals during decision periods \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:t^\prime$$\end{document} (when transfers are allowed; number of infected individuals increase with new infections and patient inflows; and decreases with recoveries and patient outflows) while Constraint (20) represents infection dynamics during non-decision periods \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:t$$\end{document} (when no transfers occur), excluding the transfer terms to avoid double counting. Accordingly, the variables \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{i,t}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{i,t{\prime\:}}$$\end{document} are dependent. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{i,t}$$\end{document} denotes the daily number of infected individuals, whereas \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{i,t^{\prime\:}}$$\end{document} represents the infection level at the beginning of each decision period. By definition, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{i,t{\prime\:}}\:$$\end{document}equals the daily infections \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{i,t}$$\end{document} for all time periods \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:t=t^{\prime\:}$$\end{document}. Constraint (21) updates the recovered population by adding the fraction of infected individuals who recover in each period, governed by the recovery rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\gamma\:}_{i}$$\end{document}. Although this constraint does not directly influence the objective function, it is included to allow tracking the recovered population over time, which indirectly affects future infection dynamics through reductions in both the susceptible and infected groups. Constraints (22)–(24) enforce spatial and distance limitations on transfers. Patients can only be transferred if the distance between counties \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:i$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:j$$\end{document} does not exceed the predefined maximum distance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:D$$\end{document}. Constraints (25)–(28) impose non-negativity and logical bounds on all decision variables to ensure physically meaningful solutions.

As mentioned before, the data for days 1 through 40 are used as the training data to estimate the time-series models and the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\gamma\:$$\end{document} parameters of the SIR model. All statistical computations were conducted in R [50]. These parameters were then incorporated as fixed inputs into the mathematical model to effectively capture the impact of patient movement between counties on the spread of the disease. The optimal patient allocation model is therefore solved for the period spanning from day \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{t}_{B}=41$$\end{document} until day \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{t}_{E}=150$$\end{document}.

The model is a mixed-integer program due to the integer and binary decision variables used in patient allocation across regions. Due to the SIR constraint equations, the model is nonlinear, which makes it computationally intensive, especially given the large number of decision variables and constraints. To improve computational efficiency and avoid suboptimal solutions, we opted to linearize the nonlinear constraints (see Appendix A in Supplementary Materials). In our problem setting, the locations of the healthcare facilities are already known and fixed. For the initial period, we estimate the demand or the number of infected patients, as well as the SIR parameters, utilizing an SIR model. The estimated values from the SIR model for the first period serve as the basis for our subsequent analysis. The following assumptions are made in formulating the model: (1) The capacity of the healthcare facilities is fixed and does not change; (2) The patients might be allocated to other regions within a known reasonable distance; (3) The decision-making horizon is bi-weekly, twenty days, or monthly; (4) The recovery time of the patients is the same for all patients within a region; (5) Each region can send and receive patients from other regions. In other words, regions can both send and receive patients within a given time period; (6) Although different regions might have several healthcare facilities, we have aggregated the total capacity and considered it as a single number representing the total capacity; (7) The distance between regions is measured based on the roadway distance between the centroids of counties since we have available resources data at the county level; (8) Patient transfers represent the movement of individual patients between regions; (9) Patient allocations do not affect the number of beds needed per infection. The nonlinear constraints (18), (19) and (20) are linearized using McCormick envelopes (see Appendix A in Supplementary Materials for details).

Results

In this section, we present the results of the proposed patient allocation method applied to the data from the COVID-19 pandemic in Florida. Also, we compare the proposed approach to a greedy optimization algorithm.

Epidemic modeling

To initialize the SIR model, the period corresponding to days 1,2, …, 40 was dedicated to estimating the initial epidemic parameters to be used at the beginning time period \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{t}_{B}=41\:$$\end{document}in the mathematical model. These parameters, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{(i,{t}_{B})}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}_{(i,{t}_{B})}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{(i,{t}_{B})}$$\end{document} (initial numbers of susceptible, infected, and recovered), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\beta\:}_{i}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\gamma\:}_{i}$$\end{document} (infection and recovery rates) for county \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:i$$\end{document}, were estimated using a maximum likelihood-based approach. This process ensured the accurate initialization of the model, laying the foundation to obtain precise predictions for use in optimal allocation problem. The infection-count time-series of the counties for the first 40 days of the study period and the estimated values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\beta\:$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\gamma\:$$\end{document} are provided in Appendix B in Supplementary Materials.

Solution of the optimal allocation problem

The proposed mathematical model was implemented in GAMS and solved using the CPLEX solver with epidemic and time-series parameters estimated from the data. The model is computationally demanding due to its scale. For a planning horizon of 150 days and 67 counties, the decision space includes patient transfer variables for every origin–destination–decision-period combination, amounting to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:67\times\:(67-1)\times\:(150/\varDelta\:t{\prime\:})$$\end{document} variables, along with epidemiological state variables (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:S,\:I,\:R)$$\end{document} for each county and day, totaling \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:3\times\:67\times\:150$$\end{document} variables, as well as capacity and unmet demand variables. Altogether, this results in hundreds of thousands of variables and constraints, yielding a large-scale mixed-integer nonlinear programming (MINLP) problem. Such problems are NP-hard, and solution times increase sharply with the number of counties, planning days, and decision frequency. To improve tractability, we linearized nonlinear constraints and relaxed selected integer variables, thereby reducing the computational burden while preserving solution quality. These adjustments allowed us to solve the model exactly within manageable computation times.

Benchmark approach: greedy patient allocation algorithm

To benchmark our proposed model, we implement a simple greedy allocation algorithm as a baseline [56]. This method allocates unmet demand to neighboring regions based on proximity. For each region with excess demand beyond its local capacity, the algorithm assigns available capacity from the closest neighbors iteratively until demand is met or no capacity remains. This method prioritizes allocating resources to the nearest regions without optimization, serving as a simple baseline for comparison. Figure 4 presents the pseudocode of the proposed greedy method. The model results obtained from both the exact solution and the greedy method are shown in Fig. 5. The proposed approach yields a higher number of patients allocated across regions than the greedy approach while achieving significantly lower unmet demand (and hence lower objective function). These findings clearly demonstrate the superiority of the exact solution over the greedy approach. The primary reason for this difference is that the greedy method only considers neighboring counties when allocating patients, which limits its effectiveness and leads to larger unmet demand values, ultimately increasing the overall objective function value.

Sensitivity analysis of the optimal solutions

To assess and quantify the influence of model parameter choices on optimal solutions and provide further insights into the results, we conducted a sensitivity analysis of critical model parameters. Specifically, the maximum allowable distance (D), decision-making solution intervals (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:t{\prime\:}$$\end{document}), and the geographic resolution of the forecasting models were considered as the three parameters for further study. In the analysis, we considered four levels for maximum distance: 30, 50, 70, and 100 miles. Distances greater than 100 miles might not be practical for allocating patients, and smaller distances than 30 miles are also impractical, as the minimum distance between counties in Florida is approximately 25 miles. Decision-making intervals of 14 (bi-weekly), 20 and 28 (monthly) days were considered. For the forecasting models, we considered the geographic resolutions of hierarchical and univariate time-series models, as shown in Table 7.

We solved the optimization model for different values of Maximum Distance (A), Forecasting Model (B) and Decision-making Frequency (C) which gave us 24 different configurations under which the optimization model was solved. To assess the performance of each configuration, we used the resulting data on the objective function under different values of not only A, B, C but also Region (D). This approach allows us to evaluate the performance of the optimization model across all regions without re-running the model for each configuration separately. By treating Region as an additional factor (Factor D) in the ANOVA framework, we are able to quantify and compare the influence of regional differences on the objective function in a statistically robust manner. We evaluated the effects of various parameter choices on the objective function through analysis of variance (ANOVA). Specifically, a four-factor ANOVA model was fitted in which the significant terms in each model are determined through a stepwise selection process (the full model included all interactions; at each step, an insignificant factor was removed until all remaining factors exhibited statistical significance). Discussion and managerial insights

Several key practical insights resulting from our sensitivity analysis, which compared the optimal solutions across a wide array of decision parameters, can be summarized as follows. It was shown that monthly or twenty-day decision-making intervals are recommended in order to minimize the amount of unmet demand and number of transferred patients, as bi-weekly updates lead to higher objective function values, indicating inefficiencies and higher operational costs. While more frequent updates allow greater responsiveness, they also increase resource usage, thus making less frequent updates is a more cost-effective and efficient option within this context. Among all factors, regional characteristics emerged as the most influential driver of system performance. Moreover, interaction effects between Region, decision frequency, and forecasting model significantly shaped outcomes, suggesting that strategies must be tailored to local infrastructure and context. Additionally, we observed that in regions with faster disease transmission (higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document}), the model performed better, likely due to more responsive patient allocation, whereas faster recovery rates (higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\gamma\:$$\end{document}) were associated with poorer outcomes, possibly because they delayed transfers under the assumption of lower urgency. These findings emphasize the importance of region-specific planning and highlight that reactive capacity decisions must account for local outbreak dynamics, not just system-wide averages.

The choice of forecasting model, however, is highly context dependent. While the hierarchical model excels in two high-capacity regions (such as Regions 1 and 7), the univariate model is superior in more vulnerable, low-capacity regions (see Fig. 6). This is because regions with a dense hospital network can absorb forecast errors, while sparse networks are highly sensitive to underpredictions, which lead to significant increases in unmet demand. This implies that there is no one-size-fits-all forecasting tool. Healthcare managers must select a model based on their region’s infrastructure, prioritizing simplicity and reliability in areas with limited bed capacity. The hierarchical model is preferable primarily for regions 7 and 1, whereas the univariate model is more suitable for other regions and difference at the Florida level is negligible. A spatial analysis of unmet demand across Florida using these models further reveals significantly different distributions of unmet demand across the state (see Fig. 10). To address spatial disparities in unmet demand, resources and any capacity expansion options such as field hospitals should be prioritized for the densely populated southern counties, particularly Miami-Dade and Broward, while central Florida requires moderate attention. Northern Florida and the Panhandle, with lower unmet demand, can serve as resource hubs, reinforcing their role as critical transfer nodes while maintaining sufficient capacity for future surges.

Conclusions and future study

This study has tackled the pressing challenge of optimizing patient allocation in regions with limited healthcare resources during the COVID-19 outbreak. Through the development and application of a mathematical model that integrates hospital capacity data, time-series analysis, and an SIR epidemiological framework, the approach can account for the complex dynamics of disease transmission in patient allocation. By effectively addressing the complexities of pandemic resource allocation across varied geographic regions, our approach provides valuable insights for policymakers and healthcare practitioners, showcasing its potential as a reliable tool for dynamic patient allocation and resource distribution during public health crises.

The results of the case study demonstrate that the performance of the forecasting models is contingent on regional hospital capacity, with no single model showing universal superiority. The hierarchical model proved advantageous in high-capacity regions, while the univariate model was more effective in regions with sparse bed availability. However, when considering the objective function at the Florida level, there is no significant difference in performance between the univariate and hierarchical models; our analysis reveals that the models result in significantly different distributions of unmet demand across Florida. Additionally, the proposed sensitivity analysis provided a convenient method for determining the optimal tuning parameters of the mathematical model, and our analysis revealed that the maximum transfer distance is not a highly influential factor in the model’s performance. Based on our findings, we recommend using a twenty-day or monthly decision-making interval and tailoring the forecasting model choice to the specific capacity of the region for patient allocation across Florida counties.

It is worth noting some limitations of the proposed approach. In particular, we assumed healthcare capacity to be fixed and did not account for potential fluctuations in available beds or resources over time. Additionally, the epidemiological parameters (transmission and recovery rates, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\gamma\:$$\end{document}) were assumed to stay constant throughout the analysis, which may not fully capture the dynamic nature of disease spread. Importantly, uncertainty was not explicitly incorporated into the model, which may be important when the variability in epidemiological parameters and operational constraints is significant. Finally, the model assumes homogeneous mixing within each county and does not explicitly account for spatial clustering, age-specific contact patterns, or superspreading events, which can influence disease transmission dynamics.

Looking forward, the scope of this study could be expanded by exploring the impact of geographic variability on the model’s performance. Regional differences in demographics, socioeconomics, and healthcare infrastructure may necessitate tailored strategies for patient allocation, ensuring that the model is adaptable to diverse contexts. Moreover, incorporating uncertainty into the mathematical model could further enhance its applicability, providing more realistic and practical insights that align closely with the challenges faced in real-world healthcare settings.
