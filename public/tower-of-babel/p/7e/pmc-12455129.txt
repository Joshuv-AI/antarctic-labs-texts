Impact of DNA damage repair alterations on real-world response to therapy in metastatic colorectal cancer
Authors: L. Miotke, C. Norton, Z. Rubnitz, V. Florou, C. Nevala-Plagemann, I. Garrido-Laguna
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlterations in DNA damage repair (DDR) pathway genes can be exploited by cytotoxic chemotherapy regimens that induce DNA damage. Platinum chemotherapy has been shown to be particularly effective in DDR-mutated populations. However, the clinical impact of DDR mutations in metastatic colorectal cancer is still unknown.Patients and methodsIn this retrospective cohort study, we utilized a de-identified nationwide (USA-based) colorectal cancer clinicogenomic database. We included 5607 patients with metastatic colorectal cancer who received a standard fluorouracil-based, first-line chemotherapy regimen. Time to next treatment (TTNT) and overall survival (OS) were summarized with Kaplan–Meier curves and compared between groups with Cox proportional hazards models. Multivariable analysis controlled for age, sex, race, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status at diagnosis, category of first-line treatment, stage at diagnosis, KRAS status, BRAF status, mismatch repair status, carcinoembryonic antigen and albumin level.ResultsDDR alterations were identified in 31.8% (n = 1785) of patients. The most frequent were in ATM (12.4%) and BRCA2 (6.7%). There was no difference in TTNT based on DDR mutational status. Median OS was greater in patients with a DDR mutation [34.3 months versus 31.9 months, hazard ratio (HR) 0.93, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.86-0.99]. Within DDR-altered patients, there was no difference in OS based on first-line treatment received. TTNT was significantly improved with an irinotecan doublet (12.7 months versus 10.3 months, HR 0.78, 95% CI 0.69-0.87) or triplet (12.4 months versus 10.3 months, HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.54-0.91) compared with an oxaliplatin doublet.ConclusionsIn this real-world population, OS was improved in DDR-altered patients with metastatic colorectal cancer compared with DDR-wildtype patients. There was no difference in OS in DDR-mutated patients based on first-line treatment received.

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality in the United States. It is projected to affect ∼152 270 Americans and cause 52 900 deaths in 2025. The mortality rate for CRC has been declining by ∼1%-2% per year, likely due to reductions in smoking, and improved screening, surgical and medical techniques. Despite these improvements, a quarter of patients still present with metastatic disease at the time of diagnosis and this fraction is rising. Even more alarmingly, the incidence of CRC is increasing in young adults, who are more likely to present with metastatic disease compared with their older counterparts. Finally, the incidence of left-sided tumors is increasing despite improved endoscopic techniques.1 As such, it is imperative that we continue to expand our knowledge of metastatic CRC (mCRC) disease biology to help direct effective treatment in this arena.

In normally functioning cells, DNA damage can be recognized and either repaired by DNA damage repair (DDR) enzymes or these damaged cells are targeted for apoptosis. This process is regulated through a number of different cellular pathways, with some redundancy.2 In tumorigenesis, an environment of genomic instability produces a survival advantage for cancer cells that have escaped a DDR pathway that would otherwise guide cells toward apoptosis. Thus, the acquisition of alterations in DNA damage repair genes is often critical for tumor development.2 In a large-scale molecular study, comprehensive tumor profiling was carried out on samples from 9321 patients with CRC.3 This study identified 29 key DDR-related genes that are altered in 13.8% of patients. It identified a clear association between patients with microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) tumors and those with DDR mutations. Additionally, the study identified an association with POLE-mutated tumors and DDR mutations. POLE proteins play a key role in accurate DNA replication and thus tumors that harbor mutations in POLE genes have a strikingly high number of base substitution mutations.4 These findings imply that DDR mutations may arise as secondary mutations in the setting of tumors with high mutational burden, either due to POLE alterations or MSI-H environments.3

In cancer cells with loss of at least one DDR pathway, there is a greater reliance on the remaining DDR pathways. This vulnerability can be exploited with existing therapies. Currently, first-line therapy for most patients with mCRC consists of cytotoxic chemotherapy with a fluoropyrimidine [5-fluorouracil (5-FU) or capecitabine] combined with an alkylating agent such as oxaliplatin, and/or a topoisomerase inhibitor such as irinotecan. Common regimens include modified FOLFIRINOX (5-FU, leucovorin, irinotecan and oxaliplatin), FOLFOX (5-FU, leucovorin and oxaliplatin), FOLFIRI (5-FU, leucovorin and irinotecan) or CAPEOX (capecitabine and oxaliplatin) with or without a targeted antibody [antiangiogenic or anti-epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)].5 Irinotecan impedes the normal function of topoisomerase I, creating single-stranded breaks that cannot be repaired by the stalled topoisomerase I machinery. These breaks then get converted to double-stranded breaks.6 Platinum chemotherapy agents induce DNA damage by creating covalent inter- and intrastrand cross-links in the DNA.7 Indeed, mutations in the DDR pathway have conferred sensitivity to platinum agents in other cancer types, although the correlation between hypothesized mechanism and clinical outcomes is not always clear cut.8, 9, 10, 11 For example, the phase III TNT trial compared docetaxel with carboplatin in triple-negative breast cancer. There was a significantly better overall response rate and progression-free survival with carboplatin in the germline BRCA1/2-mutated subgroup. There was no difference in overall survival (OS) and there was no difference in clinical outcomes between groups when examining BRCA-methylated subgroups or BRCA messenger-RNA-low subgroups. Additionally, while patients with a germline BRCA mutation had high homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) scores, which reflects the genomic landscape that may arise from a defect in the homologous recombination pathway (HRP), a high HRD score did not correlate with better clinical outcomes in response to platinum-based regimens.12

The clinical impact of DDR alterations in CRC is poorly understood. In the previous study of 9321 patients with CRC, there was no associated clinical data such as stage, treatment received or clinical outcomes.3 In one retrospective study of 49 patients, the use of a platinum-based regimen in the first-line setting correlated with improved OS compared with an irinotecan-based regimen in the 20% of patients with a DDR mutation. No difference was found in outcomes in the DDR unmutated population.13 In a recent single institution study, somatic BRCA1/2 mutations did not correlate with a survival benefit or response to platinum therapy.14 A pair of phase II trials have evaluated the use of poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi) in mCRC; neither study, however, evaluated outcomes in a population selected for by DDR alteration.15,16 In our study, we utilized a large national database to examine DDR alterations in patients with mCRC. We hypothesized that patients with DDR alterations will exhibit a better response to platinum-based triplet chemotherapy compared with other regimens.

Methods

Data source

This study used the nationwide (USA-based) de-identified Flatiron Health-Foundation Medicine CRC clinicogenomic database (FH-FMI CGDB), which included 12 329 patients. The de-identified data originated from ∼280 United States cancer clinics, representing ∼800 sites of care. The retrospective longitudinal clinical data were derived from electronic health record (EHR) data, comprising patient-level structured and unstructured data, curated via technology-enabled abstraction, and were linked to genomic data derived from Foundation Medicine Inc (FMI) comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) tests in the FH-FMI CGDB by de-identified, deterministic matching.17 Genomic alterations were identified via CGP of >300 cancer-related genes on FMI's next-generation sequencing tests. Twenty-six mutations were chosen for inclusion based on previously reported DDR gene mutations in CRC. Mutations in the nucleotide excision repair pathway were not included as there were no mutations present in the previously reported CRC sample cohort.3 This study included data from FoundationOne® CDx, FoundationOne® or FoundationOne Liquid®, FoundationOneLiquid® CDx baitsets.18,19 The data are de-identified and subject to obligations to prevent re-identification and protect patient confidentiality. Institutional review board approval of the study protocol was obtained before the study was conducted and included a waiver of informed consent.

Study cohort

The study included 12 329 patients in the FH-FMI CGDB for mCRC from 2005 to 2022. Patients >18 years, with an invasive mCRC diagnosis by International Classification of Diseases code and pathologist-confirmed histology, with CGP testing by FMI within 30 days of their diagnosis date. Patients who did not receive a standard platinum-based doublet (FOLFOX, CAPOX), irinotecan-based doublet (FOLFIRI) or triplet (FOLFOXIRI) chemotherapy regimen with or without a targeted antibody (anti-angiogenic or anti-EGFR) were excluded. Chemotherapy regimens were categorized as a platinum-based doublet (FOLFOX, CAPOX with or without a targeted agent), irinotecan-based doublets (FOLFIRI or capecitabine and irinotecan with or without a targeted agent) or triplet (FOLFOXIRI with or without a targeted agent). Patients who did not have a recorded activity in the EHR within 90 days of metastatic diagnosis, had <6 months of follow-up following metastatic diagnosis, or had FMI testing >60 days after the last documented clinic note were excluded.

Statistical analysis

Differences in baseline characteristics of patients with a DDR mutation versus wildtype were compared using a chi-square test for categorical variables and two-sided Student’s t-test for continuous variables, both of which were done using the scipy (1.11.1) package in Python.20 Real-world overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from the first-line treatment start date to the date of death. Real-world time to next treatment (TTNT) was defined as the time from the first-line treatment start date to the start of next line therapy date or death, whichever came first. Patients were right-censored by the end date of first-line treatment if they had no second-line therapy and no recorded death date. OS and TTNT were reported with Kaplan–Meier curves and compared between groups with a multivariable log-rank test, both using the lifelines (0.27.8) package in Python.21 These were also compared utilizing a univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards model using the survival (3.5-8) package in R.22 The multivariate proportional hazard model included age, sex, race, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status at diagnosis, category of first-line treatment, stage of CRC at diagnosis, KRAS status, BRAF status, mismatch repair (MMR) status, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) within 30 days of diagnosis, albumin within 30 days at diagnosis and DDR status. Missing covariable values were imputed using a multiple imputation by chained equation method, using the mice (3.16.0)23 and Hmisc (5.1-2)24 packages in R. Figures were generated using the Matplotlib (3.1.7)25 and Seaborn (0.12.2)26 packages in Python.

Results

Patient characteristics

Of the 12 329 patients in the colorectal clinic-genomic database, 9530 received treatment of mCRC within the study period. A total of 1490 patients were then excluded for receiving a clinical trial drug or a nonstandard first-line therapy. A total of 115 patients were excluded for having DDR status testing 60 days after their last clinic note (Figure 1). When stratified by DDR status, there was no significant difference in sex, race, ECOG performance status at metastatic diagnosis, first-line therapy received, stage at diagnosis, KRAS mutation status, and CEA levels and decreased albumin at metastatic diagnosis. DDR mutational profile

Of the 5607 patients who met the inclusion/exclusion criteria, DDR gene alterations were identified in 1785 (31.8%). DDR mutations were categorized by pathway: homologous recombination pathway (HRP), DNA damage checkpoint pathway (CP), Fanconi anemia, or nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ) (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105768). There were 323 patients (18.1%) with a mutation in more than one DDR pathway gene. These patients were categorized as ‘complex’. The most frequent DDR pathway mutations were in ATM (12.4%) and BRCA2 (6.7%).

Survival analysis

There was no statistically significant difference in TTNT based on DDR mutational status [11.1 months in DDR wildtype versus 11.0 months in DDR mutated; hazard ratio (HR) 1.03, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.97-1.09, P = 0.39). This was also true in the multivariable Cox proportional hazard analysis (HR 1.01, 95% CI 0.95-1.08, P = 0.69). In the DDR-wildtype group, 2839 (75%) went on to receive second-line treatment of any kind, including 1194 patients who received a standard irinotecan doublet, 319 patients who received a standard oxaliplatin doublet and 35 patients who received a triplet therapy. In the DDR-mutated population, 1339 (75%) patients went on to receive second-line treatment of any kind, including 520 patients who received a standard irinotecan doublet, 150 patients who received a standard oxaliplatin doublet and 12 patients who received a triplet therapy. In the DDR-mutated population, the median overall survival (mOS) was significantly greater than in the DDR-wildtype population (34.3 months versus 31.9 months, HR 0.93, 95% CI 0.86-0.99, P = 0.04). When controlled for age, sex, race, ECOG performance status at diagnosis, category of first-line treatment, stage of CRC at diagnosis, KRAS status, BRAF status, MMR status, CEA and albumin within 30 days of diagnosis, there was an 8% decreased risk of death in the DDR-mutated group (HR 0.92, 95% CI 0.86-0.99, P = 0.03) (Figure 2, Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105768). A total of 2302 patients (60%) in the DDR-wildtype group had a recorded death date compared with 1098 (61.5%) in the DDR-mutated group. Additionally, we compared the effect of sequence of oxaliplatin-containing therapies and irinotecan-containing therapies on OS in DDR-mutated patients. Of the 1785 DDR-mutated patients who received either a standard oxaliplatin doublet or irinotecan doublet in the first line, 539 (30%) went on to receive a standard oxaliplatin or irinotecan doublet in the second line. Of these patients, 468 patients (87%) received an oxaliplatin doublet in the first line followed by an irinotecan doublet, and 71 patients (13%) received an irinotecan doublet in the first line followed by an oxaliplatin doublet. There was no detected difference in mOS between patients who received any standard oxaliplatin-based doublet therapy in the first line followed by a standard irinotecan-based doublet in the second line compared with patients who received the reverse sequence (31 months versus 29.7 months, HR 1.01, 95% CI 0.76-1.35, P = 0.95) (Figure 4, Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105768).

In an exploratory subgroup analysis, we broke down the DDR-altered patients by which DDR pathway was altered. Patients with a CP pathway alteration seemed to have a better TTNT and OS with irinotecan doublet. Patients without a DDR mutation also had a greater mOS with an oxaliplatin doublet (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105768).

Mutational landscape in DDR-mutated patients

We evaluated the 10 most commonly co-mutated genes in DDR-mutated patients. The most commonly co-mutated gene was APC, occurring in 80% of patients with a DDR mutation. There were significantly more mutations in SOX9 and BRAF in the DDR-mutant patients compared with the DDR-wildtype patients, while there were significantly more mutations in TP53 in DDR-wildtype patients compared with DDR-mutant patients (Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105768). There were significantly more MMR-deficient patients in the DDR-mutated cohort compared with the wildtype cohort (6.7% compared with 0.5%, P < 0.001). In fact, of the 138 patients who were MMR deficient in this cohort, 119 (86%) harbored a DDR mutation. There were also significantly more patients with a POLE mutation in the DDR-mutated cohort compared with the wildtype cohort (9.4% compared with 4.0%, P < 0.001) (Table 1). Of the 319 patients who had a POLE mutation in this cohort, 168 (53%) were DDR mutated.

Discussion

The predictive and prognostic implications of DDR mutations in CRC are not clearly understood. It is hypothesized that mutations in these pathways will portend a better response to platinum-based chemotherapy, PARPi and other therapies targeting the DDR pathway (i.e. ataxia-telangiectasia mutated (ATM), Rad-3 related (ATR) inhibitors). However, this has yet to be clearly demonstrated in clinical studies. Prior studies have evaluated the frequency of DDR alterations in mCRC.3 To the best of our knowledge, this study is the largest real-world study to evaluate the impact of these alterations in treatment outcomes in patients with mCRC.

We found that DDR mutations were present in a third of patients with mCRC, which was more frequent than previously reported. This perhaps reflects differences in the USA-based population, compared with the international population studied previously.3,27 As in previous studies, our data support a correlation between DDR mutations and POLE mutations, MMR deficiency, as well as RAF mutations.

In terms of survival outcomes, we did not detect a difference in TTNT based on DDR mutational status; however, there was a slight improvement in OS in the DDR-mutated group. We, and others, have shown that there is a higher incidence of DDR mutations in patients with deficient MMR CRC. One hypothesis for this survival difference is that the improved survival of DDR-mutated patients is related to response to checkpoint inhibitor therapy in later lines.3 In one study of 271 MSI-stable patients, there was no difference detected in OS based on DDR mutation.28 In our data, however, this statistically significant difference in OS was maintained even when deficient MMR patients were excluded (Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105768). An alternative hypothesis is that DDR mutations are a marker of a different disease biology, which portend better prognosis. Supporting this hypothesis are recent studies showing better prognosis in mCRC and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma patients with ATM mutations.29,30

There was a difference of ∼2-months in TTNT for patients with a DDR mutation who received either an irinotecan-containing doublet or a triplet therapy compared with those who received an oxaliplatin-containing doublet in the first line. However, there was no difference in OS in DDR-mutated patients when stratified by first-line agent or when stratified by irinotecan-to-oxaliplatin sequence versus the reverse. There was also no difference in OS between patients who received an oxaliplatin doublet or a triplet therapy in the first line. Given this, the clinical significance of the differences detected in TTNT based on first-line therapy is unclear. As survival outcomes were similar for patients who received both irinotecan and oxaliplatin-based regimens, our data support choosing a first-line therapy based on clinical features unique to the patient, as opposed to DDR mutation status.

When we examined response to first-line therapy within each specific altered DDR pathway there was variation in response. Patients with mutations in the NHEJ pathway and those with multiple DDR mutations (complex) had a nonstatistically significant trend toward favoring an irinotecan doublet for both TTNT and OS. These findings highlight the need for further characterization of the DDR mutations in CRC and the specific alterations that predict response to therapy.

This study had a few limitations. This paper included 26 genetic alterations in various DDR pathways as previously studied.3 However, this is not an exhaustive list and other alterations have been reported in DDR pathways in CRC.31 This study should likely be repeated in the future with current and emerging targets. As a retrospective study, another limitation was the potential for confounding variables not controlled for by the multivariable regression analysis. For example, information on the location of the primary tumor (i.e. left- or right-sidedness) is not a variable included in this dataset and may have contributed as a confounder. Our dataset also did not include data on which neoadjuvant or adjuvant treatments patients received, which may have affected response to first-line treatment and/or OS. Additionally, while we attempted to address missingness with a multiple imputation strategy, this may have introduced bias.

In our large retrospective study, we were not able to predict response to first-line chemotherapy based on DDR status. However, DDR alterations seem to portend better prognosis in mCRC regardless of therapy. Before DDR mutations can be targeted effectively in CRC, more research is needed to identify which genomic or clinical markers will predict response to therapy and improved outcomes.
