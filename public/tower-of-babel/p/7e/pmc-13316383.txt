Point-of-care diagnostics are central to acute medicine delivery
Authors: Catherine Atkin, Murali Veerabahu, Daniel Lasserson, Sarb Clare
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Point-of-care diagnostics deliver rapid, actionable results to aid in clinical decision making for patients with acute illness. Ranging from in vitro diagnostics, such as high-sensitivity troponin, to point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS), incorporating point-of-care diagnostics into clinical care offers accelerated decision making and streamlined assessment pathways. Point-of-care testing has been embraced by acute medicine services, where benefits are demonstrated across the whole range of patient acuity, from resuscitation to same-day emergency care. Use extends beyond the hospital setting, with point-of-care diagnostics used in pre-hospital assessment and hospital at home services, helping patients to avoid hospital admission, benefitting patients and hospital services. Ongoing innovations in point-of-care diagnostics are reshaping acute care delivery, with acute medicine at the forefront of embedding these new technologies into routine practice.

Introduction

Urgent and emergency care (UEC) in the NHS is under severe strain, with elevated emergency department (ED) attendances, and significant delays within UEC services. In 2024–25, fewer than 75% of patients were discharged or transferred within 4 hours of arrival to the ED, well below the 95% standard,1 and almost 1.9 million people waited over 12 hours in the ED before transfer or discharge.1 12-hour waits were estimated to contribute to over 23,000 ʻexcess’ deaths in England in 2022.2 Tackling delays requires strategic improvements across multiple areas; this includes streamlining UEC pathways by expediting diagnostic processes and decision making.

Point-of-care (POC) diagnostics are performed close to the patient, delivering rapid results without the need for central processing. They span a wide spectrum, from in vitro diagnostics such as bedside blood gas analysis and rapid biochemical or infectious disease assays, to portable imaging such as point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS). By providing timely, clinically actionable information, point-of-care tests (POCTs) accelerate decision making, streamline acute pathways, and improve both the safety and efficiency of patient care.

Acute internal medicine (AIM) is at the forefront of this diagnostic revolution. Acute physicians lead the assessment, diagnosis and early management of adults presenting with urgent or undifferentiated medical problems. AIM manages the majority of medical emergency admissions, across settings that include acute medical units (AMUs), ambulatory services including same-day emergency care (SDEC) and, increasingly, acute ʻhospital at home’ services across the UK. Incorporating POCT into investigation and treatment pathways across these settings may help to optimise patient flow while maintaining high-quality care.3, 4

Acute medicine and point-of-care diagnostics

AMUs and SDEC services, led by AIM, sit at critical junctions in the UEC pathway, where early decisions must be made under significant time pressure. The acute physician’s skillset of managing broad differential diagnoses, dealing with high uncertainty, and making quick yet safe decisions can leverage the rapid results from near-patient diagnostics into UEC system benefits.

Point-of-care testing and bedside imaging offer rapid, actionable data supporting ʻrule-in’ and ʻrule-out’ pathways, contributing to patient flow and safety by reducing avoidable admissions and preventing delays in critical interventions. Although initial research focused on test accuracy,5 recent data suggest that POCTs in UEC settings can shorten time to intervention and improve disposition times.6, 7, 8, 9 Integrating POCTs into established workflows, combined with early assessment, may significantly shorten turnaround times and enhance care quality.10 Similarly, POCUS allows immediate clarification of various conditions, bypassing delays inherent in formal radiology pathways. Point-of-care testing: in vitro diagnostics

POCTs include a wide range of in vitro diagnostics that would otherwise require laboratory-based analysis, ranging from basic tests that have long been used in routine clinical practice to more complex investigations.

For some conditions, POCTs are already a cornerstone of patient care. In patients with diabetes, capillary blood glucose testing plays a vital role in both inpatient management and ongoing self-management in the community. Emergencies such as diabetic ketoacidosis demonstrate how POCTs have long been embedded in standard care for acutely unwell medical patients, with ketones monitored through capillary POCT, and rapid assessment of pH and electrolytes provided through POC blood gas analysers. The benefits of rapid POCTs are illustrated by the inclusion of blood gas analysis central to the initial management of several medical emergencies, including assessment of lactate in suspected sepsis, and pH and carbon dioxide in type 2 respiratory failure.

In AMUs, POCT for respiratory viruses enables faster isolation measures and more appropriate antiviral use, and significantly shortens length of stay.11 The COVID-19 pandemic forced rapid adoption of point-of-care testing and imaging, alongside broader service development led by AIM, where POCT played a critical role in swiftly diagnosing SARS-CoV-2 infection, reducing transmission, and guiding admission and treatment decisions.12, 13 In suspected bacterial infection, POCT can help to rationalise antibiotic use through rapid evaluation of markers such as C-reactive protein, which has shown utility in assessment of respiratory infections in primary care.14

In low acuity settings, POCTs can accelerate diagnostic pathways for specific conditions, facilitating patient assessment through SDEC or avoiding attendance completely. Chest pain is the most common cause of medical attendance,15 with acute coronary syndromes (ACS) excluded through high-sensitivity troponin testing strategies in SDEC.16 Faster results via POCT could reduce time to discharge;17 however, this approach has not yet been widely evaluated or adopted, and only one POC assay is currently approved by NICE.18 Pre-hospital POC troponin in low-risk patients with suspected non-ST elevation ACS can reduce healthcare resource use, largely by avoiding ED attendances.19 NICE guidelines for suspected venous thromboembolism support the use of POCT for d-dimer, reducing turnaround times for test results in SDEC, therefore reducing time to discharge;20 adoption in primary care could remove the need for hospital attendance in low-risk patients.21

POCUS: acute physicians at the forefront of bedside imaging

POCUS has expanded rapidly over the past two decades, with use accelerated further during the COVID-19 pandemic.12 Advances in handheld technology have made portable devices more affordable, accessible and capable of producing high-quality images. POCUS is built around focused binary clinical questions that complement traditional history taking, physical examination and preliminary investigations.

POCUS consistently demonstrates superior diagnostic performance compared with clinical examination and chest radiography, particularly in patients presenting with acute dyspnoea or haemodynamic instability. For example, lung ultrasound was shown to have higher sensitivity than chest X-ray (CXR) (92.5% versus 63.6%) for detecting pulmonary oedema among patients presenting with shortness of breath, offering more reliable bedside assessment.22 Meta-analyses and systematic reviews further support its broad diagnostic accuracy across conditions including pleural effusion, small bowel obstruction and cholecystitis.23, 24

These diagnostic advantages could translate into meaningful improvements in patient outcomes. It has been suggested that incorporating early POCUS into the clinical assessment of patients with chest pain or dyspnoea may reduce length of stay and improve 30-day survival.25 In critically unwell patients, POCUS-guided resuscitation protocols for shock may be associated with reduced 28-day mortality and shorter duration of vasoactive therapy.26 By reducing diagnostic uncertainty, enabling earlier targeted treatment and avoiding delays associated with formal imaging, POCUS supports faster decision making, shorter hospital stays and better patient outcomes.

For acute physicians managing high-acuity, undifferentiated patients daily, POCUS is an essential skill. Growing frontline experience, coupled with an expanding evidence base, led to its formal inclusion in the 2022 AIM curriculum, making POCUS competency a requirement prior to completion of AIM training.3 Acute physicians now use POCUS across a wide range of clinical scenarios, from traditional shock protocols and procedural guidance to emerging applications such as papilloedema,27 suspected giant cell arteritis28 and hepatobiliary pathology,29 reflecting the versatility of the modality. Many acute physicians extend these POCUS skills to complete training in bedside echocardiography, facilitating targeted evaluation of cardiac function.

Case

A 53-year-old man with a background of hypertension presented to the ED with chest pain that woke him at 4am, described as central heaviness radiating to the neck; his main concern was a right-sided headache. He was referred to medical SDEC as possible cardiac chest pain after initial ED nurse triage.

Observations were stable. ECG showed left axis deviation and first-degree heart block. CXR was normal with a normal mediastinum. Serial troponins taken 4 h apart were within normal limits. The unifying diagnosis was ascending aortic dissection secondary to an underlying bicuspid aortic valve. The patient was transferred to a cardiothoracic centre for urgent aortic valve and root repair and made an uneventful recovery.

Learning points

Atypical presentations, normal initial investigations and negative cardiac biomarkers can mask life-threatening pathology. Here, careful physical examination identified a murmur, prompting focused echocardiography and resulting in a life-saving diagnosis. The patient’s headache was attributable to cerebral hypoperfusion, a recognised manifestation of aortic dissection. Crucially, the availability of an appropriately skilled acute physician and immediate ultrasound enabled timely diagnosis and safe transfer for definitive management. This case demonstrates the value that physicians trained in this skill bring to the assessment of acute medical patients.

Beyond the bedside: community applications

While in vitro diagnostics and POCUS may be more familiar in an acute hospital setting, the benefits stretch beyond hospital services, and will help deliver the vision for integrated and community services described in the NHS 10 Year Plan.30

Scoping reviews in pre-hospital and ambulance settings highlight the potential for POCT to accelerate decision making and more effectively triage patients before they reach hospital.5 This can improve pressures on UEC services by ensuring that patients are directed to the most appropriate assessment location or, in some cases, avoid hospital altogether. Alongside benefits to the UEC system, POCT can benefit individual patients, not just by improving care pathways, but by facilitating treatment in their own home. The use of POCT in urgent community response, virtual ward and ‘hospital at home’ services is recommended by national guidance,31 and can include both in vitro diagnostics and POCUS.

Handheld blood analysers can deliver rapid evaluation of electrolytes, urea and creatinine in the patient’s home, supporting shared decision making on whether hospital admission can be avoided through home-based treatment.6 As discussed above, POCT for troponin, d-dimer or inflammatory markers in selected cohorts of patients can avoid the need for further investigations that would necessitate hospital attendance. Near-patient in vitro diagnostics can enable service development distant to pathology laboratories, such as those discussed in the NHS 10 Year Plan.30

The use of POCUS aids decision making and diagnostic confidence to enable management at home; the supporting technology allows remote review of imaging, facilitating specialist input or multidisciplinary team discussion as required. In a health system with increasing digital maturity, investigations and results obtained outside the hospital setting should be available to the patient’s team, whether this is based in primary or secondary care. This requires the technology able to deliver this, but also the necessary governance oversight. For virtual ward or hospital at home services, responsibility for this varies, with AIM taking responsibility in many centres.

Governance and safety considerations

Effective implementation can only be delivered with appropriate governance processes. Confidence in the results of POCT is essential for implementation into routine clinical practice. This necessitates ongoing robust processes for standardisation, calibration and quality assurance, alongside evidence of diagnostic accuracy. Multidisciplinary oversight is required to ensure reliability of results and effective incorporation into clinical workflows, extending beyond the clinical teams using the POCT. This includes pathology and radiology,31 although current practice varies, as well as the IT infrastructure necessary to store and record results within the patient record.6

In vitro diagnostics require evidence of suitable sensitivity and specificity;18 in day-to-day use, quality assurance mechanisms, including equipment maintenance, must be in place to ensure that this accuracy is maintained.

Specific risks need to be considered for POCUS. Inter-operator variability may affect reliability of results; structured training frameworks, such as focused acute medicine ultrasound (FAMUS), competencies and their inclusion in the AIM curriculum aim to reduce this risk.3 However, delivery of these training frameworks requires access to trainers, equipment and dedicated time, which may vary regionally;32 universal adoption will take time. Systems must ensure that images can be appropriately and securely documented and archived, for example by upload into digital radiology systems, with process available to obtain a second opinion of obtained images.33 Adoption of new POCT, whether imaging or pathology based, requires integration of new devices and compatibility of electronic systems. Without appropriate consideration of interoperability and sustainability, the possible improvements offered by POCT may not be realised.

Future directions and limitations

The inclusion of POCT in national UEC strategy recognises the potential benefit that POCT offers. With ongoing developments in the use of POCT, this potential continues to expand, underpinned by increasing evidence demonstrating the benefits of currently available POCT, and in ongoing developments in the technology underpinning these diagnostics. In POCUS, miniaturisation of technology enables high-quality imaging from handheld devices, facilitating use in a wider range of settings. Artificial intelligence may have a role in POCUS, suggested for aiding image interpretation, but also quality assurance, for example by advising users on technical skills and image quality.34

Realising the benefits of POCT in routine clinical practice has multiple challenges, such as financial cost, training requirements, and changes to current workflows and processes; this requires ongoing engagement from clinical teams. Widespread systems change must be supported by appropriate development within both services and individuals, such as the national recommendations for POCUS training, and guideline recommendations for POCT.3, 20, 31 The potential impact of POCT varies between hospitals and services, depending on organisational factors, such as the number of patients requiring POCT and the size of current delays associated with central laboratory and radiology testing.10

The broader evidence required for national implementation, such as larger pragmatic clinical trials understanding of organisational factors, economic evaluation and assessments of acceptability, lags behind the evidence of diagnostic accuracy.5 Implementation must consider availability of improved diagnostic pathways irrespective of time of arrival or route of access, with potential implications for workforce capabilities and training, and availability of resources. Further research is needed to ensure deliverability across settings and patient populations, with AIM well situated to drive both development of this evidence, and subsequent safe, equitable, evidence-based adoption.

Conclusion

Point-of-care diagnostics improve pathways across the breadth of patient acuity, from excluding disease in low-risk SDEC patients, to guiding resuscitation in acute deterioration. Collectively, the ongoing innovations in POCT are reshaping acute care delivery, with acute medicine at the forefront of embedding these new technologies into routine practice. At a time of intense systemic pressure, the rapid answers provided by POCT are more vital than ever to enable the wide variety of undifferentiated patients managed by acute medicine to be triaged, stabilised and diagnosed through safe and efficient pathways across a range of settings.
