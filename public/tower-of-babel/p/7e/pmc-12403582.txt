BACH1 recruits STAT3 to enhance leukemia inhibitory factor receptor activity and augments the self-renewal capacity of mouse embryonic stem cells
Authors: Jinghua Ma, Siyu Ma, Cong Niu, Siqing Wang, Xiangxiang Wei, Dan Meng, Xiuling Zhi, Jieyu Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGenomic studies have linked single nucleotide variants in the enhancer region of the leukemia inhibitory factor receptor (Lifr) gene to chromatin accessibility and the regulation of self-renewal in mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs). However, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. This study investigates the role of the transcription factor BTB and CNC homology 1 (BACH1) in regulating the Lifr enhancer and its impact on mESC pluripotency.MethodsWe performed RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) to assess the impact of Bach1 knockout on gene expression in mESCs. Background

Embryonic stem cells (ESCs) are characterized by their ability to self-renew and differentiate into multiple cell lineages [1]. These cells are derived from the inner cell mass (ICM) of blastocysts—human ESCs (hESCs) from human blastocysts and mouse ESCs (mESCs) from mouse pre-implantation blastocysts [2]. The regulation of ESCs’ self-renewal is governed by a multitude of factors, including signaling pathways, transcription factors, epigenetic regulators, cytokines, and small molecular compounds [3]. Core transcription factors such as SRY-box transcription factor 2 (SOX2), octamer-binding transcription factor 4 (OCT4), and nanog homeobox (NANOG) are essential for maintaining pluripotency in both hESCs and mESCs. These transcription factors play a pivotal role in sustaining the ground state of ESCs [4, 5]. However, distinct signaling pathways regulate the pluripotency state and self-renewal in both mESCs and hESCs. Specifically, hESCs require fibroblast growth factor/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (FGF/ERK) and Activin/Nodal signaling for the maintenance of self-renewal and pluripotency [6]. Unlike hESCs, the self-renewal of mESCs is dependent on signaling from leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF) and bone morphogenetic protein-4 (BMP4) [7]. However, it is still little known how the pluripotency signaling is fine-tuned.

LIF, a critical member of the interleukin-6 (IL-6) cytokine family, stimulates downstream signaling pathways through binding to its heterodimeric receptor comprising leukemia inhibitory factor receptor (LIFR) and glycoprotein 130 (gp130) [8, 9]. As a transmembrane receptor, LIFR is implicated in cancer progression, angiogenesis, stem cell fate determination, and embryonic development [10]. Upon binding of LIF, the LIFR/gp130 receptor complex enhances the kinase activity of Janus kinases (JAK), leading to the phosphorylation of transcription factor signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3). Phosphorylated STAT3 subsequently forms homodimers, translocates into the nucleus, and initiates the transcription of target genes [11, 12]. LIFR is also crucial for maintaining the pluripotency of mESCs. Prior studies have elucidated that the LIFR/STAT3 pathway directly modulates the expression of krüppel-like factor 4 (KLF4), which in turn promotes the transcription of SOX2 in mESCs [13]. Under hypoxic conditions, mESCs lose their self-renewal capacity and initiate differentiation due to the down-regulation of the LIFR-STAT3 pathway mediated by hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) [14]. However, the precise regulatory mechanism of LIFR in mESCs remains unclear. Recent research has identified a single enhancer variant of Lifr that influences chromatin accessibility and pluripotency in mESCs [15], yet how transcription factors regulate the enhancer activity of Lifr remains to be fully elucidated.

The transcription factor BTB and CNC homology 1 (BACH1) is ubiquitously expressed across mammalian tissues [16]. Structurally, BACH1 contains an N-terminal BTB domain that mediates protein interactions and a C-terminal bZIP domain that facilitates DNA binding and heterodimerization with small Maf proteins [17]. Functionally, BACH1 has been implicated in multiple physiological and pathological processes, including oxidative stress [18], cancer metastasis [19, 20], and heme homeostasis [21]. Previous studies have demonstrated that global Bach1 knockout (Bach1-KO) leads to embryonic sub-lethality in mice, underscoring its essential role in development [22]. Furthermore, conditional knockout of Bach1 specifically in microglial cells results in aberrant neuronal differentiation and anxiety-like behaviors, highlighting its cell-type-specific functions in the development of the central nervous system (CNS) [23]. Bach1 knockout fetal mice display increased lung fragility compared to wild-type littermates [24]. Our previous studies demonstrated that BACH1 also plays an important role in the cardiovascular system, particularly in regulating angiogenesis [25], atherosclerosis progression [26], and vascular remodeling [27]. Additionally, BACH1 is crucial for maintaining embryonic stem cell pluripotency and cell fate determination, consistent with its high expression in the inner cell mass of E4.5 blastocyst. Our research has demonstrated that BACH1 interacts with key pluripotency factors NANOG, SOX2, and OCT4 in ESCs and recruits ubiquitin-specific peptidase 7 (USP7) to stabilize NANOG [28]. Moreover, BACH1 can form a novel transcriptional complex that bridges master pluripotency regulators and mixed lineage leukemia/SET domain1 (MLL/SET1) family proteins at (super-) enhancers and promoters, thereby modulating the stem cell pluripotency network [29]. Despite these advances, the role of BACH1 in fine-tuning pluripotency signaling pathways has yet to be elucidated.

Here, we investigate the role of BACH1 in regulating Lifr enhancer activity and expression, and delineate the molecular mechanism by which this pathway maintains self-renewal in mESCs.

Methods and materials

Cell culture

mESCs (E14Tg2A line), generously provided by Dr. Fei Lan, were maintained in a culture medium consisting of 90% Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (#12800082, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (#10099-141, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA), 1% non-essential amino acids (NEAA) (#2169083, Gibco-Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA), 0.1 mM β-mercaptoethanol (#444203, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and 200 Units/ml murine leukemia inhibitory factor (mLIF) (#2739211, ESGRO, Merck-Millipore, Germany). mESCs were expanded in gelatin-coated flasks (SORFA, China). Bach1-KO mESCs and DoxBach1 mESCs were generated as previously described [29].

Cell clustering of scRNA-seq data

The analysis involved a published single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset comprising 133 cells from embryos (GEO: GSE45719) [30]. The Seurat package (Version 3.2) [31] was employed for the analysis of cellular and molecular components of vasculatures. Initially, the integrated objects underwent filtering, scaling (10,000 for mouse), and normalization. Subsequently, 2,000 highly variable genes were identified as inputs for principal component analysis. The significant principal components were then utilized to cluster cells at a resolution of 0.5.

Quantitative real-time PCR and RNA-sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, and cDNA synthesis was carried out using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Kit (#FSQ-101, TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan). Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) was conducted using the qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (#11201ES03, Yeason, Shanghai, China). The thermal cycling profile involved initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 58 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. A final extension step was performed at 72 °C for 7 min. Melting curves were generated by gradually increasing the temperature from 55 °C to 95 °C in 0.5 °C increments at 10-second intervals, visually ensuring the presence of a single peak for each primer. Threshold amplification cycle (Ct) values were determined using the CFX Manager analysis software (Bio-Rad). The primers used in RT-qPCR assays are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

For genome-wide gene expression analyses, RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) was employed. Non-ribosomal RNA was isolated from 1 µg total RNA using the TrueLib Poly (A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (Excell Bio, Shanghai, China), and sequencing libraries were prepared with the TrueLib mRNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (ExCell Bio, Shanghai, China). Sequencing was conducted on the NovaSeq 6000 and Hiseq XTen sequencer (Illumina Inc, San Diego, CA, USA) by Annoroad Company (Beijing, China) and Basepair Company (Suzhou, China).

Analysis of RNA-sequencing data

The FASTQ data underwent adaptor trimming using Trimmomatic (https://github.com/usadellab/Trimmomatic), followed by alignment to the mouse genome mm10 using HISAT2 [32]. Subsequently, assembly was performed with Cufflinks [33] to obtain fragments per kilobase million (FPKM) values for each gene. The FPKM values for each gene were reported in Supplementary Table S1. Differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 [34] with criteria of log fold change > 1.3 and P < 0.05. Gene Ontology analysis [35] and KEGG Pathway analysis [36] were carried out using David (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) [37]. GSEA analysis was carried out using GSEA v4.3.2 (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp) [38].

Immunoprecipitation (IP) and western blotting

Cells were lysed using lysis buffer. For immunoprecipitation, cell lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the specified antibodies and protein A/G plus agarose (#sc-2003, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA). Following four washes with PBS, protein SDS loading buffer was added and boiled for 5 min. The proteins were separated on SDS-PAGE gels, transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes, and probed with their corresponding antibodies. Subsequently, blots were treated with secondary antibodies, developed with ECL (#180–5001, Tanon Science & Technology Ltd, Shanghai, China), and signals were detected through chemiluminescence using a Tanon-5500 Imaging System (Tanon Science & Technology Ltd, Shanghai, China). The intensity of the bands was quantified through densitometric analysis with ImageJ software.

Antibodies to Phospho-STAT3 (#AP0705, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), STAT3 (#12640S, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), SOX2 (#23064S, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), OCT-4 A (#83932S, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), Phospho-JAK2-Y1007/1008 (#AP0531, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), JAK2 (#A19629, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), BACH1 (#sc-271211, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA), β-ACTIN (#20536-1-AP, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), LIFR (#sc-515337, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA), Histone 3 (#4620S, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), β-Tubulin (# T0023, Affinity, Jiangsu, China) were used.

GST pull down

GST-fused proteins and His-fused proteins were expressed in BL-21 Escherichia coli (# MC013, Ncmbio, Suzhou, China). His-fused proteins were purified using a His-tag protein purification kit (#P2226, Beyotime, Shanghai, China), while GST-fused proteins were purified using glutathione-Sepharose 4B beads (#L00895-2, GenScript, Shanghai, China). The proteins were incubated with glutathione-Sepharose 4B beads for 2 h at 4 °C. The beads were washed three times and resuspended in 40 µl of SDS loading buffer. SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis was performed, and proteins were detected using their respective antibodies. GST (#66001, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL) and His (#66005, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL) antibodies were used for this assay. Each experiment was repeated three independent times.

RNA interference

mESCs were transfected with Lifr and Stat3 siRNAs or control siRNAs using Lipofectamine 3000, following the provided instructions. The siRNA sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and ChIP-seq

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and ChIP-seq were conducted following established procedures [39]. Initially, cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min and quenched with 0.125 M glycine for 10 min. Subsequently, cell pellets were resuspended in low salt lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES/KOH, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton-X 100, 0.05% SDS, 10 mM EDTA, and proteinase inhibitors) and sonicated using a Q800R2 DNA shearing sonicator (Qsonica LLC, Newtown, CT) for 15 min in 30-second intervals, resulting in chromatin fragments of 200- to 400-bp lengths.

Chromatin was immunoprecipitated with the specified antibodies and Protein A/G PLUS-Agarose. For the BACH1 ChIP procedure, WT and Bach1-KO mESC were maintained in a culture medium for 2 days. Afterward, the cells underwent standard procedures for cross-linking, quenching, and washing. Cell nuclei were isolated before sonication. The resulting sonicated chromatin was subsequently incubated with anti-BACH1 overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, beads were washed with low salt lysis buffer and low salt buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 250 mM LiCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP40, 0.5% Na-Deoxycholate), and DNA was eluted in elution buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 10 mM EDTA, 1% SDS) with Proteinase K (#10401ES60, Yeasen, Shanghai, China) and RNase A (#EN0531, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA) at 65 °C for 4 h. The primers used in ChIP-qPCR assays are listed in Supplementary Table 4.

Analysis of ChIP-sequencing data

The FASTQ data underwent adaptor trimming using Trimmomatic (https://github.com/usadellab/Trimmomatic), followed by mapping to the mouse genome mm10 using Bowtie2 [40] in pair-end mode. Peaks were called using the MACS2 call peak module [27]. ChIP-seq motif analysis, as well as ChIP-seq visualization, were carried out using HOMER [41] and deepTools [42], and the results were visualized on the WashU Epigenome browser (http://epigenomegateway.wustl.edu/browser/). Heatmaps and signal plots were generated using deepTools (version 3.3.0).

Luciferase assay

The luciferase assay was conducted following established procedures [25]. In brief, WT mESCs or DoxBach1 mESCs were transfected with a β-galactosidase plasmid and the designated DNA reporter plasmid, the pGL3-Lifr-WT luciferase reporter plasmid or the pGL3-Lifr-mutant luciferase reporter plasmid. Transfection was carried out using Lipofectamine 3000, and the transfected cells were incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, mESCs were harvested, and luciferase activity was measured using a Luciferase Assay Kit (#E1500, Promega, Madison, WI). β-galactosidase (β-gal) activity was also measured. The relative luciferase activity (Luc) was calculated as the ratio of Luc/β-gal activity.

Alkaline phosphatase staining and colony assay

Pluripotent stem cells (in an undifferentiated state) exhibit significantly higher alkaline phosphatase (AP) activity than differentiated cells. After staining, a purplish-red precipitate forms in the cells, allowing for visual assessment of pluripotency. Undifferentiated stem cells display strong and uniform purplish-red staining, with clearly defined colony edges and consistent coloration. Cells that have lost pluripotency show weak staining, and the colonies appear patchy with unstained areas, indicating differentiation. The alkaline phosphatase assay was performed using an Alkaline Phosphatase Detection Kit (#APOAF-50TST, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells (1 × 104 cells/well) were cultured in 12-well plates for 2–3 days, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 1–2 min, and then washed with TBST solution (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 0.15 M NaCl, 0.05% Tween 20). A mixture of FRV-Alkaline solution (30 µl) and sodium nitrite solution (30 µl) was prepared for 2 min at room temperature. It was then combined with naphthol AS-BI Alkaline solution (30 µl) in water (1350 µl). Subsequently, the resulting mixture (1 ml/well) was added to the cells, and the cells were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 15–30 min. Afterward, cells were rinsed with TBST and covered with PBS. Colonies were categorized and counted based on differentiation status under a microscope: undifferentiated (AP-positive, exhibiting red staining, compact morphology, and defined margins), differentiated (AP-negative, forming loose aggregates with diffuse boundaries), and mixed populations (containing both cell types).

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as mean ± SD. Significance differences between two groups were assessed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test, while differences among three or more groups were evaluated through one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. All analyses were performed using Prism software, and a significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Higher expression levels of Bach1 and Lifr in ICM cells

The enhancer region of Lifr is related to chromatin accessibility and pluripotency in mESCs [15]. To identify potential transcription factors that regulate the enhancer activity and expression of Lifr, we employed JASPAR motif analysis and discovered that the enhancer region of Lifr contained a BACH1 binding motif (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. 1A). Representative snapshots of ChIP-seq tracks confirmed that BACH1 bound to the enhancer of Lifr near rs50454566 (Fig. 1A). To further investigate the gene expression relevance of Bach1 and Lifr, we analyzed a published single-cell RNA sequencing dataset of mouse blastocyst cells (GSE45719) [30]. Mammalian blastocysts comprise trophectoderm (TE) surrounding an inner cell mass (ICM) [43]. We identified two clusters: ICM cells and TE cells by ICM marker genes (e.g., Sox2, Oct4, Nanog, and Estrogen related receptor beta [Esrrb]) and TE marker genes (e.g., Gata binding protein 3[Gata3] and Caudal type homeobox 2 [Cdx2]) [44] (Fig. 1B and C). Notably, both Bach1 and Lifr exhibited higher expression in ICM cells compared to TE cells (Fig. 1C). These findings indicate that Bach1 and Lifr are preferentially expressed in ICM cells, suggesting that BACH1 may play a critical role in maintaining the pluripotency and differentiation of mESCs.

To determine whether BACH1 regulates the pluripotency in mESCs, we generated Bach1-KO mESCs using a CRISPR system and doxycycline-inducible Bach1 overexpressing (DoxBach1) mESCs by the piggyBac (PB) transposon system as previously described, and both modified mESCs formed colonies and were capable of self-renewal [29]. Embryoid bodies (EBs) recapitulate the lineage differentiation program of embryos in many aspects, and can serve as a powerful in vitro model to explore the potential of mESCs differentiation [45]. To further assess the capacity of Bach1-KO and DoxBach1 mESCs for differentiation into diverse cell lineages, we induced EB spontaneous differentiation by withdrawing mLIF for 2 or 4 days. RT-qPCR analysis showed that Bach1-KO mESCs and DoxBach1 mESCs exhibited up-regulated expression of Nestin (ectoderm), Brachyury (T), Mesoderm posterior bHLH transcription factor 1 (Mesp1) (mesoderm), and Paired box 2 (Pax2) (endoderm) during EB differentiation progression compared to day 0, which suggests that these mESCs possessed the ability to differentiate into all three germ layers in vitro (Fig. 1D and E). We then assessed the expression levels of pluripotency-associated genes (e.g., Sox2, Oct4) during EB differentiation progression. Compared to WT mESCs, Bach1 overexpression upregulated Sox2 and Oct4 expression, whereas Bach1 knockout decreased their expression during EB differentiation progression (Fig. 1F and G). These findings demonstrate that BACH1 partially maintains mESC self-renewal even under mLIF withdrawal conditions.

Loss of Bach1 diminishes the expression of Lifr in mESCs

To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying BACH1’s influence on mESC pluripotency and differentiation, we conducted RNA-seq analysis on both WT mESCs and Bach1-KO mESCs. Heatmap revealed a decrease in the expression of genes essential for maintaining stem cell pluripotency, such as Lifr, Sox2, KLF transcription factor 4 (Klf4), and suppressor of cytokine signaling 3 (Socs3), and an increase in genes associated with the differentiation of stem cell, including T, Mesp1 and Pax2 [46–48], in Bach1-KO mESCs compared to WT mESCs (Fig. 2A). Our additional analysis of the aforementioned RT-qPCR data demonstrated concordance with the sequencing data. Bach1-KO cells exhibited significantly elevated expression levels of multiple differentiation-related genes, whereas DoxBach1 mESCs displayed lower expression levels of these genes during EB differentiation progression. Specifically, mesodermal markers (e.g., T, Mesp1) and endodermal marker Pax2 were markedly upregulated, and the expression level of the neuroectodermal marker Nestin was modestly reduced in Bach1-KO mESCs compared to controls, whereas DoxBach1 mESCs demonstrated opposite effects during EB differentiation progression (Fig. 1D and E). These results demonstrate that compared to WT mESCs, Bach1-KO mESCs exhibit a higher potential for endodermal and mesodermal differentiation.

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology Biological Process (GO-BP) analysis indicated that down-regulated genes in Bach1-KO mESCs were linked to stem cell maintenance processes, such as signaling pathways regulating pluripotency of stem cells and stem cell population maintenance (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that genes related to response to leukemia inhibitory factor and embryonic stem cell pluripotency were upregulated in WT mESCs compared to Bach1-KO mESCs (Fig. 2C-D). Lifr is vital for stem cell population maintenance [13]. We further want to validate whether BACH1 affects Lifr expression. RT-qPCR assay confirmed that Lifr mRNA expression was significantly down-regulated in Bach1-KO mESCs than in WT mESCs (Fig. 2E). Conversely, Lifr mRNA expression was elevated in doxycycline-inducible Bach1 overexpressing mESCs compared to WT mESCs (Fig. 2F). Collectively, these results suggest that Bach1 knockout reduces the expression level of Lifr in mESCs.

BACH1 promotes the enhancer activity of Lifr and activates LIFR-STAT3 signaling in mESCs

Subsequently, we want to explore how BACH1 influences Lifr expression in mESCs. Motif analysis identified two potential BACH1 binding sites (TGCTGAGT) in the Lifr regulatory region: at positions -1021 bp to -1193 bp and -7600 bp to -7893 bp upstream of the Transcription Start Site (TSS). ChIP-qPCR demonstrated that BACH1 significantly bound to the region of -7600 bp to -7893 bp upstream of the Lifr TSS, compared to the control group, whereas no significant enrichment was observed in the range of -1021 bp to -1193 bp (Fig. 3A). To investigate the effect of BACH1 on the transcriptional activity of Lifr, we generated a luciferase reporter construct containing the Lifr enhancer region (from -7600 bp to -7893 bp upstream of the Lifr TSS), either with intact or mutated BACH1 binding sites. Bach1 overexpression significantly enhanced luciferase activity driven by the Lifr-enhancer, but had no effect on the reporter containing mutated BACH1-binding sites (Fig. 3B). This observation suggests that BACH1 promotes the transcription of Lifr by binding to its enhancer. Moreover, Bach1 knockout decreased the level of LIFR and phosphorylation levels of JAK2 and STAT3 in mESCs (Fig. 3C and D).

To investigate whether elevated concentrations of mLIF could rescue the effects of Bach1-KO in mESCs, we treated both WT and Bach1-KO mESCs with varying concentrations of mLIF. Our results demonstrated that Bach1-KO significantly weakened and delayed protein levels of LIFR and phosphorylation of STAT3 across multiple mLIF concentrations, with responses plateauing at a concentration of 1000 U mLIF (Fig. 3E and F, Supplementary Fig. 2). These results imply that BACH1 deficiency reduces LIFR expression and blunts LIFR/STAT3 signaling in mESCs under multiple mLIF concentrations.

Colocalization of BACH1 and STAT3 at gene enhancers in mESCs

To investigate molecular mechanisms of how BACH1 regulates Lifr transcription in mESCs, we employed ChIP-seq analysis in WT mESCs. Notably, the ChIP-seq binding heatmaps showed that STAT3, H3K4me1, and H3K27ac, but not H3K27me3, were highly co-occupied on BACH1 binding sites (Fig. 4A). A Venn diagram illustrated that 2661 genes were co-occupied by BACH1, STAT3, H3K4me1, and H3K27ac (Fig. 4B). Motif analysis showed that BACH1 binding sites were enriched in the motif of STAT3 (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Fig. 1B). Representative snapshots of ChIP-seq tracks showed the colocalization of BACH1 and STAT3 on the H3k27ac+ and H3K4me1+ active enhancers neighboring Lifr, Oct4, and Socs3 (Fig. 4D). co-IP assays revealed an interaction between STAT3 and BACH1 in mESCs (Fig. 4E). To determine whether this interaction was direct, we conducted a GST pull-down experiment using purified STAT3-His and BACH1-GST proteins. The results demonstrated a direct interaction between STAT3 and BACH1 (Fig. 4F). Together, these findings indicate that BACH1 and STAT3 can interact directly, and BACH1 may recruit STAT3 to genes’ enhancers in mESCs.

Then, we investigated whether BACH1 deficiency affected the recruitment of STAT3 to chromatin. ChIP-qPCR confirmed that the chromatin occupancies of STAT3 on the genes Sox2, Oct4, and Lifr were near the BACH1-binding sites, and the enrichments were lower in Bach1-KO mESCs than in WT mESCs (Fig. 4G). To determine whether BACH1 directly facilitates STAT3 binding to chromatin rather than merely reducing its phosphorylation, we manipulated two mLIF concentrations to ensure similar phosphorylation level of STAT3 in both WT and Bach1-KO mESCs. ChIP-qPCR revealed that the occupancy of STAT3 near BACH1-binding sites of pluripotency-related genes Sox2, Oct4, Lifr, and the canonical STAT3 target Myc [48] was significantly reduced in Bach1-KO mESCs, despite equivalent phosphorylation level of STAT3 (Fig. 4H). All these results demonstrate that BACH1 directly recruits STAT3 to the enhancers of Lifr, Sox2, and Oct4, thereby promoting their expression.

BACH1 maintains the self-renewal capability of mESCs through LIFR and STAT3

To determine whether BACH1-mediated enhancement of Lifr transcriptional activity depends on STAT3, we employed siRNA to knock down Stat3 expression and induced Bach1 overexpression using Dox. The luciferase assay demonstrated that BACH1 overexpression significantly enhanced the luciferase activity of the pGL3-Lifr-WT reporter construct, but this enhancement was abolished upon STAT3 knockdown (Fig. 5A). These findings indicate that BACH1-mediated activation of Lifr transcription is STAT3-dependent. We further elucidated whether STAT3 is crucial for BACH1-mediated regulation of mESC pluripotency and self-renewal. Our results demonstrated that Bach1 overexpression significantly upregulated the levels of LIFR and key pluripotency genes, including SOX2 and OCT4. Notably, these effects were partially attenuated by Stat3 knockdown (Fig. 5B and C). Collectively, these findings indicate that BACH1 maintains mESC pluripotency in a STAT3-dependent manner.

We then investigated whether BACH1 maintained pluripotency by Lifr in mESCs. By alkaline phosphatase (AP) staining, we observed that Bach1 overexpression increased the proportion of undifferentiated colonies, an effect that was partly reversed by LIFR knockdown (Fig. 5D and E). Furthermore, Bach1 overexpression significantly promoted phosphorylation of JAK2 and STAT3, as well as the expression of pluripotency genes such as SOX2 and OCT4. Consistently, these effects could be partially attenuated by Lifr knockdown, demonstrating that BACH1’s pluripotency-promoting activity is mediated, at least in part, through LIFR signaling (Fig. 5F and G). In all, these results indicate that BACH1 maintains the self-renewal capability of mESCs by recruiting STAT3 to the Lifr enhancer to enhance its expression, thereby facilitating the LIFR/STAT3 signaling pathway.

Discussion

Lifr is pivotal for sustaining self-renewal in mESCs. However, the transcriptional regulation of Lifr remains poorly understood. In this study, we observed higher expression levels of Bach1 and Lifr in ICM compared to TE cells by analyzing a published single-cell RNA sequencing dataset of mouse blastocyst cells. Our results indicate that BACH1 recruits STAT3 and binds to the enhancer region of the Lifr gene to elevate its expression, thereby facilitating the LIFR/STAT3 signaling pathway. Specifically, STAT3 is essential for BACH1-mediated activation of the Lifr gene enhancer. Both knockdown of Stat3 and Lifr partially abolished the BACH1-induced self-renewal in mESCs. Our findings underscore that the BACH1-STAT3 complex transcriptionally regulates Lifr expression to sustain mESC self-renewal.

The self-renewal of ESCs is largely dependent on the cooperation of multiple signaling pathways. Among these, the LIFR/STAT3 pathway plays a critical role in the fate determination of ESCs [37]. Heterodimerization between LIFR and gp130 is considered essential for LIFR/STAT3 signaling [49, 50]. However, the expression patterns of LIFR and gp130 are highly distinct in the mouse embryo. Lifr is predominantly expressed in ICM, whereas gp130 exhibits higher expression in extra-embryonic cells. Our previous research has demonstrated the high expression of Bach1 in E4.5 ICM cells, aligning with the expression pattern of Lifr in ICM cells. Here, we analyzed a published single-cell RNA sequencing dataset of mouse blastocyst cells and observed co-expression of Bach1 and Lifr in the ICM cells. However, whether BACH1 affects the expression of LIFR is still unclear. Previous studies have identified a significant presence of poly(C)-binding protein 1 (PCBP1) in the promoter region of LIFR, resulting in increased expression of LIFR, and sustaining self-renewal and invasiveness in breast cancer stem cells [51]. The enhancer activity of Lifr is vital for maintaining pluripotency. The non-coding single-nucleotide variant rs50454566 located in the enhancer of Lifr influences chromatin accessibility and Lifr expression in mESCs [15]. However, the regulatory mechanisms governing the enhancer activity of Lifr remain poorly understood. In this study, we discovered that BACH1 bound at the enhancer around rs50454566 of Lifr in mESCs. Furthermore, BACH1 increased the enhancer activity and expression of Lifr. While LIFR/STAT3 signaling activity is known to be regulated by mLIF concentration gradients [52], we determined that the role of BACH1 in maintaining the pluripotency of mESCs could not be compensated for by a high concentration of mLIF. This phenomenon may be explained mechanistically: the available LIFR receptors become saturated, making additional high mLIF unable to further enhance JAK-STAT3 signaling. These findings suggest that BACH1 critically regulates pluripotency by modulating the enhancer activity of Lifr in mESCs under multiple mLIF concentrations.

LIFR-STAT3 signaling pathway, mediated through JAKs, induces the nuclear translocation of STAT3, subsequently enhancing the transcription of pluripotent genes. Activation of the LIFR-STAT3 signaling pathway induces genomic hypomethylation through metabolic remodeling, thereby maintaining self-renewal in mESCs [53]. Previous research has revealed that suppression of linc1557 increases both the expression and phosphorylation levels of STAT3, thereby hindering the differentiation of mESCs [54]. However, the cofactor of STAT3 is still incompletely characterized. In this study, we identified that BACH1 directly interacted with STAT3 and recruited STAT3 to the enhancers of Lifr, Sox2, and Oct4 in mESCs. Importantly, Bach1 knockout decreased the chromatin occupancy of STAT3, even under equivalent phosphorylation level of STAT3, indicating that BACH1 also directly affects the occupancy of STAT3 rather than merely reducing its phosphorylation status. Further study needs to elucidate the mechanisms by which BACH1 regulates the expression of STAT3-targeted genes in mESCs. The transcription factor network orchestrating (super-) enhancer activity is essential for the maintenance of ESC pluripotency. STAT3 is localized in condensed puncta at signal-responsive super-enhancers in ESCs [55]. Consistent with this, our previous study revealed that BACH1 directly bridged NANOG and MLL/SET1 complexes, thereby enhancing the modification of histone H3 lysine 4 trimethylation (H3K4me3), enhancer-promoter interactions, and super-enhancer activity [29]. Thus, we speculate that BACH1 may recruit STAT3 to the super-enhancers and concentrate the transcription apparatus at highly expressed genes in mESCs.

Pluripotency signaling pathways exhibit prominent discrepancies between hESCs and mESCs. Unlike mESCs, hESCs don’t require LIF to maintain pluripotency and are phenotypically more similar to mouse EpiSCs [56]. Nevertheless, LIFR expression is elevated in naive hESCs compared to primed hESCs [57], suggesting that LIFR may play an undeniable role in promoting a naive pluripotent state in hESCs. Our Previous research has elucidated the critical role of BACH1 in maintaining stem cell identity in hESCs by stabilizing pluripotency factors through the recruitment of the deubiquitinating enzyme USP7 [28]. However, the potential role of BACH1 in promoting a naive pluripotent state in hESCs remains to be investigated.

Conclusions

In summary, our study demonstrates that BACH1 directly binds to the enhancer of Lifr and recruits STAT3 to promote LIFR expression. The upregulation of LIFR expression activates the LIFR-STAT3 pathway and enhances the expression of stem cell-related genes OCT4 and SOX2, thereby maintaining self-renewal in mESCs. Our study suggests that the regulatory role of BACH1 in the expression of LIFR is vital for mESCs’ self-renewal.
