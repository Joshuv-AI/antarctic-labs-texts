Facile fabrication of heteropolyacid/bimetallic Bi/Ce-MOFs hybrid catalyst for sustainable biodiesel synthesis
Authors: Qiuyun Zhang, Xingxin Li, Hanhong Wu, Nian Xu, Liangfu Chen, Lianlian Chen, Taoli Deng, Juan Ma, Yutao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are promising heterogeneous catalysts or catalyst support materials for biodiesel production due to their high surface area, tunable porosity, and diverse morphology. However, developing bimetallic MOFs that combine easy scalability, high stability, and good catalytic activity that are beneficial for large-scale synthesis and applications is still highly desirable. Herein, a fascinating bimetallic Bi/Ce-MOFs (Bi/Ce-BTC) supported heteropolyacid (HPMo) was constructed by a simple hydrothermal synthesis strategy, and its catalytic performance was evaluated in the esterification of oleic acid. A comprehensive characterization of the composite material’s structure, composition, and properties was performed using various techniques (XRD, FTIR, N2 physisorption, SEM-EDS, TG, NH3-TPD, Py-FTIR, and XPS). These characterization techniques thoroughly confirm the successful impregnation of HPMo on the Bi/Ce-BTC, and it possesses a relatively large specific surface area, meso-porosity, high acidity capacity, and synergistic catalytic effect between the substrates. Catalytic experiments demonstrated that HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC exhibited the best catalytic performance, achieving 92.7% conversion of oleic acid under optimal reaction conditions. Remarkably, the catalyst could be easily reused six times with an acceptable oleic acid conversion (79.3%). In addition, the kinetic analysis of the esterification reaction by HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalyst showed pseudo-first-order kinetics, with activation energies found to be 60.9 kJ/mol. Overall, the employment of bimetallic MOFs for impregnation of active species paves a potential way for broad applications in sustainable chemical processes.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13065-025-01662-4.

Introduction

Currently, the demand for energy (e.g., liquid fuel, coal, and natural gas) is on the rise due to economic expansion, population growth, and rapid global urbanization and industrialization [1]. Meanwhile, the use of fossil fuels also raises concerns about controlling the related environmental issues, such as reducing toxic and greenhouse gases (CO2) emissions [2]. The result has encouraged researchers to search for economical, eco-friendly, and renewable energy alternatives. Biofuels are being recognized as one of the reliable options to become independent from fossil fuels and to reduce CO2 emissions to enhance ecological sustainability [3]. Among the renewable energies, biodiesel, an alkyl fatty acid ester, is regarded as one of the suitable alternative bioenergy options to replace traditional diesel fuel because of its cleaner combustion characteristics of less harmful, strong versatility, high flash point, low cooling tendencies, and environmentally friendly [4–7]. Besides, using biodiesel as a bioenergy is also considered carbon neutral due to it recycling of CO2 into organic carbon in the photosynthesis process [8]. This biodiesel is normally prepared through transesterification, esterification, or both methods of plant oils, animal fats, algae, or waste oils using homogeneous/heterogeneous catalysts [9]. Among homogeneous catalysts, homogeneous base catalysts, such as NaOH, KOH, CH3ONa, offer high yield, but they require low free fatty acid (FFA) and water content to avoid saponification [10]. While homogeneous acid catalysts, such as H2SO4, HCl, H3PO4, are also used but are associated with badly corrosive, high energy consumptions, and operational costs due to purification and waste water treatment requirements [11]. More noteworthy, the recovery of the homogeneous catalysts is also difficult.

As cost-effective and environmentally friendly alternative, heterogeneous catalysts have received significant attention because of their reusability, low-cost recyclability, endure water contents, and complete conversion of FFA to biodiesel [12]. Interestingly, heterogeneous acid catalysts with desirable properties play a significant role in enhancing the esterification reaction for biodiesel production [13]. In the early days, several heterogeneous acid catalysts, such as metal oxide, zeolite, resins, ionic liquids, and carbon-derived catalysts had been employed in biodiesel production [14–16]. However, these catalysts faced challenges such as relatively high cost, limited reusability, poor stability, harsh reaction conditions, insufficient catalytic efficiency, and difficulty in degrading [17]. In this regard, developing a highly effective heterogeneous acid catalyst with suitable pore sizes, excellent thermal stability, and a well-defined crystalline structure remains of positive practical worth.

Among the studied catalysts, heteropolyacid catalysts have been widely used for the preparation of biodiesel because of their special properties, such as strong Brønsted acidity, phenomenal redox capabilities, and eco-friendly nature [18, 19]. However, pristine heteropolyacids (phosphomolybdic acid, tungstophosphoric acid, silicotungstic acid, etc.), exhibit low surface area, high solubility in polar solutions, and the susceptibility of leaching, which may importantly hinder the accessibility of the reactant molecules and recyclability [20]. Thus, how to improve the surface area and recyclability has become a major problem for heteropolyacid-based catalysts.

Encouragingly, the immobilization of heteropolyacid into suitable support materials with porous structures and frameworks has been demonstrated to overcome these limitations and achieve high catalytic activity and reusability [21]. More recently, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) exhibit exceptional properties, such as extensive surface area, low density, large pore size of the crystal lattice, and tunable chemical characteristics [22]. Among investigated MOFs, Bi-based MOFs attracted considerable research interest for catalysis due to abundant resources, cost-effectiveness and metallic clusters’ high degree of coordination [23]. More exciting, bimetallic MOFs may exhibit exceptional catalytic performance ascribable to the coordinated effect among two metal species, in contrast to monometallic MOFs [24, 25]. Qiao et al. synthesized Fe-Ni-MOF/P-C₃N₄ using an in-situ approach, achieving a 27.62% yield and 95.40% selectivity for the hydroxylation of benzene to phenol in 4 h [26]. Pavadai et al. used flower-like NiMo-MOF as a bifunctional electrocatalyst for overall water splitting, which can efficiently achieve a current density of 10 mAcm− 2, and the electrochemical performance obviously surpasses that of bare Ni foam, Ni-MOF, and Mo-MOF counterparts [27]. Our group also contributed to this field by developing phosphotungstic acid supported by various ligand-functionalized bimetallic (Zr/Ce)UiO-66, achieving a high oleic acid conversion of 91.6% at 110 °C for 3 h [28]. Based on the above discussion, the incorporation of heteropolyacid into bimetallic MOFs may be an efficient strategy to improve the stability and recyclability of heteropolyacid.

Inspired by these advancements, we report the fabrication of bimetallic Bi/Ce-MOFs (Bi/Ce-BTC) nano-bars as a support for impregnation of phosphomolybdic acid (HPMo) via a facile one-pot hydrothermal synthesis strategy, and a comprehensive characterization of the as-prepared catalysts was conducted to ascertain the correlation between its physicochemical properties and their catalytic performance in esterification reactions of oleic acid. Meanwhile, the reuse of the synthesized HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC composites is also discussed. Additionally, the kinetic studies were conducted to assess the reaction’s rate constant and activation energy. Lastly, the mechanism of esterification of oleic acid catalyzed by the HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalyst was also elucidated. This study provided a new insight into the development of bimetallic MOF-based composite catalysts for sustainable biodiesel production.

Materials and methods

Chemicals availability

Cerium (III) nitrate hexahydrate (Ce(NO3)3·6H2O, AR), bismuth nitrate pentahydrate (Bi(NO3)3·5H2O, AR), and 1,3,5-benzene tricarboxylic acid (H3BTC, AR) were provided by Shanghai Aladdin Industrial Inc. Phosphomolybdic acid (H3PMo12O40·nH2O, HPMo, AR), lauric acid (LA, AR), myristic acid (MA, AR), and stearic acid (SA, AR) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Anhydrous methanol (AR), anhydrous ethanol (AR), n-propanol (AR), dimethylformamide (DMF), and oleic acid (AR) were acquired by Sinopharm Chemical Regent Co., Ltd. All chemicals utilized were employed without additional purification, while deionized water was utilized overall in this study.

Synthesis of different catalysts

The heteropolyacid@Bi/Ce-MOFs composites were prepared via a one-pot hydrothermal procedure, and the synthetic process was illustrated in Scheme 1. In brief, 0.4342 g (1 mmol) Ce(NO3)3·6H2O, 0.2425 g (0.5 mmol) Bi(NO3)3·5H2O, and 0.2101 g (1 mmol) H3BTC were dissolved in a mixed solution consisting of 11.25 mL of DMF and 18.75 mL of ethanol, sonicated for 30 min. Thus, dissolving 0.5 g (0.27 mmol) of heteropolyacid (HPMo) in 10 mL of deionized water was added to the above mixture and continuously stirred for 30 min at room temperature. After that, the resultant slurry was transferred into a 50 mL Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave reactor and heated at 160 °C for 10 h. Upon cooling to room temperature, the as-synthesized product was collected by centrifugation and washed several times with DMF and deionized water, and then dried at 70 °C for 12 h, and labelled as HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC. In addition, Bi/Ce-BTC without heteropolyacid introduction was obtained by HPMo@Bi/Ce-MOFs under the same synthetic conditions as above without the addition of heteropolyacid.

General procedure of catalytic test

The esterification reaction of oleic acid with methanol is conducted in a stainless-steel high-pressure reactor (Fig. S1). In brief, oleic acid (3.0 g), the proper amounts of methanol (methanol to oil molar ratio of 20:1), and 0.10 g of HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalyst were added to the reaction vessel, and then the reaction was performed at 150 °C for 5 h. Once the reaction was complete, the catalyst was separated by centrifugation, followed by the removal of excess methanol and water from the remaining solution using rotary distillation. Finally, the acid value of the remaining liquid phase was analyzed using an acid-base titration method according to the ISO 660–2020 standard. Then, the percentage conversion of oleic acid to ester was estimated from the acid value reduction efficiency [29].

Results and discussion

Characterization of catalysts

Figure 1a shows the powder XRD patterns of the studied HPMo, Bi/Ce-BTC, and HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC materials. For pristine HPMo, the results demonstrate some characteristic diffraction peaks located at 7.6°, 8.8°, 25.9°, and 28.0° (PDF#75–1588), which were quite consistent with the previously reported diffraction peak of HPMo [30]. For Bi/Ce-BTC, the diffraction peaks corresponding to Bi/Ce-BTC were observed at 9.9°, 13.2°, 17.2°, and 24.6°, suggesting the competitive coordination between Bi and Ce with BTC and the successful preparation of Bi/Ce-BTC [31, 32]. After impregnation of HPMo on Bi/Ce-BTC, the two distinctive diffraction peaks of HPMo at 8.8° and 28.0° were shifted to 8.1° and 27.7°, implying the strong interaction between the incorporated compounds [33]. On the other hand, the characteristic XRD peaks of Bi/Ce-BTC were absent in the HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC hybrid material, indicating their lower crystallinity. This phenomenon may be because the introduction of HPMo reduces the crystallinity of the composite. Also, the results suggest a well-dispersed of HPMo was formed on the surface of Bi/Ce-BTC [34]. FTIR spectra of HPMo, Bi/Ce-BTC, and HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC materials presented in Fig. 1b reveal the existence of functional groups for each component. The results appeared in the FTIR spectrum of HPMo with four characteristic bands centered at 787, 870, 968, and 1064 cm− 1, which was ascribed to the Keggin structure of HPMo [35]. For Bi/Ce-BTC, the peaks in the range of 1498–1712 cm− 1 and 1342–1473 cm− 1 were attributed to the symmetric and asymmetric –COOH stretching vibrations [36]. The bonds of Bi–O and Ce–O appeared at 470–680 cm− 1, and the absorption bands at 710 cm− 1 is attributed to out-of-plane bending vibrations of C–H [37]. These results confirmed the successful synthesis of Bi/Ce-BTC. In HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC composite spectrum, a combination of these characteristic peaks of HPMo and Bi/Ce-BTC is present. However, these peaks showed a decrease in intensity in the HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC, and the peaks of HPMo at 870 and 787 cm− 1 have been shifted to a lower wavenumber, thus implying the HPMo species interacted with the Bi/Ce-BTC matrix and the successful formation of Bi/Ce-BTC-based complexes with HPMo. At the same time, the existence of interaction in the HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC composite might provide more active sites to improve the catalytic performance in esterification reactions [38].

According to the findings, the relatively large specific surface area and meso-porosity of HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC composite improves the accessibility of reactants to active sites and facilitates efficient transport under esterification reaction process [40], so as to achieve excellent catalytic activity.

To examine the acidity of the Bi/Ce-BTC and HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalysts, the NH3-TPD technique was performed, and the findings are demonstrated in Fig. 4a. The NH3-TPD profile of Bi/Ce-BTC and HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalysts revealed two predominant desorption peaks at approximately 150 °C and 421 °C, which were attributed to NH3 adsorbed on weak and strong acid sites, respectively [41]. This may be attributed to a stronger synergistic interaction between HPMo and Bi/Ce-BTC existed with introduction of HPMo species, which led to a large change in the growth of the acidity [42], and more strong acids were adsorbed on the surface of the HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC.

Additionally, Py-FTIR spectroscopy was also conducted to analyze the type of acidic sites (Brønsted (B) and Lewis (L) acid) on the surfaces of Bi/Ce-BTC and HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalysts, and the results are presented in Fig. 4a and Table S1. In Fig. 4a, the characteristic peaks at 1450 and 1610 cm− 1 are associated with L acid sites for both catalysts, respectively [43]. The characteristic peak at 1540 cm− 1 corresponds to the B acid sites, and the characteristic peak at 1490 cm− 1 belongs to combined B + L acid sites [44]. Also, it is significant to observe that the peak intensities of HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC are stronger than that of the Bi/Ce-BTC. Besides, the B/L value of HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC (0.16) is higher compared with Bi/Ce-BTC (0.12) at 150 °C. In conclusion, the introduction of HPMo species impacts the acid strength and distribution of acid types on the HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC surface. Thus, HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC has better catalytic activity than that of Bi/Ce-BTC alone as the catalyst.

TG analysis of Bi/Ce-BTC and HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalysts was conducted at temperatures ranging from 50 to 600 °C to assess their stability, with findings presented in Fig. 5. It is evident that two catalysts presented two decomposition signals. For Bi/Ce-BTC, the first weight loss (21.1 wt%) below 150 °C was due to the desorption of water and volatile materials. The second weight loss of 12.6 wt% from 150 to 550 °C was observed due to the partly decomposition of organic ligands. In contrast, HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC was quite thermally stable, and there was only 14.0 wt% of weight loss from 50 to 550 °C. It could be attributed to the impregnation of HPMo, which diminished the quantity of water molecules adsorbed by Bi/Ce-BTC framework, and the existence of strong interaction between two substances enhanced their stability [45]. Meanwhile, this also indirectly reflected the successful introduction of HPMo species. The TG findings illustrate that the HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC composite possessed excellent stability and was apt to catalyze the esterification reaction for biodiesel production.

To analyze the bonding configuration and surface chemical states of the prepared Bi/Ce-BTC and HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalysts, XPS observations were carried out (Fig. 6). The test results in Fig. 6a show the Bi/Ce-BTC catalyst primarily comprised C, O, Bi, and Ce. Meanwhile, this confirms the coexistence of C, O, Bi, Ce, P, and Mo elements in the HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC composite, further proving the successful impregnation of HPMo. This is consistent with the result of EDS. The Bi 4f spectra (Fig. 6b) dissociate into two independent peaks at 158.9 and 164.1 eV, assignable to Bi 4f7/2 and Bi 4f5/2, respectively [46]. Notably, the two Bi 4f peaks in the HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC displayed a red shift compared to Bi/Ce-BTC, indicating that the charge transfer was caused by the electronegative interaction between HPMo and Bi/Ce-BTC. From the Ce 3d spectrum of Bi/Ce-BTC in Fig. 6c, the peaks at 886.2 and 904.8 eV are attributed to Ce3+, whereas the peaks at 882.9, 896.2, 901.4, and 907.5 eV are assigned to Ce4+. It suggests that the part of Ce3+ was oxidized to Ce4+ during the process of material preparation. It is also notable that the Ce 3d fitted peaks of HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC were observed at slightly higher energies. This may be due to the variation in electron concentration between the material contact interfaces [47]. In addition, the + 3 and + 4 valence states of Ce may convert each other to form oxygen vacancies, which have a certain promoting effect on the catalytic performance [48]. As illustrated in Fig. 6d, the Mo 3d spectrum of HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC exhibited two distinct peaks at 231.9 and 235.0 eV, which belonged to Mo 3d5/2 orbital and Mo 3d3/2 orbital, respectively [49], and this indicated the presence of Mo5+ in the HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC composite. Figure 6e demonstrates the XPS spectra of P 2p, a weak signal at binding energies of 133.1 eV was identified, which may be associated with P 2p was surrounded by the Mo atom in the Keggin structure [50]. These results further indicated successful HPMo loading.

Catalytic evaluation in esterification

Catalytic performance of different catalysts

According to our previous study [15, 28], the pristine heteropolyacids (HPMo, HPW, etc.) gave the higher oleic acid conversion (>90%) under mild conditions. However, these pristine heteropolyacids is freely soluble in reaction solvent and evaluated as homogeneous catalysts. In this study, the catalytic ability of Bi/Ce-BTC and HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC was examined and evaluated to catalyze the esterification of oleic acid to biodiesel as an index (reaction conditions: 150 °C, 5 h, methanol/oleic acid = 20:1(molar ratio), 0.10 g catalyst). After 5 h of reaction, only 34.9% of oleic acid conversion was achieved with Bi/Ce-BTC. While with HPMo loading, the conversion of oleic acid was improved greatly, resulting in a conversion of 92.7%. Therefore, the HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalyst is more active than that of the Bi/Ce-BTC. The HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalyst by NH3-TPD, Py-FTIR, N2 physisorption, and XPS analyses showed the preferable catalytic performance in the esterification depending on the relatively large specific surface area, meso-porosity, the growth of the acidity, and the synergistic catalytic effect between HPMo and Bi/Ce-BTC. Conclusively, the HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC composite has more active sites and stronger acidity, which is the most suitable catalyst for further research.

Effect of reaction parameters on oleic acid conversion by HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC composite catalyst

Here, a single-factor experimental approach was performed to optimize and evaluate the influence of various key parameters such as reaction temperature (110–160 °C) and time (1–6 h), HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalyst loading (0.04–0.16 g), and methanol to oleic acid molar ratio (5:1–25:1) on the esterification of oleic acid to biodiesel.

Effective control of the reaction time is an important key factor that determines the conversion of oleic acid. In order to get the optimal time, the effect of time on the esterification reaction of oleic acid to methyl oleate was investigated from 1 h to 6 h, and the results are displayed in Fig. 7a. When the reaction time raises from 1 h to 5 h, the conversion of oleic acid increases with the extension of time, from 17.6% to 92.7%. It may be because the longer duration facilitated greater contact between the solid catalyst and reactants, resulting in more frequent collisions and higher oleic acid conversion. However, if the reaction time is extended from 5 h to 6 h, the conversion of oleic acid decreases due to the reconversion of the product into oleic acid. In summary, 5 h was specified as the appropriate time.

Next, temperature is considered a significant factor that significantly alters the esterification process. Thus, the effect of reaction temperature (110–160 °C) on the conversion was studied under the previously refined parameters, and the outcomes are depicted in Fig. 7b. The conversion gradually rises from 21.1% to 92.7% as the reaction temperature rises from 110 to 150 °C. The reason might be that the endothermic characteristics of the esterification reaction, and high temperature could provide additional energy to accelerate the esterification process. Beyond this optimal point, the conversion of oleic acid exhibited a minor reduction. This decline might be due to the volatility of methanol at elevated temperatures, which decreases the concentration of methanol in the reactants [51]. Therefore, the optimal reaction temperature was set at 150 °C.

Theoretically, the esterification of 1 mol of oleic acid requires 1 mol of methanol to produce 1 mol of methyl oleate. However, the esterification is a reversible reaction, and an excess of alcohol is needed in the actual reaction. The experiments were conducted with a molar ratio of methanol to oleic acid ranging from 5:1 to 25:1, and the other operating conditions were kept constant. As shown in Fig. 7c, the oleic acid conversion raises from 67.9% to 92.7% as the molar ratio increases from 5:1 to 20:1. However, further increases in molar ratio from 20:1 to 25:1, the conversion of oleic acid decreases to increase and instead begins to decline. It was due to the excess of methanol dilutes the activity of the catalyst, thus reducing the conversion rate [52]. As a result, 20:1 methanol to oleic acid was determined as the optimal molar ratio.

Lastly, catalyst loading is one of the important parameters that promotes the esterification reaction rate. According to our previous study, the conversion of oleic acid is quite slow without any catalyst [53]. Thus, the experiments were performed with catalyst amounts ranging from 0.04 g to 0.16 g. As shown in Fig. 7d, catalyst loading obviously affected the conversion of oleic acid, with the increase of catalyst amount, the conversion gradually increased, reaching a maximum of 92.7% conversion at 0.10 g. This is most likely because of the increased availability of active sites within the esterification reaction system, thus accelerating the reaction rate. Further increase in the catalyst loading to 0.16 g did not lead to additional conversion gains. This phenomenon may be due to excessive catalyst loading result in agglomeration and increased resistance to mass transfer between the oleic acid and methanol phases [54]. Hence, it was believed that 0.10 g is the appropriate catalyst loading in this study.

Based on the overall results of reaction optimization, the best process conditions were achieved with 92.7% oleic acid conversion at a methanol to oil molar ratio of 20:1, a catalyst loading of 0.10 g, a reaction time of 5 h, and a reaction temperature of 150 °C. Besides, based on the analysis of relevant literature [55, 56], a possible reaction mechanism of oleic acid esterification over HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC is displayed in Scheme 2. First, on the surface of HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC, the carbonyl group of oleic acid was activated by the proton to generate a protonated oleic acid intermediate. Subsequently, the nucleophiles (methanol) attack the carbonyl carbon, leading to the formation of a tetrahedral intermediate. Next, the proton (H+) provided by the catalyst binds to the hydroxyl oxygen form a protonated -OH2+ group on the intermediate, thus removing water. Finally, the biodiesel (methyl oleate) is attained after detaching from the HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalyst site.

Kinetics analysis

The reaction kinetics of esterification of oleic acid using the HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalyst were performed at temperatures ranging from 130 to 150 °C under fixed optimal parameters (20:1 ratio and 0.10 g catalyst), and the result is shown in Fig. S2a. From the figure, the conversion of oleic acid increased with a raise in the reaction temperature. According to the previous literature [57], the esterification reaction of various acids is expected to obey pseudo-first-order kinetics equation (- In (1-X) = k t, where X denotes the oleic acid conversion at time t, k denotes the rate constant, h− 1). Thus, in this study, Figure S2b shows the profile of the -ln(1-X) versus reaction time, it can be seen that the regression coefficients (R2) of each temperature curve are higher than 0.8, confirming the pseudo-first-order reaction kinetics to represent the esterification of oleic acid with methanol [58, 59]. Besides, the apparent Ea for the esterification of oleic acid was calculated from the ln k versus 1/T plot according to the Arrhenius equation (Fig. S2c) [60]. As a result, the Ea for esterification catalyzed by HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalyst was found to be 60.9 kJ/mol, indicating that the esterification process in this study correlates with a truly chemically governed reaction [60, 61].

Esterification of different types fatty acids and alcohols

Figure 8a shows the effects of different types of fatty acids with methanol on the conversion using HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalyst under the optimal conditions (0.10 g catalyst loading, 5 h, 20:1 ratio at 150 °C). The conversions obtained when using lauric acid, myristic acid, stearic acid, and oleic acid were 77.5%, 67.2%, 77.1% and 92.7%, respectively. This phenomenon may be related to the steric factor and impact the esterification conversion passively [62]. The esterification of oleic acid with different types of alcohols was further evaluated by using HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalyst under the optimal conditions (0.10 g catalyst loading, 5 h, 20:1 ratio at 150 °C). By observing Fig. 8b, it could be seen that three alcohols yielded conversions greater than 60%. Surprisingly, the esterification of oleic acid with methanol showed higher conversion compared to the use of ethanol and n-propanol. This behavior may be due to ethanol and n-propanol having higher steric hindrance, which causes less interaction between the catalyst, oleic acid, and alcohol [63]. In summary, HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalyst has good catalytic affinity for biodiesel synthesis from the esterification reaction of different types of fatty acids and alcohols.

Study on the reusability of HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalyst

Fig. 9(a) The reusability the HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalyst and (b) XRD spectra of the fresh and used HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalyst

(a) The reusability the HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalyst and (b) XRD spectra of the fresh and used HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalyst

The recyclability of the catalyst is one of the major advantages of using heterogeneous catalysts for biodiesel generation. HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC reutilization was explored under the optimal reaction conditions established (0.10 g catalyst loading, 5 h, 20:1 ratio at 150 °C). After the completion of each cycle, HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC was covered by centrifugation and subsequently subjected to the next reaction cycle, and the results are displayed in Fig. 9a. A slight decrease from 92.7% to 82.1% in the conversion of oleic acid to methyl oleate using HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC is observed for the fourth cycle. Meanwhile, the conversion began to decline from the 5th cycle as the conversion rate lowered to 80%. The XRD analysis (Fig. 9b) of the catalyst after its sixth reuse revealed that the main peak intensity slightly decreased after repeated reaction, suggesting that the good structure stability of HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC and the slight active species on the catalyst surface indeed leached into the product [64]. On the other hand, a leaching experiment was also conducted, and the outcomes are depicted in Fig. 10. After 2 h of the oleic acid esterification under the optimized reaction conditions, the HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalyst was filtered out of the reaction system, and then the liquid filtrate was reacted for another 3 h. As a result, the conversion of oleic acid slight increase (52.6–65.3%) in the absence of catalyst, while in the reaction system with HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalyst, the oleic acid conversion (52.5–92.7%) increased when the reaction time continued to extend from 3 to 5 h, affirming that HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC was stable in the reaction system with slight leaching, and are accordant with XRD results. As a consequence, the results showed that the HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC catalyzed the esterification reaction was indeed in a heterogeneous manner and exhibited a good chemical structural stability, which all makes the process much more practical.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed a simple synthetic method for the preparation of HPMo supported on bimetallic MOFs materials (HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC) through the one-pot hydrothermal method, and its catalytic performance was evaluated in the esterification of oleic acid. According to the results obtained, the HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC composite catalyst demonstrated excellent catalytic activity, achieving an oleic acid conversion of 92.7% at a catalyst amount of 0.10 g, a methanol/oleic acid molar ratio of 20:1, a temperature of 150 °C, and a reaction time of 5 h, and it also showed excellent cycling performance, allowing for six times reusing with an acceptable conversion efficiency. The characterization results show that the superior catalytic performance is attributed to a relatively large specific surface area (28.95 m2/g), meso-porosity (16.7 nm), sufficient B acid and L acid sites, higher acidity (2.33 mmol/g), and synergistic interactions between HPMo and Bi/Ce-BTC. Importantly, using different types of fatty acids and alcohols under optimized conditions to synthesize the esters showed that HPMo@Bi/Ce-BTC has good catalytic activity and generality. This study can offer some interesting and useful information for designing highly active bimetallic MOF-based materials applied for scalable and sustainable energy applications.
