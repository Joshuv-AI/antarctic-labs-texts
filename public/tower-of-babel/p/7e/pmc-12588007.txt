Body-temperature versus room-temperature local anaesthetic solutions for epidural-related maternal fever prevention in labour: protocol for a two-centre randomised controlled trial
Authors: Dapeng Chen, Qingge Wang, Qianyu Wu, Minhong Shen, Jisi Zhang, Xisheng Shan, Chang-Dong Feng, Huili Tang, Lei Wei, Fuhai Ji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEpidural analgesia-related maternal fever (ERMF) affects 15%–25% of parturients, posing risks to both maternal and neonatal. Although ERMF’s mechanisms remain unclear, non-infectious inflammatory responses and thermoregulatory disruption are implicated. Previous studies suggest that room-temperature local anaesthetic solutions administered during epidural analgesia may contribute to ERMF by altering spinal thermoregulation. This trial investigates whether warming local anaesthetic solutions to body temperature reduces ERMF incidence.Methods and analysisThis two-centre, double-blind, randomised controlled trial will enrol 424 parturients undergoing epidural analgesia for trial of labour. Participants will be randomised (1:1) to receive either body temperature (approximately 37°C) or room temperature (approximately 23°C) local anaesthetic solutions (0.1% ropivacaine with 0.5 µg/mL sufentanil). The primary outcome is intrapartum fever incidence, defined as oral temperature ≥38°C. Secondary outcomes include time to visual analogue scale ≤3, pulse perfusion index, maternal and neonatal antibiotic use rate, incidence of bacteraemia, intrapartum hypothermia, duration of labour, unplanned caesarean section rate, umbilical artery potential of hydrogen and Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min for newborns. Randomisation, stratified by study centre, employs permuted blocks via R software. Blinding is maintained for participants, outcome assessors and statisticians. A sample size of 424 participants (212 per group) provides 80% power (α=0.05) to detect a reduction in fever incidence from 21% to 10%. The primary analysis will be conducted in accordance with the intention-to-treat principle, with a secondary per-protocol analysis.Ethics and disseminationThe study protocol has been approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (Approval No. 2023367-2) and the Ethics Committee of Suzhou Municipal Hospital (Approval No. K-2024-258 K01), and adheres to the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants will provide written informed consent. Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals.Trial registration numberNCT06682416.

Introduction

Infections, epidural analgesia or environmental factors (such as excessively high delivery room temperatures (RTs)) are common causes of maternal fever during childbirth, with an incidence rate of approximately 33%. Among these, the occurrence of epidural analgesia-related maternal fever (ERMF) ranges from 15% to 25%.1 Numerous studies have indicated that compared with women who do not receive epidural analgesia, those who undergo epidural analgesia during childbirth have a significantly increased incidence of ERMF.2 3 This association entails significant clinical and systemic consequences, including: heightened maternal antibiotic administration; increased neonatal exposure to diagnostic evaluations (particularly sepsis workups) and empiric antibiotic therapy, frequently instigated by suspected chorioamnionitis; elevated probability of unscheduled caesarean deliveries; greater perinatal blood loss; and substantial escalation in healthcare expenditures.410 Additionally, any occurrence of hyperthermia during childbirth, whether due to ERMF or other causes, can significantly elevate the risk of neonatal brain injury, approximately by a factor of 2.79.

‘There is no other circumstance where it is considered acceptable for an individual to experience untreated severe pain, amenable to safe intervention, while under a physician’s care’. In the absence of a medical contraindication, maternal request is a sufficient medical indication for pain relief during labour’, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists and the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) assert.11 With the rising trend of opting for painless delivery, the concern surrounding ERMF is gaining increased attention.

Multiple studies demonstrate that warming local anaesthetic solutions reduces shivering1215 and suggests a trend towards reduced intrapartum fever.12 16 Substantial evidence now indicates that a non-infectious inflammatory response is a primary driver of ERMF, characterised by impaired release of anti-pyrogenic IL-1ra and elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, IL-8, PGE₂) preceding fever onset.17 18 The incidence of ERMF is positively correlated with the dosage of local anaesthetic administered.19 Taking into account the empirical administration of 40–50 mL of local anaesthetic solution per delivery, we propose that the temperature of this relatively large volume of solution acts as a key mediator in triggering the non-infectious inflammatory response, rather than solely its pharmacological action. Infusion of RT (22°C–24°C) local anaesthetic into the limited-capacity epidural space (further reduced by vascular engorgement in pregnancy) delivers a significant cold stimulus to the neuraxial compartment. This thermal challenge—stemming directly from the large volume of cool solution—could disrupt spinal cord thermoregulation and initiate or amplify the inflammatory pathways implicated in ERMF.

Therefore, we hypothesise that warming local anaesthetic solutions to maternal body temperature (BT) reduces the incidence of ERMF in labouring women.

Methods and analysis

This trial protocol follows the guidelines of Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) (online supplemental file 1).

Study design and patients

This is an investigator-initiated, two-centre, double-blind, parallel-group, randomised controlled trial. This study will include a total of 424 parturient women who undergo epidural analgesia for vaginal delivery at 2 teaching hospitals in eastern China. The leading study centre is The First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, Suzhou, China. The one participating centre is Suzhou Municipal Hospital, Suzhou, China. We plan to enrol patients between 30 November 2024 and 1 October 2027. The study flow diagram is presented in figure 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

To be included in this study, all participants should meet the following criteria: (1) requirement for epidural analgesia, (2) age ≥18 years old, (3) ≥37 weeks of gestation and (4) planned vaginal delivery. The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) contraindications to epidural analgesia, (2) prepartum fever (≥38°C), (3) use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or other types of antipyretics before delivery, (4) multiple pregnancies, (5) stillbirth, (6) severe pre-eclampsia and (7) refusal to participate.

Randomisation and blinding

An independent researcher will use R software (V.4.2.2) with the ‘blockrand’ package for randomisation, ensuring a 1:1 allocation ratio, permuted blocks of 2 and 4, and stratification by study centre. During the randomisation process in this study, the random seed for the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University was 123, and for Suzhou Municipal Hospital, it was 234.

The randomisation assignments will be securely stored in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes to ensure allocation concealment. Group allocation concealment is maintained using these envelopes. The envelope corresponding to each participant is opened only by the unblinded anaesthesiologist after the participant has consented, been enrolled and the epidural catheter has been placed, immediately prior to connecting the analgesic pump tubing and initiating the intervention (applying or not applying the warmer). Participants will be randomly assigned to either the BT group or the RT group, with 212 subjects allocated to each arm. Anaesthesiologists performing epidural analgesia remain unblinded to treatment allocation given their operational responsibility in implementing distinct intervention protocols. However, these clinicians are excluded from participant enrolment, data acquisition and analytical processes. Blinding is rigorously maintained for parturients, obstetric nurses responsible for outcome evaluations and biostatisticians conducting data interpretation. To preserve blinding of participants and outcome assessors: (1) The Barkey S-line warmer unit is physically present and identically connected in both groups, but remains switched off in the RT group. The analgesic pump, infusion tubing and the warmer unit are positioned or draped to obscure the parturient’s view of both the unit itself and any status lights indicating whether it is powered on, thus preventing visual cues about the active intervention. (2) Outcome assessors (obstetric nurses) are not present during the epidural setup and initiation of the infusion and are unaware of group assignment; (3) Statisticians received data where group allocation was coded only as ‘0’ (representing RT group) or ‘1’ (representing BT group), ensuring they remained unaware of the specific temperature condition associated with each code during analysis.

Study interventions

For both groups, after purging air from the tubing via analgesic pump priming, the output tubing is connected to the epidural catheter and passed through a Barkey S-line blood transfusion and infusion warmer (manufactured by Barkey GmbH & Co. KG., medical device registration 20182450063). In the BT group: The warmer is activated to preheat the output tubing of the analgesic pump to approximately 37°C. In the RT group: The warmer remains inactive, allowing the solution in the output tubing to maintain ambient temperature (approximately 23°C). Epidural analgesia procedure

When the cervical dilation reaches 2–3 cm, the parturient is guided into an independent labour ward to undergo standard vital sign monitoring, including non-invasive blood pressure, heart rate, pulse oximetry and BT. Concurrently, routine obstetric examinations are performed to assess the progress of labour. After successfully establishing an upper extremity intravenous access, an epidural puncture and catheterisation are conducted. With the parturient in the left lateral decubitus position, the L2–L3 or L3–L4 intervertebral space is selected for puncture, using the ‘loss of resistance’ technique to ensure that the tip of 17G epidural needle penetrates the ligamentum flavum and enters the epidural space. Subsequently, the epidural catheter is inserted into the epidural space and further advanced cephalad by approximately 3–5 cm before being securely fixed to ensure the catheter’s position remains unchanged. A test dose of 1% lidocaine (3–5 mL) containing 1:200 000 epinephrine is then administered through the epidural catheter into the epidural space. The parturient will be observed for 3–5 min for signs of local anaesthetic toxicity and subarachnoid block. During this period, the epidural analgesic solution is meticulously prepared by combining 100 mg of 0.75% ropivacaine (equivalent to 13.3 mL) with 50 µg of sufentanil (equivalent to 1 mL) in 85.7 mL of normal saline, yielding a total volume of 100 mL solution with a concentration of 0.1% ropivacaine and 0.5 µg/mL sufentanil.

In this trial, a pulsatile electronic patient self-controlled epidural analgesia (PCEA) pump, model ZZB-IV by Jiangsu Aipeng Medical Technology (national medical device registration number 20173141272), is employed with the following parameters: a bolus dose of 10 mL/hour, a patient-controlled dose of 6 mL per instance, a lockout time of 15 min and a maximum limit of 25 mL/hour. Parturients will be advised to use the PCEA button for additional analgesia when their Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score exceeds 3 on a 0–10 scale. In cases of inadequate pain relief (defined as <30% reduction in VAS score) persisting 10 min postintervention, a 6 mL bolus of 1% lidocaine was administered epidurally. The anaesthesiologists administered labour analgesia to parturients in accordance with the recommendations detailed in the ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 209: Obstetric Analgesia and Anesthesia, as previously published.20

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome measure for this trial is the incidence of intrapartum fever, as determined by oral temperature readings, throughout the entire labour process. Intrapartum fever is defined as maternal intrapartum fever with a temperature of 38°C or higher.5

Secondary outcome measures include the duration to reach a VAS ≤3 following epidural analgesia, pulse perfusion index (PI) and a range of maternal and neonatal outcome indicators such as maternal and neonatal antibiotic use rate, incidence of bacteraemia, intrapartum hypothermia, duration of labour, unplanned caesarean section rate, umbilical artery potential of hydrogen (pH) and Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min for newborns. The PI will be recorded before epidural anaesthesia, and at 10 min, 20 min and 30 min after epidural catheterisation.

Data collection and monitoring

The midwives in charge of outcome assessment will collect demographic data (age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), gestational age and nulliparous), baseline characteristics (ASA physical status, education level, smoking during pregnancy, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, pregestational diabetes, gestational diabetes, group B streptococcus status, hypothyroidism in pregnancy, hyperthyroidism in pregnancy and anaemia in pregnancy), labour analgesia characteristics (selection of interlaminar space and epidural approach, epidural puncture approach, duration of analgesia), other maternal characteristics (medications affecting BT during labour including magnesium sulfate, hormonal therapies, carbetocin, antipyretics and others, number of vaginal examinations, chorioamnionitis, normal spontaneous or operative vaginal delivery and length of hospital stay) and neonatal characteristics (sex, birth weight, and temperature at birth). Maternal temperature will be measured at baseline prior to epidural catheter insertion (T0), then at 2-hour intervals during the active first stage of labour (T1–T3 at 2, 4 and 6 hours postepidural), and additionally at the conclusion of the second stage (T4), the conclusion of the third stage (T5) and 1 hour postpartum (T6). These measurements will record both oral and tympanic membrane temperatures to accurately monitor temperature changes associated with ERMF. For parturients undergoing unplanned caesarean delivery after enrolment, temperature monitoring will continue as clinically feasible until 2 hours post partum. According to the recommendations of the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, histopathological examination (placenta, fetal membranes or umbilical cord vasculitis) should be performed postpartum for patients with suspected chorioamnionitis.21 In cases of persistent intrapartum hyperthermia with a sustained temperature ≥38.5°C at 2 hours postpartum, blood cultures (aerobic and anaerobic) will be obtained from the parturient. Bacteraemia will be diagnostically confirmed on isolation of pathogenic micro-organisms in the culture media, in accordance with standard clinical microbiology protocols. All study data will be meticulously documented in case report forms and subsequently transcribed into an electronic database, with the entire process overseen by the principal investigator (FJ). A dedicated Data Monitoring Committee will provide continuous oversight of the data collection process. on completion of data entry, the electronic database will be secured to prevent further modifications. Deidentified datasets will then be forwarded to an independent statistician for comprehensive analysis in accordance with the predefined statistical protocol.

Sample size calculation

PASS software (V.15.0, NCSS) is used to calculate the sample size. Based on the results of a previous systematic review and meta-analysis,2 the incidence of maternal fever in parturient women receiving epidural analgesia is 21%, while that in non-recipients is 6%. It is expected that the incidence of maternal fever in parturient women receiving 37°C local anaesthetic solution will be reduced to 10%. With α=0.05, 1–β=0.8, two-sided t-test, and a 1:1 ratio of two groups, a sample size of 169 per group is required, with an expected 20% loss to follow-up, a total of 424 cases are needed.

Statistical analysis

The primary analysis of this study will use the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test to compare intrapartum fever rates among two groups of parturients. We will also employ a generalised linear mixed model to analyse the impact of local anaesthetic temperature on intrapartum fever. This model will account for the hierarchical structure of the data, with repeated measurements from each participant at various time points, such as before and after epidural catheterisation and at different postpartum intervals, with participants nested within hospitals. The model will include fixed effects for baseline maternal temperature, age, gestational age and treatment group, as well as random effects for hospital random intercepts. We will also consider the effects of time and the interaction between treatment group and time to assess how different temperatures of local anaesthetic solutions influence the incidence of intrapartum fever. This method will allow us to precisely evaluate the effects of the intervention on the rate of intrapartum fever while controlling for potential confounding factors. Additionally, multiple imputation by chained equations will be used to address missing data for the primary outcome at different time points.

Data normality will be assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Continuous variables will be summarised and presented as mean±SD or median (IQR), depending on their distribution. The independent t-test will be used for normally distributed data, and the Mann-Whitney U test will be used for data with skewed distribution. Categorical variables, expressed as number (percentage), will be analysed using the χ² or Fisher’s exact test.

To assess the robustness of the primary findings, several sensitivity analyses will be performed by varying fever definitions, temperature measurement sites, local anaesthetic administration (dosage/volume) and analysis sets (intention-to-treat and per-protocol). Subgroup analyses will also be conducted to explore potential effect modification by baseline characteristics such as maternal age (<35 vs ≥35 years), BMI (<28 vs ≥28 kg/m2), nulliparity (yes vs no) and gestational diabetes mellitus (yes vs no). The interaction analysis across the subgroups will be performed using a logistic regression model.

All analyses will be conducted on an intention-to-treat basis. SPSS (V.26.0) and R (V.4.2.2) software will be used for data analysis. A two-sided p<0.05 will be considered to indicate statistical significance.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and public will not be involved in the design, recruitment, conduct or report of the study. The study results will be disseminated to the participants via email.

Ethics and dissemination

The trial protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (Approval No. 2023367-2), and subsequently by the Ethics Committee of Suzhou Municipal Hospital (Approval No. K-2024-258 K01). This trial was preregistered on ClinicalTrials.gov on 8 November 2024 (https://clinicaltrials.gov, ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06682416). The trial will adhere to the ethical guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and has been approved by the ethics committees at each participating centre. All the participants will provide written informed consent (online supplemental file 2). The results of this study will be published in a peer-reviewed journal.

Discussion

This multicentre randomised controlled trial aims to assess whether warming local anaesthetic solutions to BT reduces the incidence of ERMF in parturients receiving epidural analgesia. Recruitment is currently ongoing from 30 November 2024, with anticipated completion by 1 October 2027.

There are some limitations to this study. First, the sample size calculation is based on the known incidence of intrapartum fever, which could not be directly obtained for ERMF. Second, although we target reducing the intrapartum fever rate from 21% to 10% (a clinically ambitious effect size), the 6% background incidence in non-epidural groups may constrain achievable effect magnitudes. Importantly, this study lacks statistical power to detect smaller but clinically relevant reductions (eg, 25% relative risk reduction). Should non-significant results emerge, we will leverage these findings to design appropriately powered multicentre trials with refined effect size estimations. Furthermore, variations in maternal BT pose challenges in ensuring precise control over the anaesthetic solution temperature. To address this, we will use pulsed epidural analgesia pumps to optimise thermal stability.

We anticipate completing the study within the expected timeline and will take all precautions to minimise biases and ensure accurate data collection, as outlined by the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials guidelines.
