A Ten-Year Retrospective Analysis of Maxillofacial Trauma: Insights into Etiology, Demographics, and Management
Authors: J. M. Madhava Rao Thumari, Dharani Chowdary Kilari, K. Durga Prasad, Fawaz Abdul Hamid Baig, Anish Nelson, Ajit Kumar Swain, P. Srinivas Chakravarthi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Mandibular fractures are among the most frequently encountered maxillofacial injuries, often resulting from road traffic accidents (RTAs), interpersonal violence, and falls. Understanding the patterns, etiology, and demographics of these fractures is crucial for effective prevention and management.Materials and Methods:A retrospective analysis was conducted on 728 patients with mandibular fractures treated at the maxillofacial unit of SIBAR Institute of Dental Sciences from March 2006 to February 2016. Data on demographics, fracture sites, etiology, and contributing factors were collected and analyzed using SPSS.Results:The majority of cases involved males (86.5%), with the highest incidence in the 21-30 years age group (39.7%). RTAs were the leading cause (45.2%), followed by interpersonal violence (25.2%) and falls (13.4%). The parasymphysis was the most common fracture site (41.5%), followed by the angle (33.6%) and condyle (17.1%).Conclusion:Mandibular fractures predominantly affect young males and are largely caused by RTAs. Targeted prevention strategies, stricter traffic laws, and public awareness campaigns are essential to reduce their incidence and associated morbidity.

INTRODUCTION

Maxillofacial trauma, particularly mandibular fractures, represents a significant portion of injuries treated in healthcare facilities, often associated with substantial morbidity and economic burden. Trauma remains the leading cause of death among individuals under 40 years of age, with road traffic accidents (RTAs) accounting for a majority of cases globally, resulting in millions of injuries and approximately one million deaths annually.[12] Mandibular fractures are the second most commonly fractured bones in the maxillofacial region after nasal fractures, varying significantly based on demographic, cultural, and environmental factors.[3] Common causes include RTAs, interpersonal violence, falls, and workplace accidents, with RTAs predominating due to factors such as non-compliance with safety measures, alcohol consumption, and poor road infrastructure.[4] Understanding the epidemiology of mandibular fractures is crucial for optimizing treatment strategies and prevention measures. This retrospective study evaluates the patterns, etiology, and demographic distribution of mandibular fractures treated at a high-volume maxillofacial unit over a decade.[56]

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This retrospective study was conducted by analyzing patient records from the maxillofacial unit of SIBAR Institute of Dental Sciences over a 10-year period, from March 2006 to February 2016. Patients with all types of mandibular fractures, irrespective of treatment methods, were included. Cases with dentoalveolar fractures, severe multi-organ injuries, systemic complications requiring referral, or incomplete records were excluded. Data collected from medical records included patient demographics (age, gender), fracture characteristics (site, type), causes of trauma (road traffic accidents, interpersonal violence, falls, sports injuries, or workplace accidents), substance involvement, and time of hospital presentation. Fractures were classified based on anatomical location into midline, parasymphysis, body, angle, ramus, condyle, and coronoid. The data were systematically entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive and inferential statistics were applied to identify patterns and correlations. Ethical approval was obtained prior to study initiation.

RESULTS

A total of 728 mandibular fractures were analyzed, with patients ranging from 1 to 76 years of age. The majority of cases involved men (86.5%, n = 630), while women accounted for 13.5% (n = 98). The highest incidence occurred in the 21-30 years age group (39.7%, n = 290), followed by the 31-40 years group (20.8%, n = 155). Road traffic accidents (RTAs) were the leading cause of fractures (45.2%, n = 329), followed by interpersonal violence (25.2%, n = 184) and falls (13.4%, n = 98) [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

Mandibular fractures are among the most common injuries in maxillofacial trauma, often resulting from road traffic accidents (RTAs), falls, or interpersonal violence. The present study aligns with existing literature on the incidence, etiology, and patterns of mandibular fractures in various populations.[678]

Tirumalrao et al.[1] analyzed mandibular fractures in the Indian sub-population, highlighting RTAs as the leading cause, followed by falls and assaults. Similarly, Barde et al.[2] reported a higher prevalence of mandibular fractures in males due to their increased involvement in outdoor activities and exposure to high-risk situations. Singh et al.[3] also emphasized the predominance of RTAs as a major contributor to mandibular trauma, particularly in younger age groups, given their higher mobility and risk-taking behavior.

Patterns of mandibular fractures vary with demographic and geographic factors. Studies by Manodh et al.[4] and Al-Hassani et al.[9] reported that the condylar and parasymphysis regions are the most frequently affected sites, consistent with findings in this study. Seasonal variations have also been observed, as Shukla et al.[5] noted an increase in maxillofacial injuries during monsoon seasons due to slippery roads and reduced visibility.

Treatment modalities for mandibular fractures depend on the severity and site of the fracture. Open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) remain the standard for managing complex fractures, as highlighted by Dayalan et al.[7] While minimally invasive techniques, including laser applications in surgery, show promise, their role in mandibular fracture management requires further exploration.[8]

Postoperative complications such as pain, swelling, and infection are common concerns in mandibular trauma management. Mazhar et al.[6] demonstrated the efficacy of preemptive analgesics such as ketorolac and tramadol in reducing postoperative discomfort. These findings underscore the importance of effective pain management strategies in enhancing patient recovery and satisfaction.

Overall, this study reinforces the significance of understanding the etiology, patterns, and management of mandibular fractures to optimize outcomes. Future research should explore advancements in minimally invasive techniques and their integration with conventional methods to improve treatment efficacy and reduce complications. Additionally, public awareness campaigns targeting road safety and personal protective measures can help reduce the prevalence of mandibular fractures, particularly in high-risk populations.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, mandibular fractures predominantly affect young males, with RTAs being the leading cause. The parasymphysis is the most frequently fractured site, and age-specific patterns of etiology are evident. Preventive strategies, enhanced public health policies, and advances in treatment can significantly reduce the burden of these injuries. Further research is warranted to explore regional variations and develop tailored prevention and management protocols. This study underscores the critical need for collaborative efforts among policymakers, healthcare providers, and communities to address the multifactorial causes of mandibular trauma effectively.
