Ruptured pheochromocytoma presenting with acute myocardial injury and hemoperitoneum
Authors: Fady Elias Hananias, Gonzalo Vera, Valeria Fernández, Iván Quevedo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPheochromocytoma is an uncommon neuroendocrine tumor that may present with highly variable and misleading clinical manifestations. We report the case of a 64-year-old woman with a history of hypertension who presented with acute chest pain, electrocardiographic changes, and marked elevation of cardiac biomarkers, initially suggestive of an acute coronary syndrome. Coronary angiography revealed no obstructive coronary disease. During hospitalization, the patient developed severe hemodynamic instability with alternating hypertensive crises and hypotension. Imaging studies identified a large heterogeneous right adrenal mass with high attenuation and associated hemoperitoneum. Biochemical evaluation demonstrated markedly elevated urinary metanephrines and normetanephrines, confirming the diagnosis of pheochromocytoma. The patient was stabilized medically and treated with preoperative α-adrenergic blockade using doxazosin, followed by successful surgical resection. Histopathological examination revealed extensive tumor necrosis with minimal viable tissue. Postoperatively, the patient had an uneventful recovery, with normalization of blood pressure and no recurrence of symptoms during follow-up. This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges posed by pheochromocytoma presenting as acute myocardial injury and complicated by spontaneous tumor rupture with hemoperitoneum.

Introduction

Pheochromocytoma is a neuroendocrine tumor arising from chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla and is characterized by excessive catecholamine production [1]. Although it is an uncommon cause of hypertension, its clinical relevance lies in the wide heterogeneity of its presentations and its potential to cause severe cardiovascular and metabolic complications [2]. Excessive catecholamine may lead to hypertensive crises, arrhythmias, myocardial injury, and cardiomyopathy [3, 4]. In some cases, these manifestations may mimic acute coronary syndrome, making the initial diagnosis particularly challenging.

Spontaneous rupture of pheochromocytoma is an extremely rare but life-threatening complication associated with intra-abdominal hemorrhage and hemodynamic instability [5, 6]. This condition requires prompt recognition and management as delayed diagnosis is associated with high mortality.

We report the case of a patient presenting with acute myocardial injury and hemoperitoneum secondary to a ruptured pheochromocytoma, highlighting the diagnostic challenges and therapeutic considerations in this uncommon clinical scenario.

Case presentation

We present the case of a 64-year-old woman with a history of hypertension diagnosed in 2017, treated with amlodipine, losartan, and hydrochlorothiazide, and type 2 diabetes mellitus and dyslipidemia diagnosed in 2019, managed with insulin neutral protamine Hagedorn 14 units nightly, metformin (850 mg every 8 hours), and atorvastatin (10 mg nightly), respectively. Her regular medications also included acetylsalicylic acid (100 mg daily) and omeprazole (20 mg daily). Her body mass index was 30.4 kg/m2, consistent with class I obesity. She presented to the emergency department with acute chest pain. The pain was sudden in onset, progressive, oppressive in nature, radiating to the upper abdomen, and associated with nausea, vomiting, and palpitations, with approximately 24 hours of evolution. No relevant family history or prior surgical procedures were reported.

On initial evaluation, the patient was tachycardic, with a heart rate of 125 beats per minute. Electrocardiography showed asymmetric T-wave inversions and ST-segment depression in the lateral leads (V4 to V6 and aVL), consistent with subendocardial ischemia (Fig. 1). An initial troponin test was positive, and the patient was transferred to a tertiary care center with suspicion of an acute coronary syndrome.

Upon hospital admission, elevation of cardiac biomarkers and D-dimer levels was confirmed. Computed tomography pulmonary angiography was negative for pulmonary embolism; however, a heterogeneous right adrenal mass measuring approximately 7 cm in diameter was incidentally identified on abdominal sections (Fig. 2). The patient was evaluated by the cardiology team and admitted to the intensive care unit for monitoring and management.

During hospitalization, she developed episodes of paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia and severe hypertensive crises, with blood pressure values exceeding 200/100 mmHg, accompanied by recurrent chest pain. She required high-flow nasal cannula oxygen therapy, intravenous diuretics, and continuous nitroglycerin infusion. Subsequently, the patient progressed to sustained hypotension requiring vasopressor support with norepinephrine.

Urgent coronary angiography revealed no obstructive coronary artery disease. Transthoracic echocardiography showed a mildly dilated left ventricle with hypertrophic walls and preserved left ventricular ejection fraction, estimated at 54%.

Diagnostic assessment

Given the clinical course and initial findings, further diagnostic evaluation was completed, including laboratory tests, imaging studies, and targeted cardiologic assessment.

Abdominal computed tomography with an adrenal protocol demonstrated an expansile, heterogeneous right adrenal mass measuring approximately 7 cm in diameter, with high attenuation (approximately 60 Hounsfield units on unenhanced computed tomography), well-defined margins, and poor lipid content. Contrast-enhanced imaging showed spontaneous contrast extravasation, along with the presence of free intra-abdominal fluid consistent with hemoperitoneum (Fig. 3).

Contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography demonstrating a well-defined, expansile right adrenal mass with heterogeneous density. A focal area of contrast extravasation (arrow) is observed within the lesion, consistent with active intratumoral hemorrhage.

Based on the imaging characteristics of the adrenal mass and the associated hemodynamic instability, functional evaluation was performed using 24-hour urinary metanephrines and normetanephrines. Results demonstrated markedly elevated metanephrines at 810 µg/24 hours (International System of Units [SI]: 4.1 µmol/24 hours) [reference range, 45 to 290 µg/24 hours (SI: 0.23 to 1.47 µmol/24 hours)] and normetanephrines at 14 900 µg/24 hours (SI: 81 µmol/24 hours) [reference range, 82 to 500 µg/24 hours (SI: 0.45 to 2.7 µmol/24 hours)], adjusted for urinary creatinine, confirming the biochemical diagnosis of pheochromocytoma.

Treatment

After biochemical confirmation of pheochromocytoma and stabilization of the patient's clinical condition, medical treatment with α-adrenergic blockade was initiated using doxazosin. The dose was progressively titrated up to 4 mg daily, with good clinical tolerance and adequate hemodynamic stability.

During the preoperative preparation period, the patient remained under close clinical monitoring, without recurrence of hypertensive crises or need for vasopressor support. Routine beta-adrenergic blockade was not required. Volume expansion was performed as tolerated.

Subsequently, the patient underwent right adrenalectomy via an open surgical approach, without intraoperative complications. The procedure was carried out after adequate medical preparation, with no anesthetic or hemodynamic adverse events.

Histopathological examination of the surgical specimen confirmed the diagnosis of pheochromocytoma, with extensive areas of necrosis and less than 5% viable tumor tissue.

Genetic evaluation was performed, identifying a variant of uncertain significance in the POLE gene (c.718G>A, p.Val240Met).

Outcome and follow-up

The patient had a favorable postoperative course without hemodynamic or metabolic complications during the immediate perioperative period. No hypertensive crises, arrhythmias, or need for vasopressor support were observed after right adrenalectomy.

During the postoperative hospitalization, blood pressure values remained within normal ranges, allowing for the progressive discontinuation of antihypertensive therapy. The patient experienced no further episodes of chest pain, remained clinically stable, and was discharged in good general condition.

At outpatient follow-up, the patient remained normotensive, with no recurrence of symptoms suggestive of excessive catecholamine. No late adverse events or complications related to surgical treatment were reported at the time of the last follow-up visit.

Discussion

This case illustrates an uncommon and clinically challenging presentation of pheochromocytoma, characterized by the coexistence of acute catecholamine-induced myocardial injury, which encompasses a broad spectrum of cardiac manifestations ranging from reversible stress cardiomyopathy to myocarditis-like injury, even in the absence of obstructive coronary artery disease [7, 8] and hemoperitoneum secondary to spontaneous tumor rupture. The combination of acute cardiovascular and abdominal manifestations complicated the initial recognition of the underlying endocrine etiology, underscoring the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in atypical clinical scenarios.

From a cardiovascular perspective, the patient presented with chest pain, electrocardiographic abnormalities, and significant elevation of cardiac biomarkers, prompting evaluation for an acute coronary syndrome. The absence of obstructive coronary artery disease on angiography, together with preserved left ventricular systolic function, supported the diagnosis of myocardial injury related to excessive catecholamine. This presentation is consistent with either type 2 myocardial infarction or a non-Takotsubo form of catecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy, entities that have been described in association with pheochromocytoma and other states of intense adrenergic discharge [8-10]

Catecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy encompasses a broad spectrum of reversible cardiac dysfunction and may occur in the absence of the classic echocardiographic features of Takotsubo syndrome. Proposed mechanisms include coronary vasospasm, microvascular dysfunction, direct catecholamine-mediated myocardial toxicity, calcium overload, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial injury [8-11].

In some cases, excessive catecholamine may also lead to a myocarditis-like presentation characterized by myocardial edema and focal injury, further supporting the heterogeneity of cardiac involvement in pheochromocytoma [12].

It should be noted that biochemical testing was performed during a period of significant physiological stress, which may influence catecholamine metabolite levels. However, the magnitude of elevation observed in this case strongly supports the diagnosis of pheochromocytoma [4]

In addition to cardiovascular involvement, the patient developed an exceptional complication: spontaneous rupture of the pheochromocytoma, associated with extensive tumor necrosis and hemoperitoneum. This condition has been reported only sporadically in the literature and is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, particularly when the diagnosis is delayed [5, 6]. Tumor rupture may result from sudden increases in intratumoral pressure, ischemic necrosis, or intralesional hemorrhage and can present with hypovolemic or mixed shock.

In addition to hemorrhagic and cardiac factors, the patient's hemodynamic instability may also have been influenced by the biochemical profile of the tumor. The markedly predominant elevation of normetanephrines reflects excessive norepinephrine secretion, which has been associated with pronounced hemodynamic variability in pheochromocytoma [3, 4]. In the setting of extensive tumor necrosis and possible intermittent catecholamine depletion, this secretory pattern may have contributed to episodes of hypotension, acting as a cofactor alongside hemoperitoneum and myocardial injury.

Management of such cases requires an individualized and stepwise approach. In hemodynamically unstable patients, prompt stabilization is essential, followed by functional evaluation of the adrenal mass once allowed by the clinical condition. Definitive surgical resection should be performed after adequate preoperative α-adrenergic blockade to minimize the risk of severe perioperative complications [13, 14].

In the present case, medical stabilization allowed for biochemical confirmation of pheochromocytoma and appropriate preoperative preparation prior to adrenalectomy. The histopathological finding of extensive tumor necrosis with a low proportion of viable tissue may have contributed to the favorable perioperative course; however, this observation should be interpreted cautiously, as available evidence is largely derived from isolated case reports.

Although no metastatic disease was identified, malignancy in pheochromocytoma is defined by the presence of metastases rather than histologic features; therefore, long-term follow-up is required.

Genetic testing identified a variant of uncertain significance in the POLE gene (c.718G>A, p.Val240Met). Pathogenic variants in POLE have been associated with polymerase proofreading–associated polyposis and increased cancer susceptibility. However, their role in pheochromocytoma is not established, and the clinical significance of this finding in the present case remains uncertain [15].

In conclusion, this case highlights that pheochromocytoma may present with misleading acute cardiovascular manifestations and can be complicated by life-threatening intra-abdominal hemorrhage due to tumor rupture. Early recognition and multidisciplinary management, with emphasis on hemodynamic stabilization and adequate preoperative preparation, are crucial to optimize clinical outcomes.
