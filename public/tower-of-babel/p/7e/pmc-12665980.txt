Afatinib-associated pseudo-acute kidney injury
Authors: Jacqueline Nguyen, Devika Das, Chintan V Shah
Published: 2025

To the Editor,

Afatinib, an irreversible epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), has proven effective in treating advanced non-small cell lung cancer with both common and uncommon EGFR mutations [1–3]. Common side effects of afatinib include diarrhea, paronychia, rash, and stomatitis/mucositis, with no significant reports of acute kidney injury (AKI). However, nearly half of the patients require a dose reduction.

We report a 50-year-old female with a medical history of thoracic endovascular aortic repair for both an abdominal aortic aneurysm and a thoracic aortic aneurysm, along with a markedly atrophic left kidney and advanced chronic kidney disease due to incomplete recovery from remote history of dialysis dependent AKI, with a baseline serum creatinine (S. Cr) level of 2.7 mg/dl. Home medications included prasugrel, nifedipine, metoprolol, clonidine, and atorvastatin. She was diagnosed with metastatic adenocarcinoma of the lung harboring uncommon compound mutations of EGFR (L833V and L838V) identified through next-generation sequencing. The patient was initiated on afatinib 40 mg daily per the sensitivity report. Within 3 weeks, her S. Cr increased to 3.4 mg/dl. Afatinib was held due to increased S. Cr and loose stools, leading to a decline in S. Cr to 2.7 mg/dl. Afatinib was then resumed at a reduced dose of 30 mg daily without any diarrhea; however, S. Cr increased again to 3.4 mg/dl. Further therapy was paused pending evaluation by onco-nephrology. No cause for worsening kidney function was identified during a comprehensive onco-nephrology assessment, including a normal appearance of the right kidney with an atrophic left kidney and minimal tubular proteinuria (urine protein creatinine ratio 0.5 gm/gm of creatinine) without hematuria, and pseudo-AKI was suspected. Cystatin C, a marker for kidney function, was 2.6 mg/l while off therapy, and decreased to 2.4 mg/l after 2 weeks of resuming afatinib 30 mg daily, while S. Cr again increased to 3.2 mg/dl, measured simultaneously. The patient was diagnosed with pseudo-AKI, and full-dose afatinib 40 mg daily was resumed (Fig. 1).

Changes in S. Cr and serum cystatin C levels with afatinib treatment.

Although dose reduction is often necessary, changes in S. Cr from baseline are not consistently reported across clinical trials. Of S. Cr, 10% to 40% is actively transported across proximal tubular epithelial cells by OCT2 on the basolateral membrane and by MATE1 and MATE2-K on the apical membrane in the kidneys [4]. Several TKIs can inhibit OCT2 and MATE1, leading to elevated S. Cr levels without affecting the glomerular filtration rate, a condition known as pseudo-AKI. ALK, MET, CDK4/6, PARP, BRAF, and BCR-ABL inhibitors have been reported to cause pseudo-AKI (Table 1) [5]. Therefore, we hypothesized and confirmed, using cystatin C-based measurements of kidney function, that the increase in S. Cr levels with afatinib could result from the inhibition of active tubular secretion of creatinine without an actual reduction in kidney function. Such an effect could lead to an incorrect diagnosis of drug-induced kidney injury and might result in dose reduction or discontinuation of the drug, especially in patients with lower baseline S. Cr, where a reciprocal decrease in estimated GFR with up to a 50% increase in S. Cr could be significantly greater.

Unlike creatinine, cystatin C is freely filtered by the glomerulus and is not secreted by renal transporters. Therefore, changes in the creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio, both off and on therapy, may help identify a pseudo-decrease in kidney function. This should be especially suspected when an increase of up to 50% in creatinine occurs after starting afatinib. Further studies are necessary to confirm the mechanism and prevalence of pseudo-AKI associated with EGFR-TKI.
