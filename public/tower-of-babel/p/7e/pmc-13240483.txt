Overcoming Cognitive Bias: A Case of Disseminated Neurocysticercosis in a Muslim Woman With Remote Pork Consumption
Authors: Joseph Y. B. Nnang, Berjo Takoutsing, Astel Dongmo, Jeanne Annick Tsilla, Barah Mvuchiim, Roshina Numfor, Munteh Fang Nkwojei Randall, Elvira Aka, Ignatius Esene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTReligious prohibitions against pork do not exclude individuals residing in endemic regions from contracting neurocysticercosis. Obtaining a detailed lifetime exposure history, including prior religious and dietary practices, is essential to overcome cognitive bias and prevent diagnostic delays.

Introduction

Infections of the central nervous system (CNS) stand out among other infections due to their greatest risk of morbidity and mortality. Among these is neurocysticercosis, a parasitic infestation caused by the larval stage of Taenia solium. It is considered the most common parasitic infestation of the CNS worldwide and is a common cause of new‐onset seizures or acquired epilepsy in low‐income countries, especially in endemic regions where pig farming is practiced [1]. In these regions, the diagnosis of neurocysticercosis often relies on clinical presentation, neuroimaging, and among all an exposure history. The clinical presentation and severity of symptoms of neurocysticercosis vary and depend on the location, number, and stage of neurocysticercoids, as well as the intensity of the generated inflammatory response [2, 3, 4]. This variability and some cases being asymptomatic especially at the early stages make encephalic neurocysticercosis a diagnostic challenge, even to physicians practicing in endemic zones [3].

Evidence‐based clinical practice recommends that the elaboration of the list of differential diagnoses be influenced by perceived risk factors, some of which are rooted in sociocultural or dietary behaviors and immunological status [3]. For example, because some religious practices, such as being a Muslim, do not allow the consumption of pork, cysticercosis is sometimes excluded as a potential diagnosis in immunocompetent individuals from this religion [5]. Although this is epidemiologically grounded, given that pork is the major vector for Tenia solium transmission, its premature exclusion from the differential diagnosis, especially in individuals residing in endemic regions could lead to delayed or missed diagnosis. This preconception may introduce common cognitive biases in clinical practice: premature closure, representativeness, and anchoring biases. Several recent studies show that cerebral neurocysticercosis is often underdiagnosed or diagnosed late in endemic areas. For example, Ojo et al. [6] reported a case of a Nigerian female diagnosed with cerebral neurocysticercosis only following histopathology examination of an excised cerebral mass, owing to an atypical presentation and low index of suspicion. As a result, these cognitive shortcuts can result in significant diagnostic delay and significant morbidity, particularly in individuals with complex personal histories [7].

We report a case of encephalitic neurocysticercosis in an immunocompetent female Muslim in an African setting. This case aims to highlight the importance of obtaining a comprehensive lifetime exposure history, avoiding cognitive bias, and exploring the variable latency of neurocysticercosis in an immunocompetent host. This case was reported in line with the Case Report guidelines [8].

Case Presentation

Clinical Presentation

We present the case of a female in her early 40s who identified as a Muslim, presenting with a 6‐month history of headaches. The headaches were generalized, intermittent, progressively worsening in intensity, with occasional exacerbations with activity and relieved by rest. This was associated with photophobia and blurred vision during each bout of headache. She also experienced four episodes of new‐onset generalized tonic–clonic seizures characterized by eye‐rolling without sphincter incontinence. Each seizure lasted approximately 1–2 min, with brief post‐ictal confusion. On physical examination, the patient was oriented in person, place, and time, with vital signs within normal ranges. There was neck stiffness, papilledema, dysarthria, left upper limb monoparesis, and ipsilateral hyperreflexia. A non‐contrast cerebral computed tomographic (CT) scan was obtained (Figure 1), revealing multiple disseminated intracranial circular lesions of different densities. Based on this, a detailed social history of the patient was revisited. The patient reported that she converted to Islam 8 years prior to the onset of symptoms. During her early adulthood, while of Christian faith, she resided near a pig farm and regularly consumed pork. This information fundamentally shifted the diagnostic probability.

Investigation and Diagnosis

A cerebral magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Figure 2) was subsequently performed, confirming disseminated cerebral neurocysticerci at different stages, with significant surrounding edema. Laboratory investigations included complete blood count and basic metabolic panel results, all of which were within normal ranges. A diagnostic lumbar puncture performed was remarkable for an elevated cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) opening pressure of 30 cm of H2O. Microscopic CSF analysis showed 2 white blood cells/mm3, zero blood cells, no bacteria identified on culture and gram stain, proteinorachia of 26 mg/dL levels, glycorrhachia of 70 mg/dL and a CSF to serum glucose ratio of 0.77. These findings made the diagnosis of tuberculous, bacterial meningitis, non‐infectious inflammatory pathology or malignancy less likely. Human immunodeficiency virus serology was conducted and yielded negative results. Immunologic testing for cysticercosis via enzyme‐linked immunoelectrotransfer blot was not available at our facility. A definitive diagnosis of encephalitic neurocysticercosis was retained. This was based on the chronic presentation, the clinical syndrome of intracranial hypertension and focal deficit, characteristic neuroimaging findings, and a history of prior exposure residing in an endemic zone.

Axial sequence of T2‐Weighted Brain MRI showing disseminated small and circular hyperintensities; massive parenchymal neurocysticercosis.

Treatment

The decision was made to start a multi‐targeted empiric treatment with a plan for an eventual brain biopsy if there was no change in her signs, symptoms, or imaging findings. The patient was counseled regarding the disease and treatment options, and a treatment plan was initiated. Praziquantel 600 mg tablet, at a dose of 50 mg/kg/day orally divided in three doses for 30 days, was administered to target the larval stage of the parasite. Additionally, prednisolone was started at a dose of 1 mg/kg/day for 10 days to reduce inflammation and manage intracranial hypertension. Deparatization with albendazole at 400 mg tablet twice daily for a duration of 10 days was also implemented with the aim of enhancing systemic clearance. Throughout the treatment period, the patient was closely monitored for adverse effects on an outpatient basis.

Follow‐Up and Outcome

Following the initiation of treatment, the patient's symptoms gradually improved. A repeat cerebral CT scan done after completion of the instituted treatment showed a decrease in the size and number of the cystic lesions and a reduction in the perilesional edema (Figure 3). At the three‐month follow‐up, the patient continued to remain seizure‐free; the headaches and the focal neurological deficits resolved completely.

Discussion

This case of encephalitic neurocysticercosis in an immunocompetent Muslim woman underscores diagnostic pitfalls related to cognitive bias in low‐resource settings secondary to diagnostic assumptions. Firstly, that religious dietary prohibitions reliably exclude the diagnosis, and secondly, that prolonged latency periods diminish the relevance of remote exposures. Additionally, the management of this patient in a low‐resource setting further underscores the importance of high‐level clinical reasoning and a meticulous exposure history.

Neurocysticercosis is a major source of neurological morbidity, especially in endemic areas. While it is common with immunocompromised people, this instance demonstrates its occurrence in an immunocompetent patient, something that has been found intermittently in the literature. Despite this condition being common in sub‐Saharan Africa, which is considered an endemic zone, it is rarely reported among Muslims, as in our case [9, 10]. This is due to their religion preventing worshipers from consuming pork [5]. This association can negatively introduce cognitive bias, prompting clinicians to prematurely exclude neurocysticercosis from the differential diagnosis in Muslims patients even in endemic zones. Our case clearly illustrates this pitfall. The crucial diagnostic clue was exposed by diving deep into the patient's remote social history, revealing her pork consumption, proximity to a pig farm, and Christian upbringing, all happening about 8 years prior to symptom onset. This underscores the importance of incorporating epidemiological trends together with past social and dietary practices regardless of current religious affiliations in adult medicine clinical decision making.

The estimated eight‐year interval between presumed exposure and onset of symptoms in this patient highlights the variable and prolonged incubation period of neurocysticercosis. The transmission could have occurred through the consumption of improperly cooked pork infested with cysticerci or ingestion of embryonated eggs following the consumption of food, fruits, vegetables, or water contaminated by human feces, feco‐oral autoinfections among Taenia solium carriers inclusive [11]. As a result, this could have resulted in the implantation of the cysticerci in the brain at different periods. In most immunocompetent hosts, the robust immune response limits the number of cysts, which degenerate asynchronously and produce focal symptoms with a shorter latency. The prolonged latency and dissemination in this case can be as a result of the cysticerci‐driven immunomodulation induced by the cyst secretory products. This induces a Th2/T Regulatory cell skewing, preventing the induction of a cytokine‐mediated Th1 cell inflammatory response. The later spontaneous or induced breakdown of this established host–parasite equilibrium precipitates a switch to Th1 pro‐inflammatory +/− Th2 response leading to widespread cysticerci degeneration leading to the later disseminated encephalitic presentation [11].

Seizure is the most prevalent clinical manifestation occurring in the majority of the cases, followed by focal deficit, intracranial hypertension, and headache [2]. These features were present in this patient, starting with a mild headache that accentuated over time and later became associated with new‐onset seizures, papilledema, and a focal deficit. The clinical presentation, CT scan, and MRI findings, and environmental context led to the diagnosis of neurocysticercosis in our patient. There is no gold standard test to diagnose neurocysticercosis, and management recommendations are still emerging. However, novel diagnostic tools such as immunoelectrotransfer blots and enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assays are scarce in low‐resource settings [2]. Hence, a detailed history, laying good emphasis on the past history of the patients, together with a thorough physical examination and making use of available neuroimaging, can help establish a diagnosis.

The advantage of MRI over CT is that it eliminates the associated radiation risk while providing better parenchymal visualization, and MRI is better in the diagnosis of other intra‐ and extra‐parenchymal forms [2, 10]. On the other hand, CT can better delineate the dead or calcified forms of intraparenchymal neurocysticercosis than MRI. In our case, we did both neuroimaging studies before reaching a diagnosis. Cysticercosis is usually indicated by the presence of a scolex within a cystic lesion [2, 9]. The absence of the scolex in neuroimaging studies should prompt a search for another cause. In developing countries, the most important differential diagnosis for neurocysticercosis is tuberculoma, as both have a similar appearance on radiologic findings. Other differential diagnoses include pyogenic cerebral abscess, cerebral toxoplasmosis, cryptococcal meningitis, cerebral amebiasis, and brain tumor metastasis [2, 3, 9, 10]. The immunocompetent status of our patient, the past history of exposure to pig products, and neuroimaging studies made cerebral neurocysticercosis the most likely diagnosis.

The treatment of neurocysticercosis involves the use of multiple agents, which include antiparasitic, antiinflammatory, and antiseizure drugs [2, 9, 10]. Also, the treatment is individualized and depends on the location, number, and stage of cysticerci. In our case, the patient had multiple cysticerci at various stages disseminated throughout the brain parenchyma. Dual therapy with Albendazole and Praziquantel is recommended for the management of more than two viable cysts with significant mass effect or inflammation for a period of 10–14 days [3]. Also, corticosteroids should be given to treat any associated inflammation or cerebral edema causing intracranial hypertension. The decision to use Praziquantel as monotherapy over an extended period of 30 days in this case was an individualized treatment strategy tailored to a high disease burden in a resource‐limited setting. Current Infectious Diseases Society of America and the American Society of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene recommend aggressive dual‐therapy regimens for multiple viable cysts over a shorter duration [4]. This implementation may be constrained by drug availability, monitoring capacity, and concerns regarding treatment‐induced inflammatory response. In this context, extended praziquantel therapy was selected to allow a more gradual antigen release and hence reduce the risk of inflammation. This also ensured the degeneration of asynchronously developing cysts with prolonged treatment while providing a controlled environment for the associated inflammatory response and intracranial hypertension, which was managed concurrently with prednisolone which also mitigated other possible side effects of Praziquantel. Furthermore, the subsequent use of Albendazole served as a comprehensive systemic deparasitization strategy to eliminate potential extra‐axial or systemic larval reservoirs, ensuring a holistic therapeutic outcome in an endemic setting. We emphasize that this approach does not replace guideline‐recommended regimens but reflects pragmatic adaptation to local constraints and patient‐specific disease characteristics.

At 3 month follow‐up, the patient was asymptomatic, suggesting an effective response to the administered treatment. However, this relatively short follow‐up period represents a limitation of this report. This limits assessment of possible long‐term outcomes such as seizure recurrence, late‐onset epilepsy, neurocognitive impairment, or recurrence [1, 4]. Given these known risks of delayed complications in neurocysticercosis, long‐term follow‐up is warranted in clinical practice and extended longitudinal follow‐up is essential and should be emphasized in future studies.

Conclusions

Encephalitic neurocysticercosis is a critical differential diagnosis for any patient presenting with new‐onset seizures in endemic regions, regardless of their current immunologic or religious status. This case demonstrates that overcoming cognitive biases, specifically premature closure and representativeness bias, requires a meticulous lifetime exposure history that accounts for remote dietary practices and prior religious affiliations. Furthermore, the management of disseminated or massive parenchymal involvement may necessitate a more prolonged anthelmintic treatment supported by corticosteroids followed by systemic deparasitization. Monitoring the evolution of clinical signs and neuroimaging findings remains the most vital tool for evaluating favorable treatment responses in low‐resource settings.
