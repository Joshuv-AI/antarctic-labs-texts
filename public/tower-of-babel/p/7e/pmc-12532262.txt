Evaluating the Impact of a Smartphone Dental Application vs a Kaleidoscope on Mitigating Childhood Dental Anxiety during the Administration of Local Anesthesia
Authors: Lumbini Pathivada, Bhumika Sahu, Nishita Garg, Karthik M Krishna, Sayani Roy, Manpreet Kaur
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study aimed to compare the effectiveness of a mobile dental application and a kaleidoscope in reducing dental anxiety among children aged 6–9 years receiving local anesthesia (LA) during dental procedures.MethodologyIn this clinical study, 48 healthy children requiring invasive dental treatment were divided into two groups: the mobile dental app group (group I) and the kaleidoscope group (group II). Group I used the “Little Lovely Dentist” app before receiving LA, while group II viewed various designs through a kaleidoscope prior to anesthesia administration. Anxiety levels were assessed using physiological measures [pulse rate, oxygen saturation (SpO2), and respiratory rate] and subjective measures [facial image scale and Wong–Baker faces pain rating scale (WBFPS)] at preoperative and postoperative time points.ResultsThe kaleidoscope group demonstrated statistically significant improvements in all measured parameters compared to the dental app group postintervention (p = 0.000). Group II exhibited decreased pulse and respiratory rates, increased SpO2, and reduced pain scores, while group I showed slight increases in pain scores and significant increases in respiratory and pulse rates.ConclusionThe findings suggest that using a kaleidoscope as a distraction technique is more effective than a mobile dental application in alleviating dental anxiety and pain in children during the administration of LA.How to cite this articlePathivada L, Sahu B, Garg N, et al. Introduction

The fear of pain during dental procedures is a common cause of dental anxiety. Dental anxiety encompasses various physical, psychological, and social aspects. It has been described as a “vague, unpleasant feeling accompanied by a premonition that something undesirable is about to happen.”1 In the context of dental fear and anxiety (DFA), dental anxiety refers to significant negative emotions about dental offices and procedures, while dental phobia is an irrational form of this anxiety.2 Although often used interchangeably, “dental fear” and “dental anxiety” relate to different stages of the same psychological state. The global prevalence of dental anxiety and fear among children and adolescents ranges from 3 to 53%.3

Effective pain management is crucial for fostering positive dentist–child relationships and enhancing children's satisfaction and compliance with dental treatments. Local anesthesia (LA) is widely used for pain control in pediatric dentistry. However, despite its benefits in reducing discomfort, it can increase DFA, highlighting the need for behavioral management techniques during children's dental procedures.4

A kaleidoscope is a device that displays an endless array of captivating geometric patterns resembling flowers, created by repeating and reflecting images of colored fragments in a mirrored prism.5 The frequency of eye blinks rises as tasks become more challenging and stressful. Conversely, during calming activities such as observing a kaleidoscope, the rate of blinking decreases, which helps regulate overall physiological arousal. Although kaleidoscopes are increasingly recognized for their therapeutic benefits in medical environments, there is a scarcity of research regarding their use in pediatric dental care.

Current studies on mobile applications for behavior guidance have advanced to include virtual reality immersion. This technology shows promise as a distraction method for pediatric patients, enabling them to become familiar with the dental environment while maintaining effective communication between the child and dental professional.6

Therefore, this research aims to assess and contrast the efficacy of a mobile dental application and a kaleidoscope in reducing dental anxiety among pediatric patients receiving LA. The study seeks to determine which method is more effective in alleviating fear and stress in children undergoing dental procedures.

Methodology

This was a randomized, parallel-group study comparing the effectiveness of a mobile dental app vs a kaleidoscope for anxiety reduction in pediatric dental patients. The methodology was prepared according to Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines. The study included healthy children aged 6–9 years requiring invasive dental procedures at the department of pediatric and preventive dentistry. Inclusion criteria were parental consent and Frankl's behavior rating scale scores of positive (+) or above. Exclusion criteria encompassed children with special healthcare needs, allergies to LA, peripheral vascular disease, Raynaud's disease, or urticaria. Prior to commencing the research, approval was obtained from the institutional review board. With a statistical power of 95%, the required sample size was determined to be 48.

A total of 48 children were randomly divided into two groups using a simple random sampling technique. Each child selected a colored paper chit from an opaque bag, determining whether they would be in the mobile dental app group or the kaleidoscope group.

Due to the nature of the interventions, full blinding of participants and intervention administrators was not possible. However, outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation when collecting physiological and subjective measures. The data analyst was also blinded to group assignments until after the analysis was completed.

In group I, children used the “Little Lovely Dentist” app to learn about prophylactic cleaning. They were encouraged to virtually perform dental treatments as a dentist using the app (Fig. 1). Following this conditioning, LA was administered. Group II children were instructed to view through a kaleidoscope with one eye while rotating a cylinder, causing the designs to shift as the beads moved. Children observed various designs before receiving LA (Fig. 2).

Primary outcomes were anxiety levels, measured using: (1) physiological measures: pulse rate, oxygen saturation (SpO2), and respiratory rate (via pulse oximeter); (2) subjective measure: facial image scale. Secondary outcome was pain, assessed using the Wong–Baker faces pain rating scale (WBFPS) (Fig. 3). Measurements were taken at two time points: preoperative and postoperative.

Statistical Analysis

The data obtained in the present study were compiled and organized systematically and entered into Microsoft Excel spreadsheet 2016. The obtained data were analyzed using Statistical Package for social Sciences (SPSS) version 22.0, Windows, SPSS Inc., New York, USA. To compare pre- and postintervention pulse rate, SpO2, and respiratory rate between groups, an independent t-test was employed. Within-group comparisons were made using paired t-tests. Additionally, postoperative facial image scale and WBFPS scores were evaluated between groups using independent t-tests. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Discussion

Children's oral health-related quality of life is negatively affected by DFA, which also often results in longer treatment times. This makes DFA a crucial concern in dentistry, particularly when dealing with pediatric patients.2 Anxiety reduction strategies known as distraction techniques work by overwhelming the patient's limited attention capacity, thereby redirecting their focus away from uncomfortable procedures (noxious stimuli). These techniques can be categorized as either active or passive.7 Active methods involve the child's direct participation, such as engaging with toys and games. In contrast, passive methods, which include listening to music or watching videos, do not require the child's direct involvement.8,9 The use of distraction appears to be a secure approach that can positively influence children's DFA, consequently enhancing the quality of dental care provided.

The choice between active and passive techniques may depend on factors, such as the child's age, preferences, and the specific dental procedure being performed. Additionally, these strategies can be tailored to individual patients, potentially incorporating elements of both active and passive distraction to maximize their effectiveness.

Furthermore, the implementation of distraction techniques in pediatric dentistry may have long-term benefits beyond the immediate reduction of anxiety. By creating positive experiences during dental visits, these strategies could help children develop a more favorable attitude towards oral health care, potentially leading to better dental hygiene practices and reduced anxiety in future appointments.

In today's digital age, children across all age groups engage with mobile devices for various purposes, including gaming, knowledge acquisition, and entertainment. Research by Panchal et al.10 demonstrated that employing a smartphone application to evaluate and mitigate caries risk led to substantial improvements in participants' dietary habits and oral hygiene practices. The ongoing exploration of mobile apps for behavior guidance has given rise to virtual reality immersion, a promising distraction technique. This approach enables children to familiarize themselves with the dental environment while fostering effective communication between the young patient and the clinician. An example of such an application is “Little Lovely Dentist,” developed by Leaf Cottage software and Shanghai Edaysoft Co., Ltd., available on both Google Play Store and App Store. This app serves as an educational tool, playfully introducing children to various dental procedures, such as prophylactic cleanings, pit and fissure sealants, restorations, and extractions, while emphasizing the significance of oral hygiene maintenance, particularly interdental cleaning.6,11

A kaleidoscope is a vibrant toy that uses patterns to engage children. The ever-changing designs within a kaleidoscope are produced by light reflections inside the device.5 The structure consists of two mirrors positioned at 60° angles adjacent to each other. Various objects, such as colored glass fragments, feathers, and beads, are placed between these mirrors inside the kaleidoscope. When viewed through one end of this optical instrument, one can observe irregular polygons and other shapes that are typically asymmetrical.12

This study sought to evaluate and compare the effectiveness of a mobile dental application vs a kaleidoscope in alleviating dental anxiety among children receiving LA during dental procedures. The findings of the current investigation demonstrated that the kaleidoscope group was more effective in decreasing pulse rate, respiratory rate, and pain, while also improving SpO2, compared to the dental app group.

The release of stress hormones, such as cortisol, adrenaline, and norepinephrine, into the bloodstream triggers physiological changes associated with fear and anxiety.13 These changes include elevated perspiration, respiration rate, blood pressure, heart rate, and pulse rate. In dentistry, a noninvasive method called pulse oximetry is commonly employed to monitor these physiological alterations. This technique enables real-time measurement of various parameters, including blood pressure, pulse rate, SpO2, and body temperature.14 Dentists who treat children need to keep a close eye on certain measurements during dental procedures. This helps them manage the treatment better and be ready for any problems that might occur. By watching these important signs, the dental team can provide safer and more effective care for young patients.

Dental procedures can cause an increase in heart rate due to stress, which serves as a direct indicator of physiological arousal. Children experiencing their initial dental visit are likely to exhibit fear or anxiety in response to dental stimuli. As a result, utilizing a finger pulse oximeter to monitor heart rate provides an objective method for evaluating a child's anxiety level.15 The research findings demonstrated a notable contrast in heart rate changes between the two groups examined. While participants in the kaleidoscope group experienced a significant reduction in heart rate, those in the mobile app group showed a marked increase. The results are consistent with the previous research conducted by Anchala et al.,16 but they contradict the findings of Elicherla et al.,6 who observed a notable decrease in heart rate among children using the dental app.

According to Gandy,17 this equipment plays a crucial role in assessing blood oxygen levels and pulse rate and serves as an efficient method for detecting hypoxia in dental practices during the administration of LA. He highlighted its affordability and ease of use. This research found contrasting results between the kaleidoscope and mobile app groups. The kaleidoscope group experienced an increase in SpO2 and a notable decrease in respiratory rate. Conversely, the mobile app group showed the opposite trend. These findings suggest that the kaleidoscope intervention may have had a calming effect on participants, potentially reducing stress and promoting relaxation, which could explain the improved SpO2 and decreased respiratory rate. In contrast, the mobile app intervention might have induced a more stimulating or engaging experience, possibly leading to increased arousal and consequently affecting physiological measures in the opposite direction.

In pediatric patients, methods relying on self-reported pain are typically regarded as the most reliable approach for pain measurement, as they provide the most accurate assessment.18 The current investigation utilized the WBFPS, a pain scale employing facial images, which is considered one of the most fundamental tools for evaluating pain in pediatric dentistry.19 This scale was used to conduct pain assessments in the study. In the current study, the pain scores of group I were significantly lower than those of group II.

Studies have shown the effectiveness of kaleidoscopes as a distraction method for reducing pain perception in children during medical procedures. Karakaya and Gozen20 found that kaleidoscopes successfully decreased perceived pain in 7–12-year-olds during blood draws. In a similar vein, Kunjumon and Upendrababu21 observed that kaleidoscopes were beneficial in managing pain for children aged 4–6 undergoing intravenous cannulation. Canbulat et al.22 demonstrated that both distraction cards and kaleidoscopes were more effective than no intervention in minimizing perceived pain among 7–11-year-old children. Additionally, research by Prajapati23 indicated that kaleidoscopes outperformed standard care in alleviating pain during blood draws. Tüfekçi Güdücü et al.24 also concluded that the kaleidoscope technique was successful in reducing pain experienced by school-aged children during blood collection procedures.

A study by Karakaya and Gözen20 examined the impact of distraction on pain levels in children aged 7–12 undergoing venipuncture. Their findings revealed that for children in the mixed dentition stage, using a kaleidoscope as a distraction method was more effective than in older age groups. This result aligns with the findings of the current study. Vessey et al.25 reported comparable results in their study. They examined the efficacy of a distraction method in alleviating children's perceived pain and behavioral distress during an acute painful experience. The study focused on children aged 3–12 years who were scheduled for routine blood draws. The researchers concluded that the kaleidoscope was adequately novel and engaged multiple sensory inputs, thereby eliciting a high level of intentional capacity in the young participants.

The findings of this research indicate that utilizing a dental application as an educational tool for children was less effective in reducing anxiety and pain compared to using a kaleidoscope as a distraction technique, as evidenced by both physiological and self-reported measures. These outcomes contradict the conclusions drawn by Elicherla et al.,6 Patil et al.,26 Shah et al.,27 and Lee et al.,28 who argued that involving children with smartphone apps could be an efficient distraction method for managing behavior, and suggested that smartphones were a modest yet effective tool for decreasing preoperative anxiety in pediatric patients.

The discrepancy between the findings of this study and previous research highlights the complexity of managing anxiety and pain in pediatric dental patients. While smartphone apps have shown promise in some studies, the multisensory and novel nature of the kaleidoscope may provide a more engaging and effective distraction for young children in dental settings. This research underscores the importance of considering age-appropriate and context-specific interventions for pediatric patients.

Future Direction

Future studies could explore the factors that contribute to the effectiveness of different distraction techniques, such as the level of interactivity, sensory engagement, and novelty, to develop more targeted approaches for reducing anxiety and pain in children during dental procedures and conduct a larger-scale study with a more diverse and representative sample to enhance generalizability.

The results suggest that using kaleidoscopes in dental offices could be a straightforward and efficient method to improve patient relaxation and compliance. Nevertheless, it is important to be cautious when applying these findings broadly, as individual reactions to anxiety and preferences for distraction may differ. This research is unique in its direct comparison of a conventional visual distraction tool with a contemporary digital application within a pediatric dental environment.

Limitations

Small sample size: The study only included 48 participants, which limits the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. A larger sample size would provide more robust results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the study demonstrated that the use of a kaleidoscope was more effective than a mobile dental application in reducing dental anxiety among children, as evidenced by significant improvements in physiological parameters, such as pulse rate, respiratory rate, pain levels, and SpO2. Future research should explore the long-term effects of using a kaleidoscope for dental anxiety management and investigate its potential applications in other pediatric medical procedures.
