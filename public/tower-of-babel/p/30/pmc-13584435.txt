Current use of modern contraceptives and associated factors among women in Lesotho: evidence from the recent Lesotho demographic and health survey 2023–2024
Authors: Hiwot Altaye Asebe, Abdu Hailu Shibeshi, Temesgen Gebeyehu Wondmeneh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOne of the most important methods for reducing fertility and improving maternal health is the use of modern contraceptives. However, modern contraceptives were not widely used in developing countries, including Lesotho. Thus, this study aimed to determine the magnitude of modern contraceptive utilization and associated factors among women in Lesotho.MethodsThe 2023–2024 Lesotho Demographic and Health Survey data were used for analysis. The study comprised 6,413 women of reproductive age from 400 enumeration areas in 10 districts. A multilevel mixed-effect binary logistic regression model was built to identify individual and community-level factors associated with modern contraceptive utilization. Significant risk factors were determined by odds ratio with 95% CI along with p-value < 0.05.ResultsAround half (53%) of women used modern contraceptive methods. Increased odds of modern contraceptive use were observed among women aged 25–34 years (AOR = 2.1, 95% CI: 1.8–2.4) and 35–49 years (AOR = 1.9, 95% CI: 1.6–2.1), those with primary (AOR = 2.6, 95% CI: 1.5–4.7), secondary (AOR = 2.9, 95% CI: 1.6–5.2), and higher education (AOR = 3.0, 95% CI: 1.6–5.5), recently sexually active women (AOR = 2.1, 95% CI: 1.9–2.4), those with one (AOR = 1.88, 95% CI: 1.66–2.1) or ≥ 2 under-five children (AOR = 2.3, 95% CI: 1.9–2.9), and women from communities with high knowledge (AOR = 1.3, 95% CI: 1.13–1.6). In contrast, decreased odds were found among unmarried women (AOR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.54–0.70), those from rich households (AOR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.63–0.92), and women in households with ≥6 members (AOR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.77–0.99). Lower odds were also observed among women residing in Leribe, Berea, Maseru, Mafeteng, Qacha’s Nek, Mokhotlong, and Thaba-Tseka compared to Butha-Buthe.ConclusionAround half of women use modern contraceptive methods. Utilization is influenced by socio-demographic factors such as age, education, sexual activity, number of under-five children, household size, marital status, and wealth. In several districts, modern contraceptive use is lower. Raising community awareness remains key to improving uptake.Keywords: Use, Modern contraceptives, Women, Lesotho, Demographic and health survey

Introduction

Family planning is defined as the ability of individuals and couples to achieve their desired number of children and determine the spacing and timing of pregnancies [1]. It plays a crucial role in reducing maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality by preventing unintended pregnancies and lowering the risks associated with high-risk pregnancies [2]. Moreover, family planning contributes to improve maternal and child health outcomes by reducing fertility rates and household size [3].

Despite these benefits, the uptake of postpartum modern contraceptives remains suboptimal in many developing countries, largely due to high prevalence of home deliveries [4, 5]. Globally, in 2021, approximately 1.1 billion women of reproductive age (15–49 years) required family planning services; of these, 874 million were using modern contraceptive methods, while 164 million still had an unmet need [6]. In Yemen, 38% of married childbearing women used any form of contraception, and the magnitude of modern contraceptive utilization was 32.8% [7]. Modern contraceptive use in Africa remains low, with an overall prevalence of 26%, ranging from as low as 6% in Guinea to 62% in Zimbabwe [8]. In sub-Saharan Africa, the magnitude of modern contraceptive use ranged from 18.4% [9] to 29.6% [10, 11], while it is estimated at 45.7% in East Africa [12] and 16.6% in West Africa [13]. Modern contraceptive utilization in Ethiopia ranged from 28% [14, 15] to 37% [16]. According to a study in Sierra Leonean, the percentage of women who use modern contraceptives increased from 6.7% in 2008 to 20.9% in 2019 [17]. In Zambia, 12% of adolescent girls used modern contraceptives [18]. In a district-level study in South Africa, 84% of women were using contraceptive methods [19].

Several individual- and community-level factors influence modern contraceptive utilization. Women’s empowerments, joint decision-making with partners, exposure to media, and awareness of modern contraceptives have been shown to increase contraceptive use [10, 14, 20–23]. Additionally, age, parity, socioeconomic status, place of residence, access to health facilities, and a history of unintended pregnancy are important factors [8, 12, 14, 23–25]. However, these factors often vary across communities, highlighting the importance of considering both individual and contextual influences.

Although previous studies in Lesotho have explored contraceptive use among specific groups, such as adolescents and selected sub-populations [26, 27], there is limited nationally representative evidence that simultaneously examines both individual- and community-level determinants of modern contraceptive utilization. Furthermore, earlier studies have not adequately accounted for the hierarchical structure of population-based survey data.

Therefore, this study aimed to determine the prevalence of modern contraceptive utilization and identify its associated factors among women of reproductive age in Lesotho using nationally representative data. By applying a multilevel analytical approach, this study accounts for both individual- and community-level variations. The findings will provide robust evidence to inform policymakers and healthcare providers in designing targeted interventions, improving resource allocation, and strengthening family planning programs to enhance maternal and child health outcomes.

Methodology

Study design and setting

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted using data from the 2023–24 Lesotho Demographic and Health Survey (LDHS), implemented by the Lesotho Ministry of Health (MoH) [28]. The LDHS is a nationally representative survey conducted periodically to collect data on population health indicators. This is the fourth DHS conducted in Lesotho, following surveys in 2004, 2009, and 2014. Lesotho is administratively divided into ten districts: Butha-Buthe, Leribe, Berea, Maseru, Mafeteng, Mohale’s Hoek, Quthing, Qacha’s Nek, Mokhotlong, and Thaba-Tseka. These districts include urban, peri-urban, and rural settings. Data collection was conducted from November 27, 2023, to February 29, 2024.

Sample design and selection

The LDHS employed a two-stage stratified cluster sampling design based on the 2016 Population and Housing Census. In the first stage, 400 enumeration areas (EAs) were selected with probability proportional to size across 29 sampling strata. In the second stage, 25 households per cluster were selected using systematic sampling. A total of 10,000 households were selected, of which 9,810 were successfully interviewed (response rate > 99%). Among these households, 6,536 eligible women aged 15–49 years were identified, and 6,413 completed the interviews (response rate: 98.1%). Out of these, 69 were excluded due to missing key variables, leaving a total of 6,344 women in the final analysis. To ensure national representativeness and correct for unequal probabilities of selection and nonresponse, sampling weights (v005) were applied during the analysis. All estimates were generated using weighted data, accounting for the complex survey design, including clustering and stratification [28].

Data sources

Data were obtained from the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) program after formal registration and approval. The women’s individual recode (IR) dataset was used. Information on modern contraceptive use was collected through face-to-face interviews with women aged 15–49 years.

Study variable

Dependent variable

The outcome of interest in this study was the current use of modern contraceptives among women aged 15–49 years. Modern contraceptive utilization is categorized as “1” for those who used at least one of the following methods: Female sterilization, male sterilization, the contraceptive pill, intrauterine contraceptive device, injectable (Depo-Provera, DMPA-SC/Sayana Press, and other short-term injectables available in Lesotho), implants (Norplant, Implanon, Implanon NXT, Jadelle), male condom, female condom, diaphragm, contraceptive foam, and contraceptive jelly female condom, lactation amenorrhea method, standard days method, emergency contraception, and country-specific modern methods and other modern contraceptive methods respondents mentioned. Reproductive-age women who did not use modern contraceptives were considered those with no use or traditional methods (periodic abstinence, withdrawal, country-specific traditional methods of proven effectiveness, and folk methods).

Independent variables

Individual- and community-level variables were selected based on prior empirical studies and established conceptual frameworks on contraceptive utilization in low- and middle-income countries [8, 12, 14, 23–25]. These studies consistently highlight socio-demographic, economic, and contextual factors as key predictors of modern contraceptive use. Therefore, the selection of variables in this study was theory-driven rather than exploratory and was further guided by the availability of standardized variables in the DHS dataset. In addition, categories and thresholds for variables were determined based on either the distribution of the data (e.g., median split for community-level variables) or established classifications used in previous DHS-based studies, ensuring both methodological consistency and comparability with existing literature.

Individual-level variables included women’s age, marital status, sexual activity, knowledge of modern contraceptives, sex of the household head, household size, number of under-five children, educational attainment, husband/partner’s educational level, wealth tertiles, religion, and media exposure. Women’s age was categorized into 15–24, 25–34, and 35–49 years, consistent with reproductive age groupings commonly used in DHS studies. Marital status was recoded into two categories: married (including those married and living with a partner) and non-married (including those never in unions, widowed, divorced, or separated). Recent sexual activity was derived from the DHS variable v536, which classifies respondents into four categories: never had sex, sexually active in the last four weeks, not active in the last four weeks (postpartum), and not active in the last four weeks (not postpartum). For this study, it was recoded into a binary variable: respondents who were sexually active in the last four weeks were classified as active, while all others were classified as not active. Religion was categorized as Christian and others. Household size was classified as 1–5 and ≥6 members, following prior DHS-based studies to reflect differences in household structure and resource constraints. This variable was included alongside the number of under-five children because it captures broader household composition beyond childbearing alone. The number of under-five children was categorized as none, one, and two or more. Educational status of women and their husbands/partners was categorized as no education, primary, secondary, or higher education, following standard DHS classifications. Media exposure was constructed from the frequency of reading newspapers, listening to the radio, and watching television, and categorized as exposed versus not exposed based on access to at least one media source. The DHS wealth index was reclassified into wealth tertiles (poor, middle, and rich) by combining the poorest and poorer categories as “poor” and the richer and richest categories as “rich,” to improve interpretability and ensure adequate sample size within categories.

Community-level variables included district, residence (urban, peri-urban, rural), distance to a health facility, community literacy, community knowledge of modern contraceptives, community wealth, and community media exposure. Community-level variables were either directly obtained or constructed by aggregating individual-level characteristics at the cluster level. Residence and distance to a health facility were obtained directly from the DHS dataset. Distance to a health facility is recorded as a binary variable (a big problem vs. not a big problem) based on respondents’ perceptions. Although measured at the individual level, it was analyzed as a community-level variable because access to health services is largely influenced by shared geographic and infrastructural conditions within clusters. Aggregated community-level variables (community literacy, community knowledge, community wealth, and community media exposure) were computed by calculating cluster-level proportions and were categorized into low and high using median values as cut-off points, based on their distribution in the study sample. Community-level knowledge of modern contraceptives was derived by aggregating the individual-level binary variable (knowledge: yes/no) at the cluster level. For each cluster, the proportion of women who reported knowledge of at least one modern contraceptive method was calculated and then dichotomized using the median value. This approach captures relative differences in the concentration of knowledge across communities, even when individual-level variation is limited. Similarly, community wealth was derived from aggregated household wealth tertiles at the cluster level and categorized into low and high based on the median distribution, providing a relative measure of the socioeconomic context of communities.

Data management and analysis

Data were extracted, cleaned, and analyzed using STATA version 17.0. Sampling weights were applied to adjust for the complex survey design and ensure nationally representative estimates. Given the hierarchical structure of DHS data, where individuals are nested within clusters (enumeration areas), a multilevel logistic regression model was employed [29, 30]. This approach accounts for intra-cluster correlation and provides more accurate estimates by adjusting for the non-independence of observations within clusters. In addition, modern contraceptive use is influenced not only by individual characteristics but also by contextual (community-level) factors such as access to health services, socioeconomic environment, and shared norms. Therefore, incorporating both individual- and community-level variables allows for a more comprehensive assessment of determinants and helps disentangle the relative contribution of each level. A bivariable multilevel logistic regression analysis was first performed for all potential independent variables. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were selected and entered into the multivariable multilevel logistic regression model. Variables with a p-value ≥ 0.25 were excluded from the final model. In the multivariable analysis, factors associated with current modern contraceptive use were reported using adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Generalized variance inflation factor values were used to assess the presence of multicollinearity between explanatory variables. Although individual- and community-level wealth variables may be correlated, this did not pose a concern in the analysis. All variables had variance inflation factor values below 5, indicating no significant multicollinearity. In addition, the multilevel modeling approach is appropriate for handling such hierarchical data structures, as it separates individual (compositional) effects from community (contextual) effects. Therefore, including both levels of wealth variables allows a more comprehensive understanding of socioeconomic influences on contraceptive use. Husband’s/partner’s educational status was included as a descriptive variable only. It was not included in the multivariable regression analysis because this variable is available only for women who are currently in a union. Including it in the model would have restricted the analysis to partnered women and reduced the overall sample size. Therefore, all regression analyses were conducted using the full sample of women, and husband/partner-related variables were excluded to maintain representativeness. In this analysis, we fitted four distinct models to determine the model that best fits the data. The first model was the null model (a model without the independent variable). The null model gives the variance of the outcome variable attributed to the cluster without the independent variables (to assess the magnitude of the cluster variation in current modern contraceptive use) [31]. Model I only includes individual-level factors; model II only includes community-level factors; and model III includes both individual and community-level factors. The log-likelihood test, deviance (−2*LL), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) were employed for model selection [32, 33]. The model with the lowest deviance, AIC, and BIC, along with the highest log-likelihood, was deemed the best fit for the final analysis [34, 35].

Variations in the magnitude of current modern contraceptive use across different clusters were assessed by intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC), proportion changes in variance (PCV), and median odds ratio (MOR). ICC measures the overall variance in the extent of current modern contraceptive use that can be related to cluster differences. It was calculated as follows: ICC = Va/(Va+Vi), where Va is cluster-level variance and Vi is individual-level variance. PCV was calculated for all models using the empty model as a reference. PCV reflects the proportion of the total observed individual variation in modern contraceptive utilization that is attributable to cluster variations. PCV was computed in the following formula: (Va-Vb)/Va, where Va is the variance of the empty model whereas Vb is the variance of the subsequent models. The MOR was used to evaluate the cluster-level variance in the likelihood of current modern contraception use [36]. This was assessed by contrasting the median likelihood of current modern contraception use among women who lived in clusters with the lowest risk and those who lived in areas with the highest risk. The MOR was calculated using the following formula: Exp(√2×VA)×0.6745≈ Exp(√VA)×0.95, where VA is the area level variance [36].

Ethical approval

This study involved a secondary analysis of publicly available data from the Lesotho Demographic and Health Survey (LDHS) 2023–2024. The original DHS survey protocol was reviewed and approved by the ICF Institutional Review Board and the relevant national ethics authority in Lesotho, and was conducted in accordance with internationally accepted ethical standards, including the Declaration of Helsinki. The present analysis was classified as exempt from additional ethical review and did not constitute human subjects research, as it involved the use of fully anonymized secondary data. No personally identifiable information was available to the authors, and no attempt was made to identify survey participants. Permission to access and use the DHS datasets was obtained from the DHS Program. Further details on DHS ethical standards and procedures are available from the DHS Program.

Results

Individual level characteristics

In this study, all reported estimates are based on weighted analyses. A total of 6,341 weighted women were included in the analysis. About 36.95% of the women were aged 15–24 years, and 50.9% were unmarried. About 46.8% of women were sexually active, and nearly half (49.1%) were married. The majority of households were male-headed (56.78%), and most respondents were Christians (97.96%). Most women had secondary education (57.51%). Similarly, among husbands or partners, the largest proportions had primary (38.67%) or secondary education (38.02%). Most households (68.22%) had between one and five members, and over half of the women (56.20%) had no children under five years of age in the household. About one-third of households (30.26%) belonged to the poor wealth tertile. Nearly all women (99.31%) reported knowledge of modern contraceptive methods. Around 60.06% of women were exposed to mass media (radio, television, and newspapers) (Table 1).

Community level characteristics

About 400 clusters (enumeration areas) were considered as units of analysis. Study participants were drawn from ten districts, with proportions ranging from 2.8% in Qacha’s Nek to 33.78% in Maseru. Women residing in rural areas accounted for 46.33% of the sample. Only 8.82% of women came from communities with low literacy levels, and 9.7% were classified as having low knowledge of modern contraceptives. In addition, 36.48% of women were from low-wealth communities, and 38.37% had low media exposure. About 24.06% of respondents reported that distance to a health facility was a big problem (Table 2).

Among the weighted sample of women, 53.2% reported currently using modern contraceptive methods, while 46.8% were not. Among non-usersof modern methods, 45.1% were not using any method, 0.1% were using folkloric methods, and 1.6% were using traditional methods (Fig. 1).

Model selection and results of measure of variation

The model fitness with the highest log likelihood and lowest AIC was used to select the best-fitted model. Model IV, which includes both individual- and community-level factors, demonstrates the best fit and is selected as the final model. Compared with the null model, all subsequent models show improved fit, with Model IV achieving the highest log-likelihood and the lowest deviance, AIC, and BIC, indicating that the combined inclusion of individual and community factors provides the most robust model for modern contraceptive use.

In the null model, the ICC was 2.17%, indicating that about 2.2% of the variance in modern contraceptive use was attributable to differences between clusters, with a MOR of 1.29 confirming cluster-level heterogeneity. Introducing individual-level factors (Model II) reduced the ICC to 1.39% and explained 36.0% of the between-cluster variance (PCV). Community-level factors alone (Model III) resulted in an ICC of 1.76% and explained 18.9% of the variance. In the full model (Model IV), the ICC further decreased to 1.22% and the MOR to 1.21, indicating that the combined individual- and community-level factors partially explained the cluster-level variation. These findings suggest that individual-level variables contribute more to explaining variability in contraceptive use than community-level factors, although both play important roles. Including community-level variables in Model IV did not substantially change the direction or magnitude of the associations observed for individual-level variables in Model II, and similarly, individual-level variables did not markedly alter the effects of community-level factors from Model III. This indicates that both individual- and community-level factors contribute independently, with the combined model mainly improving the explanation of cluster-level variance (Table 3).

Individual and community-level factors associated with current modern contraceptive use among women in Lesotho

Women aged 25–34 years (AOR = 2.1, 95% CI: 1.8–2.4) and 35–49 years (AOR = 1.9, 95% CI: 1.6–2.1) were more likely to use modern contraceptive methods than women aged 15–24 years. Women with primary (AOR = 2.6, 95% CI: 1.5–4.7), secondary (AOR = 2.9, 95% CI: 1.6–5.2), and higher (AOR = 3.0, 95% CI: 1.6–5.5) education had higher odds of using modern contraceptives compared to those with no formal education. Women who were recently sexually active were 2.1 times more likely to use modern contraceptives than those who were not recently sexually active (AOR = 2.1, 95% CI: 1.9–2.4). Unmarried women had 39% lower odds of using modern contraceptives compared to married women (AOR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.54–0.70). Women from rich wealth tertiles had 24% lower odds of using modern contraceptives compared to those from poor wealth tertiles (AOR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.63–0.92). Women living in households with six or more members were 13% less likely to use modern contraceptives than those in smaller households (AOR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.77–0.99). Women who had one (AOR = 1.88, 95% CI: 1.66–2.1) and two and above (AOR = 2.3, 95% CI: 1.9–2.9) under-five children had a higher utilization of modern contraceptives than women with no under-five children. Leribe (AOR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.53–0.86), Berea (AOR = 0.76, 95% CI:0.59–0.97), Maseru (AOR = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.52–0.84), Mafeteng (AOR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.5–0.84), Qacha’s Nek (AOR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.45–0.78), Mokhotlong (AOR = 0.7, 95% CI: 0.54–0.9), and Thaba-Tseka (AOR = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.43–0.73) districts had a lower utilization of modern contraceptives than a district of Butha-Buthe. Utilization of modern contraceptives was 1.3 times higher among women with high community knowledge than among those with low community knowledge (AOR = 1.3, 95% CI: 1.13–1.6) (Table 4).

Discussion

Family planning, particularly through the use of modern contraceptive methods, remains a key strategy for achieving the United Nations sustainable development goals by reducing poverty and improving maternal and child health. Modern contraceptives are effective tools for fertility regulation and for safeguarding the health of women of reproductive age [2, 3]. Despite their benefits, utilization remains suboptimal in many low- and middle-income countries [4, 5], with substantial variation across regions [7–19].

This study provides nationally representative evidence on modern contraceptive use among women of reproductive age in Lesotho, incorporating both individual-level and community-level determinants through a multilevel analytical approach. The findings highlight not only the prevalence of contraceptive use but also the importance of contextual influences in shaping reproductive health behaviors. The overall prevalence (53.2%) observed in this study is comparatively higher than that reported in several studies conducted in other parts of Africa [12, 13, 17], including sub-Saharan Africa [8–11] and Ethiopia [14–16], as well as in Yemen [7]. These differences may reflect variations in access to family planning services [4–6], health system performance, and broader sociocultural and gender-related contexts across countries [20–23]. In contrast, the prevalence was lower than that reported in a district-level study from South Africa [19], which may be explained by differences in study populations and outcome definitions, as the South African study included all contraceptive methods rather than modern methods only.

This study identified key individual- and community-level factors associated with modern contraceptive use among women of reproductive age in Lesotho, providing actionable insights for family planning programs and policies. The findings show that contraceptive use varies by women’s age, family composition, household characteristics, community knowledge, and district of residence, underscoring the need for targeted, context-specific interventions.

At the individual level, older women were more likely to use modern contraceptives than younger women, consistent with previous studies [8, 23]. This suggests that demand for contraception increases as women progress through their reproductive life course and achieve their desired family size. Strengthening counseling and outreach for younger women, particularly adolescents and those early in their reproductive years, remains critical because they may face barriers related to access, information, and social norms. Youth-friendly family planning services could help address these challenges. Interestingly, our findings indicate that women living in larger households had lower odds of using modern contraceptives, while those with one or more under-five children were more likely to use them. This apparent contrast can be interpreted by considering the different implications of household size versus the presence of young children. Women with young children may be more motivated to space or limit pregnancies to optimize maternal and child health and manage the care of their existing children [2, 3]. In contrast, living in a larger household does not necessarily equate to having more young children; it may reflect extended family structures where caregiving responsibilities are shared, or resources are stretched, potentially limiting women’s autonomy and access to family planning services. Thus, while women with young children actively seek to control fertility, those in larger households may face practical, social, or resource-related barriers that reduce contraceptive use. These findings align with previous studies showing that women with more children are more likely to use modern contraceptives [14, 24], highlighting that contraceptive decisions are influenced by both the number of dependent children and household dynamics. Consistent with previous research [10, 12, 14, 20–23], higher educational attainment was strongly associated with modern contraceptive use. Education enhances knowledge and empowerment, enabling informed reproductive choices, as reported in a study from Sub-Saharan Africa [37]. Recent sexual activity was also associated with higher contraceptive use, reflecting the greater perceived need for protection among sexually active women [38]. In contrast, unmarried women and those from the rich wealth tertile had lower odds of using modern contraceptives, consistent with previous studies [8, 12, 14, 23–25]. This may reflect social norms and cultural expectations that create stigma or discourage unmarried women from accessing family planning services. Wealthier women, meanwhile, may rely on alternative fertility management strategies, such as private healthcare or less formal methods, which could reduce their perceived need for modern contraceptives.

At the community level, higher overall knowledge of modern contraceptive methods was positively associated with individual use, highlighting the influence of shared norms and collective awareness on health behaviors [39]. This finding is consistent with previous studies [10, 23] and underscores the importance of community-based education and information dissemination strategies such as community dialogues, health extension activities, and mass media campaigns to complement individual counseling. Substantial geographic disparities in modern contraceptive use were observed across districts. Lower utilization in several districts compared with Butha-Buthe likely reflects differences in access to health facilities, availability of family planning services, and exposure to reproductive health information. These patterns point to the need for district-specific strategies, including improved service availability, targeted outreach in underserved areas, and stronger integration of community-based education with health facility services.

Strength and limitation of the study

Methodologically, this study contributes by simultaneously examining individual- and community-level determinants using a multilevel modeling approach. By accounting for clustering and contextual influences, the analysis provides a more comprehensive understanding of contraceptive use than models limited to individual characteristics alone. The findings demonstrate that while individual factors explain a substantial portion of contraceptive behavior, community context captures additional variation that is directly relevant for program planning. This approach strengthens the evidence base for multilevel interventions that operate at both household and community levels. The most recent nationally representative data along with a large sample size were used. The data were gathered using standardized and validated tools. The limitation of this study was that the cross-sectional nature of the study did not indicate a cause-and-effect relationship between the independent factors and the outcome. Recall bias may exist because the study relies on reported contraceptive use.

Future research should build on these findings by incorporating direct measures of women’s autonomy, decision-making power, partner dynamics, and service quality to better understand pathways influencing contraceptive use. Longitudinal or mixed-methods studies could also help clarify how individual life-course changes interact with community environments over time. Such work would further enhance the ability of policymakers and practitioners to design effective, equitable family planning programs in Lesotho.

Conclusion

Approximately half of women of reproductive age in Lesotho use modern contraceptive methods. Both individual-level factors, such as age, education, recent sexual activity, marital status, and wealth, and community-level factors, including knowledge and district of residence, significantly influence utilization. Interestingly, while women with one or more under-five children were more likely to use modern contraceptives, those living in larger households had lower odds of use. This suggests that having young children motivates fertility management, whereas larger household size may limit women’s autonomy or access to family planning services. To improve uptake, policymakers and program planners should prioritize youth-friendly reproductive health services for women under 25 years of age and design targeted interventions for vulnerable groups, including unmarried women and those in larger households. Strengthening community awareness through health education, addressing geographic disparities, and implementing multilevel strategies that integrate individual and contextual interventions are essential for achieving equitable and sustained increases in modern contraceptive use in Lesotho.
