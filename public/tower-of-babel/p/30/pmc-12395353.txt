Quantification and Determination of Compatible Bacterial Consortia
Authors: Jair J Pineda‐Pineda, Jesús Muñoz‐Rojas, Yolanda E Morales‐García, Esperanza Trenado‐Sánchez, José M Sigarreta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe sustainable use of terrestrial ecosystems is crucial to ensure food security for future generations, and the formulation of plant growth–promoting bacterial consortia has proven useful not only in sustainable agriculture but also in applications such as phytopathogen biocontrol, bioremediation of contaminated soils and waters, biofertilization, organic waste degradation, and the production of industrially relevant enzymes. In this work, we developed a mathematical model to quantify and identify antagonism‐free consortia based on double‐agar‐layer assays with 20 and 33 bacterial strains from independent studies. Simultaneously, we implemented a graph‐based geometric model to analyse their topological properties and defined an index to quantify feasible consortia. The proposed methods were experimentally validated, identifying 36,851 and 376,126 subsets of compatible strains, for 20 and 33 strains, respectively. These subsets have the potential to promote plant growth and other biotechnological functions, thus providing a scalable tool for the management of agricultural resources, environmental restoration, and the design of sustainable biotechnological practices.Keywords: bacterial consortia, cliques, graph theory, PGPR, topological index

Introduction

Plant growth‐promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) play a fundamental role in the rhizosphere by interacting with plants, soil, and microfauna, directly influencing plant growth and agricultural productivity (Ulrich et al. 2019). These bacteria colonise plant roots and adapt to diverse ecological niches, even under competitive conditions (Antoun 2013). Their contributions to pathogen control, biofertiliser production, and phytohormone synthesis have made them key elements in sustainable agriculture (Antoun and Prévost 2005; Gupta et al. 2015; López et al. 2014; Vacheron et al. 2013; Vejan et al. 2016).

There is growing interest in manipulating beneficial microbial communities and understanding the ecological dynamics that shape their behaviour (Jiang et al. 2023; Mittermeier et al. 2023). Interactions such as cooperation, competition, and neutralism influence population structure and functionality within microbial consortia (Kong et al. 2018). PGPR have also been shown to induce systemic resistance in plants, enhancing tolerance to pathogens and abiotic stresses (Molina‐Romero et al. 2017; Romera et al. 2019; Singh et al. 2015).

Network‐based approaches have emerged as useful tools to explore microbial relationships. Co‐occurrence networks help reveal hidden ecological patterns (Goberna and Verdú 2022; Guseva et al. 2022; Li et al. 2023), though their interpretation requires caution (He et al. 2017; Yang, Loik, et al. 2024; Yang, Song, et al. 2024). In parallel, deterministic models such as ordinary differential equations (ODEs) have been used to simulate microbial and genetic dynamics (Karkaria et al. 2021; Rodrigo and Jaramillo 2013).

To this end, we propose a mathematical approach based on graph theory and combinatorics to model microbial antagonism and support the rational selection of compatible strains. This tool aims to facilitate the construction of functional bacterial consortia with potential for ecological integration into the soil microbiome, contributing to more efficient and sustainable agriculture. The model is validated using a public database and compared with experimental results to assess its predictive capacity. Based on the assumption that direct antagonism limits functional coexistence, it provides a simplified yet evidence‐based topological framework for the rational design of microbial communities.

Antagonistic interactions have been proposed as key factors in shaping the organisation and dynamics of microbial communities (Czárán et al. 2002), where chemical warfare may even promote biodiversity in spatially structured environments. In this context, our model assumes that such interactions constrain functional coexistence in the absence of recombination or cooperative feedback. Specifically, if two strains antagonise each other, they should not be included in the same consortium unless there is evidence of mutual tolerance or horizontal gene transfer enabling coexistence.

On this basis, the present study introduces a mathematical framework to quantify and identify functionally compatible bacterial consortia derived from experimentally determined antagonistic interactions. Graph theory and combinatorics are employed to represent pairwise relationships as discrete structures, analyse coexistence properties, identify compatible cliques, and derive topological indices that guide the rational selection of strains. This approach is intended to support the design of microbial consortia with ecological integration potential within the soil microbiome, ultimately contributing to more efficient and sustainable agricultural systems. The model is validated through its application in constructing feasible consortia using a curated public dataset (Molina‐Romero et al. (2017) and Muñoz‐Rojas et al. (2016)), and its predictions are compared with previous experimental results to assess its reliability. Although higher‐order interactions are not incorporated, the model relies on the ecological principle that direct antagonism limits functional coexistence among strains. This simplification enables the creation of a topological representation grounded in accessible and relevant empirical data, serving as a robust starting point for the rational design of microbial communities.

Methods

Antagonism or coexistence between bacterial strains is critical for the formulation of consortia that promotes plant growth. For example, Molina‐Romero et al. (2017) formulated a bacterial consortia containing four compatible and desiccation‐tolerant strains with potential to promote plant growth. For this purpose, twenty strains were used as producers of antagonistic substances and as indicator strains, applying the double agar layer method. The matrix provides information on how different bacterial strains interact with each other under controlled conditions. P indicates that the producing strain inhibited the growth of the sensitive strain, while empty cells indicate no inhibition. This matrix is key for effectively visualising the dynamics of bacterial interactions and serves as an empirical basis for the mathematical modelling discussed in the remainder of the paper. By showing which strains inhibit others, one can proceed to a more detailed analysis of coexistence and antagonism relationships, which is critical for designing effective and viable consortia. Antagonism Matrix

Microbial antagonism is a detrimental relationship in which one microorganism inhibits, impairs, or prevents the existence of another. These interactions may arise due to the production of antimicrobial substances, such as antibiotics, bacteriocins, and toxins, or through competition for resources and space (Pérez et al. 2014). This phenomenon is essential in microbial ecology and has important applications in the biological control of pathogens and the treatment of infectious diseases (Cesa‐Luna et al. 2020). In general, such relationships will be defined through a simple graph. The matrix cij, by setting P=1, becomes the adjacency matrix of an antagonism digraph G (Definition 2), which encodes directed inhibition relationships among bacterial strains. From G, one constructs a complementary coexistence matrix G′ and then extracts the subgraph H of bidirectional coexistence edges to identify viable bacterial consortia via clique analysis (Figure 1).

Let G=GV,E be a digraph in which V is the set of vertices representing bacterial strains, and E is the set of directed edges defining bacterial antagonism. An ordered pair u,v∈E, but v,u∉E, indicates that u antagonises v, but v does not antagonise u, while u,v∈E and v,u∈E, denotes mutual antagonism, represented as u,v⃡. We refer to G as the antagonism digraph. The complement of G, denoted as G′V,D′, represents the directed coexistence relations (i.e., the absence of antagonism). We refer to G′ as the coexistence digraph. The underlying subgraph H⊆G′, is a simple undirected graph that includes the unordered pairs u,v such that u,v∈D′ and v,u∈D′, meaning mutual coexistence between u and v. When represented, edge directions in H are omitted. We refer to H as the coexistence graph.

This graph‐theoretical framework captures both antagonistic (graph G) and coexistence (graph G′) interactions among bacterial strains. Within this framework, a feasible bacterial consortium of size r is corresponds structurally to an r‐clique in the undirected coexistence graph H⊆G′: a complete subgraph F⊆H of order r whose every pairwise link denotes mutual compatibility and absence of antagonism, allowing its identification via clique‐detection algorithms.

Figure 1D shows a directed antagonism graph G for six bacterial strains c1−c6, constructed as an illustrative example. Arrows indicate antagonistic relationships: an edge from ci to cj means that ci inhibits cj. Nodes are colour‐coded by out‐degree (number of strains they inhibit), with a gradient ranging from white (no antagonism) to dark red (high antagonism). For instance, c6 has the highest out‐degree, inhibiting three different strains. This visualisation helps identify more aggressive strains (strong inhibitors) and potentially more vulnerable ones. Such information is key to excluding incompatible combinations and designing functional bacterial consortia, thereby supporting the formation of stable and effective microbial communities.

Such a clique guarantees full interconnection without conflict, inherently satisfying the condition for a viable bacterial consortium. Consequently, any r‐clique in the coexistence graph H represents a feasible bacterial consortium of size r. Identifying such cliques is essential for designing synthetic microbial consortia in applied settings, such as agriculture, where communities of strains that coexist without conflict are more effective at promoting plant growth, improving soil health, and suppressing pathogens.

This index provides a structural measure of how many subsets of strains can coexist pairwise without antagonism and thus represent feasible bacterial consortia. Its value is bounded between 0 and 2n−n−1 (the proof of this statement follows directly from Equation (B2)), the number of possible non‐singleton subsets in a complete graph of n nodes. While ΘH captures the global level of pairwise coexistence, BCIH reflects the internal organisation and structural diversity of compatible combinations. Each clique is counted once as a unique subset of mutually coexisting strains, although cliques may partially overlap by sharing one or more strains. Together, these metrics allow for a robust topological characterisation of bacterial interaction networks and inform the rational design of synthetic consortia in applied settings.

Let H be a coexistence graph and define the coexistence ratio as Θ. The value of Θ≈1, indicates a high level of coexistence among bacterial strains, meaning that most pairs of strains are compatible. Biologically, this suggests a favourable scenario for the formation of dense and potentially stable bacterial consortia. In contrast, if Θ≈0, the coexistence rate is low, indicating that only a few pairs of strains can coexist. In this case, the number of edges m is much smaller than the maximum possible number, given by nn−12, implying a sparsely connected network. This can be interpreted as a high level of antagonism, where only a few strains can coexist peacefully.

In the antagonism digraph G (Figure 1D), which includes 10 of the 30 possible edges, the directed density is Θ→G=0.33; its directed complement G′ (Figure 1E) has 20 edges, giving Θ→G=0.67. In G, c6 is the most aggressive strain (out‐degree 3), and in G′, c1 is the most “peaceful” strain (out‐degree 5). Extracting only the bidirectional edges from G′ yields the undirected mutual‐coexistence subgraph H (Figure 1F), with 6 vertices and 8 of the 15 possible edges, ΘH=0.53 and BCI=11. Within this H, c1 and c5 (degree 4) is most central in cliques, followed by c3 (degree 3), while c4 and c2 (degree 2) lie at the periphery. The order−2 and order−3 cliques enumerate all pairs and triplets of non‐antagonistic strains, illustrating how both directed and undirected density metrics, together with the BCIH, capture the network's ability to form viable bacterial consortia. In the ideal coexistence graphs (blue triangles), clique counts peak at order 10 (≈5.3) for n=20 and at order 16 (≈9.4) for n=33, before symmetrically declining, whereas in the actual antagonism networks (orange circles) the maxima shift to order 7 (≈3.9) and order 8 (≈5.0), respectively, and then plunge to zero by orders 16 and 15. This stark contrast demonstrates how antagonistic interactions dramatically curtail the formation of high‐order cliques, and thus the size of viable consortia, forcing the network to fragment into smaller, more isolated subgroups. Networks with many large cliques may indicate a robust environment where many strains can coexist, which is beneficial for the ecological stability and resilience of the microbial community. In contrast, if most cliques are of low order, this could signal strong competition or antagonism between strains, or segregation into smaller, less interconnected subcommunities.

Discussion

The prediction of functional bacterial consortia from binary antagonism data represents an efficient and complementary approach to more complex methods such as co‐occurrence networks or dynamic models based on differential equations (Karkaria et al. 2021; Rodrigo and Jaramillo 2013). Based on the experimental matrix of Molina‐Romero et al. (2017), the proposed topological model enabled the identification of compatible cliques using graph theory, establishing a clear and easily interpretable coexistence structure. This approach yields predictions that align with those obtained through combinatorial methods, supporting its mathematical and ecological validity. Previous studies, such as those by Kong et al. (2018) and Berry and Widder (2014), have shown that the way microbial interactions are represented directly influences the prediction of community dynamics. In this regard, the use of cliques allows for the definition of theoretically viable configurations without requiring functional parameterisation or extensive simulations. Moreover, the BCI(G) index proposed in this work quantifies the structural complexity of the microbial system, in line with recent proposals highlighting the relevance of microbial network topology in ecosystem functionality (Guseva et al. 2022; Huang et al. 2023; Yang, Song, et al. 2024).

Although our model does not explicitly incorporate spatial or evolutionary dynamics, its graph‐theoretical structure is consistent with spatially explicit models such as that of Czárán et al. (2002), which showed that local antagonistic interactions alone can sustain microbial diversity. In our approach, this ecological pattern is captured through the identification of cliques within the coexistence graph, each representing a subset of mutually compatible strains. Interestingly, the coexistence index BCI(G) we introduce here could be interpreted as a non‐spatial analog of the hyper‐immunity regime described in this study, wherein strain diversity is maintained through non‐antagonistic configurations shaped by competitive trade‐offs.

While the model is based on the assumption that direct antagonism serves as an exclusion criterion, this methodological decision enables rapid and scalable structural evaluations. Although it does not incorporate higher‐order interactions or functional variables, it successfully recovers significant subsets of the functional consortium reported by Molina‐Romero et al. (2017) and Muñoz‐Rojas et al. (2016), suggesting that it captures ecologically coherent cores. This observation aligns with studies emphasising the importance of functional redundancy and structural compatibility in the formation of stable synthetic communities (Batista and Singh 2021; Sáez‐Sandino et al. 2023). Likewise, the model's omission of certain strains can be interpreted as evidence of subtle interactions not detected through direct inhibition assays, an issue already discussed in recent studies on microbial networks under environmental perturbation (de Andrade et al. 2023; Li et al. 2023). In sum, the present model offers a conceptual and computational tool for exploring symbiotic compatibility based on simple empirical information, laying the groundwork for future integration with more complex functional and experimental approaches.

This study combines two complementary approaches for identifying feasible bacterial consortia: a combinatorial method based on the explicit enumeration of subsets under constraints, and a topological method grounded in empirical data represented as coexistence graphs. The consortia in Table 1 were obtained using combinatorial formulas (expressions B3 and B6), which generate all possible combinations of r strains from a set of n, excluding those with known antagonistic interactions. This procedure provides a theoretical framework for estimating viable configurations based on documented antagonisms. Meanwhile, the topological approach relies on the cliques of the coexistence graph H to derive the consortia included in the Supporting Information: each consortium corresponds to an r‐clique in the graph, since all strains in the group are mutually compatible according to experimentally validated antagonism data (Molina‐Romero et al. 2017; Muñoz‐Rojas et al. 2016).

Overall, these findings demonstrate that the use of topological structures, such as cliques within coexistence graphs, not only provides a clear representation of microbial interactions but also enables the rational and scalable selection of functional consortia. Future research could integrate additional functional data, such as metabolomics or stress tolerance, to expand the applicability of the model in real agricultural environments.

Conclusions

The combinatorial and topological analysis presented in this work enabled the contextualisation of bacterial antagonism assays in controlled media within two mathematical domains: combinatorics and graph theory. In this regard, geometric methods and models were developed to explore the underlying topological features of directed biological networks. Furthermore, by complementing these with computational approaches, two databases of subsets with potential for developing feasible bacterial consortia were established.

Understanding the composition and dynamics of cliques representing bacterial consortia is essential to explain the functioning and stability of microbial communities in different environments. It may also have practical implications for applications such as bioremediation, biocontrol, and engineering of synthetic microbial communities for biotechnological purposes. Thus, it aims to advance the understanding of how bacterial interactions within the rhizosphere influence plant growth and how they can be manipulated to promote sustainable agricultural practices. In addition, the objective was to use mathematical and computational tools to quantify and identify bacterial consortia based on antagonistic and coexistence interactions, which is important to improve the management of agronomic resources and promote sustainable development. In this manuscript, only bacterial coexistence is considered, without considering other characteristics, such as resistance to desiccation and its potential use in different areas, which would allow interpolating the results obtained here in other contexts and developing new research.

The combination of the approaches presented by Lopes et al. (2024) and in this manuscript, along with the selection criteria from Molina‐Romero et al. (2017), could lead to a more holistic strategy for the design of bacterial consortia. While tools are provided to avoid negative interactions and ensure consortium viability, Lopes et al. (2024) introduce the idea that cooperation is also essential for the stability and multistability of the community. An integrated approach could enable the creation of bacterial consortia that are not only viable and productive but also resilient and stable in the long term.

Graph Theory

A graph G is an ordered pair VGEG consisting of a set VG of vertices and a set EG of edges. Each vertex is represented by a point and the edges by lines joining a pair of vertices. The degree of a vertex, denoted by du, is the number of edges, e=u,v, incident to it. In this case, vertices u and v are called adjacent or neighbours. A graph is simple if it contains no loops or multiple edges. Moreover, a graph is complete if every pair of vertices is adjacent, and it contains nn−1/2 edges, where n is the number of vertices.

The order of a graph G is the number of vertices it contains. The complement G′ of G is a simple graph whose vertex set is V and whose edges are the nonadjacent vertex pairs of G. A graph F is called a subgraph of a graph G if VF⊆VG and EF⊆EG. A complete subgraph of a graph G is known as a clique (or clan).

Although many problems can be formulated with simple graphs, this concept is sometimes insufficient. For example, in traffic flow, it is essential to know which roads are one‐way and the direction in which they flow. In such cases, a simple graph is not useful. Instead, a directed graph, or digraph, is required, where orientation is assigned to each edge. For the purposes of this manuscript, a loop‐free digraph is needed, consisting of a nonempty set of vertices and a set of directed edges, with no edges connecting a vertex to itself. Formally, a loop‐free digraph G is an ordered pair V,E, where V is a nonempty set of vertices and E is a set of ordered pairs of vertices such that, for each pair u,v of E, it holds that u≠v. This means there are no edges of the form v,v for any vertex v∈V.

If G be an antagonism digraph and H is the underlying subgraph of the complement G′ of G, then H represents coexistence.

By Definition 2, G=GV,E is the antagonism digraph in which each u,v∈E marks a directed inhibition from u to v. Its complement G′=V,D′ therefore contains precisely those ordered pairs u,v∉E, i.e., every directed coexistence edge. We then form the underlying undirected subgraph H⊆G′ by keeping an undirected edge u,v only when both u,v∈G′ and v,u∈G′. Since neither direction appears in E, u does not antagonise v and v does not antagonise u, so u and v coexist mutually. Hence H encodes exactly the bidirectional, non‐antagonistic relationships among strains and so serves as the coexistence graph.

Let G be a coexistence graph and define the coexistence ratio as Θ. If Θ≈1, it indicates a high rate of coexistence between bacterial strains, meaning that most pairs of strains coexist. On the other hand, if Θ≈0, it suggests a low rate of coexistence, indicating that only a few pairs of strains coexist. This can be demonstrated by considering that Θ=2mnn−1. When Θ≈1, it implies that m≈nn−12, which means that the number of edges m is close to the maximum possible number of edges in a complete graph. This reflects a high degree of coexistence between the strains, proving the first case. By analogy, the second case is proven as well when Θ≈0, indicating low coexistence.
