Determinants of tuberculosis treatment adherence among patients taking anti-TB drugs in Borama, Somaliland
Authors: Ubah Mohamed Ileye, Vitalis Okoth Odero, Mohamed Said Hassan, Md. Moyazzem Hossain
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTuberculosis (TB) treatment non-adherence poses significant challenges, leading to complications, disease transmission, and drug resistance. This study aimed to identify the determinants of TB treatment adherence among adult patients attending the Borama Regional Hospital (BRH) in Somaliland.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted at BRH. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire, which included a direct question on missed doses (self-report), pill counts, and a validated 8-item Self-Administered Questionnaire to measure adherence. Statistical analysis included descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, independent t-tests, and binary and multivariable logistic regression using SPSS version 26 to identify adherence-related factors. Adherence was defined as taking at least 95% of prescribed medications in the last 30 days based on a composite measure of self-report, pill count, and the self-administered questionnaire.ResultsOf the 167 participants, 74.9% demonstrated adherence to Tuberculosis (TB) treatment. Multivariable logistic regression revealed that being employed (Adjusted Odds Ratio [AOR]: 0.058, 95% CI: 0.014–0.451) and self-employed (AOR: 0.201, 95% CI: 0.050–0.981) were associated with lower odds of adherence compared to being non-employed. Conversely, correctly following overall medical advice (AOR: 2.023, 95% CI: 1.832, 4.729), experiencing stigma (AOR: 3.744, 95% CI: 1.610–7.485), and traveling a long distance to the health facility (AOR: 7.526, 95% CI: 3.472–9.419) were associated with higher odds of adherence. Good housing conditions had a protective effect against non-adherence (AOR: 0.071, 95% CI: 0.21–0.531).ConclusionThis study identified several factors significantly associated with TB treatment adherence in Borama. To improve treatment adherence, interventions should focus on addressing stigma, promoting access to healthcare, addressing socioeconomic determinants including housing, and addressing the financial and logistical limitations experienced by TB patients. Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a formidable global health threat, paradoxically persisting as a leading cause of infectious death despite being a curable disease [1–3]. In 2022 alone, 1.3 million lives were lost to TB, a figure that underscores the profound challenges in disease management worldwide [4]. The burden is disproportionately concentrated in resource-limited regions, with Africa accounting for a quarter of all cases [5]. The situation in Somalia is particularly acute, where a high TB incidence rate of 259 per 100,000 people highlights the intense and ongoing transmission within the community [6]. Central to controlling this epidemic is ensuring patients complete their prescribed treatment, yet achieving this remains a significant hurdle.

Effective treatment adherence is the cornerstone of TB control; it is essential not only for curing individual patients but also for preventing the development of drug-resistant strains and halting community transmission [7–9]. However, non-adherence is a pervasive problem. Previous research in Borama has highlighted challenges within the local TB program, underscoring the difficulties in achieving optimal treatment outcomes [10]. Unpublished data from the Borama Regional Hospital, the setting for this study revealed a concerning trend: between 2015 and 2017, out of 1,557 new patients, 323 demonstrated poor adherence, defined as treatment interruption or loss to follow-up. This local evidence points to a substantial and persistent challenge within the TB program, yet the underlying reasons for this non-adherence have remained undocumented.

While the barriers to TB treatment have been explored globally, there is a critical knowledge gap in the context of Somaliland, a region with a unique socio-economic landscape and healthcare system. Without a clear understanding of the specific local drivers of non-adherence, public health interventions are unlikely to be effective. This study, therefore, is the first of its kind in this setting to systematically investigate these determinants. By identifying the specific barriers and facilitators to TB treatment adherence in Borama, this research aims to fill a critical evidence gap and provide actionable insights for developing targeted, effective strategies to improve treatment outcomes and reduce the burden of TB in the community.

Methodology

Study design and rationale

This study employed a cross-sectional, quantitative design to investigate the factors associated with TB treatment adherence among adult patients in the Borama district of Somaliland. The study was conducted at Borama Regional Hospital (BRH) in Borama, Somaliland, the region’s primary treatment facility for TB. This hospital serves a diverse population from Borama town and surrounding rural areas. Data were collected between September and November 2023.

The standard of care for drug-sensitive TB at BRH follows the national Directly Observed Treatment, Short-course (DOTS) strategy. Patients receive counseling and education before initiating treatment. During the two-month intensive phase, patients are encouraged to attend the clinic daily or are monitored by a family member or community health worker. In the four-month continuation phase, patients typically collect medication on a weekly or bi-weekly basis. A protocol is in place for community health workers to trace patients who miss their appointments (defaulters).

Target population and sampling

The target population for this study consisted of adults (18 years and older) diagnosed with active TB and receiving treatment at BRH. Participants were recruited using a consecutive sampling approach, where every eligible patient attending the clinic during the data collection period was invited to participate until the calculated sample size was achieved. All eligible patients who were approached consented to participate.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula for categorical data, based on an estimated annual population of 300 adult TB patients seeking care in the Borama district, a 5% margin of error, 95% confidence interval, and assuming a 50% prevalence for adherence (as the true prevalence in this setting was unknown). The calculation produced a sample size of 169 patients. While it is standard practice to inflate the sample size to account for potential missing data, our recruitment met the calculated target. During the data collection period, 169 patients were recruited; however, two questionnaires were excluded from the final analysis due to missing data, resulting in a final sample size of 167 participants.

Data collection instrument

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire. The questionnaire included questions on the following:

Psychosocial factors

Reported stigma, depression, social support, and medical advice following behaviors. Adherence to medical advice was assessed by asking participants if they followed the instructions provided by healthcare workers regarding medication timing, diet, and clinic appointments.

Data collection procedure

All eligible TB patients who were receiving care at BRH and who gave their consent to participate were enrolled in the study after providing informed consent. Data were collected by research assistants who were trained in questionnaire administration and ethical research procedures. Trained research assistants administered the questionnaire to participants via face-to-face interviews using a standard protocol. At the beginning of each interview, participants were given full information about the study aims and methods and assured confidentiality. Pill counts were done when applicable and recorded in a separate sheet.

Definition of adherence

For the purpose of this study, adherence was assessed using a composite measure derived from three methods: patient self-report, pill counts, and the Self-Administered Questionnaire. An adherence level of ≥ 95% was the threshold for each measure.

A participant was ultimately classified as “adherent” if they met the ≥ 95% threshold on at least two of the three assessment methods. This composite approach was chosen to enhance the validity of our outcome measure, mitigating the known limitations of any single method (e.g., recall bias in self-report or the possibility of “pill dumping” in pill counts).

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26. … A chi-square test was used to examine the unadjusted associations between categorical independent variables and adherence. Binary and multivariable logistic regression models were then used to identify the independent predictors of TB treatment adherence. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were considered for inclusion in the final multivariable logistic regression model. A backward stepwise elimination method was used to build the model. Collinearity between independent variables was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with VIF < 5 considered acceptable. The goodness-of-fit of the final model was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated, and a significance level of p < 0.05 was used.

Results of the logistic regression model

The study identified various significant factors that affected adherence to TB treatment. Multivariable logistic regression showed that being employed (AOR: 0.058, 95% CI: 0.014–0.451) and self-employed (AOR: 0.201, 95% CI: 0.050–0.981) were associated with significantly lower odds of treatment adherence compared to being non-employed.

In contrast, several factors were associated with higher odds of adherence. Patients who correctly followed medical advice had twice the odds of being adherent (AOR: 2.023, 95% CI: 1.832–4.729). Surprisingly, those who experienced stigma were nearly four times more likely to be adherent (AOR: 3.744, 95% CI: 1.610–7.485), and those who traveled a long distance to the health facility were over seven times more likely to be adherent (AOR: 7.526, 95% CI: 3.472–9.419). Finally, having good housing conditions was protective, being associated with lower odds of non-adherence (AOR: 0.071, 95% CI: 0.21–0.531) [Table 3].

Discussion

This study provides the first empirical evidence on the determinants of TB treatment adherence in Borama, Somaliland, revealing a complex interplay of socioeconomic, logistical, and psychosocial factors. Our finding that nearly 75% of patients were adherent is encouraging, yet it leaves a substantial minority at risk of treatment failure. The significance of our study lies in uncovering the nuanced and sometimes counter-intuitive factors that shape patient behavior in this specific context, challenging one-size-fits-all assumptions about adherence.

One of the most striking findings was the negative association between employment and adherence. Contrary to studies where unemployment is a risk factor, our results suggest that employed and self-employed individuals face distinct obstacles. The rigid structure of the DOTS program, which often requires frequent clinic visits, can directly conflict with formal work schedules and the economic necessity of maintaining an income. Patients may fear losing their jobs if they disclose their status or take time off, making the unemployed—who possess greater time flexibility—more able to comply. This highlights a critical need for patient-centered care models. This socioeconomic theme is reinforced by the protective effect of good housing, which underscores that a stable living environment is foundational to successful treatment management, a conclusion supported by studies that identify broad socioeconomic variables as key determinants [11].

Equally surprising were the paradoxical findings that experiencing stigma and traveling long distances were associated with higher adherence. This is in direct contrast to numerous studies where these are cited as significant barriers. For instance, research in Northern Ethiopia identified long distance as a major obstacle to adherence [12, 13], and studies in South Africa and Kenya have consistently linked stigma to negative treatment outcomes [14, 15]. Our results, however, may suggest a powerful motivational dynamic at play: patients who feel stigmatized might be more driven to adhere meticulously to achieve a swift cure and regain social acceptance. Similarly, those who undertake arduous journeys have already demonstrated a high level of commitment, which may strengthen their resolve to complete treatment.

As expected, and in line with global evidence, following medical advice was a powerful predictor of adherence. This reinforces the indispensable role of clear communication, patient education, and a trusting patient-provider relationship in the success of TB treatment. When patients understand their regimen and feel supported by the healthcare system, they are significantly more likely to adhere. This finding aligns with research highlighting that patients not following medical advice is a key factor influencing non-adherence [16–18].

Taken together, our findings paint a comprehensive picture that aligns with evidence from neighboring countries like Ethiopia and Kenya, where distance, stigma, and socioeconomic status are also known to be critical determinants of adherence [12–15, 19]. The key takeaway from our study is that adherence is not merely a matter of patient willpower but is profoundly shaped by structural, social, and economic realities. Therefore, interventions to improve TB treatment outcomes in Borama must be multi-faceted, addressing not only clinical education but also the logistical and financial barriers that patients face daily.

Limitation of the study

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design does not allow for the establishment of causality between the identified factors and treatment adherence. Second, our adherence measures relied partly on self-report, which is subject to recall bias and social desirability bias, potentially leading to an overestimation of adherence rates. Although we used a composite measure to mitigate this, some bias may remain. Third, the study was conducted at a single hospital (BRH), which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other regions of Somaliland or Somalia. Fourthly, A limitation of this study is its purely quantitative design. Future research should incorporate qualitative methods to explore the complex personal and social contexts behind these identified factors in greater depth. Finally, the use of consecutive sampling, a non-probability method, may introduce selection bias, although it is a practical approach for clinic-based studies.

Conclusion

This study determined that various factors, including employment status, compliance with medical advice, distance, stigma, and housing, significantly affected adherence to TB treatment in Borama. To improve adherence, interventions must be multi-faceted. They should include flexible treatment options for employed patients, robust counseling to ensure understanding of medical advice, and programs to reduce stigma. Furthermore, addressing broader social determinants, such as improving housing and providing support to mitigate the costs associated with travel, is crucial for ensuring all patients can successfully complete their treatment.
