Seroprevalence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 and anti-Plasmodium falciparum and vivax antibodies in urban and rural zones in the West Region of Cameroon
Authors: Rolin Mitterran Ndefo Kamga, Nsani Clifford Ajim, Pythagore Fogue Soubgwi, Harry Noyes, Annette MacLeod, Gustave Simo, Walt E. Adamson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn Sub-Saharan Africa the precarious health situation resulting from malaria has been further complicated by the emergence of Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2). However, data on the burden of the virus in Cameroon remains limited. Here, we estimated prevalence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 and anti-Plasmodium antibodies in urban and rural areas of the West Region of Cameroon during April and May 2022.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study performed in Bafoussam (urban) and Fongo-Tongo (rural) in the West Region of Cameroon. Rapid diagnostic tests were used to detect anti-SARS-CoV-2 and anti-Plasmodium antibodies from whole blood samples collected via finger puncture. A total of 805 participants were included in the study. The association between seropositivity and recent history of common COVID-19 symptoms as well as the presence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 and anti-Plasmodium antibodies were analysed using the logistic regression model.ResultsThe overall antibody seroprevalence was 60.1% for SARS-CoV-2. In urban settings, the seroprevalence was 66.6%, including 62.11% IgG positivity, 0.9% IgM, and 2.7% co-detection of both IgG and IgM antibodies. In rural zone it was 54.0% with 51.8% of positivity due to IgG, 0.3% of IgM and 1.9% co-detection of both IgG and IgM antibodies. The overall seroprevalence of anti-Plasmodium antibodies was 22.2% and was high in rural zone with 31.4% compared to 14.8% reported in urban zone. Of the total seroprevalence, anti-Plasmodium antibodies of 21% was attributed to P. falciparum, while 2.11% was due to P. vivax. The generalised linear binomial model revealed no association between the presence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies and anti-Plasmodium antibodies. SARS-CoV-2 antibody presence was associated with symptoms such as fever, dry cough, headache, loss of taste and smell, and body aches while fever, Sore-throat diarrhoea were found as symptoms associated to anti-Plasmodium antibodies. Additionally, a positive association between SARS-CoV-2 IgM antibodies and Plasmodium antibodies was observed.ConclusionThis study demonstrated a moderate-to-high seroprevalence of both anti-SARS-CoV-2 and anti-Plasmodium antibodies in urban and rural areas of Cameroon. However, further research is required to determine the persistence delay as well as the functionality of these assayed antibodies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11938-8.

Background

COVID-19, caused by Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) [1], was first identified in Wuhan, China in December 2019 and rapidly spread worldwide, becoming a major public health crisis. This disease is transmitted through direct or indirect contact with respiratory droplets from infected individuals [2]. The rapid increase in infections and deaths led the World Health Organisation (WHO) to declare global emergency [3]. As of June 2024, WHO estimated 775,553,735 confirmed COVID-19 cases and 7,050,356 deaths [4]. In Cameroon, between January 2020 and January 2024, 125,379 confirmed cases and 1,974 deaths were reported [5]. In response, WHO recommended control measures including isolation, social distancing, and community confinement to mitigate the impact of the pandemic [6].

Serological antibody tests, used to detect previous exposure to SARS-CoV-2 are a valuable tool for assessing the proportion of a population that has been exposed to the virus. Rapid antibody tests for detecting IgM and IgG produced against SARS-CoV-2 are advantageous because they are fast, inexpensive, user-friendly, and do not require extensive training [7–10]. These tests are not only used to estimate the seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies, but enable tracking of virus circulation within communities [10–13]. Serological surveys can help to identify individuals who have been exposed to the virus, even if they did not develop symptoms [7, 13–16].

In Sub-Saharan Africa, where malaria is endemic, the COVID-19 pandemic has placed an additional burden on health systems [17–19]. Malaria, caused by Plasmodium parasites and transmitted by Anopheles mosquitos, remains a significant health issue, with approximately 241 million cases and 627,000 deaths reported worldwide in 2020 [20]. The COVID-19 pandemic led to disruptions in malaria prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of malaria, contributing to 47,000 additional deaths [21]. As malaria and COVID-19 have similar initial symptoms, including fever, breathing difficulties, fatigue, and headaches, misdiagnoses can occur [22, 23]. In Cameroon, where malaria remains highly endemic and COVID-19 continues to pose a public health challenge, investigating co-exposure to Plasmodium and SARS-CoV-2 is essential to inform integrated disease surveillance and management strategies.

As of June 2024, Africa had reported 9,579,844 COVID-19 cases and 175,510 deaths, representing 1.2% and 2.8% of global cases and deaths, respectively. These figures are substantially lower than in Europe (36% of global cases, 32% of deaths) and the Americas (25% of cases, 43% of deaths) [4]. Various hypotheses have been proposed to account for the lower incidence and mortality rates in sub-Saharan Africa, including low testing capacity, a younger population, and effective control measures [24]. Additionally, cross-protective immunity acquired from vaccination against or exposure to other diseases may play a role [25, 26]. Moreover, although the relationship between malaria and COVID-19 remains poorly understood, some studies suggest that a recent history of malaria may provide protection against the severe COVID-19 [27].

This study aims to determine the seroprevalence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 and anti-Plasmodium antibodies and exploring the association between the presence of anti-COVID-19 IgG and IgM antibodies and anti-Plasmodium falciparum and anti-Plasmodium vivax in urban and rural areas of the West Region of Cameroon.

Materials and methods

Study sites and study population

This cross-sectional study was conducted in the West Region of Cameroon from 4th April to 22nd May 2022 at the Mifi District Hospital in Bafoussam and the District Medical Centre of Fongo-Tongo.

The Mifi District Hospital is a secondary level health facility located in the central urban zone of Bafoussam, the regional capital of the West Region of Cameroon. This hospital was selected as a site of samples collection because of its central location, which provides accessibility to residents throughout the city, ensuring a representative sample of SARS-CoV-2 infection in an urban population (Fig. 1). Participants were selected using a random sampling method from the population. According to the Cameroonian National Institute of Statistics (2021), the population of Bafoussam is estimated at 450,869, with a density of 864 inhabitants/km2. The activities of this population are trade, livestock breeding and agriculture [28].

Fongo-Tongo is a rural subdivision located in the Menoua Division, approximately 15 km from Dschang (the division’s largest town) (Fig. 1). The District Medical Centre of Fongo-Tongo was chosen because it is the primary healthcare facility, serving a rural population of 18,822 inhabitants with a density of 125 inhabitants/km2. The predominant activities in this area are agriculture and livestock [29].

Sampling procedure and data collection

Participants provided information on age, sex, education level, household size, history of SARS-CoV-2 vaccination, and symptoms experienced within the past 12 months (aches and pains, diarrhoea, dry cough, fever, headache, loss of taste and/or smell, nausea, and sore throat) through a questionnaire (supplementary file). The study included individuals residing in the urban area of Bafoussam and the rural area of Fongo-Tongo. The only exclusion criterion was being a hospitalized patient.

Detection of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies

The CE-marked Biopanda COVID-19 IgM/IgG Rapid Test Kit [sensitivity > 99% (95% CI: 93.3%–100%), specificity 98.6% (95% CI: 94.9%–99.8%)], a lateral flow immunoassay, was used to screen whole blood for IgM and IgG antibodies against SARS-CoV-2. Blood samples were collected via finger-prick puncture and tests were performed according to manufacturers’ instructions. The test was used to detect antibodies targeting the nucleocapsid (anti-N) protein, which indicates prior exposure to the virus.

Results were interpreted as outlined in Table 1. In positive test detecting anti-SARS-Cov-2 antibodies, the presence of IgM indicates a recent infection (likely within 28 days prior to sampling), while IgG appears approximately 14–28 days post-infection and persists for 3–12 months [10].

Detection of Plasmodium antibodies

The Onesite Malaria Pf/Pv Abcombo Rapid Diagnostic Test (mRDT) specificity of 98.0% (95% CI: 94.8%-99.4%) and sensibility of 96.4% (95% CI: 92.8%-98.6%) was used to detect the antibodies targeting the highly reactive Pf-MSP antigen of P. falciparum and Pv-MSP antigen of P. vivax. This lateral flow chromatographic immunoassay simultaneously detects and differentiates between anti-Plasmodium falciparum and anti-Plasmodium vivax IgG and IgM. Blood was collected from finger-prick punctures, and tests were conducted following the manufacturer instructions. Results were interpreted based on the presence or absence of bands indicating anti-Plasmodium falciparum and/or anti-Plasmodium vivax IgG and/or IgM.

Data analysis

Chi-square tests were used to compare the seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 and malaria antibodies across sample sites, age groups and sexes. A generalised linear binomial model was used to test the association between the presence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies and anti-Plasmodium antibodies. Logistic regression model was employed to identify symptoms associated with the presence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics of the study population

A total of 805 participants were included in this study, with 446 were from urban zone (Bafoussam) and 359 from the rural zone (Fongo-Tongo). Study demographics are summarised in Table 2. The majority of participants were women, accounting for 566/805 (70.3%) of the sample (Table 2). The mean age of the participants was 30.6 years in the urban zone and 42.3 years in the rural zone (Table 2). Among the urban participants, 145/446 (32.5%) had previously been tested for SARS-CoV-2, compared to 50/359 (13.9%) in the rural zone. In terms of vaccination, 44/446 (9.9%) participants from the urban zone had received at least one dose of a SARS-CoV-2 vaccine, compared to 16/359 (4.4%) in rural zone. Furthermore, in the rural zone, 44/359 (12.3%) participants had not attended conventional schooling, while this was true for only 1/446 (0.2%) in the urban zone. The median household size was 9 (C.I: 7.2–13.4) in urban and 5 (C.I: 3.0-8.4) in rural zone.

Seroprevalence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgM and IgG according to localities

The overall seroprevalence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies was 60.1% (491/805). The seroprevalence in urban zone was higher than in the rural zone (p = 0.0005) (Table 3). Among the 446 participants from the urban zone, 281 (63.0%) had IgG antibodies and 4 (0.9%) had IgM antibodies against SARS-CoV-2, while 12 (2.7%) had the two types of antibodies. In the rural zone, 186/359 (51.8%) had IgG antibodies, 1/359 (0.3%) had IgM antibodies, and 7/359 (1.9%) had both types of antibodies. The seroprevalence of IgG was higher (p = 0.008) in the urban zone compared to the rural zone. Regarding IgM, 16/446 (3.6%) and 8/359 (2.2%) participants from the urban and rural zones, respectively, had this antibody type, indicating recent SARS-CoV-2 infections. There was no significant difference in IgM seroprevalence between the two zones (p = 0.24). Owing to the low IgM-only participant count, analyses combined IgM/IgG.

Seroprevalence varied significantly difference between age groups in both the urban and rural zones. In the urban zone, the highest seroprevalence (73.5%) was observed among participants aged over 35 years, while in the rural zone, the highest seroprevalence (63.0%) was found among participants aged 8–18 years (Table 4).

Seroprevalence of anti-Plasmodium antibodies in the urban and rural zones

The overall seroprevalence of anti-Plasmodium antibodies was 22.2% (179/805), with significantly (p ˂ 0.0001) higher rates in the rural zone (31.5%) compared to the urban zone (14.8%) (Table 5). In the urban zone, the seroprevalence was 13.9% for anti-P. falciparum antibodies and 0.67% for anti-P. vivax antibodies, with 0.22% of participants having antibodies against both Plasmodium species. In the rural zone, the seroprevalence was 27.9% for anti-P. falciparum antibodies and 0.2% for P. vivax, with 3.3% of participants testing positive for both. Regardless of the study zone, anti-P. falciparum antibodies were more prevalent than anti-P. vivax antibodies (p ˂ 0.0001) (Table 5).

Association between the presence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies and anti-Plasmodium antibodies in urban and rural participants

Among the 805 participants, 387 (48.1%) tested positive for only anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies, while 74 (9.2%) had only anti-Plasmodium antibodies (Table 6). Additionally, 105 (13.0%) tested positive for antibodies against both infectious agents. The logistic regression model revealed no association between the presence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies and anti-Plasmodium antibodies (P = 0.44; Estimate: -0.13). However, when considering anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgM alone, an association (P = 0.007; Estimate: 1.15) with the presence of anti-Plasmodium antibodies was detected. The same trend of association was observed between IgM anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies with P. falciparum (P = 0.005; Estimate:1.15) and P. vivax (P = 0.002; Estimate: 2.02) (Table 6).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the prevalence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 and anti-Plasmodium antibodies in rural and urban areas of the West Region of Cameroon to improve the control of COVID-19 in Sub-Saharan Africa, where malaria exacerbates the already fragile health system. The findings show that during April/May 2022, the overall seroprevalence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies was 60.1%: nearly twice the 32% reported in 2020 during the first wave of COVID-19 in Yaoundé, Cameroon [15] and higher than the 43.7% reported in 2021 in Kaduna State, Nigeria [10], approximately 650 km from the sites examined in this study. The discrepancies are likely attributed to the timings of these studies as well as demographic differences between the study sites. In fact, this study was 4–5 months later than the Nigerian study. Our data were collected more than two years after the pandemic’ onset, following several waves of infection as well as the onset of a vaccination programme which likely increased seroprevalence in the population.

Our findings indicate a higher (p-value: ˂ 0.0001) seroprevalence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies in urban areas (66.6%) compared to rural areas (54.04%), consistent with studies from South Africa [30]. This trend can be explained by the higher population density and the increased contact among urban dwellers, facilitating virus transmission. In rural areas, limited population movement and social interactions, which and mainly occurred in marketplaces, during funerals or at village meetings, likely contributed to lower transmission rates. Our results contrast with SARS-CoV-2 seroprevalence studies in Kaduna State, Nigeria [10] which reported a higher seroprevalence in rural areas. These differences could be due to the timing of the study: our study was conducted when the pandemic was largely under control, with limited restrictions to movements or social distancing, while the Nigerian data was collected immediately prior to the Omicron variant surge, when less was known about transmission. It could also be because we were far enough into the pandemic that antibodies in the urban population had depleted beyond the level required to be detected.

The low prevalence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgM in both urban (3.6%) and rural (2.2%) areas suggests that few participants had recently been in contact with the virus during the study period. These results are lower than 10.2% and 6.5% reported for urban and rural areas, respectively in Nigeria [10]; indicating variations in transmission patterns by region and time of sampling.

Age also influenced seroprevalence. In the urban zone, the highest seroprevalence was recorded in participants over 35 years old, while in rural areas, the 8–18 years age group had the highest prevalence. Although the reasons of these observations are not clear, this aligns with studies from the United States, where younger people found it more challenging to adhere to preventative behaviours [22, 31]. The observed variations in the seroprevalence across age groups and regions may be attributed to behavioural differences within each setting. Understanding these differences is crucial for tailoring public health interventions to prevent and combat future waves of the pandemic. Nevertheless, we can also speculate that the observed differences between urban and rural populations in age distribution, education levels, and vaccination rates are notable and likely reflect underlying socio-economic and healthcare access disparities. For example, higher vaccination rates in urban areas may be influenced by better availability of healthcare services, greater health awareness, and easier access to vaccination centers. Conversely, lower vaccination coverage in rural areas might be affected by limited healthcare infrastructure, reduced health literacy, and differing healthcare-seeking behaviors.

The high seroprevalence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgG: 66.6% in the urban area and 53.8% in the rural area indicates that these participants had been exposed to the virus several weeks prior to sample collection. While vaccination likely played a role in these results, with 9.4% of urban participants and 4.5% of rural participants having received at least one dose of SARS-CoV-2 vaccine, the majority of seropositivity can be attributed to natural infection. Excluding vaccinated individuals, we can infer that at least 50% (491/805) of participants had been exposed to the virus. This highlights the widespread transmission of the virus despite few officially reported cases in these areas. As the populations in these zones were reluctant to receive vaccination similar to other regions of Cameroon a recently published systematic review reported that only 31.2% of Cameroonians were willing to be vaccinated, while 68.8% expressed some degree of vaccine hesitancy [32], the high seroprevalence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgG indicates not only important contacts between inhabitants and this virus, but also some level of protection against SARS-CoV-2 since it is this class of antibodies that is involved in immune memory such informations are of great value for the implementation and monitoring of preventive control measures, especially the vaccination program. However, to know whether these naturally acquired antibodies are protective against the different variants of SARS-CoV-2, there is a need to determine the lifespan of these antibodies. An additional factor that may contribute to the higher seroprevalence observed in urban settings is household size. Studies have shown that larger families are associated with increased risk of virus transmission [10, 33].

The seroprevalence of anti-Plasmodium antibodies was significantly higher in the rural area (31.4%) compared to the urban area (14.8%), consistent with findings from Gabon [34] and previous studies in Cameroon [35]. The higher seroprevalence in rural areas could be explained by more favourable bio-ecological and environmental factors that favor the development of the Anopheles mosquito, the vector of malariae transmission [36]. In fact, rural areas often have more abundant and suitable mosquito breeding sites, such as stagnant water bodies and agricultural irrigation systems, which sustain higher vector populations. Additionally, differences in housing quality and access to preventive measures like insecticide-treated nets may contribute to sustained malaria exposure [37, 38]. These contrasting patterns highlight the importance of considering local ecological and social contexts when designing public health interventions targeting co-endemic diseases. Furthermore, differences in the implementation of malaria prevention measures between urban and rural areas likely contributed to the observed variations anti-Plasmodium antibody seroprevalence. The predominance of anti-P. falciparum antibodies over anti-P. vivax antibodies is in line with previous studies [39] reporting a high prevalence of P. falciparum compared to other Plasmodium species in Cameroon, and the identification of P. falciparum as the Plasmodium species responsible for more than 90% of malaria deaths in sub-Saharan Africa [20].

Our study found no association between the overall presence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies and anti-Plasmodium antibodies, or between anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgG and anti-Plasmodium antibodies. However, an association was observed between anti-Plasmodium antibodies and anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgM. As IgM antibodies are typically produced early in infection, these results suggest possible interactions between malaria and SARS-CoV-2 during the initial stages of infection. At the same time, the possibility of a cross-reaction between the IgM test and the malaria screening test potentially due to shared or similar epitopes that stimulate immune responses cannot be ruled out [17]. Given these possibilities, we acknowledge the need for cautious interpretation and suggest that further studies employing more specific assays, such as neutralization tests or molecular diagnostics, are warranted to clarify the nature of this association. This finding warrants further investigation to better understand the dynamics of co-infection and its implications for public health, especially in regions where malaria is endemic.

Our results confirm associations between several COVID-19 symptoms (fever, dry cough, aches and pains, headache and loss of taste/smell) and the presence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies. This observation is consistent with previous studies [10, 19, 40]. However, these symptoms alone may have limited epidemiological value as they can be caused by other infectious pathogens, such as malaria.

The association between the identified symptoms and the presence of anti-Plasmodium antibodies is in line with results of previous studies [19, 41]. Nevertheless, the differential diagnosis is crucial in regions where multiple infectious diseases are prevalent.

These findings have important implications for public health policies in regions co-endemic for COVID-19 and malaria. The high seroprevalence of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies, especially in urban areas, underscores the need to strengthen surveillance systems to better monitor viral transmission dynamics over time. Specific vaccination strategies are essential to address disparities in vaccine uptake, particularly focusing on increasing coverage in rural populations where hesitancy and access barriers persist. Given the overlapping symptoms of COVID-19 and malaria and the potential for co-infection, integrated diagnostic and case management approaches should be prioritized to improve patient outcomes and optimize resource use. Furthermore, malaria control programs must continue to be supported alongside COVID-19 interventions, recognizing that persistent malaria transmission in rural areas remains a significant public health challenge.

Limitations of this study

Although this study provided important epidemiological information, it nonetheless presents certain limitations that should be addressed in subsequent work. First of all, this study focused on exploring associations involving the two predominant Plasmodium species, P. falciparum which is the most virulent, most dangerous, and most widespread species and P. vivax widely distributed. Further investigations including additional Plasmodium species such as P. malarae and P. ovale could provide a more comprehensive understanding of these associations. Furthermore, in this study, only antibodies against COVID-19 and malaria were detected. However, molecular analysis would have allowed for the direct detection of the infectious agents during the acute phase of COVID-19 infection and particularly in the case of malaria, for the identification of Plasmodium species and the assessment of parasite load in the participants. Finaly, the data presented in this study also have geospatial and temporal limitations, as they were collected at a single point in time. This approach cannot determine whether SARS-CoV-2 and malaria exposures occurred at the same time or establish causation from the observed associations. Measuring antibodies at a single timepoint cannot assess protection levels or sequence of infection. A broader spatial and temporal scope would have allowed for a more comprehensive overview.

Conclusion

This study revealed a moderate to high seroprevalence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies in both rural and urban areas of the West region of Cameroon, with higher rates in urban zones. Anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgG was predominant in both settings, while the low seroprevalence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgM suggests minimal contact with the virus at the time of sampling, and low levels of active transmission. In contrast, anti-Plasmodium antibodies were more prevalent in the rural zone, with P. falciparum emerging as the dominant species over P. vivax. Although these results showed that a large proportion of the population was exposed to the virus, it is crucial to determine the duration of these antibodies and assess whether naturally acquired immunity provides protection against various SARS-CoV-2 variants, and to what extent. Moreover, the observed association between anti-COVID-19 IgM and malaria suggests a potential link, but this remains inconclusive and requires further research. These findings underscore the need for integrated surveillance of COVID-19 and malaria in endemic regions like Cameroon.
