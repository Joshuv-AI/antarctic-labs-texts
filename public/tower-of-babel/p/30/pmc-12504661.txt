Development and psychometric evaluation of a new genetic attitudes questionnaire in two national samples of United States residents
Authors: Aleksander Ksiazkiewicz, Seyoung Jung, Stephen Schneider, Jingyu Qian, Jane Betchley, Carl A. Gunter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
With the proliferation of direct-to-consumer genetic testing and the advent of personalized medicine, protecting genomic data and defining acceptable use cases is a pressing policy issue. Existing measures of public attitudes toward genomic data and how they should be handled rely primarily on single-item measures or ad hoc scales with unknown psychometric properties. In this paper, we rely on two surveys to develop a psychometrically sound genetic utility, inaccuracy, and privacy (GUIP) questionnaire, compare this measure to a single-item measure, examine how this measure varies across demographic and attitudinal factors, and explore its predictive power for genetic data sharing. The first survey was exploratory and included both open- and closed-ended questions to gauge genetic attitudes. We oversampled respondents with minoritized racial and ethnic identities (Black, Latinx, and Indigenous) to compare how identity relates to attitudes about genetics. The second survey facilitated a factor analysis on items developed from the open-ended responses, examined measurement invariance in the factor structure across racial and ethnic identities, and explored how the GUIP varies across demographic and attitudinal factors. Finally, we examined the GUIP as a predictor of genetic data sharing with physicians, medical researchers, pharmaceutical companies, and law enforcement and willingness to participate in genetic research.

Introduction

Genetic technologies have advanced considerably in the past two decades, resulting in an increasing number of intersections with public policy. These include the rise of direct-to-consumer genetic testing1, the advent of medical practices that are targeted to a person s genetic makeup such as personalized medicine2,3, and novel uses of genetics by the state such as the use of public genetic databases to attempt to solve cold cases4. Many of these advances are driven by reduced testing costs, new analysis techniques, like genome wide association studies5, and the advent of polygenic risk scores for a variety of medical, physical, and psychological traits6,7. The increasing prevalence of genetic technologies has also heightened concerns about potential abuses of these technologies by governments8, insurers9, and employers10 in democratic contexts11, as well as concerns about the use of genetic technologies by authoritarian regimes as a means of state repression12.

This proliferation of human genetic technologies raises two questions from a public opinion perspective. First, what do citizens know about genetics and new genetic technologies? One approach to answer this question is to systematically develop a scale to measure genetic knowledge. For example, the University of North Carolina Genomic Knowledge Scale (UNC-GKS13) and the International Genetic Literacy and Attitudes Survey (iGLAS14) aim to measure genetic knowledge in the mass public. Langer and colleagues13 distinguished four domains of genetic knowledge (“the structure and function of genes, how they are inherited in families, their relation to health, and strengths and limitations of WES [whole exome sequencing]”), while Chapman and colleagues14 concluded that their genetic knowledge measure is multidimensional without specifying a specific domain structure. These approaches, among others, provide a solid foundation for understanding what aspects of genetics are generally understood by the public and what aspects of genetics are commonly misunderstood.

In addition to genetic knowledge, scholars have considered the question: what concerns do people have about genetic technologies and their use cases? Here a primary focus has been on security and privacy concerns, as well as studies that have focused on the use of genetics by specific entities, such as law enforcement, employers, and insurers. Clayton and colleagues15 conducted a systematic literature review and identified 53 studies of public attitudes regarding the privacy of genetic information through July 2017. They identified a gap in the literature around the measurement of genetic privacy, noting that “In many cases, however, that question [of genetic privacy] was posed poorly or only in the most general terms, leaving the reader and policy makers with little idea of what specific issue or issues actually concerned respondents.” From a research perspective, the real or perceived security and privacy considerations raised by genetic data sharing are a problem because the ability to share genetic data securely is essential to progress in the field16–18. This paper aims to develop a more robust measure of genetic security and privacy attitudes as an element of a broader measure of genetic attitudes.

Many scholars have considered different facets of attitudes toward genetics in general (e.g.,19–22) and direct-to-consumer genetic testing in particular23,24. In addition to a genetic knowledge measure, the iGLAS inventory14 also includes a set of genetic opinion questions that can be helpful in characterizing public attitudes about genetics. These opinion questions tap into a variety of topics without organizing them into dimensions of genetic attitudes. Unlike the genetic knowledge items in iGLAS, no factor analysis is reported for the opinion items. This has not prevented scholars from fruitfully using these items to explore public attitudes toward genetic testing25. Other researchers have sought to characterize specific aspects of public attitudes toward genetics, such as Smith and colleagues who developed a measure of the perceived utility of genomic sequencing using a sample of adults who had undergone genomic sequencing in a clinical context26. They identified an informational utility dimension and an emotional utility dimension in patients’ attitudes. In this paper, we aim to complement this existing work by systematically characterizing the dimensionality of overall attitudes toward genetics in a general population sample. We argue that the development of a general genetic attitudes scale is vital as genetic technologies develop further, are increasingly likely to enter people’s lives, and may become topics of political contestation.

In developing a general measure of genetic attitudes, we argue that the scale should undergo rigorous psychometric testing. The alternatives of using single-question measures or ad hoc multi-item measures are less than ideal. Using single-question measures of genetic privacy attitudes can lead to false negatives due to measurement error27, such that relationships with covariates that would be detectable with a longer measure are not detected. Moreover, single-item measures will, by necessity, fail to capture the dimensionality and complexities of multifaceted constructs. Using a multi-item scale that has been constructed on an ad hoc basis for a particular study is preferable to a single-item measure insofar as the multiple items likely reduce measurement error and may capture multiple facets of genetic attitudes. Nonetheless, the use of ad hoc multi-item measures that differ from study to study presents a problem for the generalizability of knowledge about genetic attitudes, as measures may not be comparable. In addition, without starting with a wide range of items that are then narrowed down systematically (e.g., by factor analysis), it is unclear whether an ad hoc scale captures all relevant dimensions of genetic attitudes. In systematically developing a multi-item scale for genetic attitudes it will be possible to identify each of the dimensions of genetic attitudes and to then optimize the scope and length of the measure to capture each of those dimensions accurately and parsimoniously.

In developing and evaluating our measure in the American context, we rely on samples with an oversample of African American, Latinx, and Indigenous respondents to test for intergroup differences in genetic attitudes. It is important to emphasize here that race and ethnicity are not biological categories28,29. Instead, race and ethnicity are socially constructed identities. In the United States, people’s racial and ethnic identities shape their lived experiences and social attitudes30. Consistent with this psychological literature, past research has found that racial and ethnic identities affect attitudes toward genetics31. At the same time, geneticists have argued that it is vital to increase the diversity of the research populations used in genetic research32. We argue that attracting participants with marginalized identities to participate in genetic research requires identifying how genetic attitudes are similar and differ between different identity groups.Fig. 1Paper structure flow chart.

The paper is divided into three parts (Fig. 1). First, we present Study 1, which relies on a combination of open-ended and closed-ended questions to develop an extensive list of items for the proposed scale. Second, we present the results of a survey using these items in an independent sample in Study 2 and perform an exploratory factor analysis, which identifies three dimensions of genetic attitudes. Third, we explore the properties of these three dimensions by examining how they differ across demographic variables (e.g., racial and ethnic identity, age, gender, party identification), comparing them to existing measures of genetic attitudes, willingness to share genetic data, and willingness to participate in genetic research.

In short, the primary contribution of the paper is the development of a 16-item Genetic Utility, Inaccuracy, and Privacy questionnaire (GUIP). We find that public attitudes toward genetics have three dimensions (utility, inaccuracy, and privacy/security) and that these dimensions differ in their predictors, the outcomes they predict, and their relationships to demographic factors, including racial and ethnic identity. Moreover, we find that the privacy/security dimension outperforms a conventional single-item measure of genetic privacy concerns by detecting relationships that the single-item measure misses.

Study 1

The goal of the first study was to generate a list of questions that could be used for the GUIP. We approached this in two ways. First, we compiled a list of themes that have been identified in the existing literature by Clayton and colleagues15. Second, we administered a survey of the American public that contained a series of open-ended questions regarding genetics. The aim of this survey was to identify additional themes that should be incorporated into the pool of items for the GUIP. For instance, one topic that was raised by a few respondents as a reason for their hesitancy surrounding genetic testing was the fear that their genetic material would be misused for the purpose of cloning. This is not a topic that arose in the Clayton et al. review, although it has been considered in recent legal work examining the Research Consent Document used by 23andMe, a direct-to-consumer genetic testing company33. The survey was administered to 1402 respondents residing in the United States recruited through Qualtrics between December 2021 and February 2022. Because of the historic abuses by the medical community of minoritized racial and ethnic groups in the United States34,35, it was important to collect an oversample of respondents who identified as Black or African American, Hispanic or Latino, or Indigenous (American Indian or Alaska Native and Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander) to ensure that we had coverage of concerns about genetic technologies from a range of perspectives. In the survey, respondents were asked, What racial or ethnic group best describes you? with nine response options: White, Black or African American, Hispanic or Latino, American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian or Asian American, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, Middle Eastern, Multiracial, and Other. Respondents were permitted to select multiple categories. Based on their responses, we generated a series of binary variables, each indicating identification with a specific racial or ethnic group. Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of the sample. The proportion who reported having used a direct-to-consumer genetic test (22 percent) is statistically identical to an independent YouGov sample collected in February 2022 (21 percent36). For response quality, we used an attention check question to filter out the responses from the respondents who failed to answer it correctly, and we filtered out 13% of the total respondents.

Respondents were asked a series of seven open-ended questions about genetic testing. The questions started very broad about the perceived benefits and concerns regarding genetic data (What kinds of benefits, if any, are there to using a genetic test? and What kinds of concerns, if any, do you have about genetic tests?) and then focused on topics like control of genetic information (Are you concerned about who has control over your genetic information? Why or why not?), anonymity (How concerned are you about whether your genetic information can be linked back to you? Why or why not?), and who has access to their genetic data (Are you concerned about different groups or organizations using your genetic information for various purposes? If so, what group for what purposes are you concerned about using your genetic information?, How concerned are you about who can access your genetic information? Should some people be allowed to access your genetic information? If so, who should be allowed to access your genetic information?, What about people or groups who should not be able to access your genetic information? Who should not have access to your genetic data?). We also asked a series of closed-ended questions about genetic knowledge, the use and sharing of genetic data, and experience with genetic testing. Table 1 presents self-reported experiences with genetic testing in three contexts: direct-to-consumer services, medical purposes, and genomic research studies. For the first two types of testing, respondents reported on their own experiences as well as those of a family member and a close friend.

Here we focus on the responses to the open-ended questions. These were hand-coded by a team of undergraduate research assistants at the University of Illinois. Each response was coded by at least two coders to identify themes that respondents raised about genetic data and its use. These themes were then compiled alongside the concerns raised by researchers who study attitudes toward genetics. Finally, this list of themes was used by the research assistants to generate a wide variety of items related to attitudes about genetics. Although it was not feasible to include every specific concern that respondents raised, we did attempt to reflect the underlying themes of all concerns (for example, many concerns fell under the umbrella of “unauthorized use of my genetic data” or use “without consent for unknown research”). The result was the list of 73 items used to develop the GUIP measure in Study 2 (see the supplementary information for the full list).

The items were organized thematically as related to medical genetics, ancestry/genealogy, policing/crime, non-medical research, familial relationships (e.g., paternity), regulation of genetic technologies, personal control of genetic data, uncertainty about genetics, and other attitudes (e.g., learning about yourself through genetic testing is fun). The valence of items was deliberately varied and covered a variety of topics within each theme, such as the utility of genetic testing, the ability of genetic testing to generate useful knowledge, the accuracy of tests, dangers around testing, the level of concern for particular use cases, and willingness to share data paid or unpaid, among others.

Study 2: Measures and sample characteristics

The goal of Study 2 was to take the items generated in Study 1 and administer them to a diverse sample of US residents (once again with an oversample of people with minoritized racial and ethnic identities). Using these data, we aim to determine the dimensional structure of genetic attitudes, evaluate the GUIP as both a dependent variable and independent variable, and test whether it outperforms a single-item measure of genetic privacy concerns (like that which was criticized by Clayton and colleagues15). We expected to find that genetic attitudes are multidimensional without a priori assumptions about what those dimensions would be. We also expected that by comparison to the multi-item measure the single-item measure would be shown to be susceptible to false negatives (i.e., that we would find significant statistical relationships with the GUIP that would not be significant if a single-item measure were used). Once the dimensions were identified by factor analysis, we utilized item response theory to reduce the scale length while maintaining measurement accuracy.

The survey was administered to 2216 respondents via Qualtrics in February and March 2023. Similar to Study 1, we used an attention check question for response quality, and we filtered out the responses from 17% of the total respondents, who did not answer the attention check question correctly. Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of the sample and self-reported experiences with genetic testing in three contexts: direct-to-consumer services, medical purposes, and genomic research studies (question wording available in supplementary information). For racial/ethnic identification, Study 1 used multiple binary variables to reflect respondents self-identification. In Study 2, to facilitate non-overlapping group comparisons, we created a single categorical variable in which respondents were coded into one of five racial/ethnic categories: White, Black, Latinx, Indigenous, or Other (see supplementary information for coding details). Throughout the text, when we refer to racial and ethnic identity, we refer to respondents using these identity labels for brevity and clarity.

As noted in the discussion of Study 1, the initial GUIP measure consisted of 73 items. In the factor analysis below, we assigned each item to an additive scale for the dimension on which it loaded most strongly. This means that each item is weighted equally in the final scale. Alternative approaches include calculating the latent score on each dimension using a weighted average of all items (where the weights are proportional to the item-level factor loadings on each dimension), assigning items to a single dimension and weighting them by their factor loadings when constructing the additive scale, or first reducing the number of items before calculating the dimensions. We opted to perform factor analysis on these 73 items and then reduce the scale by applying an Item Response Theory analysis.

We contrast the GUIP with a single-item measure of genetic privacy concerns (How concerned are you regarding the privacy of your genetic/DNA information?). Responses to this item were on a five-point scale (not at all concerned, somewhat concerned, concerned, very concerned, extremely concerned).

When examining how other variables relate to the GUIP, we look at racial and ethnic identity, age, gender, income, education, religiosity, parental status, and genetic testing experience (direct-to-consumer testing, genetic testing in a medical context, and past participation in genetic research). We also explore party identification to explore whether genetic attitudes are politically polarized. Beyond these demographic measures, we consider measures of concerns around genetic data, respondents’ knowledge about genetics13, risk tolerance37, and trust in institutions (governmental and scientific).

When examining how the GUIP relates to other variables as an independent variable, we examine willingness to share data with a variety of entities, including the respondent’s doctor, medical researchers, pharmaceutical companies, and law enforcement (How willing would you be to share your DNA data with:, see supplementary information for exact wording of response options). We also look at respondents’ reported willingness to participate in research in the past (Have you ever been asked to participate in genetic or genomic research (where you provide a DNA sample)? and Did you participate in the genomic research study?) and, among those who say they have not participated in the past, their willingness to do so in the future (Would you participate in a genomic research study if you were asked to?). The models use a suite of demographic controls.

Study 2: Results

Factor analysis

Fig. 2Scree plot of factor loadings for GUIP factor analysis.

Scree plot of factor loadings for GUIP factor analysis.

We conducted a factor analysis of the 73 items in the GUIP. The resulting Scree plot (Fig. 2) suggests that there are three primary factors that emerge from these data (see supplementary information for details).

The first dimension, on which 30 items load most heavily, has a positive valence, emphasizing the utility of genetic data (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.94). The second dimension, on which 29 items load most heavily, has a predominantly negative valence, emphasizing concerns about the inaccuracy or pointlessness of genetic testing (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.92). We see the first dimension as identifying valence toward genetics in general (and especially a sense that genetics is useful and has value) and the second dimension distinguishing whether genetic testing is seen as inaccurate or pointless. The third dimension, on which 14 items load most heavily, is characterized primarily by subject matter, specifically the importance of security and privacy in handling genetic data (Cronbach s alpha = 0.80). In short, we refer to these three dimensions as utility, inaccuracy, and privacy.

We then calculated additive subscales from the GUIP using all 73 items. Each subscale consisted of the items that loaded most strongly on that dimension. For the third dimension, four items were reverse coded based on their factor loadings (items 10, 12, 59, and 62).

The resulting subscales are statistically distinct. The first dimension is correlated negatively with the other two dimensions (−0.09 and −0.14, respectively). The second and third dimensions, while both expressing concern about genetics, are only modestly correlated (0.21).

We also conducted configural invariance testing to assess whether the overall structure of the model is invariant across different racial and ethnic identities. The consistency of the fit indices across groups, with CFI values ranging from 0.60 to 0.65 and RMSEA values ranging from 0.070 to 0.075, suggests that the model is not specifically tailored to one particular group but is applicable across all groups.

GUIP reduced scale

We aimed to reduce the scale in order to balance length and accurate measurement. While the 73 items capture the multifaceted nature of the GUIP, a scale of this length is not practicable in many research applications. We therefore employ scale reduction to strategically enhance survey efficiency and mitigate respondent fatigue (i.e., the reduction in response quality that can result from respondents’ attention waning when surveys are too long or repetitive).Fig. 3Item information curve for each GUIP item. Each sub-figure corresponds to the items that loaded most strongly on each of the three GUIP dimensions.

Item information curve for each GUIP item. Each sub-figure corresponds to the items that loaded most strongly on each of the three GUIP dimensions.

We conducted an Item Response Theory (i.e., IRT) analysis, which is a statistical framework used for test development, calibration, and validation. IRT focuses on the inherent properties of each item and models the relationship between individuals’ latent traits and their responses to test items38,39. We calculated IRT parameters for each dimension. Item-level information clarifies how well each item contributes to estimation precision, with higher levels of information leading to more accurate estimates. For example, the slope parameter indicates discriminant power differentiating respondents with different levels of the latent trait where the larger the parameter, the more information the item provides. We present Item Information Curves (i.e., IIC) (Fig. 3), which is one of the ways to visually examine these parameters. Typically, an optimally informative item for the reduced scale will have a large location and broad category coverage.

Using the results of the IRT analysis, we sought to select items that cover both the three dimensions identified in the factor analysis and the nine topics identified from coding open-ended responses in Study 1. Below, we present the GUIP scale consisting of 16 psychometrically sound items, which reflects three dimensions (PC1: 6 items; PC2: 5 items; and PC3: 5 items) and captures eight different topics (Medical: 3 items; Ancestry/Genealogy: 2 items; Policing/Crime: 1 item; Non-Medical Research: 2 items; Familial Relationship: 3 items; Regulation: 1 item; Control of data: 2 items; and Other Attitudes: 2 items). No items from the topic uncertainty about genetics were retained for the final scale. This scale also effectively represents the keywords frequently used in the 73 items, such as ‘benefit’, ‘accuracy’, ‘concern’, and ‘comfortable’. We find that members of racial and ethnic minority groups have more reservations about genetics, but many of these differences disappear when controls are included in the model. This suggests that differences between racial and ethnic groups about genetics may be explained by other demographic, experiential, knowledge, and attitudinal factors. We compared respondents with Black, Latinx, Indigenous, and other racial identities to White identifying respondents as a baseline. We found that some identities were associated with perceptions of lower utility (Black, other racial identity), higher inaccuracy (Black, Latinx), and greater privacy concerns (Other racial identity). All of these effects are no longer significant in the full model with controls, except for the heightened privacy concerns. Indigenous respondents express lower inaccuracy concerns than White respondents, robust to controls. In sum, this scale provides limited evidence for the belief that negative attitudes and concerns about genetics are more prevalent among people of color, as the results vary considerably across different dimensions, groups, and model specifications. Moreover, when we compare racial and ethnic identities on the single-item measure of genetic privacy concern, these effects are not evident. As compared to the longer GUIP measure, the single-item measure of privacy concerns produces a false negative.

The three dimensions of the GUIP demonstrate a multifaceted pattern in relation to previous experience with genetic testing. Those with greater testing experience rate higher in utility and lower in privacy concerns, although these differences disappear in the full model. In addition, prior testing experience is also associated with a greater likelihood of viewing genetic testing as inaccurate or pointless. Whether this is an indicator that those with prior testing experience are disenchanted with what they learned (or didn’t learn) based on their testing may be worth considering in future research.

The relationship between the GUIP and genetic knowledge is complex, as different types of genetic knowledge show different associations with attitudes about genetic testing. We utilized three subscales focused on knowledge about genes themselves, genes and health, and how genes are inherited in families13. In the full model, we find that those with higher genetic knowledge about genes are more likely to view genetics as useful, less likely to view genetics as inaccurate, and more likely to have privacy concerns. A better understanding of genes and health is associated with a lower likelihood of seeing genetics as inaccurate. More knowledge on how genes are inherited in families has a negative association with viewing genetics as inaccurate and a positive association with privacy concerns about genetic data. Thus, in general (and with some variation across kinds of genetic knowledge), those with more genetic knowledge are more likely to view genetics as useful, less likely to view genetics as inaccurate, more likely to have privacy concerns, perhaps because they see the dangers of data mismanagement. Future research should consider in greater detail how specific types of genetic knowledge translate into attitudes about genetic testing and how interventions to improve genetic knowledge influence genetic attitudes.

Finally, turning to the potential political polarization of GUIP attitudes, we observe partisan differences only on the first dimension of the GUIP scale. Thus, while Democrats, Republicans, and Independents share, on average, similar privacy concerns and views on the accuracy of genetic testing, Democrats are significantly more likely than Republicans or Independents to view genetics as useful. This effect persists in the full model when accounting for the higher trust in science and trust in government among Democrats. Why this partisan difference persists in the face of these and other controls remains an open question, and one that may matter for building broad public support for further investment in genetic technologies and research.

In sum, these results demonstrate the added value of using a multidimensional measure of genetic attitudes such as the GUIP. Across these three dimensions, the predictors of genetic attitudes differ systematically. In addition, by having a longer measure of genetic privacy attitudes, we were able to detect relationships that a simple, single-item measure missed and thereby avoid false negatives. For example, if this study had relied only on a single-item measure (or an ad hoc measure that did not capture the various facets of genetic attitudes), we would have missed how racial and ethnic identity affects security and privacy concerns. Understanding how to address these concerns can benefit ongoing efforts to recruit people with marginalized identities to genetic studies to counteract bias in existing samples.

In this second set of analyses, we used the three dimensions of the GUIP as independent variables. We focus on two outcomes. First, we look at the willingness to share data with four types of entities: the respondent’s physician, medical researchers, pharmaceutical companies, and law enforcement (see Tables 5, 6). Second, we asked respondents if they would be willing to participate in genetics research in the future if asked. It is noteworthy that for each of these outcomes, there was no significant effect when a single-item measure of genetic privacy concerns was used, as is commonly utilized in the literature. Contrasted with the findings shown below, this suggests that the single-item measure produces false negatives when considering willingness to share data and willingness to participate in genetic research.

When looking at the effect of GUIP attitudes on willingness to share data, the results reported in Table 6 include controls for racial and ethnic identity, experience with genetic testing, genetic knowledge, demographics, and risk and trust attitudes. For sharing genetic data with the respondent’s physician, we find that utility predicts data sharing and inaccuracy predicts not sharing. The effect of utility is nearly three times the size of the effect of inaccuracy. There is no significant effect of privacy concerns, perhaps reflecting the generally trusting attitude that respondents may have toward their own physician. Racial and ethnic identity does not affect this outcome.

We do find effects of racial and ethnic identity on willingness to share data with medical researchers, pharmaceutical companies, and the police. Specifically, those whose racial identity does not fit into one of our four primary categories in this study (White, Black, Latinx, or Indigenous; for example, Asian respondents and people with multiple racial identities that do not also identify as Latinx), are less willing to share their data with any of these entities. Black respondents are also less willing to share their data with the police, likely for reasons to do with racial inequalities in law enforcement. Somewhat surprisingly, we do not find that people who identify as Native American, Alaska Native, Native Hawaiian, or Pacific Islander are less willing to share their data, which we expected based on the research literature and past conflicts over the mishandling of Indigenous data.

The relationship between the GUIP and willingness to share data with medical researchers, pharmaceutical companies, and law enforcement is consistent across some dimensions of genetic attitudes, but not others. In each case, those who see genetics as useful are much more willing to share their data with all three types of entities, and those with privacy concerns are less willing to do so. While inaccuracy beliefs increase the willingness to share data in Table 5 (no controls), it is not a significant predictor of willingness to share data with medical researchers and law enforcement in Table 6 (controlled). The second set of analyses focus on participation in genetic research. We distinguish between respondents who self-reported being asked to participate in genetic research in the past (21 percent of respondents of which 59 percent said they agreed to participate, see Table 7) and those who self-reported not being asked previously (79 percent of the sample of which 39 percent said they would participate if asked, see Table 8). For comparison, the proportion of respondents who report having been asked to participate in genetic research is somewhat lower than the proportion of respondents who report using a direct-to-consumer genetic test (28 percent; see Table 1). Future research could aim to understand in what context respondents are having recruitment experiences (e.g., through direct-to-consumer genetic testing companies or other entities). The analyses on willingness to participate in research complement those discussed previously about willingness to share data with medical researchers and pharmaceutical companies.

We find that in the hypothetical case, with all controls in the model Black respondents remain less likely to agree to participate in the proposed genetic research than White respondents. In the self-reported case, we find hesitancy among Latinx respondents. In contrast to the willingness questions above, the differences between White respondents and those in the Other category disappear in the full model. Some of the observed effect among Black and Latinx respondents may be accounted for by group-level differences in privacy concerns, but controlling for those concerns does not eliminate this intergroup effect.

The contrast of the effects of the GUIP dimensions on these research participation questions is puzzling. The positive effect of the first dimension on research participation is only observed in the case of a hypothetical research study, not among those who self-reported being asked to participate in the past. The privacy dimension has a consistently negative effect across both outcomes without controls, but these effects lose significance in the full model. Inaccuracy predicts lower interest in participating in a future study, but, notably, inaccuracy predicts a higher likelihood of self-reporting past genetic research participation. One possibility is that respondents are getting less benefit from participating in research than they anticipated when they volunteered for the study and therefore developing more negative attitudes toward genetic research after having participated. This is not the only plausible interpretation of this result, but it is a worrisome possibility that should be explored further.

Discussion and conclusion

This paper proposes a new measure of genetic attitudes, the Genetic Utility, Inaccuracy, and Privacy Questionnaire (GUIP). We contribute to the literature by demonstrating that public attitudes toward genetics are multifaceted and cannot be accurately captured with a single-item measure. The GUIP distinguishes three dimensions of attitudes: a dimension that captures the idea that genetic testing is positive and useful, a dimension that captures the idea that genetic testing is inaccurate or pointless, and a dimension that captures security and privacy concerns regarding genetic data. These dimensions can be summarized as encompassing utility, inaccuracy, and privacy.

It is worth considering how these three dimensions fit with theories of attitudes in general. Following the terminology from Fabrigar and colleagues40, what we refer to as genetic attitudes is an inter-attitudinal structure about genetic testing. This belief system can be thought of as made up of a series of genetics-related nodes that each have an evaluation (positive or negative) and attributes attached to them. These nodes are linked to each other in a network of associations. Our goal has been to identify how these nodes are clustered together (i.e., which nodes are linked together and covary) in order to identify the distinct dimensions of the overall inter-attitudinal structure.

Our data-driven approach to identifying the dimensions of genetic attitudes was agnostic about the number of dimensions that we would identify in this belief system and the attributes that those dimensions encompass. The three dimensions that we found (utility, inaccuracy, privacy) speak theoretically to the elements that make up the inter-attitudinal structure of genetic testing in the mass public in the United States. The analysis suggests that these sets of attitudes are largely, although not entirely, orthogonal, meaning that users can be high or low in any of these three dimensions. Pragmatically, these three dimensions refer to three sets of objections that genetic testing users, such as patients, clients, customers, or research participants, may have to utilizing genetic testing. Prospective users are more willing to engage with genetic testing if they believe that 1) there is value to be found in genetic information 2) that existing technologies are sufficiently accurate to unlock that value and 3) that their genetic information will be adequately protected to prevent misuse.

We find that each of these three dimensions has unique relationships with demographic and attitudinal factors (including heightened security and privacy concerns among some people of color), that each of these dimensions differentially predicts willingness to share genetic data and participate in genetic research, and that these relationships are obscured when we conducted these same analyses using a single-item measure of genetic privacy concerns (i.e., the single-item measure results in a false negative result where a real relationship is not detected). In short, we argue that the GUIP presents a significant step forward in understanding public attitudes in this rapidly evolving technological space and that there is a pressing need for scholars to better understand the nature of genetic attitudes as public policy debates related to human genetics are becoming increasingly relevant.

Although the GUIP scale has been designed to be used in its entirety, there may be circumstances where future researchers want to measure only one dimension (e.g., if survey space is limited). For instance, a scholar investigating genetic privacy attitudes may want to utilize only the privacy subscale. While the data presented here suggest that using the privacy subscale will provide a more accurate measure of people’s attitudes than a single-item approach, some caution is also warranted. Because the three dimensions are moderately correlated with each other, using one subscale in an analysis without controlling for the other dimensions may confound the analysis with unwanted attitudinal variance. In short, a more accurate estimate of the effect of any one dimension can be achieved by controlling for the other two.

There are several avenues for future research on public attitudes toward genetics that stem from this project. First, researchers should continue to examine the tradeoff between a longer, multi-dimensional measure like the GUIP and single-item measures of genetic attitudes. For substantive research, the evidence suggests that a longer measure may reveal relationships that are otherwise obscured, but there may be use cases where a single-item measure will suffice when survey space is at a premium (although it may lead to a false negative). Our ongoing work on responses to new genetic testing frameworks bears out the value of using this longer measure to understand public responses to new genetic technologies.

Second, more research is needed on how demographic differences and life experiences shape attitudes toward genetics and knowledge of genetics. This fits into a broader conversation about trust in science, techno-optimism or techno-skepticism, and the adoption of personalized medicine.

Third, we did find evidence for partisan differences in viewing genetics as useful (higher among Democrats) and willingness to share genetic data with law enforcement (higher among Republicans). Researchers should examine these relationships further to understand whether attitudes toward genetics are susceptible to political polarization and how public attitudes are evolving on police and immigration enforcement use of genetic technologies like genetic genealogy.

Fourth, it is worthwhile to consider how the GUIP can be of use in applied settings. In the clinical context, it may be a tool to understand the concerns of patients about participating in medically-recommended genetic testing. Clinicians could then focus their communication depending on a patient’s concerns on the benefits of the test, its accuracy, or the steps that are taken to ensure the security and privacy of patient data. In the context of legal representation, a similar approach could be useful in addressing client concerns. Moreover, at the institutional level, understanding public attitudes toward genetics is an important component of building trust in constructing state-run datasets. For example, in designing a genetic database as part of a public biobank or for the purpose of forensic genealogy testing by the police, it would be desirable to foster public buy-in by designing the system to be responsive to public beliefs and concerns about genetic testing.

Fifth, the GUIP was developed in English in the United States and although we made efforts to capture the views of a diverse range of American residents, it may or may not generalize to other linguistic or national contexts. On the one hand, people around the world are confronting the emergence of new genetic technologies in the 21st century, so questions addressing the utility, inaccuracy, and privacy of genetic testing are likely to be relevant in a variety of contexts. On the other hand, countries vary widely in how they regulate genetic technologies, how accessible genetic testing is in medical and direct-to-consumer contexts, and the degree to which direct-to-consumer testing is popular with the public. Determining the cross-cultural validity of the GUIP, that is to what extent it generalizes to other countries and to what extent it needs to be adapted to local realities, is an important area for future research.

Sixth, it would be worthwhile to systematically test the relationships among various aspects of genetic attitudes. We point to several promising scales of genetic knowledge and perceptions of genetic utility in the introduction. Conducting studies to better understand how those measures relate to the GUIP could shed new light on the nuances of genetic attitude testing and reveal what use cases each measure is best suited for.

In short, our hope with the GUIP is to provide a tool to the research, clinical, and legal communities to better understand the nature of attitudes toward genetics, and to examine the antecedents and consequences of those attitudes.
