Glomus tumor of the lower extremity
Authors: Anirudh Vemulapalli, Brooke Presny, Indrani Sen
Published: 2026

A 72-year-old man presented to the clinic for evaluation of a painful, localized, touch-sensitive nodule on the left knee. The nodule had been present for 5 years but became increasingly painful in the past year. He was first referred to orthopedics because he had undergone a left total knee replacement 1 year earlier. He was evaluated for this nodule and referred to vascular surgery following imaging; computed tomography angiography showed this lesion to be an arteriovenous malformation (A). On clinical examination, the nodule was firm, well circumscribed, and tender; bedside ultrasound examination confirmed it was hypoechoic (B), with no internal flow. The 1.1 × 0.7 cm fleshy nodule was excised completely under local anesthesia (C/Cover). No other lesions were detected. There was no named/axial artery or vein associated with this lesion. The preoperative diagnosis was suspected to be a glomus tumor and confirmed on histology (C). There were no synchronous tumors or satellite lesions. No other lesions were detected. Postoperatively, the patient recovered without complication. He reported complete and immediate resolution of pain; the incision had healed well at the 2-week follow-up. The patient consented to this publication.

Glomus tumors are perivascular tumors that present as small, painful digital (subungual) nodules. These rare, predominantly benign neoplasms arise from the neuromyoarterial glomus body, a thermoregulatory structure involved in arteriovenous shunting.1, 2, 3 Despite the term glomus tumor, glomus tumors of the extremities are distinct neoplasms as compared with glomus tumors of the head and neck or carotid body tumors. They have different cells of origin, histologies, immunophenotypes, and genetics.2,4,5 One clinical association common to these unrelated tumor types is neurofibromatosis type 1; these patients are predisposed to glomus tumors of the digits as well as pheochromocytomas and paragangliomas.4,5 There are no specific congenital abnormalities associated with lower extremity glomus tumors.1

A significant proportion of extradigital glomus tumors are frequently misdiagnosed, with an average diagnostic delay of >7 years.1 Glomus tumors of the leg are very rare; in a series of 56 patients with extradigital glomus tumors, only 3 cases involved the leg. Other differential diagnoses include vascular myoma, hemangioma, neuroma, and nodular hidradenoma. The symptom triad of a painful nodule, localized point tenderness, and cold sensitivity is considered characteristic and strongly indicative of the diagnosis; however, all three symptoms may not be present in extradigital glomus tumors. Preoperatively, the diagnosis may be suspected on imaging like magnetic resonance imaging (decreased signal intensity on T1-weighted images, and increased signal intensity on T2-weighted images) or ultrasound examination (round or ovoid hypoechoic masses, and have been reported as hypervascular), or a computed tomography scan (soft tissue mass that enhances with contrast). A definitive diagnosis is established through histopathological evaluation, and complete surgical excision remains the treatment of choice. Malignant transformation and metastasis are exceedingly rare.
