SIRT3 deficiency impairs mitochondrial bioenergetics via hyperacetylation of TCA cycle enzymes in chronic heart failure
Authors: Yichen Liao, Xuxin Tan, Guanglin Peng, Yan Ren, Ruixue Liu, Haitang Liao, Zhenchun Luo, Zhezhe Cao, Yaguang Wu, Milad Ashrafizadeh, João Conde, Chenyang Duan, Jun Hu, Ruiyan Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMitochondrial dysfunction is a hallmark of heart failure (HF), but its upstream regulatory pathways remain incompletely understood. Sirtuin 3 (SIRT3), a mitochondrial deacetylase, is crucial for maintaining enzymatic activity through deacetylation. This study investigates whether SIRT3 downregulation leads to mitochondrial metabolic impairment in HF by enhancing acetylation of tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle enzymes.MethodsA chronic HF model was established in male C57BL/6J mice via transverse aortic constriction (TAC). Cardiac function and morphology were evaluated by echocardiography, histological staining, and transmission electron microscopy. Mitochondrial function was assessed using ATP quantification, Seahorse XF analysis, and enzymatic activity assays. Protein acetylation and SIRT3 expression were examined through Western blotting and acetyl-proteomics. Angiotensin II-treated cardiomyocytes served as an in vitro model.ResultsTAC-induced HF led to cardiac remodeling and mitochondrial damage. Proteomic analysis revealed increased global protein acetylation, especially in mitochondrial proteins related to the TCA cycle. SIRT3 was significantly downregulated, corresponding with hyperacetylation and suppressed activity of pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH), succinate dehydrogenase (SDH), and citrate synthase (CS), which was associated with impaired respiration and reduced ATP output. Ang II-treated cells showed similar changes.ConclusionSIRT3 downregulation in HF drives TCA enzyme hyperacetylation and metabolic dysfunction, suggesting a key mechanism linking protein acetylation imbalance to mitochondrial impairment in disease progression.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-07973-x.Keywords: Chronic heart failure, SIRT3, Protein acetylation, Mitochondrial dysfunction, Cardiac metabolism

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is an increasingly prominent global public health challenge [1]. The incidence and mortality of HF continue to rise worldwide, making it a major threat to human health [2, 3]. Mitochondria communicate their metabolic fitness to the nucleus through multiple signaling pathways, most notably via TCA cycle metabolites, which regulate gene expression, cell fate, stress responses, and disease by influencing chromatin, DNA methylation, and protein function [4]. This condition not only leads to the progressive deterioration of myocardial structure and function but also triggers a series of complex metabolic disturbances, further compromising patients’ quality of life and clinical outcomes [5, 6]. As the most metabolically active organ in the body, the heart requires substantial energy to maintain its contractile and diastolic functions. This high energy demand is primarily provided by the abundant mitochondria in the cardiomyocytes. Under physiological conditions, cardiac ATP production is predominantly derived from fatty acid oxidation (accounting for 60–90%), while the remaining 10–40% originates from pyruvate oxidation. Alternative substrates such as ketone bodies and amino acids serve as secondary energy sources under normal conditions. The relative contribution of each substrate to the ATP production can vary considerably depending on energy demand, substrate availability, and hormonal status.

HF is a growing, costly condition with high mortality and preventable hospitalization rates, prompting to identify which interventions most effectively reduce (re)hospitalizations in patients [7]. The TCA cycle, also known as the Krebs cycle, has been emerged as a particularly critical focus. This mitochondrial pathway plays a central role in oxidizing nutrients to generate energy, serving as the core of cardiac and systemic metabolic activity [8, 9]. Mitochondrial dysfunction can influence cellular fate and function by disrupting the TCA cycle. Once considered merely metabolic byproducts, TCA cycle intermediates are now recognized as key regulators of chromatin remodeling, DNA methylation, and post-translational protein modifications [10–12]. Therefore, elucidating and targeting the role of the TCA cycle in HF may offer novel therapeutic avenues and strategies.

Protein acetylation is a widely occurring post-translational modification in cells and plays a pivotal role in numerous physiological and pathological processes [13, 14]. This modification is critically involved in the cellular signal transduction, energy homeostasis, and antioxidant defense mechanisms. Specifically, protein acetylation refers to the transfer of an acetyl group from acetyl-CoA to the lysine residues of target proteins, a process that effectively neutralizes the positive charge of lysine side chains. Among the key regulators of this process, the sirtuin family of proteins has garnered significant attention.

In humans, this family comprises seven members, SIRT1 through SIRT7 [15]. Sirtuins (Sirt1–7) are NAD+ -dependent class III histone deacetylases that link cellular metabolism to epigenetic regulation and broad stress-response programs, affecting aging, energy balance, inflammation, apoptosis, mitochondrial biogenesis, and circadian control. In the heart, different sirtuins modulate mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, hypertrophy, ischemia–reperfusion injury, and HF (notably Sirt1, Sirt3, Sirt6, and Sirt7), making them context- and dose-dependent regulators and potential therapeutic targets in cardiovascular disease [16]. Notably, among the sirtuin family, SIRT3, SIRT4, and SIRT5 are primarily localized in the mitochondria. SIRT3, in particular, serves as the major mitochondrial deacetylase and holds a central role within this organelle [17]. Full-length SIRT3 is a 44 kDa protein that is directed to the mitochondria via a specific mitochondrial targeting sequence, where it undergoes cleavage to produce a 28 kDa catalytically active form. Importantly, SIRT3 has been shown to deacetylate and activate several key enzymes involved in the mitochondrial energy metabolism. These include long-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (LCAD) involved in fatty acid oxidation, as well as succinate SDH and isocitrate dehydrogenase 2 in the TCA cycle. More information can be found in these studies [18–20].

Although previous studies have implicated SIRT3 in mitochondrial function, its systemic role in coordinating TCA cycle acetylation remodeling and myocardial metabolic reprogramming remains unclear. Therefore, from a systems metabolic regulatory perspective, this study focuses on SIRT3-mediated mitochondrial protein deacetylation to elucidate its regulatory effects on TCA cycle activity and myocardial energetic homeostasis, and to define the mechanistic link between SIRT3, TCA cycle acetylation, and mitochondrial bioenergetic dysfunction.

Methods

Materials

MitoTracker (Deep Red, M22426) was purchased from Invitrogen (California, USA). The ATP Assay Kit (S0026), HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H+L) (A0208), HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H+L) (A0216), JC-1 fluorescent probe (C2005), Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) Assay Kit (S0033S),and One-Step TUNEL Apoptosis Detection Kit (C1088) were obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). The BCA Protein Assay Kit (23227) and Hoechst 33,342 were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Massachusetts, USA). The 24-well XFe Cell Culture Microplates (102340–100), Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (103260–100), Seahorse XF DMEM Medium (103575–100), Seahorse XF 100 mmol/L Pyruvate Solution (103575–100), and Seahorse XF 200 mmol/L Glutamine Solution (103579–100) were purchased from Agilent Cell Analysis Technologies (California, USA). Antibodies against SIRT3 (5490), GAPDH (2118), and β-tubulin (2146) were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Boston, USA). Antibodies against CS (67784–1-Ig), SDHA (14865–1-AP), and DLAT (13426–1-AP) were purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). The pan-acetylation antibody (PTM-105) was purchased from PTM Bio (Hangzhou, China). The Mitochondrial Stress Test Complete Assay Kit (ab197243), Pyruvate Dehydrogenase Activity Assay Kit (ab109902), Succinate Dehydrogenase Activity Assay Kit (ab109908), SIRT3 Activity Assay Kit (ab156067) and Citrate Synthase Activity Assay Kit (ab119692) were obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). The Minute™ Mitochondria Isolation Kit (MP-007) was purchased from Invent Biotechnologies, Inc. (Minnesota, USA). Lentiviruses for SIRT3 overexpression and corresponding controls were provided by GeneChem (Shanghai, China).

Animals

All animal procedures were approved by and conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No.: IACUC-SAHCQMU-2025–0150). Male inbred C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Animals were housed in a temperature-controlled (22 ± 2°C) and humidity-controlled facility at the Animal Experiment Center of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, with free access to fresh water and standard laboratory chow. Experiments were conducted using 10- to 12-week-old mice weighing 25–28 g. Donor mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (35 mg/kg). To induce HF, the abdominal aortic constriction (AAC) model was established. C57BL/6J mice underwent transverse aortic constriction, left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery ligation, or sham surgery. After intubation, ventilation was maintained at a pressure of 15 cm H₂O. A thoracotomy was performed, and a 9–0 Prolene suture was placed and ligated around the LAD at the level of the aortic arch or 2 mm below the left atrium. In the sham group, the ligation was omitted. The thoracic cavity was closed by suturing the adjacent ribs and skin. Mice were euthanized 8 weeks after surgery.

RNA isolation and real-time reverse transcription (RT)-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cardiac tissue samples using TRIzol reagent, followed by reverse transcription to synthesize complementary DNA (cDNA). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using BeyoFast™ SYBR Green qPCR Mix (2X) on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., California, USA). Real-time qPCR was carried out for 40 amplification cycles. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: a. Initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 minutes; b. Denaturation at 95 °C for 15 seconds; c. Annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 seconds; d. Repeat steps b and c for a total of 40 cycles; e. Melting curve analysis (optional): 95 °C for 15 seconds, 60 °C for 15 seconds, and 72 °C for 30 seconds; f. qPCR data were analyzed using the system software. The relative expression level of each gene was calculated based on the Ct values using the following formula: Relative expression = 2^−ΔΔCt. ΔΔCt = (Ct_target − Ct_GAPDH) experimental − (Ct_target − Ct_GAPDH) control. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Evaluation of cardiac function in mice by color doppler echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed using the high-frequency color Doppler ultrasound system (Vevo 9, GE Healthcare, Boston, USA) under inhalational anesthesia with isoflurane (0.25%–0.50%). Cardiac function parameters, including left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD), left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVESD), ejection fraction (EF, %), and fractional shortening (FS, %), were measured. The values were averaged from three consecutive cardiac cycles for analysis.

Establishment of the cellular HF model

The HL-1 cardiomyocyte cell line was purchased from Haixing Bio (China) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). To establish the HF cellular model, cells were treated with 100 nM angiotensin II (Ang II) for 48 hours prior to subsequent experiments.

Immunoprecipitation (IP) and western blot analysis

HL-1 cells were lysed on ice for 2 hours in IP lysis buffer containing phosphatase inhibitors, EDTA, and protease inhibitors. Lysates were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4 °C to remove cell debris. The supernatant was collected and divided into three groups: input, IgG control, and IP. The IgG control group was incubated with an isotype-matched control IgG antibody, while the IP group was incubated with a target-specific primary antibody. Both groups were gently rotated overnight at 4 °C. Washed Protein A/G agarose beads (approximately 50 μl) were then added to the IgG and IP groups, followed by 3 hours of incubation at 4 °C with gentle rotation. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 2 minutes at 4 °C to discard the supernatant. The beads were washed 3–5 times with 500 μl of ice-cold lysis buffer. Beads were then resuspended in 50 μl of 2× SDS loading buffer and mixed thoroughly. Samples were heated at 100 °C for 10 minutes to dissociate immune complexes.

Equal amounts of protein were resolved by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE, with specified acrylamide concentration) and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in blocking buffer at room temperature for 1 hour, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer. After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer for 1 hour at room temperature. Chemiluminescent signals were detected using an ECL detection kit, and signal intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Following euthanasia, cardiac tissues were rapidly excised and cut into small blocks (1–2 mm3), then immediately fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde. Post-fixation was performed in 1% osmium tetroxide at 4 °C for 1–2 hours to enhance membrane contrast. Samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, 100%) and acetone, with each step lasting 10–15 minutes. The dehydrated samples were then infiltrated with epoxy resin and polymerized at 60 °C for 24–48 hours to form hardened blocks. Ultrathin sections (50–70 nm) were prepared using an ultramicrotome and mounted on copper grids. Sections were sequentially stained with 2% uranyl acetate and lead citrate for 10 minutes each to enhance electron density contrast. Mitochondrial ultrastructure was visualized and imaged using a transmission electron microscope (H-7500; Hitachi, Japan).

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining

Cardiac tissue sections were deparaffinized and subsequently stained with hematoxylin to visualize cell nuclei, followed by eosin staining to highlight the cytoplasm and extracellular matrix. Myocardial injury was assessed based on parameters including cellular disruption, nuclear loss, and architectural disorganization. The extent and severity of cardiomyocyte damage were scored using the following criteria: 0 = normal; 1 = <25% of the area affected; 2 = 25–50%; 3 = 50–75%; 4 = >75%.

Proteomics and bioinformatics analysis

Samples were lysed in urea buffer to extract proteins, and protein concentrations were determined using the Bradford assay. Equal amounts of protein were used for downstream acetylated peptide enrichment. Proteins were reduced with dithiothreitol, alkylated with iodoacetamide, and digested with trypsin, followed by C18-based desalting of peptides. Acetylated peptides were enriched using anti-acetyl-lysine antibody-based affinity chromatography, washed, and eluted from the antibody beads with trifluoroacetic acid

LC-MS/MS analysis was performed using a Q Exactive HF/HFX mass spectrometer in a data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode to obtain high-resolution MS and MS/MS spectra. Raw MS data were searched using MaxQuant against the UniProt mouse database (uniprot_Mus_musculus_87890_20200217.fasta), with carbamidomethylation of cysteine set as a fixed modification and methionine oxidation and lysine acetylation set as variable modifications. The false discovery rate was controlled at 1% at both peptide and protein levels. Only acetylated peptides with localization probability ≥ 0.75 were retained, and reverse sequences and potential contaminants were removed. Given that individual proteins contain multiple acetylation sites, all quantitative and differential analyses were performed at the modified peptide level. Peptides detected in ≥ 2 biological replicates in at least one experimental group were considered quantifiable. Intensity values were log2-transformed prior to statistical analysis. When ≥ 3 replicates per group were available, two-tailed Student’s t-tests were applied; otherwise, MaxQuant Significance A was used. Differentially acetylated peptides were defined by fold change > 2.0 or <0.5 and p < 0.05. Peptides detected exclusively in one group but absent in all replicates of the other group were defined as presence/absence acetylated peptides; these were excluded from volcano plots and clustering analyses but were included in downstream functional annotation and pathway enrichment. No numerical imputation was applied for missing values; instead, biological replicate consistency filtering was used, retaining only peptides detected in ≥ 2 biological replicates in at least one group for quantitative analysis. The final set of differentially acetylated peptides was used for volcano plots, hierarchical clustering, and Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses, with enrichment p values adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Because acetylated peptides were enriched by antibody-based affinity purification and quantified based on MS intensity, pathway enrichment analyses were interpreted as reflecting relative trends in the acetylation landscape rather than absolute acetylation stoichiometry.

Mitochondrial isolation

Mitochondria were extracted using a mitochondrial isolation kit for muscle tissue and cells. Approximately 40 mg of tissue was minced and transferred into a centrifuge column tube. Then, 80 mg of tissue grinding powder and 200 μl of pre-chilled Buffer A were added, and the mixture was thoroughly homogenized for 2–3 minutes. Subsequently, 300 μl of Buffer A was added, and the tube was gently inverted to mix. The sample was centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 30 seconds, after which the column was discarded and the collection tube retained.

The pellet was briefly vortexed to resuspend and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 minutes. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube and centrifuged at 11,000 × g for 10–20 minutes. The supernatant was discarded, and 200 μl of Buffer B was added to the pellet, which was resuspended by pipetting up and down 30–40 times, followed by vortexing for 20 seconds. The sample was centrifuged again at 11,000 × g for 10 minutes. After centrifugation, the supernatant was transferred to a new 1.5 mL centrifuge tube, and 0.3 mL of pre-chilled PBS was added, then vortexed thoroughly to mix. Finally, centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 20 minutes was performed to obtain the mitochondrial fraction.

Enzyme activity assay

PDH, SDH, and CS activities were measured using colorimetric assays with kinetic absorbance detection at wavelengths of 450 nm, 600 nm, and 412 nm, respectively. SIRT3 deacetylase activity was determined using a fluorescence-based assay, in which changes in fluorescence intensity were recorded in real time with excitation at 340–360 nm and emission at 440–460 nm. All assays were performed in strict accordance with the manufacturers’ instructions.

Measurement of cellular respiration

Cellular oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured using the Seahorse Bioscience XF24 extracellular flux analyzer. HL-1 cells were seeded in XF24 microplates at a density of 5.0 × 10^4 cells per well and cultured for 24 hours prior to measurement in low-glucose (1 g/L) DMEM supplemented with 1% fetal bovine serum, penicillin (10 U/mL), and streptomycin (10 U/mL). Complete cellular respiration was assessed under basal conditions (10 mM D-glucose, 10 mM pyruvate, 0% serum) and after treatment with mitochondrial inhibitors, including oligomycin (1 μM). The mitochondrial uncoupler trifluoromethoxy carbonylcyanide phenylhydrazone (1 μM), and electron transport chain inhibitors antimycin A (1 μM) and rotenone (1 μM) were also applied.

According to Seahorse Bioscience protocols, respiratory parameters were quantified by subtracting the respiration rates before and after the addition of electron transport chain inhibitors: basal respiration was calculated as the baseline respiration minus AA-dependent respiration; maximal respiration was defined as the maximum oxygen consumption rate achieved after FCCP addition.

Measurement of mitochondrial respiration using liquid-phase oxygen electrode

Mitochondrial OCR in cells or tissues was measured using a Clark-type liquid-phase oxygen electrode system (Channel Technology, CHLOROLAB 2) to evaluate mitochondrial respiratory function. During the experiment, freshly collected myocardial tissues were rapidly transferred into pre-chilled respiration buffer, minced into small pieces, and gently homogenized on ice. Equal masses of tissue homogenates were loaded into the reaction chamber and oxygen consumption was measured at 37 °C using the Clark-type oxygen electrode system with continuous stirring to ensure homogeneity. Glutamate (5 mM) and malate (2.5 mM) were supplied as respiratory substrates. Oxygen content was measured after stabilization of the oxygen electrode system.

Assessment of mitochondrial morphology and function

Mitochondrial morphology was evaluated using MitoTracker staining. Cells were incubated with MitoTracker staining solution at 37 °C for 30 minutes, followed by washing and imaging with a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus SpinSR) using an excitation wavelength of 633 nm and an emission wavelength range of 655–670 nm. Mitochondrial membrane potential (Ψm) was assessed using the JC-1 probe. Cells were incubated with JC-1 staining solution at 37 °C for 30 minutes, washed, and then imaged with the confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus SpinSR). The ratio of red fluorescence (excitation 525 nm/emission 590 nm, JC-1 aggregates) to green fluorescence (excitation 490 nm/emission 530 nm, JC-1 monomers) was calculated. All image data were analyzed using ImageJ software (http://imagej.net/ij/), and mitochondrial length was quantified using the Mina plugin [21] within ImageJ for the assessment of mitochondrial morphology.

Intracellular ROS measurement

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After washing cells with PBS, they were incubated with 10 μM ROS staining solution (DCFH-DA) and thoroughly mixed. The cells were then incubated at 37 °C in a cell culture incubator for 30 minutes, followed by washing with PBS. Imaging was performed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus SpinSR) with an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength range of 501–563 nm. Subsequent image analysis was conducted using ImageJ software.

Lentiviral construction and infection

To achieve SIRT3 overexpression and knockdown, Jingrui Bio constructed a lentiviral overexpression vector (LV-SIRT3) and a lentiviral vector carrying Sirt3-specific shRNA (sh-SIRT3), with empty vector or non-targeting shRNA used as controls. HL-1 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in 24-well plates and infected at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100 for 48 h. After 72 h of culture, SIRT3 expression efficiency was verified by Western blotting, and the cells were then used for subsequent functional assays.

TUNEL assay for apoptosis detection

TUNEL staining was performed using a one-step TUNEL apoptosis detection kit. Cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde, blocked with serum, and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100. Subsequently, 50 μl of the mixed TUNEL working solution was added, and cells were incubated at 37 °C for 1 hour in the dark. Imaging was performed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus SpinSR). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Apoptotic cells were identified by the colocalization of TUNEL and DAPI signals. Image analysis was conducted using ImageJ software.

Intracellular calcium detection

Intracellular calcium flux in HL-1 cells was assessed using the Fluo-4 calcium detection kit. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the culture medium was removed, and cells were washed once with PBS. An appropriate volume of Fluo-4 staining solution was added, followed by incubation at 37 °C in the dark for 30 minutes. After incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS, and fluorescence was observed under a fluorescence microscope. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS (version 22.0.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) or Prism (version 8.3.0, GraphPad Software, LLC) and are presented as mean ± SD. Differences between two groups were compared using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. One-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess differences among three or more groups. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Chronic heart failure in mice induces cardiac dysfunction and mitochondrial damage

To investigate the effects of chronic HF on cardiac function, we first established a mouse model using TAC in 8–10-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (Fig. 1A). Eight weeks after surgery, echocardiographic analysis revealed that, compared to the Sham group, the TAC group exhibited reduced left ventricular ejection fraction and end-diastolic volume, along with a significant increase in left ventricular mass and the heart weight-to-body weight ratio (Fig. 1B–C, p < 0.05). Structural parameters further indicated pronounced ventricular remodeling: left ventricular posterior wall thickness (LVPWd) and interventricular septal thickness (IVSd) were increased in the TAC group, indicating myocardial hypertrophy, whereas left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD) and end-systolic diameter (LVESD) were also enlarged, reflecting pressure overload–induced dilated remodeling (Supplementary Figure 1D–F, p < 0.05). Histological analysis with HE staining showed that in the Sham group, cardiomyocytes were arranged in an orderly manner with aligned muscle fibers, clear cross-striations, and evenly distributed nuclei, indicating preserved myocardial structure without obvious pathological alterations. In contrast, the HF group displayed disrupted myocardial architecture, disorganized cardiomyocyte alignment, loosened myofiber structure, blurred striations, widened intercellular spaces, and visible nuclear pyknosis and fragmentation (Fig. 1D). Further quantitative image analysis demonstrated a significant increase in cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area in the HF group, indicating myocardial hypertrophy (Fig. 1E, p < 0.05). In addition, the area of myocardial fibrosis was markedly greater in the HF group compared to the Sham group (p < 0.05), suggesting the presence of myocardial fibrosis and structural remodeling in the context of HF (Fig. 1E, p < 0.05). TEM revealed pronounced mitochondrial swelling in the HF group, with partially disrupted or ruptured membranes, disorganized or fractured cristae, and visible vacuolar degeneration in some regions. These findings indicate severe mitochondrial structural damage under HF conditions, which may impair mitochondrial energy metabolism and contribute to the pathological progression of HF (Fig. 1F, p < 0.05). To further evaluate the severity of HF and the inflammatory status of the TAC model, circulating and myocardial biomarkers were assessed. Plasma NT-proBNP levels were significantly elevated in TAC mice compared with Sham controls (Supplementary Figure 1A, p < 0.05). Meanwhile, the mRNA expression of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α in myocardial tissue was markedly increased, indicating pronounced local inflammatory activation in failing hearts (Supplementary Figure 1B–C, p < 0.05).

Previous studies have shown that lysine acetylation is a well-recognized, reversible post-translational modification that plays a critical role in regulating mitochondrial energy metabolism and function in cardiomyocytes [22, 23]. Using pan-acetyl-lysine antibodies, we performed Western blot analysis on total myocardial proteins from Sham and TAC groups. The results showed that global protein acetylation levels were significantly elevated in the TAC group compared to the Sham group (Fig. 1G, p < 0.05).

Therefore, these results indicate that chronic HF in mice leads to cardiac dysfunction and mitochondrial damage, accompanied by increased levels of protein acetylation.

Acetylated proteins in heart failure are enriched in mitochondrial metabolic pathways

To investigate the potential role of protein acetylation in chronic HF, we performed an acetyl-proteomic analysis of myocardial tissues from Sham-operated mice and those subjected to TAC (Supplementary Figure 2A). Total cardiac proteins were extracted, digested enzymatically, and subjected to mass spectrometry-based identification and quantitative analysis. Acetylated peptides were normalized at the protein level. Global acetylome profiling identified a total of 295 acetylated proteins and 936 acetylation sites (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Figure 2B), providing important evidence for elucidating the functional implications of acetylation in cardiac remodeling. Further statistical analysis revealed that 54.92% of acetylated proteins contained at least two or more acetylation sites (Supplementary Figure 2C). Subcellular localization analysis showed that nearly 50% of the acetylated proteins were localized to mitochondria (Fig. 2B), suggesting that mitochondria may serve as a major regulatory target of acetylation.

To further characterize the functional properties of differentially acetylated proteins, we conducted GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. GO biological process analysis indicated that these proteins were significantly enriched in energy metabolism-related processes, including the generation of precursor metabolites and energy, ATP metabolic processes, and oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 2C). Consistent with this, KEGG pathway analysis revealed that the differentially acetylated proteins were primarily involved in mitochondrial energy metabolic pathways such as oxidative phosphorylation, TCA cycle, and fatty acid degradation (Fig. 2D). Additionally, domain enrichment analysis showed that the functional domains of these acetylated proteins were primarily distributed among key mitochondrial metabolic enzymes, including ATP synthase and CS (Fig. 2E).

These pathway enrichment results are presented as hypothesis-generating proteomic patterns rather than definitive evidence of causal pathway regulation. To further dissect the interaction landscape of differentially acetylated mitochondrial proteins, a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed and subjected to MCODE clustering analysis. The highest-scoring module (Cluster 1) was predominantly composed of TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)–related proteins, including Dihydrolipoamide acetyltransferase (DLAT), CS, Succinate dehydrogenase subunit A (SDHA), and multiple electron transport chain subunits (Figs. 2F, 3).This module exhibited dense interconnectivity, indicating coordinated acetylation remodeling of mitochondrial energy metabolism associated proteins in chronic HF.

Hence, these findings suggest that protein acetylation in the context of chronic HF is highly enriched in mitochondrial metabolic pathways, implying that acetylation may play a critical regulatory role in the reprogramming of cardiac energy metabolism during HF.

Increased acetylation of TCA cycle-related enzymes is accompanied by impaired mitochondrial respiratory function in heart failure

Based on acetyl-proteomics, several mitochondrial proteins exhibited multi-site acetylation change trends. To determine whether mitochondrial protein hyperacetylation under chronic HF conditions affects energy metabolism, we focused on key metabolic enzymes within the TCA cycle that exhibited uniformly increased acetylation. DLAT and CS are rate-limiting enzymes in the TCA cycle and play pivotal roles in regulating metabolic flux [24, 25]. SDH, as the only multi-subunit enzyme of the TCA cycle embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane, serves as a critical metabolic hub linking oxidative phosphorylation and the TCA cycle in eukaryotic cells [26]. Therefore, investigation of these three enzymes provides a systematic and comprehensive understanding of TCA cycle alterations and their potential link to mitochondrial energy dysregulation in chronic HF. We first assessed the acetylation levels of DLAT, SDHA and CS following TAC using co-immunoprecipitation (CO-IP) assays (Fig. 4A–B). The results demonstrated a significant increase in acetylation levels of all three enzymes after TAC treatment. Enzymatic activities were evaluated using commercial assay kits. Compared with the control group, enzymatic activities of both the PDH and SDH complexes were significantly reduced in the HF group (Fig. 4C–D, p < 0.05), indicating suppressed enzymatic function. In addition, CS activity was also markedly decreased (Fig. 4E, p < 0.05), further supporting impaired mitochondrial energy metabolism under HF conditions. Taken together with the globally elevated acetylation levels, these results suggest that mitochondrial protein hyperacetylation associated with HF may contribute to disrupted myocardial energy metabolism by impairing the activity of key enzymatic complexes.

(H) Time-dependent OCR changes in myocardial tissues of TAC and Sham mice (n = 3/group). (I) RCR comparison in TAC and Sham mice (n = 3/group). (J) ATP content in TAC and Sham myocardial tissues (n = 3/group). a indicates p < 0.05 compared with the Sham or normal group

To further validate the contribution of mitochondrial dysfunction to cardiac metabolic disturbances, we established an in vitro HF model by treating HL-1 cardiomyocytes with Ang II. Ang II exposure markedly increased global lysine acetylation (Kac) levels and concomitantly enhanced the acetylation of key mitochondrial enzymes, including DLAT, SDHA, and CS (Supplementary Figure 3A–B), indicating that the cellular model successfully recapitulated the mitochondrial protein hyperacetylation phenotype observed in vivo.

Mitochondrial respiration was subsequently assessed using a Seahorse XF Analyzer. The OCR in the Ang II-treated group was significantly lower than that of the control group across multiple respiratory phases (Fig. 4F), indicating mitochondrial respiratory chain dysfunction. Further analysis showed that both basal respiration and maximal respiratory capacity were markedly reduced in Ang II-treated cells (Fig. 4G, p < 0.05). To confirm mitochondrial dysfunction at the tissue level, we measured the oxygen consumption rate of mouse myocardial tissue using a liquid-phase oxygen electrode system. Myocardial tissue from the TAC group displayed a significant reduction in oxygen consumption (Fig. 4H, p < 0.05), indicating a decline in overall mitochondrial respiratory capacity. Consistently, the respiratory control ratio (RCR) was significantly lower in the TAC group (Fig. 4I, p < 0.05), suggesting decreased mitochondrial coupling efficiency. Moreover, ATP quantification revealed a substantial reduction in myocardial energy levels in the TAC group (Fig. 4J, p < 0.05).

These findings demonstrate that in chronic HF, SIRT3 downregulation is closely associated with mitochondrial protein hyperacetylation, coincident with reduced activity of key TCA cycle enzymes and altered mitochondrial metabolic function.

SIRT3 improves mitochondrial quality and respiratory function through deacetylation and regulates cardiomyocyte function

As the primary mitochondrial deacetylase, SIRT3 plays a crucial role in maintaining mitochondrial metabolic homeostasis and redox balance [27]. Therefore, investigating the regulatory effects of SIRT3 on these key metabolic enzymes under HF conditions may help deepen our understanding of the pathophysiology of HF. We first examined the expression levels of SIRT3 in Sham and TAC groups. Western blot analysis revealed a significant reduction in SIRT3 expression following TAC treatment (Fig. 5A, p < 0.05), and this decrease was also validated in the in vitro cardiomyocyte model (Supplementary Figure 3A). qPCR analysis further confirmed this downregulation at the transcriptional level (Supplementary Figure 2D, p < 0.05). To further clarify the functional role of SIRT3 in mitochondrial metabolic regulation, the intracellular NAD+/NADH ratio and SIRT3 enzymatic activity were assessed under different treatment conditions. As shown in Supplementary Figure 3C–D, Ang II treatment significantly reduced the NAD+/NADH ratio and suppressed SIRT3 enzymatic activity compared with the Normal group.

To determine whether SIRT3 directly mediates Ang II–induced mitochondrial metabolic disturbances, SIRT3 expression was genetically manipulated in HL-1 cardiomyocytes via lentiviral overexpression or knockdown, and the modulation efficiency was confirmed by Western Blot analysis (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Figure 2E, Supplementary Figure 4A, p < 0.05). CO-IP analysis of key mitochondrial metabolic enzymes demonstrated that, compared with the Ang II-treated group, SIRT3 overexpression significantly reduced the acetylation levels of DLAT, CS, and PDH (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Figure 2F, p < 0.05). Enzymatic activity assays showed corresponding increases in DLAT, CS, and PDH activity (Fig. 5D–F, p < 0.05). Conversely, SIRT3 knockdown increased the acetylation of DLAT, SDHA, and CS under basal conditions and further exacerbated their hyperacetylation under Ang II stimulation, while total protein expression remained largely unchanged (Supplementary Figure 4B–C).

Consistent with these enzymatic changes, quantitative assessment of mitochondrial function further supported the above findings. Mitochondrial ROS levels were reduced by 78.86% in the SIRT3 overexpression group compared with the model group (p < 0.05), while the JC-1 red/green fluorescence ratio, reflecting mitochondrial membrane potential, increased by 69.3% (p < 0.05), indicating reduced oxidative stress and restoration of membrane potential (Fig. 5I). In contrast, SIRT3 downregulation induced ROS accumulation and a decrease in the JC-1 ratio even under basal conditions and further aggravated mitochondrial depolarization and oxidative injury in the presence of Ang II (Supplementary Figure 4D-E), indicating pronounced mitochondrial dysfunction. Morphological analysis further supported these functional changes. Confocal imaging showed that SIRT3 upregulation preserved mitochondrial network integrity, with a 28.3% increase in mitochondrial aspect ratio (Fig. 5I, p < 0.05), whereas SIRT3 downregulation caused enhanced mitochondrial fragmentation and structural disorganization (Supplementary Figure 4F). Functionally, Seahorse analysis demonstrated a bidirectional effect of SIRT3 on mitochondrial respiration: SIRT3 upregulation significantly increased maximal respiratory capacity, whereas SIRT3 downregulation reduced basal respiration, maximal respiration, and ATP-linked respiration, with Ang II stimulation further aggravating mitochondrial respiratory impairment (Fig. 5G–H, Supplementary Figure 4 G, p < 0.05).

At the cellular outcome level, TUNEL staining showed that Ang II significantly increased apoptosis, whereas SIRT3 overexpression markedly reduced the TUNEL fluorescence ratio compared to the Ang II group (p < 0.05), indicating an anti-apoptotic effect. Fluo-4 AM calcium imaging showed that Ang II induced abnormal intracellular calcium accumulation, manifested as a time-dependent increase in fluorescence intensity (Fig. 6B). SIRT3 overexpression significantly alleviated intracellular calcium overload, reduced peak calcium levels, and improved abnormal calcium handling (Fig. 6C, p < 0.05), indicating an improvement in aberrant intracellular calcium load.

(B) Calcium transient analysis in HL-1 cells of the three groups, scale bar = 20 μm (n = 3/group). (C) Statistical analysis of apoptosis rate and calcium fluorescence intensity at 6, 18, and 30 min in each group (n = 3/group). a indicates p < 0.05 vs. Normal group; b indicates p < 0.05 vs. Ang II group

Collectively, these findings suggest that SIRT3 mitigates cardiomyocyte apoptosis and oxidative damage by regulating the reversible acetylation of mitochondrial metabolic enzymes, thereby improving mitochondrial respiratory function and intracellular calcium homeostasis.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that chronic HF impairs cardiac function and is accompanied by structural and functional mitochondrial damage. This is characterized by reduced ejection fraction, decreased ventricular end-diastolic volume, increased heart-to-body weight ratio, and evident myocardial mitochondrial injury. Acetyl-proteomic analysis revealed that chronic HF leads to an overall increase in protein acetylation levels in cardiomyocytes, with approximately half of the acetylated proteins localized to mitochondria. Functional enrichment analysis further showed that these acetylated proteins are predominantly involved in mitochondrial energy metabolism pathways, including the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation. Moreover, we observed a significant downregulation of SIRT3, a key mitochondrial deacetylase, in the failing heart. This reduction was associated with hyperacetylation of critical metabolic enzymes such as PDH, CS, and SDHA, and was associated with reduced enzymatic activity. Such mitochondrial dysfunction ultimately disrupts cardiomyocyte energy metabolism and exacerbates cardiac dysfunction.Importantly, overexpression of SIRT3 effectively reduced mitochondrial protein acetylation, improved mitochondrial structure and function, and mitigated myocardial injury. These findings highlight SIRT3 as a promising therapeutic target for preserving mitochondrial function in the context of chronic HF.

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a hallmark of HF [28, 29]. SIRT3 plays a critical role in regulating mitochondrial metabolism, particularly in the heart, where energy production primarily relies on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. SIRT3 modulates the activity of key enzymes involved in the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation, thereby ensuring efficient ATP production. More information on SIRT3 can be found in these studies [30, 31]. A study by Chen et al. demonstrated that SIRT3 influences lipid accumulation and utilization in the heart by deacetylating enzymes such as long-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase [32]. Beyond its role in chronic HF, SIRT3 also plays an essential role in mitochondrial regulation in various cardiac pathologies. In diabetic cardiomyopathy, the induction of SIRT3 reverses diabetes-induced hyperacetylation and functional impairment of mitochondrial enzymes [33]. In sepsis, SIRT3 deficiency leads to the hyperacetylation of key TCA cycle enzymes, promoting a metabolic shift toward anaerobic glycolysis in cardiomyocytes. This shift increases lactate and NADH production, ultimately exacerbating septic cardiac dysfunction [34]. These findings align with our data, which show that SIRT3 enhances the activity of mitochondrial metabolic enzymes such as DLAT, CS, and PDH by reducing their acetylation, thereby ameliorating septic-induced cardiac dysfunction. In ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury of H9C2 cardiomyoblasts, upregulation of SIRT3 via nicotinamide riboside (NR) reduces mitochondrial oxidative stress, thereby mitigating mitochondrial damage and suppressing apoptosis during I/R injury [35]. Similar regulatory effects of SIRT3 have also been reported in other conditions, including renal I/R injury [36] and rheumatoid arthritis [37]. Therefore, although the functional roles of SIRT3 may vary across different pathological contexts, its central role in regulating mitochondrial metabolism and function is well established. Our findings further support the notion that targeting SIRT3 may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for the treatment of HF.

The association between TCA enzyme deacetylation and HF has garnered increasing attention. Enzymes involved in the TCA cycle play pivotal roles in mitochondrial energy metabolism, and their acetylation status significantly influences energy production. PDH, a key enzyme that links glycolysis to the TCA cycle, promotes intracellular acetyl-CoA and ATP generation. In HF, impaired pyruvate production, reduced PDH activity, and downregulation of mitochondrial respiratory complexes collectively suppress mitochondrial energy generation. A study by Li and colleagues further demonstrates the factors involved in cardiac remodeling. PKM1 is downregulated in failing hearts, and it is required to boost glycolysis, mitochondrial respiration, and ATP production under pressure overload, so losing PKM1 worsens pressure-overload HF, while restoring it protects the heart [38]. Inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) drives HFpEF development by promoting mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and Akt S-nitrosylation, which impairs insulin-mediated glucose uptake in cardiomyocytes. Both genetic deletion and pharmacologic inhibition of iNOS alleviate these abnormalities and improve HFpEF in mice, highlighting iNOS as a potential therapeutic target [39].

Impaired autophagy lowers cardiac NAD+ levels by activating SQSTM1–NF-κB signaling, which induces NNMT and diverts nicotinamide away from NAD+ synthesis, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and HF. Restoring NAD+ with NMN or inhibiting NNMT rescues mitochondrial and cardiac function, identifying a new autophagy–NAD+ regulatory pathway critical for heart health. [40]. SIRT3 protects neurons in a Parkinson’s disease cell model by deacetylating and activating mitochondrial citrate synthase, thereby preserving mitochondrial enzyme activity and counteracting MPP+ -induced dysfunction [41]. Our results support the crucial role of SIRT3 in regulating mitochondrial function and cardiac energy metabolism.

HF is linked to early and extensive mitochondrial protein hyperacetylation caused by disrupted acetyl-CoA homeostasis, connecting the buildup of metabolic intermediates to the posttranslational control of energy metabolism. Hyperacetylation of succinate dehydrogenase A hinders TCA cycle function and complex II respiration, directly leading to mitochondrial malfunction and energy failure in the affected heart [42]. Excessive acetylation may compromise myocardial energy production and render the heart more vulnerable to stress conditions, such as ischemia or pressure overload. Our study further reveals that in HF models, decreased SIRT3 expression associated with increased acetylation of several key metabolic enzymes, including PDH, CS, and SDH. This aberrant acetylation may impair the efficiency of the TCA cycle, disrupt mitochondrial metabolic function, and ultimately reduce energy supply to cardiomyocytes, contributing to progressive cardiac dysfunction. These findings suggest that modulating SIRT3 expression or activity may help restore metabolic homeostasis in cardiomyocytes and offer a promising therapeutic strategy to improve HF outcomes. Although previous studies have reported associations between SIRT3 and HF, the central strength of our study lies in not only confirming SIRT3 downregulation but also systematically demonstrating the high enrichment of its downstream affected proteins within the TCA cycle. In contrast to studies focusing on individual proteins, we identified a coordinately regulated DLAT/CS/SDHA module, providing new evidence for targeting HF from a metabolic network perspective. In HL-1 cardiomyocytes, SIRT3 overexpression markedly reduced the acetylation levels of multiple TCA enzymes, enhanced their enzymatic activities, and improved mitochondrial structure and function. These results further confirm the essential role of SIRT3 in maintaining cardiomyocyte metabolic function. Thus, therapeutic strategies that activate SIRT3 or inhibit aberrant acetylation of critical TCA enzymes may alleviate mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolic imbalance, providing new avenues for the treatment of chronic HF.

In addition, SIRT3 may affect cell death mechanisms and autophagy [43]. We also observed an increase in ROS levels following HF, suggesting that oxidative stress may be a key mediator in the progression of HF regulated by SIRT3. Supporting this notion, Jayant Sethd et al. reported that eicosapentaenoic acid and/or docosahexaenoic acid ameliorate HF by suppressing oxidative stress-induced cardiac damage and impairment of systolic or diastolic function [44]. We demonstrate that downregulation of SIRT3 impairs mitochondrial function by promoting the hyperacetylation of key TCA cycle enzymes, including DLAT, CS, and SDHA. This enzymatic inhibition leads to reduced mitochondrial metabolic activity, decreased ATP synthesis, increased ROS production, and loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), ultimately contributing to mitochondrial dysfunction and energy collapse in cardiomyocytes. To provide a visual overview of this mechanism, we generated a schematic illustration (Fig. 7), which summarizes how SIRT3 deficiency disrupts mitochondrial metabolism in HF. This integrative model underscores the central role of SIRT3 in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and highlights its potential as a therapeutic target in the treatment of HF.

Schematic illustration of the mechanism by which SIRT3 downregulation leads to mitochondrial dysfunction in heart failure. In a TAC-induced mouse model of heart failure, SIRT3 expression is markedly downregulated and is associated with hyperacetylation of key mitochondrial metabolic enzymes including DLAT, CS, and SDHA. This hyperacetylation inhibits the enzymatic activity of the TCA cycle, impairs mitochondrial metabolism, and leads to elevated ROS production, decreased mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), and reduced ATP synthesis. Collectively, these changes contribute to mitochondrial dysfunction and the progression of cardiac energy failure in chronic heart failure

In this study, certain limitations remain. First, although equal amounts of total protein were used for acetylated peptide enrichment, matched global proteomic quantification was not performed; therefore, acetylation stoichiometry could not be accurately determined, and potential effects of protein abundance changes on acetylation signals cannot be fully excluded. In addition, the antibody-based enrichment strategy may introduce sequence- or structure-dependent capture bias, which could influence the interpretation of pathway enrichment results. Building on these observations, future studies may apply lysine-directed mutagenesis (K→R or K→Q) to functionally validate key acetylation sites and more directly assess the regulatory effects of site-specific acetylation on enzymatic activity.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that SIRT3 plays a critical role in regulating myocardial mitochondrial energy metabolism. In chronic HF, downregulation of SIRT3 expression leads to hyperacetylation of key mitochondrial metabolic enzymes, correlated with decreased activities of PDH, CS, and SDH, thereby impairing mitochondrial function and ultimately exacerbating the progression of HF. Modulating SIRT3 activity to enhance mitochondrial metabolic efficiency holds promise as a novel therapeutic strategy for HF. Our findings provide important scientific evidence for further elucidating the role of SIRT3 in the pathogenesis of HF and for the development of SIRT3-based targeted therapies for this condition.
