Determinants of birth weight in a low- and middle-income country: Sri Lanka Child Growth Cohort (SLCGC)
Authors: V Pujitha Wickramasinghe, Guwani Liyanage, Harendra De Silva, Shreenika De Silva Weliange, Yasaswi Walpita, Heshan Jayaweera, Indika Siriwardena, Dhammica Rowel, Abner Daniel, Upul Senarath, V Pujitha Wickramasinghe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLow birth weight (LBW), a substantial global public health problem, is associated with a range of both short-term and long-term consequences affecting human capital. The present study aims to investigate sociodemographic and pregnancy-related determinants of birth weight through analysis of Sri Lanka Child Growth Cohort (SLCGC) data.MethodsSLCGC is a retrospective cohort study that recruited 1875 children aged 12–24 months, born at term, using a stratified cluster sample across the country. The present analysis included 1563 mother-baby pairs, with data collected through the Child Health Development Record (birth weight, gestational age at birth, sex), Pregnancy Record (birth order, pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), gestational weight gain, diabetes, hypertension, haemoglobin), through interview (household assets to calculate wealth index, maternal age, education and employment, birth order, birth interval, supplements used) and through anthropometric measurements (maternal and paternal height). Univariate regression analyses were carried out to estimate the effects of a series of independent variables on birth weight, followed by a multiple regression analysis using the generalised linear model adjusting for confounders at a significance considered at p<0.05.ResultsMean birth weight was 2926 g (SD 406.9) and the LBW prevalence in term-born children was 12.5%. Adjusted analyses show that birth weight (g) of term-born children increases significantly with their parental height (maternal β=9.7, 95% CI 6.7 to 12.7, p<0.01; paternal β=2.4, 95% CI 1.4 to 4.5, p<0.01), pre-pregnancy BMI (β=19.2, 95% CI 15.2 to 23.1, p<0.01), gestational age at birth (β=131.3, 95% CI 114.5 to 148.0, p<0.01) and weight gain during pregnancy (β=20.9, 95% CI 16.9 to 24.9, p<0.01). Birth weight is significantly higher in males in contrast to females, the second or higher birth orders compared with first-born and those living in urban and rural sectors in contrast to the estate sector (plantations). Having diabetes during pregnancy has increased birth weight significantly, whereas hypertension reduced it. These associations were considered significant at p<0.05ConclusionBirth weight is determined by multiple factors. The consistent associations of parental height, pre-pregnancy BMI, weight gain during pregnancy, gestational age at birth and presence of diabetes and hypertension in pregnancy may have programmatic implications to achieve optimal birth weight in low- and middle-income countries.

Introduction

Optimum growth during the intra-uterine period is critical since a child’s future growth pattern is ‘programmed’ during fetal life. Low birth weight (LBW) is an important determinant of undernutrition in childhood and has intergenerational effects perpetuating a cycle of poor nutrition. LBW is defined as a birth weight below 2500 g regardless of gestational age or sex.1 Reduction of LBW prevalence by 30% between 2012 and 2025 is one of the global nutrition targets.2 Worldwide, the estimated LBW prevalence was 14.7% (95% Credible Interval 13.7 to 16.1) in 2020.3 4 Of the LBW newborns, 91% were from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), mainly South Asia (48%) and sub-Saharan Africa (24%). In Sri Lanka, the prevalence of LBW has declined from 18.7% in 1993 to 14.5% in 2016.5 6 The average birth weight has improved gradually over the years, but the pace of improvement has slowed down considerably in the last 10 years.7 Birth weight determines subsequent survival, growth and development in children. LBW newborns also have a higher risk of mortality, morbidity, stunting in childhood, poor developmental scores and long-term physical ill health such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes mellitus later in life,4811 especially when accompanied by rapid weight gain. Several studies in Sri Lanka highlighted the association between birth weight and child undernutrition and revealed that LBW is the most significant determinant of stunting, wasting and underweight of children under 5 years of age.7 12

Birth weight, which is a reflection of fetal growth, is determined by a multitude of factors ranging from biological, and socioeconomic, to environmental and health services. Global evidence reveals that extremes of maternal age (especially younger than 16 years of age or older than 40 years), multiple pregnancy, obstetric complications, chronic maternal conditions (eg, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy), infections (eg, malaria) and nutritional status of the mother are significant predictors of LBW.4 Global large cohort studies have found that maternal weight gain during pregnancy increases birth weight independent of genetic factors.13 14 In Sri Lanka, determinants of LBW have been reported previously through extended analysis of Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) 2006/2007 data.15 16 However, the effects of pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), pregnancy weight gain, gestational age at birth and maternal diabetes have not been investigated due to non-availability of such data in the DHS 2006/2007. The Institute of Medicine/National Research Council guidelines outline the optimum weight gain during pregnancy according to four categories of pre-pregnancy BMI to ensure adequate growth of the fetus.17 The maternal and child health (MCH) services programme in Sri Lanka has also identified action to achieve a normal BMI prior to conception through pre-pregnancy care/inter-pregnancy care service packages while promoting adequate weight gain in pregnancy.

The present study analyses Sri Lanka Child Growth Cohort (SLCGC) data to investigate the sociodemographic and pregnancy-related determinants of birth weight. It is hypothesised that there is a consistent association between pregnancy weight gain and birth weight, independent of pre-pregnancy BMI and other important factors. Identification of such determinants would be useful to provide evidence to effect health programme reforms and policy decisions to achieve the global nutrition target relevant to birth weight in an LMIC.

Methods

Study design and participants

SLCGC is a retrospective cohort study that recruited 1875 children aged 12–24 months, born at term, covering all three residential sectors (urban, rural and the estate consisting of plantations) as strata, and gathered data from selected Medical Officer of Health (MOH) areas within each stratum. In Sri Lanka, around 90% pregnant women are registered with the MOH to receive clinic and domiciliary-based MCH services.18 This cohort included mother-baby pairs with babies born at 37 weeks’ gestation or more (term babies). Out of this, seven twin pregnancies were excluded and the analysis was restricted to 1563 records without any missing values. The details of the methods are described in the cohort profile.19 This study adheres to the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology statement for cohort studies and the checklist is available as an online supplemental file 1.

Exposures and outcomes

A structured pre-tested data collection tool was used to gather data from the mothers and relevant records, including pregnancy record and the Child Health Development Record (CHDR).

In Sri Lanka, nearly 99% of the births occur at hospitals and are attended by healthcare professionals. Further, all children are issued a CHDR with birth weight and other perinatal details being recorded. The birth weight (in grams) for this study was extracted from the CHDR, which is available with mothers, or the field health worker (Public Health Midwife). The birth interval in years, birth order, gestational age at birth and the sex of the child was also extracted from the CHDR.

The presence of diabetes and hypertension before or during pregnancy, the haemoglobin level, pre-pregnancy BMI (weight divided by height squared) and weight gain during pregnancy was extracted from the pregnancy record. Micronutrient supplementation before and during pregnancy was extracted from the records, and the intake of it was verified through interview. Demographic and socioeconomic characteristics of the family, such as education, employment, household details to calculate the wealth index and access to social safety nets such as the food basket or cash transfers during pregnancy, were gathered by interviewing the mother.

Weight measured at the first antenatal clinic visit was used as a proxy for pre-pregnancy weight and was obtained from the pregnancy record. The height of the mother was measured using standard anthropometric instruments at the session where the mothers were interviewed. Thereafter, the BMI was calculated based on the above measurements and was classified according to WHO definition of BMI for adults.20 Pregnancy weight gain was the difference between the last recorded weight before delivery and the weight at the first antenatal clinic visit available in the pregnancy record. The adequate gestational weight gain was defined as: 12.5–18 kg for the underweight; 11.5–16 kg for the normal, 7.0–11.5 kg for the overweight and 5–9 kg for the obese women categorise according to prepregnancy BMI.17 Anaemia was determined from the haemoglobin level of <110.0 g/L in the first and second trimester, or 105.0 g/L in the third trimester, after making an adjustment for the altitude in the area which they were living. Calculating the asset score, using principal component analysis on a series of household level variables, created the household wealth index.15

Patient and public involvement

At the stage of designing the study, mothers of children under 2 years of age from the three residential sectors were consulted and their views were considered. As individual data analysis was not done, there are no specific dissemination plans to the individuals. However, the findings have been communicated to the relevant officials in the Ministry of Health, Government of Sri Lanka and are explained in detail.19

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS statistical software. The outcome variable birth weight visually indicated normal distribution through PP plot and QQ plot. However, statistical tests using Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests showed non-normal distribution. Linear regression analysis was performed with birth weight (g), as the dependent variable against a series of independent variables. Residential sector, household wealth quintile, mother’s education level, mother’s employment status, presence of diabetes, hypertension and anaemia during pregnancy, supplementation during pregnancy, having access to social service nets such as the food basket during pregnancy, birth interval, birth order and the sex of the child were entered as categorical variables and the mother’s age, mother’s height, father’s height, pre-pregnancy BMI, gestational age at birth and pregnancy weight gain were entered as continuous variables. This was followed by a multiple regression analysis using the generalised linear model (GLM), where all independent variables were entered while non-significant variables were eliminated in a stepwise manner. All predictor variables retained in the final model were adjusted for each other to account for the potential confounding factors. The GLM presents the main effects as β (95% CI), and p value for each factor or covariate. P value <0.05 is used as statistically significant. A subgroup analysis of final GLM was performed according to the residential sector and pre-pregnancy BMI category.

Birth weight

Among the term babies the mean birth weight was 2926 g (SD=406.9) while the proportion of children with birth weight <2500 g was 12.5%, and those with birth weight 2500–3499 g and ≥3500 g were 79.5% and 7.9%, respectively. The univariate analysis is presented in table 1.

Determinants of birth weight

In assessing the determinants of birth weight, multiple regression analyses led to a statistically significant model that explains the independent variables effect on birth weight after adjusting for confounders. The variables retained in the final model are summarised with their parameter estimates in table 2. Results revealed that a 1 kg of weight gain during pregnancy was associated with a 21 g increase in birth weight (β=20.9, 95% CI 16.9 to 24.9, p<0.01), and for 1 week in gestational age the increase in birth weight was 131 g (β=131.3, 95% CI 114.5 to 148.0, p<0.01), while holding the other variables constant. Similarly, the increments in birth weight for one unit increase in pre-pregnancy BMI were 19 g (β=19.2, 95% CI 15.2 to 23.1, p<0.01), independent of other variables. According to different pre-pregnancy BMI categories, the mean birth weight among underweight, normal weight, overweight and obese mothers was 2791 g, 2905 g, 3020 g and 3038 g, respectively. The constitutional factors such as mother’s height and father’s height improved birth weight by 9.7 g (β=9.7, 95% CI 6.7 to 12.7, p<0.01) and 2.4 g (β=2.4, 95% CI 1.4 to 4.5, p<0.01) for each unit of change (cm), respectively. The residential sector emerged as a major determinant where the children living in the urban (β=173.2, 95% CI 110.4 to 236.1, p<0.01) and rural (β=186.6, 95% CI 136.8 to 236.5, p<0.01) sectors had significantly higher birth weight in contrast to those in the estate sector. The girls showed a significantly lower birth weight than boys (β=−122.4, 95% CI −155.6 to −89.1, p<0.01). Diabetes during pregnancy increased the birth weight (β=158.3, 95% CI 101.5 to 215.2, p<0.01) while having hypertension during pregnancy reduced the birth weight (β=−63.8, 95% CI −126.6 to −1.0, p<0.01).

Discussion

The present analysis reveals that the birth weight of term-born children tends to be significantly higher in offsprings with higher parental heights, pre-pregnancy BMI, and weight gain during pregnancy and gestational age at birth. Birth weight was lower in females in contrast to males, the first-born compared with subsequent births, and those living in the estate sector (plantations) in contrast to urban and rural sectors. Diabetes during pregnancy tends to increase birth weight, whereas hypertension reduces it. The pathophysiological process of diabetes during pregnancy tends to increase birth weight, whereas hypertension reduces it, as reflected in our analysis.

A key strength of the present analysis is the establishment of a consistent association of birth weight with pregnancy weight gain, adjusted for pre-pregnancy BMI categories. The main hypothesis of this study was whether weight gain during pregnancy is associated with increased birth weight of the offspring. Local evidence on this hypothesis was not available until the present study, which proved this hypothesis while adjusting for pre-pregnancy BMI, which is the basis for the recommended weight gains during pregnancy. A high percentage of mothers (62%) reported inadequate weight gain recommended for their pre-pregnancy BMI. Weight gain during pregnancy was inadequate, especially among mothers who are thin or have a normal BMI. This is supported by the increase in mean birth weight observed with higher BMI. Effects of pre-pregnancy BMI and pregnancy weight gain on birth weight of offspring have been described globally.2124 In a population-based study in the USA, an analysis of 1, 164, 750 births noted a consistent association between pregnancy weight gain and birth weight (β=7.4, 95% CI 7.1 to 7.6, p<0.01).13 However, there was no adjustment for pre-pregnancy BMI. In contrast, the present study found a more pronounced association between the two variables (β=20.9, 95% CI 16.9 to 24.9, p<0.01) after adjusting for pre-pregnancy BMI and other specific confounders. The study found that about half of the women enter pregnancy with suboptimal BMI, either underweight or overweight. Only 50% pregnant mothers had a normal BMI at the first antenatal clinic visit, while 17% were thin and 34% were overweight or obese. Maternal obesity is known to be associated with increased incidence of type 2 diabetes in the offspring.24 25 Thus, the findings highlight the need for evidence-based interventions to achieve optimum BMI during pre-pregnancy or interpregnancy care, targeting both underweight and overweight/obese women.

A previous study using 2006 Sri Lanka DHS data concluded that maternal BMI, height, education, altitude and Indian Tamil ethnicity were the major determinants of LBW;16 however, it lacked information on pre-pregnancy BMI and weight gain during pregnancy. The present study made an adjustment for the altitude in which they were living in the analysis of haemoglobin and found the estate sector, as an important determinant.

Parental height is an important predictor of LBW, which leads to short stature, making it a vicious cycle. According to DHS 2016, the mean height of ever married women 15–49 years of age was 152.7 cm (SD 6.1).5 The mean height in the present study (154.1 cm (SD 5.8)) is higher. The mean height in women in the estate sector was significantly lower than that of rural and urban women. This could be a likely contributor to the high levels of LBW in the estates over the years. This shows that improvement of LBW is a generation-long process. Therefore, one window of opportunity to improve the height of the female child is to concentrate on the female child’s nutrition, physical and mental health during early puberty and reap the maximum advantage of the pubertal growth spurt. Additionally, national nutrition policies and strategies should focus more on addressing stunting in order to break this intergenerational phenomenon.

The findings of the present study show that the birth weight of the offspring of primipara (first-born) was lower than those of multiparous women and is consistent with the trends shown in literature.26 This association was positive independent of their pre-pregnancy BMI and weight gain during pregnancy, thus other reasons such as biological and psychosocial factors should be explored through further research.

Around 10% of the mothers reported having diabetes while around 8% of mothers had hypertension during pregnancy, highlighting the importance of close monitoring and appropriate management beyond routine antenatal care, in order to minimise effects of these conditions on fetal growth. Diabetes mellitus during pregnancy tends to increase birth weight of the offspring.27 28 The most serious adverse outcomes of diabetes during pregnancy are congenital anomalies, stillbirths and excessive fetal growth.29 The effect of diabetes on increasing birth weight was observed in the present study. Hypertensive disorders in pregnancy substantially increase the risk of LBW,30 and the current findings are consistent with such evidence. Early detection of diabetes during pre-pregnancy or pregnancy period, and appropriate management through dietary, lifestyle interventions and pharmacotherapy are recommended as an urgent intervention.

Birth weight is one of the strongest predictors of childhood malnutrition—those who have LBW are more likely to become underweight, stunted and wasted, and those with higher birth weight are more likely to be overweight during childhood.73133 Moreover, it is advisable to ensure that women enter pregnancy with a normal BMI through pre-pregnancy and interpregnancy services. Findings suggest the importance of targeting mothers with low BMI and promoting dietary and other interventions to ensure optimum weight gain during pregnancy. Weight gain should ideally be monitored according to BMI categories during antenatal care services.

There are few studies that provided evidence on interventions to provide optimum weight gain during pregnancy in the local settings. An improvement in weight gain during pregnancy and birth weight of the offspring was demonstrated by training the Public Health Midwives on nutrition counselling.34 A health promotion approach using community mobilisation was found to be effective in improving the mean birth weight.35

Furthermore, the results of the present study indicate that the longer the period of gestation, the higher the birth weight even among the term-born babies. A gradual rise in birth weight with advancing gestational age was persistent even after 37 weeks of gestation, with a difference of approximately 500 g between 37 and 42 completed weeks.36 Therefore, the findings suggest avoiding early induction or delivery unless there is a strong indication.

The residential sector emerged as a major factor related to birth weight, with the estate sector having lower birth weights compared with the rural and urban sectors. The estate sector represents a population descending from South Indian Tamils living in tea and rubber plantations located in the central hills and surrounding zones. Their living environment has pushed the community towards a low status of social identity.37 Poor nutrition status of the estate sector is associated with socioeconomic factors along with the biological factors and differences in healthcare services.38 Global evidence shows that intergenerational effects have large impacts on LBW, especially in poor settings, mediated by epigenetic mechanisms.39 Further research is needed in the estate sector to identify their unique issues at ground level including detailed sociocultural, genetic and living environmental (climate and cold weather, altitude) factors, health and dietary practices (low consumption of food of animal origin and fruit and vegetables), alcohol use and domestic violence.

In LMICs, iron-folate or multiple micronutrient supplementation during pregnancy has shown strong evidence in reducing LBW. The balanced energy protein supplementation in pregnancy led to a 40% reduced risk of LBW.40 The present study did not find any significant association of birth weight and maternal anaemia status. Further, food or micronutrient supplementation did not show any positive impact on birth weight, although globally recognised as evidence-based beneficial intervention.41 Although this study did not show a positive impact of such supplementations, they may have helped in maintaining the status score and preventing any deterioration in the nutritional status of the mother as well as providing any other non-measured health benefits such as keeping infections low, preventing birth defects etc. Some previous studies have shown that iron supplementation prevents further deterioration of maternal iron status rather than improving haemoglobin levels.42 There could be other aetiological factors for anaemia as well.43 Therefore, further studies are required on the return on investment of such supplementary programmes as well as to understand aetiopathological factors.

Strengths and weaknesses of the study

One major strength of this study is having a large sample of data from different geographical, socioeconomic as well as ethnic backgrounds, where it used growth-monitoring data that is routinely collected at child welfare clinics and health facilities, which are readily available at the point of assessment.

The weaknesses are that this is retrospective data collection where there could be recall bias and perception bias of the mothers. Some may not have received the services available, as well as there could be disparity in receiving services, which could have attributed to the outcome.

Conclusion

Birth weight is possibly determined by several modifiable factors such as pre-pregnancy BMI, weight gain during pregnancy and gestational age at delivery, socioeconomic standards and maternal illness. Optimum pre-pregnancy BMI and gestational weight gain result in a newborn with a good birth weight. Further increases in maturity would result in a better birth weight. It is recommended that the national MCH programmes should focus on addressing these factors, including minimising early delivery.
