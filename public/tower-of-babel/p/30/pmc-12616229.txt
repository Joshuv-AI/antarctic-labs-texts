Silent circulation of Bluetongue and Schmallenberg viruses in Culicoides biting midges in northwestern Italy: implications for the 2024 BTV outbreak
Authors: Angela Maria Catania, Elena Grego, Laura Tomassone, Cagnotti Giulia, Di Muro Giorgia, Ferrini Sara, Antonio D’Angelo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractVector-borne viruses like Bluetongue (BTV) and Schmallenberg virus (SBV) pose an ongoing threat to livestock health across Europe. Transmitted by Culicoides biting midges, these pathogens can circulate silently, escaping early detection. In this study, we investigated the presence and infection status of Culicoides on 2 cattle farms in northwestern Italy, where congenital malformations and abortions had previously been reported. Between June and October 2023, over 4,000 Culicoides were captured using UV-light suction traps; over 95% belonged to the C. obsoletus complex. Molecular screening of pooled insects revealed a low viral prevalence (0.5%) for both BTV and SBV. Notably, no official outbreaks were recorded in the area during 2023, suggesting a silent viral circulation without clinical signs in animals. The persistence of BTV in vectors and possible animal reservoirs, including wildlife, may have contributed to its reemergence in 2024 when a new BTV outbreak occurred in northern Italy. Our findings highlight the critical role of continuous entomological and virological surveillance in anticipating and mitigating future outbreaks.

Introduction

Culicoides biting midges (Diptera: Ceratopogonidae) are small hematophagous insects (1 to 3 mm in length) with a global distribution (Mellor et al. 2000, Carpenter et al. 2013). Adult females require blood meals for egg development (Borkent and Dominiak 2020) and are predominantly active from sunset to sunrise (Kettle 1977). Culicoides are globally widespread, with different/specific groups depending on the country (Mellor et al. 2000). Over 1,400 Culicoides species have been described worldwide, but only a fraction are competent vectors (Carpenter et al. 2013). Species identification is commonly based on wing patterning (Mathieu et al. 2012), and PCR markers targeting ribosomal (16 s rDNA, 28 s rDNA, ITS 1, and ITS 2), and mitochondrial DNA (CO1 and cytochrome b oxidase—Cyt b) (Pagès et al. 2009, Schwenkenbecher et al. 2009). In Europe, Culicoides imicola Kieffer, is the primary vector in Mediterranean areas, while species in the Culicoides obsoletus complex are more common in northern regions (Meiswinkel et al. 2004, Versteirt et al. 2017). Culicoides midges are important biological vectors of several arboviruses with significant veterinary and public health implications, including the Bluetongue Virus (BTV) and Schmallenberg Virus (SBV) (Mellor et al. 2000).

BTV, a non-contagious Orbivirus affecting ruminants (Maclachlan et al. 2009), is listed among the notifiable diseases by the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH) due to its severe clinical and economic implications. At least 35 BTV serotypes are recognized globally, with varying pathogenicity (Maan et al. 2016, Ries et al. 2020, Ries et al. 2021). The Bluetongue virus serotype 8 (BTV-8) was responsible for a major epidemic in northern Europe in 2006 (Purse et al. 2005). Italy first reported BTV serotype 2 (BTV-2) in Sardinia in 2000 (Calistri et al. 2004). Then, the disease continued to spread extended into central and southern Italy involving multiple BTV serotypes, including BTV-9, BTV-4, and BTV-16 (Giovannini et al. 2004). However, in the summer of 2024, a major BTV outbreak occurred in northern Italy, resulting in considerable economic losses (BENV 2024). A national surveillance plan is in place, to reduce the circulation of BTV serotypes in the Italian territory and prevent their spread to the part of the territory not affected by the infection. Surveillance is based on serological and virological controls on sentinel animals and vector trapping.

SBV belongs to the Orthobunyavirus genus and affects domestic and wild ruminants, mainly cattle and sheep (Claine et al. 2015). SBV appeared in Europe in 2011, first identified in dairy cattle farms in Germany and the Netherlands (Hoffmann et al. 2012). In Italy, SBV was detected for the first time in the tissues of a goat fetus in northern Italy in February 2012 (Goffredo et al. 2013). Culicoides midges belonging to the C. obsoletus complex have been identified as SBV vectors (De Regge et al. 2012, Balenghien et al. 2014).

Culicoides midges acquire BTV and SBV during blood feeding on infected hosts (Saminathan et al. 2020). After replication in the midgut and salivary glands, they remain infectious for life and can transmit viruses to other susceptible hosts during subsequent blood meals (Wilson and Mellor 2009). Temperature strongly influences viral replication. Several studies showed that BTV replication is optimal at 20 to 25 °C, at these temperature range the extrinsic incubation period (EIP) takes 10 to 16 d (Foster and Jones 1979). Time for EIP completion decreases at temperatures up to 30 °C (Wittmann et al. 2002). At temperatures below 15 °C, viral BTV RNA polymerase activity decreases, but the virus remains latent, potentially facilitating overwintering in the vectors (Mullens et al. 1995). SBV also appears able of overwintering, although the mechanism remains unclear (EFSA 2014).

In this study, we collected and identified Culicoides spp. from two cattle farms in northwestern Italy, where reproductive disorders, including malformations and abortions, had previously been reported. Moreover, we investigated their infection by BTV and SBV, which were not officially reported in the tested area at the time of the study.

Materials and Methods

Study Area and Farms

Culicoides specimens were collected from two cattle farms located in the Piedmont region of northwestern Italy, one in Turin (farm A) and one in Cuneo (farm B) province. These farms were selected based on previous reports of congenital malformations and abortions. Additionally, serological analyses conducted on bovine sera collected during the summer of 2022 revealed the presence of antibodies against BTV and SBV (data not shown).

Both farms exclusively rear Piedmontese cattle, a local breed primarily raised for meat production, reflecting the typical regional industry.

Farm A hosts approximately 90 animals, including 70 breeding cows. The animals are kept in a semi-intensive housing system, with seasonal access to pasture. Calves are housed in group pens and fed natural colostrum. The feeding regimen includes forage and concentrate. Routine vaccinations are performed for bovine viral diarrhea (BVD) and infectious bovine rhinotracheitis (IBR). Both natural mating and artificial insemination are practiced. Farm A averaged 10% stillborn or aborted calves annually in the 5 yr before sampling, equating to about 6 out of 60 births each year. The facilities include two recently constructed barns.

Farm B includes 73 animals, of which 50 are breeding cows. The herd is kept year-round in fixed housing within older barn structures. Calves are fed natural colostrum, and the diet includes hay, silage, and multivitamin supplementation. Like farm A, routine vaccination for BVD and IBR is performed, and both mating methods are employed. The farm recorded a 10% rate of stillborn or aborted calves (2 cases out of 20 to 25 births) in the year before the study; in previous years, no cases were reported.

Culicoides Sampling

Biting midges were captured using BG-pro UV-light suction traps (Biogents AG, Regensburg, Germany), positioned outdoors near the animal barns, close to the cattle. Two traps were placed on each farm and activated at least 2 h before sunset until 2 h after sunrise. In the morning, the collected insects/samples were transferred into polystyrene containers with dry ice and then stored at −20 °C until further processing.

Trapping was conducted weekly between mid-June and mid-October 2023, spanning a total of 16 wk. To standardize our sampling, we implemented a precise schedule: on each farm, sampling was conducted on 2 separate days per week. With 2 traps placed on each farm during these sampling days, this resulted in a total of 64 trap collections for Farm B (16 wk × 2 sampling days/week × 2 traps/day). For Farm A, we performed 62 trap collections due to logistical issues limiting sampling to 1 day during one of the 16 wk. The number of traps per sampling night was factored into the analysis to determine the average number of biting midges collected in each period.

To facilitate data analysis, the collection period was divided into 2 sub-periods per month: day 1 to 15 (“Month 1”) and day 16 -end of the month (“Month 2”). Since sampling in June began on the 16th, this period was labelled “June 2.” Conversely, in October, sampling occurred only from the 1st to the 15th, designated as “October 1.”

Vector Identification

Collected insects were sorted, counted, and identified under a stereomicroscope. Morphological identification was based on wing patterns and features using established taxonomic keys (Goffredo and Meiswinkel 2004, Dyce et al. 2007). Additionally, molecular identification was performed on one-third of the collected samples. Total DNA was extracted from individually selected specimens using TrizolReagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, United States), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of the DNA were measured by a Nanodrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, United States). The integrity of nucleic acids and the absence of PCR inhibitors were verified by amplification of partial GAPDH (Homan et al. 1997) and ATPase (Woodall et al. 1997) gene fragments. For species confirmation, a 710-bp fragment of the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) gene was amplified using primers LCO1490 5′-GGTCAACAAATCATAAAGATATTGG-3′ and HCO2198 5′-TAAACTTCAGGGTGACCAAAAAATCA-3′ (Folmer et al. 1994). PCR was conducted in 25 μl reaction volumes containing 1× MyTaq Red Reaction Buffer, 200 nM each primer, 5 U MyTaq Red DNA Polymerase (Bioline-Meridian Bioscience, Inc., Cincinnati, Ohio, United States), and 50 to 100 ng of DNA. Thermocycling conditions were 95 °C for 1 min; followed by 35 cycles at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 46 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 1 min; and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. PCR products were analyzed by electrophoresis on 2% agarose gel stained with SYBR Safe (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, United States). PCR products were purified with ExoSAP-ITkit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, United States) and sent to an external service (BMR Genomics, Padua, Italy) for Sanger sequencing. Positive samples were confirmed with Seq-Studio Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, Massachusetts, United States). Subsequently, sequences were analyzed and compared to previously published sequences in the GenBank database using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BLAST).

Virus Detection

The virological screening was conducted on Culicoides samples to detect BTV and SBV. On each collection day, non-engorged specimens were pooled into groups of 10 to 50 individuals, depending on the total number of biting midges collected, while engorged females were tested individually. RNA was extracted using TrizolReagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, United States), following the manufacturer’s instructions. A one-step RT-PCR was performed targeting the partial NS3 gene of BTV and the S segment of SBV. The primers for BTV were forward primer: 5′-TATCCGGGCTGATYCRAAGG-3′, and reverse primer: 5′-GCGTACGATGCGAATGCAGC-3′ (Grego et al. 2014). The primers for SBV were forward primer: 5′-TGACTGCTGAAGAATG-3′, and reverse primer: 5′-GAATCCAGATTTGGCCCA-3′. Reactions were prepared in 25 μl volumes using the MyTaq One-Step RT-PCR Kit (Bioline-Meridian Bioscience Inc., Cincinnati, Ohio, United States) with the following components: 100 ng/μl RNA, 12.5 μl of MyTaq One-Step mix (2×), 0,5 μl of RiboSafe RNase Inhibitor, 0,25 μl Reverse transcriptase, 0.03 μM of BTV primers, and 0.05 μM of SBV primers. Reactions were carried out under the following conditions: reverse transcription at 48 °C for 20 min, followed by Taq enzyme activation at 95 °C for 1 min, 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 35 s, 72 °C for 30 s, and a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min. Negative and positive controls were included in every PCR round. RT-PCR products were confirmed through an electrophoresis run, subsequently purified and sequenced, and the sequences were analyzed as described above.

Statistical Analysis

Data were stored in an electronic spreadsheet and analyzed with R software (R Core Team 2024). The difference in viral prevalence among pool types (engorged/unengorged midges) was evaluated with the Fisher Exact test; the statistical significance level was set at 0.05. The pooled prevalence calculator in EpiTools (Sergeant 2018) was used to estimate infection prevalence, with 95% confidence intervals (CI), considering different insect pool sizes.

Results

Culicoides Collection and Identification

A total of 4,465 Culicoides were collected from 2 sampling sites: 1,464 from farm A and 3,001 from farm B (Table 1). Sampling efforts were consistent across both farms, with a total of 62 traps catches at farm A and 64 traps at farm B (Table 1). Morphological identification based on wing patterns revealed that the majority (95.5%, n = 4,264/4,465) of collected midges belonged to the C. obsoletus complex. The remaining specimens (n = 201) could not be identified due to damaged or adhered wings. Molecular analysis of the cytochrome oxidase I gene confirmed the morphological identification.

Ten pools tested positive for BTV RNA, including 8 pools of C. obsoletus plus one pool of Culicoides spp. along with a single C. obsoletus engorged female (Table 2). Most BTV-positive samples (n = 8/10) originated from farm A indicating a higher viral circulation at this site (Table 2). The overall pooled BTV prevalence was 0.5% (95% CI: 0.3 to 0.9), with farm A showing a prevalence of 1.1% (95% CI: 0.5 to 2) and farm B 0.1% (95% CI: 0 to 0.4) (Table 2). Sequencing confirmed the presence of BTV; however, only a partial sequence of segment 10 (NS3), which is highly conserved among multiple BTV serotypes, was successfully sequenced. This limitation prevented serotype differentiation but still confirmed viral circulation.

Similarly, 10 pools tested positive for SBV, consisting of 8 C. obsoletus pools and 2 C. obsoletus engorged females (Table 2). The overall pooled SBV prevalence was 0.5% (95%CI: 0.2 to 0.8) with both farms exhibiting similar infection rates: 0.5% (95% CI: 0.16 to 1.2) in farm A and 0.4% (95% CI: 0.18 to 0.9) in farm B (Table 2). RT-PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing confirmed SBV presence.

No statistically significant difference was found in the positivity rates between pools of engorged and unengorged midges for both viruses (P > 0.05).

The number of positive pools varied in the different collection periods (Table 3). The highest prevalence of BTV (15.4% with 95% CI: 2.8 to 58.3) and SBV (1.3% with 95% CI: 0 to 5.8) was registered in the first half of September (Table 3).

Discussion

This study was conducted on two farms where previous serological evidence of SBV and BTV had been detected, alongside cases of malformations and miscarriages. We aimed to assess the presence of Culicoides vector populations and the circulation of BTV and SBV in these settings.

We collected over 4,000 Culicoides, mostly identified as belonging to the C. obsoletus complex group, suggesting a widespread distribution of this species complex in northwestern Italy. These findings align with previous studies (Mignotte et al. 2020) and data from the VectorNet EFSA-CDC database, which reports the presence and distribution of vectors in Europe and the Mediterranean basin (Medlock et al. 2018). Members of the C. obsoletus complex are recognized vectors of BTV and SBV in Europe (Carpenter et al. 2006), with laboratory studies confirming their competence (Carpenter et al. 2008).

BTV and SBV emerged in Europe between 2006 and 2011, with SBV reaching Italy in February 2012. An intense SBV circulation was reported in Sardinia and Piedmont region, in the provinces of Turin and Cuneo, in 2013 (IZS 2025). Viral RNA from SBV was detected in C. obsoletus midges overwintering in northern Italian farms shortly after the SBV outbreak (Goffredo et al. 2013). Although no further cases were reported until 2015 (BENV 2024), it remains unclear whether this reflected the true absence of virus circulation or under-detection. SBV infections in adult ruminants are generally mild and transient but can cause severe fetal malformations if infection occurs during early gestation, leading to abortions, stillbirths, or congenital malformations associated with the arthrogryposis-hydranencephaly syndrome (Herder et al. 2012, Claine et al. 2015, Afonso et al. 2014).

Similarly, BTV-8 circulated widely before control measures led to its elimination in Europe by 2010. However, in 2015, BTV-8 re-emerged in several European countries. The precise mechanisms underlying the re-emergence of BTV-8 remain a subject of ongoing scientific debate. Genomic analyses have demonstrated an unexpected lack of genetic divergence between strains isolated during the 2006 to 2009 European outbreaks and those detected in 2015, despite the relatively high substitution rate typically observed for BTV (Pascall et al. 2020). This genetic stasis has led to the hypothesis that the 2015 resurgence may have resulted from the accidental release of frozen contaminated biological materials, such as infected semen, rather than from undetected ongoing circulation. Nonetheless, alternative explanations are plausible. It is possible that BTV-8 persisted at undetectable levels in domestic or wild ruminant populations across several seasons, aided by mechanisms, such as prolonged viraemia, transplacental transmission, or latent infections (EFSA 2017). For instance, Grego et al. (2014) detected low levels of BTV RNA in red deer (Cervus elaphus), roe deer (Capreolus capreolus), and chamois (Rupicapra rupicapra) sampled in Piedmont (northwestern Italy) between 2008 and 2011. Although the authors concluded that wild deer played only an epiphenomenal role in local outbreaks, more recent insights suggest that such low-level, subclinical infections may contribute to the reduced evolutionary rate observed in wildlife reservoirs. Wild ruminants are increasingly recognized as potential reservoirs for orbiviruses. Species such as red deer and roe deer can harbor BTV asymptomatically, thereby facilitating silent virus maintenance even after apparent eradication from livestock populations (Niedbalski 2015; EFSA 2017). Moreover, environmental factors—particularly those driven by climate change—likely play a critical role in BTV re-emergence. Rising temperatures can shorten the extrinsic incubation period of the virus within Culicoides vectors, enhance vector abundance, and prolong seasonal vector activity (EFSA 2017). Additionally, milder winters may increase the likelihood of viral overwintering, thereby enabling persistence and re-emergence in endemic region.

During our sampling, BTV and SBV prevalence in Culicoides was approximately 0.5%. According to the Italian Veterinary Epidemiological Bulletin, no Bluetongue or Schmallenberg outbreaks were reported in northwestern Italy during that period (BENV 2024). However, by July 2024, a significant BTV outbreak involving serotypes 3, 4, and 8 occurred, particularly affecting Sardinia and Piedmont (BENV 2024), with severe animal health and economic consequences. The outbreak in Piedmont was particularly concerning and was likely linked to BTV circulation in France in the summer of 2023 (Gondard et al. 2024). Indeed, in August 2023, a BTV-8 outbreak caused significant mortality in cattle and sheep in the southern part of the Massif Central (France). In 2024, over 1,000 BT outbreaks were reported in Italy, with serotype 8 identified in almost half (BENV 2024). Our sequence analysis of Culicoides-positive samples confirmed BTV and SBV presence in vectors, but unfortunately only a fragment of BTV segment 10 (NS3) was sequenced, limiting serotype identification. Sequencing of additional genomic segments is needed to identify the circulating strains.

Our study revealed significant variations in Culicoides abundance across different months, emphasizing the influence of seasonal factors on vector population dynamics. While Culicoides activity in temperate regions is generally well-documented to peak during the warmer months, typically from June to September (Mellor et al. 2000, Baldet et al. 2008, Santman-Berends et al. 2010, Larska et al. 2013, Veldhuis et al. 2013), our data showed variations between farms (Fig. 1). Specifically, the highest number of midges at Farm B was collected in June-July, whereas Farm A exhibited its peak later, in September to October. This trend aligns with previous studies indicating that Culicoides populations increase during warmer months due to optimal breeding conditions, including higher temperatures and humidity (Mellor et al. 2000, Baldet et al. 2008). These fluctuations suggest that vector populations emerge and persist as long as conditions remain favorable. This seasonal pattern suggests a higher risk of virus transmission in summer, since the peak abundance of Culicoides corresponds to the period of highest likelihood for viral circulation. However, infection prevalence of BTV and SBV varied across the study period; notably, the highest prevalence for both viruses was registered at the beginning of September, a period when the overall number of midges collected was lower. This observation aligns with many studies in Europe that found an increased circulation of arboviruses, including BTV and SBV, in late summer-beginning of autumn (Wilson and Mellor 2009, Carpenter et al. 2013, Veldhuis et al. 2013, Afonso et al. 2014, Balenghien et al. 2014, EFSA 2014, EFSA 2017). However, it is acknowledged that UV traps might underestimate the true abundance and activity of midges, especially during cooler months or crepuscular periods, as host-seeking activity for some Culicoides species can peak before sunset, and light traps may not capture diurnal activity (Gerry et al. 2009).

Differences in midge abundance between the 2 farms were notable, with farm B recording more than twice (∼3,000) the number of midges compared to Farm A (∼1,500). Several environmental factors can influence the size and diversity of trap collections, including local environmental factors, such as weather conditions and the availability of breeding sites, and the specific trap locations (eg distance from livestock) (Conte et al. 2007, Tschuor et al. 2009, Veggiani Aybar et al. 2010). Such variations can affect virus transmission between the 2 sites. Further studies are needed to elucidate these influences in the local context.

Detection of both BTV and SBV in Culicoides samples collected between June and October 2023, despite no reported clinical cases, suggests a phase of silent viral circulation. In this phase, the virus may persist in asymptomatic domestic or wild carriers, and/or sustained by low-level transmission within the vector–host cycle. Such silent circulation is an expected epidemiological phase for arboviruses like BTV and SBV, particularly during interepizootic or early season periods (Carpenter et al. 2013, EFSA 2017). The prevalence of both viruses in our samples (∼0.5%) was significantly lower than the levels typically recorded during BTV outbreaks. Previous studies have shown that virus detection rates in Culicoides tend to be higher during outbreaks, reflecting more intense circulation (Wilson and Mellor 2009, Balenghien et al. 2014). The 0.5% prevalence detected in 2023 suggests that, while BTV and SBV were present in the vector population, transmission dynamics were not yet sufficient to trigger widespread infections (EFSA 2014). The subsequent BTV outbreak in July 2024 highlights the amplification of viral circulation. A similar re-emergence of acute disease apparently was not observed for SBV. A possible explanation could be related to SBV’s known epidemiological pattern, which typically involves subclinical infections in adult animals and pronounced impacts on reproduction (eg abortions, stillbirths, congenital malformations) rather than widespread acute epidemics (Afonso et al. 2014, EFSA 2014).

In Piedmont, the presence of wild ruminants such as red deer and roe deer may have contributed to the persistence and circulation of these viruses in Culicoides populations during 2023 and 2024. Their presence also sustains vector populations by providing continuous blood sources outside farms. Seasonal movements and interactions between wild and domestic ruminants could facilitate spillover events, influencing outbreak dynamics.

Integrated surveillance programs including livestock, wildlife, and vector monitoring are essential to understand transmission ecology and improve early viral detection. Such surveillance should extend beyond periods of peak midge activity, to detect low-incidence viral circulation and better inform control strategies. This approach is essential for anticipating and mitigating future arboviruses outbreaks, particularly in light of the changing climatic conditions.

Conclusion

The results of this study underscore the critical importance of continuous entomological surveillance to detect the early circulation of arboviruses and to assess the potential risk of outbreaks. The implementation of enhanced surveillance strategies encompassing real-time vector monitoring, systematic serological testing in livestock, and predictive modelling based on climatic, ecological, and epidemiological data may significantly improve early warning systems for vector-borne diseases. Furthermore, the integration of wildlife monitoring is essential to account for the potential reservoir role of wild ruminants in the maintenance and transmission of viruses such as BTV and SBV. Strengthening long-term, multidisciplinary surveillance frameworks will enable veterinary and public health authorities to develop more effective and timely control measures, thereby mitigating the impact of future outbreaks in northwestern Italy.
