Cost-Effectiveness of Universal Low-Dose Computed Tomographic Lung Cancer Screening in Singapore
Authors: Ruijie Li, John Abisheganaden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionIs low-dose computed tomographic lung cancer screening cost-effective in Singapore when extended to include individuals who have never smoked?FindingsIn this economic evaluation using a Markov cohort model, targeted ever smoker screening was highly cost-effective (incremental cost-effectiveness ratio below 9000 Singapore dollars [SGD] [US $6716] per quality-adjusted life-year [QALY]) but reached only approximately 8% of males and 2% of females. Extending to universal biennial screening yielded sequential incremental cost-effectiveness ratios of SGD 86 312/QALY (US $64 412/QALY) for males and SGD 80 881/QALY (US $60 359/QALY) for females.MeaningThese findings suggest that biennial screening including never smokers falls below conventional cost-effectiveness thresholds and warrants consideration in Singapore.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death in Singapore, with 82% late-stage diagnosis and 5-year relative survival of 22.1% for males and 38.0% for females.1,2,3 Approximately 48% of cases occur in individuals who have never smoked (hereinafter never smokers),4 driven by the never smoker, East Asian female adenocarcinoma phenotype with high epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutation frequency.5 The pattern extends across East and Southeast Asia, where rising adenocarcinoma rates in female never smokers are documented.6,7,8

Landmark trials have established low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening efficacy, including the National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) (20% mortality reduction),9 the Dutch-Belgian NELSON (Nederlands–Leuvens Longkanker Screenings Onderzoek) trial (24% mortality reduction in men),10 and a 9-trial meta-analysis (pooled relative risk [RR], 0.84 [95% CI, 0.76-0.92]).11 The 2021 US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) recommendation covers adults aged 50 to 80 years with a smoking history of 20 pack-years or more,12 and the American Cancer Society 2024 update removed the quit-time limit.13

These criteria miss most cases in Singapore, where only 38.1% of patients with lung cancer meet NLST criteria,14 and no never smokers met the Prostate, Lung, Colorectal, and Ovarian Cancer Screening Trial model (PLCOm2012) extended to all smoking statuses (PLCOall2014) threshold of 1.51%.15 Asian programs targeting never smokers report meaningful detection: the Taiwan Lung Cancer Screening Trial for Never-Smokers (TALENT) achieved a 2.6% rate in never smokers (96.5% stage 0 or I),16 prompting the establishment of the first national program for screening never smokers in 2022,17 and the Singapore Lung Cancer Screening Trial reported 1.9% detection (all stage I, with EGFR variation).18,19 No validated risk prediction model exists for Asian never smokers,20,21,22 and LDCT promotion in Taiwan raised early-stage (stages I and II) incidence without a corresponding decrease in late-stage incidence, suggesting overdiagnosis.23

In a systematic review of 43 economic evaluations, most suggested that LDCT screening is cost-effective.24,25 Two studies from China examined never smoker screening with risk factors,26,27 but none have used Singapore data when evaluating strategies that include never smokers. In the present study, we developed a Markov cohort model parameterized with demographic, epidemiologic, and clinical data from Singapore to evaluate 4 LDCT strategies that include never smokers (no screening, USPSTF 2021, TALENT-style universal, and hypothetical Singapore expanded biennial), quantify the efficiency-coverage trade-off, and identify the minimum risk model performance required for targeted never smoker screening.

Methods

This economic evaluation followed the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) reporting guideline. The study used aggregate, publicly available data and did not include individual-level patient data; therefore, no institutional review board approval or informed consent was required per the Common Rule (45 CFR 46).28 The analysis adopted a Singapore health care system perspective restricted to direct medical costs over a lifetime horizon (ages 30 through 100 years in annual cycles). Costs and outcomes were discounted at 3% per year per Agency for Care Effectiveness (ACE) Singapore guidelines, varied from 0% to 5% in 1-way sensitivity analysis (OWSA).

Model Structure

A discrete-time Markov cohort model with annual cycles and 9 mutually exclusive health states was developed (Figure). Patients diagnosed in the early state transitioned to the diagnosed early–cured state at an annual probability of 0.18, yielding approximately 75% eventual cure consistent with stage I non–small cell lung cancer long-term survival; the diagnosed early–cured state carried no lung cancer mortality. Full mathematical details (transition matrix, outcome equations, validation checks) and an example Markov trace are in eAppendix 1 and eFigure 1 in Supplement 1.

Data Sources and Natural History

Three Singapore-specific data sources were used to parameterize the model: SingStat 2023-2024 complete life tables (single-year-of-age [qx] by sex, ages 0-100 years),29 National Registry of Diseases Office Cancer Registry incidence (2013-2017, scaled to 2019-2023 using age-standardized incidence rate ratios),1 and Health Promotion Board National Population Health Survey 2024 daily smoking prevalence,30 with ever smoker prevalence derived from sex-specific multipliers incorporating former daily smokers (eTables 1-3 in Supplement 1). Cause-deleted life tables isolated other-cause mortality using the standard proportional hazards approach,31 stratified by smoking status (RR, 1.5 for ever- vs never smokers).32 Age-specific incidence is decomposed into ever smoker and never smoker rates using a mixing equation with RR of 12.32 Separate Markov traces are run for smokers and never smokers, then combined using cohort-entry ever smoker weights.

Sojourn times are 4.0 years (early, microsimulation screening analysis–lung calibration33) and 0.6 years (late, reflecting Singapore’s stage IV predominant distribution). Registry rates are shifted forward by the mean sojourn time to recover biological onset rates, avoiding the lead time bias that would otherwise inflate screen-detected survival. Lung cancer mortality rates (0.05/y for early sojourn and 0.35/y for late sojourn) followed Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results 5-year survival. LDCT sensitivity was 94% (early sojourn) and 70% (late sojourn); specificity was 95%.9,10 Adherence was modeled as a bimodal split (persistent attenders vs nonattenders). Full parameter values and unit-cost derivations are in eTables 4 and 5 in Supplement 1.

Screening Strategies

Table 1 summarizes the 4 strategies. The USPSTF strategy screened eligible ever smokers (≥20 pack-years, approximately 40% of ever smokers; effective enrollment approximately 28% after 70% adherence), consistent with the Academy of Medicine Singapore 2025 guidelines.12,34 For USPSTF-enrolled individuals, the model multiplied population incidence by an enrichment factor of 1.5 to reflect higher risk among those meeting pack-year criteria. The factor approximated the PLCOm2012 cancer-capture ratio of the top 40% of smokers (approximately 62% of cancers; ratio approximately 1.55),35 conservatively rounded down for Singapore’s less extreme smoking intensity distribution relative to the US derivation cohort. The TALENT-style strategy was inspired by the Taiwan trial16 and extended to screen all adults. The Singapore expanded strategy was a hypothetical biennial program with an age range of 50 to 75 years, supported by the Multicentric Italian Lung Detection trial.36 Adherence of 60% to 70% was a plausible but optimistic assumption; observed US uptake was much lower (3.3% to 3.9%37). Subgroup filtering and full eligibility rationale are in eAppendix 2 in Supplement 1.

Outcomes and Perspective

Costs are reported in 2024 Singapore dollars (SGD) with 3% annual discounting per the ACE Singapore reference case for cost-utility analysis.38 Indirect and societal costs (productivity losses, informal caregiving) were not modeled, in keeping with payer decision-making. Phase-based unit costs spanned initial treatment (early, SGD 40 000 [US $29 851]; late, SGD 60 000 [US $44 776]), continuing care, terminal care (SGD 30 000 [US $22 388]), screening (SGD 350 [US $261] per LDCT screen), and workup of false-positive screening results (SGD 910 [US $679]).39,40 Utility weights were 1.00 (well), 0.85 (diagnosed early), 0.60 (diagnosed late), and 0.95 (cured).25 Outcomes included life-years, quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs), costs, incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs), and number needed to screen (NNS). Program overheads (call-recall, information technology, staff training) were excluded; only per-screen marginal costs enter the analysis.

Statistical Analysis

OWSA varied each parameter individually at base-case values (eTable 6 in Supplement 1). Probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) used 1 000 000 Monte Carlo iterations sampling from beta, log-normal, and gamma distributions (eTables 7 and 8 in Supplement 1). Probability of cost-effectiveness was computed using the net monetary benefit framework at willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds of SGD 50 000/QALY [US $37 313/QALY], SGD 75 000/QALY [US $55 970/QALY], and SGD 100 000/QALY [US $74 627/QALY]. A risk-threshold analysis identified the minimum area under the receiver operating characteristics curve (AUROC) for a never smoker risk model to match the USPSTF ICER, using the binormal equal-variance receiver operating characteristic framework with sensitivity over the cancer–competing-mortality correlation (ρOC [eTable 9 in Supplement 1]). All analyses were conducted with R, version 4.4.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing) using the data.table and Rcpp packages; 95% credible intervals (CrIs) were derived from PSA quantiles. Values are percentages from 1 000 000 PSA iterations; 95% CrIs are Wilson score intervals for a binomial proportion: [(p̂ + z2/2n) ± z√(p̂(1−p̂)/n + z2/4n2)]/(1 + z2/n) with z = 1.96. Wilson is preferred over the Wald normal approximation because it remains valid at the boundary cases (p̂ = 0, here for TALENT-style at all thresholds). The narrow widths reflect the large Monte Carlo sample size.

Results

Cost-Effectiveness

Under the no screening strategy, baseline lifetime lung cancer death probability was 6.01% among males and 3.92% among females, with a late-stage fraction of approximately 79% closely matching the National Registry of Diseases Office–reported 82%.2 Full deterministic outcomes, WTP-threshold tabulations, and validation checks against incidence, never smoker fractions, and mortality projections are provided in eAppendix 3 and eTables 10 and 11 in Supplement 1.

Table 2 presents per-strategy outcomes and the sequential cost-effectiveness frontier, with per-scan detection rate, stage distribution of screen-detected and interval cancers, and absolute discounted costs and QALYs. Under USPSTF eligibility, the model yielded 11.6 screen-detected cancers per 1000 LDCT scans, concordant with NLST round 1 (11.1/1000 scans)41 and NELSON (8/1000 to 11/1000 scans) strategies.42 Screen-detected cancers were 92% to 99% for stages I and II across strategies, bracketing the 62% (NLST), 77% (NELSON), and 96.5% (TALENT) trial benchmarks.16,41,42 Modeled interval cancers were 77% to 78% for stages III and IV, consistent with NELSON’s 83%.42 All screening strategies fell in the northeast quadrant of the cost-effectiveness plane (eFigures 2 and 3 in Supplement 1).

The USPSTF strategy achieved the lowest mean ICER (SGD 8741/QALY [US $6523/QALY] for males and SGD 6480/QALY [US $4836/QALY] for females), driven by 1.5 times incidence enrichment on the highest-risk subgroup. Its narrow eligibility (approximately 8% among males and 2% among females) limited population mortality reduction to 0.84 percentage points (pp; 14.0% relative; NNS, 7) and 0.27 pp (6.9% relative; NNS, 7), respectively. All screening strategies reduced the late-stage fraction (USPSTF to 57.7% and 68.3%, Singapore expanded to 54.2% and 57.5%, and TALENT-style to 52.1% and 55.7% in males and females, respectively) (eFigures 4 and 5 in Supplement 1); universal strategies achieved any stage shift among never smokers only.

Universal strategies achieved greater mortality reductions: Singapore expanded averted 1.05 pp among males and 0.62 pp among females, with coverage especially decisive for females where TALENT-style averted 0.68 pp vs 0.27 pp for USPSTF (eFigure 6 in Supplement 1). The ratio of false-positive findings to cancer rose from 4:1 under USPSTF to 19:1 to 62:1 under universal strategies (eTable 12 in Supplement 1).

Sequential analysis ranked strategies by cost; extending from USPSTF to Singapore yielded sequential ICERs of SGD 86 312/QALY (US $64 412/QALY) among males and SGD 80 881/QALY (US $60 359/QALY) among females, below the SGD 100 000/QALY (US $74 627/QALY) benchmark. The TALENT-style sequential ICER from the Singapore expanded strategy (SGD 352 608 [US $263 140/QALY] among males and SGD 920 604 [US $687 018/QALY] among females) reflected the diminishing returns of annual vs biennial screening. Subgroup decomposition (eTable 13 in Supplement 1) showed population ICERs driven by the cost-effective smoker component (SGD 6138/QALY [US $4581/QALY] to 8430/QALY [US $6291/QALY]); the never smoker component exceeded conventional thresholds (SGD 88 762/QALY [US $66 240/QALY] to 211 615/QALY [US $157 922/QALY]).

Risk Prediction Model Threshold

A risk prediction model selecting high-risk never smokers could improve cost-effectiveness by enriching the screened population. At f = 1% (fraction of never smokers selected for screening), the threshold AUROCs were 0.82 (95% CrI, 0.73-0.95) among males (sensitivity, 15.1%; enrichment, 15.1 times; ICER, SGD 8578/QALY [US $6401/QALY]) and 0.81 (95% CrI, 0.71-0.89) among females (sensitivity, 13.9%; enrichment, 13.9 times; ICER, SGD 6071/QALY [US $4531/QALY]) (Table 3 and eFigures 7 and 8 in Supplement 1). Even moderate discrimination (AUROC, 0.80) reduced the targeted never smoker ICER to SGD 9910/QALY (US $7396/QALY) among males and SGD 6601/QALY (US $4926/QALY) among females) (eTable 14 in Supplement 1). Inverting the framing, eTable 15 in Supplement 1 reports the maximum cost-effective f at a given AUROC: 1% to 2% at AUROC of 0.85, 2% to 3% at 0.90, and 4% to 5% at 0.95. Calculators with AUROC less than 0.82 yielded no cost-effective f.

Thresholds also depended on the correlation between cancer-risk score and competing mortality risk (ρOC). Models selecting on shared environmental or clinical factors (eg, air pollution, frailty) imply ρOC greater than 0; germline genetic models (eg, EGFR risk alleles, adenocarcinoma-enriched polygenic scores) approached ρOC = 0. The base case sets ρOC = 0.3; under ρOC = 0, the threshold decreased to 0.78 (both sexes), and under ρOC = 0.5, the male threshold increased to 0.91 (eTable 9 in Supplement 1; full derivation in eAppendix 4 in Supplement 1).

PSA (1 000 000 iterations) yielded a median threshold AUROC of 0.83 (95% CrI, 0.73-0.95) for males and 0.81 (95% CrI, 0.71-0.89) for females. These assume zero risk-test cost; a modest per-round cost (SGD 50 [US $37]) increased the targeted never smoker ICER by an order of magnitude (eTable 16 in Supplement 1), favoring low-cost stratification tools (eg, polygenic risk scores administered once at cohort entry).

Sensitivity Analyses

In OWSA, the discount rate, LDCT cost, and baseline incidence were the 3 most influential ICER drivers (eFigure 9 in Supplement 1). Under zero discounting, the Singapore expanded strategy ICER among males decreased to SGD 20 661/QALY (US $15 419/QALY), well below the SGD 75 000/QALY (US $55 970/QALY) threshold.

Under perfect adherence (1.0 across strategies), per-strategy ICERs vs no screening were unchanged because the Bernoulli adherence filter scaled both incremental cost and incremental QALY by the same factor. Sequential ICERs shifted modestly because base-case adherence differed across strategies (USPSTF, 0.70; Singapore expanded, 0.65; TALENT-style, 0.60). The Singapore expanded strategy sequential ICER decreased from SGD 86 312/QALY (US $64 412/QALY) to SGD 77 224/QALY (US $57 630/QALY) among males and SGD 80 881/QALY (US $60 359/QALY) to SGD 77 987/QALY (US $58 199/QALY) among females, with the verdict unchanged (eTable 17 in Supplement 1).

In 1 000 000 iterations for PSA (Table 4), 95% CrIs for the change in QALY were positive for all strategies and both sexes (eTables 18 and 19 in Supplement 1). At SGD 50 000/QALY (US $37 313/QALY), USPSTF has the highest probability of being optimal (89.9% [95% CrI, 89.9%-90.0%] for males and 93.7% [95% CrI, 93.7%-93.8%] for females); at SGD 100 000/QALY (US $74 627), the Singapore expanded strategy overtook USPSTF (61.1% [95% CrI, 61.0%-61.2%] vs 38.9% [95% CrI, 38.8%-39.0%] for males, 72.1% [95% CrI, 72.0%-72.2%] vs 27.9% [95% CrI, 27.8%-28.0%] for females), reflecting the efficiency-coverage trade-off (eFigures 10 and 11 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

This economic evaluation simulated 4 LDCT screening strategies in Singapore using a 9-state Markov cohort and found a fundamental efficiency-coverage trade-off. USPSTF-style screening was highly cost-effective (ICER, <SGD 9000/QALY [<US $6716/QALY]) but its narrow eligibility left 37% of patients with lung cancer unaddressed, with population mortality reduction limited to 0.84 pp for males and 0.27 pp for females. Universal biennial screening achieved greater mortality reductions at sequential ICERs below the SGD 100 000/QALY (US $74 627/QALY) benchmark.

The Singapore expanded strategy lacks formally endorsed WTP guidance: the ACE conducts health technology assessments without publishing a maximum acceptable ICER,38 and published Singapore cost-utility analyses anchor at fractions of gross domestic product (GDP) per capita.43,44 The conventional 1 times the GDP-per-capita threshold is overly generous for a high-income setting; we therefore capped the upper bound at SGD 100 000/QALY (US $74 627/QALY), approximately 0.8 times Singapore’s 2024 GDP per capita.45 The upper bound is consistent with the Cancer Intervention and Surveillance Modeling Network LDCT benchmark (SGD 97 000/QALY [US $72 564/QALY]).46,47

Singapore preventive programs typically achieve ICERs well below this anchor: polygenic risk–tailored mammography was projected to be cost saving compared with age-based screening,48 and the National Steps Challenge was cost saving at a WTP of SGD 10 000/QALY (US $7463/QALY).49 The Singapore expanded biennial LDCT screening strategy at SGD 86 312/QALY (US $64 412/QALY) for males and SGD 80 881/QALY (US $60 359/QALY) for females therefore exceeded the typical preventive range by close to an order of magnitude. ICERs in this range have been accepted in Singapore for late-stage curative oncology (eg, atezolizumab plus bevacizumab for advanced hepatocellular carcinoma at SGD 102 988/QALY [US $76 857/QALY]50), although a life-prolonging treatment for a diagnosed cancer is a different policy proposition from screening of asymptomatic adults.

Population-level screening uptake in Singapore varies by domain. The 2024 National Population Health Survey reported composite screening for chronic disease among 66.4% of adults aged 40 to 74 years; cancer screening rates were lower (35.2% for mammography, 44.9% for cervical, and 44.9% for colorectal), and all were below the World Health Organization target of 70%.30 LDCT screening plausibly fell between these extremes, and OWSA varied in adherence from 0.35 to 0.90 (eTable 6 in Supplement 1). The per-QALY ICER was mathematically invariant to adherence under proportional scaling: a Bernoulli filter scaled incremental cost and QALY gained identically. This was a model property, not a clinical one; individuals who attended screening self-selected on health-seeking behavior, so the marginal benefit per attendee may be at low adherence, biasing the clinical per-QALY ICER upward. Absolute program impact (eg, deaths averted, budget, population mortality reduction) scaled linearly with uptake, so lower adherence diminished public-health value even when the verdict was preserved.

Risk-targeted screening returned the cost into the range of established preventive programs. At an AUROC of 0.82 or greater among males or 0.81 or greater among females, targeted never smoker screening yielded ICERs of SGD 8578/QALY (US $6401/QALY) and SGD 6071/QALY (US $4531/QALY), respectively (Table 3), comparable to risk-tailored mammography in Singapore48 and the smoker arm under USPSTF eligibility. A practical immediate step is therefore to adopt USPSTF-aligned screening for ever smokers; universal expansion to never smokers should be deferred until risk prediction tools reach the threshold AUROC.

Currently validated calculators fall short. PLCOm2012 achieved an AUROC of 0.80 (95% CI, 0.78-0.83) in 27 727 Japanese American Multiethnic Cohort Study participants,22 below both thresholds. PLCOall2014 reported an AUROC of 0.85 (95% CI, 0.83-0.86) in the PLCO derivation cohort but has no validation among Asian patients; the original report recommended against screening never smokers because fewer than 0.1% of PLCO never smokers reached actionable risk.15 TALENT16 defined rule-based eligibility rather than a continuous score; modeled as a universal adult biennial strategy here, it carries a median PSA ICER of SGD 59 109/QALY (US $44 111/QALY) (males) and SGD 91 105/QALY (US $67 989) (females) (Table 4). A 21-variant polygenic risk score for never smoking Asian women achieved an AUROC of 0.71 in a 55 266-person cohort from China,51 also below threshold. Multimodal imaging classifiers52 and genome-wide models53 remain promising but unvalidated for Asian never smokers (eTable 20 in Supplement 1 maps each calculator’s AUROC to the implied ICER).

Structured nodule management, smoking-cessation counseling, and prospective overdiagnosis monitoring should be implemented from program inception.23,34,54 The framework transfers most cleanly to high-income East Asian settings (ie, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Korea, and Japan) with similar never smoker adenocarcinoma fractions and EGFR prevalences; applicability to South and Southeast Asia is more limited, owing to differing smoking and never smoker incidence profiles, lower WTP thresholds, and uneven LDCT infrastructure.

Smoker-based screening ICERs among Asian populations range from US $13 057/QALY to US $19 683/QALY,55,56,57 and the Cancer Screening Program in Urban China cohort found no significant mortality reduction among screened nonsmokers,58 reinforcing the importance of risk enrichment. High targetable EGFR prevalence (40%-50%) in Asian never smokers suggests screen-detected cases may derive greater survival benefit from targeted therapies,59 potentially making the true cost-effectiveness more favorable.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Conservative assumptions (ie, uniform biennial interval regardless of prior scan results, static treatment costs and mortality, period life tables) likely overestimate the true cost per QALY; optimistic assumptions (60%-70% adherence, omission of radiation-induced cancer) work in the opposite direction. Cost scope is restricted to direct screening, workup of false-positive screening results (SGD 910 [US $679] per event), diagnosis, treatment, and terminal care; the downstream pathway for incidental findings (eg, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or coronary calcification) is excluded in full, with uncertain net effect on the ICER. Adherence is modeled as independent across rounds, but attendance is autocorrelated within individuals, so a fixed per-round rate reaches fewer ever attendees than the independence assumption implies. Because program overheads are excluded, the ICER’s invariance to adherence is an upper bound at high uptake; with material fixed costs, higher adherence would amortize overhead across more screens, and the ICER would fall with adherence. The Markov cohort framework does not track individual histories,60 so it treats clinically localized cancers as a single early-stage compartment without separating occult systemic disease at detection (in CALGB [Cancer and Leukemia Group B] 9761, 13.8% of resected clinical stage I cancers harbored immunohistochemistry-detected micrometastases61); the early-stage QALY benefit may therefore be modestly overestimated. The base-case RR of 12 is from Western cohorts32 because no large Asian ever- vs never smoker cohort study exists. All uncertain parameters are varied in the PSA; on balance, conservative biases likely outweigh optimistic ones. Detailed discussion of each limitation is found in eAppendix 5 in Supplement 1.

Conclusions

In this economic evaluation of LDCT lung cancer screening, including never smokers, in Singapore, targeted ever smoker screening was highly cost-effective (SGD 8741/QALY [US $6523/QALY] among males and SGD 6480/QALY [US $4836/QALY] among females) but left approximately 37% of lung cancers unaddressed. Extending to universal biennial screening including never smokers yielded sequential ICERs of SGD 86 312/QALY (US $64 412/QALY) for males and SGD 80 881/QALY (US $60 359/QALY) for females, below the SGD 100 000/QALY (US $74 627/QALY) benchmark. A risk prediction model with AUROC of 0.82 or greater for males or 0.81 or greater for females would make targeted never smoker screening as cost-effective as smoking-based criteria. These findings support adopting ever smoker screening as an immediate priority in Singapore, with investment in risk prediction models for Asian never smokers to enable efficient targeted screening.
