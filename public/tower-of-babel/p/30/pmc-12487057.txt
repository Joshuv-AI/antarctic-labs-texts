Classify the fNIRS signals of first-episode drug-naive MDD patients with or without suicidal ideation using machine learning
Authors: Lan Mou, Yuqi Shen, Qian Tan, Boyuan Wu, Jiayun Zhu, Zefeng Wang, Zhongxia Shen, Xinhua Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMajor Depressive Disorder (MDD) has a high suicide risk, and current diagnosis of suicidal ideation (SI) mainly relies on subjective tools.Neuroimaging techniques, including functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), offer potential for identifying objective biomarkers. fNIRS, with its advantages of non-invasiveness, portability, and tolerance of mild movement, provides a feasible approach for clinical research. However, previous fNIRS studies on MDD and suicidal ideation have inconsistent results due to patient and methodological differences.Traditional machine learning in fNIRS data analysis has limitations, while deep - learning methods like one-dimensional convolutional neural network (CNN) are under-explored. This study aims to use fNIRS to explore prefrontal function in first-episode drug-naive MDD patients with suicidal ideation and evaluate fNIRS as a diagnostic tool via deep learning.MethodsA total of 91 first-episode drug-naive MDD patients were included and categorized into two groups based on their scores on the suicidal item of the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17): 40 patients with suicidal ideation (SIs) and 51 patients without suicidal ideation (NSIs). Concurrently, 39 healthy controls (HCs) were recruited. We utilized fNIRS to measure the hemodynamic responses in the prefrontal cortex of each group during the verbal fluency task (VFT). A Kruskal-Wallis test was conducted to analyze the changes in oxyhemoglobin concentration among the three groups, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated for each region of interest.ResultsCompared to HCs, NSIs exhibited significantly reduced activation in the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (lDLPFC), frontopolar prefrontal cortex (FPC), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), and ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (VLPFC), while SIs showed significantly decreased activation in the entire prefrontal cortex. The activation values of SIs in DLPFC, FPC, and OFC were significantly lower than those of NSIs. The highest accuracy for the three-class classification was observed in the lFPC, reaching 69.80%. The SIs group had the largest area under the ROC curve (AUC = 0.88) in the rFPC, while the NSIs group had the largest area under the ROC curve (AUC = 0.88) in the rDLPFC. The HCs group exhibited the largest area under the ROC curve (AUC = 0.92) in the rDLPFC and rVLPFC.ConclusionDLPFC, FPC, and OFC may serve as biomarker brain regions for identifying suicidal ideation in first-episode drug-naive MDD patients. The fNIRS-VFT task can be utilized clinically as an auxiliary diagnostic tool for mental disorders.Keywords: Near-infrared spectroscopy, Major depressive disorder, Suicidal ideation, Verbal fluency task, Machine learning

Background

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is a prevalent chronic mental illness characterized by persistent low mood, loss of interest or pleasure in almost all activities, and is accompanied by fatigue, sleep disturbances, anxiety, and neurocognitive impairments [1]. The Lifetime prevalence of MDD in China is 4.76% [2], making it a disorder associated with high rates of morbidity, mortality, suicide, and recurrence. Suicide is the most dangerous clinical symptom of MDD, primarily manifesting as suicidal ideation (SI), suicide attempts (SA), suicidal behavior (SB), and suicidal success (SS). Up to 15% of MDD patients end their lives by suicide [3]. A recent meta-analysis indicated that among individuals with depression in China, 53.1% have experienced suicidal ideation at some point in their lives [4]. Suicidal ideation refers to the early psychological processes leading to suicidal behavior, encompassing thoughts or considerations of death, suicide, and severe self-harm, including plans, steps, and outcomes related to suicide [5, 6]. It is one of the primary risk factors for suicidal success, with a follow-up study showing that MDD patients with suicidal ideation exhibit a significantly increased risk of suicide compared to the general population [7]. Currently, the clinical diagnosis of suicidal tendencies in MDD primarily relies on suicide-related items in assessment tools such as the Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD) [8], the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) [9], or the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) [10]. Therefore, identifying neuroimaging biomarkers that can effectively detect suicidal ideation in first-episode MDD patients could enhance the prediction of suicide risk, facilitate the monitoring of suicidal ideation, and enable early intervention.

Neuroimaging advancements have provided strong evidence for the exploration of the etiology of MDD and its associations with suicide [11]. Techniques such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) [12], positron emission tomography (PET) [13], and functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) have been utilized in this research [14]. fMRI is a powerful technique that can provide detailed spatial information about brain function, allowing researchers to observe brain activity across large areas of the brain with high spatial resolution [15]. This makes it useful for mapping complex neural networks involved in MDD and suicidal behavior. However, it is relatively expensive, requires participants to remain still in a large, noisy scanner, and has lower temporal resolution compared to some other techniques. PET can detect specific neurotransmitter systems and metabolic changes in the brain, offering unique insights into the biochemical processes underlying MDD [16]. But it involves the use of radioactive tracers, which may limit its application in some populations, and it also has high costs associated with equipment and tracer production. fNIRS, as a novel non-invasive optical imaging technique [17], has gained considerable attention in recent years due to its numerous advantages. fNIRS reflects dynamic brain activity by measuring the concentrations of oxyhemoglobin (oxy-Hb), deoxyhemoglobin (deoxy-Hb), total hemoglobin, blood volume, and cerebral oxygen saturation [18]. Compared to commonly used brain imaging techniques, fNIRS has unique technical advantages. It offers higher safety, lower cost, greater portability, easier operation, non - invasiveness, and less restriction on participants’ physical movement. Consequently, fNIRS has been widely applied in fields such as cognitive science, cognitive neuroscience, brain disorders, neonatal brain injury monitoring, and psychiatry [19].

Despite the fact that there has been a relative scarcity of previous investigations utilizing fNIRS in the context of suicide research, the VFT has emerged as a valuable tool in the realm of fNIRS - based cognitive research [20]. Studies have demonstrated that the VFT can reflect various aspects of cognitive ability, including memory, language skills, attention, and executive function [21]. These cognitive functions are known to be impaired in MDD [22], and previous research has suggested that they may also be closely related to suicidal ideation [22–24]. A study using fNIRS to examine MDD patients in language fluency tasks found that the concentration of oxygenated hemoglobin in the prefrontal cortex decreased in patients with suicidal ideation, and significant damage was observed in the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (rVLPFC) [25]. It has further been demonstrated that changes in oxy-Hb levels within the lVLPFC mediate the indirect effect of MDD severity on the intensity of suicidal ideation [26]. Although previous studies suggest that fNIRS may serve as a valuable objective tool for assessing the risk of suicidal ideation [27], the results exhibit heterogeneity, potentially influenced by factors such as whether patients are first-episode MDD cases, the duration of the illness, medication history, and types of medication. Therefore, in this study, we selected first-episode drug-naive MDD patients with a duration of illness less than one year to enhance the rigor of our findings.

Previous studies have explored computational and machine learning applications in fNIRS data analysis, with notable contributions including Bulgarelli et al.‘s dynamic causal modeling (DCM) framework [28] and Gabrieli et al.’ s crowdsourced quality control model [29]. These works established foundational methods for fNIRS data processing. Our study introduces a one-dimensional convolutional neural network within a deep learning framework to address emerging challenges in temporal feature extraction. The 1D-CNN inherently captures sequential dependencies in fNIRS signals through hierarchical convolution operations, eliminating reliance on manual feature engineering [30]. This architecture uniquely handles the high-dimensional complexity of fNIRS time-series data by learning discriminative representations through massive parameter optimization, demonstrating superior robustness against noise and variability compared to traditional methods prone to overfitting/underfitting in small datasets [30]. By integrating regularization techniques and adaptive training strategies, the CNN effectively addresses overfitting issues while preserving robust generalization capabilities. Furthermore, its modular design allows for dynamic adjustments to data characteristics and task demands, such as modifying convolutional kernel dimensions or inserting pooling layers, ensuring adaptability to evolving fNIRS analysis requirements [31]. When applied specifically to the three-class classification task of differentiating healthy individuals, MDD patients with suicidal ideation, and non-suicidal MDD patients, deep neural architectures utilize hierarchical feature learning and end-to-end optimization to introduce innovative analytical approaches that strengthen the discriminative capacity of fNIRS signals for subgroup characterization.

This study aims to utilize fNIRS to explore prefrontal cortex activation in first-episode drug-naive MDD patients with suicidal ideation, first-episode drug-naive MDD patients without suicidal ideation, and healthy participants during VFT. By employing one-dimensional convolutional neural networks to analyze the differences in hemodynamics among the three groups, the research seeks to identify specific brain regions or activation patterns that effectively distinguish first-episode MDD patients with suicidal ideation. This approach aims to provide a more objective basis for predicting suicide risk and monitoring suicidal ideation.

Methods

Participants

A total of 91 first-episode drug-naive MDD patients were recruited from the Third People’s Hospital of Huzhou between November 2022 and August 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) meeting the diagnostic criteria for MDD according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), confirmed by two psychiatrists; (2) aged 18 to 60 years; (3) first episode with a duration of illness ≤ 1 year; (4) Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD-17) score ≥ 17; (5) no prior use of antidepressants, antipsychotics, or benzodiazepines prior to enrollment; (6) right-handedness. The exclusion criteria included: (1) presence of other psychiatric disorders, such as schizophrenia or bipolar disorder; (2) history of significant physical illness; (3) undergoing electroconvulsive therapy (MECT) or repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) within the past month; (4) history of substance or alcohol abuse or dependence. The 91 MDD patients were assessed for suicidal ideation based on the suicide item of the HAMD-17, with a score of 0 indicating “no suicidal ideation” and a score of 1 or above indicating “suicidal ideation.” Based on the presence or absence of suicidal ideation, MDD patients were classified into two groups: those with suicidal ideation (SIs) and those without suicidal ideation (NSIs). Concurrently, 39 healthy controls (HCs) were recruited from the community, matched for gender and age with those in the patient groups, and screened to ensure they had no suicidal ideation (HAMD-17 score < 7). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third People’s Hospital of Huzhou, and informed consent was obtained from all participants, prior to enrollment.

fNIRS data collection

Equipment

The study employed the ETG-one functional near-infrared brain imaging system produced by Hitachi, Japan, to measure the concentration of oxyhemoglobin (oxy-Hb) and deoxyhemoglobin (deoxy-Hb). This device operates at a sampling frequency of 10 Hz. A fixed optical headband was placed on the participants’ prefrontal region. Each pair of optical sensors measured blood oxygen concentration through 22 channels (Fig. 1). Using the International 10–20 electrode placement system, we utilized the Fpz position to determine the corresponding brain regions for each channel (Fig. 2). Specifically, channels 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 9, 14, and 18 corresponded to the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), channels 6, 8, 10, 11, 12, and 13 corresponded to the frontopolar prefrontal cortex (FPC), channels 15, 17, 20, and 21 corresponded to the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), and channels 19 and 22 corresponded to the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (VLPFC).

Verbal fluency task

After the completion of the recruitment process and confirmation that all participants met the inclusion and exclusion criteria, each participant underwent a fNIRS assessment. The VFT employed in this study consisted of a semantic-related task, including a 30-second pre-task resting period, a 60-second task period, and a 70-second post-task resting period (Fig. 3). During the task phase, participants were instructed to generate as many words as possible using the common Chinese characters “hua”, “he”, and “jiang”. During the resting periods, participants repeated the sequence “12345” at a consistent volume. Prior to the task, participants were provided with a standardized script to ensure they understood the testing process. The testing environment was kept quiet and comfortable, minimizing disturbances from noise, strong light, or other external factors. Participants were instructed to avoid movements of the head and neck, frequent blinking, and chewing during the measurement.

fNIRS data analysis

fNIRS data preprocessing was conducted using the cross-platform NIRS-KIT toolbox based on MATLAB [32]. The raw light intensity data collected during the assessment were converted into optical density data, which were subsequently transformed into levels of oxyhemoglobin (oxy-Hb), deoxyhemoglobin (deoxy-Hb), and total hemoglobin concentrations using an improved version of the Beer-Lambert law, which accounts for light absorption in biological tissues during measurements [33]. The converted hemoglobin concentration data were then saved in the NIRS-KIT data format. The increase in oxy-Hb, indicative of cortical activation, is calculated by subtracting the mean value of oxy-Hb during the resting period from that during the task period. Activation in each region of interest is assessed based on the respective channels (Fig. 2).

Statistical methods

Data management and analysis were conducted using SPSS 26.0 statistical software and the NIRS-KIT toolbox. Categorical data were presented as frequencies or percentages (%), and group comparisons were performed using the chi-square test. For continuous data, the Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to assess the normality of distribution. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (‾x ± s) and were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For non-normally distributed data, the median and interquartile range [M(P25, P75)] were reported, with group comparisons performed using the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test, followed by Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Spearman correlation analysis was used to examine the relationship between activation values in each brain region across the three groups and the HAMD-17 suicide item score (Item 3). The confidence interval (CI) for all statistical analyses was set at 95%, and differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05 (Bonferroni correction).

Machine learning methods

In the data preprocessing phase, we performed thorough cleaning and normalization of the collected data, removing outliers to enhance dataset quality and reliability. To simulate environmental disturbances and promote generalization, we applied data augmentation by adding normally distributed noise (mean = 0, standard deviation = 0.02) to the original data.

Our model utilized a three-layer, one-dimensional convolutional neural network (CNN) based on an analysis of EEG signal characteristics and preliminary experiments. The first convolutional layer used a kernel size of 9 with 22 input channels (one per brain region) and 64 output channels, enabling feature extraction over a broad region. The second layer used a kernel size of 5, with 64 input and 128 output channels, while the third used a kernel size of 3 with 128 input and 256 output channels. The decreasing kernel sizes allowed progressively deeper feature exploration. Each layer included ReLU activation, batch normalization to improve stability, and a max pooling layer (pool size and stride of 2) to retain essential features while reducing data dimensionality and computational load. This structure and configuration enhanced training efficiency while maintaining model performance.

After CNN feature extraction, we concatenated these features with integral values and fed them into a fully connected layer. The first fully connected layer had an input dimension of (39,936 + number of input channels) and an output dimension of 256. The second layer took 256 as input and output 128, while the third layer Transformed the input of 128 into an output of 3. We applied batch normalization, a dropout rate of 0.6 (optimized through experimentation), and the ReLU activation function to the first two layers to prevent overfitting and improve generalization. A final softmax layer converted the output into probability distributions for three categories: HCs, SIs, and NSIs.

The model used cross-entropy loss for multi-class classification, optimized by the AdamW algorithm (learning rate = 0.0002, weight decay = 0.001), which were determined through experiments for optimal convergence speed and performance. Training batch size was set to 16, with 200 total epochs and an early stopping patience of 20. We randomly shuffled the dataset and conducted 50 replicate experiments, each time randomly divided the dataset into 80% training and 20% test sets, and further used the KFold cross-validation method to divide 80% of the training sets into 10 folds to ensure the reliability of the experimental results. Forward propagation calculated the loss, followed by backpropagation for parameter updates. Model accuracy and other metrics were monitored on the validation subset, with the learning rate adjusted by the dynamic learning rate scheduler or early stopping applied as needed to ensure reliable and stable results.However, the difficulty in finding the compliance requirements, namely the publicly available dataset using the same instrument and acquisition method, leads to no rigorous external validation. This makes some limitations to the evaluation of the model generalization ability, and can only infer the performance of the model on different data based on internal cross-validation and test set evaluation.

Discussion

In this study, we utilized fNIRS to examine prefrontal cortex function in patients with MDD and healthy adults. The aim was to explore the differences in brain function between MDD patients with suicidal ideation and those without, to identify neuroimaging biomarkers for recognizing suicidal ideation in first-episode, drug-naive MDD patients. This could ultimately enhance the prediction of suicide risk and monitoring of suicidal ideation. A significant innovation of our research is the application of a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (CNN) for classification in first-episode, drug-naive MDD patients. Although numerous studies have confirmed the feasibility of deep learning methods, to our knowledge, this is the first application of such an approach to this specific patient population. Our findings not only provide a new perspective for identifying suicidal ideation in individuals with depression but also lay the groundwork for future related research. Moving forward, we hope that this method can play a greater role in clinical practice, facilitating precise treatment and suicide prevention strategies for patients with depression. A standout feature of our research is the application of a one - dimensional convolutional neural network (CNN) for classification in first - episode, drug - naive MDD patients. Differing from previous studies that may have used more traditional analysis methods or targeted more heterogeneous patient groups, our approach is novel in this specific context. By using CNN, we can leverage its ability to automatically extract complex features from fNIRS data, which is especially valuable considering the complexity of neural signals related to MDD and suicidal ideation. This not only offers a more in - depth analysis of the data but also provides a new perspective for understanding the neural mechanisms underlying suicidal ideation in this patient population. For example, compared with studies that rely solely on statistical analysis of mean values, CNN can capture subtle patterns and relationships in the data, potentially leading to more accurate identification of biomarkers.

In this study, we observed that, firstly, the activation values of various prefrontal cortex regions during the VFT task in the SIs group were significantly lower than those in the HCs group. The NSIs group also exhibited significantly reduced activation in the lDLPFC, FPC, OFC, and VLPFC regions during the VFT task. This indicates a decrease in hemodynamic responses in most prefrontal regions among MDD patients during the VFT task, consistent with previous research. Prior studies have found that the VFT task activates the prefrontal cortex in healthy individuals, whereas patients with depression show reduced activation and decreased oxyhemoglobin concentration during this task [34]. Current evidence indicates that PFC is involved in stress perception, emotional regulation, and working memory [35]. In MDD, dysregulation of prefrontal circuits and alterations in PFC function and structure may play a significant role in mood-related disorders [36], with prefrontal dysfunction also being critical for cognitive control [37]. However, another study found that during the VFT, the activation levels in the bilateral ventrolateral prefrontal cortex and anterior temporal cortex of MDD patients were lower than those of healthy controls [38]. Feng et al. reported reduced activation in the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, bilateral ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, bilateral orbitofrontal cortex, and left inferior frontal gyrus in the MDD group compared to the HCs group during the VFT task [39]. The heterogeneity in previous studies may arise from differences in participant age, medication status, and whether the patients are first-episode cases. Additionally, pathological changes are a dynamic process, leading to variability in the extent of pathological changes across different brain regions in patients, which results in differences in hemodynamic responses and may contribute to varying research outcomes. Our study, by focusing on first - episode, drug - naive patients, helps to isolate the effects of the disease itself on prefrontal function, reducing the confounding factors present in other studies. This allows for a more direct understanding of the relationship between MDD and prefrontal cortex activation in the context of suicidal ideation.

Secondly, compared to the NSIs group, the SIs group showed significantly reduced activation in the DLPFC, FPC, and OFC regions. PFC has long been recognized as the central executive hub of brain function. Lesions in the PFC not only alter cognitive functions but also affect executive abilities and decision-making, and most importantly, they can influence emotions and mood [40, 41]. Suicide is considered a disorder of cognitive control, involving decision-making processes, emotional regulation, and inhibitory control. This suggests that patients with suicidal ideation are likely to exhibit abnormalities in prefrontal function. Different regions of the prefrontal cortex are responsible for various functions: the DLPFC is primarily involved in memory, planning, and attention, while the VLPFC participates in attention and language functions. The OFC is more associated with behavioral inhibition, decision-making, and emotional regulation [42]. The FPC, located at the forefront of the prefrontal cortex, is believed to be crucial for regulating emotional stability; damage to this area often results in flat affect, sluggish response, inattentiveness, and impaired memory [43]. Zahid et al. posited that individuals with suicidal ideation exhibit specific blood oxygen activation patterns in the DLPFC, which distinguishes them from individuals without suicidal ideation [44]. Dombrovski suggested that reduced activation in the DLPFC among suicide attempt survivors enhances the connection between decision-making, cognitive control, and suicidal behavior [45]. Furthermore, Pu et al. found that MDD patients with suicidal ideation exhibited significantly lower activation in the DLPFC, OFC, and FPC compared to those without suicidal ideation [46]. Other studies have indicated that dysfunction of the OFC may increase suicide risk in MDD patients through heightened impulsivity and aggression levels [47]. All of these findings support our observations. Our study not only confirms these previous findings but also further explores the differences in a more homogeneous patient group. By focusing on first - episode, drug - naive patients, we can better understand the initial neural changes associated with suicidal ideation in MDD, which may be masked by long - term illness or medication use in other studies.

Thirdly, the activation values in various prefrontal regions of MDD patients were significantly negatively correlated with their suicidal ideation scores. This aligns with previous research. One study utilizing NIRS to monitor depressed patients with suicidal ideation found that hemodynamic changes in the DLPFC, OFC, and FPC were significantly negatively correlated with the severity of suicidal ideation [46]. Another study found that reduced activation in VLPFC during the VFT was associated with the severity of suicidal ideation, with changes in Oxy-Hb levels in this region promoting the intensity of suicidal ideation indirectly [48]. Our results not only confirm the reliability of previous studies but also suggest that lower activation of the prefrontal cortex indicates enhanced suicidal ideation.

Fourthly, this study’s deep learning approach, using a three-layer one-dimensional CNN with fully connected layers, offers a new method to classify brain function data from fNIRS in MDD patients and healthy adults. The CNN can automatically learn complex features from the fNIRS data, which improves classification accuracy. The model achieved its highest accuracy of 69.80% in the lFPC, where clear differences were found in brain activation among HCs, SIs, and NSIs. This suggests that the model effectively distinguishes these groups, making it useful for studying brain function differences in MDD. The high performance of the lFPC suggests that this region may be particularly significant in distinguishing between HCs, SIs, and NSIs. This finding corroborates previous research, highlighting that the FPC is one of the critical areas of the brain, playing a vital role in cognitive, emotional, decision-making, and social functions, and serving as a central hub in the decision-making and execution systems [49]. Another study discovered that the activation of the FPC in patients with MDD accompanied by suicidal ideation is significantly reduced compared to those with MDD without suicidal ideation [46]. Sensitivity and specificity scores vary across regions, with some areas being more effective in identifying true cases and others better at confirming absence of conditions. In contrast to traditional methods, our CNN - based approach can adapt to the complex and high - dimensional nature of fNIRS data. It can capture non - linear relationships and complex patterns that are difficult to detect with conventional methods, providing a more comprehensive and accurate understanding of the differences in brain function between different groups. This is a significant step forward in the field, as it allows for a more detailed exploration of the neural basis of MDD and suicidal ideation.

While promising, there is still room for improvement. Although 72.45% accuracy is strong, further work—such as testing different model settings, expanding the dataset, or exploring new designs—may improve results. Integrating fNIRS data with other information, such as clinical assessments or genetic data, might also provide a more complete understanding of MDD. In mental health applications, careful consideration is necessary due to the sensitive nature of the data and the potential consequences of errors. Thorough testing is essential before real-world application, and extra steps are required to ensure safety and accuracy. Overall, these results provide a strong foundation, though future studies may benefit from adapting the model to each region’s unique characteristics or combining data types for better insight into MDD. Our study’s focus on first - episode, drug - naive patients also provides a unique starting point for future research. By understanding the neural mechanisms in this relatively ‘pristine’ patient group, we can better explore how these mechanisms change over time, with treatment, or in the presence of other factors. This can lead to the development of more targeted and effective treatment strategies for MDD patients with suicidal ideation.

The above results indicate that fNIRS can serve as an auxiliary tool for identifying suicidal ideation in patients with first-episode MDD, aiding in better prediction of suicide risk and monitoring of suicidal ideation, thereby facilitating timely interventions. However, this study has Limitations. First, due to the limited penetration capability of fNIRS, it cannot detect deeper structures involved in emotional processing, such as the amygdala and anterior cingulate cortex. Future research should integrate fNIRS with other neuroimaging tools, such as fMRI, to address its shortcomings and explore changes in brain function from different dimensions. Additionally, our study focused on first-episode, drug-naive MDD patients, thus avoiding the confounding effects of medication and disease duration. However, it lacked long-term observational follow-up and did not delve into post-treatment MDD patients, highlighting the need for dynamic monitoring of treatment outcomes to further clarify the brain mechanisms underlying MDD. Lastly, we assessed suicidal ideation using item 3 of the HAMD, rather than a specialized tool for measuring suicidal ideation, such as the Beck Suicide Ideation Scale [50]. While this non-invasive and easily manageable approach is useful for initial screening [51], it does not provide an in-depth assessment of suicidal behavior.

Conclusions

The findings of this study indicate that DLPFC, FPC, and OFC may serve as biomarker brain regions for identifying suicidal ideation in first-episode drug-naive MDD patients. The fNIRS-VFT task shows promise as an auxiliary diagnostic tool for differentiating mental disorders in clinical settings.
