Utilization of advanced resuscitation skills among doctors and nurses in Uganda
Authors: Peter Kavuma, Mary Ellen Lyon, Doreen Okong Alaleit, Jonathan Byasi, Janat Tumukunde, Daniel Obua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSudden cardiac arrest remains a major global health challenge, contributing to approximately 3.7 million deaths annually. In Uganda, in-hospital cardiac arrest incidence stands at 2.3%, with only 7.4% achieving return of spontaneous circulation. Since the formal introduction of advanced resuscitation training in 2019, over 840 Ugandan healthcare professionals had been trained. However, no structured assessment has been conducted to evaluate the real-world utilization of the acquired knowledge and skills or to identify influencing factors. This study provides evidence to improve utilization shortfalls for better patient outcomes following cardiac arrest.ObjectiveTo determine the level and factors affecting the utilization of advanced resuscitation knowledge and skills among doctors and nurses in Uganda.MethodsA cross-sectional telephone survey was conducted among 413 doctors and nurses who had received advanced resuscitation training between 2020 and 2023. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire by trained research assistants. A generalized linear model with robust standard errors was used to identify factors associated with utilization. Prevalence ratios (PRs) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Variables with p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.ResultsAmong 413 doctors and nurses analysed, 70.5% reported frequent use of knowledge concepts and skills. Frequent use was 15% less common among males compared to females. Frequent use was highest (85.4%) among specialist doctors followed by medical officers (71.7%) and nurses (66.5%). Clinicians in higher level facilities reported frequent use more often (77.2%) compared to those employed in lower-level units (66.3%). Most ICU health workers (92.2%) reported frequent use while less than half of those in outpatient departments (45.6%) reported frequent use.ConclusionWhile approximately 70% of clinicians reported frequent use of advanced resuscitation skills, utilization varied significantly across gender, professional cadres, facility levels, and clinical departments. These differences likely reflect variation in clinical exposure, role expectations, and resource availability rather than differential competency. Enhancing consistent utilization of advanced resuscitation skills requires strengthened reinforcement across health systems, supported by improved access to resuscitation equipment and supplies.

African relevance

• Highlights post-training gaps in utilization of emergency resuscitation skills.

• Identifies key barriers to skill use across departments in low-resource health facilities.

• Provides evidence to aid improvements in emergency resuscitation.

• Reinforces the need for equipping staff in high-impact, low-resource emergency care units.

Introduction

Sudden cardiac arrest (SCA) is a major global health burden, responsible for an estimated 3.7 million deaths annually [1,2]. In low and middle income countries (LMICs), SCA is often precipitated by cardiovascular diseases and trauma, particularly from road traffic accidents, which remain prevalent due to underdeveloped emergency care systems [3,4]. Optimal survival from cardiac arrest depends on healthcare providers' ability to rapidly and competently implement advanced resuscitation techniques. These include manual defibrillation, advanced airway management, drug administration during cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), and arrhythmia management—skills typically taught in advanced life support (ALS) and advanced cardiac life support (ACLS) courses [5].

Since 2019, Uganda has scaled up ALS and ACLS training through Emergency Care Courses (ECC)—a private non-profit training organisation—and Cure Children's Hospital Uganda (CCHU)—a private non-profit paediatric specialty hospital, using standardized curricula from the European Resuscitation Council (ERC) and the American Heart Association (AHA) (2018/2020 versions) [6]. Most healthcare workers attend these courses through workplace sponsorship, though some self-fund; training is not currently mandatory for specific positions but is increasingly encouraged for staff in high-acuity areas. More than 840 healthcare workers had received this training by December 2023 [6]. However, limited data exists on whether the knowledge and skills gained are consistently applied in clinical settings. A study at Mulago National Referral Hospital—Uganda's largest public tertiary referral hospital with approximately 1,800 beds serving a national catchment population—found an in-hospital cardiac arrest incidence of 2.3%, with return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) in only 7.4%—a stark contrast to the European in-hospital ROSC average of 45% [4,7]. This discrepancy is partially attributed to underutilization of acquired resuscitation skills among healthcare workers [4].

Although more than 840 clinicians have now been trained, it is not clear how often these skills are used in real emergencies, especially in settings with limited resources. Understanding this gap is essential for improving cardiac arrest survival. This study aimed to determine the level and factors affecting utilization of advanced resuscitation knowledge and skills among doctors and nurses in Uganda.

Methods

Study design

This was a descriptive cross-sectional study conducted between 2020 and 2023. It assessed the self-reported utilization of advanced resuscitation knowledge and skills among doctors and nurses trained on ERC, ALS, and AHA, ACLS courses. The study applied the third level of the Kirkpatrick evaluation model, focusing on behaviour change—specifically, the translation of trained competencies into clinical practice.

Study setting

The study included doctors and nurses trained in advanced resuscitation at two training centers in Uganda: ECC (private non-profit) and CCHU (private non-profit). ECC accounts for approximately 80% of national training and together, both centers train around 210 healthcare professionals annually [6]. Participants were contacted via phone using contact information retrieved from institutional databases. Uganda's health system comprises both public (national, regional, and district referral hospitals; health centres) and private facilities, which were represented in this study.

Study population and sampling

The study targeted all Ugandan doctors and nurses trained in ERC ALS or AHA ACLS at ECC or CCHU between 2020 and 2023. Eligibility criteria included holding an active ALS/ACLS certificate from one of the two institutions and being actively practicing in Uganda. Individuals working outside the country during this period were excluded.

A sample size of 413 participants was determined using the Leslie Kish (1965) formula based on a proportion (40.5%) of emergency care providers who had good knowledge of ALS [8]. The total eligible population was approximately 840 individuals trained between 2020 and 2023. Using proportional allocation (ECC:CCHU = 4:1) based on each institution's training volume, 330 participants were selected from ECC and 83 from CCHU. Systematic sampling was applied by ordering each database alphabetically and selecting every second individual. Ineligible participants were replaced by the next available candidate.

Demographic characteristics (age, sex, cadre, years of experience) and institutional characteristics (facility level, urban/rural location, leadership role, availability of resuscitation supplies and equipment, clinical department) were collected.

Utilization of advanced resuscitation knowledge and skills was assessed using a 19-item Likert scale questionnaire. The instrument was developed following review of published ALS/ACLS competency assessment tools and adapted to the Ugandan context. Content validity was assessed by three emergency medicine specialists. The questionnaire was pretested with 20 clinicians trained in 2024 at ECC, and modifications were made to improve clarity and relevance. A composite score was derived. Utilization was classified as "Frequent use" or "Infrequent use" using a cut-off score calculated as: Cut-off = (H−L)/2 + L, where H = maximum possible score (95) and L = minimum possible score (19). Participants scoring ≥57 were classified as frequent users.

Data collection

Data were collected via telephone between April and October 2023 using a structured, pretested questionnaire administered by trained research assistants. Phone numbers were retrieved from institutional databases with prior permission from ECC and CCHU programme directors. These numbers had been collected during course registration for routine communication; participants had not previously consented to research contact. Accordingly, all potential participants received a pre-call text message introducing the study and informing them that a researcher would call within 72 h. Research assistants made up to three call attempts at different times of day before recording non-response. Calls averaged 18–22 min and were conducted between 09:00–20:00 EAT at participants' preference.

At call commencement, research assistants read a standardized information script detailing study purpose, procedures, risks, benefits, confidentiality protections, and the voluntary nature of participation. Participants were offered the opportunity to ask questions before providing verbal consent. Verbal consent was documented in the data collection form with the researcher's attestation and timestamp. Responses were captured electronically using Open Data Kit (ODK) software and submitted to a secure cloud-based server (ODK Aggregate, hosted on encrypted servers located in Kampala, Uganda) in real time.

Participants received mobile airtime credit valued at 5000 UGX (approximately USD 1.35) following survey completion as compensation for their time and phone usage. This amount was determined based on average local mobile data costs for a 20-minute call. Compensation was provided via mobile money transfer after confirming survey completion.

Data management and analysis

All data were anonymized using unique study identification codes and stored securely on encrypted servers accessible only to the principal investigator. Data were cleaned and exported to Stata software (version 17) for analysis. Descriptive statistics were generated for demographic and professional characteristics, reported as frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables and medians (interquartile ranges) for continuous variables. A Generalized Linear Model (GLM) with Poisson distribution and log link was used to estimate prevalence ratios (PRs) for factors associated with frequent utilization. Robust standard errors were applied to account for the common outcome (>10%) and to provide conservative confidence intervals [9].

Variables with p < 0.20 in bivariable analysis were included in multivariable analysis. Confounding was assessed using a 10 % change-in-estimate criterion, with age, facility level, and cadre evaluated as potential confounders. Model fit was assessed using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and deviance goodness-of-fit tests. Adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) with 95 % CIs and p-values are reported. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Quality control

The questionnaire was pretested with 20 participants trained in 2023 at ECC. (The study period was extended to early 2024 to complete data collection; the pretest was conducted in January 2024 with recently trained clinicians not included in the main sample.) Feedback informed final revisions. Research assistants received training on standardized data collection procedures, including use of neutral prompts, avoidance of leading questions, and standardized responses to participant queries. Where signs of potential bias were identified (e.g., inconsistent responses, hesitation), research assistants were trained to rephrase questions neutrally or, if necessary, pause the interview and document concerns. No interviews were terminated due to bias concerns. Daily supervision and data checks ensured accuracy and completeness.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was granted by the Makerere University School of Medicine Research and Ethics Committee (REF No. Mak-SOMREC-2024–855). Permission to access participant data was obtained from ECC, CCHU, and the Department of Anaesthesia. As described above, informed consent was obtained verbally before data collection. Data were anonymized, encrypted, and stored on secure servers. Participants received compensation for their time.

Factors associated with utilization of knowledge and skills.

Gender effects were complex. Overall, males had 15% lower prevalence of frequent use compared to females (aPR: 0.85, 95% CI: 0.74–0.98, p = 0.028). However, significant interaction effects revealed that male gender was associated with higher utilization within specific departments: males in medical/surgical wards (aPR: 1.87, 95% CI: 1.10–3.19, p = 0.021) and outpatient departments (aPR: 1.75, 95% CI: 1.16–2.62, p = 0.007) reported more frequent use than females in the same units. This pattern likely reflects departmental gender distribution and role assignment patterns—males in these settings may be disproportionately assigned to procedures or emergency response roles rather than reflecting inherent gender differences in competency [10].

Cadre effects also varied by department. Specialist doctors in maternity units (aPR: 2.61, 95% CI: 1.19–5.70, p = 0.016) and outpatient departments (aPR: 2.60, 95% CI: 1.23–5.47, p = 0.012) showed substantially higher utilization compared to medical officers in the same settings, suggesting that senior clinicians may retain and apply skills even in low-acuity environments.

Obstacles to utilization

The most frequently reported barrier was lack of essential equipment, cited by 342 (82.8%) participants. Other notable obstacles included lack of essential medications (279, 67.5%), limited cooperation from team members (132, 32.0%), and few emergency or cardiac arrest cases (105, 25.4% and 112, 27.1%, respectively). Legal limitations (e.g., scope of practice restrictions, liability concerns) were reported by 38 (9.2%).

Discussion

This study examined the level and factors associated with utilization of advanced resuscitation skills among Ugandan doctors and nurses trained in ALS/ACLS. Rather than simply restating results, this discussion critically examines why observed patterns emerged and their implications for emergency care strengthening.

Utilization reflects opportunity more than competency. The finding that 70.5% of clinicians reported frequent skill use is encouraging and aligns with global estimates [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. However, the wide variation across departments—from 92.2% in ICUs to 45.6% in outpatient settings—strongly suggests that clinical exposure, not differential ability, drives utilization. Clinicians cannot apply skills they do not have opportunity to practice. This interpretation is supported by the dominance of equipment and medication shortages as cited barriers, consistent with research from other low-resource settings [17,18].

Skill decay is selective. Basic competencies (ABCDE approach, non-technical skills) were nearly universally applied, while advanced interventions (defibrillation, pacing, cardioversion) were used infrequently. This pattern raises important questions about the cost-effectiveness of training all clinicians in complex, low-frequency procedures when systemic barriers limit their application. Task-shifting and tiered training models—where advanced skills are concentrated among staff in high-acuity units while others maintain core competencies—may represent a more efficient use of limited training resources [[18], [19], [20], [21]].

Departmental assignment is the strongest determinant of utilization. The 67% lower prevalence of frequent use in outpatient settings (aPR 0.33) represents the largest independent effect in our model. This is neither surprising nor indicative of failure; rather, it reflects appropriate task allocation within health systems. The challenge is ensuring that clinicians in low-acuity settings maintain sufficient proficiency to respond to unexpected emergencies, even if their baseline utilization is low. Periodic simulation-based refresher training and rotational placements through high-acuity departments warrant evaluation as potential solutions [[22], [23]].

Gender findings require careful interpretation. Our analysis revealed an apparent paradox: males had lower overall utilization (aPR 0.85) yet higher utilization in specific departments (medical/surgical wards, outpatient departments). These findings are reconcilable when considering compositional effects and role distribution. Females were overrepresented in nursing (72% of nurses were female) and underrepresented in specialist roles (31% of specialists were female). Because nurses had lower baseline utilization than doctors (66.5% vs. 71.7–85.4%), the aggregate gender effect partly reflects professional cadre distribution. Simultaneously, within mixed-gender departments, males may be preferentially assigned to procedural or emergency response roles—a pattern documented elsewhere [10,24]. This interpretation does not support claims of gender-based competency differences, which literature does not substantiate when opportunity is equal [[25], [26]]. Rather, it highlights the need to examine how workplace roles and expectations shape differential practice opportunities.

Health system factors overshadow individual characteristics. Notably, after full adjustment, neither cadre nor facility type remained independently significant. This suggests that proximal determinants of utilization—departmental assignment, resource availability, clinical exposure—mediate the effects of more distal individual characteristics. Interventions targeting these proximal factors are therefore more likely to improve utilization than those focused solely on individual clinician attributes.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include a large representative sample, systematic sampling, and analytic approaches that accounted for the common outcome and complex interactions. These methods enabled nuanced examination of disparities in skill utilization across multiple levels.

Limitations include the cross-sectional design; causality cannot be inferred. Self-report is subject to recall and social desirability bias, which may overestimate true utilization. Unmeasured factors—including facility safety culture, training quality, and team dynamics—likely influence utilization and could not be assessed. Research assistants were trained to use neutral, non-leading questions and to document concerns; however, bias cannot be fully eliminated. The study was conducted in Uganda and may not generalize to other LMIC settings with different training and health system configurations.

Conclusion

Approximately 70% of Ugandan clinicians trained in ALS/ACLS reported frequent use of their acquired skills. However, utilization varied markedly across clinical departments, reflecting differential exposure to emergency cases rather than differential competency. Basic resuscitation skills were well-maintained, while advanced interventions were applied infrequently due to equipment shortages and limited clinical opportunities.

These findings suggest three priority actions for policy and practice. First, resources for resuscitation equipment and medications should be concentrated in high-acuity units (ICU, ED) where advanced skills can be applied, while ensuring basic resuscitation capacity across all settings. Second, rotational placements through high-acuity departments should be evaluated as a strategy to maintain proficiency among staff based in low-utilization areas. Third, the complex gender patterns observed warrant further investigation of how workplace roles, assignment practices, and professional hierarchies shape differential practice opportunities—with attention to ensuring equitable access to skill-maintaining clinical experiences. Simply calling for "more training" will not address the systemic barriers documented here; what is needed is deliberate alignment of training investment with the clinical realities, resource constraints, and role distributions that characterize Ugandan emergency care.
