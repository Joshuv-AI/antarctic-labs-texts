Myocardial Bridge Causing Ventricular Tachycardia in a Competitive Athlete
Authors: Daniel J. McClintick, Joseph A. Dearani, Jamie A. Decker, Eli M. Friedman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArrhythmias associated with syncope and chest discomfort in competitive athletes include a wide differential. Evaluation of coronary artery anatomy is an essential component of work-up for a potential etiology.Case SummaryA competitive high school swimmer presented to sports cardiology clinic with multiple episodes of exertional chest discomfort, syncope, and wide complex tachycardia. Work-up revealed a left anterior descending myocardial bridge of 5 mm depth and 2.5 cm length. After evaluation, he underwent surgical division of the myocardial bridge and returned to sport without symptoms or recurrent episodes of ventricular tachycardia.DiscussionMyocardial bridging is a common finding that is typically benign; however, it may be underrecognized as a cause for chest discomfort, wide complex tachycardia, syncope, and sudden death in athletes. In the appropriate setting, surgical division of the bridge can be a definitive management strategy for athletes with clinically significant myocardial bridging.Take-Home MessagesCoronary artery anomalies are a known cause of sudden cardiac arrest in athletes. The coronary arteries should be thoroughly evaluated in any athlete with symptoms concerning for a cardiac etiology. Although commonly benign, a clinically significant myocardial bridge may be an etiology for chest discomfort and syncope in competitive athletes. Surgical division of the bridge is a potential management strategy.

He was in his usual state of health until approximately 3 years before this presentation when he was playing basketball and had an episode of chest discomfort and dizziness, followed by loss of consciousness. Emergency medical services arrived and noted a WCT (Figure 1). Amiodarone and metoprolol were given which converted the rhythm, and he was transported to a local hospital and admitted for further testing.Figure 1Presenting Rhythm Captured on EMS StripPresenting monomorphic wide complex tachycardia as captured by emergency medical services (EMS) during the index event.

During the admission, he underwent an electrocardiogram (ECG), echocardiogram, and cardiac magnetic resonance. Echocardiogram showed a structurally normal heart with normal origins of the coronary arteries, and cardiac magnetic resonance was normal with no late gadolinium enhancement. Electrolytes and thyroid studies were normal. He had an electrophysiology study which demonstrated AV node reentrant tachycardia with aberrant conduction. He underwent ablation of the slow pathway.

He continued to have episodes of exertional chest discomfort, palpitations, and presyncope. He described the chest discomfort as “an elephant sitting on his chest.” He completed a maximal effort stress test which was unremarkable with no arrhythmias or ischemic ECG changes and was cleared to return to competitive swimming. Two years after his initial event he represented to his electrophysiologist as an outpatient with a similar episode, but without syncope and had a WCT captured on his Apple Watch (Figure 2). He had a repeat electrophysiology study that was negative and underwent placement of an implantable loop recorder (ILR). He also had genetic testing that was negative for any inherited arrhythmia syndromes. Approximately 9 months later, he had an event with exertional chest discomfort, palpitations, and presyncope. This event corresponded to a WCT captured by the ILR with different morphology from the prior arrhythmia (Figure 3). Past Medical History

The patient has no significant past medical history. His only medication is nadolol, which was prescribed for the aforementioned WCT. He has no family history of sudden cardiac arrest (SCA) or death, no inherited cardiac conditions, no family history of drowning, or family history of unexplained motor vehicle accidents. The athlete consumes no caffeine or energy drinks, he does not use performance-enhancing drugs or stimulants, and he takes no supplements.

Differential Diagnosis

The initial episode of chest discomfort and loss of consciousness with WCT in the setting of physical exertion raises the possibility of ventricular tachycardia vs supraventricular tachycardia with aberrancy. Given that he had a supraventricular tachycardia ablation and continued to have symptomatic WCT of different morphology, ventricular tachycardia was the leading diagnosis.

Etiologies for chest discomfort and ventricular tachycardia include the following: coronary artery anomalies, coronary artery vasospasm, structural heart disease, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, long QT syndrome, Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome, scar mediated ventricular tachycardia (VT), vasospasm, and myocarditis.

Investigations

Given the exertional component to the symptoms and high index of suspicion for a coronary anomaly, the patient was referred for a coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA). This demonstrated a proximal to mid left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery myocardial bridge (MB) of 2.5 cm length and 5 mm depth (Figure 5). The patient underwent cardiac catheterization which confirmed the LAD bridge. To further elucidate whether the MB was responsible for his symptoms, provocative testing with dobutamine infusion was performed and showed severe systolic compression with physiological flow limitation (instantaneous wave free ratio: 0.85).Figure 5Coronary CT AngiogramCoronary computed tomography (CT) angiogram showing myocardial bridging of the mid left anterior descending. Management

Given the size and extent of the MB along with the patient's history and objective findings, the patient was referred for surgical intervention. Division of the MB (Figure 6) was performed with an uncomplicated postoperative course. An implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) was discussed for secondary prevention. With shared decision-making, however, ICD placement was deferred. The patient recovered well and returned to sport with no restrictions and no further episodes of syncope, presyncope, palpitations, or chest discomfort.Figure 6Surgical Division of Myocardial BridgeIntraoperative photos showing the left anterior descending (LAD) covered by myocardial bridge (A) and the LAD after undergoing surgical division of the bridge (B).

Discussion

We present the case of a competitive swimmer with recurrent episodes of exertional chest discomfort and WCT with loss of consciousness due to an LAD MB found on CCTA. After his index hospitalization, the patient underwent extensive work-up and had an ablation for AV node reentrant tachycardia with aberrancy, but continued to have symptoms and a documented WCT with varying morphologies. After undergoing surgical division of the bridge, he was able to return to competitive swimming with no symptoms or events. This case highlights a common condition that uncommonly causes symptoms and emphasizes the need for a multidisciplinary approach in caring for athletes with cardiovascular disease.

The differential diagnosis for exertional chest pressure, syncope, and WCT in competitive athletes is broad, but requires a thoughtful clinical approach given the concern for etiologies that predispose an individual to SCA.1 Syncope that occurs during exercise, as in this case, is a red flag and requires a holistic cardiac evaluation.1,2

In athletes undergoing echocardiograms, coronary artery origins can be visualized >90% of the time and should be included as part of the protocol in those undergoing the study, especially in those with cardiac symptoms and/or concerns. Despite coronary anomalies being responsible for SCA in athletes, echocardiography laboratories report the proximal coronary artery anatomy <20% of the time.3, 4, 5 For those who have suffered SCA, a high index of suspicion for coronary artery anomalies should be considered as supported by the 2024 Heart Rhythm Society Expert Consensus Statement on Arrhythmias in the Athlete. If the coronary anatomy is unknown or symptoms persist with a high index of suspicion for a coronary artery anomaly, further testing should be undertaken to confirm coronary artery origin and to rule out other anomalies. Our patient accordingly underwent CCTA, which demonstrated normal coronary origins and the MB of the proximal to mid LAD. Although MB is not typically associated with adverse clinical outcomes, certain features such as depth >2 mm and length >2.5 cm increase the likelihood of silent ischemia, ventricular arrhythmias, and rarely SCA.6 Prior case reports suggest that LAD bridges that cause ischemia in particular have been associated with SCA in athletes.7,8 In this patient with exertional chest discomfort and recurrent WCTs, the index of suspicion was high for a coronary anomaly.

For the present case, given MB is an uncommon cause of VT and SCA, cardiac catheterization was performed with pharmacologic stress testing to demonstrate inducible ischemia in the territory of the bridge. Although bridging is frequently asymptomatic, the instantaneous wave-free ratio of 0.85 suggests this MB was clinically significant and implicated the bridge as the most likely etiology of VT. A potential mechanistic relationship between the MB and VT include demand ischemia during exercise, systolic compression (which is more vigorous and occurs during a larger proportion of the cardiac cycle during exercise due to higher heart rates), or local catecholamine mediated toxicity from ischemia resulting in VT. Although there are no formal guidelines on the clinical management of MB, surgical treatment is generally only pursued after failure of medical management.6 However, patients who have high-risk features (eg, SCA, sustained VT, myocardial infarction), surgical intervention can be considered for early treatment as was pursued in this case. If there is a high index of suspicion for a clinically significant MB, physiological testing in the catheterization laboratory can help determine the relevance of the bridge. In athletes with exertional symptoms, it is intuitive that provocation with dobutamine or equivalent agent be pursued to replicate the effects of exertion on the heart.

Postoperatively, ICD implantation for secondary prevention was discussed with the patient and his parents. However, because the bridge was strongly thought to be the etiology of the VT and the patient underwent definitive surgical management, ICD implantation was deferred using a shared decision-making model as recommended by the 2025 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology scientific statement on cardiovascular abnormalities in competitive athletes.9

Follow-Up

The patient is currently 18 months postsurgical division of the MB and doing well without recurrent symptoms. He continues to compete at a high level without further episodes. He no longer takes any medication.

Conclusions

The evaluation of an athlete with exertional symptoms and a WCT requires an intensive cardiac work-up to fully evaluate and adjudicate all potential etiologies that could lead to SCA. All effort must be made to rule out structural, electrophysiological, and environmental etiologies of those symptoms. Coronary artery evaluation is an important component in the evaluation of athletes who have cardiac symptoms to assess for coronary artery anomalies. MB is an uncommon cause of VT but should be considered if there are high-risk features and in the absence of other explainable causes of VT. As with all aspects of sports cardiology, shared decision-making should be used to determine work-up, treatment, and return to play.
