PV-10 enhances immune responses in hepatitis B vaccination through STING pathway
Authors: Patrick Sipila, Yusheng Zhao, Satbir Thakur, Mehr Malhotra, Chunfen Zhang, Lian Willetts, Luis Murguia-Favela, Aru Narendran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDespite the efficacy of current hepatitis B vaccines, approximately 10% of immunocompetent individuals remain non-responsive, underscoring the urgent need for novel adjuvants to enhance vaccine-induced immunity. In this study, we investigated PV-10, a 10% Rose Bengal solution, for its potential to activate the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway and enhance both innate and adaptive immune responses. Through molecular docking, we demonstrated that Rose Bengal binds to the ligand-binding domain of STING with an affinity of −7.1 kcal/mol (−7.0 to −9.0 kcal/mol for moderate binding), promoting dimer stabilization via hydrophobic and hydrogen-bonding interactions. In the human acute monocytic leukemia cell line THP-1, treatment with PV-10 induced the phosphorylation of key downstream signaling proteins, including TBK1, IRF3, and NF-κB p65, and uniquely generated a high molecular weight STING band indicative of dimer formation. Cytokine profiling revealed a time-dependent increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines following PV-10 treatment. Furthermore, in an in vitro model, dendritic cells were pulsed with hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg)-derived peptides, HBV-1 (TVELLSFLPSDFFPSV, extended HBsAg epitope) and HBV-2 (FLPSDFFPSV, minimal cytotoxic T lymphocyte epitope) and then the pulsed DCs were used to prime CD8+ T-cells. HBsAg–primed CD8+ T-cells exhibited significantly enhanced IFN-γ secretion when co-cultured with HBsAg-positive hepatoma cells in the presence of PV-10 compared to vehicle-treated controls. These findings indicate that PV-10 functions as a potent STING agonist, stabilizing STING dimerization, and eliciting an immune microenvironment conducive to robust antigen presentation and T-cell activation, thereby demonstrating its potential as a novel adjuvant for improving hepatitis B vaccine efficacy, particularly in vaccine non-responders.

Introduction

Hepatitis B (HB) is a significant global health issue caused by the infection of hepatitis B virus (HBV) that attacks the liver. HBV infection can lead to acute and chronic diseases and put people at high risk of death from cirrhosis, liver failure, and hepatocellular carcinoma.1 It is estimated that approximately 254 million people live with chronic HBV infection, with new incidences of 1.2 million infections each year globally.2 Hepatitis B can be effectively prevented by vaccination. HB vaccines are designed to induce an immune response protecting the human body from HBV infection by eliciting the production of neutralizing antibodies against hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg). The HB Vaccination Global Initiative has significantly improved vaccination rates, thereby reducing the spread of the disease. However, studies have revealed that approximately 10% of the healthy immunocompetent population are vaccine non-responders.3,4 These non-responders do not develop protective levels of anti-HBs after full vaccination, with an antibody titer below 10 mIU/mL.5 Although several disease conditions and lifestyle risk factors were identified, the molecular mechanism underlying this is still unclear.6,7 Proposed factors include variability in HLA haplotypes that limit epitope presentation, insufficient activation of innate immune sensors, and impaired T-cell priming despite overall immune competence.8 Immune adjuvants are substances that can boost, accelerate, or extend the immune response to antigens commonly used in vaccines. Immune adjuvants function by recruiting immune cells, inducing cytokine production, and enhancing antigen presentation, among other mechanisms.9 Aluminum salts, including aluminum hydroxide and aluminum phosphate, are the most common immune adjuvant in HB vaccines. These salts function by adsorbing the antigens and stimulating antibody production by promoting Th2-type immunity.10 Some HB vaccines incorporate CpG oligodeoxynucleotide (ODN) as the immune adjuvant in the formulation. ODN is a synthetic DNA sequence containing unmethylated CpG motifs that are designed to mimic bacterial DNA to stimulate the immune system. It can activate Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) and prime the immune system for infections by triggering interferon regulatory factor 7 (IRF7) activation, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and type I interferon production.11 Another immune adjuvant liposomal delivery system used in HB vaccines is AS04, which consists of monophosphoryl lipid A (MPL), a Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) agonist, and aluminum salt to prolong antigen retention and immune stimulation.12 AS04-adjuvanted HB vaccine can boost the immune response in immunocompromised individuals.13,14 Despite enhanced efficacy in HB vaccines using these immune adjuvants, they cannot fully address the vaccine non-responders. There is an urgent need for a novel immune adjuvant that is underpinned by unique biological mechanisms for vaccine non-responders. These immune adjuvants should provide a more balanced and robust Th1/Th2 response, offer an improved safety profile, and demonstrate reduced reactogenicity.15

As the mechanisms underlying non-responsiveness are multifactorial and incompletely defined, targeting upstream pathways that broadly enhance antigen presentation and T-cell activation may help overcome this barrier. In this regard, the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway is a crucial component of both innate and adaptive immune responses. It is the key mechanism by which cells detect cytosolic DNA, including DNA from pathogens and damaged cells. This facilitator connects the upstream DNA sensor molecule, cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS), to the downstream signaling molecules, including TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1), interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3), and nuclear factor κB (NF-κB).16 When this pathway is triggered by the sensing of cytosolic DNA, cyclic GMP-AMP monophosphate (cGAMP) generated by cGAS binds to the ligand-binding domain (LBD) of STING. Cyclic di-GMP, a second messenger produced by bacteria, can also bind to STING. Upon binding, STING dimerizes and translocates from the endoplasmic reticulum to the Golgi apparatus, which results in phosphorylation and recruitment of downstream effector proteins.17 Activation of the cGAS-STING pathway can stimulate stronger immune responses through enhanced antigen presentation and cytokine production. Studies have shown that it can elicit a more balanced Th1/Th2 response compared to traditional immune adjuvants.18 Therefore, the cGAS-STING pathway has been exploited in the development of vaccines and cancer immunotherapies. Several STING agonists have shown potential to enhance immune responses in conjunction with vaccines against SARS-CoV-2 infection.19–21 The first generation of STING agonists was cyclic dinucleotides (CDNs) that mimicked the structural features of its substrate, such as cyclic-di-GMP, and cGAMP. Because these CDNs had high molecular weight and hydrophobicity, they demonstrated poor drug properties, including limited bioavailability. The cyclic structure in CDNs also suffered from rapid degradation by ectonucleotide pyrophosphatase/phosphodiesterase 1 (ENPP1) and quick renal clearance.22 Thus, the second generation of STING agonists is primarily non-CDN small molecules that have improved potency and pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles. However, some of these agonists have significant hepatic toxicity, which limits their further development.

Rose Bengal (4,5,6,7-tetrachloro-2,’4,’5,’7’-tetraiodofluorescein disodium, RB) is a synthetic dye that has been clinically used in ophthalmology and liver function diagnoses. PV-10, a 10% RB solution in 0.9% saline, has shown promising anti-tumor activities in a diverse range of solid tumors and leukemias, including melanoma, metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, refractory neuroblastoma, and pediatric leukemias.23–26 In murine models, PV-10 administration led to regression of lesions and metastases through T-cell activity, increased IFN-γ, and infiltration of dendritic cells (DCs), which highlights its potential use as an immune adjuvant.27,28 However, the molecular mechanism underlying this phenomenon is still largely unknown. Our preliminary studies suggested that PV-10 may act as a stabilizer of STING dimerization.29 In the present study, the molecular docking study indicates that PV-10 can bind to the LBD of STING with high affinity, acting as a molecular glue that stabilizes the functional dimer assembly of STING. To further investigate the mechanism of action, we examined activation of the STING pathway in cells treated with PV-10. As a proof of concept, we also studied the effect of PV-10 on IFN-γ secretion by HBsAg-primed CD8+ T-cells in response to HBsAg-positive hepatoma cells. Together with cytokine profiling of cells treated with PV-10, we provide the first comprehensive evidence that PV-10 functions as a STING agonist that stabilizes its dimerization and can potentially be used as an immune adjuvant to enhance the immune response to HB vaccines.

Materials and methods

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was performed with the AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 program.30,31 The crystal structure of human STING in complex with amidobenzimidazole (PDB ID: 6DXL) was used for docking.32,33 The binding pocket was created by selecting the ligand amidobenzimidazole, covering critical residues from both STING monomers that participate in intermolecular interactions with the ligand, including Tyr167, Arg238, Tyr240, Asn242, Glu60, and Thr263, from both STING monomers. The structure of RB was introduced in ChemDraw 22.2.0, and the structure file was sent for energy minimization in Open Babel toolbox with classical steepest descent algorithm under MMFF94 force field, followed by conjugate gradient algorithm until convergence was reached.34 The docking results were analyzed and visualized with BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer. The best docking pose with the lowest energy (−kcal/mol) was selected. The 2D interaction map was generated under the protein–ligand interaction program in Discovery Studio Visualizer.

Cell culture

Human acute monocytic leukemia cell line THP-1 cells were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). PLC/PRF/5 hepatoma cells were provided by Dr. Gerald Minuk at the University of Manitoba. The cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. CD8+ T-cells and dendritic cells (DCs) were isolated from three healthy volunteers and cultured in complete RPMI supplemented with cytokines (PeproTech). All volunteers confirmed that they were in good health, with no ongoing medical concerns, current symptoms, history of chronic illness, or use of immunosuppressive or other relevant medications. No additional laboratory testing was performed to further evaluate the immune status. The detailed culture and differentiation conditions are described in IFN-γ ELISpot assay. All study specimens were collected following informed consent and approval by the Health Research Ethics Board of Alberta (HREBA Ethics ID: HREBA.CC-16–0286). Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Immunoblotting

THP-1 cells were cultured in a 6-well plate at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well. The cells were treated with vehicle control, 5 µM cGAMP (MedChemExpress HY-100564A), or 100 µM PV-10 (provided by Provectus Biopharmaceuticals, USA) for 24 hours before harvesting. After incubation, cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors on ice for 15 min. The concentration of protein i the samples was measured by the DC protein assay (Bio-Rad) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples containing 20 µg of protein were loaded into each lane on 10% polyacrylamide gels. Proteins were then separated by gel electrophoresis and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes through the Bio-Rad Turbo transfer system. Membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in tris-buffered saline containing Tween 20 (TBST) for 90 min, followed by three times washing with TBST. It was then incubated with primary antibodies (1:1,000 dilution in TBST with 5% BSA) overnight at 4°C, followed by washing three times with TBST. The secondary antibodies (1:3,000 in TBST) were then incubated with the membranes for 45 min at room temperature. After washing three times with TBST, the membranes were incubated with Clarity Western ECL substrate (BioRad) as per the manufacturer’s protocol and imaged using the ChemiDoc imaging system. The following STING pathway-related primary antibodies used for immunoblotting were purchased from Cell Signalling Technology: STING (13647); TBK1/NAK (3013); phospho-TBK1/NAK Ser172 (5483); IRF3 (4302); phospho-IRF3 Ser396 (4947); NF-κB p65 (8242); NF-κB phospho-p65 Ser536 (3033). Loading control β-actin primary antibody (MA1-140) was obtained from Thermo Fisher. HRP-linked anti-rabbit (7074) and anti-mouse (7076) secondary antibodies were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology.

Immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS)

THP-1 cells were incubated with 100 µM PV-10 for 24 hours, then washed with PBS and lysed in RIPA buffer as described above. The pre-cleared cell lysate was incubated with STING antibody at 4°C for 2 hours, followed by incubation with the antibody-STING complex and protein A beads at 4°C overnight. The immunoprecipitated STING and interacting proteins were eluted from the beads and then resolved using 10% SDS-PAGE. After gel electrophoresis, proteins were dyed using Coomassie Blue, and bands corresponding to ~70 kDa were excised. These proteins were treated with trypsin, and the peptides were analyzed on a coupled mass spectrometer and nanoflow liquid chromatography system at the University of Calgary’s Southern Alberta Mass Spectrometry facility. Lumos raw data files were generated and converted into Mascot Generic Format (MGF) using RawConverter (Scripps) operating in data-dependent mode. The MGF file was used to search a database using the Mascot algorithm.

Cytokine and chemokine profiling

Conditioned cell culture media from THP-1 cells treated with 100 µM PV-10 for 0–48 hours was collected by centrifugation and stored at −80°C until analysis. A panel of immune and inflammatory cytokines and chemokines was assayed using a multiplex array by Eve Technologies. The analytes included the following: fractalkine (CX3CL1), Fms-like tyrosine kinase 3 ligand (FLT-3L), granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF), granulocyte-macrophage colony stimulating factor (GM-CSF), growth-regulated oncogene alpha (GRO-α), interferon gamma (IFN-γ), interferon gamma-induced protein 10 (IP-10/CXCL10), interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL1-RA), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-8 (IL-8), interleukin-18 (IL-18), monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), and monocyte chemoattractant protein-3 (MCP-3).

IFN-γ ELISpot assay

CD8+ T-cells and dendritic cells (DCs) were isolated from healthy donor peripheral blood as previously described.35 Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated using a standard Ficoll-Paque (Cytivia) gradient with centrifugation at 2,000 rpm for 30 minutes. Monocytes were subsequently isolated using the plastic adherence method by incubating 2 × 108 cells in the T75 culture flask for 2 hours, after which non-adherent cells were carefully washed off with RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS.36,37 Monocytes were then differentiated into DCs in complete RPMI supplemented with 100 ng/mL GM-CSF and 25 ng/mL IL-4 for 5 days. The HBV-1 (ATVELLSFLPSDFFPSV) and HBV-2 (FLPSDFFPSV) antigen peptides representing HBsAg were synthesized by Shanghai Royobiotech. Both peptides are well-known HBsAg-derived T-cell epitopes. HBV-2 corresponds to the minimal cytotoxic T lymphocyte epitope, whereas HBV-1 encompasses HBV-2 sequences which requires uptake and proteasomal processing by antigen presenting cells.38,39 The use of these two peptides represents two complementary modes of antigen recognition by the immune system. DCs were pulsed overnight with HBV-1 or HBV-2 peptides at a concentration of 50 µg/mL.40 CD8+ T-cells isolated from PBMCs using anti-CD8 magnetic beads were co-cultured with the pulsed DCs for 5 days (DCs to CD8+ ratio of 1:10) in complete RPMI supplemented with 10 ng/mL IL-2. The primed CD8+ T-cells were then co-cultured with PLC/PRF/5 cells at a 1:1 ratio with 5 µM PV-10. CD8+ T-cell responses were examined upon stimulation by HBsAg-positive PLC/PRF/5 hepatoma cells using IFN-γ ELISpot assay (R&D Systems EL285) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. The images were obtained using the EVOS M7000 system. Data were analyzed by ImageJ (1.53k)41 and presented as relative IFN-γ spot forming cells. Recombinant IFN-γ was used to confirm antibody target specificity.

Results

RB has high binding affinity for STING in silico

To provide insight into the effect of PV-10 on the STING pathway, we performed molecular docking to model the interaction between RB and STING. Our in silico analysis showed that RB interacts with the LBD of STING, occupying the same active pocket as its endogenous ligand cGAMP (Figure 1(A)). RB binds to the deep pocket of the LBD with a moderate affinity of −7.1 kcal/mol (−7.0 to −9.0 kcal/mol for moderate binding). The two ring systems of RB, connected by a flexible carbon–carbon single bond, adopted a similar V-shape to that of the LBD pocket. Several hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds anchor RB to the STING dimer assembly (Figure 1(B)). The fused aromatic ring of RB undergoes pi-pi stack type hydrophobic interaction with Tyr167 (5.19 Å), whereas the iodines in the same ring undergo halogen-pi type hydrophobic interactions with both Tyr167 (3.92 Å) and Tyr240 (4.88 Å). Previous studies have shown that Tyr240 is an important residue that is involved in ligand binding, as mutation of Tyr240 abrogates CDN binding to STING.42 On the other side, the carbonyl oxygen in the fused aromatic ring undergoes strong hydrogen bonding with Thr263 of the other STING monomer with a distance of 2.16 Å. Furthermore, the chlorinated benzene ring in the structure of RB interacts with the alkyl side chain of Pro264 through pi-alkyl type hydrophobic interactions with a distance of 5.32 Å. Overall, the chlorinated benzene ring in RB reaches deep within the solvent-inaccessible region of the LBD via hydrophobic interactions, while the fused tricyclic structure brings the two monomers together through both hydrophobic and hydrogen bonding interactions. Tyr240 capped RB at the active site of LBD and stabilized the STING dimer assembly. The docking results suggest that RB can function as a STING agonist and promote its dimerization (Figure 1). PV-10 induces STING dimerization and activates the STING pathway

To study the effect of PV-10 on STING dimerization and subsequent activation, THP-1 cells were treated for 24 hours with 100 µM PV-10 compared to vehicle control or 5 µM cGAMP, the canonical activator of STING, followed by immunoblotting. Interestingly, STING dimerization was observed with PV-10 treatment, indicated by the distinct higher molecular weight band in addition to the monomeric form observed in all the treatment groups (Figure 2(A)). To further validate dimerization, STING was immunoprecipitated from cells treated with PV-10, which was then resolved by gel electrophoresis, and the corresponding higher molecular weight band was excised and submitted for mass spectrometry analysis (Figure 2(B)). STING was identified as an abundant protein in the sample compared to vehicle-treated control, further suggesting the presence of a highly stable dimer upon PV-10 treatment. To investigate the effects of PV-10 on the STING pathway, the level of key signaling molecules and their phosphorylation status were examined in THP-1 cells. In response to cGAMP, the levels of phosphorylated TBK1, IRF3, and p65 were robustly upregulated without affecting the total levels of these proteins (Figure 3). Similarly, PV-10 stimulated STING pathway activation through increased phosphorylation of TBK1, IRF3, and p65. PV-10 upregulates cytokines and chemokines involved in the immune response

To further understand the role of PV-10 as a STING agonist in stimulating both innate and adaptive immune responses, cytokine profiling was performed in conditioned media from cells treated with 100 µM PV-10 for 0–48 h. Levels of several key cytokines and chemokines exhibited a time-dependent increase up to 48 h post-treatment (Figure 4). The release of pro-inflammatory cytokines steadily accumulated in the conditioned media stimulated by PV-10 (Figure 4(A)). Among these, IL-6 increased the most, with a 31-fold change at 48 h compared to baseline. Other important cytokines, FLT-3L and IL-18, were also increased by 7.4 and 4.5 fold, respectively. G-CSF, GM-CSF, and IL1-RA were increased by a factor of 3–4 times upon PV-10 treatment. Many of these cytokines are also known to induce IFN-γ, which doubled in conditioned media from PV-10-treated cells. In addition to these cytokines, upregulation of various chemokines was also observed in conditioned media with PV-10 (Figure 4(B)). Notably, the level of fractalkine increased 31-fold after treatment. MCP-1 and IL-8 levels increased 6.5 and 5.9 fold, respectively. PV-10 treatment induced four times higher levels of IP-10. In the conditioned media, the maximum level of GROα and MCP-3 was increased by 3.3 and 2.7 fold, respectively. Taken together, PV-10 induced a robust production of various cytokines and chemokines that are essential for innate and adaptive immune response. PV-10 enhances IFN-γ secretion by primed CD8+ T-cells

To examine the immunomodulatory potential of PV-10, we examined its ability to enhance IFN-γ secretion from HBsAg-primed CD8+ T-cells following stimulation with HBsAg-positive hepatoma PLC/PRF/5 cells. DCs pulsed with HBV antigen peptides representing HBsAg were used to prime CD8+ T-cells from healthy donors. The primed T-cells were then co-cultured with HBsAg-positive PLC/PRF/5 hepatoma cells, followed by quantification of IFN-γ secretion by ELISpot assays (Figure 5(A)). As expected, T-cells primed with HBV-1 and HBV-2 peptides exhibited increased IFN-γ secretion upon stimulation with HBsAg-positive cells, with minimal secretion observed in vehicle- and PV-10-treated cells without HBV peptides (Figure 5(B)). In primed T-cells, treatment with PV-10 significantly increased IFN-γ secretion by greater than double relative to vehicle control. The results indicate the ability of PV-10 to stimulate IFN-γ secretion by HBsAg-primed CD8+ T-cells when stimulated with HBsAg-positive cells, highlighting its potential as an immune adjuvant. Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that PV-10, a 10% formulation of RB, acts as a potent stimulator of the STING pathway, thereby enhancing immune responses to vaccination. STING is a 378 amino-acid protein consisting of three main domains.43 The transmembrane domain is composed of four transmembrane helices (residues 1–138, Figure 1(A)) that anchor the protein to the endoplasmic reticulum membrane before translocating to the Golgi apparatus. The LBD located in the cytoplasm (residues 139–336, Figure 1(A)) interacts with its ligands, such as cyclic di-GMP and cGAMP.44 The C-terminus (residues 337–379) contains specific motifs that recognizes and recruits downstream signaling proteins, including TBK1 and IRF3. These motifs are only revealed upon ligand binding to STING. In its apo state, the LBD exhibits a V-shape open pocket in the dimer STING model. The ligands interact with the LBD deep in the pocket right above the dimer interface, without being fully buried by the binding pocket. Upon binding, both monomers undergo an inward rotation toward the ligand-binding site. The inward movement closes the pocket to form a tighter V-shape with reduced tip distance. This conformational change exposes the C-terminal motifs, which are critical for protein interactions and STING pathway activation.45 The conformational shift is mainly driven by the interactions between the ligand and LBD residues through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. Our molecular docking analysis showed that RB, the active ingredient of PV-10, binds to the LBD of STING with high affinity and promotes dimer assembly through hydrophobic and hydrogen-bonding interactions. The dimerization of STING and stabilization effect, which appears to be unique to PV-10 treatment, was further validated in vitro, as evidenced by the appearance of a higher molecular weight band corresponding to STING that was absent in controls, including cGAMP-treated cells. Immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry further validated the presence of a stable STING dimer upon PV-10 treatment.

Canonical activation of the STING pathway depends on cGAMP produced by cGAS upon sensing cytosolic DNA,46 whereas non-canonical activation is independent of cGAS/cGAMP.47 Even though these pathways are induced by different triggers, they converge on downstream signaling molecules. Activation starts with dimerization of STING, which is then translocated to the Golgi apparatus where TBK1 is recruited and phosphorylated.48 Activated TBK1 then phosphorylates IRF3, which translocates into the nucleus to induce type I interferon production. In addition, the NF-κB pathway is generally activated by the STING pathway, especially in non-canonical STING activation.49 The activation of NF-κB results in phosphorylation of the p65 subunit, which stimulates pro-inflammatory cytokine production. Activation of the STING pathway in PV-10-treated cells was confirmed by upregulated phosphorylation of key signaling molecules, TBK1, IRF3, and NF-κB p65.

Consistent with activation of the STING pathway, PV-10 upregulated numerous pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines that are critical for both innate and adaptive immune responses. For example, studies have shown that several of the cytokines upregulated by PV-10, including FLT-3L and GM-CSF, are critical in promoting the development and activity of DCs.50,51 Many of these cytokines, especially IL-18, work in synergy with other factors to induce IFN-γ, which was also increased in conditioned media from PV-10-treated cells.52 G-CSF is involved in regulating other immune cells, such as neutrophils, in the initial innate immune response.53 In mouse models, higher levels of IL1-RA have been correlated with enhanced HBsAg neutralizing antibody levels, which may benefit vaccine non-responders.54 In addition to these factors upregulated by PV-10, chemokines such as IP-10, GROα, and MCP-3 also contribute to the recruitment of various immune cells to the vaccination site and thus enhance antigen uptake.55 This cytokine and chemokine milieu induced by immune adjuvants is particularly important for vaccine efficacy to enhance antigen presentation, T-cell priming, and the generation of a balanced Th1/Th2 immune response.

Using an in vitro model with HBsAg-primed CD8+ T-cells, we demonstrated that PV-10 significantly increased IFN-γ secretion upon stimulation with HBsAg-positive hepatoma cells. HBV-1, which is a longer peptide capable of processing by the immunoproteasome into multiple antigenic peptides with variable affinity to diverse alleles of the MHC,56 induced a strong response on its own and that was further boosted by treatment with PV-10. On the other hand, PV-10 also conferred responsiveness to HBV-2, which is a short peptide that individually exhibited reduced response, possibly due to weak binding affinity to the MHC allele in these specific healthy donor T-cells.57 A limitation of this study is the lack of validation in PBMCs from documented HB vaccine non-responders, who, despite being immunocompetent, represent a clinically important cohort for confirming the translational potential of PV-10. Importantly, PV-10 has been well characterized in preclinical and clinical studies, consistently demonstrating minimal systemic toxicity and a favorable safety profile.58–60 Animal studies have confirmed its safety across a range of doses with negligible off-target effects, while Phase I and II clinical trials in melanoma, neuroendocrine tumors, and other cancers reported that PV-10 was generally well tolerated, with adverse events largely limited to mild, localized injection-site reactions. These prior data provide strong evidence that PV-10 can be safely repurposed as an immune adjuvant. In conclusion, given that a subset of immunocompetent individuals fails to mount sufficient responses to current vaccines, this targeted enhancement of the immune response underscores the potential of PV-10 as a novel immune adjuvant through its unique modulation of the STING pathway for HB and other antiviral and anticancer vaccines.
