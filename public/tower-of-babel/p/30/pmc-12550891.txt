Introduction

Pulmonary tumour thrombotic microangiopathy (PTTM) is a rare but potentially fatal complication observed in patients with certain types of cancer, consisting on embolization of tumour cells into the pulmonary capillary beds, contributing to an increase in pulmonary vascular resistance. Most cases described in literature develop progressive pulmonary hypertension and right ventricular (RV) failure. Less frequently, this process is more acute.

We present the case of a patient with acute RV acute pressure overload following implantation of transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) and a suspected localized urothelial tumour.

Case presentation

We present the case of an 82-year-old male with medical history of hypertension, dyslipidaemia, and type 2 diabetes mellitus treated with insulin. The patient reported macroscopic haematuria lasting several months, associated with iron-deficiency anaemia, for which he was awaiting transurethral resection due to suspected urothelial tumour. Cardiologically, he had degenerative aortic valve disease resulting in a severe aortic stenosis, which had remained asymptomatic until hospital admission.

The patient was admitted following a witnessed, resuscitated sudden cardiac arrest caused by a shockable rhythm, which required advanced cardiopulmonary resuscitation manoeuvres, including orotracheal intubation and two shocks with an automated external defibrillator. Time to return of spontaneous circulation was 8 min. The subsequent electrocardiogram showed no signs of acute myocardial ischaemia (Figure 1). Upon admission to the Intensive Care Unit, the patient required low-dose norepinephrine infusion for the first 48 h due to post-cardiac arrest syndrome. Patient evolution was satisfactory with extubation within the first 24 h and was transferred to the Cardiology Step-Down Unit after 72 h, and later to the Cardiology Ward.

Initial electrocardiogram showing sinus rhythm at 90 b.p.m. with normal PR and QRS interval. Lateral leads ST rectification due to left ventricular overload.

During hospitalization, a transthoracic echocardiogram revealed critical aortic valve stenosis (mean gradient of 89 mmHg, Vmax of 5.6 m/s, AVA 0.46 cm², indexed AVA 0.22 cm²/m²), with preserved biventricular function and no other significant valvular disease. Coronary angiography showed no significant coronary stenoses. Given the presence of critical aortic stenosis and the absence of other heart diseases, the cardiac arrest was assumed to be secondary to pressure overload and ischaemia related to his valvulopathy and workup for TAVI was initiated.

As part of the pre-operative evaluation, a thoracoabdominal computed tomography (CT) scan was performed to assess access routes for the procedure. The CT scan reported thickening of the posterolateral wall of the urinary bladder, suggestive of an infiltrative malignancy, but no signs of distant metastatic dissemination were observed.

After consultation with the Urology Department, it was agreed to prioritize treatment of the valvulopathy before completing the evaluation of the suspected bladder malignancy. Aortic valvuloplasty was decided as a bridging therapy. Following valvuloplasty, a slight reduction in the mean aortic gradient (70 mmHg) was observed, so finally, a 29 mm Evolut Pro + TAVI was successfully implanted via the transfemoral route without complications. An echocardiogram after TAVI implantation showed a normal function of the prosthesis with preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and a non-dilated, normocontractile RV.

Patient remained hospitalized due to myopathy after prolonged hospitalization. Four days after TAVI implantation, the patient presented sudden hypotension, desaturation, and tachypnoea without chest pain, requiring treatment with fluid therapy, initiation of norepinephrine and dobutamine, and oxygen therapy.

The electrocardiogram showed signs of right ventricular strain (S1Q3T3 pattern) and a new-onset right bundle branch block (Figure 2). Arterial blood gas analysis revealed a pH of 7.15, pCO2 of 30 mmHg, bicarbonate of 17 mEq/L, and lactate of 6 mmol/L. Urgent blood tests showed a haemoglobin level of 90 g/L (similar to previous values) and slightly elevated high-sensitivity troponin T of 30 ng/L (upper limit of normal: 13 ng/L).

Electrocardiogram shows sinus rhythm at 90 b.p.m. with normal PR interval. Newly developed right bundle branch block, with 2 mm ST elevation in lead III. S1Q3T3 pattern.

Bedside echocardiography revealed a dilated RV with signs of pressure overload and McConnell’s sign (Figure 3). The patient had not received prophylactic heparin due to haematuria and could not tolerate pneumatic compression devices for deep vein thrombosis prophylaxis. An urgent thoracic CT angiography ruled out the clinical suspicion of pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE). Another possibility was a delayed coronary obstruction following TAVI implantation, but the patient had no chest pain and echocardiogram showed normal LVEF.

Echocardiogram shows a dilated right ventricle with systolic flattening of the interventricular septum and akinesia of the right ventricular lateral wall, with preserved contractility of the apex (McConnell sign).

The patient’s clinical condition deteriorated, with persistent signs of shock despite inotropic support (SCAI-D), developing a cardiorespiratory arrest secondary to electromechanical dissociation that did not respond to advanced life support measures, resulting in patient death. Given the absence of a clear cause for the acute RV dysfunction, a clinical autopsy was requested.

Post-mortem findings from the clinical autopsy showed a normally positioned TAVI with patent coronary arteries. Additionally, an invasive urothelial carcinoma was diagnosed at stage pT2b pM1b, with tumour-associated microangiopathic thrombosis in the pulmonary and hepatic vasculature (Figure 4). The cause of death was attributed to microangiopathic involvement, leading to obstructive shock due to acute RV pressure overload.

Histological section stained with haematoxylin–eosin showing pulmonary parenchyma with massive invasion of venous vascular, angiolymphatic, and capillary structures by tumour thrombi (black arrows).

Discussion

Pulmonary tumour thrombotic microangiopathy is a rare but potentially fatal complication observed in patients with certain types of malignancy, particularly those with advanced solid tumours such as gastric, urothelial, and ovarian carcinoma.1,2 This condition involves the embolization of tumour cells into the pulmonary, hepatic, and renal capillary beds, where they adhere to the vascular endothelium, leading to microvascular alterations such as platelet activation, microthrombus formation, and endothelial dysfunction. The pulmonary consequence is an increase in pulmonary vascular resistance, contributing to the development of pulmonary hypertension, which may present gradually or, as in this case, acutely with a rapid and fatal progression.3–5

In patients with progressive evolution, the main differential diagnosis is with chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension, both presenting with progressive dyspnoea and cough as they main symptoms. Chest CT scan and ventilation-perfusion scans show distinctive features in these entities. Right heart catheterization can help both in diagnosis (by obtaining samples for cytology) and in assessing the patient’s haemodynamic status and ultimately guiding the use of pulmonary hypertension-targeted therapy. FDG-PET has also been described as tool for an ante-mortem diagnosis, but unfortunately in many cases, the definitive diagnosis is established on autopsies.

The treatment in the few cases diagnosed ante-mortem is based on improving pulmonary pressures and cardiac output with pulmonary vasodilators in addition to treatment of the malignancy. Other agents such as imatinib (a tyrosine kinase inhibitor) or bevacizumab (a vascular endothelial growth factor receptor Inhibitor) have also been used with promising results,6,7 but at the moment, there is no high-quality evidence or specific guidelines regarding the optimal management of the disease.

In other patients such in our case, PTTM can lead to an abrupt respiratory failure and shock, very similar to PTE. This distinction makes the diagnosis particularly challenging in clinical practice, and most patients are initially misdiagnosed with PTE. Regarding diagnosis, conventional imaging techniques, such as CT or magnetic resonance imaging, may not be enough to detect tumour-related microembolization, often leading to post-mortem diagnosis. There is no specific treatment for acutely onset PTTM due to its rarity and delayed diagnosis. Most patients receive palliative care aimed at managing complications of pulmonary hypertension and RV failure.

Although this condition is thought to be very rare, PTTM is probably underdiagnosed due to the difficulty of an ante-mortem diagnosis and the assumption that symptoms are related to malignancy.

Conclusions

This case is particularly relevant as it illustrates a rare but important cause in the differential diagnosis of acute pressure overload in the RV, especially in patients with known malignancy. Early diagnosis of PTTM could allow for more appropriate intervention and symptom management. Unfortunately, autopsy remains the most common tool for identifying this severe complication.
