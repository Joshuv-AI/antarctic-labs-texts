Psychometric methods and signal detection theory uncover subtle differences in the perception of tone contrasts in speakers of Kam (Dong)
Authors: Dan Dediu, Luchang Wang, Patrick C. M. Wong, Manxiang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With few exceptions, modern psychometric concepts and methods are not part of the “standard” toolkit used to analyze linguistic data. Data from Kam (or Dong), an under-studied language of China with a very complex tone system, is used to illustrate how a psychometric approach can help investigate inter-individual differences in the perception of tone contrasts in an AX task, and the factors that may influence them, including working memory, age, gender, and years of formal education. We show that these methods uncover an unexpected structure of the responses, as some items designed as “different” based on the language’s phonology were instead perceived as “same”, prompting a recoding of these items in line with their perception. The subsequent use of % correct responses, Signal Detection Theory, multiple regression, mediation, and path analysis, found a complex network of influences on the AX task: there are direct effects of age, education and working memory, but only an indirect effect of gender. Therefore, even such a “simple” AX task can benefit from this approach, and we argue, in this primarily methodological paper which also includes directly usable extensive computer code in R, that modern psychometrics should be more widely used in linguistics.

Introduction

About half of the world’s languages are tone languages1, but, despite its prevalence, linguistic (including lexical) tone is far from being well understood. It is clear that the cross-linguistic distribution of tone is not random, but the factors influencing this are not entirely clear: inheritance from a common ancestor and contact probably play major roles2,3, but other factors, such as climate4,5 and even genetics6,7, may matter as well. Likewise, the neuro-cognitive mechanisms and inter-individual differences in tone perception, and their interaction with the structure of the phonological system still remain to be satisfactorily described8,9. While numerous studies have examined tone perception by native speakers, including of Sinitic languages spoken in China (e.g., Mandarin Chinese10,11 and Cantonese7,12), very few have investigated non-Sinitic languages in China.

Many acoustic cues have been identified for the perception of lexical tones across languages such as Cantonese, Hmong, Thai, and Yoruba; these include pitch height, the direction of pitch change (rising, falling or level), the slope of pitch contour, the source spectrum slopes, and duration13–17. However, to our knowledge, no study has been conducted on the perception of lexical tones in Kam. Due to differences in the tonal system, listeners across languages rely on individual perceptual cues to different degrees in tone perception. For instance, the classic study of Gandour and Harshman16 investigated how the language background influenced listeners’ use of different perceptual cues in tone perception, focusing on a comparison between speakers of Thai and Yoruba. It found that Thai speakers relied more on the cues of duration and contour slope than Yoruba speakers. Gandour15 compared speakers of Thai and several Chinese languages, including Mandarin, Cantonese, and Taiwanese, for their perception of linguistic tones. It reported that speakers of all these Chinese languages relied less on the direction of pitch change in discriminating tones compared to Thai speakers. Adult speakers often show ceiling-like performance in perceiving lexical tones in their native language; however, when encountering non-salient tone contrasts, their performance can decline12,18. In Ciocca and Lui’s study18, participants were presented with objects featuring minimal pairs of tone contrasts and were expected to select the object corresponding to the speech stimuli they heard. For most tone contrasts, native adult Cantonese speakers achieved nearly 100% accuracy in their responses, but for the high rising vs. low rising tone contrast, they attained an accuracy rate of only approximately 80%. With a similar tone identification task, Lee et al.12 found that adult Cantonese speakers showed ceiling-like performance for all tone pairs except the high rising vs. low rising tone contrast and the mid level vs. low level tone contrast. Musical experience has been identified as an important factor influencing individuals’ tone perception abilities. Using the AX or ABX discrimination task, many studies have demonstrated that musical training offers an advantage in tone perception among non-tone language speakers19–21. Moreover, it was recently shown22 that the advantage of musical training could extend to speakers of a tone language in the perception of lexical tones in their native language, Cantonese: participants with professional musical training outperformed those without music experience in the discrimination and identification of the non-salient Cantonese tone pairs.

Here, we focus on Kam (or Dong; Glottolog sout2741)23,24, an under-studied language spoken in the Sanjiang county, China, characterized by a very complex tone system with 10 phonemic (i.e., contrastive) tones realized as 15 phonetic tones (and affected by various tone sandhi rules and language contact-induced ongoing changes). Focusing on Kam has broader relevance for three main reasons: first, its tonal system is arguably the most complex in the Tai-Kaidai language family. Second, the Kam people are famous for their folk songs, and it might well be that there are links between this tonal and musical complexity. Finally, it is generally important to dedicate research to understudied languages in general, given the rapidly dwindling linguistic diversity25. Donohue and Wu24 study the effect of aspiration, which occurs with a subset of tones and phonologizes into a standard effect on F0, and propose that there are certain tone-segment co-occurrence restrictions. For example, tones 452, 335, 332, 45 and 324 occur in syllables with aspirated initial consonants, while tones 52, 452, 23, 35, 335, 212, 32, 332 and 33 occur in unchecked syllables, and tones 5, 45, 3, 34, 324 and 21 appear in checked syllables. Tones 5 and 3 occur in checked syllables with a non-aspirated onset, whereas tones 45 and 324 appear in checked syllables with aspirated onsets. However, tone 21 differs from the other stopped tones (i.e., tones that appear in checked syllables) in that it appears in checked syllables with long vowels and a non-aspirated onset, such as paːk21 ’white’, and maːk21 ’soil’. Thus, the distribution of tone in Sanjiang Kam is subject to certain restrictions, one being the aspiration of the initial consonants.

Given the complexity of the Kam tone system, we wondered (i) how do its native speakers perceive the contrastive tones, (ii) how do the native speakers’ perceptions differ, (iii) whether there are any systematic differences between pairs of tones, and (iv) what factors might explain these differences. While a previous study26 has found that Kam speakers’ pitch perception performed better than Mandarin speakers, otherwise little is known about how speakers of Kam perceive and process their native language’s complex tone system. Kam’s large inventory of tones creates the opportunity for confusion and contains tones of different shapes and levels that a simpler tonal system may not have, therefore providing a very interesting opportunity to study the factors that affect tone perception in a most comprehensive fashion.

We designed a simple AX task that contrasts pairs of phonological tones, and we collected data from almost 500 speakers of Kam from two main locations covering a wide range of relevant socio-linguistic variables, including the participant’s gender and age, given their general influence on many aspects of language production and perception. The AX task has several advantages, including a relatively low memory load compared to tasks with more stimuli27, and it can be used to measure discrimination accuracy across different phonetic categories, such as between syllables based on voice onset time (VOT)28. However, the AX task is susceptible to response biases in the sense that the participants may favor one response type (e.g., “same”) when acoustic differences are minimal, which can skew the results27–29. To better understand the nature and distribution of the variation between individuals and items, we introduce here a three-pronged methodological approach: the widely-used (arguably a-theoretical) percent of correct responses, the Signal Detection Theory (SDT)-derived30 sensitivity and bias, and a psychometric31–33 approach based on classical test theory and Item Response Theory (IRT)34,35. These three approaches have very different assumptions and aim to capture quite different aspects of performance and of its between-participant (and possibly between-item) variation. We show here that they allow us to capture subtle (but potentially crucial) aspects of tone perception that would be missed were we to just use the percent of correct responses. We therefore urge the field to diversify its methodological toolkit when it comes to analyzing inter-individual differences even in such apparently simple tasks.

Our main findings are first, that, while the AX task is probably too easy, with most participants performing at, or very close to, the ceiling, the psychometric methods described here help detect a subset of “different” items (which we call “weird”) that are consistently heard as “same” by our participants. Second, when recoding the “weird” items as “same” and analyzing the results using appropriate methods, we do capture a complex network of inter-individual and possibly sociolinguistic aspects of variation in the participants’ performance on this AX task.

Therefore, our paper has two main aims: first, it introduces a set of concepts and methods (with actual R code and pointers to the wider literature), methods that have not been widely used to analyze linguistic data so far but which, we believe, should be part of the practitioners’ toolkit; therefore, we describe them quite in detail in the Sect. Methods, discussing their assumptions and intended application, and providing references to the primary literature, aiming at a public that may not be familiar with them. Second, we use actual data from an understudied language with a complex tone system in order to not only illustrate how these methods work, what advantages and disadvantages they have, but also to, in the process, shed light on the perception of tone in Kam by its speakers. We hope that our descriptions, directly usable R code applied on a linguistic dataset, and references to the primary literature, will encourage more language scientists to apply them to their own datasets and questions, and to rethink the use of common measurement and analysis procedures in our (and related) fields. Finally, it is important to note that the goal of this paper is not to study a group of individuals at a particular location in China, but to study tone perception by native speakers of a complex tone language, which are abundantly available in Southwestern China.

The paper is structured as follows: we describe the participants and the experimental tasks (Sect. Data), followed by a detailed description of the methods (Sect. Methods), continuing with their application to the Kam AX tone task (Sect. Results), and ending with a Discussion.

The participants and the language

We collected data from participants at several locations in Southwestern China, who are speakers of a Southern dialect of Kam23 (probably Glottolog sout2741), characterized by a very complex tone system23,24. WALS assigns a “Complex tone system” to this variety (“Chapter 13A” for Language Dong (Southern)), but neither PHOIBLE nor LAPSyD seem to contain any information about it. It is important to note that the Dong people do not use a writing system based on the Chinese characters, but a Pinyin Dong system created specifically for this language in 1956. This writing system is widely used in the Dong areas, especially by Dong scholars, but also for the mastery and transmission of Dong folk songs, Dong opera, and Dong storytelling. Moreover, even Dong people with low exposure to formal education, including, unfortunately, many Dong women, have little knowledge of Chinese characters, but are exposed to Pinyin Dong.

The working memory task

The working memory task consists of 15 trials fixed across participants, each trial showing a sequence of between 3 and 7 colored rectangles, each color appearing only once per trial, and the participant has to reproduce the colors in the correct order. We decided to use colors rather than linguistic stimuli as we wanted a control task that is clearly different from the experimental task and that could be easily administered automatically. If we represent here, for brevity purposes, the colors by the first letter of their English names, with CAPITALS used for disambiguation, [b]lue, [B]lack, [g]reen, [G]ray, [p]urple, [r]ed and [y]ellow, the 15 trials are, in order: rgb, Bpy, GgB, rbpG, yBgb, grBy, bBGyr, Gpygb, BrbGg, gBpbGy, ypBrgG, Gpbrgy, rgbBpyG, bGBgrpy & yrbgGB (Fig. S2). The score of a trial represents the number of colors reproduced in the correct position.

The tone task

This is an AX task in which the participant is presented, in a given trial, with a pair of syllables that may differ only in tone, and has to decide if the two syllables are the “same” or “different”. For a given tone pair or item (in general, we denote tones with superscript small letters a and b), there are two syllables with different segmental content (denoted using capital letters A and B), resulting in the following four (syllable + tone) combinations: Aa, Ab, Ba and Bb. With these, we have the following possible items: 4 “same”: AaAa, AbAb, BaBa and BbBb (the tones and segmental content of the two syllables are the same), and 4 “different”: AaAb, AbAa, BaBb, BbBa (the tone order differs), resulting in 4 pair \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} order items.

Each item was repeated twice and the response to a given trial was scored as “correct” (if the tones were different and the response was “different”, or if the tones were the same and the response was “same”), and as “incorrect” otherwise. Each participant was presented with a random (unique) order of the items. Some of the possible items were not included, as the participants performed at ceiling during the initial pilot studies.

In the test session there were 208 trials in total, while in the training session we had 6 trials. The interstimulus interval (ISI) between the two sounds in one trial was 500 ms, but there was no fixed ISI between two trials as the start of a trial was triggered by the participants’ own response. Therefore, the total experiment duration was not fixed, depending on how quickly the participant responded. For each tone pair, the position of each tone in the sequence is counter-balanced: for example, the tone pair c-h (see below for details of notations), in half of the trials, tone c comes before tone h; in the other half, tone h comes before tone c. Thus, each tone pair has 8 trials (4 “different” trials and 4 “same” trials), presented in a randomized order, with the carrier syllables being counter-balanced.

We used the 9 (out of 10) phonological tones in the language that occur in unchecked syllables, down from all the 15 possible phonetic allotones, as those that occur in checked syllables are very difficult to distinguish24. With these, the “same” items are given in Table 1, while the “different” items are in Table 2. The items sem35:sem335 (Ist) and sɐm35:sɐm335 (Hst) are considered as “difficult” by the task designers (MW and LW); more generally, tone pairs 35:335 and 32:332 are difficult to distinguish (see also Fig. 1, especially panels C and D).

In Sanjiang Kam the distribution of tones is subject to certain restrictions, one being the aspiration of the initial consonants. For example, the tones 1’ (452), 3’ (335) and 5’ (332) occur in syllables with aspirated initial consonants, and tones 1, 1’, 2, 3, 3’, 4, 5, 5’ and 6 occur in unchecked syllables (please note that the apostrophe ’ represents here a tone occurring on a syllable with an initial aspirated consonant such as /kh/ and /th/). We used naturally recorded syllables with their duration normalized at 550ms and the intensity normalized at 65 dB. The analysis of the tone task is described in the Sect. Results.

Methods

We have two related main goals here: (i) to understand the properties of the AX tone task (i.e., what it measures and what its structure is), so that (ii) we can describe and explain any inter-individual differences in its performance. However, it is important to point out that these two goals are intimately related, in that the properties of the task may tap into, and may reveal patterns of inter-individual variation, while such patterns may help, in turn, understand the AX task itself. In consequence, we draw heavily on various psychometric concepts and techniques, in particular on Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA)37, Item Response Theory (IRT)35,38 and Mokken Scaling Analysis (MSA)39 and associated techniques, as well as on dimensionality reduction techniques such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA)36,40. Given the methodological nature of the paper, we describe these methods in relative detail, aiming these descriptions to their potential users in linguistics. All these methods are implemented in R41. Please see the Analysis Report for full details, including the actual R code, the exact version of, and references to R and the packages used. The main document is a self-contained HTML file with hyperlinks, and accessing various figures and tables can be done by simply clicking on the corresponding label in the list following immediately the TOC at the beginning of that document.

Percent correct responses (pcr)

Here, the % of correct responses (shortened to pcr) was computed as the number of “correct” responses divided by the total number of responses multiplied by 100 (this is equivalent with the proportion of correct responses). pcr assumes that it is a good approximation of a latent “propensity” of a given participant to hear (and appropriately respond to) the stimuli, especially as the number of stimuli and/or trials increases. Moreover, it is assumed that this “propensity” is a (relatively) stable characteristic of the participant, and that individual stimuli are essentially “the same” with respect to the task (and hypothesis), a finite sample from a (potentially infinite) universe of stimuli with certain relevant properties or confounds (here, presentation order, segments and tones). We argue that one should not use pcr “by default”, without checking that its assumptions are met to a reasonable degree, both theoretically and for the data at hand. Here, we use pcr to quantify the alignment of a participant’s actual responses to the expected responses for each item, i.e. as “same” or “different”, and we further used this estimate as the primary outcome in our models.

Signal detection theory (SDT)

An approach to modeling a participant’s responses to a binary decision task is to see it as fundamentally about detecting a signal from a noisy channel by a detector that is potentially biased. Signal Detection Theory (SDT)30, frequently used in psychophysics but also in linguistics42–44 quantifies the relationship between the presence or absence of the signal in the data, and the decision made by the detector (signal detected or not). For an AX task we have the following four possibilities: when the signal is indeed present (i.e., A \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ne$$\end{document} X), a response of “different” is called a “hit” (H) while a response of “same” is a “miss” (M), and when there’s no signal (A \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$=$$\end{document} X), a response of “different” is a “false alarm” (FA) while a response of “same” is a “correct rejection” (CR). Obviously, “hits” and “correct rejections” are correct decisions, while the other two are incorrect, but in subtly different ways. Let us define the hit rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h = \frac{\#H}{\#S}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\#H$$\end{document} is the number of “hits” and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\#S$$\end{document} the number of signals \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\#H + \#M$$\end{document}, and the false alarm rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$fa = \frac{\#FA}{\#NS}$$\end{document} with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\#NS$$\end{document} the number of non-signals \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\#FA + \#CR$$\end{document}. In SDT, the only relevant measures are h and fa because the relationship between them captures, on one hand, the capacity of the detector to discriminate between noise and signal and, on the other hand, any directional bias the detector might have to simply report detecting a signal or not. In “classical” SDT, the discrimination d’ \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$= z(h) - z(fa)$$\end{document}, where z(.) is the inverse of the cumulative density function (cdf) of the normal distribution, capturing the idea that both the signals and the non-signals are random variables following normal distributions, and that the capacity to correctly detect the signals depends on how different these distributions are44. While theoretically d’ can take any real value, in practice \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\le$$\end{document} 0.0 represent no sensitivity, with higher values representing higher sensitivity (usually an upper limit of about 3.0 is used, but it can go higher). Please contrast this with the proportion of correct responses, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{\#H + \#CR}{n}$$\end{document} where n is the total number of responses, which focuses just on the “hits” and “correct rejections”.

However, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d'$$\end{document} neglects any bias the participant might have, namely any preferences for answering “same” or “different”. Various measures of this bias exist, the best known being \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} and c: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} is defined as the ratio of the normal density functions at the selection criterion (the threshold between the two types of responses) of the z(h) and z(fa), with an unbiased observer having a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta \approx 1.0$$\end{document}, while if the bias to report detection (i.e., to say “different”, or being “liberal”) increases (resulting in a higher h and fa), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta \rightarrow 0.0$$\end{document}, with the opposite bias (i.e., to say “same”, or being “conservative”) being represented by values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta > 1.0$$\end{document} (without a theoretical upper limit). The criterion location, c, tries to address some of the issues with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}, and is defined as the (negative of the) number of standard deviations from the midpoint between z(h) and z(fa) distributions, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c = -\frac{z(h) + z(fa)}{2}$$\end{document}, which captures the distance between the selection criterion and the midpoint of these distributions; c is symmetric around 0.0 (unbiased), ranging from negative values (biased towards “different” responses, aka “liberal”), to positive values (biased towards “same” responses, aka “conservative”). Non-parametric estimates of discrimination and bias exist as well45,46, with a discrimination \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A'$$\end{document} near 0.5 representing chance and closer to 1.0 good detection, and the bias \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B''_D$$\end{document} varying between -1 (biased towards “different” responses, “liberal”) and 1 (biased towards “same” responses, “conservative”), with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B''_D = 0$$\end{document} representing an unbiased observer. See Table S1 for examples of response patterns on an AX task which are not distinguished by the percent correct responses but are by SDT-derived discrimination and bias. Here, we use \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d'$$\end{document} and c to quantify a participant’s capacity to discriminate between pairs of stimuli that are, by design, different, from those that are identical, while also estimating any bias the participant might have, and we further used \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d'$$\end{document} as the primary outcome in our models.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)36 is a well-known dimensionality reduction technique that arguably is not based on an explicit measurement theory. It allows the “recoding” of a multidimensional dataset into an equivalent dataset described by the same number n of new variables, called Principal Components and denoted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$PC_{1}$$\end{document} ... \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$PC_{n}$$\end{document}, such that the new variables are not linearly correlated and are ordered by the amount of variation they explain. They are linear combinations of the original variables with weights chosen such that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$PC_{1}$$\end{document} explains the maximum possible amount of variance in the dataset, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$PC_{2}$$\end{document}, explains the maximum possible amount of remaining variance, etc.

While for the working memory task we used the actual score for each item (i.e., the number of colors reproduced in the correct position), for the tone task we recoded the responses as 0 for “incorrect” and 1 for “correct”. Importantly, the resulting PCs do not necessarily reflect any “deep” latent structure in the data, but are simply the result of “compressing” the dataset such that most of the variance is captured by fewer dimensions than the original number of items. Therefore, there is information loss the fewer PCs are used and the more evenly spread the variance explained is between them. Thus, it is in principle risky to interpret the number of PCs and their explained variance, or to attribute them “meaning” based on the weights, e.g. as reflecting the cognitive mechanisms giving rise to the individual scores. Nevertheless, they are usually informative, especially when coupled with other, more theoretically-grounded approaches, and when the dataset is large and representative. Here, we use PCA to guide our understanding of the structure of the working memory and AX tasks in terms of shared variation between their component items.

Factor Analysis (FA), exploratory and confirmatory

Factor Analysis (FA)37,47 is based on an explicit conception of measurement, of the structure of human performance, and of inter-individual variation31–33, namely that the measured (or observed) variables (or indicators), capture only imperfectly the unmeasured (and possibly unmeasurable) latent (or underlying) variables (or factors). More precisely, each indicator may load (linearly) on one (or more) latents, allowing also for the explicit inclusion of measurement errors: the indicators are linear combinations of (some of) the latents, plus an error term; likewise, each latent loads (also linearly) on one or more indicators, also with measurement error. In more complex Structural Equation Models (SEM)37,48, some latents may load on other latents, and various types of relationships, either symmetric (correlations) or asymmetric (regressions) between both types of variables can be included. A particular case of SEM models is represented by path models37, where there are no latent variables and which, therefore, represent only relations between the measured variables. Importantly, while some such models may be intended to capture causal relationships, their causal interpretation is far from simple and uncontroversial (especially for observational data), and usually requires the application of various causal modeling approaches49–51.

A distinction is usually made between exploratory (EFA) and confirmatory (CFA) FA: while the latter assumes an a priori structural model that is being tested through comparison with the data, the first does not and, just like other exploratory methods, queries the data about the possible existence of an FA model that might fit it. Testing a CFA model, just as for SEM in general, usually involves a goodness-of-fit test (gof) that tries to reject the hypothesis that the a priori model could have generated the data; therefore, a significant gof \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^{2}$$\end{document} test means that the data significantly diverges from the models’ predictions and that the model can be rejected. However, this binary significance-based approach has downsides, and multiple fit measures exist, that quantify, in a continuous manner, how well the data fits the model in a certain sense. For example, the Comparative Fit Index (CFI) measures how much better the a priori model fits the data relative to an independent model, and should be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document} 0.95 for a good fit, while the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) should be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\le$$\end{document} 0.0552. Here, we combine the two approaches, by checking the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^{2}$$\end{document} gof test, also looking at CFI, TLI (the Tucker Lewis index, useful for small samples), RFI (the Relative Fit Index) and RMSEA, arriving at a synthetic evaluation of the model’s fit to the data.

EFA is entirely data-driven and we are here primarily interested in the most probable number of latent factors and in their loadings. We first test if the data is appropriate for EFA34 using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) Measure of Sampling Adequacy (MSA)53 which should be close to 1 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document} 0.6, the Bartlett’s test54, which should be significant, and the correlation matrix of the indicators should be invertible. Then we estimate the most likely number of latents34 through a comparison with random matrices of the same dimension as the actual data matrix, the Very Simple Structure (VSS) criterion, complexity, and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)34,55. We then estimate the EFA model with this number of factors (using the “promax” rotation, allowing the factors to be correlated), obtaining not only the percent of variance explained by the model but also performing a formal \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^{2}$$\end{document} gof test.

Here, we first perform EFA (including the tests of appropriateness), followed by CFA with the given number of latent factors and their suggested structure. We use EFA first, followed by CFA to understand the structure of the working memory and AX tasks in terms of a possible latent structure with regard to their component items, and, for the AX task, also in the identification of the “weird” items (see Sect. The unexpected structure of the tone task for details). Please note that, while usually CFA is used to test a pre-existing structural model, we use it here only to better understand the findings from the other approaches, in particular PCA and EFA, treating it effectively as another exploratory technique. We do not further use the resulting loadings, but only the finding that the working memory task has a uni-factorial structure, and to “flip” the “weird” items in the AX task. This approach remains therefore fully exploratory and avoids any “double dipping”.

Non-parametric IRT and Mokken scaling analysis

Item Response Theory (IRT)35 is a model of measurement that also assumes an unmeasured latent that is approximated by the observed items, but, unlike FA and SEM, for each item it models a specific probability depending (through what is called the item response function or IRF) on the item’s own “difficulty” (irrespective of the participant) as well as on the participant’s own “ability” (irrespective of the item). The items and the participants are placed on the latents’ range (i.e., they are ordered in terms of their “difficulty” and their “ability”, respectively), the midpoint of this range being where a participant of average “ability” has a 50% chance of correctly solving an item of average “difficulty”. The main advantages of IRT over FA is that it simultaneously models varying latent item “difficulties” and participant “abilities” from observed participant responses, accounting for various types of errors and noise. There are several types of IRT models depending on the type of item (binary, ordinal, continuous) and the degrees of freedom (parameters) modeled (e.g., how guessing is accounted for)34,38. However, IRT makes the strong assumption that the latent is continuous and has specific requirements about the sampled responses34, issues which are addressed by non-parametric IRT56 and Mokken Scaling Analysis (MSA)39 in particular. There are several preconditions that need checking34, namely unidimensionality (the items can be consistently ordered on a single latent such that the “easier” items are correctly solved with a higher probability than “difficult” items), local independence (the items are independent conditional on the latent), and monotonicity (participants with higher “ability” should have a higher probability of solving an item that those with a lower “ability”). There are various approaches for testing these assumptions, including looking at the distribution of the Guttman errors57,58 (cases where a participant solves correctly a “difficult” item but not an “easy” one), estimate the coefficients of homogeneity (or scalability), H, for each item individually and for the whole set of items59), and applying the Automatic Item Selection Procedure (aisp) to partition the set into subsets of items that form unidimensional subscales at increasing homogeneity levels59. There are more steps for a complete Mokken/IRT analysis34,59, but, as described in Sect. Results, for our data these further steps may not be applicable. Here, we use IRT/MSA to have a deeper understanding of the AX task seen as composed of a set of items that may form one (or more) (sub)scale(s), each item having its specific properties in what concerns the participants’ responses (“difficulty”) and its own contribution to the overall (sub)scale(s); in particular, we use them to identify and understand the behavior of the subset of “weird” items (see Sect. The unexpected structure of the tone task for details).

Regression, mediation and path models

All the statistical analyses reported here were performed in the frequentist paradigm. We tested the clustering by family using mixed-effects models and the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC), but this information was missing for most participants. As there are only two main locations (A and B), we modeled it as a fixed effect, using contrast coding with A as the baseline. Most measures have a single value per participant, so we modeled them as fixed effects: age is numeric, gender is binary (contrast coded with females as the baseline), years of music and years of education are counts, wm_norm is normalized \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\in [0.0, 1.0]$$\end{document}, while the various results of the PCA, FA and SDT for the tone task are numeric; for some techniques we converted the binary variables gender and location to numeric such that the baseline is coded as 0 and the contrasting level as 1. When playing the role of a dependent (predicted) or mediator variable, for the binary variables we use logistic regression, for the unbounded numeric ones we use linear regression, for the count variables we use Poisson regression, and for the bounded variables we use Beta regression.

The tone task can be seen, for a given participant, as a set of individual binary items (“correct” vs “incorrect”) or as a single numeric measure of performance (pcr or any of the measures derived from PCA, FA and SDT). In the first case, the appropriate model is a mixed-effects logistic regression with participant and item as crossed random effects, while for the others, a non-random-effect model of the appropriate type (linear, Poisson, Beta...) would be needed. In particular, we compared (a) the mixed-effects logistic regression of the binary item responses with (b) the non-mixed-effects Beta regression of pcr, and we found that both approaches agree not only in the point estimates but also in their standard errors and p-values, that method (a) is slower and may have convergence issues for complex models, that (b) is more similar with using other estimates of performance derived from PCA, FA and SDT, and, finally, that (b) is easier to use in mediation and path models that include other variables that have a single value per participant: therefore, we will use (b) as the main approach, with (a) as a sanity check.

While multiple regression is widely-used for modeling the simultaneous influence of several predictors on a dependent variable (DV), it has major shortcomings, especially when the intention is to test a more refined model. Therefore, we also use mediation analysis60 to explicitly test models where one (or more) treatment variable(s) may affect the DV, called outcome (the total effect) both directly (through a direct effect) and indirectly (the indirect effect), the latter mediated through one (or more) mediator variable(s). Focusing on the simplest case of a single treatment, T, influencing the outcome O through a single mediator M, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T \xrightarrow {\nearrow M \searrow } O$$\end{document}, we have the direct effect \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T \rightarrow O$$\end{document} and the indirect effect composed of two influences, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T \rightarrow M \rightarrow O$$\end{document}, each arrow (representing regressions) with its own path coefficient (regression slopes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}, which may or may not be significant). However, even mediation is limited in the kind of relationships it can model, so we also test more complex path models37 where multiple observed variables are connected through directed edges (representing regressions, with the path coefficients being the slopes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}) or bidirectional edges (representing correlations, with path coefficients being Pearson’s r). While causal interpretations have to be done carefully for any statistical technique49,50, mediation and path models are better suited in the sense that we can superpose a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) on them, allowing, for example, to test the existence of the direct effect in a mediation model, using d-separation61.

Here, we use multiple regression as a first approach to modeling the influence of the potential independent variables (age, gender, years of formal education, location, and working memory) on the Results

Due to space constraints we present here a summary of the results, with the full details available in the Analysis Report.

The unexpected structure of the tone task

A preliminary analysis of the tone task revealed that a subset of the “different” items behaves not as the other “different” items but like the “same” items. This subset, denoted as W (from “weird”), is composed of the items Bhx, Bxh, Hst, Hts, Ist, Its, Kkl, Klk, Llp and Lpl. First, W have very low pcr compared to the other items, as shown in Table S2. Second, their tetrachoric correlations with the other items tend to be negative, and a heatmap and hierarchical clustering derived from these do set W apart as well (Fig. S5). Third, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$PC_1$$\end{document} of the PCA on all items, explaining \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20.1\%$$\end{document} of the variance, opposes most of W to most of the other items, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$PC_2$$\end{document} (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5.5\%$$\end{document}) opposes the “same” to the non-W “different” items (Fig. S6A and C and Table S3). Fourth, EFA (preliminary tests: Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.89 > 0.6$$\end{document}, Bartlett’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^2_{21528}=61167.2$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p=0$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$det(cor(data))=7.5\times 10^{-64} > 0$$\end{document}) finds that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FA_1$$\end{document} combines all the “same” items and the W items (with opposite signs), while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FA_2$$\end{document} comprises all the non-W “different” items (Fig. S6B and Table S3). Fifth, 73 participants out of 492 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$14.8\%$$\end{document}) have more than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(Q3 + 1.5 \times IQR) = 3184.8$$\end{document} Guttman errors, the complete item set has a homogeneity H (with 95%CI) of 0.20 [0.19, 0.22] significantly lower than the recommended threshold of 0.3 (suggesting that the scale is not homogeneous), and the homogeneity of the individual items is also low and generally lower than 0.3, ranging between -0.54 and 0.31, with mean 0.18 and median 0.22; consistently, the Automatic Item Selection Procedure (aisp) strongly suggests that the item set is not unidimensional (Table S4). Sixth, a k-means clustering analysis (Fig. S7) supports the same idea, in that the W items go with the “same” and not with the “different” ones. It is important to note that those items that contain a real word in the language are not over- nor under-represented among the “weird” items (Fisher’s exact test \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p = 0.64$$\end{document}), and that, in general, they do not seem to behave any different from the other items.

To test the hypothesis that the items in W are interpreted by the participants as “same” items, we created a new dataset (denoted as the “recoded” dataset) where all the items in W are transformed into “same” items by inverting their responses: an originally “correct” response is recoded as “incorrect” and vice-versa. Now (Table S5), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$pcr \in [47.4\%, 94.1\%]$$\end{document}, with mean 83.8% (median 87.4%) and sd 9.2% (IQR, 8.2%). The tetrachoric correlations, the heatmap, hierarchical clustering and mean correlations (Fig. S8) do not identify any worrisome grouping of items. PCA finds that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$PC_1$$\end{document} explains 20.1% of the variance and groups the vast majority of the items, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$PC_2$$\end{document} (5.5%) opposes the “same” together with the recoded W items versus the non-W “different” items (Fig. S9A and C). As before, FA is applicable, and the most likely number of factors now seems to be 2 (Fig. S9B), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FA_1$$\end{document} being composed now of all the “same” and the recoded W items, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FA_2$$\end{document} being composed of all the non-W “different” items. In fact, as expected, the PCA and EFA loadings of the non-”weird” items stays the same, while those of the “weird” items only change their sign. Therefore, it seems that now the main difference is between the (extended) “same” (which includes the recoded W as well) and the non-W “different” items, which supports our hypothesis. Nevertheless, MSA suggests that the conditions for a meaningful IRT are still far from ideal: the complete item set has an H of 0.22 [0.21, 0.24] \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$< 0.3$$\end{document}, few items have \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H \ge 0.3$$\end{document}, and aisp (Table S6) still suggests that the set is not unidimensional. Following ref34, we tried to reduce the set of items by keeping only the 2nd presentation and, for the “different” items, only one order of tones (the 1st in alphabetical order), which slightly improves H to 0.23 [0.210, 0.249]. We then iteratively removed 21 unscalable items (Mch1, Chv1, Kkl1, Mc1, Ip1, Dch1, Ecx1, Hst1, Llp1, Dcx1, Lpv1, Gx1, Jk1, Icp1, Lkl1, Ix1, Lp1, Mh1, Ist1, It1, and Iks1), and we were left with 45 items (Ak1, Akt1, At1, Bh1, Bhx1, Bs1, Bsv1, Bv1, Bx1, Ch1, Cv1, Dc1, Dh1, Dx1, Ec1, Ecl1, El1, Elx1, Ex1, Fs1, Fsv1, Fv1, Gt1, Gtx1, Hs1, Ht1, Ic1, Ik1, Ipv1, Ipx1, Is1, Iv1, Jkx1, Jx1, Kk1, Kl1, Lk1, Ll1, Lv1, Mcl1, Mhl1, Ml1, Nk1, Nkp1, and Np1) identified by aisp for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c \le 0.3$$\end{document}, comprising both “same” and “different” items, and which seem to (more or less) form a single scale (especially at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c = 0.3$$\end{document}) and show a much better H of 0.29 [0.27, 0.32] \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx 0.3$$\end{document} (Table S7). However, 22 items do not meet the local independence criterion and were further removed, leaving only 23 items with a subscale H of 0.34 [0.29, 0.39] \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$> 0.3$$\end{document}, and all the individual items with H close to or above 0.3, but all these items are “same” items. All these suggest that MSA may not be appropriate for further analyzing these data, but these techniques were, nevertheless, extremely useful in helping to detect the W items.

Tone task: datasets and performance measures

Given the above analyses, we treat the W items by performing three independent analyses using identical methods, as follows: (1) keep these items as they are (i.e., the “original” dataset), or (2) remove these items, resulting in a reduced dataset (“removed”), or (3) recode them as “same” items (“recoded” dataset). All three tone datasets have the same number of participants, but different number of items: 208 (“original”), 188 (“removed”), and 208 (“recoded”).

Likewise, we will summarize the participants’ performance as the pcr across all items (as suggested by the PCA and EFA analyses), as well as the SDT-derived sensitivity and bias across all items. pcr is highly correlated across the three datasets (Spearman’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho \ge 0.9$$\end{document}), suggesting that we can use any one of them. The SDT results are a bit more complex, not least because we have several possible measures of sensitivity and bias. The two sensitivity measures are very highly intercorrelated across datasets (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho \ge 0.93$$\end{document} for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d'$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho \ge 0.95$$\end{document} for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A'$$\end{document}), as well as between them (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.91 \le \rho \le 0.96$$\end{document}). Within each dataset, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} and c have high correlations of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho \approx 0.9$$\end{document}; between datasets, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} for “removed” seems to show the best intercorrelations with the other datasets (0.92 with “recoded” and 0.89 with “original”), while the correlation for “original” and “recoded” is 0.75. However, c shows much higher intercorrelations across datasets (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.82 \le \rho \le 0.95$$\end{document}). \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B''_D$$\end{document} for “original” is moderately correlated with the other two datasets (0.73 with “recoded” and 0.90 with “removed”), while these two correlate at 0.90. Within datasets, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B''_D$$\end{document} correlates very strongly with c (around 0.92) and almost perfectly with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho \ge 0.99$$\end{document}). Coupled with the more natural interpretation of some of these estimates, we will focus primarily on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d'$$\end{document} and c.

In all three datasets, there is an overall conservative bias, i.e. towards answering “same” (highly significant \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c > 0$$\end{document}), but much smaller (and statistically highly significantly so) for the “removed” (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c = 0.3$$\end{document}) and especially for the “recoded” (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c = 0.2$$\end{document}) than for the “original” (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c = 0.5$$\end{document}) dataset. While in the “original” dataset there are quite a few biased participants (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|c| \ge 1$$\end{document}), there are fewer in the “removed” and the “recoded” datasets, again supporting the idea that the W items are treated as “same”.

Thus, we will focus on the “recoded” dataset, but also keep the “original” for reference, retaining the primary measures pcr and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d'$$\end{document} on the “original” and the “recoded” datasets, and the secondary measures pcr and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d'$$\end{document} on the “removed” dataset, and the measure of bias c.

Correlations between variables

Exploring the pair-wise correlations between the variables of interest is generally undertaken before more complex analyses, as, on the one hand, they help reveal issues with the data or variable coding, while, on the other hand, they may suggest relationships that should or should not be pursued in more complex models. Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlations between the variables are very similar (Fig. S10): age is negatively correlated with all the other variables, and especially strongly with education and working memory, and much less so with the various measures of tone performance, gender is correlated with education (higher for males), location correlates with education (slightly higher in A) and with the performance on tone (better for A). Importantly, despite this, the VIFs (variance inflation factors) and aGVIFs (adjusted generalized standard error inflation factors) are generally below 3; nevertheless, given that gender seemed to have higher VIFs in some cases, we also preformed the analyses excluding it, but the results are virtually identical. Another potential issue concerning gender is the imbalance between the number of males and females: while extremely unlikely to affect our results, we did check it by redoing the analyses on a subsample of our data obtained by randomly sampling 159 females, matching thus perfectly the number of males in the data; this subsample has similar properties to those of the full sample, and the results of the analyses are virtually identical to those on the full sample, including the effect of gender (for full details please see Appendix III in the full Analysis Report). The “original” and “recoded” datasets are very similar for tone: the pcr and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d'$$\end{document} are negatively affected by age, differ between locations (better in A) and genders (slightly better for males), and are positively effected by working memory and education. However, c behaves strikingly differently between the two datasets: while on the”original” it is negatively affected by age and positively by education and working memory, on the “recoded” it does not correlate with any such covariate.

Discussion

The AX task is widely used to capture individual differences in various domains, including speech perception and language processing. Its simplicity in both design and participant instruction and experience, coupled with its ability to capture subtle patterns of inter-individual differences, make it highly attractive. However, a closer inspection reveals at least three potential points of concern: (a) the selection of the stimuli, (b) the calibration of its difficulty and (c) its analysis and interpretation. We designed here an AX task aimed at capturing tone perception in an understudied minority language of China, Kam (or Dong, Glottolog code sout2741)23,24, with the following questions in mind: do the native speakers of Kam differ in how well they perceive the tonal contrasts of their language, and, if so, are these differences predictable (to some degree) by differences in individual characteristics (age, gender, formal education and musical training) and location?

While all three points are important, the crux of our paper is, in many ways, represented by (c). The AX task, by itself, can be interpreted and analyzed in several ways, each based on its own (explicit or implicit) measurement model and assumptions. Arguably the simplest approach equates the performance of a participant with the percent of correct responses she gives across the full set of items (including multiple presentations of the same item), but, on closer inspection, this makes a set of strong assumptions. First, there is no explicit conceptualization of measurement error in the sense that a participant’s responses are taken at face value (granted, the multiple repetitions of the same item aim, among others, at implicitly “smoothing out” such errors), which is theoretically problematic, and usually results in a point estimate without any quantification of uncertainty or error. Second, all items and presentations of the same item are deemed equally informative, in the sense that their contribution to the percent correct responses is the same (i.e., they have equal weight). Third, as all items are treated equally, it is impossible to differentiate the contribution of more difficult items from that of simpler ones, which might or might not matter for the question at hand. Fourth, despite the substantive expertise involved in the design of the items and the multiple rounds of piloting, it might very well be that not all items capture the same kind of performance: e.g., it is possible that the contrast between some pairs of phonological tones is based on phonetic cues different from those relevant for other pairs of phonological tones. This implies that fundamentally different perceptual and cognitive mechanisms may be involved in perceiving and responding to them, and that, therefore, some participants might show differential performance pointing to inter-individual processing differences that may be theoretically and/or practically relevant. Thus, a participant’s % correct responses potentially amalgamates measurement error, items of varying difficulty and items measuring different things altogether in a single number of inflated perceived robustness and precision (usually without any quantification of uncertainty) that is hard to interpret and compare between participants. Signal detection theory (SDT) addresses the fact that participants might differ not just in their performance but also in their systematic biases, such as towards answering “same” (“conservative” bias) or towards answering “different” (“liberal” bias). SDT distinguishes between the “bias-free” performance, as estimated by various discrimination measures, and bias; this bi-dimensional summary of the participant’s responses allows, on the one hand, the “de-contamination” of the actual performance estimate from any biases the participant might have, and, on the other hand, the identification of very biased participants, but, just like % correct responses, still fails to account for measurement error and for the differences between items.

Here, we argue that the proper way to understand and analyze an AX task should be based on an explicit psychometric approach31–33, which allows the conceptualization of performance in terms of one (or more) latent factor(s) (or construct(s)) that, while not directly measured by the task itself, can be estimated because they load on the actual items. Even such apparently simple tasks like the AX task, need to be approached from an explicit psychometric perspective in order to really understand what they measure, how the items are related to each other and to the participants’ responses, and what any inter-individual differences we might find actually mean. We sketched here a template of analysis and interpretation, that can be summarized as follows: first and foremost, do not simply compute the % correct responses or even the SDT-derived discrimination and bias, because we need to ensure that they do make sense, in that our items measure only one thing, in roughly the same way and with comparable strength. This not only allows to better understand our items, but also to identify any items that are wrongly coded, interpreted in unexpected ways by the participants or that, indeed, contradict the theory subtending the design of the task. This was precisely our case: without this psychometrically-informed analysis, we would probably not have identified the “weird” items that are interpreted by the participants as “same” instead of the intended “different” type. This, in turn, allowed us to recode them in the direction that fits the participants’ interpretation, restoring inter-item consistence.

Zooming in on these “weird” items, there are only ten of them, which involve only four tone pairs (in both orders): 212 vs 33, 335 vs 35, 332 vs 52, and 52 vs 452. Interestingly, while 212 and 33 belong to phonological tones 4 and 6, respectively, and 332 and 52 belong to phonological tones 5 and 1, respectively, the other two pairs are allophones belonging to tones 3 and 1, respectively (see Fig. 1 and Table 2-10 on page 32 in ref23). In summary, we found that our participants differ systematically in their performance on the AX tone task (questions i and ii in the Introduction), that the items of the AX task are perceived differently by the participants (question iii), and that these differences between participants can be explained, at least in part, by a complex interplay between several of their characteristics (question iv). More precisely, we found that the location of the participants, their age, the amount of formal education, and their performance on the working memory task do seem to affect the AX task performance, in the sense that older participants from location A that benefited from more formal education and have a higher working memory capacity, tend to outperform the other participants on the AX tone task. Unfortunately, due to a floor effect where the vast majority of our participants reported no formal musical training, we could not ascertain if it has any effects on the AX task. (In future iterations of this study, this question should be formulated in a more culture-specific manner, focusing not on formal musical training but on measures of involvement with the traditional songs.) Importantly, gender did not affect the AX tone performance directly, but only mediated through age and formal education (as female participants tend to have less formal education and to be somewhat younger than the male participants in our sample), and working memory performance is increased by more formal education, but negatively affected by age. Given our data, we can only speculate on the mechanistic reasons of why age and education have such a mediation effect, but it is possible that the promotion of Mandarin may disproportionately affect tone perception in younger people. This complex network of influences suggests that the performance of native speakers on an arguably simple task taping into a fundamental aspect of their native language is affected by a multitude of factors, arguably ranging from dialectal to sociolinguistic, educational and cognitive. However, we should avoid jumping to simplistic explanations for these differences in performance, especially given the exploratory nature of our study and the various issues with the task and the data that we have highlighted throughout: while replication, in the same community or in a comparable one will be difficult, particularly aiming for a similar or, preferably, larger sample size, replicate we must before drawing strong conclusions. Nevertheless, the relative advantage of the participants from location A might be due, at least partially, to the fact that the design of the AX task was based on the reference grammar of Kam23, which used the information provided by informants from this location. It is important, however, to highlight that extensive expertise with the actual sociolinguistic situation (MW) clearly suggests that, while there are, indeed, some slight differences in the phonetics of tone between these locations and also possibly between the language’s registers, these clearly do not interfere with mutual intelligibility in real life. This might be one of those cases in which “stressing the system” experimentally (here, using an AX task that forces the participants to rely only on the information provided by F0 without any other phonetic or contextual cues) makes “visible” very tiny differences that, in normal circumstances, would remain “invisible” and irrelevant.

While a superficial conclusion could be that our theory-heavy and methodologically costly use of psychometrics was but a fancy detour through the abstruse mystic lands of psychological measurement for no real gain, this would be a massive misunderstanding: it is precisely because we took this theoretically motivated stance, accompanied by a well developed and supported methodology, which allowed us not only to identify and “fix” the “weird” items, but to justify the use of the much simpler summaries of performance on the “recoded” dataset. All could have very well turned out differently: it is entirely possible that the performance could have been, in fact, composed of two or more relatively independent factors subtended by cognitively different processes, which would have rendered meaningless the use of a single averaging measure of performance per participant; or that the items differed clearly in difficulty, with some much more difficult than the others, requiring some sort of weighting or the use of a full IRT approach. Therefore, we urge that such a preliminary analysis be conducted before applying an off-the-shelf summary of the performance for any task: if there is so much hidden theory and potential structure in a “simple” AX task, one can begin to imagine the complexities hidden in much more complex tasks and experimental measures. Luckily, psychometrics does exist, this literature is indeed accessible and constantly evolving, and its practitioners provide how-to’s, tutorials and even ready-to-run computer code for users such as ourselves (such as34, which we used extensively here).
