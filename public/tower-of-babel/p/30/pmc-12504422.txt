Silicon and thiourea synergy boosts wheat cultivars’ grain quality under salinity via coordinated ion and antioxidant responses
Authors: Elahe Gheisary, Seyed Abdolreza Kazemeini, Maryam Samadi, Mozhgan Alinia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Salinity stress severely impacts wheat productivity and grain quality. This study investigated the effects of application of plant growth promoting substances (non-primed control (NP), hydropriming (HP), combined priming (1 mM silicon + 250 mg L− 1 thiourea) (SiP + TUP), combined TU priming (250 mg L− 1 thiourea) + Si foliar application (1 mM silicon) (TUP + SiF), combined TU foliar application (250 mg L− 1 thiourea) + Si priming (1 mM silicon) (TUF + SiP) on salt tolerance and grain quality of two wheat cultivars (salt-tolerant Barzegar and salt-sensitive Amin) under three salinity levels (0.5, 4 and 10 dS m− 1). The experiment used a completely randomized factorial design with three replications under greenhouse conditions. Salinity reduced grain yield by 42–60% (more severely in Amin), decreased ash content, moisture, carbohydrates, fiber, and gluten, while increasing grain hardness and protein. The combined TUP + SiF treatment significantly mitigated these effects, particularly in Barzegar, restoring 10.5–39.0% of yield versus 34.4–50.0% in Amin. Mechanistically, the treatment: (1) enhanced K+/Na+ ratio (1.7–2.2-fold) via upregulation of transporters, (2) reduced oxidative damage (MDA decreased by 33.8–60.9%) through boosted antioxidant enzymes (SOD 12–14%, POD 33%, APX 25–26%, CAT 55–94%), and (3) improved grain quality by increasing gluten (8.8–9.8%) and carbohydrates (6.7–7.5%) while reducing hardness and protein. Silicon’s role in epidermal silica deposition reduced Na+ uptake, while thiourea’s thiol group directly scavenged ROS. Cultivar differences revealed Barzegar’s superior ion homeostasis and antioxidant capacity. The TUP + SiF combination effectively counters salinity impacts through synergistic physiological mechanisms, with greater efficacy in tolerant cultivar. These findings provide practical strategies for wheat cultivation in saline soils, though cultivar-specific optimization may enhance results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-18660-0.

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.), with an area harvested of 220,407,070 hectares and a yield of 3625 kg ha− 1, serves as a vital nutritional source, providing carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, vitamins, and essential minerals1,2. It has a salt tolerance threshold of 6.0 dS m− 1 and is cultivated under irrigated, dryland, and rain-fed conditions. While wheat exhibits moderate salinity tolerance, its productivity can be significantly affected by water or soil salinity3.

Salinity stress disrupts numerous physiological and biochemical processes in wheat, including antioxidant defense mechanisms, nutrient acquisition, and ionic homeostasis, ultimately affecting grain yield and quality parameters such as gluten content, carbohydrate composition, protein levels, and moisture retention4,5. Under saline conditions, excessive sodium (Na⁺) and chloride (Cl⁻) ions accumulate in the soil, leading to osmotic stress, ion toxicity, and oxidative damage6. In response, wheat plants activate antioxidant enzymes (e.g., superoxide dismutase, catalase, and peroxidase) to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) and mitigate cellular damage7,8. However, prolonged salinity can overwhelm these defense systems, impairing plant growth and metabolic functions. Additionally, salinity interferes with the uptake of essential nutrients such as potassium (K+), calcium (Ca2+), and magnesium (Mg2+), further exacerbating stress and reducing grain filling efficiency9. The impact of salinity on wheat grain quality is multifaceted10. Gluten proteins, which determine dough elasticity and baking quality, are particularly sensitive to salt stress, often resulting in altered glutenin and gliadin ratios11,12. Furthermore, salinity can influence starch biosynthesis and carbohydrate metabolism, leading to changes in grain texture and nutritional value. Protein content may either increase due to stress-induced accumulation or decrease due to impaired nitrogen assimilation. Moisture retention in grains is also affected, influencing storage stability and milling properties11–13.

Soil salinity is a growing threat to agricultural productivity, particularly in fragile ecosystems where intensive farming accelerates land degradation. To combat this, sustainable strategies such as seed priming14 and foliar application of plant growth-promoting substances (PGPS) have emerged as effective solutions15,16. These substances including phytohormones, microbial inoculants, and organic biostimulants optimize physiological and biochemical processes at both metabolic and whole-plant levels, thereby enhancing innate abiotic stress tolerance17. When combined with mineral nutrients or bio-regulators, PGPS can significantly improve crop resilience and productivity15,16.

Silicon (Si), the second most abundant element in the Earth’s crust, improves plant productivity under stress despite its non-essential status18. Research confirms that Si enhances germination, growth, photosynthesis, grain quality and yield in salt-stressed crops such as wheat19 wheat20 fenugreek21 chia22 and guar17. Key physiological and mechanistic benefits include: regulation of antioxidant systems and photochemical efficiency, higher biomass production and chlorophyll retention suppressed pigment degradation23 and promoting K⁺ retention and restricting Na⁺ uptake, strengthening antioxidant defenses20 stabilizing cell membranes via elevated anthocyanins and antioxidant enzymes24. These synergistic mechanisms effectively reduce oxidative stress under salinity.

Thiourea (TU) is a sulfur-rich compound (42% sulfur, 36% nitrogen) that acts as an effective growth regulator and mitigates oxidative stress in plants25. As a synthetic thiol, TU exhibits strong redox-modulating properties, helping plants combat cellular damage caused by abiotic stressors26,27. In wheat, recent research highlights TU’s role in boosting salinity tolerance by stabilizing cell membranes, increasing antioxidant enzyme activity, and improving photosynthetic efficiency, resulting in higher crop yields28.

Although silicon and thiourea independently mitigate stress, their combined effects on wheat cultivars under salinity remain unstudied. This study hypothesized that silicon-thiourea synergy would improve ion-antioxidant responses, and grain quality in wheat cultivars. Specifically, the research aimed to: (1) evaluate their complementary effects on salt stress mitigation, and (2) examine resulting alterations in quality and yield of grain, mineral homeostasis, and antioxidant defense under saline growth conditions.

Materials and methods

Experimental site, plant material, and growing conditions

The experiment was conducted in February 2022 in the research greenhouse of Shiraz University’s School of Agriculture (elevation: 1810 m above sea level). Two wheat cultivars Amin (salt-sensitive, tolerance threshold: 3.67 dS m− 1) and Barzegar (salt-tolerant, tolerance threshold: 7.13 dS m− 1) were obtained from the Seed and Plant Improvement Institute (Karaj, Iran). Their salt sensitivity was determined based on prior assessments of photosynthetic parameters, redox status, and ionic homeostasis29. Greenhouse temperature was maintained at 21 ± 1 °C during the vegetative growth and vernalization stages, then increased to 29 ± 2 °C during flowering and grain filling to meet physiological requirements for reproductive development. Relative humidity was kept at 80% under natural photoperiod conditions. Plants were grown in 18 cm × 20 cm pots filled with 4.5 kg of loam soil, with the following properties: Organic matter (1.2%), total nitrogen (0.12%), CaCO3 (59%), ECe (0.64 dS m− 1) and pH (7.20). The study followed a completely randomized design with three replications, structured as a three-factor factorial (salinity stress, priming, and cultivar). Pots were arranged on benches by treatment groups. Further details on factor levels are provided in subsequent sections.

Experimental design and treatments

Sodium metasilicate (Na2O3Si·5H2O; Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) and thiourea (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) were used for seed priming. Preliminary trials identified the optimal concentrations and durations by assessing wheat emergence and early seedling growth. Seeds of both cultivars were soaked in silicon (0.25, 0.50, 0.75 and 1 mM) and thiourea (20, 500, 750 and 1000 mg L− 1) solutions for 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 and 12 h. The optimal conditions 6 h priming with 1 mM silicon or 250 mg L− 1 thiourea were selected (data not shown). After priming, seeds were rinsed thoroughly with distilled water to remove residues and dried in the dark at 25 °C until reaching their original weight. Surface sterilization was performed using 3% NaOCl (3 min), followed by three distilled water washes before sowing. In addition to individual silicon (Si) and thiourea (TU) priming, five others treatments were tested (Table 1).

Three salinity levels were tested: 0.5 (control), 4, and 10 dS m− 1 (corresponding to 0, 2.24, and 6.08 g L− 1 NaCl, respectively). Each plastic pot was sown with seven seeds, which were later thinned to five uniform seedlings after germination. Salinity treatments were initiated at the three-leaf stage (Zadoks growth stage 1330). By the flag leaf sheath expansion stage (Zadoks 4730), young leaves from three plants per pot were collected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored for subsequent biochemical analysis. Saline irrigation was maintained until harvest.

Leaf nutrient concentrations

To analyze nutrient concentrations in leaf, two digestion methods were employed. The first involved sulfuric acid digestion for nitrogen (N) determination, while the second used a di-acid mixture to assess potassium (K+), calcium (Ca2+), sodium (Na+), and silicon (Si). Standard analytical protocols were followed: N was measured using Kjeldahl distillation, and K+, Ca2+, Na+, and Si by flame photometry and titration31,32.

Antioxidant enzyme activity measurement

Leaf samples were homogenized in ice-cold potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.8) containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), followed by centrifugation to obtain the supernatant for enzyme assays33. The extract was used to measure the activity of:

SOD activity was determined by measuring the inhibition of nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) reduction under fluorescent light at 4 °C. The assay mixture contained the enzyme extract, and activity was quantified by comparing the sample’s reaction inhibition with the fully reduced NBT control (blank). Absorbance was recorded at 560 nm using a Jenway 7315 UV/VIS spectrophotometer (Staffordshire, UK), following the method of Beauchamp and Fridovich34.

POD activity was determined using a reaction mixture containing enzyme extract, potassium phosphate buffer, and guaiacol. The reaction was started by adding H2O2, with absorbance measured at 470 nm for 60 seconds35.

The CAT assay was performed using a reaction mixture of phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), H2O2, and enzyme extract. Enzyme activity was determined by monitoring the decrease in H2O2 absorbance at 240 nm over 60 s, using an extinction coefficient of 0.0394 Mm− 1 cm− 136.

Ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activity was measured according to Nakano and Asada37. The reaction mixture consisted of 50 mM potassium buffer (pH 7.0), 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM ascorbate (AsA), 0.1 mM H2O2, and the enzyme extract. The decrease in absorbance at 290 nm was monitored for 1 min using a spectrophotometer. APX activity was calculated using an extinction coefficient of 2.8 mM− 1 cm− 1.

Lipid peroxidation (MDA) and ROS production (H2O2) measurement

For MDA determination, leaf samples were homogenized in trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and centrifuged at 4 °C. The supernatant was mixed with TBA (thiobarbituric acid) dissolved in 20% TCA, heated at 100 °C for 30 min, and then rapidly cooled on ice. MDA content was measured spectrophotometrically at 532 nm and 600 nm, using an extinction coefficient of 155 mM− 1 cm− 138.

For H2O2 determination, all steps were conducted under low-light conditions. After centrifuging the TCA leaf extract, potassium phosphate buffer and potassium iodide were added. H2O2 concentration was determined by measuring absorbance at 390 nm, comparing against a standard curve39.

Grain quality parameters

The evaluated wheat quality traits including ash content (%), grain hardness (%), moisture (%), protein content (%), carbohydrate (%), fiber (%), and gluten (%) were analyzed using standardized methods to ensure accuracy and reproducibility. Ash content was determined via dry ashing in a muffle furnace at 550 °C (08-01)40, while grain hardness was assessed using the near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) (08–21)40. Moisture content was measured by near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) (44-19)40, and protein content was quantified through the Kjeldahl (46-10)40. Carbohydrate content was calculated by near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS)41, and fiber content was analyzed via the enzymatic-gravimetric method (32-10)40. Gluten content was evaluated using the wet gluten test (38 − 12)40.

Grain yield

At physiological maturity, plants were cut at the soil surface. The seeds were carefully separated from spikes and air-dried at room temperature for 48 h. The dried grains from each pot were then weighed using a digital balance with 0.01 g precision to determine grain yield per pot.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute). Normality was verified through Shapiro-Wilk tests, supported by skewness and kurtosis evaluation. Significant treatment effects (ANOVA, p < 0.05) were further analyzed using Fisher’s LSD test. Pearson correlation coefficients between physiological and biochemical parameters were computed using Origin Pro 2024. Volcano plot were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0.

Results

Leaf ion accumulation in the wheat cultivars

The results regarding Na+, K+, Ca2+, Si and N accumulation in the leaves and K+/Na+ ratio of two wheat cultivars exposed to the salinity levels with or without the application of Si and TU are shown in Table 2. Under 10 dS m− 1salinity level, the Na contents in the leaves (2.4- and 3.1-fold) were prominently (P < 0.05) increased in the plants of Barzegar and Amin cultivars accordingly, over the 0.5 dS m− 1 salinity level. While K+, Ca2+, Si and N concentration in the leaf and K+/Na+ ratio were prominently reduced by 1.5-, 1.3-, 1.3-, 1.3- and 3.6- fold in the Barzegar cultivar and decreased by 1.73-, 2.0-, 1.4-, 1.4- and 5.4- fold in Amin cultivar, respectively, when grown under 10 dS m− 1salinity level compared to the 0.5 dS m− 1 salinity level. Nevertheless, the TUP + SiF treatment under 10 dS m− 1salinity level remarkably improved the K+, Ca2+, Si and N concentration in the leaf and K+/Na+ ratio by 1.3-, 1.3-, 1.5-, 1.2- and 1.7-fold in the Amin cultivar and by 1.2-, 1.1-, 1.5-, 1.1- and 2.2-fold in Barzegar cultivar, respectively, when compared with corresponding salinity level without TUP + SiF amendment. Moreover, TUP + SiF supply under 10 dS m− 1 salinity level caused a significant reduction in Na+ concentration in the leaf (1.3- and 1.7-fold) of Amin and Barzegar cultivars, respectively, compared to 0.5 dS m− 1 salinity level. The scatter plot analysis revealed significant negative correlations (p < 0.05) between Na+ content and all other measured traits: K+, K+/Na+ ratio, Ca2+, Si, N, and grain yield (Fig. 1A). Significant differences in nutrient content between the Barzegar and Amin cultivars were identified through volcano plot analysis (Fig. 1B and S1 Table), highlighting distinct metabolic responses to salinity stress.

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and malondialdehyde (MDA) contents of wheat cultivars

Under high salinity conditions (10 dS m− 1) in NP treatment, both wheat cultivars showed significant (P < 0.05) increases in oxidative stress markers compared to the control (0.5 dS m− 1). The Barzegar cultivar exhibited 40.2% higher MDA and 9.2% higher H2O2 levels, while the more sensitive Amin cultivar showed greater increases of 73.8% for MDA and 68.5% for H2O2 (Table 3). However, the TUP + SiF treatment effectively counteracted these stress effects. In Barzegar plants, it reduced MDA and H2O2 levels by 37.9% and 4.4% respectively, while in Amin plants, the reductions were 25.3% for MDA and 13.0% for H2O2 compared to untreated plants under the same salinity conditions.

Antioxidant enzyme activities of wheat cultivars

The activities of various enzymatic antioxidants in wheat cultivars were significantly affected by salinity stress and subsequent priming treatments (Table 3). The results indicated that superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), catalase (CAT), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activities increased substantially under elevated salinity. The highest increase was observed at 10 dS m− 1 in the Barzegar cultivar, with levels rising by 1.3-, 2-, 3.6-, and 1.4-fold, respectively, compared to the control (0.5 dS m− 1) under non-primed (NP) conditions. In contrast, applying the TUP + SiF treatment to salt-stressed plants enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities. Specifically, SOD increased by 14.2% (Barzegar) and 12.6% (Amin), POD by 33.1% (Barzegar) and 33.8% (Amin), APX by 26.7% (Barzegar) and 25.9% (Amin), and CAT by 55.3% (Barzegar) and 93.9% (Amin) compared to combination of salt-stressed plants with NP treatment. Scatter plot analysis revealed significant (p < 0.05) correlations between grain yield and oxidative stress parameters (Fig. 2A). The negative yield relationships with MDA and H₂O₂ indicate that oxidative damage strongly limits crop productivity. Elevated antioxidant enzymes (POD, CAT, APX) typically represent compensatory stress responses, explaining their inverse association with yield. Significant differences in biochemical traits between the Barzegar and Amin cultivars were identified and visualized through volcano plot analysis (Fig. 1B and S1 Table).

Quality traits and grain yield of wheat cultivars

The quality traits and grain yield of two wheat cultivars (Barzegar and Amin) under three levels of salinity (0.5, 4 and 10 dS m− 1) and priming treatments are given in Table 4. The presented data indicated that ash, moisture, carbohydrate, fiber, gluten and grain yield were prominently reduced (P < 0.05) by 13.6%, 14.5%, 13.3%, 22.5%, 20.6% and 42.3%, respectively in Barzegar cultivar and reduced by 24.6%, 15.8%, 14.4%, 30.4%, 18.6% and 59.4%, respectively in Amin cultivar grown under 10 dS m− 1 salinity level, compared to the 0.5 dS m− 1. However, the application of TUP + SiF treatment to the salt-stressed wheat cultivars resulted in an increment (P < 0.05) in ash (8.9% and 9.5%), moisture (6.7% and 7.3%), carbohydrate (7.5% and 6.7%), fiber (12.8% and 13.5%), gluten (9.8% and 8.8%) and grain yield (10.5% and 34.4%) of Barzegar and Amin cultivars under 4 dS m− 1 salinity level respectively, compared to NP. Under the salinity level of 10 dS m− 1, the application of TUP + SiF treatment significantly improved various parameters in both Barzegar and Amin cultivars compared to NP treatment. Specifically, ash content increased by 9.9% and 10.6%, moisture by 7.3% and 8.0%, carbohydrates by 6.6% and 6.0%, fiber by 15.0% and 16.0%, gluten by 11.1% and 9.8%, and grain yield by 39.0% and 50.0% in Barzegar and Amin cultivars, respectively. The correlation analysis demonstrated significant negative relationships between grain yield and Na⁺, MDA, H2O2, grain hardness, and protein content (p < 0.05), indicating that ionic toxicity and oxidative stress negatively impact yield potential. Conversely, strong positive correlations were observed between yield and moisture content, carbohydrates, fiber, gluten, ash, nitrogen, Si, Ca2+, K⁺/Na⁺ ratio, and K⁺ levels, suggesting that maintaining optimal nutrient homeostasis, osmotic regulation, and stress-protective elements is crucial for enhancing yield under stress conditions. These findings collectively highlight that while Na⁺ accumulation and oxidative damage limit productivity, improved K⁺/Na⁺ ratio and sustained acquisition of beneficial nutrients (Si, Ca2+, K⁺) and quality components (carbohydrates, gluten) are positively associated with yield (Fig. 3A, B).

Discussion

The study revealed that salinity stress (10 dS m− 1) significantly increased Na+ accumulation (2.4- to 3.1-fold) while reducing K+, Ca2+, Si, N, and K+/Na+ ratio in both wheat cultivars (Table 2), with stronger negative correlations between Na+ and other nutrients in Amin (Fig. 1A, B). This disruption likely stems from Na+ induced competitive inhibition of membrane transporters, impairing K+ uptake and nitrate assimilation. However, TUP + SiF treatment mitigated these effects, restoring the K+/Na+ ratio (1.7- to 2.2-fold) and nutrient levels (Table 2) through synergistic mechanisms: Si deposited in root cell walls to block Na+ influx while upregulating HKT1/SOS1 transporters and enhancing antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, POD, CAT and APX) to reduce oxidative stress, whereas TU scavenged ROS to protect transporters and enhanced nitrogenase activity, and modulated plasma membrane ion transporters to improve K⁺ retention and reduce Na⁺ accumulation. The cultivar-specific recovery (Fig. 1B) underscores the efficacy of combined osmotic and ionic stress interventions in salinity adaptation42–46.

The study revealed that salinity stress (10 dS m− 1) triggered excessive ROS generation, leading to oxidative damage as evidenced by significant increases in MDA (40.2–73.8%) and H2O2 (4.4–68.5%) levels in both cultivars (Table 3). This oxidative burst, caused by impaired electron transport and Na+ induced membrane destabilization47–49 was effectively mitigated by TUP + SiF treatment, which reduced MDA by 33.8–60.9% and H2O2 by 4.6–14.9% compared to salt-stressed controls (Table 3). The treatment worked through multiple mechanisms: TU directly scavenged ROS via its thiol group while both compounds enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, POD, CAT, APX), which increased by 12.6–93.9% under TUP + SiF treatment (Table 3). These findings were supported by significant negative correlations between oxidative markers (MDA, H2O2) and grain yield (Fig. 2A), and cultivar-specific responses visualized in the volcano plot (Fig. 2B). The coordinated action of TU and Si not only boosted enzymatic defenses (SOD converting O2•⁻ to H2O2, CAT/APX breaking down H2O2)50–52 but also stabilized membranes18 demonstrating their synergistic role in enhancing oxidative stress tolerance under saline conditions18,50–52.

Salinity stress (10 dS m− 1) significantly reduced wheat grain quality in both cultivars, decreasing ash content (13.6–24.6%), moisture (14.5–15.8%), carbohydrates (13.3–14.4%), fiber (22.5–30.4%), and gluten (18.6–20.6%) while increasing grain hardness, as shown in Table 4. These effects stemmed from ionic interference (Na⁺ vs. K⁺/Ca²⁺), oxidative damage, and impaired nitrogen metabolism, evidenced by negative correlations between yield and Na+/MDA/H2O2 (Fig. 3A, B). However, TUP + SiF treatment effectively mitigated these effects, improving ash (8.9–10.6%), moisture (6.7–8.0%), carbohydrates (6.0-7.5%), fiber (12.8–16.0%), and gluten (8.8–11.1%) at 4–10 dS m− 1 salinity (Table 4). The treatment worked through multiple mechanisms: enhancing mineral uptake (Si, K+, Ca2+; supported by positive yield correlations in Fig. 3A), preserving carbohydrate metabolism via thiourea’s ROS scavenging, improving cell wall structure through silica phytoliths, and optimizing gluten synthesis while reducing non-functional proteins50,53–56. The stronger recovery in Barzegar reflects its genetic advantage in stress adaptation.

Grain yield declined substantially under salinity (42.3–59.4% at 10 dS m− 1), with Amin showing greater sensitivity (Table 4), consistent with its weaker osmotic adjustment and antioxidant capacity. TUP + SiF treatment significantly restored yields (10.5–50.0%), particularly at 10 dS m− 1 (39.0–50.0% improvement; Table 4), through synergistic actions: (1) thiourea’s thiol group (–SH) directly scavenged ROS57,58protecting photosynthesis59; (2) Si reduced root-to-shoot Na⁺ translocation18; (3) both compounds upregulated SOS1/NHX1 transporters52; (4) enhanced antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, APX) protected reproductive structures6,42–46; and (5) improved assimilate partitioning boosted grain filling50,53–56. The treatment’s superior efficacy in Barzegar (Fig. 3B) underscores the importance of genetic background, suggesting that cultivar-specific optimization of TUP + SiF could maximize salinity tolerance in wheat production systems. These results demonstrate the potential of thiourea-silicon co-application as a practical solution for saline agriculture.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that salinity stress significantly impairs wheat grain yield and quality, with cultivar-specific responses highlighting the importance of genetic background in stress tolerance. The tolerant cultivar (Barzegar) exhibited superior performance due to enhanced osmotic adjustment, K+/Na+ homeostasis, and antioxidant capacity compared to the sensitive cultivar (Amin). The combined application of thiourea and silicon effectively mitigated salinity-induced damage through synergistic mechanisms, including ROS scavenging, ion homeostasis regulation, and structural protection. Notably, thiourea’s redox-modulating properties and silicon’s role in reducing Na+ uptake collectively improved photosynthetic efficiency, assimilate partitioning, and grain filling. The treatment not only restored yield but also enhanced grain quality parameters by optimizing nutrient balance and storage protein synthesis. These findings underscore the potential of thiourea-silicon applications as a sustainable strategy to enhance wheat productivity in saline environments. However, the differential response between cultivars suggests that genotype-specific optimization may be necessary to maximize treatment efficacy. Future research should explore molecular mechanisms underlying cultivar-specific responses to further refine these interventions for precision agriculture in salt-affected regions.
