Pulmonary artery pressure via endobronchial ultrasound: A feasibility and safety study
Authors: S Michael Goertzen, Joshua Boster, Georgie Eapen, Roberto Casal, Horiana Grosu, Mona Sarkiss, Jean-Bernard Durand, Zohra Ali, May Celestino, David Rice, Reza Mehran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPulmonary hypertension significantly influences perioperative outcomes in thoracic surgery. Right heart catheterization, the standard method to assess pulmonary artery pressure, is invasive and time-consuming. We aimed to assess the feasibility and safety of direct pulmonary artery pressure measurement through endobronchial ultrasound-guided puncture of the right pulmonary artery.MethodsTwenty patients underwent ultrasound-guided puncture of the right pulmonary artery using a 22-gauge needle under real-time ultrasound guidance. Systolic, diastolic, and mean pulmonary pressures were recorded via a pressure transducer. Procedural success and complications were assessed.ResultsPulmonary artery puncture was successfully completed in all 20 patients (100%). Mean systolic pulmonary pressure was 26.5 ± 7.6 mm Hg, mean diastolic pulmonary pressure was 16.4 ± 6.3 mm Hg, and mean pulmonary pressure was 20.7 ± 6.6 mm Hg. No complications, including bleeding, vessel injury, pneumothorax, or infection occurred.ConclusionsEndobronchial ultrasound guided puncture of the right pulmonary artery appears to be a safe and effective method for direct pulmonary artery pressure measurement, potentially improving preoperative risk assessment without additional procedural risks.Key Words: pulmonary hypertension, endobronchial ultrasound, preoperative evaluation, lobectomy, pneumonectomy, non–small cell lung cancer

EBUS, Endobronchial ultrasound; PA, pulmonary artery.

Central Message

EBUS-guided pulmonary artery puncture appears to be a feasible and safe method for measuring pulmonary pressures as part of the preoperative risk assessment in patients undergoing major pulmonary surgery.

Perspective

This study explored the feasibility and safety of endobronchial ultrasound-guided pulmonary artery puncture as a streamlined method for measuring preoperative pulmonary artery pressures for patients with known or suspected lung cancer.

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) has been known to significantly influence perioperative outcomes in thoracic surgery, most notably after pneumonectomy with some experiencing right ventricular hypertrophy and postpneumonectomy pulmonary edema.1,2 Retrospective reviews of lobectomy for non–small cell lung cancer showed no statistically significant difference in outcomes between groups with and without PH, although these studies were significantly limited by sample size.3 Routine assessment for PH in prospective surgical patients is often deferred due to the invasiveness of right heart catheterization (RHC) and the inaccuracy of transthoracic echocardiography (TTE).4, 5, 6 Although TTE may also be an option, it entails an additional procedure with attendant risks and complications.7 In a previous study, we demonstrated that pulmonary artery pressure (PAP) measured by endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) Doppler did not achieve sufficient reliability compared with right heart catheterization.8

Patients with lung cancer routinely undergo EBUS for staging, offering a unique opportunity to evaluate pulmonary artery (PA) hemodynamics. Direct measurement of PAP via pulmonary arterial puncture remains underexplored in human beings. Prior reports have described puncture of the vascular structures during EBUS for purposes of biopsy of a deep structure of interest with minimal complications.9 Additionally, the use of EBUS to puncture the PA has been successfully used to treat PA thrombosis in pig models10 and direct puncture of the PA has been used to measure pulmonary pressure in rats.11 Building on the animal models and limited human data that suggest safety, we investigated the feasibility and safety of EBUS-guided puncture of the right PA to directly measure PAP in 20 patients, hypothesizing it could provide precise hemodynamic data with minimal risk.

Methods

Study Population

Following institutional review board approval (2018-0500) approved in 2018, 20 patients (9 men, 11 women; mean age 68.6 ± 9.7 years) with early-stage lung cancer or suspected lung cancer who require preoperative evaluation by EBUS for oncologic staging and/or diagnosis were prospectively enrolled between March 2024 and January 2025. No prior evaluation for potential pulmonary hypertension was performed in this cohort. Inclusion criteria was age 40 years or older and those who were considered a potential candidate for surgery based on clinical and radiographic staging and National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines and scheduled to undergo EBUS for diagnosis or staging. Exclusion criteria included age younger than 40 years; patients who were not candidates for surgical resection due to comorbidities; and presence of any medical, psychiatric, cognitive, or other conditions that compromised the patient's ability to understand or give consent. All patients underwent routine evaluation for thrombocytopenia and coagulopathy before the procedure, with platelets >50,000 and international normalized ratio <1.5 as institutional cutoffs for EBUS. Antiplatelet and anticoagulant medications, except aspirin, were held before EBUS, with apixaban stopped 48 hours prior. Informed consent was obtained from the patients before the procedure, including consent for publication of obtained data. Pulmonary hypertension was defined as a mean PAP ≥20 mm Hg.12

We estimated the adverse event rate in this cohort using the Clopper-Pearson exact 95% CI. If the estimated adverse event rate is 5% and the bounds of the exact 95% CI are 0.001 to 0.249 with 20 patients.

Procedure

Following consent, patients were placed under general anesthesia per institutional protocol which, in our institution, typically entails propofol and neuromuscular blockade. Following anesthetic induction a laryngeal mask airway was placed. Topical anesthesia was applied via bronchoscope to the tracheobronchial tree and an airway examination completed. Following airway exam, navigational bronchoscopy was performed as planned in 2 patients followed by staging EBUS in all patients, which was performed using a convex-probe EBUS bronchoscope (Olympus BF-UC180 F). The lymph node stations sampled varied between patients ranging from 1 to 6 stations. The right PA was visualized adjacent to the right main bronchus. A 22-gauge needle (Olympus NA-201SX-4022) was advanced through the bronchial wall into the artery under real-time ultrasound guidance. Needle placement was confirmed by ultrasound imaging. A pressure transducer was then connected via saline-filled tubing to the end of the EBUS needle. The channel was flushed, leveled, and zeroed by the anesthesia team. Once setup was complete, systolic, diastolic, and mean PAP over 10 respiratory cycles were obtained and recorded. The needle was withdrawn, and staging EBUS was completed per institutional protocol. Emergency protocol for control of bleeding was kept on standby, which included direct tamponade, endobronchial blocker, and surgical backup if needed. Patients were monitored for an hour postprocedure for complications (eg, hemoptysis, pneumothorax, or hemodynamic instability).

Data Collection and Analysis

PAP values were recorded as systolic, diastolic, and mean during mechanical ventilation. Success rate and complications were documented. Descriptive statistics were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 10.1 (GraphPad Inc).

Results

Patient Characteristics

The cohort comprised patients with known or suspected lung cancer with final tissue diagnoses, including adenocarcinoma (n = 12), squamous cell carcinoma (n = 5), small cell carcinoma (n = 1), carcinoid tumor (n = 1), and bronchogenic carcinoma (n = 1). Most patients were White (95%) and mean age was 68.6 ± 9.7 years.

Procedural Outcomes

EBUS-guided puncture was successful in all 20 patients (100%). One patient had an aberrantly low pressure (PAP 9/4 mm Hg with mean = 5 mm Hg) due to equipment malfunction and was therefore excluded from pressure measurement data analysis but included for needle puncture safety analysis. Table 1 presents individual PAP measurements. Mean systolic PAP was 26.5 ± 7.6 mm Hg (range, 15-38 mm Hg), diastolic PAP was 16.4 ± 6.3 mm Hg (range, 8-29 mm Hg), and mean PAP was 20.7 ± 6.6 mm Hg (range, 12-33 mm Hg). PH (mean PAP ≥20 mm Hg) was present in 45% of patients.12 No complications were observed during or after the procedure.

Discussion

This study suggests EBUS-guided puncture of the PA is a feasible method for directly measuring PAP in patients being considered for thoracic surgery. Notably, no adverse events were noted in this cohort. This suggests that measurement of the PA pressure could be seamlessly integrated into presurgical staging EBUS without the need for additional procedures. There was a 100% success rate of puncture. The absence of complications is consistent with prior reports of PA puncture safety in animal models and limited human cases with normal pressures.9,10 Uniquely, we demonstrate the safety of obtaining PA pressures even in the presence of occult pulmonary hypertension in a small group of patients. The mean PAP of 20.7 ± 6.6 mm Hg reflects a mixed population, with 45% meeting PH criteria (mean PAP ≥20 mm Hg), consistent with the oncologic and comorbid profiles of thoracic surgery candidates. However, further study in patients with severely elevated pulmonary pressures is needed for extrapolation of safety to this patient population.

Unlike the indirect pulmonary vasculature acceleration time (PVAT) method described previously,8 direct puncture provides potentially definitive pressure readings, likely surpassing the diagnostic accuracy of screening tools like TTE or EBUS-PVAT. Although RHC remains the gold standard, its invasiveness contrasts with the less invasive nature of EBUS, which leverages an existing procedure. The ease of the procedure and lack of complications suggest this technique could be seamlessly integrated into routine staging, while saving 1 extra step, in patients needing an evaluation for pulmonary artery pressure for preoperative risk stratification.

It is important to note that the change of the cutoff for PH in recent European Society of Cardiology and European Respiratory Society guideline updates12 increases the sensitivity for PH but also makes the clinical relevance of this designator more ambiguous in the perioperative risk evaluation of patients undergoing major pulmonary resections.

Further questions remain as to rare complications that may be associated with the procedure and their clinical implications. In 1 case series, a pseudoaneurysm was noted in 1 patient who underwent biopsy of a target lesion deep to the aorta9; however, no complications were noted with PA puncture. Other rare complications associated with EBUS, such as mediastinitis, which occurs in <0.1% of EBUS cases, highlight the potential risk of infection following needle puncture from a nonsterile space into the vasculature, could not be elucidated in this small sample size.13

Limitations of this study include the small sample size, a single-center setting, and absence of direct comparison to RHC, although the external transducer setup is the same as that used in RHC, further validation with concurrent RHC data would be useful in further studies. The patient population in the present study differed somewhat from the indirect PVAT method previously described by our group in that the patient selection was not focused on those with comorbid conditions that would raise the probability of PH.8 The number of patients with PH in this group was lower, with 45% meeting criteria for PH versus 65% with the group preselected for risk factors in a prior study.8 Finally, having easy access to the measurement of the PAP opens the opportunity for a large, longitudinal study looking at the importance of this variable in postoperative outcomes because these patients were excluded in the National Emphysema Treatment Trial.14 Finally, multicenter involvement as well as validation of safety in lower-volume centers would provide valuable insight into broad applicability of the procedure during staging EBUS.

Conclusions

Direct PAP measurement via EBUS is feasible and was safe in this small group of patients, offering valuable hemodynamic insights that could improve preoperative risk assessment in patients undergoing thoracic surgery. Although no complications occurred in this group, validation with a larger sample size would be required to determine more accurately the safety of EBUS-guided puncture of the PA.
