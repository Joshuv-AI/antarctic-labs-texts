Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is a complex hematologic disorder characterized by chronic hemolysis, episodic vaso‐occlusion, and progressive end‐organ dysfunction. Patients with SCD may experience acute episodes, known as sickle cell crises. These are sudden, severe events often presenting with intense pain or organ‐specific complications resulting from vaso‐occlusion. Hepatic involvement, collectively termed sickle cell hepatopathy, encompasses a spectrum of acute and chronic complications that significantly impact morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Acute hepatic complications include sickle cell hepatic crisis, hepatic sequestration, liver fibrosis, and intrahepatic cholestasis. In contrast, chronic manifestations, such as persistent cholestasis, iron overload, and cirrhosis, may develop secondary to recurrent vaso‐occlusive episodes, transfusion‐related hemosiderosis, and coexistent viral hepatitis [3, 4, 5]. Autoimmune liver disease is also common in SCD populations. [6] Although acute liver dysfunction is more commonly reported, cirrhosis is increasingly recognized as a consequential comorbidity in SCD, contributing to systemic inflammation, hemodynamic instability, and hepatic decompensation [4, 7].

Despite the growing recognition of hepatic pathology in SCD, the impact of cirrhosis on in‐hospital outcomes remains inadequately characterized. Emerging evidence suggests that hepatic dysfunction in this population may potentiate systemic inflammatory responses, exacerbate coagulopathy, and worsen cardiopulmonary status, collectively increasing the risk of adverse clinical outcomes [7, 8]. However, data quantifying the independent effect of cirrhosis on hospitalization‐related morbidity and mortality in SCD remain scarce. This study leverages the 2021 National Inpatient Sample (NIS) to investigate the association between cirrhosis and key in‐hospital outcomes, including mortality, need for mechanical ventilation, and vasopressor utilization, in adults hospitalized with SCD.

Methodology

Data Source and Study Design

This study used data from the 2021 NIS, a component of the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP) curated by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ). The NIS is the largest publicly available all‐payer inpatient database in the United States, designed to provide nationally and regionally representative estimates of hospital utilization, costs, quality, and clinical outcomes.

The NIS employs a stratified probability sampling methodology encompassing all non‐federal acute care hospitals nationwide. Stratification is based on key hospital characteristics, including ownership, bed capacity, teaching status, urban versus rural location, and geographic region. Within each stratum, a random 20% sample of hospitals is selected, and all inpatient discharges from these institutions are recorded. To ensure national representativeness, sampling weights are applied. The 2021 dataset includes data from 49 statewide organizations (48 states plus the District of Columbia), covering over 97% of the US population. The NIS provides granular discharge‐level data, including patient demographics, primary payer information, hospital characteristics, principal and secondary diagnoses, and procedural details.

Inclusion Criteria and Study Variables

The study cohort was derived from the 2021 Nationwide Inpatient Sample (NIS) using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐10‐CM) codes. We included adult inpatients (≥ 18 years) with a principal discharge diagnosis of SCD. Among these, only patients hospitalized for a sickle cell crisis, an acute complication of SCD, were included, regardless of genotype (HbSS, HbC, or sickle cell–thalassemia), as identified by the ICD‐10‐CM codes presented.

Patients were stratified into two groups based on the presence or absence of liver cirrhosis, identified via secondary diagnoses. Cirrhosis was defined using the following ICD‐10‐CM codes (with corresponding diagnostic terms): K70.30 (alcoholic cirrhosis of liver without ascites), K70.31 (alcoholic cirrhosis of liver with ascites), K74.0 (hepatic fibrosis), K74.00 (hepatic fibrosis, unspecified), K74.01 (hepatic fibrosis with hepatic sclerosis), K74.02 (hepatic fibrosis with hepatic cirrhosis), K74.1 (hepatic sclerosis), K74.2 (hepatic fibrosis with hepatic sclerosis), K74.3 (primary biliary cirrhosis), K74.4 (secondary biliary cirrhosis), K74.5 (biliary cirrhosis, unspecified), K74.6 (other and unspecified cirrhosis of liver), K74.60 (unspecified cirrhosis of liver), K74.69 (other cirrhosis of liver), K70.3 (alcoholic cirrhosis of liver), K70.2 (alcoholic fibrosis and sclerosis of liver), and K70.9 (alcoholic liver disease, unspecified). Cases with ICD‐10‐CM codes lacking explicit reference to cirrhosis, sclerosis, or fibrosis were excluded.

SCD was identified using the following ICD‐10‐CM codes (with corresponding diagnostic terms): D57.0 (sickle cell anemia with crisis), D57.00 (sickle cell anemia with crisis, unspecified), D57.01 (sickle cell anemia with acute chest syndrome), D57.02 (sickle cell anemia with splenic sequestration), D57.03 (sickle cell anemia with other specified crisis), D57.04 (sickle cell anemia with unspecified crisis), D57.09 (other sickle cell anemia with crisis), D57.21 (sickle cell/Hb‐C disease with crisis), D57.211–D57.219 (sickle cell/Hb‐C disease with acute chest syndrome or splenic sequestration), D57.41–D57.419 (sickle cell thalassemia with crisis), D57.43–D57.439 (sickle cell thalassemia with acute chest syndrome or splenic sequestration), D57.45–D57.459 (other sickle cell thalassemia with crisis), D57.81–D57.819 (other sickle cell disorders with crisis).

Key study variables included demographic data (age, sex, and race/ethnicity), socioeconomic indicators (proxied by neighborhood income quartiles derived from ZIPINC_QRTL), and insurance type (Medicare, Medicaid, private, self‐pay, and other). Relevant comorbidities, including the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), asthma, and heart failure, were assessed.

Primary and secondary outcomes were analyzed to evaluate the impact of cirrhosis on in‐hospital morbidity and mortality among SCD patients presenting with vaso‐occlusive crises. Baseline characteristics, inpatient outcomes, and predictors of mortality were systematically examined to provide a comprehensive assessment of this high‐risk population.

Outcomes Measured

The primary outcome was in‐hospital mortality among patients admitted with a principal diagnosis of SCD, stratified by the presence or absence of liver cirrhosis. Secondary outcomes were the need for mechanical ventilation, identified using ICD‐10‐PCS procedure codes 5A1935Z, 5A1945Z, and 5A1955Z; and vasopressor administration, determined by ICD‐10‐PCS procedure codes 3E033XZ and 3E043XZ. These secondary outcomes were chosen to reflect a more severe inpatient course, with vasopressor use serving as an objective marker of hemodynamic instability and mechanical ventilation as an indicator of respiratory failure.

Statistical analyses were conducted using STATA Basic Edition, Version 18.0, employing methodologies designed to account for the complex, stratified survey structure of the NIS. Baseline characteristics of patients with and without cirrhosis were compared using Student's t‐test for continuous variables and Pearson's chi‐square test for categorical variables. To appropriately incorporate the NIS's survey design, STATA's survey data analysis commands were used, integrating sampling weights, stratification variables, and clustering adjustments to ensure nationally representative estimates.

Univariate logistic regression models were initially employed to estimate unadjusted odds ratios (ORs) for both primary and secondary outcomes. These were followed by the development of multivariate logistic regression models to adjust for potential confounders, including demographic characteristics, comorbid conditions, and hospital‐level factors, yielding adjusted ORs with corresponding 95% confidence intervals.

All statistical tests were two‐tailed, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05. This rigorous analytical framework facilitated a robust evaluation of the impact of liver cirrhosis on the clinical outcomes of patients hospitalized with sickle cell crises while mitigating potential biases inherent to large administrative datasets.

Missing Data

We assessed missingness for all variables included in our regression models within the sickle cell crisis subpopulation and reported it in the limitations. Missing data were minimal: 0.1% for the mortality outcome, < 0.1% for sex, 3.5% for race, and 1.6% for income quartile (Table S3); all other variables were complete. Survey‐weighted estimates were similar (Table S1). Given these low rates, listwise deletion was unlikely to meaningfully affect the results.

Results

Study Population and Baseline Characteristics

The study included a total of 15,953 unweighted participants, of whom 15,776 had no cirrhosis, and 177 had cirrhosis. After applying survey weights, these numbers correspond to an estimated 79,764 individuals in the population, comprising 78,880 without cirrhosis and 884 with cirrhosis (1.1%), indicating that 1.1% of the population had cirrhosis. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Among adults presenting with sickle cell crisis, those without cirrhosis had a mean age of 29.3 ± 12.6 years (range 18–86), whereas those with cirrhosis were older (41.5 ± 12.5 years; range 21–75) and exhibited a significantly higher comorbidity burden, as reflected by the Charlson Comorbidity Index (2.66 vs. 0.64; p < 0.001). Gender distribution was similar between groups, with a slightly higher proportion of females among cirrhotic patients (51.41% vs. 54.32%).

Socioeconomic disparities were evident: a greater proportion of patients with cirrhosis were in the lowest income quartile (56.82% vs. 47.98%), whereas non‐cirrhotic patients were more frequently represented in higher income quartiles. In‐Hospital Clinical Outcomes

In‐hospital outcomes are summarized in Tables 2 and 3. Patients with cirrhosis experienced a more severe clinical course. Cirrhosis was associated with substantially higher odds of inpatient mortality and invasive mechanical ventilation among adults hospitalized for SCD. In adjusted analyses, cirrhosis remained a significant predictor of mortality (adjusted OR, 3.89; 95% CI, 1.52–9.92; p = 0.005) and mechanical ventilation (adjusted OR, 3.55; 95% CI, 1.42–8.89; p = 0.007). The association with vasopressor requirement was not statistically significant (adjusted OR, 2.53; 95% CI, 0.25–25.31; p = 0.43) (Table 3).

Among patients with SCD who required mechanical ventilation (weighted n = 320), 50.0% developed acute chest syndrome, 20.3% had acute or acute‐on‐chronic heart failure, and 17.2% (95% CI, 10.0–28.0) had a history of pulmonary hypertension. In patients with cirrhosis, acute chest syndrome (ACS) occurred, but no deaths were recorded during the index hospitalization, precluding mortality. Some required mechanical ventilation; however, the association between cirrhosis and mechanical ventilation in ACS was not statistically significant (OR 0.92, 95% CI 0.12–6.90; p = 0.93). Across all SCDs, ACS occurred in 7294 cases (9.1%), splenic sequestration in 749 cases (0.94%), and cerebrovascular accidents in 40 cases (0.05%) (Table S2). Mortality or severity modeling could not be performed for splenic sequestration or cerebrovascular accidents due to the small number of events among patients with cirrhosis.

Multivariate Predictors of Inpatient Mortality

Multivariate logistic regression (Table 4) identified additional independent predictors of in‐hospital mortality. Increasing age was associated with a higher risk of death (OR 1.06 per year; 95% CI, 1.04–1.09; p < 0.001), and heart failure substantially elevated mortality risk (OR 4.31; 95% CI, 2.32–8.01; p < 0.001). Cirrhosis remained a strong independent predictor (OR 3.79; 95% CI, 1.50–9.54; p = 0.005). In contrast, female sex, race, and socioeconomic status were not significantly associated with mortality in adjusted models (Table 4).

Discussion

SCD is the most common monogenic disorder, with a rising global prevalence [7]. In the United States, approximately 100,000 individuals are affected by SCD [8]. The disease is characterized by recurrent sickling of red blood cells under hypoxic conditions, resulting in widespread vaso‐occlusion and multi‐organ damage [9]. While acute hepatic complications, including sickle cell hepatic crisis, intrahepatic cholestasis, and hepatic sequestration, are well described [10, 11, 12], the long‐term impact of chronic hepatic dysfunction, particularly cirrhosis, on in‐hospital outcomes has been less well characterized. Chronic liver disease affects up to 25% of patients with SCD and contributes to roughly 10% of disease‐related deaths [13, 14]. Cirrhosis in SCD may result from repeated ischemic insults, iron overload from chronic transfusions, viral hepatitis, and cholestatic injury [4, 15]. Reported prevalence rates of cirrhosis in SCD range from 11% to 14% [15, 16].

Cirrhosis likely exacerbates outcomes in SCD through several interconnected pathophysiological mechanisms. Hepatic dysfunction can impair the synthesis of clotting factors and albumin, contributing to coagulopathy and fluid shifts, while portal hypertension may exacerbate splanchnic congestion and increase the risk of gastrointestinal bleeding [17]. Chronic liver disease is also associated with immune dysregulation, which may heighten susceptibility to infection, a known trigger of sickle cell crises [6]. Furthermore, cirrhosis is a pro‐inflammatory state, amplifying systemic inflammation and endothelial activation, which can worsen vaso‐occlusion and multi‐organ injury [18]. These mechanisms together help explain the observed associations between cirrhosis and severe in‐hospital outcomes, including mortality and the need for invasive interventions.

Our findings highlight the impact of social vulnerability on survival in adults with SCD. In the United States, Medicare is a federal health insurance program primarily for adults aged ≥ 65 or for younger individuals with certain disabilities or end‐stage kidney disease, whereas Medicaid provides coverage for low‐income individuals and families [19]. Prior studies have shown that individuals who qualify for Medicare due to disability, end‐stage renal disease (ESRD), or dual‐eligibility for Medicare and Medicaid experience higher mortality than those covered by Medicaid alone [20]. Dual‐eligible status, in particular, identifies a population with complex medical needs, multiple comorbidities, and limited socioeconomic resources. These structural and social determinants likely contribute to the observed disparities in survival, indicating that adverse outcomes in SCD are driven not only by disease biology but also by systemic inequities in access, care coordination, and support.

As this was an association study, causality cannot be inferred. Nevertheless, several factors may contribute to the observed increase in mortality among SCD patients with liver cirrhosis. In our research, cirrhotic patients had a higher Charlson comorbidity index, reflecting greater comorbidity burden, including heart failure, which may exacerbate hypoperfusion. Cirrhosis was consistently associated with higher in‐hospital mortality among patients with sickle cell crisis, and potential mechanisms for this association have been outlined. Previous studies have reported cirrhosis in 16%–29% of SCD patients at autopsy [15, 21, 22]. Histopathological findings typically include Kupffer cell hyperplasia, erythrophagocytosis, sickled red blood cells within hepatic sinusoids, and anoxic necrosis with fibrosis [15]. Decompensated cirrhosis, particularly complications such as variceal bleeding, may further contribute to the increased mortality observed in hospitalized SCD patients. Additionally, hepatorenal sequestration crisis (HRSC), characterized by rapid blood pooling in hepatic sinusoids leading to hypovolemia, may worsen outcomes in patients with concurrent cirrhosis and SCD.

Diagnosis is typically made using imaging modalities such as ultrasonography, CT, elastography, or MRI, with liver biopsy reserved for select cases due to the high risk of complications, particularly in patients experiencing acute crises [2, 23, 24, 25]. Notably, in SCD and thalassemia, serum ferritin levels correlate poorly with hepatic iron burden, complicating the assessment of iron overload‐related liver disease [26, 27, 28]. Our study demonstrates that liver cirrhosis is an independent predictor of in‐hospital mortality among adults admitted with SCD‐related crises. Although cirrhosis was present in a small subset of patients, its association with poorer hospital outcomes was significant. Cirrhotic patients had higher odds of requiring mechanical ventilation and exhibited an increased risk of mortality, even after adjusting for demographic and clinical variables. These findings underscore the critical role of hepatic dysfunction in influencing hospital outcomes in SCD patients and highlight the need for enhanced screening and management strategies to mitigate liver‐related morbidity and mortality in this population.

Cirrhosis likely exacerbates systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, further contributing to poor clinical outcomes. Moreover, hepatic dysfunction may impair drug metabolism, potentially compromising pain management in patients experiencing vaso‐occlusive crises. Notably, our study found that SCD patients with cirrhosis had significantly higher odds of requiring mechanical ventilation, even after adjusting for multiple variables, suggesting increased morbidity. This increased need for ventilatory support may be driven by comorbidities such as acute chest syndrome, sepsis, or cirrhosis‐related cardiopulmonary impairment, including volume overload and hypoalbuminemia. Similarly, chronic kidney disease in SCD was associated with increased ventilatory support, underscoring the impact of cumulative organ damage on clinical outcomes [29]. Among the various SCD‐related crises, acute chest syndrome remains the most frequent indication for ventilatory support due to impaired oxygenation and persistent intrapulmonary sickling [30, 31]. However, despite the increased need for ventilation in cirrhotic patients, the requirement for vasopressor support was not statistically significant.

Our findings underscore the association between liver cirrhosis and worse in‐hospital outcomes, including increased mortality and morbidity in SCD patients. Early identification and a multidisciplinary management approach are essential to improving outcomes in this high‐risk population. A key strength of this study is the use of a large, nationally representative dataset, enhancing the generalizability of our findings.

Limitations

However, several limitations should be considered. The use of ICD‐10 codes for identifying SCD crises and cirrhosis may introduce misclassification bias. Additionally, the absence of granular clinical data, such as laboratory markers, diagnostic methods for cirrhosis, specific etiologies, and inpatient treatments, limits our ability to assess disease severity and treatment effects comprehensively. Residual confounders not accounted for in our analysis may also influence outcomes. Missing data were minimal for all covariates and outcomes, with the highest being 3.5% for race. While listwise deletion was used, the low proportion of missingness suggests minimal potential for bias

Conclusion

In conclusion, liver cirrhosis is a significant predictor of increased mortality and worse hospital outcomes in patients admitted with SCD. These findings emphasize the need for enhanced clinical vigilance and tailored management strategies for this high‐risk group. Future studies should focus on targeted interventions, including early detection and optimized treatment strategies, to mitigate the impact of cirrhosis on SCD‐related morbidity and mortality.
