Drug supply chain disruptions and outpatient medication shortages in the Veterans Health Administration, 2017–2020
Authors: Sherrie L. Aspinall, Clara Kima, Xinhua Zhao, Carolyn T. Thorpe, Francesca E. Cunningham, Walid F. Gellad, Peter A. Glassman, Chester B. Good, Jennifer Martin, Mina Tadrous, Katie J. Suda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeWe sought to describe how often and circumstances in which supply chain disruptions were associated with outpatient drug shortages within the Veterans Health Administration (VHA).MethodsWe conducted a descriptive analysis of VHA purchasing data for drugs used to treat chronic conditions for outpatients with a supply chain report from 2017 to 2020. In primary analyses, a VHA drug shortage was defined as a ≥ 10% absolute decrease in percentage of doses ordered that were filled by the wholesaler and/or a ≥ 10% relative decrease in total number of doses filled, comparing the 3 months after the reporting date with the 6 months before. We also examined longitudinal ordering and filling data over 12 months before and after the reporting date by whether the medication resulted in a shortage to VHA.ResultsOf 64 medications with supply chain disruptions, 67% (n = 43) resulted in a shortage to VHA. Bivariable analyses did not identify covariates significantly associated with VHA experiencing a shortage. For medications with a shortage, dosage units ordered sharply increased over the 4 months before the reporting date, peaked at the reporting date (336.0%), and then generally decreased over the remaining 11 months. The monthly dosage units filled slowly declined leading up to the reporting date, dropped more quickly to 60% at two months post-reporting date, and then increased.ConclusionsAmong ambulatory-focused medications for chronic conditions, a high proportion of those with supply chain disruptions were associated with shortages to VHA. Sharp increases in medication ordering may serve as an early signal of shortages.

Drug shortages remain an ongoing problem in the United States. Active drug supply chain disruptions reported by the American Society of Health-System Pharmacists (i.e., ASHP) reached a record high of 323 in the first quarter of 2024.1 Pharmaceutical companies are required to notify the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) if they are discontinuing or interrupting the manufacture of a medication that could result in a supply unable to meet demand (i.e., a supply chain disruption).2 However, the primary reason for shortages frequently remains unknown or undisclosed.3 Not all reported supply chain disruptions result in a clinically important decrease in supply (i.e., a drug shortage) because mitigation efforts, such as inventory management processes, can optimize the ability of pharmacies to provide medication for patients.4, 5

When drug supply chain disruptions occur, they can make it more difficult for pharmacies to maintain adequate inventory levels and lead to an increase in medication errors and adverse drug events, loss of disease control, and poor outcomes, including death.6, 7, 8 Much has been published on drug supply chain disruptions and shortages in the acute care setting and emergency department,9, 10, 11, 12 as well as with specific patient populations (e.g., oncology, pediatrics)13, 14, 15, 16 and medication classes (e.g., chemotherapy, antibiotics).7, 17, 18, 19 However, drug supply chain disruptions are also important in outpatients with chronic conditions due to the size of the population affected and the potential challenges in accessing alternatives.

While the majority of medication use occurs in outpatients, few publications focus on medication shortages in ambulatory care.20 We found descriptive studies of drug supply chain disruptions in an ambulatory pediatric population13 and potential causes and effects of supply chain disruptions involving inpatient and outpatient cardiovascular drugs.21 There was also a single site study, combined with a national survey of Veterans Health Administration (VHA) facilities, regarding the impact of shortages involving “select” injectable and oral medications on pharmacy costs and adverse patient events,8 with some of the oral medications relevant to the ambulatory care setting. Finally, a recent study assessed whether reported supply chain disruptions resulted in drug shortages before and during the COVID-19 pandemic using prescription medication purchases for outpatient and inpatient settings.5 None of these studies focused solely on medications in an adult ambulatory population, and results may differ from those seen with drugs used in acute care, for example, which could lead to alternative mitigation strategies.

Our study seeks to address this gap by describing reported supply chain disruptions and subsequent shortages of medications available to health care providers to manage chronic conditions among adult outpatients.3 Specifically, we primarily sought to describe how often drug supply chain disruptions coincided with clinically important decreases in the amount of these medications obtained by VHA – the largest integrated healthcare system in the United States. In addition, identifying factors that make it more or less likely that a reported drug supply chain disruption results in an actual shortage of the drug within health systems may have implications for policymakers and stakeholders when attempting to mitigate effects of supply chain disruptions. Thus, our secondary objective was to identify drug and supply chain report characteristics associated with a supply chain disruption converting into an actual shortage experienced by VHA.

Methods

Design, framework, and data sources

This was a descriptive report of drug supply chain disruptions listed by the FDA22 and ASHP23 between 1/1/2017 and 12/31/2020, and amounts of medications that VHA ordered and received, for medications used in the outpatient management of chronic conditions. To identify medications with supply chain disruptions, we used reports from both FDA and ASHP, as each organization's reports may vary due to differences in their definition of supply chain disruptions, reporting mechanism, and other factors.22, 23 We utilized the first date of the supply chain disruption reported by either FDA or ASHP as the index date. All data were aggregated at the medication-formulation level (e.g., valsartan tablet) because supply chain disruptions can affect one dosage form but not another of the same medication. The VHA Pharmaceutical Prime Vendor (PPV) (i.e., contract wholesaler) database provided information, by date, on the amount of medication ordered by each VHA pharmacy and subsequently filled (i.e., invoiced) by the contract wholesaler. Of note, PPV data include purchases for patients in all settings, but the majority of purchases for the medications included herein were for outpatient prescriptions. The project was approved by IRBs at the Edward Hines, Jr. VA Hospital and the VA Pittsburgh Healthcare System.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria for drug supply chain disruptions

Using the list of drugs with FDA- or ASHP-reported supply chain disruptions between 1/1/2017 and 12/31/2020, two of the authors (SLA and KJS) selected those medications used to treat outpatients with chronic conditions. The same medication could be included more than once if different dosage forms (e.g., oral solution and capsule) had supply chain disruptions. We excluded medications with <3 months of PPV data before and/or after the supply chain disruption reporting date (i.e., index date) because there was not enough information to identify a drug shortage to VHA (defined below). Medications with <10% of ordered doses filled in the 3–6 months prior to the reporting date were excluded because the supply chain disruption appeared to have already converted into a shortage experienced by VHA.

Definition of drug shortage to VHA

For each drug supply chain disruption included, we defined a variable to indicate whether VHA experienced a drug shortage, conceptually defined as a clinically important decrease in the amount of these medications that VHA was able to obtain from its wholesaler. Because there is no widely accepted or thoroughly validated definition, we defined a drug shortage as either 1) a ≥ 10% absolute decrease in the percentage of doses ordered across VHA pharmacies that were filled by the wholesaler, when comparing the average of 3 months after the index date with the average of 6 months before the index date, or 2) a ≥ 10% relative decrease in the number of doses filled by the wholesaler, using the same time periods before and after the index date (See Supplemental Fig. 1 for a detailed example). This definition allows for incompletely filled orders (i.e., first criterion), but pharmacy procurement staff may also change the quantity ordered when they know a medication is unlikely to be available. If they decrease the amount ordered, a shortage would only be detected by a decrease in the number of doses filled by the wholesaler. Therefore, we also allowed shortages to be identified using the second criterion. For both criteria, we used ≥10% to define a shortage based on methods used by VHA Pharmacy Benefits Management Services (PBM) to monitor shortages of acute care medications (Personal Communication; Francesca Cunningham, Associate Chief Consultant, PBM, April 24, 2023). We also deemed this somewhat liberal definition appropriate for primary analyses because we wanted to guard against overlooking any potentially important decreases in supply. As described below, we conducted sensitivity analyses using several more conservative definitions.

To further contextualize drug supply chain disruptions that resulted versus did not result in a drug shortage to VHA by our definition, we also examined data on medications ordered by VHA and filled by the wholesaler in the 12 months before and 12 months after the supply chain disruption reporting date. For each drug in each month, we examined the dosage units ordered, dosage units filled, and the proportion of dosage units filled divided by dosage units ordered.

Covariates for objective 2

We defined drug and supply chain characteristics to examine an association with the occurrence of a VHA drug shortage, by our definition. Characteristics were selected based on published studies, input from co-investigators, and availability of data.7, 24, 25 They included the reason for supply disruptions from FDA/ASHP (e.g., manufacturing delays, drug discontinued), availability of medication from alternative manufacturers without supply disruptions, availability of therapeutic equivalents, VHA formulary status (i.e., on the VHA national formulary or not), VHA drug class (e.g., cardiovascular), years since earliest FDA approval date, FDA essential medication status, availability of a generic version(s), single versus multiple manufacturers of the medication prior to the supply chain disruption reporting date, percentage of orders filled prior to the supply chain disruption reporting date, and whether the COVID-19 pandemic (3/11/2020–12/31/2020) overlapped the 6 months before or 3 months after the supply chain disruption reporting date.26

Statistical analysis

For the primary objective, we determined the proportion of reported supply chain disruptions for outpatient medications that met our definition of a shortage to VHA. We calculated descriptive statistics summarizing the amount of medication ordered and received by VHA, stratifying medications into two groups based on whether they met our definition of a shortage to VHA. We then used line plots to longitudinally depict the average of monthly measures of dosage units ordered, dosage units filled, and the proportion of dosage units ordered that were filled across medications to assess differences in patterns 12 months before and after the index date, categorized by whether the medication met our definition of a shortage to VHA. The number of monthly units ordered and filled were divided by units ordered from the earliest month (−6 to −12) available for each medication to standardize the data. We standardized the data because the magnitude of the average monthly ordering over the study time frame varied widely across drugs. Data were censored when the supply chain disruption ended, one year after the index date, or the end of the study period, whichever came first.

To further support the descriptive findings, we used interrupted time series (ITS) analyses to formally test for changes in the three continuous measures of doses ordered, doses filled, and proportion of ordered doses that were filled during the 12 months before versus after the index date, categorized by whether the medication met our definition of a shortage to VHA. Ordinary least squares regression models were used to estimate the level change at the supply chain disruption reporting date and the slope changes before and after the supply chain disruption reporting date.27 We tested higher-order temporal terms in each model by adding a quadratic term for the pre-supply chain disruption reporting date slope and the change in post-reporting date slope from the pre-reporting date slope. The quadratic term was included in any models where it was significant. Based on the Durbin-Watson statistic,27 there was no autocorrelation in the final models.

For the secondary objective, we evaluated the association between drug shortage characteristics and the outcome of a drug shortage to VHA using chi-square/Fisher Exact-test for categorical variables and Student-t-test for the continuous variable. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Sensitivity analyses

We performed three sensitivity analyses that varied the number of months after the index date and/or the percentage decrease used in the definition of a drug shortage to VHA, to assess the effect on the number of supply chain disruptions that were deemed as resulting in a drug shortage to VHA informed by prior work.5, 28, 29 These included: 1) using ≥33% rather than ≥10% as the threshold for the absolute decrease in proportion of ordered doses that were received, or the relative decrease in doses received, to qualify as a VHA drug shortage, 2) using six months after the index date (instead of three months) as the measurement period for determining VHA shortages and 3) simultaneously using both a ≥ 33% threshold and 6-month measurement period.

Trend in monthly dosage units ordered, dosage units filled, and the proportion of dosage units filled divided by dosage units ordered

Consistent with the descriptive patterns above, for the medications meeting our definition of a shortage to VHA, there was an increasing trend (i.e., a positive, statistically significant slope estimate) in dosage units ordered and a corresponding decreasing trend (i.e., negative, statistically significant slope estimate) in dosage units filled/dosage units ordered prior to the index date, with no significant level change at the index date, suggesting that the effect of the supply chain disruption started before the supply chain disruption reporting date versus at the time of the report. For medications not meeting our definition of a shortage to VHA, there was also an increasing trend in dosage units ordered prior to the reporting date, but a large and statistically significant positive level change in both dosage units filled and dosage units filled/dosage units ordered at the reporting date, which is consistent with no shortage occurring in VHA.

Sensitivity analyses

Using a ≥ 33% decrease in the definition (versus 10%), 28 (43.8%) medications had a shortage to VHA (Supplemental Table 1). The results were essentially unchanged when 6 months versus 3 months of data were used after the index date. Combining the ≥33% decrease and 6-month timeframe in the definition, 20 (31.3%) drugs had a shortage. In the sensitivity analysis of trends in monthly dosage units ordered, dosage units filled, and the proportion of dosage units filled divided by dosage units ordered, using the ≥33% decrease and 6-month timeframe in the definition of a medication shortage to VHA, the results were similar to those seen in the primary analyses (Supplemental Figs. 3a and 3b). However, the peaks were slightly higher, and the troughs were somewhat lower, in the sensitivity analysis of medications with a shortage to VHA. For medications that did not meet our definition of a shortage to VHA, the monthly values were slightly lower for most time points and fluctuated less in the sensitivity analyses.

Discussion

The results of this descriptive study address an important gap in literature and provide information on the possible use of medication ordering and filling data to identify potential drug shortages before supply chain disruptions are reported by FDA/ASHP. Among medications for chronic conditions in Veterans with reported supply chain disruptions, we found about two-thirds resulted in a shortage to VHA between 1/1/2017 and 12/31/2020 based on our definition. Interestingly, for those medications with a shortage, dosage units ordered by VHA pharmacy started to sharply increase three months before the supply chain disruption reporting date. Dosage units filled by the wholesaler gradually trended downward until the supply chain disruption reporting date. Then, they dropped more sharply for the next 2 months before starting to recover. In contrast, for medications that did not meet our definition of a shortage, dosage units ordered by pharmacy never spiked prior to the supply chain disruption reporting date, and dosage units filled by the wholesaler remained relatively stable until an increase in the month after the index date. Although a supply chain disruption may have started prior to the reporting date, this suggests a possible signal, or early alert, of a developing drug shortage and may be generalizable to non-VA health-systems. Nationally, the next step is to use PPV data close to real-time to see if the medications that go on to develop a shortage in VHA can be identified in advance of the FDA/ASHP report.

In our study, the percentage of supply chain disruptions that resulted in a drug shortage to VHA (67%) was higher than the result reported by Callaway Kim and colleagues.5 They found that 13.7% of FDA/ASHP reported supply chain disruptions for prescription medications purchased for US outpatients and inpatients between 2017 and 2021 resulted in drug shortages. They defined a shortage as a ≥ 33% decrease in units invoiced (i.e., filled) in the six months after the reported supply chain disruption versus the three months before.5 When we used their definition, 20% of the medications in our study would have met the definition of a shortage.5 Thus, the definition greatly influences the number of shortages identified. There is no universally accepted definition of a clinically important decrease in supply, meaning one that results in a medication shortage to pharmacies and that could negatively affect patient care. In the absence of a gold-standard, we used the definition employed by our health system. More research is needed to develop a meaningful definition that has validity in terms of predicting negative impacts on patient care or patient outcomes - a direction of our future work.

As noted, dosage units ordered increased sharply three months before a supply chain disruption was reported for those medications having a VHA shortage. A potential explanation is that local VHA pharmacy procurement staff noticed they were receiving increasingly less medication from the wholesaler and anticipated future difficulty in order fulfillment. As a result, they increased the number of units ordered hoping the wholesaler would also increase the units filled (i.e., in proportion to what was ordered). The increased ordering peaked at the drug supply chain disruption reporting date and then, trended down. At this point, local pharmacies may have learned through the wholesaler that only an allotted amount of medication would be shipped to them each month, or the FDA and/or ASHP reports indicated the manufacturer did not know when supply would return to previous levels.

Tracking pharmacy ordering and wholesaler filling data may be a useful approach for health-systems to identify shortages because none of the drug or supply chain characteristics we examined were associated with a medication shortage to VHA, which limits our ability to develop potential mitigation strategies. This could be related to the small number of medications in the study (i.e., a power issue) or the use of a homogenous cohort of drugs (i.e., mostly oral medications to treat chronic conditions in outpatients). However, manufacturing delays and discontinuations were numerically more common among the medications meeting our shortage definition. The study by Callaway Kim et al. found that non-parenteral formulations (versus parenteral), brand (versus generic), and World Health Organization non-essential medicine (versus essential) were associated with increased adjusted odds of a drug shortage.5 There are numerous factors involved in supply chain disruptions (e.g., problems affecting supply of active pharmaceutical ingredients, regulations, financial/business decisions),3, 26 but more information is needed earlier for VA and in the private sector to help discern variables associated with a developing drug shortage.

Our results reinforce prior findings that not all reported supply chain disruptions result in a drug shortage.4, 5 Pharmacy staff at individual VA Medical Centers and Consolidated Mail Outpatient Pharmacies may take steps to mitigate the effect of supply disruptions on filling patient prescriptions. For example, local pharmacy procurement personnel may purchase the medication in advance, borrow from another facility, or purchase a therapeutic alternative, if possible. In addition to identifying other sources or alternatives for a drug, prescriptions may be limited to a 30-day supply to help assure continuity among current users. These strategies can extend supply until more medication becomes available.

Our study has limitations. First, we had limited power to detect factors associated with a drug shortage to VHA due to the small number of medications. Second, PPV ordering and filling data include purchases for patients in all settings; we were not able to exclude those for inpatients. However, the bulk of the medications included are purchased for outpatient use. Third, the changes in units ordered by pharmacy and filled by the wholesaler reflect the average for medications with and without shortages by our definition; the pattern for individual medications may vary. However, ordering and filling data could still be used to identify potentially problematic drugs. Fourth, the PPV data are multiple years old, but would likely yield similar results as supply chain disruptions have not appreciably improved. Finally, we do not know if the identified shortages impacted Veteran access to medications or clinical outcomes. These are important areas for study that are currently underway.

Conclusion

Among ambulatory-focused medications for chronic conditions, a high proportion of those with reported supply chain disruptions resulted in shortages to VHA. In a possible signal, the amount of medication ordered sharply increased while the units filled gradually decreased before supply chain disruptions were reported. More information and additional efforts are needed to recognize and mitigate the impact of drug shortages in the ambulatory care population.
