“It’s All My Fault”: A Qualitative Case Study of Self-Disgust in Women with Gynecological Malignancies
Authors: Rong-Hui Wang, Qi Tang, Zhi-Yuan Wang, Na Li, Xin-Ru Liu, Long-Hui Xu, Bei-Bei Dai, Cui-Ping Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo explore the psychological experience of self-disgust among patients with gynecological malignancies, providing a basis for developing targeted psychological care plans.MethodsUsing purposive sampling, a descriptive qualitative study was conducted with 15 patients with gynecological malignancies admitted to the gynecology departments of two hospitals in Shandong Province, China, between May and August 2025. Semi-structured interviews were conducted, and the interview data were analyzed using thematic analysis.ResultsPatients’ psychological experiences of self-disgust can be categorized into four themes: impaired body image and functional loss (decompensated excretory functions, treatment-related physical image alterations); dual roots of negative self-perception (internalization of disease stigma, delayed medical care due to lack of health knowledge); Impaired psychological resilience under dual pressures (sexual function anxiety in intimate relationships, difficulties in family role transitions, experiences of social alienation); High levels of psychological distress caused by treatment side effects (loss of reproductive organs and fertility meaning, fear of disease progression and recurrence).ConclusionGynecologic malignancies patients commonly experience multidimensional self-disgust, closely linked to body image, negative self-perception, social functioning, and psychological distress. Healthcare providers should address patients’ negative self-perception through strategies including positive psychological interventions, stigma reduction, enhanced social support, and fertility counseling to improve psychological well-being and quality of life. Given the single-region sample and qualitative research design, large-scale multicenter longitudinal studies are warranted to explore how self-disgust evolves over time and across diverse cultural contexts.

Introduction

Gynecological malignancies are among the most common cancers affecting women, primarily including cervical cancer, ovarian cancer, endometrial cancer, and vulvar cancer. According to data from the International Agency for Research on Cancer,1 approximately 1.47 million new cases of malignant gynecological tumors were reported globally in 2022, Currently, the early diagnosis rate for gynecological malignancies remains relatively low. The average age at diagnosis for cervical cancer is 53 years, with an average age at death of 59 years, though variations exist across countries and regions.2 Additionally, approximately 50% of ovarian cancer cases occur in women over the age of 65,3 while the average age at diagnosis for endometrial cancer is 61 years.4 Treatment primarily involves surgery and radiotherapy/chemotherapy, often in combined modalities. Treatment often involves surgery and radiotherapy/chemotherapy, leading to altered body image, impaired sexual function, and multiple symptom burdens, which may contribute to self-disgust.5

Self-disgust is a persistent or recurrent feeling of disgust toward aspects of oneself, manifesting as cognitive and behavioral avoidance.6 Essentially, it is a more profound and destructive negative emotion, which will have a continuous negative impact on the psychological health, treatment compliance, and social function recovery of patients with gynecological tumors. Internalized stigma is a cognitive process in which an individual transforms external negative stereotypes into self-deprecation. Its essence is the internalization of social evaluation.7 The mechanisms of self-loathing and internalized stigma can be analyzed from two aspects: the individual’s internal factors and external social factors. At the individual level, after being diagnosed, patients undergo treatments such as surgery and radiotherapy, and generally face multiple psychological challenges including physiological changes, fear of recurrence, uncertainty about prognosis, and distortion of self-image. The accumulation of negative emotions can easily lead to an overall rejection and aversion towards oneself.8 The structural and functional changes in the sexual organs caused by surgical treatment often disrupt the marital relationship and intimate life of patients. The sexual function impairment is more severe and irreversible compared to that of patients with common gynecological diseases. It not only reduces the quality of life but also triggers deep psychological distress.9 For patients who have not yet completed childbearing, the loss of fertility further exacerbates anxiety regarding their self-worth and identity.10 These physiological functional changes and disruptions to self-image continuously erode patients’ self-evaluation systems, generating a series of negative emotions such as helplessness and shame, and may ultimately evolve into disgust toward the self.11 At the societal level, misunderstandings about such diseases (such as mistakenly associating them with sexually transmitted diseases) have exacerbated the sense of internalized stigma. This not only deepens the patients’ self-loathing but also may lead to avoidance of behavior and resistance to treatment, hindering recovery and social reintegration.12

Current research on self-disgust is mostly quantitative; qualitative methods are limited. Changes in body shape, abnormalities in urination and defecation, and loss of fertility have been confirmed as the main physical reasons for self-hatred.13 However, In the Chinese cultural context, topics such as sexual organs, fertility, and marital relationships are often taboo, making qualitative exploration essential. Guided by cognitive behavioral theory, this study uses a descriptive qualitative design to explore the emergence of self-disgust and provide a basis for culturally sensitive psychosocial interventions.

Theoretical Framework

Guided by cognitive behavioral theory, this study adopts the interaction among the individual, behavior, and environment as its core analytical perspective. This theory posits that individuals’ emotional experiences do not arise in isolation but are shaped and reinforced through continuous interaction with the external environment.14 For patients with gynecologic malignancies, the formation of self-disgust involves not only their negative evaluations of their own bodies and self-worth but is also closely related to social behavioral responses and profoundly influenced by environmental factors such as family, cultural norms, and public opinion. Therefore, employing this theoretical framework, this study utilizes a descriptive qualitative research design and conducts in-depth interviews to explore the context in which patients’ self-disgust emerges. The CBT “cognition-emotion-behavior” framework guided how we asked interview questions and initially grouped codes; Inductive process: Open coding, grouping codes, and extracting themes were completely guided by the data.Through conversational and situational analysis, this study aims to authentically capture the internal perspectives of patients with gynecologic malignancies and further elucidate the contributing factors to their self-disgust, thereby providing a theoretical basis for developing psychosocial interventions that are more imbued with humanistic care and cultural sensitivity.

Method

Design

This study employed a qualitative descriptive research approach to gain a comprehensive understanding of the true thoughts and feelings of self-disgust in patients with gynecological malignancies. Qualitative descriptive research provides a naturalistic and pragmatic method, aiming to produce a clear and comprehensive summary of individuals’ experiences using their own language and everyday terms.15 This approach maintains proximity to the data, focusing on the surface meanings of language and events, and allows researchers to describe a phenomenon as it is, without imposing pre-existing theories or testing hypotheses. It is particularly valuable for addressing “what” questions and generating rich, context-based descriptions of underexplored topics.16 Therefore, this methodology was selected for the present study. This manuscript strictly complies with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) throughout data collection, analysis and manuscript writing, and all SRQR core reporting items are fully covered in Methods and Results sections.

Setting and Participants

Using purposive sampling, we selected patients with gynecological malignant tumors who were hospitalized at two hospitals in Shandong Province from May to August 2025 as the study subjects. The inclusion criteria were: meeting the diagnostic standards outlined in the expert consensus on the diagnosis and treatment of common gynecological malignancies; being pathologically diagnosed with a gynecological malignancy (including ovarian cancer, cervical cancer, endometrial cancer, and vulvar cancer), at either initial diagnosis or recurrence; and being hospitalized for treatment; aged 18–80 years; receiving treatments such as surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy at the hospital; being aware of their own condition; and providing informed consent to voluntarily participate in the study. Exclusion criteria included: having psychiatric disorders or cognitive impairments; having communication barriers, such as deafness or aphasia; having concomitant severe diseases or other cancers; or simultaneous participation in similar studies.

Instruments

A descriptive qualitative study was conducted with the aim of depicting the real-world situation and attempting to present the experiences of patients with gynecological malignancies without interpretation. Relevant literature was reviewed based on the research objectives, and a preliminary interview outline was developed using cognitive behavioral theory as the framework. To test the applicability of the outline, one patient with ovarian cancer and one with cervical cancer were selected for pre-interviews. These pre-interviews not only helped familiarize the participants with the research process but also allowed the researchers to assess the clarity and guiding effectiveness of the questions, while gaining initial insights into potential manifestations of self-disgust. Taking into consideration feedback from the interviewees and consultation suggestions from six experts (including three chief physicians specializing in gynecologic oncology, two gynecologic nursing specialists, and one psychology expert), the research team held discussions to revise and finalize the formal interview guide.

The researcher initiated the interview with the open-ended question, “Could you please share your experience of being diagnosed with the illness?” This approach aimed to establish an open conversational space, enabling participants to autonomously narrate the core events and experiences of personal significance throughout their illness journey, thereby minimizing the potential influence of the study’s presupposed concepts on the direction of participants’ narratives. To improve the transparency of our research, we added the following questions, eg., “When you feel uncomfortable about your body changes, what thoughts come to your mind?”, “How do these feelings affect your daily communication with your family?”To systematically explore the psychological experience of self-disgust in patients, this study employed a progressive probing strategy based on cognitive behavioral theory during the interviews.17 The researcher did not pose direct questions related to disgust. Instead, following the logical sequence of “cognition-emotion-behavior” inherent in cognitive behavioral theory, progressive probing was initiated when participants mentioned narrative threads related to physical symptoms, changes in social interaction, or self-evaluation. This strategy aimed to guide participants from describing external facts towards reflecting on their self-relationship, allowing them to naturally articulate their internal perceptions of their own bodies or identity. Through this iterative probing process, while avoiding directive intervention, the researcher effectively captured participants’ embodied emotional experiences characterized by repulsion towards the self. Data Collection

Prior to the commencement of interviews, researchers explicitly assured participants that all their responses would be subject to strict confidentiality and anonymization.The interviewers are two graduate students in nursing, both of whom have received qualitative interview training and have 2 years of clinical experience in gynecologic oncology. All demographic data, interview recordings, and text transcripts were de-identified using pseudonyms to maximize the protection of participant identity. All interviews were conducted 2–4 weeks after the patients completed their main surgery, during their hospital recovery period, to avoid the acute shock phase right after the initial diagnosis.To minimize psychological pressure on participants, interviews were conducted in a private, secure, and undisturbed space to fully ensure their privacy and emotional safety. The interviewer continuously monitored participants’ emotional states throughout the process. If a participant exhibited heightened levels of negative emotion—such as when discussing sensitive topics like death—the interview would be paused or terminated as appropriate. The unit nurse manager would be promptly informed, and a referral to psychological specialists for support would be arranged when necessary. Participants were clearly informed that they could unconditionally withdraw from the study at any time, particularly if they felt discomfort, and that audio recording would only proceed after obtaining their informed consent.

The sample size for this study was determined based on the principle of data saturation; data collection ceased when additional interviews no longer yielded new themes or insights. Data saturation was achieved after 13 participants had been interviewed. Two additional interviews were then conducted to confirm that no new information emerged and to validate the robustness of the identified themes. Each interview lasted between 30 to 45 minutes to fully understand participants’ genuine thoughts and feelings.

Data Analysis

Data analysis commenced immediately following the first interview and strictly adhered to the requirement of completing verbatim transcription within 24 hours after each interview. To ensure data accuracy and reliability, the transcripts of each recording were jointly reviewed and verified by the first and second authors, who implemented necessary revisions. During the analysis process, NVivo 12.0 software was employed to assist with data categorization and coding; any discrepancies arising during transcription and analysis were resolved through consultation with a third researcher.

Methodologically, the study adopted the inductive thematic analysis approach proposed by Braun & Clarke.18 Initially, researchers repeatedly read the transcripts to gain an in-depth understanding of the data, taking notes to deepen familiarity. As new data emerged, researchers generated and refined initial codes by extracting keywords, statements, and emotional expressions related to self-disgust, resulting in 42 initial codes. Subsequently, these initial codes were organized and categorized, with duplicates removed and similar codes merged, forming 18 secondary codes. The research team convened weekly analysis meetings to discuss discrepancies in coding categorization and theme identification, with a third researcher who was not involved in frontline interviews serving as an arbitrator to ensure consistency and accuracy. Similar codes were then grouped into distinct categories and further organized into potential themes. Researchers examined the relevance of these themes to the codes and the entire dataset, subsequently defining, naming, and discussing themes among authors. Through this collaborative process, four core themes and nine sub-themes were ultimately identified. Co-authors critically reviewed the analysis and provided constructive feedback, based on which categories within themes were revised and consensus reached through collaborative discussion.; CBT was used for interpreting and discussing final themes, rather than constraining initial coding.

As the study was conducted in China, all interviews were carried out in Mandarin. To preserve the authenticity of participant expressions, transcription and preliminary data analysis were performed in Chinese. Findings were translated into English only after the data analysis was completed. To this end, the study employed a rigorous translation and back-translation procedure: first, the themes, sub-themes, and illustrative quotations were translated into English by the third author, who is proficient in both Chinese and English and has overseas research experience; subsequently, the English version was independently back-translated into Chinese by the first author, who is also bilingual. The research team carefully compared the two versions to identify and resolve discrepancies, ensuring that the final English translation accurately conveyed the nuances and conceptual connotations of the original Chinese data. The entire team, including the last author, collaboratively reviewed the translations, resolving any ambiguities through consensus-based discussion.

Furthermore, to enhance the rigor of the analysis and ensure the credibility of the findings, a member checking procedure was implemented: participants were provided with a Chinese summary containing the identified themes, sub-themes, and relevant interview quotations, enabling them to clarify, confirm, or elaborate on the interpretations as needed.

Ethics

The study protocol was approved by the Shandong Provincial Qianfoshan Hospital (YXLL-KY-2025(078)). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment, in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Rigor

The criteria established by Guba and Lincoln, encompassing credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability,19 were adopted to ensure the trustworthiness of this study. Prior to analysis, the authors immersed themselves in the data and actively sought participant feedback on the findings and interpretations, ensuring the results accurately reflected participant perspectives. Transferability was further enhanced by collecting rich, saturated data and through ongoing discussions among the co-authors, which contributed to a deeper understanding of the findings. They also received extensive training in qualitative research methods, possessing the necessary skills and knowledge to conduct reliable research and maintain the validity of the outcomes. Three of the authors are clinical practitioners and researchers with substantial nursing experience, while the other two are university scholars, collectively providing integrated expertise from both clinical practice and theoretical perspectives for this study. To enhance confirmability, a foreign professor of nursing reviewed the data, and the coding and categories were adjusted based on her feedback.

To minimize the potential influence of the researcher’s dual role as both the primary nurse and the interviewer on the study findings, three methodological strategies were implemented. Given that the researcher had established a therapeutic rapport with patients during clinical care, there was a risk that participants might provide responses they perceived as desirable to their nurse or withhold information on sensitive topics. To address this, at the beginning of each interview, the researcher explicitly clarified her role as an academic investigator, emphasizing that the interview was independent of clinical care and that all responses would be anonymized and used solely for research purposes, thereby alleviating participant concerns. Throughout the data collection process, the researcher maintained a reflective journal to monitor her own positioning and adjust interviewing techniques as needed. Regular debriefing sessions were also conducted with the research team, including members not involved in clinical work, who provided external perspectives to identify potential blind spots. These combined measures enhanced the researcher’s awareness of her potential impact on the data, reduced bias, and ultimately strengthened the credibility and trustworthiness of the study.

Theme 1: Impaired Body Image and Functional Loss

Excretory Function Decompensation

The primary treatment for gynecological malignancies is radical surgery. Due to the complexity of human anatomy, there is a risk of iatrogenic urinary system injury. Some patients experience various postoperative complications, such as abnormal vaginal discharge and urinary incontinence. These complications not only cause physiological suffering but also, because they involve loss of excretory control and difficulties in managing body odor, trigger strong feelings of shame and self-objectification disgust.

Treatment-Related Somatic Changes

Due to the effects of treatments such as chemotherapy, patients with gynecological malignancies are highly susceptible to symptoms of body image disturbance like alopecia, constipation, and emaciation, leading to self-repulsion.

Theme 2: Dual Roots of Patients’ Negative Self-Perception

Internalization of Disease Stigma

Public stigma persists regarding gynecological malignancies, often attributing the etiology to promiscuous sexual behavior and considering it contagious. This stigmatization seriously threatens patients’ social image, triggers negative emotions and self-deprecation, and reinforces self-disgust.

Delayed Medical Help-Seeking Due to Lack of Knowledge

Many patients with gynecological malignancies lack sufficient knowledge of early clinical manifestations of the cancer, easily misattributing symptoms like loss of appetite and gastrointestinal discomfort to digestive system issues, thus leading to delays in seeking medical care. Patients often attribute this misjudgment of their health status and missed intervention timing to their own failings, generating self-blame. As the disease progressively develops, such self-blame often evolves into profound regret and may ultimately solidify into persistent disgust directed at their own bodies.

Theme 3: Impaired Psychological Resilience Under Dual Pressures

Sexual Function Anxiety in Intimate Relationships

Some patients who undergo radical surgery for gynecological malignancies experience damage to the integrity of their sexual organs, leading to impaired sexual function. This often results in worry and fear regarding intimate relationships. This anxiety stems not only from potential self-disgust towards their body image and function but also includes concerns about their partner’s reaction and potential social biases.

P15: My husband has become very cautious, taking great care of me in daily life, but I feel he doesn’t dare touch me much, and speaks very carefully (wry smile), as if afraid of saying the wrong thing and upsetting me.

Difficulties in Family Role Transition

Patients with gynecological malignancies often hold specific functional roles within the family and society. However, due to hospitalization and surgical treatment needs, they must temporarily or permanently withdraw from these original roles. Some patients struggle to completely disengage, experiencing significant adaptation difficulties and role conflict during the transition from their social role to the patient role. This conflict is often accompanied by strong negative self-evaluation, manifesting as guilt and self-disgust.

Theme 4: High Levels of Psychological Distress Due to Treatment Side Effects

Loss of Reproductive Organs and the Meaning of Fertility

Young women often face societal and familial expectations regarding childbearing. However, radical treatment for gynecological malignancies often requires the removal of reproductive organs like the uterus. This not only causes permanent damage to the patient’s female physiological integrity but also results in the complete loss of reproductive function. This dual loss not only directly impacts sexual function and fertility but also profoundly impacts the patient’s identity in multiple roles, such as partner and mother. Consequently, psychological distress permeates the entire process of treatment and rehabilitation for gynecological malignancy patients, and their experience of self-disgust is often more intense.

Discussion

Cultural Beliefs Affect Patients’ Feelings of Self-Loathing

Within the traditional cultural context profoundly influenced by Confucian thought, female identity is often closely intertwined with women’s multiple roles within the family, particularly their roles as wives and as mothers bearing the responsibility of continuing the family line.20 Simultaneously, bodily integrity is closely associated with societal standards of moral judgment.21 For the interviewees in this study, gynecologic malignancy represents not only a physiological health threat but also affects social status, marriage and filial piety. This cultural perspective helps explain why internalized stigma towards illness is internalized and why there is sexual anxiety (P14, P15).This reflect societal moral scrutiny of female sexuality. Likewise, the profound distress resulting from the loss of fertility (P10, P11) is significantly amplified by cultural expectations rooted in traditional notions of lineage continuation. Self-disgust among patients with gynecologic malignancies arises not only from individual psychological responses to bodily changes but is also influenced by patients’ fear of diminished familial and social value. Clarifying this cultural embeddedness holds significant theoretical and practical implications for assessing the transferability of these findings to other populations and for developing culturally sensitive clinical intervention protocols.

Self-Loathing, Impaired Body Image, Internalized Stigma, and Multiple Impairments in Social Functioning

The findings of this study demonstrate that self-disgust is prevalent among patients with gynecological malignancies. It is primarily manifested as impaired body image, internalized stigma, social dysfunction, and obvious psychological distress. Previous studies have reported that self-hatred is influenced by self-efficacy and internalized stigma.7,22 However, our findings further reveal that a lack of social support and poor psychological resilience can significantly exacerbate this kind of suffering.This discrepancy may stem from the specificity of the study population. As gynecological malignancy patients are exclusively female, and existing evidence suggests that anxiety and depressive symptoms generally progress faster in women than in males,23 they also rely more on social support to buffer stress. Once social support is insufficient, they are prone to self-deprecation and self-loathing.

Specifically, body image disturbance represents a core issue closely associated with self-disgust, aligning with the findings of Clarke’s study.12 The patient experiences a strong sense of self-rejection regarding the specific physical changes. For instance, P12’s admission, “I find it disgusting myself,” in reference to urinary incontinence, and P7 described herself after chemotherapy as being like a “strange monster”. Research has demonstrated that body image dissatisfaction following chemotherapy can readily lead to reduced sleep quality, significant psychological distress, and may provoke feelings of inferiority in daily life, thereby exacerbating self-disgust.24 Medical staff should pay attention to the physical sensations of patients. They can try positive psychological interventions such as mindfulness and cognitive restructuring to help patients deal with the automatic negative thoughts that arise, and gradually establish a more accepting self-awareness.Through guided positive thinking, perspective-shifting, and exposure to positive role models, it is possible to diminish patients’ negative self-perceptions and automatic thoughts, thereby helping them construct a positive self-concept.25

Internalized stigma related to illness is also an important factor contributing to self-loathing, consistent with Jin’s study.7 For example, on P14,”I have only ever been with my husband! How could I get something like that?”,This statement reveals that moral condemnation can intensify self-loathing. Also, P5 and P14 blamed themselves for the delay in seeing a doctor and kept repeating, “It’s all my fault.” Studies have shown that higher levels of self-disgust are associated with greater perceived stigma.11 Intense stigma can undermine patients’ confidence in treatment and recovery, diminish their initiative and capacity to cope with the disease, and even adversely affect prognosis.26 Therefore, when assessing stigma, healthcare providers should consider first evaluating the degree of self-disgust. For individuals exhibiting pronounced self-disgust, interventions such as high-arousal emotion induction27 and enhanced self-compassion training28 may be implemented to mitigate such feelings. Furthermore, by conducting online scientific education and community lectures to correct the public’s misunderstandings about gynecological tumors, prejudice can be reduced and patients can be prevented from delaying treatment due to self-blame.

This study further reveals that self-disgust in patients with gynecological malignancies significantly damages their social functioning, a distinction from previous research.29 The research revealed that, in Chinese culture, the value of women is often closely tied to their family roles, such as wife or daughter. This moral restraint can cause patients to be overly concerned about becoming a “burden” to their families when they are ill, leading them to actively distance themselves from their relatives and friends. Eventually, this can result in more complex psychological problems and seriously impair their social functions. As an important external resource, social support plays a positive role in enhancing patients’ social functioning and rebuilding social relationships.29,30 Therefore, medical staff should take into account the characteristic of Chinese people that “family comes first”, and help the family members create a supportive family environment.

Specific Fertility Anxiety and Recurrence Fear Among Young Patients

We also found that the psychological distress of young patients with gynecological tumors is rather complex, distinct from previous research findings,31 and this study shows that this kind of psychological distress is related to self-loathing. Psychological distress is an unpleasant emotional experience encompassing psychological, social, and spiritual dimensions, which can evolve from common negative emotions into psychological crises such as anxiety, fear, and depression.32 It mainly stems from two core pressures - the loss of reproductive function and the fear of disease recurrence. For young patients, they not only have to endure the immense pain caused by cancer treatment, but also have to accept the objective fact of impaired fertility. This double blow is far more than ordinary sadness or anxiety. This kind of pain continuously permeates their daily lives, further intensifying their self-loathing and seriously damaging their mental health and quality of life.Regarding the issue of fertility, medical institutions should establish a referral pathway for fertility preservation, enabling patients to obtain professional consultation in a timely manner. Internet-based psychoeducational interventions can provide timely professional fertility preservation counseling and personalized plans, offering substantial support.33

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although patients were recruited from two hospitals to mitigate single-center bias, the use of purposive sampling and the modest sample size limit generalizability. Participants were enrolled from two hospitals in Shandong Province only, which may lead to selection bias. Subsequent research should extend the recruitment cycle and adopt large-sample, multi-center designs to enrich the evidence base. Second, despite the researchers’ efforts to build rapport and emphasize confidentiality to encourage open disclosure, the interviewer’s dual role as the patients’ primary nurse may have to some extent influenced participants’ openness when expressing strongly negative or private emotions. Third, the cross-sectional design captures only a snapshot of psychological experiences at a specific treatment stage, precluding analysis of how self-disgust may evolve across the disease trajectory. Fourth, Recruitment from only two hospitals in Shandong, China, limits transferability to other cultural contexts. Specifically, the Confucian emphasis on family lineage and female purity may amplify self-disgust expression; therefore, findings may not directly apply to Western or other non-Confucian populations. Future research should employ longitudinal designs and include more geographically and culturally diverse samples.

Conclusion

This study reveals that self-disgust in patients with gynecological malignancies originates from treatment-related body image impairment and functional loss, which become internalized as stigma and undermine treatment confidence, with low psychological resilience and insufficient social support further intensifying this process—particularly among younger patients facing fertility loss and fear of recurrence. Clinically, interventions should target self-disgust through psychological support, fertility counseling, and family-social integration to improve body image and stigma-related distress, thereby facilitating recovery. However, the generalizability of these conclusions is constrained by the Confucian cultural setting, where female roles and bodily morality are uniquely constructed, potentially limiting applicability to other cultural groups; hence, cross-cultural longitudinal studies are needed for further validation.
