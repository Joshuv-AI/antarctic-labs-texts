Summary of present design of discrete choice experiments for patient preferences in lung cancer based on the ISPOR ESTIMATE checklist
Authors: Takehiro Hirai, Yoshiharu Horie, Tetsuhisa Kitamura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung cancer is one of the most common cancers worldwide and is associated with a high mortality rate. Addressing unmet medical needs in lung cancer treatments from the perspective of patient preferences has become an important research focus. Discrete Choice Experiments (DCEs) are commonly used to assess patient preferences, therefore it is essential to interpret their analysis methods and results appropriately. This scoping review was intended to examine DCE studies on patient preferences for lung cancer treatments published after the release of the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) ESTIMATE checklist in 2016, and to identify challenges in applying the checklist.MethodsLiteratures meeting the eligibility criteria for DCE studies on patient preferences for lung cancer treatments were identified through PubMed and Cochrane Library searches and reviewed independently by two reviewers according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) Scoping Review extension. All literatures were reviewed and assessed using the ISPOR ESTIMATE checklist.ResultsTwelve DCE studies published between January 2017 and June 2022 were identified. All studies used efficacy and side effects as attributes while less than half studies used cost. Our assessment revealed that all studies completed the checklists of Interpretation, Method, and Assumptions domains. However, the checklists in Evaluation, Stochastic, and Trade-offs domains were not completed in 83.3%, 41.7%, and 33.3% of the 12 studies, respectively. This suggests that further consideration is needed to ensure the appropriateness of the statistical analysis and interpretation of the results of the DCE studies for lung cancer treatments.ConclusionsOur scoping review using the ISPOR ESTIMATE checklists found that the consideration points remained the focus of Evaluation, Stochastic, and Trade-offs in DCE studies on patient preferences for lung cancer treatments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15116-6.Keywords: Discrete choice experiment, Lung cancer, Patient preference, ISPOR ESTIMATE checklist, Scoping review

Background

Lung cancer had the second highest incidence worldwide in 2020, with an estimated 2.21 million new cases [1]. It was also the leading cause of cancer-related deaths, resulting in approximately 1.80 million deaths globally [1]. There are recently various treatment options for patients with lung cancer, such as chemotherapy, molecular targeted drugs, and immunotherapy, making patient preferences crucial in selecting a treatment. Since the approval of Osimertinib in the United States in November 2015, advancements in combination therapy and personalized medicine have diversified treatment options [2]. Those new treatments made patients select their own treatments based on their preferences. Addressing unmet medical needs in lung cancer treatments from the perspective of patient preferences has become an important research focus. We think justifying the choice of analysis method, describing the analysis, and interpreting the results in the literatures of patient preferences for lung cancer treatments are helpful for understanding unmet medical needs for future treatments in lung cancer.

An enhanced patient-centric approach is important to elicit responses that reveal the preferences, priorities, and relative importance of individual features associated with healthcare interventions or services. This study focused on quality-of-life measurements and patient healthcare records. However, patient preferences are only partially captured through these data sources, and insights derived from them may have limited accuracy in fully reflecting patient perspectives. Discrete choice experiment (DCE) is classified as a quantitative preference elicitation method using a stated-preference survey and was the most-cited method (n= 57, 27.4%) according to a systematic literature review [3]. DCE is a method used for understanding patient or physician preferences for treatments. DCE can quantify the importance placed by patients on various factors such as the effectiveness of the drug, side effects, route of administration, hospitalization requirements, and cost. Thereby, a desired treatment profile from the patient’s perspective can be created.

DCE has been used to elicit patient preferences in the healthcare context. In DCE studies, respondents are asked to choose among hypothetical alternatives described by systematically varying attribute levels, such as drug efficacy, severity of adverse effects, cost, or route of dosing. The identification and selection of attributes and levels are critical for obtaining valid results. Additionally, proper selection and description of preferences are required [4, 5]. By controlling the attribute levels experimentally and asking respondents to choose among sets of profiles in a series of choice questions, the impact of changes in attribute levels on choice can be quantitatively estimated, which reflects the strength of preference for changes in attribute levels [6].

Uncertainty surrounds good research practices for the statistical analysis of data from DCE permits. Therefore, the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) published two reports regarding the checklist for good DCE practice in 2011 and the ESTIMATE checklist for a good statistical analysis of DCE in 2016. The latter includes a list of questions to consider when justifying the choice of analysis method, describing the analysis, and interpreting the results, to understand the characteristics and appropriate analysis of preference in DCE studies, which are considered critical to conducting a well-designed DCE [6].

In a systematic review of DCE studies on cancer treatments [4, 5], the PREFS checklist was used for the quality assessment of DCE. The PREFS checklist assesses the quality of DCE in terms of five aspects of yes/no questions: Purpose, Respondents, Explanations, Findings, and Significance. The total score ranges from 0 to 5. The PREFS checklist was created by Joy SM and colleagues in 2013but not developed by the academic society [7]. On the hand, the ISPOR ESTIMATE checklist was created in 2016 with the aim of being applied to subsequent DCE studies based on DCE studies conducted before it. A particular area of concern was that the PREFS checklist missed several important criteria that should be reported in DCE studies [4, 5]. The systematic reviews using the PREFS checklist pointed out limitations, including study quality issues caused by methodological problems with data analysis, a lack of transparency in statistical analysis [5], and the omission of important criteria such as the analysis of preference heterogeneity [4].

Proper design and statistical analysis are essential in DCE studies to ensure reliable and valid results, which in turn support accurate interpretation and informed treatment decisions. Prior to the introduction of the ISPOR ESTIMATE checklist in 2016, no systematic guidelines existed for reporting statistical analyses in DCE research [6]. To date, key aspects such as design, statistical analysis, and interpretation in these studies have not been thoroughly assessed using the ISPOR ESTIMATE checklist. Therefore, the two independent reviewers conducted a scoping review of DCE studies on patient preferences for lung cancer treatments published between 2017 and 2022 to evaluate their analysis methods and reporting practices based on the checklist, and to identify challenges in applying the checklist [8]. In addition, collecting information on attributes and levels of cancer treatments across studies can provide valuable insights into the relative importance of specific attributes and levels to patients, such as efficacy, side effects, dosing route, and cost, as well as the study characteristics of sample size and the experimental design of choice sets, to better understand the context in which patient preferences for lung cancer treatments are evaluated.

Methods

Identification, screening, eligibility, and inclusion of literature

According to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [8], scoping review was conducted, and the PRISMA-ScR checklist was used to ensure quality (Supplementary Table 1). The protocol was not registered. The eligibility criteria, information sources and their evidence for selection, search strategy, data charting process, data items, and the assessment of literatures and its process were described in the main text and Fig. 1.

PRISMA flow diagram for the scoping review process

PubMed is one of the most widely accessible resources in the world and delivers a publicly available search interface for biomedical and life science articles, including MEDLINE and other resources, using MeSH vocabulary. Cochrane Library is a collection of evidence for healthcare decision-making and includes literatures cited in the evidence, including Embase. Two widely used databases, PubMed and the Cochrane Library, were searched up to June 2022 to ensure comprehensive coverage of literatures. The search terms used were developed using the following keywords: we considered some combinations of search terms and used the one that gave the most results. (”discrete choice experiment” and cancer) or (“conjoint analysis” and cancer) with filters of abstract applied including MeSH at the identification stage.

Next, at the screening stage, we selected literatures published from 2017 onwards. We reviewed research articles on patient preferences for cancer treatments identified in PubMed. In addition, we extracted research articles from the Cochrane Library for review. We included only literatures with abstracts that reported DCE studies on patient preferences for cancer treatments without adjuvant therapy, and we excluded studies that were not DCEs or focused on physician or caregiver preferences.

Then, at the eligibility stage, we excluded duplicate literatures and those not related to lung cancer. We included only the literature that focused lung cancer treatments. For scoping review, the two independent reviewers selected DCE literatures written in English, published between 2017 and 2020, that reported patient preferences for cancer treatments without adjuvant therapy in the abstract and focused on lung cancer.

The titles and abstracts of all retrieved publications were screened by one reviewer to remove non-eligible literature. Other independent reviewers reviewed this process. Two independent reviewers assessed the eligibility of the remaining publications through full-text examination. Any inconsistent assessments between reviewers after the selection process were discussed and addressed by consensus.

Data synthesis

We independently performed a qualitative analysis of the targeted studies. Then the independent assessments of the two researchers were harmonized as unified. The following study characteristics were extracted and summarized: publication year, country, fund, tumor type, sample size, sample size setting method, and survey method. In addition, DCE characteristics were collected and summarized, including choice set design, software used for choice set design, number of choice sets, number of blocks, number of dominant choice sets, number of choice sets per patient, number and type of attributes and levels, opt-out option, pilot, analysis model, and analysis software. Attribute types were threefold: outcome, process, and cost. Outcome attributes were subclassified according to efficacy, adverse effects, and quality of life. Process attributes were subclassified into mode of administration, waiting times, frequency of dosage, hospitalization, subsequent treatment options, and genetic test treatment delay. The synthesis focused on identifying patterns and methodological differences in the targeted studies.

Review and assessment of literature

Review and assessment were conducted according to the ESTIMATE checklist developed by ISPOR to identify challenges in applying the checklist [6]. This checklist covers eight domains relevant to quality evaluation in DCE studies from a statistical perspective, including Estimates, Stochastic, Trade-offs, Interpretation, Method, Assumptions, Transparent, Evaluation. Each domain has three questions. Quality assessment in a literature was performed using total 24 questions (three questions in each of the eight ESTIMATE domains) in the ESTIMATE checklist. The assessment score was based on the number of questions answered in the literature, with a minimum of 0 and a maximum of 24. Two reviewers independently performed quality assessments using the ESTIMATE checklist and discussed and addressed inconsistent assessments by consensus. A domain was considered clarified if both reviewers agreed that at least one question in the domain had been answered in the literature.

Results

Study selection

A total of 483 literatures were identified by searching PubMed, and 61 libraries were retrieved from the Cochrane Library (Fig. 1). Literatures on non-DCE and non-cancer treatments, such as screening tests, were excluded. As a result, 70 literatures from PubMed and 5 literatures from the Cochrane Library were extracted as DCE studies targeting patients with cancer between January 2017 and June 2022. After excluding two duplicates and including studies on lung cancer treatments, 12 DCE literatures on patient preferences for lung cancer treatments were finally included in the scoping review (Table 1).

DCE studies in lung cancer (n = 12)

Study characteristics of DCE studies

Twelve studies targeted patients with lung cancer from five countries (Table 2). Pharmaceutical companies funded six studies (50.0%). The median (min., max.) of the sample size was 196.5 (65, 466). The sample size was set using the Orme method in four studies (33.3%) while the method was not described in six studies (50.0%). The surveys were conducted through face-to-face interviews in four studies (33.3%) and web forms in three studies (25.0%). The choice set was developed using a D-efficient design in seven studies (58.3%), whereas the design was not described in three studies (25.0%). The median (min., max.) of the number of choice sets per respondent was 12 (10, 18). The dominant choice set for the reliability check of the respondents’ answers was included in the choice sets of three studies (25.0%). The opt-out option was used in only one study (8.3%). SAS, NGene, R, or Sawtooth software was used for the choice set design. The median (min., max.) number of attributes was 6 (5, 10), with the number of attribute levels ranging from 2 to 4. The attributes of outcomes related to efficacy and adverse effects were selected in all 12 studies, those of mode of administration in six studies (50.0%), those of cost in four studies (33.3%). In outcomes, progression-free survival (PFS) was the most frequently used attribute for treatment efficacy in seven studies (58.3%), followed by overall survival in four studies (33.3%). Fatigue or general malaise was the attribute most frequently associated with the adverse effects of the treatment in seven studies (58.3%), followed by nausea or vomiting in five studies (41.7%), and rash in five studies (41.7%). In all 12 studies, pilot study was conducted to optimize the number of attributes, type of attributes, number of attribute levels, and questionnaire description in the DCE survey was conducted. A mixed logit model was used for statistical analysis in six studies (50.0%). R, STATA, or SPSS software was used for the statistical analysis.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this scoping review is the first to systematically assess the quality of DCE studies investigating patient preferences for lung cancer treatments using the ISPOR ESTIMATE checklist, published in 2017. The ESTIMATE checklist, developed by the ISPOR task force, provides essential guidance for transparent reporting and robust justification of statistical methods in DCEs, facilitating accurate interpretation of study findings [6]. Consistent and comprehensive application of this checklist is fundamental to improving research rigor; remaining gaps highlight opportunities for future enhancement in DCE reporting. By summarizing study characteristics and key DCE parameters, our review offers a valuable reference for future investigations on patient preferences for lung cancer treatments.

We assessed 12 DCE studies as showing a lower quality in the Evaluation, the Stochastic, and the Trade-offs and high quality in the Interpretation, the Method, and the Assumptions to appropriately interpret patient preferences inferred from the model and ensure the appropriateness of the statistical analysis method.

In terms of the Evaluation, it is recommended that the goodness of fit of the statistical analysis model be demonstrated [6]. For that purpose, it is considered necessary to construct both models including and not including explanatory variables and describe why the model including exploratory variables fits the data better than the model not including exploratory variables using a likelihood ratio test or McFadden’s pseudo R-squared, because this shows that the statistical analysis model is constructed appropriately. Our review suggests the needs for DCE studies on patient preferences for lung cancer treatments to describe the goodness of fit of the model.

In terms of the Stochastic and Trade-offs, it is recommended to describe the appropriate relationship between different attribute levels [6]. To that end, it is considered necessary to describe the formula of a statistical analysis model and explain the assumption for the exploratory variables for all attribute levels from Stochastic point of view. The model formula indicates whether all attribute levels are estimated independently of each other, that is, the Trade-offs. If all attribute levels are estimated independently, we can interpret the results properly and understand that the estimated preference weight for the attribute level is preferred if it is more positive and less so if it is more negative. Our review suggests that the authors should explain the Trade-offs, such as the statistical analysis model. In the Trade-offs, the relative importance of each attribute over the range of levels included in DCE is recommended to show the magnitude of the effect of each attribute on patient preferences assuming that the independent of each attribute level [6]. It is an effective way to show the priority of attributes. Our review suggests that the authors should explain the relative importance in DCE literatures on patient preferences for lung cancer treatments.

Our reviewers initially expressed differing opinions when applying the ESTIMATE checklist in the Trade-offs domain, specifically concerning how to describe the magnitude and direction of the attribute-level coefficients. This disagreement arose about the interpretation and visualization of results from regression models of DCE data, particularly when the explanatory variable was categorical with three or more levels. In these models, regression coefficients are estimated for each level relative to either a reference level (using dummy-variable coding) or the mean effect of the attribute (using effects coding). If the category levels are equally spaced, connecting the estimates with lines appropriately visualizes the underlying trend. However, when the levels are not equally spaced (e.g., 11, 17, or 30 months for median overall survival; 16%, 48%, or 70% for serious adverse effect [19]) or not continuous (e.g., iv, oral on an empty stomach, or oral with or without food for mode of administration [10]), connecting estimates with lines may misrepresent relationships and lead to misinterpretation. In such cases, presenting the estimates as a bar chart, as shown in the ISPOR guideline [6], more accurately conveys the categorical relationships in DCE studies on patient preferences for lung cancer treatments. In addition, a positive coefficient for an attribute level indicates a favorable patient preference, while a negative coefficient indicates an unfavorable direction of preference. For categorical variables, the estimated values themselves cannot be strictly interpreted due to confounding by the subjective scale of utilities. Instead, it is considered scientifically appropriate to interpret patient preferences based on the relative impact (i.e., the relative importance) of each attribute calculated from these values [21].

A PubMed search indicated that the article [6] containing the ESTIMATE checklist has been cited more than 500 times. Among the DCE studies on patient preferences for lung cancer treatments included in our review, ten studies cited this article, while two studies (Zhang M [20], Liu F [16]) did not. The assessment scores in Table 3 were numerically lower for the studies that cited the checklist. This observation suggests that citation of the article may be perfunctory and does not necessarily reflect the quality of the statistical analysis. Of the nine studies cited the article [6] as a reference for analysis models such as the conditional logit model, random-parameters logit (mixed-logit) model, hierarchical Bayes model, and latent-class finite-mixture model, none specifically intended to adhere to the ISPOR ESTIMATE checklist. Only one study (Sun H [11]) followed the ISPOR ESTIMATE checklist as part of the ISPOR documents, suggesting that the ESTIMATE checklist is not widely recognized.

The ISPOR guidelines for DCE research methodology, description and interpretation of the analysis results have been developed to evaluate the quality of DCE research [6]. Our review suggests that the quality of the 12 DCE studies was not yet including sufficient information about the statistical methods and results; therefore, further consideration is necessary for researchers in DCE studies. The reason was considered (1) The Estimate checklist was not sufficiently known to researchers. (2) There is insufficient understanding of the value for the ESTIMATE checklist. (3) The researchers do not understand completely how to use the ESTIMATE checklist (some contents in the ESTIMATE checklist may be difficult to understand without help from a statistician). The ESTIMATE checklist includes a list of questions to consider when justifying the choice of analysis method, describing the analysis, and interpreting the result [6]. Thereby, the ETSIMATE checklist can help ensure good research quality for statistical analysis of a DCE study. Authors of DCE studies should answer the ESTIMATE checklist questions and explain DCE studies appropriately, such as by describing the statistical analysis model and the goodness of fit of the model. In addition, authors should provide sufficient information to ensure the reproducibility of the DCE experiment, including the parameter conditions for creating choice sets, how to select attribute levels, how to explain each attribute level in the DCE survey, and how to present them.

Because patient characteristics were different in each regimen of each cancer type, the patient preferences about treatment were considered different in each cancer type [4]. All DCE studies included drug efficacy and side effects as attributes, indicating that these factors are important in lung cancer treatment selection. Although the results of the scoping review do not directly affect patient preferences for lung cancer treatments, encouraging the use of appropriate statistical analysis methods in DCE studies on patient preferences for lung cancer treatments is considered to lead to the establishment of a process for making right decisions about treatment with patients based on the results of a scientifically appropriate interpretation of patient preferences for treatment. Also, our review suggests that cost was recently given a lower priority in attribute identification for patient preferences in lung cancer treatments than that in all cancers in a prior systematic review [2]. It might be because reimbursement systems for anti-cancer drugs are different among countries.

Our scoping review had some limitations. First, our review was limited to articles published in English. It may have excluded relevant DCE studies published in other languages. This language bias could affect the generalizability of our findings. Second, the use of specific keywords, although comprehensive, database limitations, and search strategy excluding articles before 2017 may not have captured all relevant DCE studies on patient preferences for lung cancer treatments. This exclusion may have resulted in the lack of significant DCE studies that could provide additional insights into our review. Finally, there are differences in patient preferences among cancers. We targeted lung cancer because various treatments, such as chemotherapy, molecular targeted drugs, and immunotherapy have been developed, then patients can select their treatments based on their preferences. All 12 DCE studies included drug efficacy and side effects as attributes. Although the results of the scoping review do not directly affect patient preferences for lung cancer treatments, the use of the appropriate statistical analysis method and correct interpretation of DCE studies on patient preferences for lung cancer treatment are considered crucial for pharmaceutical companies to understand unmet medical needs that align with patient needs and desires. Despite these limitations, we believe that our scoping review offers the useful insights for future DCE studies on patient preferences for lung cancer treatments.

The ESTIMATE checklist was developed by the ISPOR task force based on ideal quality standards for the statistical analysis of DCE studies. In eleven DCE studies on patient preferences for lung cancer treatment, the ESTIMATE checklist is not recognized, and there is room for qualitative improvement in both statistical analysis methods and the reporting of results. Therefore, we recommend using this checklist as much as possible. Although it may not be feasible to address every item, we believe that increased adherence will help move closer to these ideals and contribute to proper statistical analysis and accurate interpretation of findings in DCE studies.

Conclusions

Our scoping review using the ISPOR ESTIMATE checklists found that the consideration points remained as part of the Evaluation and Trade-offs in DCE studies on patient preferences for lung cancer treatments. The quality of DCE research is improved by considering the model formula and model fitting.
