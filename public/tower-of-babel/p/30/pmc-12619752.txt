Rapidly progressing after umbilical cord blood transplantation in a boy with acute myeloid leukemia with KAT6A::EP300 fusion transcript
Authors: Yongren Wang, Wei Zong, Jingjing Fan, LiuCheng Rong, Yongjun Fang
Published: 2025

Dear editor:

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and myeloid sarcoma (MS) are classified into different risk categories based on cytogenetic abnormalities. The KAT6A::EP300 fusion in AML is a rare subtype of AML, primarily characterized by the fusion of the KAT6A (also known as MOZ) and EP300 genes. This genetic anomaly has been associated with a poor prognosis in some adult patients, complicating treatment strategies [1–4]. However, recent studies have reported that in certain cases, particularly in hereditary forms of leukemia carrying this fusion gene, spontaneous remission can occur [5, 6]. This phenomenon adds complexity to treatment decisions, particularly in infants, where the course of the disease remains unpredictable. As a result, there is significant debate regarding surrounding the appropriate therapeutic strategy for infants with this fusion gene, with ongoing discussions on whether aggressive treatment is necessary or if a more conservative approach might be warranted given the potential for spontaneous remission. In this report, ,we present the case of an infant with acute myeloid leukemia (AML, M5 subtype) complicated by myeloid sarcoma, carrying the KAT6A::EP300 fusion gene, who experienced rapid relapse and disease progression within five months after undergoing post umbilical cord blood transplantation (UCBT).

A 20-month-old previously healthy boy was referred to our hospital with a 20-day history of fever. Peripheral blood tests revealed a white blood cell count of 23.68 × 10^9/L with 10% blasts, a hemoglobin level of 8.4 g/dL, and a platelet count of 106 × 10^9/L. MRI showed a mass of approximately 45 mm × 17 mm × 24 mm adjacent to the vertebral body at T5-T7, with osteolysis of the ribs and vertebral body, and adjacent structures (Fig. 1A/B). Bone marrow aspiration showed 46% blast-like immature monocytic cells. Surface marker analysis indicated that blast cells expressed CD11b, CD15, CD56, CD33, and CD41, leading to a diagnosis of AML (FAB M5b) complicated by myeloid sarcoma(Fig. 1C). Further cytogenetic analyses of bone marrow cells revealed an abnormal karyotype of 47,XY, t(8;22) (p11.2;q13), +der(8) t(8;22) [14]/46,XY [6] (Fig. 1D). RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed using the Ribozero method to remove ribosomal RNA, followed by cDNA synthesis. Sequencing libraries were constructed and analyzed using the Illumina Hiseq X platform, generating 13.71 Gb of data and 45.69 million reads. Sequencing reads were aligned to the UCSC hg19 reference genome using STAR software. Variants were detected with VarScan, including single nucleotide variants(SNVs) with base quality score correction, and gene fusion was predicted using STAR_fusion. The KAT6A (exon 16)::EP300 (exon 2) fusion was confirmed (Fig. 1E). Data were annotated using databases such as HGMD, ClinVar, dbSNP, and 1000genomes.

The patient started induction therapy according to the CCLG-AML-2019 protocol, which included the use of cytarabine, daunorubicin, homoharringtonine, and idarubicin. After two cycles of induction chemotherapy, bone marrow (BM) assessment was negative, while extramedullary lesions remained positive. Subsequent treatment with two additional cycles of cytarabine, etoposide, and homoharringtonine led to complete remission (CR). Subsequently, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation was considered. However, due to the unavailability of a suitable donor within the China Marrow Donor Program (CMDP), and following comprehensive discussions with the patient’s parents, the decision was made to proceed with umbilical cord blood transplantation (UCBT) as the donor source, in accordance with the parents’ preferences. The patient successfully underwent an 8/10 HLA-matched UCBT after myeloablative conditioning, which included cytarabine 1 g/m² every 12 h for 2 days, busulfan 1.2 mg/kg every 6 h for 12 doses, fludarabine 40 mg/m² once daily for 5 days, cyclophosphamide 50 mg/kg once daily for 4 days, carmustine 200 mg/m² for 1 day, and melphalan 140 mg/m² for 1 day. Five months after transplantation, the patient experienced disease relapse. Salvage chemotherapy was administered but was ineffective, and the patient ultimately died from disease progression.

The KAT6A gene encodes a histone acetyltransferase that plays a crucial role in regulating the cell cycle and maintaining stem cell function. Fusions involving KAT6A with CREBBP, NCOA2, NCOA3, ASXL2, and EP300 have been reported in extremely rare or isolated cases of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [7]. P300, encoded by the EP300 gene, is a key lysine acetyltransferase (KAT) involved in regulating cellular proliferation and signal transduction. It functions as both a transcriptional cofactor and a chromatin remodeler. In AML, EP300 helps prevent disease progression by maintaining epigenetic stability, inhibiting Myb expression, and controlling the proliferation and self-renewal of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) [8]. Loss of EP300 accelerates leukemogenesis, primarily by disrupting epigenetic regulation and enhancing Myb-driven oncogenesis.

AML cases with the KAT6A::EP300 translocation are exceptionally rare, with only eight instances documented in the literature to date [1–6, 9–11]. The prognosis of these patients varied significantly. Three adult patients died within 5–7 months of diagnosis, another died from transplant-related complications, two with congenital AML experienced spontaneous remission without treatment, and one achieved long-term survival following allogeneic transplantation. Due to the rarity of AML with the KAT6A::EP300 fusion gene, the mechanisms underlying spontaneous remission in a small subset of infant patients remain unclear. We hypothesize that genetic and environmental factors may contribute to this phenomenon, though further research is required to fully elucidate the underlying mechanisms. Given the unique characteristics of this subtype, treatment strategies must be individualized. Factors such as the patient’s age at onset, dynamic monitoring of clinical symptoms, and laboratory parameters should guide personalized therapeutic approaches for each patient. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of an infant with KAT6A::EP300 fusion AML treated with UCBT, although the patient ultimately died from rapid relapse of the leukemia. The optimal treatment for this rare leukemia subtype is unclear, emphasizing the necessity of individualized therapeutic strategies.
