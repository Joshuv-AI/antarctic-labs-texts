Optimizing cutoffs for clinical interpretation of brain amyloid status using PET/MRI: a multisite study
Authors: Hanxiao Xue, Sheng Bi, Meixi Wang, Xiaohua Zhu, Feng Wang, Yong Wang, Yujie He, Yan Wang, Xiaocao Liu, Bixiao Cui, Na Li, Zhilian Zhao, Chun Zhang, Liyong Wu, Zhigang Qi, Shaozhen Yan, Jie Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmyloid-β PET (Aβ-PET) imaging is playing an increasingly important role in the diagnosis and treatment of Alzheimer’s Disease (AD). The Centiloid (CL) scale has been developed to standardize the measurements of Aβ PET imaging. Previous CL threshold settings were based on PET/CT, while our team’s preliminary study found that CL values from PET/MRI are higher than those from PET/CT. Therefore, there is an urgent need to establish clinical interpretation cutoffs for Aβ status via PET/MRI to facilitate application in clinical practice.MethodsThe clinical performance of Aβ PET/MRI and cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers were evaluated in a multisite cohort of 720 participants. Aβ-PET scans were visually read and quantified using CL method. A two-cutoff approach identified thresholds maintaining > 90% sensitivity/specificity and ≤ 20% intermediate cases, selected by maximal Youden index. Group comparisons of cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers and cognition used generalized linear models adjusted for age and sex.ResultsThe two-cutoff approach categorized Aβ-PET status as negative (CL ≤ 18.7), gray-zone (18.7–44.2), and positive (CL > 44.2). In Xuanwu cohort, it achieved an accuracy of 94.1%, a positive predictive value of 97.4%, a negative predictive value of 86.1%. In External cohort, the accuracy was 97.4%, positive predictive value 98.5%, negative predictive value 90.8%. Patients in the gray-zone group had lower cerebrospinal fluid(CSF) Aβ-42, Aβ-42/40 levels and a higher P-tau/Aβ-42 ratio.ConclusionsThis study established optimal CL-cutoff, providing a clinically applicable framework for interpreting Aβ PET/MRI findings. Incorporating a two-cutoff system facilitates the tracking of the AD continuum and supports individualized therapeutic decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13195-026-02071-y.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, Amyloid-β, PET/MRI, Centiloid, Apolipoprotein E

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disease and a leading cause of disability and mortality in the elderly, primarily characterized by progressive memory impairment [1]. The accumulation and aggregation of amyloid-β (Aβ) is one of the earliest neuropathological events in AD, beginning decades before the onset of clinical symptoms [2]. The apolipoprotein E ε4 (APOE ε4) gene is the strongest genetic risk factor for AD. It heightens AD susceptibility by promoting Aβ aggregation and impairing its clearance [3]. To objectively confirm the presence of Aβ pathology in living individuals, there are two main in vivo detection methods: cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) testing and Aβ positron emission tomography (PET). While CSF biomarkers offer an indirect correlate, Aβ-PET is distinguished by its direct visualization and quantification of brain-wide Aβ deposits. This non-invasive capability to map Aβ burden topographically has established Aβ PET as a cornerstone of clinical diagnosis, patient monitoring, and therapeutic assessment in the era of anti-amyloid therapies [4].

Commonly used clinical Aβ-PET tracers include [¹¹C] Pittsburgh compound B (PiB) and several ¹⁸F-labeled compounds: 18F-florbetapir (FBP/AV45), 18F-flutemetamol, and 18F-florbetaben (FBB/AV1) [5]. PiB, the first Aβ probe, has limited clinical utility due to its short half-life (20 mins) and reliance on cyclotron production [6]. ¹⁸F-labeled tracers offer a longer half-life (110 mins) and greater practicality. Among these, FBP, characterized by its rapid kinetics and high pathological correlation, has become the most widely used Aβ tracer in clinical practice internationally [7]. Others such as [18F] flutemetamol (GE067) and FBB are also in use, while next-generation tracers like [18F] AZD4694 are under development for their higher affinity and lower non-specific binding [8]. The assessment of Aβ-PET imaging traditionally relies on visual reading, which is widely used in clinical practice due to its operational simplicity and no need for complex post-processing. However, visual assessment is inherently subjective, susceptible to factors such as brain atrophy and reader bias, with 15%–20% of scans being difficult to judge [9]. To overcome these limitations, quantitative measurement methods have been developed, most notably the Centiloid (CL) method. The CL value resolves such variability by converting Aβ burden into a unified numeric value on a scale with anchor points at 0 and 100, allowing direct comparison across different tracers and research sites [10, 11]. CL method offers a range of benefits and clinical utilities, such as enhancing diagnostic confidence, predicting cognitive decline, and optimizing patient management strategies [12]. CL value has been used in multiple studies to establish thresholds for amyloid-positive status, with its sensitivity and specificity comparable to those of pathological findings [13, 14]. Therefore, establishing validated CL value cutoffs is essential for determining Aβ status objectively in clinical practice [7]. Validation studies have reported variable CL value cutoffs for Aβ positivity, with the thresholds ranging from 12 to 42 CL across different research [15–19]. This variability is likely attributed to differences in scanning equipment, patient selection criteria, the gold standards adopted, and the calculation methods used for determining the cutoffs in these studies [20].

The established CL cutoffs, as discussed above, were predominantly validated on PET/CT systems. However, PET/MRI holds greater significance for diagnosing and monitoring therapy in AD by enabling simultaneous acquisition of both PET and MRI images, thereby eliminating radiation exposure and leveraging superior soft-tissue resolution, thus facilitating the real-time and precise fusion of anatomical structure and functional data [21–23]. Compared to PET/CT, PET/MRI has significant technical differences, including its method of attenuation correction and a longer axial field of view [24, 25]. With the increasing clinical use of PET/MRI, the application of anti-Aβ therapies necessitates accurate Aβ quantification for determining patient eligibility and monitoring therapeutic efficacy. However, our team’s previous research has demonstrated that PET/MRI yields systematically higher CL values compared to PET/CT, indicating the necessity of developing PET/MRI-specific CL value cutoffs to ensure accurate Aβ status classification [26]. Integrated PET/MRI has thus gained critical importance by allowing concurrent assessment of Aβ burden and detailed anatomical information. It underscores the urgent need to establish large-sample diagnostic cutoffs for this modality to enable precise Aβ status classification, thereby supporting both clinical diagnosis and treatment evaluation [27–29].

In the present study, we aim to establish PET/MRI-specific CL cutoffs for accurate Aβ status classification using a two-cutoff approach in a large multicenter cohort. Next, we will characterize the clinical and CSF biomarker profiles of individuals falling into the gray-zone identified by the two-cutoff approach.

Methods

Study participants

This retrospective, cross-sectional, bicentric study included participants from two independent cohorts: the memory clinic Xuanwu (ethical approval: [2023]044) at Xuanwu hospital (Beijing, China), n = 611, and patients recruited through three specialized memory clinics in China from three hospitals, n = 109. Group A comprises 61 patients from Tongji Hospital of Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Wuhan, China); Group B includes 32 patients from Nanjing First Hospital (Nanjing, China); Group C consists of 16 patients from the First Hospital of Hebei Medical University (Shijiazhuang, China). Xuanwu cohort included health controls (HC), and cognitively impaired patients (categorized as having mild cognitive impairment [MCI], AD dementia, or other neurodegenerative disorders). Diagnoses of MCI and probable AD dementia were based on core clinical criteria according to the recommendations of the National Institute on Aging and Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AA) [30, 31]. Thirty-six cases of frontotemporal dementia, 21 cases of vascular dementia, and 8 patients with dementia with Lewy bodies were diagnosed according to the respective criteria [32–36]. Patients diagnosed with cerebral small vessel disease, epilepsy, depression, as well as those with other undetermined diagnoses, are categorized under the other group. The External validation cohort was composed of HC, cognitively impaired individuals clinically diagnosed with MCI attributed to AD and probable AD dementia, in accordance with the core clinical criteria outlined in the NIA-AA recommendations [30, 31]. All patients underwent Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) tests to evaluate their cognitive function.

All participants received comprehensive diagnostic assessments, consisting of neurological examinations, neurocognitive testing, structural MRI scans, and routine laboratory tests. Additionally, Aβ PET scans were performed, along with CSF testing for core AD biomarkers—specifically Aβ-42, Aβ-40, total tau (T-tau), and tau phosphorylated at threonine 181 (P-tau181). The Aβ-42/40 ratio and P-tau/Aβ-42 were then calculated based on these measurements.

Participants were stratified by APOE genotype into ε4 carriers (ε3/ε4 and ε4/ε4) and ε4 non-carriers (ε2/ε3, ε2/ε2, and ε3/ε3), with all ε2/ε4 carriers excluded from analysis due to their antagonistic allelic effects. In the Xuanwu cohort, 191 individuals (41.6%) were carriers of the APOEε4 gene, and 268 individuals (58.4%) were non-carriers.

CSF AD biomarkers

CSF samples were collected via lumbar puncture following standardized international guidelines [37]. CSF biomarker analyses were performed using standardized automated and manual assays [38]. Aβ-42, Aβ-40, P-tau181, and T-tau concentrations were determined using commercial ELISA kits (Fujirebio). All measurements were conducted by researchers blinded to clinical and biomarker data, following previously described protocols.

Aβ PET/MRI imaging acquisition and analysis

All integrated PET/MRI exams were performed using simultaneous TOF-enabled hybrid imaging systems (uPMR 790, United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China; Signa, GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI, USA). Aβ-PET scans were performed with the FBP (n = 479) or PiB (n = 70) tracers in the Xuanwu cohort and with the FBP (n = 95) tracer in the External validation cohort. The imaging session commenced 50 min post-injection and lasted for 20 min. MRI scans were obtained concurrently during the PET scanning process. The following pulse sequence configuration was used to acquire three-dimensional T1-weighted imaging (3D T1W): flip angle = 10°, voxel size = 1.00 × 1.00 × 1.00 mm3, 192 slices, and repetition time/echo time = 7.2/3 ms. A 2-point Dixon scan was acquired simultaneously with the PET data for MRI-based attenuation correction. The tomographic reconstruction employed a hybrid iterative framework combining TOF, spatial resolution modeling via point spread function, and ordered subsets optimization. This computational architecture was configured with 32 subset divisions per iteration across 8 reconstruction cycles, implementing 3-mm full-width at half-maximum Gaussian smoothing during image post-processing.

The PET and structural MRI data were additionally processed through partial volume correction (PVC) and spatial normalization, with both procedures implemented using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12, Wellcome Department of Imaging Neuroscience, Institute of Neurology, London, UK) within MATLAB R2024a (The MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA), as described in our earlier studies [39, 40]. This correction was performed using an iterative reblurred Van Cittert method, with a 3D Gaussian kernel of 8 mm full width at half maximum (FWHM) serving as the spatial smoothing function and a step length (α) of 1.5. The iterative process was terminated when the relative percentage change between successive corrected images reached 51% [41]. The mean standardized uptake value ratio (SUVr) for cortical regions was derived by computing the cortex-to-cerebellum uptake ratio [42]. This calculation employed the CL-standardized cortical region of interest (ROI) and the complete cerebellar reference region, utilizing predefined volumetric masks (1 mm isotropic resolution; cortical mask: voi_ctx_1mm, whole cerebellum mask: voi_WhlCbl_1mm) obtained from the Global Alzheimer’s Association Interactive Network (GAAIN, www.gaain.org) [10].

All Aβ-PET images were initially interpreted by two readers (Reader 1, SB; Reader 2, SZY). Both readers were blinded to the individuals’ clinical details, had completed relevant training, and had experience in assessing over 1000 scans. In cases where the two readers’ results were inconsistent, Reader 3 (ZGQ), who has 10 years of clinical experience, conducted a visual interpretation, and the final result was based on this. To ensure the accuracy of the quantitative PET data analysis, we excluded data with minor head movement or suboptimal image quality. The final statistical analysis included 644 subjects: 549 from the Xuanwu cohort, and 31, 51, and 13 from Group A, Group B, and Group C, respectively.

Statistical analysis

We first performed ROC analysis using visually interpreted Aβ-PET results as the gold standard to evaluate the classification performance of CL value, with the area under the curve (AUC) calculated to determine diagnostic accuracy. Single-cutoff approach for Aβ-PET CL value threshold was determined by maximizing the Youden index, which optimizes both diagnostic sensitivity and specificity simultaneously. In this approach, we quantified four key metrics: accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and sensitivity to evaluate the diagnostic performance, which is consistent with the methods reported in previous literature [43].

To enhance diagnostic precision while minimizing false-positive classifications, we implemented a two-cutoff approach following established methodological guidelines [43–49]. Our objective was to identify all two-cutoff combinations that simultaneously maintained > 90% sensitivity and specificity while restricting the proportion of intermediate patients to ≤ 20%. From the resulting candidate thresholds, we ultimately selected the optimal pair demonstrating the maximal Youden index. The two-cutoff approach created three groups (negative, gray-zone and positive) according to lower and upper CL cutoffs as previously described [47, 49]. Individuals with CL value between the lower and upper cutoffs were classified as gray-zone group. Histogram density plots with kernel smoothers were created to visualize CL value differences in concentration distributions between positive and negative PET visual results. We evaluated this approach by assessing the accuracy, PPV and NPV and the number of patients categorized as gray-zone. Statistics were determined as the mean value of bootstrapped samples (1,000 resamples with replacement, stratified by the output), and from these, we further computed the 95% CI.

Furthermore, we compared CSF markers (that is, Aβ-42, T-tau, P-tau, Aβ-42/40 and P-tau/Aβ42) and cognitive function between three groups (negative, gray-zone and positive) stratified by CL values using independent generalized linear models (GLMs), adjusted for age and sex. The negative group served as the reference group (with its mean normalized to 0); hence, the standard beta (β) estimates for the gray-zone and positive groups represented the standard deviation from the reference group.

Considering the important role of APOE genotype in the AD spectrum, we further divided participants into APOE ε4 carriers and non-carriers, and compared their clinical parameters and CSF biomarker profiles. Estimates, 95% CI, and figures were generated with R statistical computing language and environment (version 4.3.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Clinical performance of CL cutoffs for Aβ-PET status classification

The ROC analysis established 32.0 as the optimal CL single-cutoff for discriminating Aβ-PET status (positive or negative) using Aβ-PET visual results as the gold standard, demonstrating diagnostic accuracy (AUC = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.93–0.97) with balanced sensitivity and specificity (both 89%). In Xuanwu cohort, CL single-cutoff achieved an accuracy of 88.9% (95% CI: 86%, 91%, Table 2), a PPV of 95.3% (95% CI: 93%, 98%), an NPV of 76.2% (95% CI: 70%, 82%), and a sensitivity of 88.7% (95% CI: 85%, 92%, Fig. 3). In External cohort, CL single-cutpoff achieved an accuracy of 91.4% (95% CI: 85%, 97%, Table 2), a PPV of 92.0% (95% CI: 85%, 98%), an NPV of 88.8% (95% CI: 70%, 100%), and a sensitivity of 97.3% (95% CI: 93%, 100%, Fig. 3).

To enhance diagnostic sensitivity and specificity while minimizing false-positive classifications, we implemented a two-cutoff approach following established methodological guidelines [44, 45]. We aimed to identify two-cutoff combinations that concurrently achieved > 90% sensitivity and specificity while limiting gray-zone classifications to ≤ 20% of participants, ultimately selecting the optimal threshold pair based on maximal Youden index performance. Figure 1 displays the distribution of CL values stratified by Aβ-PET visual results, with classification thresholds demarcating negative (CL ≤ 18.7), gray-zone (18.7 < CL ≤ 44.2), and positive (CL > 44.2) groups. In Xuanwu cohort, CL two-cutoff achieved an accuracy of 94.1% (95% CI: 92%, 96%), a PPV of 97.4% (95% CI: 96%, 99%), an NPV of 86.1% (95% CI: 80%, 92%), and the proportion of the gray-zone is 19.9% (95% CI: 17%, 23%, Table 2; Fig. 3). In External cohort, CL two-cutoff (Lower CL ≤ 18.7, Upper CL > 44.2) showed consistent predictive performance: it had an accuracy of 97.4% (95% CI: 93%, 100%), a PPV of 98.5% (95% CI: 95%, 100%), an NPV of 90.8% (95% CI: 70%, 100%), and the proportion of the gray-zone is 16.1% (95% CI: 10%, 24% Table 2; Fig. 3). We also applied the guideline-recommended dual cut-points of 10/30 CL to our dataset and evaluated their diagnostic performance in this cohort. The detailed results are presented in Table S2.

Group differences in CSF biomarkers and cognitive function across CL value classifications

Participants with abnormal CL value had significant abnormalities in CSF results (Aβ-42, Aβ-40, P-tau181, T-tau, Aβ-42/40 and P-tau/Aβ-42) and cognition (P < 0.001 in all cases) compared with those with normal CL value. Specifically, individuals are assigned to the negative group when their CL value is below the lower cutoff, while those whose CL values exceeds the upper cutoff are categorized into the positive group. For the remaining individuals—whose CL value falls within the range between the lower and upper cutoffs—they are placed in the gray-zone group. Significant differences from the negative group were considered when the range of 95% confidence interval of the mean difference did not include zero, as shown in Fig. 4. To further characterize the pathological continuum across negative, gray-zone, and positive subgroups, we performed one-way ANOVA with post-hoc comparisons (Table S3). We next investigated whether individuals who fell into the gray-zone also had abnormal CSF results and cognitive changes. Compared with the negative group, the positive group showed significant differences in the MMSE and MoCA scores, and all other CSF biomarkers (Aβ-42, P-tau181, T-tau, Aβ-42/40 and P-tau/Aβ-42). Specifically, compared with the gray-zone group, the positive group had significantly lower MMSE and MoCA scores, and a significantly lower level of Aβ-42 and Aβ-42/40; in contrast, it had significantly higher levels of T-tau, P-tau, and the Aβ-42/T-tau. Intriguingly, participants in the gray-zone exhibited significantly lower Aβ-42, Aβ-42/40 and higher P-tau/Aβ-42 ratios. No significant difference in CSF tau-related biomarkers (P-tau, T-tau) and cognitive function was observed between the negative and gray-zone groups. Regarding cognitive measures, although no statistically significant differences were observed, the gray-zone group demonstrated a trend toward cognitive decline relative to the negative group. For CSF P-tau and T-tau biomarkers, the gray group exhibited a trend toward abnormal tau protein alterations, although statistical significance was not reached.

Comparison of clinical characteristics, Aβ-PET and CSF biomarkers between APOE genotypes

Given the critical role of APOE ε4 genotype in the AD continuum, we performed subgroup analyses comparing APOE ε4 carriers and non-carriers with respect to clinical characteristics and CSF biomarker levels. Participants were stratified by APOE genotype into ε4 carriers (ε3/ε4 and ε4/ε4) and ε4 non-carriers (ε2/ε3, ε2/ε2, and ε3/ε3), with all ε2/ε4 carriers excluded from analysis due to their antagonistic allelic effects.

We compared clinical characteristics, Aβ-PET and CSF biomarker profiles between APOE ε4 carriers and non-carriers (Table 3). APOE ε4 carriers were significantly older (65.6 ± 7.7 vs. 63.4 ± 8.6 years, P = 0.016) than non-carriers, while no significant differences were observed in gender distribution, MMSE score, or MoCA score (all P > 0.05). Notably, APOE ε4 carriers exhibited a markedly higher Aβ-PET CL value (82.9 ± 54.6 vs. 55.6 ± 55.9, P < 0.001) compared to non-carriers. Regarding CSF biomarkers, carriers showed significantly lower Aβ-42 levels (579.2 ± 256.5 vs. 703.4 ± 343.2 pg/ml, P < 0.001) and Aβ-42/40 ratios (0.05 ± 0.023 vs. 0.06 ± 0.026, P = 0.005), alongside significantly higher t-tau (520.7 ± 303.1 vs. 437.4 ± 296.4 pg/ml, P = 0.012), P-tau (105.8 ± 69.4 vs. 86.8 ± 55.9 pg/ml, P = 0.005), and P-tau/Aβ42 ratios (0.21 ± 0.18 vs. 0.17 ± 0.18, P = 0.038), reflecting a more pathological biomarker profile consistent with greater Aβ and tau pathology burden in APOE ε4 carriers.

Discussion

In this multicenter study, we established PET/MRI-specific two-cutoff CL values for Aβ-PET interpretation, which achieved an accuracy of 97.4% in the validation cohort. Our findings demonstrated that the gray-zone individuals exhibited early cognitive decline and abnormal CSF biomarkers, requiring further confirmatory testing and longitudinal follow-up. Our findings advance the role of PET/MRI in the accurate quantitative assessment of Aβ deposition, paving the way for improved patient stratification and monitoring in AD clinical trials and practice.

Our results demonstrate the feasibility of applying the two-cutoff approach for the assessment of Aβ-PET status. Furthermore, based on the analysis of Xuanwu cohort and External cohort rigorous statistical verification, we propose two specific cutoffs: a lower cutoff of 18.7 and an upper cutoff of 44.2. In recent years, considerable attention has been focused on refining the threshold for Aβ PET and enhancing the early detection of cerebral amyloid pathology [20]. Previous studies conducted in clinical settings, which employed a single-cutoff approach based on visual results to determine the CL cutoff, have reported a relatively high range of cutoff values for CL (ranging from 12 CL to 42 CL) [14–16, 18, 19, 50]. The threshold ranges vary considerably, likely due to multiple factors. First, the limited number of cases with amyloid pathology in these studies may have restricted a more comprehensive evaluation of CL thresholds. Second, variability in reader expertise, particularly when assessing more challenging scans featuring early Aβ-PET uptake, contributes to this discrepancy. Additionally, different thresholds are established based on considerations of distinct clinical scenarios: lower thresholds prioritize early detection, while higher thresholds focus on maximizing specificity.

Current clinical guidelines have integrated these considerations and proposed a dual threshold of 10/30 CL by synthesizing evidence from multiple studies using various gold standards [20]. In the present study, our derived PET/MRI-specific dual thresholds of 18.7/44.2 CL are slightly higher than this guideline-recommended benchmark. Based on our team’s prior research, we attribute this minor discrepancy in threshold values to fundamental differences in imaging modality: our data were exclusively acquired on integrated PET/MRI scanners, whereas the majority of prior studies were conducted using conventional PET/CT systems [26]. The observed differences may be attributed to inherent technical discrepancies between PET/MRI and PET/CT, including MRI-based attenuation correction and a longer axial field of view, which systematically influence CL quantification [25]. In our study, we included a larger cohort of participants and, drawing on real-world clinical settings, achieved a patient distribution more aligned with actual clinical pathways. This enhances the authenticity and reliability of our patient enrollment, and accordingly, the two-cutoff CL values demonstrate excellent accuracy, PPV and NPV. Importantly, our dual thresholds have demonstrated robust diagnostic performance and reproducibility when tested in an independent external validation cohort, further supporting their clinical utility for PET/MRI-based Aβ quantification.

Currently, the two-cutoff approach has been widely applied to the thresholds of blood and CSF biomarkers for AD, which has significantly improved clinical accuracy [45, 47–49]. However, some individuals with intermediate CL values between the two thresholds require further longitudinal confirmatory tests to determine whether they are at a higher risk of developing AD pathology. In this study, we found that participants in the gray-zone exhibited a certain degree of cognitive decline, and CSF biomarkers indicated more Aβ deposition and tau aggregation. Therefore, when adopting the two-cutoffs method, confirmatory tests and longitudinal follow-up are important and necessary for patients in the gray-zone, as they may currently be in the early stage of AD but are at risk of rapid pathological and clinical progression. On the other hand, patients in the gray-zone may be non-primary AD patients or have the possibility of mixed dementia, requiring further other clinical examinations for differential diagnosis. This two-cutoff strategy provides the possibility for the early and accurate identification of individuals with AD pathological changes, and is particularly important for the early intervention of AD or the revelation of the characteristics of early AD-related pathology. Another important and novel observation of this study is that participants in the gray-zone group exhibited significantly lower Aβ-42 levels and higher P-tau/ Aβ-42 ratios compared with the Aβ-negative group, in the absence of significant cognitive impairment. This pattern may support the view that the gray-zone reflects a preclinical pathological stage along a continuous spectrum of amyloid deposition. This two-cutoff classification system may therefore improve the identification of individuals in the earliest pathological stages, who may be the most suitable candidates for early intervention strategies and enrollment into disease-modifying clinical trials.

The APOE gene is well-established to play a role in the pathogenesis of AD. Notably, the APOE ε4 allele has a robust association with increased AD risk, accelerated Aβ deposition, and earlier clinical onset [51, 52]. In current amyloid-targeting immunotherapy, restrictions have also been imposed on the APOE ε4 population, which further underscores the role of the APOE ε4 gene in Aβ deposition [53–55]. Given these findings, experts are currently considering that APOE ε4 status should be explicitly factored in when establishing diagnostic thresholds for AD-related biomarkers [5]. Previous study had also explored the influence of APOE 𝜀4 on the diagnostic performances and thresholds of CSF biomarkers, revealing that the thresholds vary according to APOE genotype [56]. We identified significant between-group differences in CSF biomarker profiles across APOE ε4 genotype subgroups, which suggests that APOE ε4 status may need to be considered as a relevant factor when establishing individualized treatment decision-making criteria based on CL.

Some limitations of this work should be considered. First, a potential limitation of this study is that the rationale for establishing PET/MRI-specific CL cutoffs partially relies on our team’s prior small-sample research demonstrating higher CL values in PET/MRI versus PET/CT. Whether this modality-specific CL difference is consistent across larger, more diverse populations remains unvalidated and warrants further investigation in future multicenter studies. S econd, the study lacks longitudinal follow-up data, which limits the ability to track the dynamic changes in CL values over time and evaluate the long-term prognostic value of the identified thresholds, especially for individuals in the gray-zone. Third, pathological results were not included as a gold standard for validating the CL thresholds, which may restrict the comprehensive assessment of their accuracy in reflecting true Aβ pathology. Fourth, all PET images in this study were reconstructed using the Dixon-based attenuation correction method; while this approach has been validated and is widely used in clinical practice, we acknowledge that different attenuation correction methods could introduce variability in absolute PET quantification and may therefore influence CL values. Therefore, future multicenter studies employing standardized attenuation correction protocols or cross-method comparisons can further validate and generalize the thresholds. Furthermore, the external validation cohort, while geographically diverse, has a relatively small sample size, potentially affecting the generalizability of the proposed thresholds to broader populations.

In conclusion, this study confirms that in the interpretation of Aβ-PET, we proposed CL thresholds for PET/MRI using the two-cutoff approach (18.7, 44.2), which have stable accuracy, PPV, and NPV and have been validated externally. The gray-zone identifies individuals with early cognitive decline and abnormal CSF biomarkers, who may be in a critical window for timely intervention to slow disease progression. This framework advances Aβ quantification, aiding anti-amyloid therapy eligibility and monitoring, and strengthens PET/MR’s role in optimizing cutoffs for clinical interpretation of brain amyloid status, enhancing global AD screening, treatment, and trial management.
