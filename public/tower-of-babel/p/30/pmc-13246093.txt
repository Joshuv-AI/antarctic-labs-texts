Global temporal trends and projections for the incidence of chlamydial infection among women of childbearing age over the next decade
Authors: Yutong Kang, Xuanming Xu, Chenyang Zhao, Qiheng Yuan, Yanying Feng, Meiqin Zheng, Zhenjun Li, Yajie Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Women of childbearing age (WCBA) are at unreasonable risk of excruciating pregnancy-related complications associated with chlamydial infection, constituting a paramount peril to global public health. This study aims to demonstrate the temporal tendencies of chlamydial infection in WCBA and prognosticate its future incidence, providing momentous illumination for precise prevention and management blueprints. Chlamydial infection incidence data was from Global Health Data 2021. An age-period-cohort (APC) model was implemented to probe trends in distinct age groups, periods, and birth cohorts, and a Bayesian APC model was utilized to previse future epidemiological trajectories. Between 1990 and 2021, global chlamydia cases rose significantly from 65,012,610 to 99,376,268. age-standardized incidence rate exhibited a relatively stable trajectory, increasing only slightly from 4861.3 per 100,000 to 5099.2 per 100,000 with a global net drift of −0.003%. High sociodemographic index areas diverged from global trends, demonstrating a yearly positive net drift of 0.643%. For the age effects of chlamydia incidence rate, all of the models displayed a similar “n”-shaped distribution, with the highest risk being noticed in childbearing aged 25 to 34 years, followed by a subsequent decline as advanced ages. The Bayesian APC model predicted a total of 102.05 million global cases in WCBA by 2030, with an expected moderate diminution in the age-standardized incidence rate. The expectancy for the management and prevention of chlamydial infection was inadequate in certain countries, including Turkmenistan. This study revealed a persistent rise in chlamydial infection among women of childbearing age, with the highest incidence observed in those aged 25 to 34 years. Despite the slight predicted decline in global incidence by 2030, significant management gaps still remained, particularly in countries like Turkmenistan. Targeted interventions and improved healthcare access are crucial to reduce the burden and enhance disease control efforts.

1. Introduction

Chlamydial infection is a common bacterial sexually transmitted infection (STI), primarily caused by chlamydia trachomatis, an obligate intracellular gram-negative bacterium.[1,2] In 2020, more than 1.5 million cases of chlamydial infection were reported (approximately 480 cases per 100,000 people), and the incidence has continued to rise steadily.[3] Females are consistently recognized as a high-risk group for chlamydial infection, with more than 60% of infected women remaining asymptomatic.[4,5] Moreover, since individuals aged 15 to 29 are typically in their peak reproductive years, untreated infections during this period can result in serious reproductive complications.[4,6,7] Alarmingly, few global epidemiological overviews have addressed the incidence of chlamydial infection among women of childbearing age (WCBA), indicating an insufficient focus on this critical population.

During the childbearing years, the risk of chlamydia infection in women is primarily influenced by the type of sexual activity, frequency of sexual contact, and duration of sexual partnerships.[8] In 2016, a study estimated the global prevalence of urogenital chlamydial infection among WCBA to be 3.8%, with over 127 million new cases reported that year.[9] Ascending chlamydial infection commonly leads to various inflammatory conditions, including cervicitis, urethritis, and pelvic inflammatory disease (PID).[10–12] PID can also provoke a severe inflammatory response that results in tubal scarring, ultimately leading to tubal factor infertility.[3,10] It has been reported that up to 45% of tubal factor infertility cases were attributable to chlamydial infection.[11] However, these complications often become apparent only when women attempt to conceive. Furthermore, a retrospective cohort study from the United Kingdom revealed that pregnancy could double the likelihood of acquiring a chlamydial infection.[13] Chlamydial infection during pregnancy is significantly associated with adverse birth outcomes, including premature rupture of membranes, preterm delivery, and low birth weight.[5,14,15] In addition, infants born via vaginal delivery may contract conjunctivitis or pneumonia due to exposure during birth.[16,17]

The prevalence of chlamydia among WCBA varies significantly across countries with different levels of development. A study conducted in sub-Saharan Africa reported a 7.8% prevalence of chlamydia among WCBA.[18] Similarly, a comprehensive study in Swaziland found a comparable prevalence of 7.0%.[19] Furthermore, a review conducted in India showed that the prevalence ranged from 0.1 to 1.1% among low-risk WCBA and from 2.7 to 28.5% among high-risk groups.[20] A study from a tertiary hospital in northern Nigeria reported a chlamydia prevalence of 26% among WCBA.[21] In northeast Brazil and Vietnam, surveys focusing on WCBA attributed more than 4.3% of STIs in this population to chlamydia.[22,23] Therefore, increased attention and proactive intervention strategies are urgently needed to address chlamydia infection.

To date, reliable data on the global epidemiological profile of chlamydial infection among WCBA remain limited and are scattered across studies conducted in various geographical settings. To inform the development of effective strategies and policies to mitigate adverse reproductive health outcomes associated with chlamydial infection, we analyzed the spatio-temporal patterns of chlamydia burden at national, regional, and global levels and examined its association with the sociodemographic index (SDI). We described the incidence trends of chlamydial infection in WCBA using age-standardized rates and case counts. Additionally, we employed the age-period-cohort (APC) model to investigate the temporal dynamics of chlamydia incidence and forecast future epidemiological trends using the Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model.

2. Methods

2.1. Data sources

This study presents a comprehensive analysis of incidence data on chlamydia infection among women aged 15 to 49 years, based on the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 dataset. The GBD database, developed by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation, provides extensive data on disease burden across 204 countries and territories, covering 371 diseases and injuries.[24] By quantifying health loss across diverse demographics, time periods, and geographical regions, GBD 2021 offers critical insights that support improvements in global health systems and the reduction of health disparities. Chlamydia infection is categorized at the fourth level of the GBD 2021 disease hierarchy, highlighting its recognized importance among infectious diseases.

This study utilized the Global Health Data Exchange query tool to extract key indicators of chlamydia infection among women aged 15 to 49 from the GBD 2021 database, including global incidence counts and rates from 1990 to 2021. The study also integrated the SDI, a composite metric that reflects levels of economic development, educational attainment, and fertility across countries and regions.[25] According to SDI values, GBD 2021 classifies countries into 5 levels of development: low, low-middle, middle, high-middle, and high, with higher SDI values typically indicating greater socioeconomic advancement. The objective of this study is to examine disparities in the burden of chlamydia infection and prevention strategies among women aged 15 to 49 across countries with differing SDI levels, with the goal of informing the formulation and implementation of global health policies.

2.2. Study population

This study examines chlamydia infection among WCBA, defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as females aged 15 to 49 years.[26] This life stage is characterized by active reproductive capacity and significant hormonal fluctuations. Adolescence, spanning from ages 10 to 19, represents a transitional phase between childhood and adulthood. Data on the incidence and incidence rates of chlamydia infection among WCBA from 1990 to 2021 were retrieved from the GBD 2021 database.[27] The Institutional Review Board of Beijing Ditan Hospital determined that ethical approval was not required because the study utilized publicly available data. The study adheres to the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting for cross-sectional research.

2.4. APC model analysis

The APC model is a sophisticated tool widely employed in health and socioeconomic research, providing distinct advantages over conventional methods. The model facilitates the estimation of net drift and local drift, which capture broad and specific temporal trends, respectively. Additionally, the model examines critical temporal factors: age, period, and birth cohort.[29–31] In this study, the APC model was utilized to examine the incidence trends of chlamydia infection among WCBA across various age groups, time periods, and birth cohorts. The APC model estimated both overall and age-specific temporal trends. The overall trend, or net drift, represented the annual percentage change in incidence, capturing the influence of calendar time and successive birth cohorts. Age-specific trends, referred to as local drift, reflected the annual percentage change in incidence for each age group. The Wald χ2 test was used to assess the statistical significance of these trends.

2.5. Frontier analysis

Frontier analysis explored the relationship between chlamydia infection incidence among WCBA and sociodemographic development. The frontier, a nonlinear boundary representing the minimum achievable burden based on a country’s development status, was constructed using nonparametric data envelopment analysis, as described in previous studies.[32] The observed deviation of the ASIR from the frontier, termed the “effective difference,” highlights unrealized health improvements relative to the region’s development level.

2.6. Bayesian prediction

The BAPC model was employed to project the incidence and case numbers of chlamydia infections among WCBA from 2022 to 2030. To improve prediction accuracy, the model was integrated with the Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation framework, addressing issues of mixing and convergence associated with traditional Bayesian methods using Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling. Analyses were performed using the R packages “BAPC” (v0.0.36) and “Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation” (v24.02.09), with statistical analyses and visualizations executed in Stata 16.0 (https://www.stata.com) and R 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

2.7. Statistical analysis

All estimates, including absolute counts and rates, were reported with 95% confidence intervals(CIs) or uncertainty intervals (UIs), derived from the 25th and 97.5th percentiles of 1000 posterior draws. All rates were age-standardized and expressed per 100,000 population. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2; R Core Team).

3. Results

3.1. Trends in chlamydial infection incidence globally and across SDI regions

From 1990 to 2021, global chlamydial infection cases increased significantly from 65,012,610 to 99,376,268. While the ASIR demonstrated a relatively stable trajectory, increasing only slightly from 4861.3 per 100,000 to 5099.2 per 100,000. Over the past 30 years, the global chlamydial infection burden has generally followed an upward trend, although distinct patterns have emerged across the SDI regions. However, middle SDI regions bore the highest burden; high-SDI regions exhibited the most pronounced increase (Table 1 and Fig. 1A). Notably, ASIR in middle and middle-high-SDI regions consistently exceeded the global average, whereas ASIR in high-SDI regions remained below this benchmark. Temporal patterns of ASIR have varied, with high-SDI regions showing consistent upward trends. In contrast, the other SDI regions exhibited fluctuations similar to the global trend, transitioning from an upward phase to a decline and ultimately increasing again (Fig. 1B). Particularly, the ASIR declined visibly globally (dropping from ~5200 to 4800) around 2010. Most SDI regions exhibited an analogous trend; for instance, the ASIR in middle SDI regions dropped from a peak of nearly 7000 to approximately 6200, while that in high-middle SDI regions declined from around 6000 to about 5000. But this downward shift represented only a temporary phase, as all the above-mentioned regions later underwent a notable rebound in ASIR values.

Temporal trends in chlamydial infection incidence among WCBA differed markedly across SDI regions and age groups. Globally, the highest incidence over the past decade was observed in the 20 to 29 age group, with the AAPC peaking in the 25 to 29 age group (local drift coefficient: 0.04), indicating a rising trend in individuals aged 20 to 34. In high-SDI regions, incidence increased among individuals aged 25 to 49, whereas it declined among those under 25. Similarly, in low-SDI regions, incidence declined among individuals under 29 but rose in those aged 29 and above. Notably, a consistent decline in AAPC among females aged 15 to 19 was observed globally and across all 5 SDI regions.

Based on the APC models, Figure 3A presented the estimated effects of age, period, and birth cohort across different SDI quintiles. Regarding the age effect on chlamydial infection incidence, all models revealed a similar “n”-shaped distribution, with the highest risk observed among women aged 25 to 34 years, followed by a decline with advancing age. Additionally, countries in the high-SDI group exhibited lower incidence rates compared to other regions, with relatively minimal variation among different age groups.

In general, the period effects over the past 3 decades remained relatively stable across different SDI regions, except for high-SDI countries (Fig. 3B). In high-SDI regions, the incidence of chlamydial infection increased steadily until the 2012 to 2016 period, followed by a more rapid rise during the subsequent 5 years. Additionally, period risks reached their lowest levels in the 2012 to 2016 period for middle and high-middle SDI countries, while the lowest risks for low and low-middle SDI countries occurred during the 2002 to 2006 period. Notably, the incidence rates in these countries showed a marked upward trend after reaching the lowest risk period, potentially indicating the onset of an adverse period effect. Overall, the findings consistently suggested that the period effects on chlamydial infection incidence among WCBA had gradually increased globally over the past 5 years.

After confirming the stability of age and period effects, the global successive birth cohorts (Fig. 3C) demonstrated a clear pattern of incidence risk, characterized by a general decline before the 1977 to 1986 cohort, followed by an increase, and then a significant decrease after the 1982 to 1991 cohort. Meanwhile, the trends observed in middle and high-middle SDI regions were broadly consistent with the global pattern. In these regions, birth cohorts prior to 1982 to 1991 exhibited a higher risk of chlamydial infection, while cohorts born after this period experienced comparatively lower risks. Furthermore, among individuals born in low and low-middle SDI countries before the 1977 to 1986 cohort, the increase in chlamydial infection risk was less pronounced, followed by a notable decline thereafter. In contrast, high-SDI regions exhibited a clear upward trend in incidence risk, peaking in the 1982 to 1991 cohort, which had the highest risk compared to the other 4 SDI regions. A downward trend was subsequently observed over the following 15 years.

3.4. Frontier analysis based on ASIR

This study analyzed chlamydia infection data from 1990 to 2021 using frontier analysis of ASIR and the SDI to identify potential areas for intervention across countries with varying levels of development. The results revealed a “V-shaped” relationship between ASIR and SDI, with ASIR initially increasing and subsequently decreasing as SDI rose. In countries with an SDI below 0.6, ASIR consistently increased, alongside widening disparities between nations. Conversely, in regions with an SDI above 0.6, ASIR peaked and subsequently declined, accompanied by a narrowing gap between countries (Fig. 4A).

The analysis identified 15 countries with substantial potential for improving chlamydia control, including South Africa, Turkmenistan, Tonga, Kazakhstan, Mongolia, Tajikistan, Vanuatu, Armenia, Georgia, Azerbaijan, Fiji, Kyrgyzstan, Papua New Guinea, Samoa, and Uzbekistan. 3.5. Projected global burden of chlamydial infection by 2030

The BAPC model projected global mortality and population size from 2022 to 2030. Globally, the number of chlamydial infection cases was anticipated to rise steadily, reaching approximately 102.05 million (95% UI: 76.14–127.96 million) by 2030. In contrast, the ASIR was expected to moderately decrease to 4889.74 (95% UI: 3685.69–6093.79) (Fig. 5A). Projections for China and India, the 2 most populous countries, indicated an increase in cases in both nations, with a rise in ASIR in China and a decrease in ASIR in India (Figs. 5B and 5C).

Projected ASIR and number of chlamydial infections among WCBA from 2022 to 2030 in (A) the global population, (B) China, and (C) India. ASIR = age-standardized incidence rate, WCBA = women of childbearing age.

Our study further projected the incidence of chlamydial infection in the 15 countries most distant from the frontier fit line, as determined by frontier analysis (Fig. S1). In Kyrgyzstan, both the number of cases and the ASIR were expected to continue increasing. These findings suggested that, without substantial public health interventions, the burden of chlamydial infection in Kyrgyzstan was unlikely to decline in the near future. Moreover, the gap between disease burden and sociodemographic development was expected to widen. For the remaining 14 countries farthest from the frontier, projections indicated that the number of chlamydial infections would continue to rise in Turkmenistan, Tonga, Tajikistan, Papua New Guinea, Samoa, Kazakhstan, and Vanuatu, despite declines in ASIR. In Mongolia and Uzbekistan, the number of cases was projected to stabilize, with a concurrent decline in incidence. Conversely, in Azerbaijan and South Africa, both the number of cases and ASIR were expected to decrease. In Georgia, while the number of cases was projected to decline, ASIR was anticipated to remain stable. In contrast, Armenia was expected to experience a reduction in case numbers, but with a simultaneous increase in ASIR.

4. Discussion

This study is the first to employ the APC model and BAPC model to comprehensively analyze the prevalence of chlamydial infection among WCBA at global, regional, and national levels, offering valuable insights into the temporal trends of the disease and guiding epidemiological and public health decision-making. The WHO’s 2030 Sustainable Development Goals underscore the importance of preventing and controlling STIs and improving reproductive health worldwide. A comprehensive understanding of the epidemiological trends of chlamydial infections in this demographic is crucial for informing public health strategies and achieving global health objectives. For the age effects of chlamydia incidence rate, all of the models manifested a comparable “n”-shaped distribution, with the highest risk being perceived in childbearing aged 25 to 34 years, followed by a successive abatement as advanced ages. The BAPC model conjectured a total of 102.05 million global cases in WCBA by 2030, with an expected moderate curtailment in the ASIR. This study analyzed the prevalence of chlamydial infection among WCBA using incidence cases and ASIR and elaborated temporal patterns and forecasted future trends holistically. Given the increasing trend of chlamydial infection among WCBA, there is an urgent need for strengthened surveillance and targeted interventions to mitigate long-term reproductive health consequences.

Chlamydial infection continues to pose a significant global public health challenge, particularly affecting high-risk groups such as WCBA. This population is highly susceptible to complications such as PID, ectopic pregnancy, and infertility.[9,10,33] Furthermore, untreated chlamydial infection in pregnant women can lead to adverse outcomes such as preterm birth, low birth weight, neonatal pneumonia, and conjunctivitis.[15–17] Treatment should be commenced as soon as possible to avoid complications.[34] Nowadays, the generally recognized first-line drug treatments are azithromycin and doxycycline. Alternative medicaments include penicillin and macrolides such as levofloxacin. Anyway, doctors must consider the side effects of these medications on the fetus during pregnancy and breastfeeding. Furthermore, long-term follow-up is chiefly required after medication, which may persist for 3 to 12 months.[35] This long-drawn-out monitoring and medication regimen is immensely hampered in low-SDI areas by insufficient disposable income, finite healthcare facilities and physicians, and even drug scarcity.[36] Theoretically, drug undersupply should escalate the prevalence of chlamydia infection in WCBA. Surprisingly, our study found that the increase in some high-SDI areas appears to be related to other factors. The increased prevalence of chlamydia infection in WCBA is doubtlessly the repercussion of multiple factors interacting together.

At the global level, the highest incidence rate of chlamydia infection was observed in the 20 to 29 years age group over the past decade, with the AAPC peaking in the 25 to 29 years age group, followed by a progressive trend in the 20 to 34 years age group. Previous studies have documented a conspicuous nonlinear pattern in chlamydia infection, suggesting that young women aged 20 to 24 bear a disproportionately high disease burden driven by a complex interplay of behavioral, biological, and cultural factors.[37,38] WCBA infected with chlamydia are at a significantly higher risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes, thus emphasizing the need for targeted interventions, such as routine screening, for couples planning pregnancy and other at-risk populations. Early detection and timely treatment are effective measures to prevent reproductive harm caused by chlamydia infection.[39,40]

In general, the period effects across different SDI countries have remained relatively unchanged over the past 30 years. However, high-SDI regions exhibited unfavorable period and cohort effects, which differed from those observed in other regions. In high-SDI regions, the incidence of chlamydial infection steadily increased until 2012 to 2016, after which it continued to rise rapidly over the next 5 years. The cause of these period and cohort effects might be related to the diverse chlamydia genotypes found in various regions.[41] High-income countries have implemented early detection and treatment for many years, providing extensive screening, which was reflected in the data as the true prevalence. However, low-SDI countries faced constraints related to financial and medical personnel shortages, leading to reported cases that were often underreported compared to the actual prevalence. Since 2000, an increasing number of studies have reported the sexual transmission of chlamydia, particularly in high-SDI regions.[41] Additionally, high-SDI regions tend to have more advanced sexual cultures. These factors could help explain the increased risk of chlamydial infection in high-SDI regions.

We analyzed ASIR and SDI data using comprehensive datasets, identifying substantial opportunities to improve the management of the chlamydial infection burden. Consistent with previous research, population-based surveillance studies in certain low-SDI regions (e.g., Morocco and Mongolia) have reported a declining trend of chlamydial infections in specific populations.[42,43] Remarkably, low-SDI regions such as Senegal, Niger, Benin, Gambia, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo have shown effective management of chlamydial infections despite limited resources. These regions have adopted successful strategies that have improved health outcomes in resource-limited settings. This progress can be attributed to various factors, including international funding and the expansion of STI prevention programs since 2003.[43] In contrast, high-SDI regions such as Singapore, Taiwan (Province of China), Sweden, Lithuania, and South Korea hadn’t achieved commensurate progress in addressing chlamydial infections. Since chlamydial infection was largely asymptomatic, the reported incidence was heavily influenced by testing coverage. In high-SDI regions, governments often prioritized screening asymptomatic infections among WCBA, which might introduce bias in the estimation of chlamydial infection incidence and partially explain the observed increase in reported cases.[44] Further research is warranted to better understand the determinants of success in high-performing regions and to identify the challenges confronting less successful areas.

Our BAPC model predicted that the global number of chlamydial infections would steadily increase, reaching approximately 102.05 million by 2030. In contrast, the ASIR is expected to decline slightly. Moreover, the gap between disease burden and development level is projected to widen. Among the 14 countries furthest from the frontier of elimination, chlamydial infections are projected to increase in Turkmenistan, Tonga, Tajikistan, Papua New Guinea, Samoa, Kazakhstan, and Vanuatu, despite a decrease in ASIR. These concerning trends place greater demands on health infrastructure, public health policy formulation, and international medical cooperation. A recent study on global chlamydia infections projects that gender-specific trends will gradually diverge over the next 2 decades with rising female age-standardized incidence and prevalence rates, thereby indirectly corroborating our findings.[38] Surveillance of chlamydial infection among WCBA is of critical importance. Local governments should actively promote effective prevention strategies, including safe sex practices and proper personal hygiene habits. prepregnancy screening for chlamydia is recommended to assess the risk of infection during pregnancy. A chlamydia vaccine represents a promising solution; however, its development remains time-consuming, requiring global scientific collaboration.

Despite these advancements, several inherent limitations of this study must be acknowledged. Firstly, the limited availability of medical resources in some low- and middle-SDI countries hampers accurate diagnosis and screening for chlamydial infection, potentially resulting in underestimation of the actual incidence. Secondly, dependence on GBD modeling introduces uncertainty, especially in countries lacking primary data, which may affect the precision of estimates regarding age, period, and cohort effects. Thirdly, the absence of pregnancy-specific chlamydial infection data in the GBD database precludes further analysis of global incidence trends in this subpopulation. Future epidemiological studies are warranted to address these data limitations and enhance the accuracy of global burden estimates.

5. Conclusion

The study revealed a complex epidemiological landscape of chlamydial infection among WCBA, characterized by rising global incidence alongside a declining ASIR. The burden of chlamydial infection was projected to remain substantial among WCBA by 2030. Moreover, young WCBA and regions with a high-SDI warrant particular attention. The recommendations of this study emphasized the need for region-specific strategies to mitigate the projected increase in chlamydial infection incidence and to align with the WHO’s 2030 targets. Meanwhile, countries around the world should reinforce the construction of public health databases and assimilate more diversified information, such as data on WCBA chlamydia infection during pregnancy, so that scientists can contribute more precise strategies.
