Current trends in the production of xylitol and paving the way for metabolic engineering in microbes
Authors: Namrata Pandey, Dilshad Ahmad, Muzaffar Hasan, Devendra Kumar Choudhary, Ajesh Kumar, Manoj Kumar Tripathi, Shahida Anusha Siddiqui, Mohd Asif Shah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Xylitol, a five-carbon sugar alcohol, is recognized as a desirable sugar alternative due to its low-calorie content and metabolism independent of insulin. Its commercial production generally involves the chemical hydrogenation of D-xylose, an approach that is energy-demanding and environmentally unfriendly. Although fermentation offers a biological alternative, it often suffers from low conversion efficiency and limited yields. However, xylitol is an intermediate metabolite in various microbial species, and its biosynthesis can be enhanced through metabolic engineering. Genetically modifying microbial cell factories—such as bacteria, fungi, and yeast—has shown significant improvements in xylitol production. Furthermore, the precursor xylose, which is utilized by microbes, can be derived from lignocellulosic biomass through hydrolysis, offering a more sustainable and cost-effective production route. This review discusses recent advances in the bioproduction of xylitol and highlights various metabolic engineering strategies employed to enhance xylitol yield in microbial cell factories.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

The term “Xylitol” is derived from the Greek word “Xylo,” meaning wood, and the suffix “-itol,” which denotes sugar alcohols. Xylitol is an alcoholic compound that occurs naturally in plants and is also produced by certain fungi and bacteria [30, 88]. It is categorized as a polyalcohol or sugar alcohol. Its molecular formula is CH2OH(CHOH)3CH2OH, with a five-carbon backbone [33, 122]. Physically, it appears as a white, solid crystalline, or granular Substance that dissolves easily in water. A French and a German chemist independently identified xylitol in 1891 [101]. The purification of xylitol crystals was successfully done after a few years, and it gained popularity and was utilized as an alternative to normal sugars during World War II to address sugar shortages [102].

The potential utilization of xylitol has several applications in various industries. It has been applied in various food product production to strengthen the shelf life, taste, texture, and color. Since xylitol is resistant to the Maillard reaction, it maintains the nutritional value of proteins and prevents darkening. Xylitol is frequently incorporated into confectionery products for both children and adults. It is utilized either on its own or blended with other sugar alternatives in the creation of sugar-free items, including pastilles, wafer fillings, hard candies, various diabetic-friendly treats, chewing gum, and chocolate [78]. Incorporating xylitol into baked goods improves their taste and appearance, though minor browning can still occur due to the reducing sugars naturally found in flour. In the case of sugar cakes, xylitol proves to be an excellent replacement, producing results that are comparable to sucrose-based cakes in both appearance and texture [100]. Xylitol serves as both an excipient and a sweetener in a variety of pharmaceutical formulations. Xylitol offers various health benefits and versatile applications in pharmaceuticals and nutrition. Similar to its use in food, its benefits include having non-cariogenic properties and being ideal for diabetic patients [98]. Its non-fermenting and chemically inert properties make it ideal for medications, providing dental health benefits and a pleasant cooling sensation. As a low-calorie, low-glycemic-index sweetener, xylitol is suitable for diabetic patients, aiding in glucose control, lipid management, and weight regulation while also supporting post-operative recovery and metabolic health [30, 116].

Despite the numerous advancements and role of xylitol in food, nutraceutical domains, and pharmaceutical large-scale synthesis still faces challenges due to the high cost and limited efficiency of traditional chemical synthesis methods. Nevertheless, the reliance on chemical synthesis methods remains costly and unsustainable, leading to a shift toward biological methods for converting sugars to xylitol. Chemical production of xylitol requires elevated temperature and pressure, rendering the process highly energy-intensive and labor demanding. [13]. While the feedstock required for xylitol production is available worldwide, industrial synthesis remains concentrated in a few regions, mainly in Asia, Europe, and the US, due to the costly nature of the synthesis procedure [6].

Microbial fermentation has emerged as a promising alternative, offering eco-friendly and cost-effective solutions. Since 2013, biological approaches—particularly microbial and enzymatic processes—have gained considerable traction, marking a significant shift in research focus. This momentum has been driven by advancements in yeast fermentation technologies and the optimized utilization of hemicellulosic feedstocks. Further Supported by innovations in biocatalyst development, process scale-up, and downstream recovery, biological methods saw a Surge in scientific output, with notable peaks in publication activity in 2016 and 2019. By 2023 and 2024, biological strategies had emerged as the dominant and preferred route for sustainable xylitol production.

However, the metabolic engineering of microbial strains for xylitol synthesis remains underexplored, particularly in optimizing yield, reducing by-products, and improving substrate utilization. While recent advances in metabolic engineering hold significant potential, further research is essential to address these challenges and fully harness the capabilities of engineered microbial systems for efficient and sustainable xylitol synthesis. This review provides insights into the role of metabolic engineering in advancing xylitol synthesis.

Methods for xylitol production

Xylitol can be produced from xylose via chemical and biological processes. Both processes utilize the lignocellulose biomass (LCB) to produce xylitol from xylan or xylose.

Lignocellulose biomass (LCB)

Lignocellulosic biomass (LCB), a plentiful resource frequently treated as waste, offers significant promise as an eco-friendly alternative to traditional fossil-derived chemical sources (bioplastics, biopolymers, biofertilizers, xylitol, organic acids, and enzymes) and fuels (biogas and biofuel) [54]. The present emphasis is on harnessing lignocellulosic substrates and minimizing industrial carbon emissions to create an efficient, carbon–neutral resource.

Abundant polysaccharides, such as cellulose and hemicellulose, are the major components of LCB and are widely used in the production of bioethanol (C2H5OH) and various other chemicals. Hemicellulose, a complex mixture of heteropolysaccharides, contains both C5-pentose and C6-hexose sugars and is valuable for synthesizing a variety of chemicals (Fig. 2) [70]. While the conversion of C6 sugars to sugar alcohols is well-documented, comparatively little research has focused on converting C5 sugars like xylose into high-value chemicals. Xylitol (C5H12O5) is commonly produced through the bioconversion of hemicellulose-rich lignocellulosic biomass [28, 75]. Xylose assimilation route

Xylose assimilation occurs via two pathways in filamentous fungi, yeast, and other eukaryotes: (i) the redox pathway, where xylose reductase converts xylose into xylitol using NADPH (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate) as a coenzyme, and (ii) the isomerization pathway, where D-xylose is isomerized into D-xylulose by D-xylose isomerase, followed by phosphorylation by D-xylulose kinase (XK, EC 2.7.1.17), to produce 5-phospho-D-xylulose. Among these, the redox pathway is the primary mechanism for xylose uptake and utilization in yeasts [127]. Xylitol biosynthesis primarily involves two key enzymes—D-xylose reductase (XR, XYL1, Xyl1p) and D-xylose dehydrogenase (XDH; EC 1.1.1.9, XYL2, Xyl2p). In the first step of D-xylose metabolism, XR catalyzes D-xylose to xylitol by transferring the hydride ion from NADPH to the aldehyde group of D-xylose. Xylitol is further oxidized in D-xylulose, and NAD + is reduced to NADH in the XDH-catalyzed reaction. XK (EC 2.7.1.17) transfers the γ-phosphate from ATP to the 5thC of D-Xylulose and generates D-xylulose-5-phosphate. This XK-catalyzed reaction links xylose metabolism to the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) and subsequently supports the generation of ethanol and glycerol [5]. XR requires NADPH or NADH, and XDH requires NADP or NAD for activity. The XR is dual-dependent on NADPH and NADH cofactors in some yeasts, with NADPH-XR activity being the highest, whereas XDH predominantly relies on NAD + as a cofactor [104]. Xylose is first converted to xylulose by xylose isomerase (XI, EC 5.3.1.5) in bacteria (Fig. 2b), before being phosphorylated by XK into xylulose-5-phosphate for entry into the pentose phosphate pathway [83]. XI, also commonly referred to as D-glucose isomerase, because XI also converts D-glucose to D-fructose [74], Saha et al. [73, 105]. Divalent cations such as Mg2⁺ and Mn2⁺ stimulate this enzyme, whereas Ca2⁺ acts as an inhibitor. This enzyme does not require cofactors produced from pyridines to function.

Xylitol synthesis by chemical method

In industry, the mass-level production of xylitol occurs through the chemical hydrogenation method, in which Raney nickel hydrogenates xylose (xylan), which is obtained from the hydrolysis of hemicellulose-rich biomass. This whole process is classified in five steps: (i) Acid based hydrolysis of LCB, (ii) xylose separation and purification, (iii) catalyst-based hydrogenation of xylose at high temperature (353–413 K), (iv) separation and purification of xylitol, (v) crystallization of xylitol [6]. Approximately 50–60% of xylan in LCB hydrolysates is converted into xylitol through this chemical method [79]. Noble-metal-based catalytic systems, particularly those utilizing ruthenium (Ru), have also shown high efficiency in xylose-to-xylitol hydrogenation [11, 76, 128]. More recently, non-noble metal catalysts, such as cobalt supported on silica, have emerged as promising and cost-effective alternatives [8]. Shetty et al. (2025) transformed the lignocellulose-derived xylose fraction to xylitol using a novel Ni–Al catalyst supported on hexagonal mesoporous silica (HMS). The transformation of xylose to xylitol using this one as an innovative catalyst represents an environmentally friendly approach, as it requires significantly reduced metal pressure compared to traditional Raney nickel catalysts. Additionally, it avoids the use of precious metals like Ru, Pd, Pt, or Rh and eliminates the issue of metal leaching, making it more advantageous than previously reported catalysts. Nonetheless, the substitute compound (current chemical) procedure is not sustainable; in huge quantity production, it remains expensive because of excessive power consumption and the generation of by-products, which demand costly separation and purification processes.

Xylitol synthesis by biological method

Biological methods mainly include fermentation and enzymatic methods.

Microbial fermentation approach

Utilizing microbial fermentation process of xylose to produce xylitol transforms waste into valuable products, conserves resources, and helps safeguard the environment [127]. Microbial transformation specifically refers to the metabolic alteration of xylitol from xylose via enzyme, xylose reductase in microorganisms. This method operates under mild conditions, avoiding the need for high temperatures or pressures, making it relatively straightforward to regulate. Additionally, due to the unique properties of microbial strains and the specificity of their enzymes, the process generates a simpler product profile, thereby reducing synthesis costs [98]. Microbe-based synthesis of xylitol mainly depends on the xylose assimilation pathway of microbes, which is different in fungi and bacteria.

Among these, yeasts have proven particularly effective, with species such as Candida parapsilosis, Candida tropicalis, and Candida guilliermondii that can natively assimilate xylose and convert it into xylitol without requiring any genetic or metabolic engineering [22, 23, 116, 127]. These strains possess inherent xylose reductase activity that facilitates efficient bioconversion, making them ideal candidates for industrial xylitol production. To attain the highest xylitol yield with the most suitable yeast strain, determining the ideal fermentation conditions is also essential, as these factors significantly influence the efficiency of the synthesis process. For example, Eryasar and Karasu-Yalcin [26] reported maximum sugar alcohol (xylitol) yields of 83.28 g L−1 and 54.07 g L−1 from 100 g L−1 xylose using C. tropicalis. Similarly, Lorliam et al. [64] observed high xylitol production (71.59 g L−1) in C. tropicalis A26 with media containing 80 g L−1 xylose, 4 g L−1 peptone, 5 g L−1 yeast extract, 20% (v/v) inoculum, and at a beginning pH of 5.5.

In another finding, 25.8–109.5 g L−1 of xylitol has been obtained by Candida tropicalis originating out of rice straw and sugarcane bagasse ([97, 118], XY [142]). LCB sources like sugarcane bagasse, olive pits, and corn cob, another microbial procedure using such microbes like Pichia fermentans and Kluyveromyces marxianus has produced very good amounts of xylitol (24.2–86.6 g L−1) [24, 81, 94, 142].

Zhang et al. [137] also observed how oxygen deprivation and pH affected the fermentation of d-xylose by N. crassa AS 3.1602. Research has established that high pH inhibits the fermentation of d-xylose. Additionally, ethanol and xylitol yields are greatly affected by the initial D-xylose concentration, with earlier research indicating that D-glucose presence suppresses D-xylose utilization [44, 71].

In conclusion, because filamentous fungi can generate the hydrolytic enzymes required for the breakdown of d-xylose-rich hemicellulose and the subsequent generation of xylitol by the same fungi, they provide intriguing possibilities in the bioproduction of xylitol [71, 106]. A technique called consolidated bioprocessing (CBP) makes it possible to convert biomass by combining many processing steps into a single, simplified process.

Industrial enzyme-based approach

In contrast to microbial methods, the enzymatic approach allows for nearly complete conversion of xylose into xylitol [19]. Xylose reductase (XR) serves as the main industrial enzyme for this transformation [67]. Kitpreechavanich et al. [50] reported that combining XR with the Candida pelliculosa methanogen oxidoreductase system for NADPH regeneration can achieve up to 90% conversion of xylose to xylitol. Alternative NADPH regeneration strategies, including those based on glucose dehydrogenase (GDH) and formate dehydrogenase (FDH), have also been explored [127]. High-yielding yeasts such as Candida tropicalis, Candida tenuis, and Candida guilliermondii are widely utilized for XR production [35, 49, 121]. At present, hemicellulose hydrolysates serve as the main feedstock for enzymatic processes that yield significant amounts of xylitol [95], Rafiqul et al., SPS:refid::bib92[92].

Biotechnological approaches for xylitol synthesis

Biotechnological xylitol production offers a promising alternative to chemical methods, as it can employ hemicellulosic biomass, lower the energy and costs of substrate purification, and function under mild, adaptable conditions.

Challenges in hydrolysate utilization: inhibitor tolerance

Lignocellulosic hydrolysates provide an abundant and cost-effective Substrate for xylitol production, but their effectiveness is Limited by inhibitory compounds generated During biomass pretreatment. The main inhibitors include furfural and 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) from sugar degradation, phenolic derivatives from lignin breakdown, and organic acids such as acetic, formic, and levulinic acids [113]. These molecules impair microbial growth and productivity by damaging membranes, inhibiting essential enzymes, and disrupting redox homeostasis, ultimately diminishing xylitol yields. Optimizing parameters such as solid–liquid ratio, acid concentration, pressure, temperature, and reaction time is crucial for maximizing xylose recovery while minimizing the formation of unwanted byproducts.

To mitigate inhibitor toxicity, four main strategies have emerged. In situ detoxification applies chemical or biological agents during fermentation to reduce inhibitor levels; overliming with calcium hydroxide and activated carbon adsorption are proven to neutralize furans and phenolics [91]. Adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) subjects’ strains to prolonged cultivation in inhibitor-rich media, enriching for mutations that enhance robustness [3, 53]. Efflux pump engineering boosts the active export of toxic compounds by overexpressing ATP-binding cassette (ABC) or major facilitator superfamily (MFS) transporters, improving tolerance to furfural and acetic acid in yeast [58, 143]. Redox cofactor balancing alleviates oxidative stress, with elevated NADPH supply supporting furan detoxification via NADPH-dependent reductases—Almeida et al. [4], Heer et al. [37].

An increasingly preferred alternative is to construct inhibitor-tolerant microbial strains that eliminate the need for detoxification. An Escherichia coli CRP mutant (IS5-dG) produced 82 g L⁻1 xylitol directly from non-detoxified corncob hydrolysate [130], while Candida tropicalis K21D, generated through EMS mutagenesis, exhibited strong resistance to mixed inhibitors and maintained high yields without detoxification [116]. Broader metabolic and evolutionary engineering approaches—including transcription factor modification, adaptive evolution, and overexpression of detoxifying enzymes—continue to expand the microbial tolerance landscape for lignocellulosic bioconversion [56, 60]. By addressing the toxicity bottleneck in hydrolysate utilization, these strategies can significantly improve the feasibility of xylitol production from lignocellulosic biomass at industrial scale.

Metabolic engineering to enhance xylitol production

Metabolic engineering refers to altering an organism’s metabolic pathway by using genetic engineering tools like enzyme engineering, inhibiting undesirable pathways, and overexpressing rate-limiting enzymes to enhance the desirable compounds. Metabolic engineering offers a promising approach to enhance sugar alcohol (xylitol) that originated from waste or raw products to cater to the demand at the industrial level by decreasing the expression level of XDH and enhancing the XR expression/activity [55, 129, 139, 140]. Xylitol is converted from D-xylose, resulting in a xylitol yield that can be enhanced by either constitutively increasing the expression level of native XR or by expressing the heterologous XR encoding genes [29, 75, 87]. Xylitol production using lignocellulose biomass through metabolic engineering

Here; Batch-SSF is batch mode of operation for Solid-State Fermentation (SSF) and batch- PSSS is fed-batch Pre-saccharification Simultaneous Saccharification and Fermentation (PSSF)

The strategies of metabolic engineering for xylitol production from LCB primarily focus on pathway optimization and sugar co-utilization. Key fermentative sugars include xylose, glucose, and arabinose [31, 33]. When microorganisms metabolize mixed sugars, carbon catabolite repression (CCR) leads to preferential sugar consumption, thereby lowering carbon conversion efficiency. By applying metabolic engineering approaches to overcome CCR, simultaneous utilization of mixed sugars can be achieved, enhancing overall conversion efficiency. A cyclic AMP-independent mutant is a good replacement for the place of native crp, which improved xylitol production in E. coli and overexpressed xylose reductase, resulting in 38 g L−1 xylitol in fermentation [15]. Kim et al. [48] created a mutant of an E. coli GX50 strain, which was able to utilize both glucose and xylose simultaneously, unlike the wild E. coli strain. E. coli GX50 was able to use xylose even in the presence of glucose and rapidly converted xylose to xylitol (4.8 g L−1), which means it overcame the CCR problem. Metabolic pathway engineering for increased xylitol production includes an enhanced expression of the XR gene, XDH gene deletion, an increased supply of cofactor, and heterologous expression of xylose transporters to boost yield [21]. An engineered strain, such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae PE-2 GRE3, using directed evolution and enhanced XR variants, produced 148.5 g L−1 xylitol from xylose-rich media, with high productivity and yield [10]. Knocking out the XDH gene can enhance xylitol production. Zhang et al. [136] created a C. tropicalis mutant by deleting the XDH gene and adding a regeneration system for NADPH. The mutant C. tropicalis XZX-B4ZG produced xylitol (97.10 g L−1 from xylose mother liquor (XML in 120 h, with a 92.40% conversion rate (Xu et al., [127]; [136]). Boosting microbial efficiency in xylitol production—by optimizing xylose metabolism and minimizing by-product generation—depends heavily on metabolic engineering interventions. Careful substrate selection is essential, as lignocellulosic feedstocks differ in fermentable sugar profiles and demand tailored pretreatment methods to achieve high yields while keeping production costs low [99].

Genetically engineered bacterial strains

Bacterial assimilation of d-xylose is made possible via the oxidoreductive route. By co-expressing the genes for XR and GDH, Zhang et al. [135, 141] modified Gluconobacter oxydans from 30 g L−1 d-arabitol to produce 12.23 g L−1 xylitol, which is 1.6 times more than wild strains. Furthermore, a bacterial strain of Pseudomonas putida that produces d-xylose reductase was published by Lugani and Sooch [66, 71]. After a short incubation time, this strain demonstrated an elevated d-XR (xylose reductase) activity of 48.76 IU/mg. With a titer of 35.2 g L−1 and total output 0.98 g L−1 h−1, the strain was able to accomplish an effective xylitol bioconversion. According to Ahuja et al. [2], Pseudomonas gessardii produced 48.49 g of crystalline xylitol after detoxification, with 0.64 g g−1 of xylitol produced originating from hydrolysate obtained from Sugarcane bagasse. A 0.9 g L−1 of xylitol was generated by Burkholderia sacchari during the production of poly-3-hydroxybutyrate [71].

Depending on the fermentation procedure, the recombinant may yield 14.13–29.61 g L−1 of sugar alcohol (xylitol) obtained from hydrolysate from corncob [10, 56]. Additionally, by optimizing SSF, the resultant recombinant produced 71.7 g L−1 of sugar alcohol (xylitol) obtained from hydrolysate from corncob [9, 56].

Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) are excellent in producing polyols because of their fermentative metabolism, simplicity of genetic manipulation, FDA compliance, and redox control during sugar catabolism, which facilitates effective sugar alcohol synthesis [71, 77]. While some LABs employ the XR-XDH route to metabolize d-xylose, others, such as L. pentosus and L. brevis, use the XI pathway. Using d- d-glucose and xylose combinations, Lactobacillus recombinant strains generated xylitol [71]. Nyyssölä et al. [84] created a mutant Lactococcus lactis NZ9800 that co-expressed D-xylose reductase (XYL1) and xylose transporter (xylT). The simultaneous expression of xylose reductase and transporter in Lactococcus lactis achieved 1.0 mol of xylitol per mol of xylose with 2.72 g L−1 h−1 productivity.

Furthermore, E. coli strains produced sugar alcohol (xylitol) from sugars obtained from hemicellulose [71, 80]. By interfering with native d-xylose catabolism (xylA, xylB), catabolite repression (ptsG), and xylitol phosphorylation (ptsF), Su et al. [119] obtained xylitol from a modified E. coli strain [71]. Corynebacterium glutamicum of the wild type converted xylose to 0.6 g L−1 xylitol at a rate of 0.01 g L−1 −1h. Engineering steps, including integration of araE, disruption of ldhA, expression of mutant CtXR (K274R), and deletion of XylB and PTSfru, yielded strain CtXR7. The fed-batch culture of CtXR7 was an effective method that obtained the highest bacterial xylitol production, 166 g L−1 xylitol at 7.9 g L−1 h−1 [110]. In another work, they deleted the pgi gene (glucose-6-phosphate isomerase) in E. coli (DptsG△xylAB△ptsF-4 copies of N. crassa xyl1) and escalated two genes of the pentose phosphate pathway, zwf (glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase) and gnd (phosphogluconate dehydrogenase). The recombinant generated 162 g L−1 of xylitol from corncob hydrolysates in fed-batch fermentation, 13% more than the host strain [56, 131]. To increase the inhibitory resistance in a later investigation, they altered the CRP gene in the previously stated E. coli strain. While host-designed E. coli only generated 30 g L−1 of xylitol, the newly engineered E. coli mutant (I112L, T127G, and A144T) was able to create 82 g L−1 of xylitol from un-detoxified condensed corncob hydrolysate by fed-batch fermentation [56, 130]. Similarly, from detoxified corncob hydrolysate, another mutant (I112L, T127I, and A144T) generated 164 g L−1 xylitol [132].

Under oxygen deprivation, genetic changes of the Corynebacterium glutamicum strain CtXR7 enhanced xylitol synthesis to 166 g L−1 at 7.9 g L−1 h−1. With a 97% yield, recombinant Corynebacterium glutamicum expressing XYL1 generated 34.4 g L−1 of xylitol and 0.092 g g−1 h−1 of productivity.

Genetically engineered fungal strains

By altering the XDH catalytic activity, which converts xylitol to D-xylulose, the accumulation of xylitol can be enhanced. Enhancing xylitol accumulation can be achieved by partially inhibiting XDH activity, thereby reducing the conversion of xylitol to D-xylulose [29, 52, 69, 75].

In Aspergillus niger, sugar alcohols arabitol, sorbitol, and xylitol are converted to D-xylulose through L-arabitol dehydrogenase (ladA), sorbitol dehydrogenase (sdhA), and xylitol dehydrogenase (xdhA), respectively. Further, D-xylulose kinase (xkiA) catalyzes the D-xylulose into D-xylulose-5-phosphate [14].

Meng et al. [75] created a triple mutant ΔladAΔxdhAΔsdhA and a single gene mutant ΔxkiA of A. niger N593 Δku70 and observed that xylitol accumulation was increased. ΔxkiA accumulated about 5 mM of xylitol and L-arabitol intracellularly in a D-xylose/L-arabinose mixture. The infusion of fermentation media with D-xylose and L-arabinose Helped to boost xylitol accumulation to about 20 mM in the triple mutant. The strain could produce 1.49–2.20 mM xylitol in a shake flask from 1% beechwood xylan, wheat bran, and cotton seed hulls, respectively. Additionally, they eliminated xkiA (xylulose kinase) to stop xylulose from being diverted to the PP pathway. However, there was no advantage to the deletion [56, 75]. As demonstrated by C. tropicalis expressing Neurospora crassa XR under a constitutive promoter, codon optimization enhanced enzyme production in non-native hosts, overcoming glucose suppression and increasing catalytic efficiency [44, 71]. Significant increases in xylitol output have been made possible by sophisticated methods such as mutagenesis in ambient and room-temperature plasma (ARTP). For instance, compared to natural strains, Candida tropicalis mutants generated 22% more of xylitol because of increased XR gene expression [71].

A five-carbon compound, xylitol, was obtained from a wild-type fungal strain that was able to grow on growth media which have only d-xylose as a carbon source. When cultivated on d-xylose media, the zygomycete Thamnidium elegans could yield 31.13 g L−1 of xylitol [71, 144]. Trichoderma reesei was genetically altered to increase xylitol synthesis, reaching 22.8 mM with three major modifications: d-glucokinase activity was decreased by silencing xyiH, xylitol levels were raised by disrupting xdh, and production was increased by 1.08 times by combining XR overexpression with Δxdh [40, 71].

The genome of certain fungal species encodes d-xylose reductase, an important enzyme for d-xylose reduction and xylitol synthesis. XyrA, the gene code for d-xylose reductase present in the fungus Aspergillus niger, was initially identified by Khosravi et al. [46]. This gene catalyzes the reduction of d-xylose via the pentose catabolic pathway (PCP). Terebieniec et al. [120] were able to identify another gene, XyrB, that also codes for the d-xylose reductase gene from the same fungus species, A. niger. Díaz-Fernández et al. [20] engineered A. gossypii for xylose-based biolipid production. Overexpressing the xylose pathway achieves 97% xylose-to-xylitol conversion, while introducing phosphoketolase and blocking beta-oxidation increases Lipid content by 69% (Table 2). Coniochaeta ligniaria NRRL30616 improves biomass fermentability by metabolizing inhibitors. A mutant, C8100, prepared by Nicholas and Saha, was able to generate 0.48 g g–1 of xylitol from hydrolysate obtained from 0.59 g g–1 of xylose in rich medium.

Genetically engineered yeast strains

Significant progress has been made in genetically modifying naturally occurring yeasts that produce xylitol. Overexpression of the XR gene and disruption of the XDH gene quadrupled the yields of xylitol in Meyerozyma guilliermondii ATCC 6260 [7, 71].

By using NADPH regeneration, recombinant Pichia pastoris expressing XR and glucose dehydrogenase was able to reach an 80% conversion efficiency, even in hydrolysates that were not detoxified [65, 71]. [34]) increased Saccharomyces cerevisiae’s xylitol synthesis by showcasing enzymes on the cell Surface, resulting in a 44% conversion rate and a 3.7 g L−1 xylitol output after 96 h. Likewise, the disruption of the fructose phosphotransferase system (pts) that is dependent on phosphoenolpyruvate hindered the possible route for xylitol phosphorylation. The oleaginous yeast Kluyveromyces marxianus was modified to make xylitol and ethanol from Lignocellulosic biomass at the same time. XR overexpression increased the output of xylitol by 1.62 times without affecting the synthesis of ethanol [18, 71]. They overexpressed six duplicate copies of the XR (xylose reductase) from strain N. crassa xyl1 and three duplicate copies from S. cerevisiae gal2-N376F strain for xylose transporter in a host strain of K. marxianus (Dhxk1-glk1 + +) to eliminate glucose suppression.

A study conducted by Zhang et al. [138] obtained an engineered strain of K. marxianus (Dxyl2, 4 duplicate copies for xylose/glucose facilitator xyl1 from N. crassa and C. intermedia co-overexpressed two XR (xylose reductases) generated from N. crassa and C. tropicalis. The resultant strain demonstrated the capacity to generate 105.22 g L−1 of sugar alcohol (xylitol) from hydrolysate produced from corncob. This strain overexpressed and showed an elevated amount of xylitol, producing 1.25 g L−1 h−1 in terms of productivity, with glycerol supplementation through a fed-batch fermentation method [56, 138]. A similar kind of result was reported by Baptista et al. [10] from native xylose reductase (gre3) that overexpressed in S. cerevisiae. Hua et al. [41] also reported xylitol production from hydrolysate produced from corncob.

These developments highlight the potential of modified microorganisms for the long-term bioproduction of xylitol. Through transcriptomic, phylogenetic analyses, and biochemical analyses, the newly identified gene XyrB has been associated with the depletion of C5 sugars from hemicellulosic materials, namely d-arabinose and d-xylose. NADPH-dependent d-xylose reductase from Emericella nidulanss was described in vitro and in silico by [71]. The enzyme used xylose as a substrate to generate xylitol. Additionally, it showed a high level of enzyme activity and was able to withstand large quantities of xylose.

It performs bioconversion, enzymatic hydrolysis, and cellulase synthesis all at once [71, 111]. According to Mahmoud and Abd El-Zaher [61, 68] and Liu and Qu (2019), CBP is a perfect strategy that has effectively enabled the manufacturing of second-generation chemicals and fuels. Additionally, lignocellulosic fungi may absorb C5-sugars from lignocellulosic biomass. Accordingly, these Sugars may be reduced in a single step, producing Sugar alcohols or lactones with a theoretical yield of 100% [61, 71]. It has been shown that the fungus N. crassa has a high d-xylose reductase activity in the presence of xylitol [125].

Furthermore, it has been discovered that N. crassa expresses the xylitol dehydrogenase gene, xdh. The capacity of N. crassa to produce xylitol should be further investigated since it can express the two crucial enzymes in the xylitol metabolic pathway. Furthermore, it is reported that the fungus could use the three primary monosaccharides found in plant biomass: d-glucose, d-xylose, and d-arabinose [59, 71].

Although research on filamentous fungus producing xylitol is scarce, several researchers have identified strains that show promise for producing the polyalcohol industrially.

This study engineered Kluyveromyces marxianus by modifying ergosterol biosynthesis genes (Erg2–Erg6), altering membrane sterol composition, and enhancing stress tolerance. Notably, Erg5 knockout improved thermotolerance, Erg4 overexpression enhanced acid tolerance, and Erg6 overexpression increased tolerance to heat, hypertonicity, and acidity. According to Ren et al. [103], the engineered (YZB453 strain) generated 101.22 g L−1 of 5-carbon compound (xylitol) at 45 °C and 75.11 g L−1 at 46 °C. In comparison to the control, the modified strain LXT2 demonstrated a 25% increase in xylitol production (1.14 g L−1 h−1) and a 29% increase in xylose absorption rate. This study shows how optimizing transporter expression can improve xylose consumption in glucose/xylose combinations [43].

The XR gene of Neurospora crassa was used to develop many Kluyveromyces marxianus strains to obtain high xylitol production. Strain YZJ015, with xylitol dehydrogenase, reached 1.49 g L−1 h−1 of excellent productivity at 42 °C, producing 71.35 g L−1 after 20 cell recycling cycles. Strain YZJ017, lacking the xylitol dehydrogenase gene, produced 100.02 g L−1 xylitol with a 1.01 g g−1 yield, demonstrating the potential for high-temperature xylitol production [139]. Kluyveromyces marxianus YZJ017 overexpressed three transporter genes (KmFPS1, CiGXF1, and CiGXS1) to increase the synthesis of xylitol at high temperatures. CiGXF1-containing strain YZJ074 generated up to 149.6 g L−1 of xylitol at 42 °C and 101.3 g L−1 at 45 °C. High-temperature xylitol production capability was demonstrated by fed-batch fermentation, which produced a record 312.05 g L−1 of xylitol [140]

Xylitol was able to be synthesized from hydrolysates produced from lignocellulosic biomass and assessed in Candida intermedia FL023. It produced 45.7 g L−1 of xylitol with 0.38 g L−1 h−1 productivity and 0.57 g g−1 yield while tolerating inhibitors at the time of fermentation process during xylitol production [126]. Wang et al. employed Bacillus subtilis Bs12 and Candida tropicalis X828 in a single-pot system to convert waste xylose mother liquor (WXML) into xylitol. Purification, biotransformation, and detoxification were all integrated in a bioreactor. The method achieved 95 g L−1 xylitol from 400 g L−1 WXML with a yield of 0.75 g g−1 xylose, depleting sugars from by-product.

Conclusion

Xylitol is widely used as a sweetener in the food and pharmaceutical industries due to its health benefits and functionality as a sugar substitute. Currently, the industrial production of xylitol primarily relies on chemical methods, which convert purified xylose derived from biomass hydrolysate. However, to make biosynthetic production viable and competitive at an industrial scale, further research is essential to increase xylitol yield from cost-effective and abundant feedstocks, such as glucose and lignocellulosic sugars. Compared to conventional chemical methods, biotechnological approaches offer several advantages for xylitol production, including sustainability and the potential for process optimization.

Significant opportunities exist to improve xylitol bioproduction, pre-treatment with the breakdown of lignocellulosic biomass and continuing through to the final crystallization of the product. However, several bottlenecks hinder commercial application. These include inefficient and costly hydrolysis methods for biomass conversion, generation of toxic by-products (e.g., furfural, HMF, and organic acids) during pre-treatment that inhibit microbial fermentation, and limited tolerance of natural microbial strains to such inhibitors. Moreover, native xylitol producers often have slow growth rates and limited sugar utilization capacities, while engineered strains face challenges in maintaining genetic stability and performance under industrial conditions.

At the scale-up level, maintaining consistent fermentation performance in large bioreactors is difficult due to issues such as oxygen transfer limitations, substrate gradients, and foaming. Additionally, downstream processing for xylitol purification (e.g., removal of fermentation broth impurities and crystallization) remains energy-intensive and cost-prohibitive for commercial deployment.

Advances in each of these stages can contribute to increased overall yields. Moreover, the advent of CRISPR-Cas9 technology has revolutionized genome editing, offering powerful tools for microbial strain improvement. Despite its promise, several challenges remain, such as the development of robust genetic tools, efficient CRISPR/Cas9 systems, and enhanced transformation techniques for effective gene editing.

Future efforts should thus focus on addressing these critical challenges through integrated approaches: improving biomass hydrolysis and detoxification techniques, engineering or screening robust microbial strains with higher resistance to inhibitors and better sugar co-utilization, and developing scalable, cost-effective fermentation and downstream processes. Such multidisciplinary advances are essential to realize economically feasible and environmentally sustainable biotechnological production of xylitol.
