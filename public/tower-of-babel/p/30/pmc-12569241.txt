Optimal architecture for a sentiment analysis transformer with multihead attention and genetic crossover
Authors: Wiam Saidi, Boutaina Satouri, Abdellatif El Abderrahmani, Khalid Satori
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSentiment analysis, a key component of natural language processing, is of paramount importance in various fields such as strategic surveillance, online image management, and customer satisfaction assessment. However, despite recent advances, improving the accuracy and adaptability of models remains a crucial challenge. This paper presents a cutting-edge method that combines the Transformer with evolutionary optimization techniques to improve sentiment analysis results. We rely on the use of pre-trained models to obtain embeddings and language features, thus guaranteeing a rich and relevant textual representation. Our method, called OAST-MAGC (Optimal Architecture for a Sentiment Analysis Transformer with Multihead Attention and Genetic Crossover), is distinguished by incorporating a multihead attention mechanism optimized using a genetic crossover technique. The originality of our approach lies in the integration of dynamic pruning, which selects the most relevant attention heads before starting genetic optimization. This step reduces the search space, improves convergence speed, and produces a lighter and more efficient final model. Additionally, the weights of the final layers are combined through genetic crossover, which promotes more efficient generalization and reduces the risk of overfitting. This architecture, which merges the effectiveness of pre-trained models with a dynamic optimization approach, offers a significant improvement in the accuracy and efficiency of sentiment analysis tasks. Experiments have proven that our method outperforms current techniques in terms of performance and robustness, achieving an accuracy of 95.96% and an F1-score of 96%, opening the way to new possibilities in processing complex textual data.Keywords: Transformer, Sentiment analysis, Dynamic pruning, Multihead attention, Genetic crossover, Embeddings

Introduction

Sentiment analysis, often called “opinion mining”, is a discipline of natural language processing that aims to extract and classify emotions or opinions expressed in a text. It plays a crucial role in various fields such as marketing, politics, or public health, allowing us to understand user trends and perceptions1. This ability to interpret emotions from massive text data has become essential in the digital age, particularly on social networks2. Analyzing the feelings expressed in a text is a particularly complex task due to the richness and diversity of language. Each text reflects not only the thoughts and emotions of its author but also their personal history, social environment, cultural context, and level of education3. For example, the words and phrases chosen can vary considerably depending on lived experiences, local traditions, or linguistic influences. Moreover, language is deeply nuanced, including implicit elements such as irony, humor, or double entendres, which add an extra level of subtlety to the interpretation of feelings. This complexity is further accentuated by the diversity of dialects and linguistic registers, which make automatic analysis all the more challenging. Take English, for example, one of the most widely used languages in the world, which is characterized by an impressive variety of dialects and expressions. These differences can often lead to misunderstandings, including among native speakers4. Furthermore, languages are constantly evolving, regularly enriching themselves with new words and seeing existing terms take on new meanings. This dynamic nature of languages further complicates sentiment analysis, as opinions and their expression are continually evolving2.

In this context, social media plays a central role as platforms for expressing feelings. Millions of people use sites like Facebook, Instagram, and especially Twitter to share their ideas, emotions, and reactions. Twitter stands out for its unique format: short, dynamic messages, rich in emojis, hashtags, and stylistic devices like irony and sarcasm5. This wealth, although valuable, poses challenges for effective automatic analysis.

Historically, sentiment analysis emerged with approaches based primarily on classical machine learning methods. These first techniques exploited tools such as TF-IDF (Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency) vectors, which make it possible to represent a text in the form of vectors by weighting words according to their relative importance in a given corpus. Once these vectors were constructed, algorithms such as support vector machines (SVM), Naïve Bayes (NB) models, logistic regression, or k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) were used to classify the sentiments expressed in the texts6. These methods enabled significant advances to be made at a time when text data volumes were smaller and contextual complexity requirements were limited.

However, these approaches had several intrinsic limitations. First, they relied heavily on feature engineering, a step that required considerable time and expertise to manually identify relevant aspects of the text. For example, n-grams (sequences of adjacent words) or statistical metrics like word frequency were often used to capture salient features of a text, but these methods remain too simplistic to capture complex linguistic structures. Also, these models are limited in their ability to consider the overall context of a text. They typically analyze words or word sequences in isolation, without a true understanding of the relationships that may exist between words located at different positions in a sentence or document. This poses a major problem for handling language nuances, such as irony or sarcasm, which require deep contextual understanding7. Moreover, these models are poorly suited to handling massive and diverse datasets, characteristic of modern platforms like Twitter. Social media data is often noisy, multilingual, and full of new terms, abbreviations, and emojis. Classical machine learning algorithms cannot easily adapt to these rapid variations and linguistic dynamics. These limitations have gradually paved the way for more sophisticated approaches, based on deep learning techniques and, more recently, Transformer architectures. These new methods aim to overcome the weaknesses of traditional approaches by enabling a more refined understanding of natural language, notably thanks to their ability to efficiently capture global context and long dependencies between words.

In 2017, a major breakthrough was made with the introduction of Transformers by Vaswani et al. in the article "Attention is All You Need." This new architecture, based on the Multi-Head Attention mechanism, revolutionized natural language processing. Transformers make it possible to efficiently capture relationships between words, even when they are far apart in a sentence, while processing sequences in parallel8.

Overall, our OAST-MAGC approach represents a notable innovation by combining the power of pre-trained models with the evolutionary optimization capabilities of genetic algorithms. In particular, our major methodological innovation is to combine dynamic pruning and genetic crossover within the Transformer framework. Initially, we implement a heuristic pruning procedure that evaluates attention heads based on relevance scores determined from the evolution of loss, attention entropy, and gradient weights. This criterion, which is both simple and effective, allows us to keep only the most informative heads, thus reducing redundancy and the space required for search before optimization. Subsequently, instead of using all heads uniformly, we implement a genetic crossover strategy not only to refine the choice of these heads, but also to merge the weights of the final layers. This cooperation stimulates accelerated convergence, improved generalization, and enhanced resistance to noisy information. Unlike previous studies such as Voita et al. (2019), which relied on static head pruning, our method uses evolutionary optimization to dynamically refine both the choice of heads and the adjustment of layer weights. This strategic choice allows us to benefit from a rich linguistic representation, acquired from a very large unannotated corpus. Using BERT not only accelerates the training process (through transfer learning), but also reduces the need for task-specific annotated data, while ensuring better capture of contextual dependencies in texts.

Related work

Sentiment analysis is a major area of interest in natural language processing (NLP), and numerous approaches have been proposed to improve its accuracy and robustness. These approaches can be classified into three main categories:

Classical methods

These approaches rely on traditional statistical models and machine learning algorithms such as Naïve Bayes, support vector machines (SVM), and random forests. They were the first to be used in sentiment analysis and remain relevant in certain contexts.

First, methods based on the Random Forest algorithm have demonstrated the encouraging effectiveness of term weighting methods when using this algorithm for sentiment analysis.

In this context, the author9 focuses on the study of sentiments as a case of text classification, specifically for the Indonesian language, in their article entitled "Random Forest Approach for Sentiment Analysis in Indonesian Language." The aim is to examine the application of the Random Forest algorithm for sentiment classification and to analyze the influence of various term weighting techniques on the performance of the sentiment analysis system.

The experiment is based on a corpus of 386 reviews collected on FemaleDaily, a platform where users express their views in Indonesian. Sentiments are classified as positive or negative based on the reviews. It also examines the use of Bag of Words (BOW) model features with different variants of term weighting techniques such as raw TF, binary TF, TF-IDF, and logarithmic TF9.

The results indicate that the sentiment analysis system based on the Random Forest method displays positive performance, with an average Out of Bag (OOB) score of 0.829. The four term weighting techniques show encouraging results. However, the variations in scores are not significant, which means that the selection of the term weighting method is of little importance in sentiment analysis using Random Forest9.

There are also methods based on maximum entropy, the naive Bayesian classifier, Random Forest, and gradient boosting, which provide a kind of comparison in terms of performance and limits between the four algorithms.

In this context, the authors10 propose a comparative study of the classification algorithms used in the analysis of feelings. This research focuses on four frequently used algorithms: maximum entropy, the naive Bayesian classifier, Random Forest, and well-detailed boosting gradient in their article " Comparative Study of Classification Algorithms used in Sentiment Analysis ".

The study is based on a dataset comprising textual reviews, which are frequently used in sentiment analysis. The dataset contains comments where people share their thoughts about a product, thus providing more detailed feedback regarding various aspects of the product. Subsequently, sentiment analysis engines process these comments iteratively and produce results by assigning them polarities such as negative, positive, or neutral10.

The analysis examines the performance of four selected classification techniques. Each algorithm is detailed based on fundamental principles, common use cases, and strengths and weaknesses. The comparative evaluation of classification algorithms provides insights into their effectiveness in sentiment analysis10.

Despite its simplicity, Naïve Bayes shows appreciable effectiveness and is recommended when limited resources and training time are important constraints. Maximum entropy shows superior effectiveness compared to Naïve Bayes, especially when it relies on past results. Gradient Boosting offers fast learning without compromising accuracy, while being able to handle various kinds of predictor variables, while Random Forest shows remarkable performance on different datasets and is robust to irrelevant information, making it a technique well suited for generalization10.

Deep learning methods

With the rise of deep neural architectures, models such as CNN, RNN and more recently Transformers (BERT, RoBERTa) have significantly improved the quality of sentiment analysis by capturing complex relationships between words.

Firstly, methods based on deep learning overcome the difficulties encountered during NLP and provide encouraging results.

Indeed, in their article named "Sentiment Analysis Based on Deep Learning: A Comparative Study", the authors confirmed that the effectiveness and accuracy of sentiment analysis are affected by the difficulties encountered in natural language processing (NLP).

Studies have recently proven that deep learning models are a promising answer to the challenges of natural language processing.

The results indicate that combining deep learning methods with word embeddings rather than TF-IDF is more advantageous when performing sentiment analysis. They also demonstrated that CNN outperforms other models, offering a good trade-off between accuracy and CPU execution speed. Generally, RNN exhibits slightly higher reliability compared to CNN with most datasets, however, its processing time is significantly higher. A final conclusion of the analysis is that the effectiveness of the algorithms relies significantly on the properties of the datasets, which highlights the need to experiment with more datasets to encompass a wider range of features11.

Finally, methods based on the combination of several Deep Learning methods (hybrids) show satisfactory results for sentiment analysis.

In fact, the authors suggest a novel method of sentiment analysis based on aspects in an opinion text, as mentioned in their article " Analyse de sentiments à base d’aspects par combinaison de réseaux profonds: application à des avis en français ". This method integrates several deep networks: b-LSTM, CNN, a CRF model, and an attention memory network. Evaluation on three sets of French comments revealed superior performance compared to existing methods, demonstrating the advantage of this combination for aspect extraction and for sentiment analysis attached to each extracted aspect12.

Hybrid methods

These approaches combine traditional techniques and deep learning models to leverage the advantages of each method. These methods optimize parameter selection and improve overall performance.

First, methods based on BERT and CBRNN provide significantly higher performance compared to conventional models such as CNN and LSTM, especially with regard to accuracy.

Indeed, in his article entitled "Transformer-based deep learning models for the sentiment analysis of social media data", the author proposes a hybrid model based on BERT, combined with a bidirectional recurrent convolutional neural network (CBRNN), to improve sentiment analysis on social media data. This model uses BERT to extract contextual representations from texts, then applies a dilated convolution mechanism to extract local and global features, as well as a Bi-LSTM to capture long-term dependencies in text sequences. The experimental results, evaluated on four datasets from various domains, show that this approach outperformed other classical models such as CNN and LSTM in terms of precision, recall and AUC. The model performed particularly well on tweet datasets, achieving high scores in sentiment classification. The paper demonstrates that integrating BERT with convolutional and recurrent networks significantly improves the handling of complex social data, thus providing a robust method for sentiment analysis in unstructured and noisy environments13.

Second, methods based on the combination of MHA and CNN aim to overcome the shortcomings of CNNs while providing a robust foundation for sentiment analysis.

In fact, in their article "Improved Aspect-level Sentiment Analysis Method based on Multi-Head Attention Mechanism," the authors confirmed that, to date, the model combining the attention mechanism and the recurrent neural network, with its variants, is emerging as the preferred method for tackling aspect-level sentiment analysis tasks. However, this approach is constrained by the inherent weaknesses of the RNN: the training time is too long, and the relationship between words tends to diminish with increasing distance. Even with the integration of the attention mechanism, the previous problems will persist. Thus, this paper presents the integration of the multi-head attention mechanism into the GCAE (Gated Convolutional Network with Aspect Embedding) model, and then proposes the GCAE-MHA (Improved GCAE Model based on the Multi-Head Attention mechanism) model15.

This model simultaneously treats the context and the particular elements of the textual sequence while assimilating the interactive relationship between them. And finally, it combines the two elements to serve as the basis for sentiment classification. To overcome the limitation of the convolutional neural network that can only capture local dependencies between words, the GCAE-MHA model also incorporates the use of a dilated convolutional neural network. This approach allows extracting semantic information over long distances and defining a specific dilation to obtain a more enriched semantic feature. Finally, tests are carried out on the SemEval2014 dataset and the Twitter dataset. The experimental tests reveal that the GCAE-MHA model can significantly optimize sentiment analysis at the aspect level while preserving the simple network structure of the model15.

Thirdly, methods based on the readjustment of pre-trained models are models that favor improving the effectiveness of the latter as well for sentiment analysis.

In this context, other authors consider in their article entitled "Transformers in Sentiment Analysis: A Paradigm Shift in Deep Learning Research" that it is the innovative architecture and unparalleled effectiveness of Transformers that have revolutionized the sentiment analysis paradigm, causing considerable repercussions in the field of deep learning. An attention mechanism focuses on relevant features of the input in the self-attention layer, leading to a reevaluation of both pre-trained models such as RoBERTa, BERT, and GPT at each stage of the sentiment analysis process. These models leverage self-attention processes, giving them the ability to capture semantic and syntactic dependencies more effectively compared to convolutional and recurrent networks. This results in notable improvements in different missions of neuro-linguistic programming (NLP)16.

A methodical approach was implemented, including fine-tuning the pre-trained transformers on various datasets and comparing their performance with state-of-the-art techniques. Multiple trials demonstrated improvements in precision, accuracy, and recall in various domains such as customer reviews, social media sentiment, and financial data analysis. The analysis unveils major findings that illustrate the flexibility of the models in responding to the particular challenges of the domain through transfer learning, as well as their performance in handling imbalanced datasets16. Additionally, the paper also discusses the tradeoffs between scalability, computational cost, and other factors when implementing transformers in real-world applications. This research, which highlights the effectiveness of models based on the transformer for understanding semantics and syntax, proves that these deep learning architectures are redefining performance standards in the field of sentiment analysis. They also provide a solid foundation for developing more explainable superclass models, suggesting considerable potential to influence current and future trends in natural language processing and beyond16.

Finally, methods based on transformer substitutes are models that demonstrate their effectiveness, particularly in Arabic sentiment analysis.

Indeed, other authors have also proposed, in their article entitled "Switch-Transformer Sentiment Analysis Model for Arabic Dialects That Utilizes a Mixture of Experts Mechanism," a substitute model, the switch-transformer, to overcome the limitations of self-attention in five-polarity Arabic sentiment analysis. This challenge is particularly important when dealing with the Arabic dialect, recognized as a language with limited resources. Faced with these challenges, this article proposes a new approach to sentiment analysis in Arabic text. This method uses multi-task learning (MTL), combined with the common switch-transformer encoder, to optimize the model’s adaptive capacity and improve sentence representations17.

By adopting a method of decomposing the problem into simpler subproblems through a mixture of experts technique, the model gains the ability to handle elongated sequences and complex input–output relationships. This proves beneficial for five-level, three-polarity Arabic sentiment analysis tasks. The suggested model effectively detects emotions in dialectal Arabic sentences17.

The remarkable performance is highlighted by empirical data, with accuracy rates reaching 84.02% for the Hotel Arabic-Reviews Dataset (HARD), 67.89% for the Hotel Arabic-Reviews Dataset (BRAD), and 83.91% for the Large-Scale Arabic Book Reviews (LABR). These results come from evaluations carried out on these different datasets17.

The following sections will detail these three families of methods, highlighting their strengths, limitations, and applications in the field of sentiment analysis.

Model used: OAST-MAGC

The use of Transformers incorporating Multi-Head Attention (MHA) combined with genetic crossover, a key element of genetic algorithms, represents an optimal architecture for sentiment analysis. Its self-attention mechanism, which evaluates the relationships between different tokens in a sequence, is at the heart of Transformer’s capabilities. It has proven effective in natural language processing (NLP). Genetic algorithms, on the other hand, have the ability to optimize multiple parameters simultaneously.

In addition to the dataset split and computational environment, we report here the main hyperparameter settings used in our experiments, in order to ensure reproducibility and facilitate comparison.

The proposed model is built upon a pre-trained BERT-Base encoder consisting of 12 layers, a hidden size of 768, and 12 initial attention heads. The maximum sequence length was set to 128 tokens, with a dropout rate of 0.1 and the GELU activation function. Training was performed using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 3 × 10 − 5, a batch size of 32, and a total of 5 epochs. A linear learning rate decay scheduler with warmup and gradient clipping fixed at 1.0 were also applied.

For the pruning phase, approximately 40% of the least significant attention heads were removed based on an importance criterion derived from attention scores. The genetic algorithm for head selection was configured with a population size of 30, 80 generations, a crossover probability of 0.8, and a mutation probability of 0.1. The fitness function was defined as the accuracy (or F1-score) measured on the validation set.

Self-attention, which involves evaluating the relationships between different tokens in a sequence, is at the heart of the Transformer’s operation. A simple and empirically effective feature of MHA is that multiple attention heads operate simultaneously and autonomously from each other, which has disadvantages such as the low-rank limitation problem in attention score matrices22,23 reduces expressive capacity, as well as the issue of redundant heads which leads to wasteful parameters and computation24,25.

To optimize the dynamic selection of the most appropriate attention heads, we use a method based on adaptive genetic crossover.

Genetic crossover

To avoid attention head redundancy and optimize their expressiveness, we introduce a dynamic selection mechanism based on genetic crossover. Rather than using all attention heads uniformly, our approach applies an initial filtering to retain only the most relevant heads, evaluated according to their importance score in the attention distribution.

The evolution of attention heads is then guided by a genetic algorithm, which optimizes their combination over generations. At each iteration, the best-performing heads are selected, crossed, and mutated to produce an improved set, thus reducing redundancy and promoting better generalization capacity.

Additionally, this algorithm allows for initial filtering, avoiding the inclusion of redundant heads from the outset. It promotes progressive evolution by dynamically selecting the most relevant heads and optimizing their interactions. Indeed, the use of genetic crossover ensures proper exploration without disrupting the best-selected heads.

In their article entitled "Remaining Useful Life Estimation of Aeroengine Based on Multi-head Attention LSTM Model and Genetic Algorithm", the authors present a new joint prediction model based on the Multi-head Attention LSTM model and the genetic algorithm (MHALN-GA) to address the inefficiency in extracting multidimensional engine data and the lack of guarantees regarding optimal model solutions. The system is designed for counting medical images using RNA sequences. The initial steps involve extracting tricolor canvases, converting them into vectors, and applying genetic crossover to create a new RNA gene. This gene improves performance and enables targeted attacks. The system has been tested on various images from several databases27.

Genetic algorithms are optimization methods based on processes from natural evolution and genetics such as selection, crossover, and mutation. It was proposed by J.H. Holland in the early 1970s and is used in fields such as function optimization, finance, economics, differential game theory, and repeated game theory29.

It is based on data coding, where each parameter of a solution is treated as a gene. All possible values for that parameter represent the alleles of that gene. A method must be established to code each allele uniquely when creating the initial population. It is during the initialization of the genetic algorithm that the initial individuals are created, while selection is used to identify individuals that could be crossed within a population. Several selection methods can be cited, such as selection by rank, roulette, or tournament. Crossover, on the other hand, merges the attributes of a group of parents (often two) previously chosen to produce new offspring individuals. Mutation, on the other hand, represents a possible random change of a gene on a chromosome, serving to prevent premature convergence of the algorithm30.

Statistical validation of the results

To validate the robustness of the distinctions noted between our OAST-MAGC model and the reference models, two additional statistical tests were implemented.

First, we used a paired Student t test, which is appropriate when two models are examined on the same data segments. This test evaluates whether the average deviation between pairs of results (such as the accuracy of OAST-MAGC and RoBERTa for each run) is significantly different from zero. Technically, the test statistic is defined as follows:

where is the mean of the differences, the standard deviation of the differences and n the number of pairs (here n = 5). The null hypothesis H0 states that there is no difference between the two models ( = 0), while the alternative hypothesis H1 states the existence of a significant difference ( ≠ 0). In our case, the results show that the gains in accuracy and F1-score brought by OAST-MAGC are highly significant with p < 0.01.

Furthermore, in order to improve methodological rigor and avoid any presumption of normality of distributions, we also implemented the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Unlike the t test, this analysis does not rely on assumptions of Gaussian distribution. It evaluates absolute deviations between pairs, assigns ranks, and compares the sum of positive and negative ranks. The null hypothesis H0 states that the median of the deviations is zero, while the alternative hypothesis H1 states that the median is not equal to zero. Our findings indicate that the Wilcoxon signed-rank test validates the results of the t-test, while also demonstrating high statistical significance (p < 0.01).

Applying these two tests together strengthens our inferences: the progress made with OAST-MAGC is not accidental, but reflects a significant and statistically confirmed improvement in performance.

Conclusion

In this article, we introduced OAST-MAGC, an innovative architecture for sentiment analysis, combining the strengths of pre-trained Transformers and dynamic pruning with a multi-head attention mechanism optimized by genetic algorithms. Indeed, our goal is not to present a radical redesign of Transformer architectures, but rather to provide a progressive and pragmatic improvement in response to the limitations already identified in existing work. Our method is distinguished by the strategic union of two complementary processes: a heuristic dynamic pruning that reduces the redundancy of attention heads, and an evolutionary optimization by genetic crossover implemented both on the chosen heads and on the weights of the final layers. Although this methodological association relies on familiar techniques, it offers a proven empirical advantage: improved stability, faster convergence and enhanced resilience to data perturbations.

Empirical results on the US-Airline Tweets database highlight a substantial improvement in performance compared to classic methods (SVM, Random Forest), Deep Learning approaches (CNN, RNN), as well as standard Transformers (mBERT, RoBERTa). OAST-MAGC obtains an accuracy of 95.96% and an F1-score of 96%, clearly outperforming the reference models.

Furthermore, our model is distinguished by increased robustness to noisy data, demonstrated through perturbation tests, as well as high inter-class discrimination capacity, validated by ROC-AUC analysis (macro-AUC of 0.98, micro-AUC of 0.97). These results confirm the effectiveness of genetic crossover as an optimization mechanism in Transformer architectures, particularly in tasks sensitive to noise and linguistic subtleties.

Our findings, statistically corroborated by Student and Wilcoxon tests as well as 95% confidence intervals, demonstrate that the observed benefits are significant and reproducible. Therefore, the contribution of this work lies in the careful strengthening and refinement of existing approaches, providing an effective and practical solution for large-scale sentiment analysis, rather than claiming to be a radical break in terms of methodology.

In sum, OAST-MAGC opens promising prospects for the development of more intelligent, adaptive and robust NLP models, particularly suited to sentiment analysis on large-scale platforms such as social networks.
