Optimized multi-modal action recognition using multi-agent systems and adaptive temporal attention
Authors: Essam H. Houssein, Mohamed A. Mahdy, Mohammed Kayed, Nagwan Abdel Samee, Waleed M. Mohamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The recognition of human actions through visual input poses considerable difficulties owing to the diverse ways in which individuals perform the same actions, the temporal variations that are intrinsic to these actions, and the differing viewpoints from which they are perceived. In order to address the limitations of single-modality methods, researchers have increasingly adopted multimodal visual data fusion strategies. This study presents an optimized deep learning model designed for action recognition by combining multiple data modalities, such as depth, skeleton, and inertial data through applying multi-agent systems. The study utilized a frame selection method based on the displacement of skeleton joints with uniform resampling for selection over the temporal-space; further, the study employed the HOG extractor on depth selected frames, 1D convolutional block for skeleton and inertial features extracting, and LSTM to process temporal patterns. Furthermore, an adaptive cross-attention method is developed to dynamically assess and implement the relative significance of the learned long-range temporal dependencies from each input modality. The effectiveness of the model is evaluated using the publicly available UTD MHAD dataset, demonstrating enhanced performance compared to existing leading action recognition techniques, achieving accuracy of 96.99%. A thorough assessment methodology, which includes confusion matrices, ROC curves, t-SNE visualizations, and average attention heatmaps, is utilized to confirm the model’s robustness and to provide insights on its performance. The results underscore the model’s capability in utilizing multi-modal data fusion and sophisticated temporal feature extraction, presenting a promising strategy for advancing action recognition tasks.

Introduction

Human Action Recognition (HAR) has become a prominent research domain in computer vision, motivated by its diverse applications that include autonomous surveillance, navigation, intelligent transportation monitoring, and video explanation1. In recent years, action recognition has made significant advancements. Nevertheless, it continues to encounter substantial challenges arising from several factors, such as the vast diversity of action categories, the complex nature of actions, contextual ambiguities, differences in perspective, background noise, variations in viewpoint, and the differences in how individuals perform actions.

Furthermore, the precision of recognition is influenced by variations in the size and resolution of individuals depicted in videos. The presence of analogous poses across different actions adds to the challenges of action recognition. Additionally, the intricacy of recognition can intensify when several individuals engage in complex actions. The analysis of substantial volumes of video data requires significant computational resources, thereby heightening the complexity of HAR.

The fundamental methods for HAR discussed in previous research2 predominantly utilized traditional RGB video. The extensive accessibility of affordable depth sensors, such as the Microsoft Kinect3, has facilitated the creation of multi-modal action data, which encompasses RGB, depth, skeleton, and additional formats4. Each type of data modality has its unique advantages and disadvantages. Segmenting the human body in particular contexts is challenging for techniques that depend on RGB data, as they are susceptible to various elements that can degrade image quality, including intricate backgrounds, lighting fluctuations, and clothing colors. Additionally, RGB data lacks three-dimensional information, which is essential for proficient action recognition. In contrast, depth information significantly enhances the segmentation of foreground objects within a crowded background. Furthermore, it can reliably identify an action irrespective of the subject’s clothing color. This has prompted efforts to create a robust action feature descriptor based on depth data for implementation in AR systems. Nevertheless, as noted in5, depth data remains vulnerable to noise due to several factors.

The application of skeleton data in HAR has become increasingly popular, largely due to its effectiveness and robustness against variations in lighting conditions6. This skeleton data includes details about the movement paths of the body’s joints. Additionally, depth data, which is commonly employed in HAR, offers valuable insights into the three-dimensional configuration and spatial distance metrics. A considerable amount of research in HAR has primarily focused on a single modality throughout the recent research timeline7. Numerous studies on HAR have been undertaken, with some concentrating on skeleton data8,9 and others emphasizing depth data10,11. To enhance the efficacy of HAR systems, researchers have shifted their focus towards the integration of various data modalities and the exchange of information among these modalities6,12.

The impressive achievements of deep learning (DL) models in the realm of image classification13 have motivated researchers to extend these methodologies to video-based HAR. At first, most approaches relied on models based on Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). CNN models have made significant strides in identifying unique features and capturing contextual information from images. Nevertheless, they face challenges in extracting temporal data from sequential inputs such as videos6. RNN and their variations are frequently employed to model temporal relationships in HAR. Recent developments in DL have prominently integrated Attention mechanisms to emphasize critical features for various applications, including visual description14 and machine translation15.

The extraction of multi-scale spatial-temporal features from sequential data presents a significant challenge, particularly in contexts like HAR, where actions exhibit varying temporal structures and dependencies. The research conducted in16 highlights the shortcomings of shallow Convolutional CNN architectures in their ability to capture hierarchical temporal features within video sequences, thereby underscoring the necessity for deeper, multi-scale architectures to proficiently model complex temporal dynamics. The researchers in17 demonstrated that the hierarchical integration of spatial and temporal streams significantly enhances action recognition performance. However, they note that single-stream models frequently struggle to adequately extract multi-scale temporal features, thereby highlighting the necessity for architectures that are inherently designed to facilitate hierarchical feature extraction. The investigation presented in study18 examines the use of Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs) for the extraction of hierarchical temporal features in action segmentation. Nevertheless, TCNs may still face limitations in terms of depth and flexibility when compared to hierarchical architectures, which are more adept at effectively modeling long-range dependencies.

The researchers in19 employ CNN networks on mobile sensor data for activity recognition; however, they note that the limitations of fixed kernel sizes impede the model’s ability to detect hierarchical temporal patterns. This finding emphasizes the need for a strategy that gradually reduces kernel sizes, optimize the size to get the proper value. The study in20 combines CNNs with LSTMs to enhance activity recognition, yet the authors highlight that the shallow layers of CNNs fail to adequately extract hierarchical features across various temporal dimensions. Another noted challenge is the insufficient scalability and responsiveness to variations in hyperparameters, which underscores the difficulties in developing models that can adapt to diverse datasets, tasks, or computational requirements, arising from fixed architectural designs and manually adjusted hyperparameters. The research in21 indicates that models with fixed configurations frequently do not generalize effectively across different datasets. Consequently, the flexibility of the model can be achieved through hyperparameter optimization, which alleviates this limitation by allowing systematic adjustments for optimal performance.

The research conducted by22 provides an extensive examination of hybrid CNN-LSTM architectures employed in activity recognition, pinpointing the challenges associated with temporal modeling that are intrinsic to deep CNNs as a significant concern. It highlights the importance of integrating CNNs for spatial feature extraction alongside LSTMs to enhance temporal modeling. Employing smaller kernel sizes has been shown to be more effective regarding parameter efficiency. In contrast, larger discrete kernels require a considerable number of parameters, leading to significantly poorer performance23. These results indicate that a viable solution is found in a balanced methodology, where different kernel sizes can flexibly identify the most appropriate kernel size for the given task which can be applied through parameter optimization.

These results provide a need for the introduction of a new model that tackles these challenges by utilizing an innovative combination of HOG and 1D Convolution feature extraction, attention-based feature enhancement, and LSTM temporal modeling, specifically tailored for multimodal depth, skeleton and sensor.

In contrast to traditional multimodal HAR architectures that experience significant computational expenses, our proposed framework exhibits enhanced efficiency, attaining a throughput of 472.45 FPS while maintaining a compact parameter count of 0.979 M. Additionally, we address the issue of ’modality noise’ through a multi-agent approach. Related work

The integration of various HAR data modalities, which includes skeletal joint coordinates obtained from motion capture technologies, inertial measurements from wearable sensors, depth information from time-of-flight cameras, and RGB video frames—offers a detailed representation of human movement, encapsulating spatial, temporal, and contextual insights that individual modalities might not convey adequately on their own24. This multimodal strategy effectively mitigates the drawbacks associated with unimodal systems, such as the vulnerability to occlusion in RGB data, interference in inertial sensor outputs, or the lack of comprehensive joint information in skeletal data, thus enhancing the robustness and precision of action recognition tasks25.

The research presented in26 investigates the combination of skeleton data and inertial signals within a fusion framework aimed at enhancing human action recognition. It assesses two types of fusion: decision-level and feature-level. The experiments utilized a publicly accessible dataset known as CZU-MHAD. In the study27, a technique for fall recognition is introduced, which leverages the interrelations among human joints to enhance the accuracy and reliability of fall detection. This method employs skeleton representations and spatial-temporal graph networks (ST-GCN) in conjunction with human motion data sourced from inertial measurement units (IMUs). The authors in the research28 propose a multi-sensing modality framework aimed at recognizing fine-grained human activities and detecting abnormal behaviors. This is accomplished by integrating skeleton and acceleration data at the feature level. Deep temporal convolutional components are utilized for feature learning and managing temporal dynamics. The feature map produced through convolutional layers is then routed into two fully connected layers for the purpose of making predictions.

The research presented in29 introduces an innovative approach for generating skeleton images derived from sequences of skeleton joints that encapsulate motion information. The images representing motion are produced from depth, RGB, and skeleton images, with each type being trained independently on Convolutional Networks. The resulting softmax scores are then integrated at the decision level. Furthermore, the study referenced in30 has proposed a dual-stream network that simultaneously processes skeletal and RGB data, enhanced by pose estimation techniques to improve feature extraction. Additionally, the research in31 reveals a new multimodal architecture designed to combine skeletal and inertial data, with the objective of enhancing the efficacy of HAR. This proposed method employs specialized CNNs for each data modality, followed by a late fusion of their outputs to better capture spatial-temporal patterns. Moreover, the study32 presents an advanced HAR system specifically designed for multi-sensor surveillance, integrating RGB, RGB-D, and inertial sensors. It executes the framing and segmentation of multi-sensor data, applying filtration techniques to minimize noise and inconsistencies, while extracting features into a structured matrix. Furthermore, a genetic algorithm is utilized to merge and optimize the constructed matrix by eliminating redundant information. Ultimately, the aggregated data is classified using an enhanced Convolutional Neural Network - Gated Recurrent Unit (CNNGRU) classifier to identify specific human actions.

The research presented in33 introduced a multimodal convolution transformer known as Poster, which emphasizes the extraction and integration of complementary spatial and temporal data. The experiments conducted utilized public datasets, specifically UTD-MHAD and Berkley-MHAD. In the study detailed in34, the authors created a multimodal learning model designed for human action recognition, which combines skeleton data with depth sequential information entropy maps (DSIEM) through an action fusion process. This model was assessed using the UTD-MHAD dataset, achieving an accuracy of 88.37%. Nonetheless, it may face difficulties regarding interpretability and may struggle to identify actions that are not represented in the training data. In35, the authors developed a multimodal IoT framework for locomotion classification, utilizing the HWU-USP and Opportunity++ datasets. They employed a recursive neural network to integrate data from multiple sources, including inertial, ambient, and visual sensors. By implementing a 10-fold cross-validation method, the framework attained a commendable accuracy of 87%. The model proposed in36 integrates human skeleton data with time-series analytics, achieving impressive accuracies of 94.76% on the Berkeley-MHAD dataset and 95.19% on the UTD-MHAD dataset. However, it is constrained by issues related to computational complexity and interpretability. In contrast, the deep learning framework developed by37 combines various sensor modalities and achieved accuracies of 93.75% and 92.33% on the HWU-USP and UTD-MHAD datasets, respectively, but necessitates effective parameter tuning to enhance its performance. The Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) denote a distributed architecture composed of multiple interacting intelligent agents that work together, coordinate, and may compete to achieve a specified set of goals38,39. In this framework, an “agent” is characterized as an autonomous entity that perceives its environment through sensors, processes the gathered data, and acts upon that environment using effectors40. The fundamental traits of agents within MAS generally include: (1) Autonomy: Agents operate independently of direct human supervision and retain control over their internal states and actions. (2) Reactivity: Agents have the ability to sense their environment and respond swiftly to any changes that occur. (3) Pro-activeness: Agents exhibit goal-directed behavior by taking initiative. (4) Social Ability: Agents are equipped to communicate with other agents (and potentially humans) using a specified agent communication language to align their actions, collaborate, or negotiate38,40. MAS are designed to tackle complex issues that are difficult or impractical for a single agent or a monolithic system to handle due to aspects such as scale, complexity, distribution, or robustness39. They offer advantages such as improved scalability, fault tolerance, effective resource utilization, and the ability to operate in dynamic and uncertain environments. The collective intelligence that emerges from the interactions among individual agents often surpasses the capabilities of isolated entities38,41. The principles that form the foundation of MAS are particularly adept at addressing a wide range of issues related to HAR. The recognition of HAR often involves the analysis of large amount of varied sensor data collected from multiple sites. The previously mentioned multiagent keys inspired researchers to develop collaborative distributed learning methods, which encompass the following approaches: The research detailed in42 introduces a collaborative distributed learning framework grounded in multi-agent principles, wherein users of sensor-equipped devices operate as agents within a decentralized network, collectively participating in the comprehensive process of learning and classifying human activities. In reference43, an advanced prototype architecture based on multiple agents is presented for the recognition of human activities. This architecture incorporates a domain ontology and comprises a set of six agents assigned with tasks such as individual localization and the aggregation of data from diverse sources. Concurrently, the research in44 proposes an agent-oriented signal processing approach designed for monitoring human activities, primarily focusing on wireless body sensor networks. Within this framework, agents operate as sensor nodes, engaging in data collection, preprocessing, and the transmission of information to a central coordinator for subsequent analysis. In the study45, the authors introduce a multi-agent attention-driven deep learning algorithm referred to as the Multiagent Transformer Network (MATN), which aims to improve multimodal human activity recognition.

Recent advancements in video analytics have concentrated on automating the examination of extensive footage to reduce human error in surveillance. Researchers frequently merge various visual streams to encompass both static appearances and dynamic movements. For example,46 analyzes video by segmenting it into RGB frames, optical flow, and saliency maps. This research employs Inception V3 and ResNetV2 for feature extraction while utilizing the Grasshopper algorithm to enhance selection optimization46. In a similar vein,47 combines RGB frames and optical flow through bidirectional gated recurrent units (Bi-GRUs) to guarantee thorough sequential modeling. Other methodologies, such as48, illustrate that integrating spatial features from VGG16 with temporal features from optical flow significantly boosts classification accuracy.

Temporal modeling continues to be a fundamental aspect of recognizing intricate human actions.49 introduces a modified Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) that integrates an attention mechanism to concentrate on essential segments of an input sequence. This emphasis on detailed intricacies is further exemplified in50, which utilizes a VGG16 backbone and attention layers for effective hand gesture recognition. To improve spatial accuracy, certain frameworks implement semantic segmentation techniques.46 employs a SegNet architecture with binary masks and a BiGRU backbone to underscore vital areas for action recognition. This particular method applies frame-ranking to highlight motion dynamics and capture subtle temporal variations46. The research presented in46,49, and47 demonstrates a high level of accuracy; however, it is dependent on extensive RGB and optical flow streams. Techniques that employ semantic segmentation as noted in46 or transfer learning as discussed in50 are accurate but may exhibit sensitivity to background noise.

In contrast, our study departs from these RGB-centric methodologies by implementing a trimodal Multi-Agent System. We circumvent the substantial computational demands associated with video-based processing as observed in49 and48. Rather, we focus on utilizing skeleton, depth, and inertial data. This strategy effectively addresses issues related to occlusion and background disturbances. Our framework achieves a benchmark accuracy of 96.99%.

The incorporation of Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) with depth data has demonstrated considerable potential in improving human detection and action recognition tasks. By utilizing depth information, researchers have created techniques that enhance accuracy and minimize errors linked to conventional visible light image processing. HOG features derived from depth maps offer supplementary spatial information, which aids in alleviating challenges such as variations in illumination and occlusions that commonly impact images captured in visible light. Experiments utilizing depth data from devices such as Kinect v2 have shown remarkable precision and recall in tasks related to human detection, facilitating the development of more sensitive classifiers51 Depth Motion Maps (DMMs) are employed to derive HOG features for the purpose of identifying human actions, which enhances accuracy across a range of applications including surveillance and healthcare52. Methodology

The introduced multimodal HAR approach is optimized using the Categorical cross-entropy loss function and the RMSprop optimizer56, the learning rate is decreased by rate 0.1 in case there is no improvement in validation loss for 5 epochs, and lower decreasing bound is 1e-5. The HOG extraction process is illustrated in the Fig. 1, further the optimized model architecture, illustrated in the flowchart Fig.2, outlines a methodical three-phase procedure: (a) Input-Initialize: in this stage, the search space for model hyperparameters is established as provided in Table 2. (b) Tune-Evaluate: this phase represents the essence of the optimization process, where each trial incrementally modifies the hyperparameters and evaluates the model’s performance, with validation accuracy serving as the primary metric, complemented by early stopping (with a patience of 15 epochs) and a learning rate scheduler to mitigate overfitting and ensure convergence57. It is important to emphasize that the Test Set was strictly held out and not utilized during these 100 trials to ensure the final performance metrics remain unbiased and representative of the model’s generalization capabilities. The model explained in section 3.2 (c) Feedback-Update: this phase integrates a feedback mechanism to enhance the hyperparameter search based on the results of the trials. The optimization method introduced in section 3.7, Optuna’s TPE algorithm adjusts the probability distribution of hyperparameters, prioritizing areas of the search space that produce superior validation accuracies. The scientific justification for this architecture is rooted in its systematic methodology for hyperparameter tuning, which guarantees adaptability, robustness, and optimal convergence in deep learning applications (Fig. 2).

HOG extractor

The Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) serves as a robust feature descriptor that effectively captures local object appearance and shape characteristics by encoding the distribution of edge orientations within an image58. When utilized with depth images, HOG aids in depicting the 3D structure and contours of objects or human poses, which is particularly significant for tasks such as action recognition, where comprehending shape and movement is essential59. Although 3D-CNNs and ViTs possess significant expressive capabilities, the depth agent within this MAS framework employs HOG descriptors to emphasize computational efficiency and feature variety. This decision guarantees that the system delivers supplementary geometric insights to the skeleton agent, all while preserving the real-time performance essential for multi-agent coordination. As illustrated in the Gradient Cell grid, the image is divided into a systematic grid of cells (set to 8×8 pixels per cell). For every pixel located within a cell, its gradient orientation is categorized into a predetermined number of bins. In this study 10 bins are utilized, spanning from 0° to 180° (for unsigned gradients) or from 0° to 360° (for signed gradients). Each bin is associated with a specific range of angles. The gradient magnitude of each pixel provides a weighted vote to its corresponding orientation bin within that cell’s histogram. Pixels exhibiting greater gradient magnitudes extend a stronger influence on their respective bins, thereby highlighting more significant edges. This methodology results in the accumulation of a histogram of gradient orientations for each cell. The arrows depicted in the gradient cell grid visually indicate the predominant gradient orientations within each small area.

The number of selected frames is 32, for the modality channels, inertial data with 6 channels, skeleton data with 60 channels [20 joints × 3 coordinates]) and depth data with [240, 320] channels. Frame selection

The selection of frames based on joint velocities derived from skeleton data plays a vital role in improving the precision and effectiveness of action recognition systems. This procedure entails pinpointing essential frames and joints that are pivotal for the accurate identification of actions, which in turn minimizes computational demands and enhances overall performance. Numerous approaches have been suggested to refine this selection process, utilizing a combination of conventional and cutting-edge machine learning methods61: (1) Utilizing key joints and velocity histograms: The application of velocity histograms for joints serves as a technique to enhance the spatiotemporal representation of skeletal data. By concentrating on the variations in velocity and position of key joints, including the hands, head, and legs, a more comprehensive feature set is generated for action classification. This methodology has been proven to increase recognition accuracy and robustness in action recognition tasks26. (2) Employing Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) for joint selection: DRL has been utilized to identify the most informative joints for each frame. By conceptualizing joint selection as a Markov Decision Process, DRL can dynamically detect key joints that are essential for recognizing various activities. This approach has shown effectiveness across multiple datasets and classifiers, underscoring its potential to enhance current classification frameworks62 (3) Optimization in keyframe selection: The process of selecting keyframes based on the velocity and momentum of body parts represents another strategic approach. By reformulating keyframe selection as a multi-objective optimization problem, this method identifies inflection point frames that encapsulate significant pose alterations. This strategy has been validated across several datasets, demonstrating considerable enhancements in recognition accuracy63. (4) Temporal graph optimization through key frame selection: The refinement of skeleton data representation is achieved through the selection of key frames. By choosing salient posture frames and refining them based on intra-frame correlations, a compact and informative temporal graph is constructed. This technique employs graph neural networks to effectively capture spatial-temporal relationships, attaining state-of-the-art performance on challenging datasets64. Although these approaches emphasize the optimization of frame and joint selection by taking into account velocity and various dynamic characteristics, it is crucial to also evaluate the computational expenses and intricacies linked to these methods. Striking a balance between precision and efficiency continues to be a significant challenge in the domain of skeleton-based action recognition. The main keypoint of choosing key frames according to joint velocity is that frames demonstrating considerable motion or alterations in pose tend to be more informative and indicative of the action or movement being executed compared to static or gradually changing frames. This principle is extensively applied in motion analysis, human activity recognition, and animation. The developed keyframe selection employ the joints displacements to adaptively distinguish the informative frames relating to the performed action, the implementation steps are illustrated as follows: (1) Computing of joint displacement:

Depth preprocessing

Salient Region Segmentation and Resizing: This approach emphasizes the extraction of the main foreground object that represents the human, by utilizing depth data, followed by cropping and resizing. It integrates various image processing methods, including normalization, adaptive thresholding, morphological operations, contour detection, and bounding box extraction.

The initial step is to normalize the intensity spectrum of the depth frame, making it suitable for further image processing tasks later. Then, attempt to separate the foreground (person) from the background using Otsu’s method to find an optimal global threshold value, if Otsu’s method fails to produce any foreground pixels, the adaptive threshold method is applied to calculate a local threshold for small regions of the image, making it more robust to varying lighting conditions or depth gradients. After that the opening/closing morphological operations are applied to enhance the binary mask by eliminating noise, bridging gaps, and refining the edges of the segmented object, thereby rendering the contours more dependable for extraction. Then find contour with the biggest area and localize the object with the Bounding Box, finally the segmented object is resized to shape (64, 64). Applying HOG extractor: This step receive the salient region segmentation returned from the previous phase, then apply normalization on it. The next step is to apply the HOG outlined in 3.1 on the segmented objects to extract the features. The HOG parameter values are as follow: Size of detection window (win_size: (16, 16)), number of cells in each block (block_size:(16, 16)), the distance between adjecent blocks (block_stride: (8, 8)), The size of a HOG cell (cell_size:(8, 8)), the number of angular bins used to make the histograms (nbins: 10). Finally, the output of the HOG input to a 1D adaptive-pooling layer and return a 1D feature vector of size 92 for each sample depth frames.

Attention block

In the field of multimodal learning, efficient fusion strategies are crucial for utilizing the complementary information from various data streams. The attention mechanism utilized in the introduced model serves as a lightweight, learned modality-level gating mechanism. Although it is similar to the general concept of cross-attention, it introduces more robust and simple process rather than the complex, fine-grained attention paradigms65. The keypoint of the attention mechanism is its capacity to dynamically assess and implement the relative significance of the learned representations from each input modality. After the independent processing of depth, skeleton, and inertial data, and extracting spatial and temporal patterns through specialized LSTM based branches, each resulting in a fixed-size feature vector (i.e. depth_out, skel_out, iner_out), these individual modality embeddings are merged into a singular, all-encompassing feature vector, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F=[F_{depth}, F_{skel}, F_{iner}]$$\end{document}, where each item represents the mean-pooled output from its corresponding LSTM branch.

This combined vector F subsequently acts as the input for a compact, fully-connected neural network:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} A=Softmax(Linear_2(Tanh(Linear_1(F)))) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Linear_1$$\end{document} maps input features F to another \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3\times hidden\_size$$\end{document} dimensional space, after that the Tanh as a non-linear activation function is applied. Later, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Linear_2$$\end{document} projects the modified representation into a 3-d vector, which corresponds to the unnormalized scores for each of the three modalities. In final, the Softmax function is utilized along the modality dimension to convert these scores into a set of weights that resemble probabilities, denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W=[w_{depth},w_{skel},w_{iner}]$$\end{document}, where the sum of each weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_m$$\end{document} equals 1. The obtained weights W are subsequently extended and utilized through element-wise multiplication with the original concatenated feature vector F:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_{weighted} =F\odot W_{expanded} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_{expanded}$$\end{document} reformats W to align with the dimensions of F, thereby scaling the features of each modality according to its learned attention weight. This adjusted representation, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{weighted}$$\end{document}, then progresses into the ultimate classification layers.

This attention mechanism is distinct from from conventional attention paradigms as follows:

Modality-Level Gating versus Fine-Grained Interaction: In contrast to “self-attention,” which assesses relationships among elements within a singular sequence (i.e how each joint in a skeletal frame correlates with other joints), or standard “cross-attention” in Transformers that captures complex interactions between individual features or tokens from two separate sequences, this approach functions at a more elevated, modality-summary level. It does not compute attention scores for individual components (like specific HOG features or joint coordinates); rather, it directly learns the overall significance of each modality’s condensed representation.

Direct Weight Prediction versus QKV Compatibility: Traditional attention mechanisms entail the extraction of Queries (Q), Keys (K), and Values (V) from input features, followed by a compatibility function like dot product between Q and K to derive attention scores. Conversely, the introduced method directly learns to forecast the attention weights through a straightforward feed-forward network. There is no explicit pairwise similarity calculation between a query and a collection of keys that represent the modalities. The concatenated feature vector implicitly acts as a context from which the network derives the weights, rather than being explicitly broken down into Q, K, V components.

Element-wise Scaling versus Weighted Sum: The ultimate application of attention involves element-wise multiplication of the modality features by their learned weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(F\odot W_{expanded})$$\end{document}, instead of creating a new context vector through a weighted sum of values. This indicates that the original feature structure of each modality is maintained but scaled, functioning as a learned feature gating rather than a transformative combination into a completely new context vector.

Model optimizing

The optimization method tackles the difficulties associated with the manual tuning of DL models, which proves to be both labor-intensive and inefficient due to the vast hyperparameter space67. The proposed model’s optimization strategy employs the Optuna framework, an advanced hyperparameter optimization library, to systematically enhance the model’s architecture and training parameters. Optuna utilizes the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) algorithm as its primary sampling method, which skillfully traverses the hyperparameter landscape by modeling the distribution of successful parameter sets based on previous trial data68. The action recognition model is structured as a fitness function that accepts a candidate solution, represented as an array of dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$dim = 7$$\end{document} hyperparameters, as input and produces the validation accuracy, aiming to maximize this accuracy as the objective function. Experiments, results, and analysis

Dataset

The UTD-MHAD dataset69 consists of 27 unique human actions that were recorded using depth and inertial sensors. Each action was performed four times by a group of eight participants, evenly split between four males and four females. In total, the dataset comprises 861 samples that depict a range of human actions. This research utilizes skeleton and depth data collected from the Kinect v1 depth sensor. Throughout the data collection phase, the Kinect depth sensor was fixed in place to capture images of an individual’s body. The evaluation of this study follows the established Cross-Subject (CS) protocol, which involves using samples from subjects with odd identifiers for training, while the remaining samples are allocated for testing. CZU-MHAD70 comprises 880 samples distributed across 22 distinct actions, executed by 5 individuals, with each individual performing every action 8 times. The dataset includes three modalities of temporally synchronized data, which consist of depth videos and skeletal positions captured by a Kinect v2 camera, in addition to inertial signals from 10 wearable sensors. The dataset features common gestures (such as Draw x, Draw circle), daily activities (such as Sur Place, Clap, Bend down), and training actions (such as Left body turning movement, Left lateral movement). Consistent with prior research70, the samples from subjects 1, 2, and 5 are designated as training data, while the remaining samples are allocated for testing data.

To prevent optimistic bias, the hyperparameter tuning was conducted solely using training and validation subsets, while the results presented in the following sections reflect the model’s performance on the unseen subjects reserved for the test set.

Performance evaluation metrics

The experimental results presented in Table 3 display a thorough comparison of the model’s performance across the experiments fusion of the Depth + Skeleton + Inertial multi-modal data. This evaluation is conducted through four essential metrics: Accuracy, Recall, Precision, and F1-Score, which are relevant to 27 unique activity classes. These metrics yield significant insights into the model’s capability to accurately classify activities, recognize true instances of each class, ensure prediction reliability, and maintain a balance among these factors. The performance metrics are discussed in relation to class-specific trends and the effects of integrating the multi-modal depth data with inertial and the skeleton; therefore, the explanation is structured into three cases as follows:Case 1: High Performance Activities Several classes demonstrate outstanding performance, highlighting the model’s capability to differentiate between various motion patterns. The activities ’front clap’, ’bowling’, ’squat’, ’pick and throw’, and ’stand to sit’ attain perfect scores (1.00) in accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. This indicates that the Conv1D LSTM pipeline of the skeleton branch effectively captures unique joint configurations and temporal dynamics, while depth and inertial data further enhance these signatures. The perfect recall (1.00) signifies that there are no missed instances, and the perfect precision (1.00) confirms the absence of false positives, reinforcing the hypothesis that skeleton data alone is adequate for these actions. Similarly, ’baseball swing’, ’tennis swing’, ’arm curl’, and ’tennis serve’ also achieve outstanding metrics (1.00), likely attributable to their distinct spatial trajectories. In contrast, ’draw x’ (accuracy 1.00, precision 0.94, recall 1.00, F1-score 0.97) and ’draw circle (CCW)’ (accuracy 1.00, precision 0.94, recall 1.00, F1-score 0.97) exhibit slight reductions in precision (0.94), indicating minor false positives arising from similar motions. This exceptional performance highlights the robustness of the multi-agent design in modality-specific feature extraction, although subtle variations necessitate further tuning of attention.Case 2: Challenge Activities Certain classes present challenges, highlighting the limitations of the model’s discriminative capabilities. The activity ’Draw circle (CW)’ shows the lowest performance, achieving an accuracy of 0.75, a recall of 0.75, and an F1-score of 0.86, despite attaining perfect precision (1.00). This indicates that 25% of instances are misclassified, for instance, as ’swipe to the right’ or ’draw circle (CCW)’. Such misclassifications suggest confusion arising from overlapping circular patterns, a challenge similarly observed in the task ’draw triangle’ (accuracy 0.94, recall 0.94, F1-score 0.97, precision 1.00), where 6% of instances are overlooked. The task ’Catch’ poses considerable challenges, with an accuracy of 0.81, a recall of 0.81, and an F1-score of 0.90, despite achieving perfect precision (1.00). This implies that 19% of instances are likely misidentified as ’knock door’ or ’two hand push’. The task ’Knock door’ (accuracy 1.00, precision 0.89, recall 1.00, F1-score 0.94) successfully detects all instances, yet experiences a precision decline (0.89) due to false positives. The activities ’Two hand push’ (accuracy 0.94, precision 0.88, recall 0.94, F1-score 0.91) and ’jogging’ (accuracy 0.94, recall 0.94, F1-score 0.97, precision 1.00) also reveal minor issues, with error rates ranging from 6% to 12%. These challenges are likely attributed to overlapping arm or body movements, where the temporal resolution of inertial data or the weighting of fusion may be inadequate, necessitating enhanced feature extraction or augmentation of the dataset. This performance drop in circular motions can be attributed to three primary technical factors: Limitations of 1D-Conv Kernels: Although they are highly effective for sequential data, conventional 1D-Convolutional kernels used on inertial and skeletal data are proficient at capturing linear temporal dependencies but face challenges in modeling the continuous, non-linear spatial dependencies that are characteristic of complete circular trajectories.Frame Sampling Discretization: The fixed rate of frame sampling leads to the discretization of rapid, fluid circular motions. When the sampling rate is inadequate in relation to the speed of the action, the resulting skeletal sequence appears as a collection of disjointed linear swipes instead of a seamless arc, which can cause confusion with actions such as ’swipe to the right.’Sensor Noise (Kinect v1): The depth maps produced by the Kinect v1 sensor are susceptible to inherent noise and exhibit lower resolution near object edges. During overlapping circular movements (CW vs. CCW), this noise obscures the subtle distinctions in hand poses that are necessary for accurately determining the direction of rotation.Case 3: Notable Effects of Implementing Fusion The attention-driven fusion mechanism has a profound impact on performance by combining depth, skeleton, and inertial features. Classes exhibiting robust metrics (e.g., ’front clap’, ’bowling’) imply that fusion effectively prioritizes skeleton data, which offers intricate joint information, while depth and inertial data contribute to enhanced robustness. The exceptional accuracy (1.00) and recall (1.00) for these classes suggest that fusion mitigates modality-specific noise. Nevertheless, ’draw circle (CW)’ (accuracy 0.75, recall 0.75) and ’catch’ (recall 0.81) indicate that fusion encounters challenges with nuanced or overlapping motions, potentially due to insufficient attention weighting.

Efficiency and computational complexity analysis

Table 6 presents the computational analysis of the proposed architecture across distinct modalities and various fusion strategies. To evaluate the feasibility of the introduced framework for edge deployment, we have calculated the FLOPs and measured the inference latency on a NVIDIA T4 x2. The benchmarking reveals a surprising yet highly beneficial outcome: the fusion multimodal framework necessitates considerably fewer computational resources (0.979 M parameters; 23.431 M FLOPs) compared to both the Depth-only baseline (4.368 M parameters; 134.772 M FLOPs) and the bimodal Skeleton + Inertial configuration (2.130 M parameters; 55.692 M FLOPs). This efficiency underscores the effectiveness of the proposed soft computing fusion mechanism. Instead of simply concatenating the most resource-intensive feature extraction branches, the multimodal architecture projects the high-dimensional data into an optimized, shared latent space. This reduction of redundant spatial and temporal parameters significantly decreases the theoretical computational complexity. Furthermore, latency and throughput metrics validate the framework’s suitability for real-time HAR on devices with limited resources. While the unimodal skeleton and inertial models achieve exceptionally high throughputs exceeding 1300 FPS—ideal for stringent edge-deployment, the fusion multimodal sustains a highly competitive inference speed of 472.45 FPS (2.12 ms latency). Conclusion and future work

The development and evaluation of the introduced model mark a significant advancement in action recognition, particularly through its optimized hyperparameter tuning for multi-modal data fusion and innovative architectural enhancements. Relating to spatial features, the study employed the HOG extractor on depth selected frames, 1D convolutional block for skeleton and inertial features extracting. The long temporal features are handled through applying the LSTM units. Furthermore, an adaptive cross-attention method is developed to dynamically assess and implement the relative significance of the learned long-range spatio-temporal dependencies from each input modality. Evaluation of the UTD MHAD dataset demonstrates that the proposed methodology surpasses current state-of-the-art techniques, as shown by robust performance metrics obtained from confusion matrices, ROC curves, t-SNE visualizations, and attention heatmaps. These findings highlight the model’s ability to effectively utilize fused depth, skeleton and inertial data. Nevertheless, challenges persist, especially in distinguishing similar activities and maintaining consistent performance across all classes, as reflected by lower Precision for specific actions. Future research should focus on enhancing the model’s feature extraction for overlapping motion patterns and investigate further optimization strategies to improve generalization and robustness across various datasets and real-world applications.
