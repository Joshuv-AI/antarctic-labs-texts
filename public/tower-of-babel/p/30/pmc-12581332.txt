Itaconate-functionalized tetrahedral framework nucleic acids break the dry eye vicious cycle through dual antioxidant and anti-inflammatory actions
Authors: Kai Jiang, Zhenyi Lin, Mingrui Cheng, Yanmin Chen, Yiru Jiang, Changhua Xu, Lijuan Zhu, Bo Gong, Chuan Zhang, Jiaxu Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDry eye disease (DED) is a prevalent ocular surface disorder characterized by chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, significantly impacting vision and quality of life. Although reactive oxygen species (ROS) serve as a key upstream regulator that initiate and perpetuate inflammatory cascades in DED, current clinical therapies predominantly target downstream inflammatory pathways, leading to suboptimal treatment outcomes. To address this limitation, we developed a novel mucin-1 (MUC1)-targeted nanotherapeutic platform (tFNA-IM) by integrating the dual antioxidant and anti-inflammatory agent itaconate (ITA) with ROS-scavenging tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (tFNA) for efficient DED treatment. The engineered tFNA-IM exhibits uniform nanostructure and optimal size distribution, enabling deep penetration into ocular tissues, while the conjugated MUC1 aptamer significantly enhances cellular internalization in human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs), as demonstrated by confocal microscopy and flow cytometry analyses. Functionally, tFNA-IM effectively neutralizes ROS, reduces apoptosis, and downregulates pro-inflammatory cytokines in vitro, demonstrating potent anti-oxidative and anti-inflammatory capabilities. In a murine DED model, tFNA-IM exhibits prolonged ocular retention and superior therapeutic efficacy, markedly improving corneal epithelial integrity and suppressing inflammatory responses. Introduction

Dry eye disease (DED) is a prevalent ocular surface disorder characterized by tear film instability, hyperosmolarity, and chronic inflammation, leading to significant discomfort and visual impairment [1, 2]. The pathogenesis of DED involves a self-perpetuating vicious cycle wherein oxidative stress and inflammatory cascades mutually reinforce each other, resulting in epithelial cell apoptosis and tear instability that exacerbate ocular surface damage [3, 4]. This multifactorial pathogenic cycle poses a major challenge in DED management. Current therapeutic agents (e.g., cyclosporine A, corticosteroids), which primarily target downstream inflammatory pathways, have gained considerable attention in recent decades and represent significant treatment advances [5–7]. However, these anti-inflammatory drugs are limited by their delayed therapeutic onset, incomplete modulation of oxidative stress, and adverse effects such as ocular irritation and blurred vision [5]. Thus, developing safer and more effective therapeutic strategies for DED remains an urgent priority.

Emerging evidence implicates reactive oxygen species (ROS) overexpression as a pivotal upstream mediator in DED progression [8, 9]. Excessive ROS induces lipid peroxidation, oxidative DNA damage, and subsequent ocular surface inflammation and lacrimal gland dysfunction, further aggravating the disease [10]. Antioxidant—such as Reproxalap [11], vitamin A [12], visomitin (SKQ1) [13], quercetin [14], tempo [15], and gallic acid derivatives [16]—have demonstrated efficacy in mitigating oxidative stress. However, current antioxidant agents exhibit a sacrificial mechanism, being rapidly depleted upon ROS neutralization and cleared from the ocular surface due to tear turnover and blinking. These limitations underscore the need for novel interventions that simultaneously target oxidative and inflammatory pathways in DED.

Itaconate (ITA), an immunomodulatory metabolite derived from the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, has emerged as a promising therapeutic candidate due to its dual antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [17, 18]. Recent studies demonstrate that ITA activate nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2)-mediated antioxidant pathways, modulates the ATF3/IκBζ axis activity, and suppress pro-inflammatory cytokine production in various disease models, including autoimmune and neurodegenerative disorders [19–21]. Mechanistically, ITA may exert therapeutic effects in DED by reinforcing antioxidant and anti-inflammatory gene pathways. However, its clinical translation for ocular applications is hindered by poor corneal permeability and rapid ocular clearance.

In recent years, nucleic acid-based nanocarriers have gained prominence as versatile drug delivery systems due to their structural programmability and precise functionalization [22, 23]. Among these, tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (tFNAs) have shown exceptional promise in nanomedicine owing to their stable three-dimensional architecture and multifunctional cargo-loading capacity [24]. Their inherent biocompatibility, structural stability, and tunable encapsulation properties facilitate the delivery of diverse therapeutics, including small molecules, proteins, and nucleic acids [25]. In ophthalmology, tFNAs have demonstrated significant potential for gene therapy and ocular drug delivery, particularly in addressing complex ocular pathologies [26–28]. Recent studies highlight their efficacy in delivering therapeutic agents to both retina and anterior segment tissues, overcoming critical barriers in ocular drug administration. Notably, tFNAs mitigate oxidative stress by scavenging excess ROS, modulate inflammatory responses, and promote corneal epithelial regeneration [29, 30]. Furthermore, they alleviate dry eye symptoms by restoring goblet cell function and enhancing tear secretion.

In the present study, we proposed a novel therapeutic strategy, termed tFNA-IM, for ocular-targeted delivery of ITA to treat DED (Fig. 1). Technically, ITA was first chemically modified to conjugate with the predesigned DNA strands, which were then assembled with a mucin 1 (MUC1)-targeting aptamer (AptMUC1). The incorporation of the MUC1 aptamer significantly improved the cellular uptake efficiency. Upon internalization, ITA was released and exerts dual biological effects. Unlike conventional antioxidants such as Reproxalap, which directly scavenge ROS and are rapidly consumed, the proposed tFNA-IM system integrates multiple biological effects with efficient drug delivery. Specifically, on one hand, ITA modulated the ATF3/IκBζ signaling pathway to suppress inflammatory responses and remodel the inflammatory gene network. On the other hand, ITA activated the NRF2/heme oxygenase-1(HO-1) antioxidant axis, significantly upregulating the expression of key antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase-1 (SOD-1), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx-1). This enhanced antioxidant capacity effectively scavenges excessive ROS, alleviated oxidative stress-induced damage, and simultaneously regulated the anti-inflammatory pathways mediated by HO-1. By concurrently regulating HO-1-mediated anti-inflammatory pathways and antioxidant defenses, tFNA-IM disrupted the oxidative stress-inflammation cycle in DED, demonstrating synergistic therapeutic effect. This multifunctional nanoplatform presents a promising paradigm for ocular surface diseases.

Synthesis of bromo-itacinate (Br-ITA)

As shown in Fig. S2A, itaconic anhydride (1 g, 8.9 mmol) and 4-bromomethylbenzyl alcohol (1.7 g, 8.5 mmol) were suspended in a 1:1 (v/v) toluene/n-hexane mixture (100 mL) and stirred at 60 ℃ for 36 h. After evaporation, the resulting colorless oil was dissolved in ethyl acetate (250 mL) and extracted three times with saturated NaHCO₃ solution (100 mL each). The aqueous phase was then washed with diethyl ether (100 mL), acidified to pH 2 using concentrated HCl, and filtered to collect a white precipitate. The product, Br-ITA, was obtained after washing with n-hexane and vacuum drying. Finally, Br-ITA was characterized by nuclear magnetic resonance hydrogen spectroscopy (1H-NMR, AVANCE III HD 500, Germany) and high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS, Waters Xevo G2-XS QTOF, USA).

The synthesis of ITA-modified DNA strand

5 OD phosphorothioate (PS) modified single-stranded DNA in 1.5 mL tube was lyophilized under vacuum for approximately 1 h. Subsequently, Br-ITA solution (40 mM in DMSO) was added into the above tube at a 20:1 molar ratio of Br-ITA and PS group with the final DNA concentration of 200 µM and reacted at 50 ℃ for 120 min. After reaction, unreacted Br-ITA was removed via triple extraction using ethyl acetate for ten times, followed by the concentration with n-Butanol. Finally, the obtained product was analyzed by 20% denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and Matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF-MS, SHIMADZU, Japan).

Preparation and characterization of tFNA-IM

The preparation of tFNA-IM was followed according to the previous studies. In detail, 5ITA-S1, 5ITA-S2, 5ITA-S3, 5ITA-S4 and AptMuc-1 were added into tube at the molar ratio of 1:1:1:1:4 in the TAE/Mg2+ buffer, and then heated at 95 ℃ for 10 min, followed by the storage at 4 ℃ for 20 min. Afterward, the synthesis of tFNA-IM was verified by native 10% PAGE. The size distribution and zeta potential were quantified by Zetasizer Nano ZS90 unit (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK). The morphology was determined by atomic force microscope (AFM, Bruker Multimode 8, Germany) and transmission electron microscope (TEM, ThermoFisher Talos F200X G2, USA).

To evaluate the release behavior of itaconate (ITA) from DNA, a model strand (5ITA-S1) bearing five ITA modifications via benzyl ester linkages at phosphorothioate (PS) sites was synthesized. For esterase-triggered release studies, 5ITA-S1 was incubated with esterase (60 U/mL, Sigma, USA) in PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 ℃ for different time intervals (1, 2, 4, 8, 12 and 24 h). At each time point, samples were collected and subsequently analyzed using 20% denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE). The electrophoretic mobility shift of 5ITA-S1 over time was compared with the unmodified S1 strand to confirm ITA release.

For the stability study, tFNA-IM was incubated for different time (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12 and 24 h) in either 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) or PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 ℃. Samples were collected at predetermined time intervals and analyzed by 4% native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) to assess the structural integrity of tFNA-IM under physiological conditions.

Cell culture

Human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs) and human conjunctival epithelial cells (HConjECs) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, MeilunBio®, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, MeilunBio®, China) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (BasalMedia, China) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. Human monocytic cells (THP-1) were maintained in 1640 medium (Procell, China) under the same culture conditions. Mouse corneal epithelial cells (MCECs) were cultured in specialized immortalized mouse corneal epithelial cell medium (IMMOCELL®, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells at logarithmic growth phase were used for subsequent experiments.

Cellular uptake of tFNA-IM

Flow cytometry (FCM) and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) were utilized to evaluate the cellular uptake of human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs) by tFNA-I and tFNA-IM. The experimental procedure was as follows:

HCECs were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 8 × 104 cells per well and incubated overnight to adhere. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced with 100 mM NaCl solution, and the cells were incubated for additional 12 h. Afterward, the supernatant was removed, and Cy3-labeled tFNA-IM with various concentrations (10, 25, 50 and 100 nM) were added for further incubation over a period of 6 h. Following this, the supernatant was discarded, and the cells were rinsed twice with PBS. The cells were then harvested using trypsin, centrifuged, resuspended in 300 µL of PBS, and analyzed by CLSM (Olympus FV3000, Japan).

In parallel, the uptake of tFNA-IM (Cy3 concentration of 100 nM) by HCECs was investigated at multiple time points (2, 4 and 6 h), and FCM was applied for analysis. In addition, CLSM was employed to visualize the internalization of both tFNA-I and tFNA-IM. Cell slides were placed into confocal dishes and subjected to identical experimental conditions as those used for FCM (Cy3 concentration of 100 nM, incubation for 6 h). After the incubation, the supernatant was removed, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature for 25 min. Following fixation, the cells were washed again with PBS, and the nuclei were stained with hoechst 33342 at room temperature. After staining, the cells were rinsed three times, and PBS was added before fluorescence imaging was performed using CLSM (Olympus FV3000, Japan).

Cell viability assessment

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) and Calcein/PI Cell Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit were used to access the cell viability. The specific experimental steps are as follows:

HCECs were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 7 × 10³ cells/well and cultured overnight. HCECs were treated with DMEM medium containing different concentrations of 4-Octyl Itaconate (4-OI), tFNA, tFNA-M, tFNA-I, tFNA-IM in hypertonic conditions (NaCl, 100 mM) for 24 h. Subsequently, HCECs were washed with PBS for three times, and then fresh medium containing CCK-8 reagent was added into the plates and incubated for 30 min. The absorbance was then measured using Microplate readers (TECAN Infinite 200 PRO, Switzerland). The cell proliferation rate was calculated using the following formula: (ODsample - ODblank)/(ODmock - ODblank) × 100%, where ODsample represents the absorbance value of cells after treatment with the sample, ODblank represents the background absorbance value of medium alone (without cells), and ODmock represents the absorbance value of cells cultured in medium without NaCl and the sample.

For Calcein/PI staining, HCECs were seeded in 12-well plate at the density of 1 × 105 cells per well and treated with 4-OI, tFNA, tFNA-M, tFNA-I, tFNA-IM (ITA concentration of 10 µM, tFNA concentration of 500 nM) in hypertonic conditions. After the treatment, HCECs were washed with PBS and stained with Calcein-AM/PI working solution at 37 ℃ for 30 min in the dark. Finally, HCECs were washed with PBS and imaged by fluorescence microscope (Olympus CKX53, Japan).

To further determine mitochondrial repair capacity of tFNA-IM, JC-1 probe was used after the treatment. In detail, HCECs were seeded in 4 Chamber glass bottom dishes at a density of 8 × 104 cells per well and cultured overnight. HCECs were treated as above mentioned. Then, HCECs were rinsed three times with PBS and stained with JC-1 working solution for 20 min. Next, the solution was discarded and washed with PBS. Finally, HCECs were imaged by CLSM.

ROS-scavenging assessment

HCECs were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 1.4 × 105 cells per well, and incubated with a medium containing 400 µM hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) for 4 h to induce excessive ROS production in HCECs. Subsequently, 4-OI, tFNA, tFNA-I, and tFNA-IM (ITA concentration of 10 µM, tFNA concentration of 500 nM) were added to the cells for co-incubation for 24 h. After the incubation, HCECs were treated with DCFH-DA probe according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and finally imaged using CLSM.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and Western blot

For RT-qPCR experiments, after cells were seeded in 12-well plates and treated as above mentioned, the total RNA was isolated according to the protocol of the SteadyPure Rapid RNA Extraction Kit (AG21023, Accurate Biology, China). Afterward, the mRNA samples were converted into cDNA with the Hifair® AdvanceFast 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Yeasen, China). The qRT-PCR reaction mixture was then prepared using Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, China), and the quantification was performed by qPCR (Roche LightCycler 96, Switzerland). The experimental protocols for THP-1 and MCECs were conducted following the same procedures as those used for HCECs. The primer sequences were listed in Table S1.

For western-blot experiments, HCECs were seeded in 6-well plates and treated as above mentioned. After that, the cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and protein concentrations were determined using BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotech Inc.). Equal amounts of protein (30 ~ 50 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE on 4 ~ 20% polyacrylamide gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated with primary β-actin, HO-1, SOD1, CAT, GPx-1, IL-1β and IκBζ antibodies (1:20000, 1:2000, 1:1000, 1:1000, 1:5000, 1:1000 and 1:1000, respectively) overnight at 4 ℃. After washing with Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 detergent (TBST), the membranes were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (1:5000) at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Shanghai Linde Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) and detected by a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad). β-actin was used as a loading control.

In vitro Immunofluorescence (IF)

According to the procedure of cellular uptake, after the treatment, cells was washed with PBS for three times, and then fixed in ice-cold methanol at −20 ℃ for 10 min. Subsequently, the cells were blocked with a blocking solution containing 0.3% Triton™ X-100 and 5% goat serum in PBS at room temperature for 1 h. After blocking, the cells were incubated with primary antibody (1:200) at 4 ℃ overnight. Following the removal of the antibody, cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline with Tween® detergent (PBST) and then incubated with the secondary antibody conjugated to Alexa555 (1:2000) at room temperature for 2 h. After the secondary antibody incubation, HCECs were washed again and stained with hoechst 33342 and Actin-Tracker Green-488. Finally, HCECs were washed with PBS, and the samples were analyzed by CLSM.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy of tFNA-IM

All tests were approved by the Eye and ENT Hospital attached to Fudan University’s Animal Ethics Committee in accordance with a statement made by the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO) (Number: EENT-IRB20220222). Experimental dry eye disease (DED) model was established in low humidity environment cabinet. In detail, C57BL/6J mice were housed in low humidity environment cabinet for a duration of 14 days, with environmental conditions maintained at a relative humidity of 22% ± 4%, an air velocity of 3 to 4 m/s, and a temperature ranging from 23 ℃ to 25℃. Subsequently, these parameters were kept stable throughout the experimental period. Specifically, the mock group was kept under normal environmental conditions, with environmental conditions maintained at a relative humidity of 55% ± 5%, an air velocity of 3 to 4 m/s, and a temperature ranging from 23 ℃ to 25 ℃. After 14 days, the mice were divided into different treatment group.

For in vivo precorneal retention: The experimental animals were randomly assigned to three groups: the Mock group, the tFNA-I-Cy5 group, and the tFNA-IM-Cy5 group. A total of 5 µL of the corresponding drug solution was applied to the corneal surface of each eye without anesthesia. After administration, the eyes were collected at 3, 10, and 20 min, respectively. Subsequently, the ocular tissues were rapidly embedded in OCT compound and then frozen in liquid nitrogen. Thin tissue Sect. (10 μm thick) were prepared and mounted on slides using an antifade mounting medium. Finally, the specimens were examined using CLSM.

For in vivo therapeutic efficacy evaluation: The animals were randomly divided into six experimental groups and subjected to the phenol red thread test and sodium fluorescein staining following the established protocol described in previous study [31]. Afterward, each group was administered with 1× PBS, 4-OI (100 µM), tFNA-M (5 µM), tFNA-I (5 µM), or tFNA-IM (5 µM). A volume of 5 µL of the respective formulation was applied to each eye twice daily over a 14-day period. At the end of the treatment, both fluorescent dye staining and the phenol red thread test were conducted.

For immunohistochemical staining: After sacrifice, the eyeballs were collected for fixation and subsequent paraffin embedding. Afterward, 8-µm-thick sections were prepared from the embedded tissues and deparaffinized. Before immunostaining, antigen retrieval was performed using microwave heating. Subsequently, the sections were blocked with a solution of 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) containing 0.1% Triton X-100 at 37 ℃ for 1 h. Then, the slides were incubated with the primary antibody F4/80 (1:200, Proteintech) overnight at 4 ℃. After three washes in PBS, the sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled secondary antibodies (1:500, Invitrogen) at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, the slides were mounted and visualized under a microscope.

For IF staining: After sacrifice, the corneas were harvested and immediately fixed in 4% PFA for 1–2 h at room temperature. Subsequently, under a stereomicroscope, each cornea was cut into a “four-leaf clover” shape along the margin. Following washing with PBS, the corneal tissues were blocked with a solution containing 1% Triton X-100 and 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at 37 ℃ for 1.5 h. The corneas were then incubated with primary antibodies against CD86 (1:100, Invitrogen) and CD206 (1:500, Servicebio) at 4 ℃ overnight. After three washes with PBS, the tissues were incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 (goat anti-rabbit, 1:500, Invitrogen) and Alexa Fluor 555 (goat anti-rat, 1:500, Invitrogen) at 4 ℃ overnight. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI and analysis by CLSM.

For TUNEL staining: 10-µm-thick sections were prepared from the embedded tissues. Before proceeding to immunostaining, the tissue sections were blocked and permeabilized. Subsequently, the samples were incubated with 450 µL of fluorescently labeled dUTP and 50 µL of terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT) in a TUNEL reaction mixture (Roche Diagnostics) under humid conditions at 37 ℃ for 1 h in the dark. Afterward, the sections were washed three times and then co-incubated with the CK3 primary antibody (1:200, Abcam) at 4 ℃ overnight. Following this, the sections were incubated with secondary antibodies, and nuclear staining was performed using DAPI. Finally, fluorescent images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope.

For PAS staining: The procedure was carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions of the PAS staining kit (G1280, Solarbio). Briefly, 4-µm-thick paraffin sections were prepared and deparaffinized, followed by hydration. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with 1% periodic acid solution for oxidation and then washed thoroughly with distilled water. After that, the samples were stained with Schiff reagent and hematoxylin, respectively. Finally, images were captured using a digital optical microscope. The number of positively stained conjunctival goblet cells was quantified with ImageJ software and normalized to the number in the Mock group, expressed as a percentage of the control.

For safety evaluation: After treatment, blood samples were collected from the eye sockets of the mice. The blood samples were left at room temperature for 2 h to allow the blood cells to sediment. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 4 ℃ with a speed of 12,000 rpm for 10 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was transferred into a new centrifuge tube for biochemical analysis. In addition, the eyeballs and major organs were harvested for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Data were expressed as mean ± SD. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test was used for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance is indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ***p < 0.0001 and ns (non-significance) p > 0.05.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of tFNA-IM nanostructure

To improve the corneal permeability and bioavailability of ITA, AptMUC−1 modified tFNA was used as a DNA structure-based nanoplatform for ITA delivery. As shown in Fig. S1, five PS-modified DNA strands with sticky end overhangs (DNA-S1, DNA-S2, DNA-S3 and DNA-S4) were designed to conjugate ITA in a site-specific manner. Firstly, ITA-Br was synthesized via a one-step esterification reaction and subsequently incorporated into DNA strands according to the synthetic route in Fig. S2A. The chemical structure of ITA-Br was characterized using 1H NMR, with all observed signals corresponded to ITA-Br (Fig. S2B). The measured m/z value at 336.9869 shown in Fig. S2C was consistent with the theoretical value, also confirming the successful synthesis of the ITA-Br. Subsequently, Br-ITA was grafted onto DNA according to the previous method [32]. The resulting ITA-DNA conjugates were characterized by 10% native PAGE. As shown in Fig. 2A, ITA-DNA conjugates exhibited slower migration compared to free DNA strands, indicating successful conjugation. To quantify ITA loading, MALDI-TOF MS was performed. Taking single-stranded DNA-S1 as an example, the observed m/z values for the ITA-DNA-S1 conjugate was shown in Fig. S3. And according the our previous work [32], the mass difference confirmed full conjugation at all five PS-modified sites. The corresponding ITA-DNA conjugates were designated as 5ITA-S1, 5ITA-S2, 5ITA-S3, and 5ITA-S4.

Size distribution (C) and the ζ-potential (D) of tFNA, tFNA-M, tFNA-I, and tFNA-IM. (E) AFM and (F) TEM images of tFNA-IM. (G) pH and osmotic pressure of different formulations (PBS, tFNA-M, tFNA-I, and tFNA-IM). Scale bar: 200 nm

Subsequently, tFNA-IM was synthesized via a one-pot reaction based on a previously reported tFNA method. 8% native PAGE verified successful assembly, with tFNA-IM exhibiting the slowest migration rate due to increased molecular weight (Fig. 2B). The hydrodynamic size and zeta potential of tFNA-IM were measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS). The results showed that the hydrodynamic diameter of tFNA-IM was 33.27 nm, with a zeta potential of −24.4 mV (Fig. 2C and D). Compared to the hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of tFNA (19.05 nm, −12.96 mV), tFNA-M (29.47 nm, −17.00 mV), and tFNA-I (28.15 nm, −5.39 mV), tFNA-IM exhibited a moderate size enlargement and altered surface charge. The size increases likely results from steric effects of the hydrophobic benzyl group, charge redistribution, and altered DNA backbone interactions. The less negative zeta potential of tFNA-I suggests partial charge shielding by benzyl-itaconate. AFM and TEM images in Fig. 2E and F showed that tFNA-IM exhibited a uniform well-distributed circular or approximately triangular nanostructures, suggesting ordered assembly. To evaluate ocular compatibility, the osmolarity and pH of tFNA-IM eye drops were measured. As shown in Fig. 2G, the pH and osmotic pressure of PBS, tFNA-M, tFNA-I and tFNA-IM fell within the physiological range of human tears [33], indicating minimal ocular irritation and supporting tFNA-IM as a safety therapeutic candidate for DED treatment. The intracellular release of itaconate (ITA) is essential for its multiple biological functions following cellular uptake of tFNA-IM. In the present drug delivery system, ITA is conjugated to DNA via a benzyl ester linkage, which is susceptible to hydrolysis by intracellular esterases. To verify the release behavior, we employed 5ITA-S1 as a model and incubated it with esterase, followed by analysis using denaturing PAGE. As shown in Fig. S4A, the electrophoretic mobility of 5ITA-S1 gradually increased with prolonged incubation, and after 24 h, it closely resembled that of the native S1 strand. These results demonstrate that ITA can be efficiently cleaved from the DNA backbone, thereby confirming successful esterase-responsive release. To further assess the potential for clinical translation, we next evaluated the physicochemical stability of tFNA-IM under physiological conditions. As shown in Fig. S4B, tFNA-IM maintained its structural integrity with negligible degradation in both 10% FBS and PBS over the tested time, indicating excellent stability under simulated physiological environments.

In vitro cellular uptake of tFNA-IM

As an important component of the tear film, mucin plays an indispensable role in maintaining ocular surface hydration and stabilizing the tear film. Among them, MUC1 is a major transmembrane protein, widely expressed on corneal epithelial cells, participating in various physiological processes and modulating intercellular interactions. In DED pathophysiology, both human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs) and human conjunctival epithelial cells (HConjECs) are essential for maintaining ocular surface homeostasis, regulating tear film composition, and modulating inflammatory responses. To confirm MUC1 expression in HCECs, IF staining was performed on untreated and hypertonic-treated HCECs and HconjECs, with HepG2 cells serving as a negative control. As illustrated in Fig. 3A and Fig. S5, both untreated and hypertonic-treated HCECs and HConjECs exhibited higher red inflorescence signal than HepG2 cells. However, hypertonic treatment slightly reduced fluorescence intensity, consistent with prior reports of MUC1 downregulation under hyperosmotic conditions. This finding aligns with the pathophysiological changes observed in DED, underscoring MUC1’s role in ocular surface homeostasis.

To evaluate the cellular uptake of tFNA-IM by HCECs, both CLSM and FCM were employed to assess the time-dependent and dose-dependent internalization. As shown in Fig. 3B and Fig. S6A, after 2 h of incubation, tFNA-I and tFNA-IM exhibited detectable red fluorescence. As the incubation time increased, the intensity of the red fluorescence gradually enhanced. Moreover, the red fluorescence in tFNA-IM treated group was significantly stronger than that in tFNA-I group-treated group, suggesting that MUC-1 aptamer modification enhances uptake efficiency. FCM results in Fig. 3C and D further validated the time-dependent cellular uptake of tFNA-IM. In addition, both tFNA-I and tFNA-IM exhibited progressive cellular internalization, but tFNA-IM uptake was over 1.5-fold higher at 6 and 12 h. Fig. S6B and S6C demonstrated that both tFNA-I and tFNA-IM exhibited a concentration-dependent cellular uptake. After modification with MUC-1 aptamer, the uptake efficiency of tFNA-IM was significantly enhanced compared to that of tFNA-I. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that MUC1 aptamer-functionalized tFNA enhances cellular uptake, potentially optimizing its therapeutic efficiency for DED.

In vitro ROS-scavenging and cell-protective activities assessment of tFNA-IM

Emerging evidence reveals that DED-driven hyperosmolar stress disrupts redox homeostasis, provoking pathological reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, mitochondrial fission, DNA damage, and subsequent pyroptosis in corneal epithelial cells [9]. Due to the poor cellular permeability of ITA, 4-OI was selected as a control in subsequent comparative experiments. To access the cytoprotective effect of tFNA-IM against hyperosmotic-induced damage in HCECs, cell viability was measured through CCK-8 assay and live/dead cell staining. After 24 h of hyperosmotic treatment, cell viability decreased to 70%, whereas 4-OI, tFNA-M, tFNA-I, and tFNA-IM treatments restored the cell viability in a dose-dependent manner. At 500 nM tFNA-IM (equivalent to 10 µM 4-OI), cell viability was restored to 85% (Fig. S7 and Fig. 4B). Live/dead staining in the Fig. 4A indicated that hyperosmotic stress induced apoptosis, whereas tFNA-IM treatment group exhibited a marked reduction in cell apoptosis, suggesting that tFNA-IM exerts a strong protective effect against HCEC apoptosis under hyperostosis conditions.

(B) Cell viability of HCECs treated with different drug formulations (PBS, 4-OI, tFNA-M, tFNA-I, tFNA-IM) at the equal concentration of tFNA of 500 nM assessed by CCK-8 assay. To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, mitochondrial function and oxidative stress were evaluated. Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), a key indicator of mitochondrial health, was measured using the JC-1 fluorescent probe. A shift in JC-1 fluorescence from red (healthy mitochondria) to green (depolarized mitochondria) reflects ΔΨm loss, which precedes apoptosis. As shown in Fig. 4C, all drug-treated groups exhibited enhanced red fluorescence and diminished green fluorescence compared to the hyperosmotic control. Notably, the tFNA-IM-treated group exhibited the most pronounced increase in red fluorescence, accompanied by a reduction in green fluorescence, suggesting that AptMUC1 improved the cellular uptake of ITA, thereby restoring ΔΨm more eddecitively. In addition, tFNA-M partially mitigated mitochondrial dysfunction, as evidenced by reduced green fluorescence compared to the PBS group. Intracellular ROS level was determined by 2′′,7′′dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) assay. The PBS-treated group showed a strong green fluorescence signal in HCECs cells under hyperosmotic stress, indicative of elevated ROS, while all treatment groups showed significant ROS reduction (Fig. 4D). Both tFNA-M and 4-OI moderately attenuated ROS levels, but tFNA-IM achieved ROS elimination comparable to baseline (mock group), further confirming that AptMUC1 enhances ITA uptake and the synergistic antioxidant activity of tFNA and ITA. Taken together, these results indicate that tFNA systems scavenge ROS and restore mitochondrial function. Moreover, the incorporation of the AptMUC1 significantly improved the ITA delivery, amplifying the therapeutic efficacy against hyperosmotic stress-induced damage.

NRF2, a master regulator of the cellular antioxidant response, activates the expression of cytoprotective genes, including HO-1 (heme oxygenase 1, HMOX1, HO-1), which plays a pivotal role in ROS detoxification and redox homeostasis under oxidative stress conditions [34, 35]. As shown in Fig. 4E, NRF2 and HO-1 expression was significantly downregulated in the DED group under the hyperostosis stimulation. In contrast, all drug-treated groups exhibited a marked upregulation of both NRF2 and HO1, whereas all drug-treated groups exhibited pronounced upregulation of both genes. Compared to tFNA-I, tFNA-IM synergistically enhanced the bioavailability and delivery efficiency of itaconate, elevating NRF2 expression by 1.5-fold (p < 0.0001) and HMOX1 expression (p < 0.01). In addition, compared to the PBS group, tFNA-M significantly increased the mRNA levels of both NRF2 by approximately 1.37-fold and HMOX1 by approximately 1.34-fold. Notably, tFNA-IM group showed the most pronounced restoration of HO-1 protein expression (Fig. 4G), with levels approximately double those in the DED group. These findings suggested that tFNA-IM confers protection against oxidative stress by modulating key antioxidant pathways, particularly through NRF2–HO-1 axis. Beyond NRF2/HO-1 signaling, we also examined the effects of tFNA-IM on matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), a bidirectional regulator of oxidative stress that functions both as a downstream effector and a mediator exacerbating oxidative damage through feedback mechanisms [36]. As shown in Fig. 4F, tFNA-I reduced MMP-9 mRNA level by 63% relative to the PBS group, while tFNA-IM further suppressed expression by 68%. These findings suggest that tFNA-IM may interfere with the positive feedback loop between MMP-9 and oxidative stress, thereby attenuating the pathological progression of DED.

In addition to the regulation of NRF2/HO-1 signaling and MMP-9 expression, the activity of endogenous antioxidant enzymes plays a crucial role in maintaining cellular redox homeostasis and protecting against oxidative damage. SOD1, CAT, and GPX-1 are critical enzymatic antioxidants that work synergistically to neutralize ROS. Specifically, SOD1 converts superoxide radicals into hydrogen peroxide, which CAT and GPX-1 subsequently degrade into water and oxygen [37]. To evaluate the impact of tFNA formulations on antioxidant capacity, we evaluated the protein levels of these enzymes using Western blot analysis. As shown in Fig. 4G, both tFNA-I and tFNA-IM significantly upregulated the expression of SOD1 (by approximately 1.2-fold and 1.3-fold, respectively), CAT (by approximately 1.4-fold and 1.5-fold, respectively), and GPx-1 (by approximately 1.7-fold and 1.9-fold, respectively) compared to the PBS control group. Notably, tFNA-IM exhibited superior induction of all three enzymes, indicating that the aptamer modification enhances ITA bioavailability and antioxidant efficacy. Taken together, these findings suggests that tFNA-IM not only activates the NRF2-HO-1 axis but also amplifies the ocular surface’s overall antioxidant defense.

The in vitro anti-inflammatory assessment of tFNA-IM

As a naturally occurring metabolite of the tricarboxylic acid cycle, ITA has been widely recognized for its potent anti-inflammatory properties, primarily mediated through the inhibition of inflammatory pathways such as regulating ATF3/IκBζ axis activity and the activation of NRF2. Given prior evidence demonstrating the protective effects of tFNA-IM against oxidative damage through NRF2 activation, we further investigated its anti-inflammatory activity at the cellular level. Anti-inflammatory effects were evaluated using RT-qPCR, Western-blot, and IF.

First, RT-qPCR analysis revealed the downregulation of key inflammatory genes (NFKBIZ, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β) at the RNA level. As shown in Fig. 5A, the expression levels of these inflammatory genes were significantly increased in the PBS group after hyperosmotic treatment. Notably, tFNA-IM further reduced the mRNA levels compared to tFNA-I group with reduction of 56% (NFKBIZ), 44% (IL-6), 64% (TNF-α), and 50% (IL-1β), relative to the PBS group. In contrast, tFNA-M group showed milder effects (16–30% reduction for NFKBIZ, IL-6, IL-1β and 46% for TNF-α. These results suggested that MUC1-targeting aptamer in tFNA-IM significantly enhances anti-inflammatory gene suppression. Consistent results were observed in THP-1 cells (NFKBIZ and IL-6 inhibition; Fig. S8) and MCECs (Fig. S9).

In vitro anti-inflammatory evaluation of tFNA-IM in HCECs. (A) RT-qPCR analysis of inflammatory gene expression (NFKBIZ, IL-6, TNF-α and IL-1β) in HCECs. (B) Western blot analysis of IκBζ and IL-1β protein expression. (C) Immunofluorescence staining of IκBζ. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3). Significance: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001

Western blot analysis was performed to further confirm the anti-inflammatory effects of tFNA-IM on IκBζ and IL-1β at the protein level (Fig. 5B and S10). It could be seen that under the hyperosmotic stress, the expression of IκBζ protein was upregulated in HCECs. Moreover, tFNA-IM exhibited a concentration-dependent effect on the expression of IκBζ protein. At 500 nM, tFNA-IM led to a marked decrease in IκBζ and IL-1β protein expression, with a reduction of 66% and 63%, respectively, outperforming tFNA-I (54% and 53%) and tFNA-M (moderate inhibition). These data corroborated with the qPCR results. IF staining results (Fig. 5C) showed that the fluorescence intensity within the cell nuclei significantly increased after hyperosmotic stimulation. After treatment, the fluorescence signal was reduced. Compared with the tFNA-I group, the nuclear fluorescence intensity in the tFNA-IM group was notably decreased, indicating a significant reduction in IκBζ protein expression. Collectively, these findings suggest that tFNA-IM exerts anti-inflammatory effects via suppression of the NF-κB signaling pathway, as evidenced by IκBζ and inflammatory cytokines downregulation. Additionally, our previous data have shown that tFNA-IM activates the Nrf2/HO-1 axis, which may further contribute to its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects. This dual modulation of both the NF-κB and Nrf2 pathways highlights the potential of tFNA-IM as a promising multifunctional therapeutic agent for inflammatory diseases.

In vivo therapeutic effects of tFNA-IM

Given the favorable in vitro biocompatibility, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory effects of tFNA-IM, the DED mice model was established to evaluate its corneal permeability, in vivo therapeutic efficacy, and the safety. Prior to therapeutic assessment, an ocular retention assay was performed. To induce DED, mice were housed in a low-humidity chamber for 14 days. Subsequently, Cy5-labeled tFNA-I and tFNA-IM were topically applied to the ocular surface of non-anesthetized mice. At 3-, 10-, and 20-min post-administration, the mice were sacrificed and their eyes were enucleated and cryo-sectioned for CLSM imaging to evaluate corneal nanoparticle retention. As shown in Fig. S11, tFNA-I exhibited weak fluorescence signals on the ocular surface at 3 min, which gradually diminished over time, and became undetectable by 20 min. In contrast, tFNA-IM exhibited strong and persistent fluorescence signals at both 3 and 10 min, with significant signal retention even at the 20 min. These results demonstrate that MUC1 aptamer modification significantly enhances the ocular retention of tFNA, leading to prolonged ITA residence on the ocular surface.

The therapeutic efficacy of tFNA-IM was further investigated. According to the schematic diagram (Fig. 6A), mice were housed in a low-humidity chamber to establish DED model, and were divided into six groups and treated with different formulations, including PBS, 4-OI, tFNA-M, tFNA-I, and tFNA-IM, twice daily for 14 days. Fluorescein sodium staining (CFS) and the phenol red thread test (Schirmer test) were performed on day 0 and day 14. As shown in Fig. 6B, Fig. S12A and Fig. S12B, all DED groups except the mock group exhibited green fluorescence signals on day 0 with reduced tear secretion, confirming successful model establishment. After 14 days of treatment, the mock group exhibited minimal staining with no visible fluorescent spots, indicating intact epithelial integrity (Fig. 6B and E). In contrast, the PBS-treated group showed significant widespread fluorescent areas and a CFS score of 12.3 ± 1.0, signifying severe epithelial damage. Among the treatment groups, tFNA-IM demonstrated the strongest protective effect, with significantly reduced staining fluorescent intensity and lower scores (mean score: 3.1 ± 0.8, p < 0.0001 vs. tFNA-I). tFNA-I also showed moderate improvement, with a mean score of 6.3 ± 1.2 (p < 0.0001, tFNA-I vs. PBS), while tFNA-M exhibited a slight benefit (mean score: 8.1 ± 0.9, p < 0.0001 vs. PBS). Notably, tFNA-IM treated group outperformed tFNA-I treated group, suggesting that the aptamer modification enhances the accumulation of ITA and improves the therapeutic efficacy. Tear secretion was quantified using the Schirmer’s test to assess the functional recovery of the lacrimal gland in dry eye model mice. As presented in Fig. 6C and F, after14 days of treatment, tFNA-IM treated group significantly increased tear secretion with an average value of 5.8 ± 1.0 mm, compared to the tFNA-I treated group with an average value of 3.0 ± 0.8 mm. In contrast, the PBS group exhibited a marked reduction in tear secretion, with an average of only 1.3 ± 0.5 mm. The ocular surface mucin is primarily produced by conjunctival goblet cells, which are essential components of the tear film and play a crucial role in maintaining its stability. Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) glycogen staining (Fig. 6D and G) revealed that DED mice exhibited goblet cell shrinkage and reduced cell density compared to mock controls. Quantitative analysis revealed that the PBS-treated group showed only 35.4% of the goblet cell number observed in the Mock group. In contrast, all treated groups demonstrated an increase in goblet cell numbers, with the tFNA-I group reaching 74.3% of the Mock level. Notably, the tFNA-IM group exhibited the most pronounced recovery, with goblet cell density increasing to 93.0% of that in the Mock group. In summary, tFNA-IM promotes corneal epithelial repair, enhances goblet cell recovery, reduces CFS scores, and increases tear secretion, demonstrating superior therapeutic efficacy in DED.

Next, we investigated the impact of tFNA-IM on macrophage infiltration and polarization. F4/80 immunostaining shown in Fig. 7A revealed stronger positive signals in the PBS-treated group than that in the Mock group, predominantly localized in the corneal layer, indicating that macrophage accumulation in the inflammatory regions of the cornea. In contrast, both the tFNA-I and tFNA-IM treated groups showed significantly reduced macrophage infiltration. Moreover, CD86/CD206 staining results in Fig. 7B showed that PBS group mainly exhibited M1-type macrophage infiltration, as indicated by the strong CD86 fluorescence and weak CD206 green fluorescence, suggesting a pronounced inflammatory state in the ocular surface. In contrast, both treatment groups showed significantly reduced CD86 fluorescence and markedly increased CD206 fluorescence in the cornea, indicating that the infiltrated macrophages had polarized from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. RT-qPCR analysis confirmed the differential expression of several representative inflammatory (Nfkbiz, IL-1β, TNF-α) and the antioxidant gene (Nrf2). As shown in Fig. 7C, the PBS group showed a significant upregulation of Nfkbiz, IL-1β, and TNF-α compared to the Mock group, indicating a strong inflammatory activation. Importantly, tFNA-IM treatment significantly suppressed the expression of these inflammatory markers (Nfkbiz, IL-1β, and TNF-α), with reductions of 62.1%, 71.2%, and 84.0%, respectively, demonstrating potent anti-inflammatory activity. In contrast, the expression of Nrf2 was significantly downregulated in the PBS group. However, tFNA-IM treatment restored Nrf2 expression to near-normal levels, with a 101.5% increase compared to PBS group. This finding is consistent with the observed reduction in oxidative stress and supports the hypothesis that tFNA-IM exerts protective effects through both anti-inflammatory and antioxidant pathways. Other treatment groups (tFNA-I, tFNA-M) showed partial efficacy but less effective modulation of these gene expressions, reinforcing the superior modulation of tFNA-IM in targeting multiple pathological processes associated with DED.

To evaluate the impact of the treatments on the viability and regenerative capacity of the ocular surface epithelium, we performed TUNEL staining for apoptosis and CK3 immunostaining for cell proliferation. As shown in Fig. 7D and Fig. S13, PBS group exhibited a large number of red TUNEL-positive cells on the corneal surface, along with weak green CK3 fluorescence, indicating increased apoptosis and reduced cell proliferation in the corneas of DED mice. In contrast, the tFNA-IM group showed significant suppression of apoptosis and the highest number of CK3-positive cells, suggesting enhanced cell repair and recovery of the damaged cornea. These findings were consistent with the results obtained from in vitro cell experiments.

In vivo safety evaluation

Following the comprehensive evaluation of the therapeutic efficacy of tFNA-IM, its biological safety was further investigated to ensure its potential for clinical application. Serum biochemical parameters, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CREA), were measured to evaluate potential organ toxicity. Fig. S14 showed that all these markers remained within normal physiological ranges, suggesting that tFNA-IM exhibited good biocompatibility with no systemic toxicity or adverse effects on hepatic or renal function. This indicates that there was no apparent hepatic or renal toxicity in any of the treatment groups. Furthermore, Histopathological analysis of corneal and retinal tissues (Fig. S15) and major organs including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney (Fig. S16) was performed using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to assess any morphological changes or tissue damage. No significant histopathological abnormalities were observed in the cornea, retina, or any of the major organs in any treatment groups. These findings collectively confirm that tFNA-IM exhibits excellent biocompatibility and minimal toxicity, supporting its potential for further preclinical development and future clinical translation.

Conclusion

In summary, we have successfully developed a mucin-1-targeted tetrahedral DNA nanostructure (tFNA-IM) for efficient ocular delivery of itaconate, addressing the critical challenges of corneal permeability and bioavailability in DED treatment. The rationally designed tFNA-IM system combines the intrinsic antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of ITA with the enhanced cellular uptake and tissue targeting capabilities of the DNA nanostructure, creating a synergistic therapeutic platform. Through phosphorothioate-mediated conjugation and precise self-assembly, the resulting construct demonstrated remarkable efficacy in disrupting the oxidative stress-inflammation cycle characteristic of DED pathogenesis. The tFNA-IM platform significantly improved corneal epithelial integrity, reduced inflammatory markers, and showed excellent biocompatibility in vivo, while overcoming the limitations of conventional therapeutic approaches. This work not only establishes a novel targeted delivery strategy for DED treatment but also provides a generalizable framework for developing nucleic acid nanoplatforms to address various ocular surface disorders, bridging the gap between metabolic immunomodulation and precision nanomedicine in ophthalmology.
