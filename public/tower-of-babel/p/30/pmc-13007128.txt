Systematic review of prediction models and meta-analysis of risk factors for invasive fungal infection in children
Authors: Jianing Liu, Ruonan Gao, Liang Huang, Yu Zhu, Guo Cheng, Linan Zeng, Qin Yu, Hailong Li, Lingli Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo review the application of prediction models and risk factors identified by prediction models for invasive fungal infection (IFI) in children, and assess model performance, methodological rigour and applicability.DesignThis is a systematic review of diagnostic prediction models and a meta-analysis of the risk factors. This study was registered on PROSPERO and performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis and Prediction model risk of bias assessment tool.Data sourcesPubMed, Embase (Ovid), Medline, Cochrane Library and four Chinese Databases were searched on 10 Mar 2025.Eligibility criteriaWe included original studies that developed diagnostic prediction models for IFI in children and excluded the informal records.Data extraction and synthesisOdds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated for risk factors, and a random-effects meta-analysis was applied to factors reported in at least two studies. For prediction models, a descriptive analysis was conducted to summarise model characteristics, model performance and the risk of bias.ResultsNine studies were included from 4069 articles. Nine studies developed ten diagnostic prediction models, and logistic regression was the most commonly used method. The predictive performance showed an area under receiver operating curves (AUROC) ranging from 0.76 to 0.95, but meta-analysis of AUROC was not conducted due to heterogeneity. All studies were identified as having a high risk of bias in critical appraisal, particularly in the analysis, mainly due to the lack of validation, as well as the failure to appropriately evaluate model performance and overfitting. Only two of nine studies that developed prediction models used internal or external validation.ConclusionsLogistic regression is a common method for predicting IFI in children, although machine learning methods have been popular in prediction models. Our study identified all studies as high risk of bias. To reduce bias, studies should use calibration measures, internal and external validation more frequently, and consider shrinkage methods when developing models.

Introduction

Invasive fungal infection (IFI) is an infectious disease in which fungi invade the human body, grow and reproduce in tissues, organs or blood, and cause inflammation and tissue damage.1 Invasive candidiasis (IC) and invasive aspergillosis (IA) are the main types of IFI. At present, the overall incidence of IFI is not clear. The incidence of IC and IA ranges from 4.3 to 19 per 10 000 children per year, and the case fatality rate is 10% to 29%, which can lead to a significant extension of hospital stay.2

IFI is a condition that can occur in children with a range of underlying conditions, including haematological diseases, immune system diseases and malignant tumours. Other risk factors for IFI include receiving solid organ or bone marrow transplantation, being hospitalised in the paediatric intensive care unit (PICU), and using immunosuppressive agents.3 4 The lack of specificity in clinical signs and imaging findings of IFI limits the sensitivity and specificity of current diagnostic methods. Therefore, it is challenging to identify and diagnose IFI clinically.5 Consequently, the timely identification of high-risk populations for IFI and the initiation of antifungal therapy as soon as possible will help to control fungal spread and improve the prognosis of children.6

Prediction models can be used to assess the probability of patients suffering from a certain disease currently or the likelihood of a certain outcome in the future.7 The development of predictive models can be based on traditional modelling methods, including logistic regression, multiple linear regression and Cox regression, and new methods such as machine learning methods. However, after fitting the prediction models, it is essential to assess the performance of the prediction models, decide on the final model and perform a decision curve analysis.8 The performance of the prediction model could be evaluated by parameters such as discrimination and calibration.9 Besides, internal and external validation of clinical predictive models also needs to evaluate their reproducibility and generalisability for new and different patients.10 The methodological defects may overestimate the prediction performance of the development queue, resulting in misclassification of the results and inaccurate risk estimation, which limits the application of the model in the real world.11 Prediction models mainly include diagnostic models and prognostic models,7 and the diagnostic models can assist in the identification of high-risk groups at an early stage, thereby enabling the implementation of timely prevention and treatment measures. Several fungal-specific scoring systems, such as the Candida scoring system, have been demonstrated to facilitate the administration of antifungal therapy to adult IFI patients at an early stage.12 Diagnostic prediction models for diseases such as hypertension, breast cancer and so on have also been shown to possess application value.12 13 A systematic review of diagnostic predictive models for adult patients with IC showed that clinical prediction models may be useful for early identification of the risk for IC,14 highlighting the importance of the development of prediction models. Although diagnostic prediction models have been developed for IFI in children, their performance and potential methodological limitations remain unevaluated. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to review the current diagnostic clinical predictive models of IFI in children and to evaluate the rigour and applicability of model methodology and performance. Besides, we also conducted a meta-analysis of the risk factors revealed by prediction models.

Method

Protocol and registry

The study followed the guidelines provided by the Preferred Reporting Project for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020.15 This study was registered in PROSPERO under the registration number CRD42024513930.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Children who <18 years old with IFI; (2) Original research that develops a diagnostic prediction model and (3) Research that reports the results of model performance, including model coefficients, simplified model and area under receiver operating curves (AUROC).

Exclusion criteria: (1) Repeated publication or without full text; (2) Studies identifying individual predictors instead of developing a prediction model and (3) Informal publications, such as commentaries, editorials and meeting abstracts.

Search strategy

We searched PubMed, Embase (Ovid), Medline (Ovid), the Cochrane Library, the Chinese BioMedical Literature Database (CBM), China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), the Wanfang Database and VIP Database from inception to 10 March 2025, and also searched references to include additional studies. The searches were initially conducted in January 2024 and renewed in March 2025.

The search strategy was adjusted specifically for each database. It took a combination of subject words and free-text terms, including “Infant”, “Child”, “Adolescent”, “Pediatric”, “Invasive Fungal Infection”, “Clinical prediction rule” and others. Detailed search strategies are provided in online supplemental appendix 1.

Study selection

After removing duplicates, two authors (JL and RG) independently reviewed the titles and abstracts according to the inclusion criteria. Then, the same authors independently reviewed the full text of potentially relevant articles for inclusion. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion or consulting a third reviewer (HL).

Data extraction

Two authors (JL and RG) independently extracted data using Microsoft Excel according to CHecklist for critical Appraisal and data extraction for systematic Reviews of prediction Modelling Studies (CHARMS) .16 Any disagreements were resolved by discussion or consulting a third reviewer (HL). The items for extraction include basic information such as study design, patient characteristics and sample size; risk factors of IFI were identified by multivariable analysis; methods to develop the model and model performance.

Quality assessment

We assessed the risk of bias for each article by using the Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool (PROBAST, developed by the Cochrane Prognosis Methods Group).11

Data synthesis

For the risk factors of IFI, the Odds Ratio (OR) and 95% Confidence Interval (95%CI) will be calculated using Stata V.17.0 (Stata Statistical Software, Stata). We conducted a meta-analysis for risk factors reported by more than two studies, and the descriptive analysis was used to describe risk factors only reported by one study. For the meta-analysis, heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, and the meta-analysis was performed using the random-effects model regardless of heterogeneity. Only one study did not report the OR, and we calculated the OR using the formula OR=eβ based on the model coefficients and did not include this study in the meta-analysis.

For the results of the prediction model, due to the high heterogeneity of diagnosis, inclusion of population, clinical department, study setting and study region, we did not conduct a meta-analysis. Instead, descriptive analysis was conducted to provide an overview of the prediction models for IFI in children and summarised the model types, methods of development and model performance. Besides, we also provided percentages of studies in high, low and unclear risk of bias per risk of bias item, to highlight which areas should be focused on.

Results

Literature search

A total of 4069 studies were searched, including 4065 studies through search and 4 studies identified by screening the references. Nine out of 2389 non-duplicated studies were finally included in our study, and the 9 studies developed 10 diagnostic predictive models, of which Alali17 developed two prediction models (figure 1).

Study characteristics

Seven studies were cohort studies (four for retrospective and three for prospective), while the other two were case–control studies. The number of children included in these studies ranged from 81 to 1515 (median=153, IQR 126–667). The children were mainly recruited from the Children’s Hospital (6/9), followed by the Department of Paediatrics of the General Hospital (2/9), and only one study is a multicentre study, in which children were recruited from the Neonatal Research Network. Four papers included children in the ICU, four included all hospitalised children, and one study included children after surgery (table 1).

Three studies only included children in the PICU, without limitations on the population. For diagnostic criteria, seven studies were based on guidelines (five based on the EORTC/MSG and three based on the Chinese guidelines, with one on both), and two based on positive results of blood cultures (more details are shown in online supplemental table S1).

Risk factors of IFI

Eight types of variables, including fever, length of stay, use of antibiotics, haematological indicators, central catheter, comorbidity, pattern of nutrition and others such as age, were included in 10 prediction models. However, nine prediction models included all data collected during clinical encounters, and only one prediction model restricted risk factors to data available at the time of diagnosis. Therefore, some of the variables they included were risk factors for IFI in children, whereas others were clinical features more likely to be observed in paediatric patients with IFI. Thus, we only reported variables available at the time of diagnosis as risk factors of IFI (more details in online supplemental table S2).

For the risk factors, seven types of risk factors are reported in the included studies, including length of stay, use of antibiotics, haematological indicators, central catheter, comorbidity and pattern of nutrition. However, the specific variables of each type are inconsistent; most of the risk factors were only reported by one study; therefore, only part of the research results is pooled by meta-analysis (table 2). The results of the meta-analysis showed that the use of more than two antibiotics (OR 9.19, 95% CI 4.77 to 17.68, I²=0.0%, number of children [n]=521, Number of studies [N]=2, online supplemental figure 1), higher C reactive protein (CRP) (OR 1.06, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.12, I²=68.2%, n=279, N=2, online supplemental figure 2) and central venous catheter (OR 2.28, 95% CI 1.36 to 3.83, I²=88.2%, n=1645, N=2, online supplemental figure 3) are risk factors for IFI.

Prediction model

And the others did not report whether there were missing data and how the missing data were handled (table 3, more details shown in online supplemental table S3).

Modelling method

Logistic regression was employed in 9/10 prediction models to develop predictive models, and generalised linear mixed models were used in the other one (table 3). All of these were traditional statistical methods, without incorporating new modelling methods, such as deep learning. Among the eight prediction models that mentioned the selection method for candidate predictors, five included statistically significant predictors in univariate analysis, and two included all candidate predictors. For the selection method for predictors in the modelling process, the Stepwise selection method was used in five prediction models, and the other five included statistically significant predictors in multivariate analysis.

Model predictive performance

Evaluating predictive model performance primarily involves calibration and discrimination.16 Only one of the 10 prediction models reported calibration methods and results,18 and all of them reported the C-statistic or presented the AUC graph. The values of AUROC ranged from 0.76 to 0.95 (table 3). Due to the heterogeneity in patient disease, sources and clinical departments, the meta-analysis was not conducted.

Model evaluation

One of the prediction models contains internal validation (table 3). Li et al conducted internal validation by 1000 bootstrap resamples and calculated the variance inflation factor (VIF) for each predictor variable, considering overfitting in their study.18

Only one of nine models performed external validation18 (table 3). Ding et al conducted external validation by including different visit times. The validation group results revealed a 9.69% clinical IFI positive rate vs a 9.25% predicted rate by the scoring system, with Kappa=0.769. In the validation group, the sensitivity was 75.0%, and the specificity was 98.1%, aligning well with the results of the training set (sensitivity 77.8%; specificity 92.2%).19

Subgroup analysis

We conducted a subgroup analysis for studies set in Children’s Hospital. There are six studies conducted in Children’s Hospital, developing seven prediction models. All of these studies developed prediction models based on logistic regression, and five of them are retrospective cohort studies. The EPV of these studies is lower than 10, most of which range from 2 to 7.4. Although all of them reported C-Statistic, AUROC graph and AUROC (ranging from 0.76 to 0.95, median=0.89), only one study reported missing data, calibration and clinical utility.

Assessment of bias

The results of the PROBAST evaluation are shown in table 4. Seven prediction models were identified as high risk of bias in the PARTICIPANTS domain, mainly due to irrational data sources (non-nested case–control and retrospective cohort studies), the data of which are from existing sources. All models were identified as low risk of bias in the PREDICTORS domain. Four models were identified as high risk of bias in the OUTCOME domain, primarily due to the predictors not being excluded from the outcome definition (3/4), or the lack of blinding of predictors to outcome (2/4). For the ANALYSIS domain, all models were identified as high risk of bias for the low EPV. Regarding the handling of missing data (Item 4.4), most studies were marked as ‘No Information’ for lack of reports of missing data and handling methods, so it is impossible to determine whether the correct method was used to handle missing data. For APPLICABILITY, all models were identified as low risk of bias (more details are shown in online supplemental table S4).

Discussion

In this study, we systematically reviewed the current research status of prediction models for children with IFI and the risk factors that the prediction models indicate. The results of the literature survey indicated that the models were mainly targeted at children in ICUs, and most of the prediction models were based on single-centre data, which may have certain defects in clinical applicability. Logistic regression was the most commonly used modelling method in the included studies, and all of them employed traditional statistical methods for modelling.

For the risk factors in IFI, our study shows that >2 antibiotics, higher CRP and the use of a central catheter are the potential risk factors for IFI. However, due to the heterogeneity, more studies are needed.

The development of predictive models can be based on traditional modelling methods and new methods such as machine learning. Traditional modelling methods primarily include logistic regression, multiple linear regression and Cox regression, the advantages of which are that the resulting model is simple in form, has good transparency and interpretability, and the results are relatively robust.20 In recent years, machine learning, one of the approaches to artificial intelligence that allows for a data-driven approach to develop models and reduce the decision-making burden on modellers, has been used in the medical field. Although this method faces challenges such as extrapolation potential due to overfitting, its proficiency in fitting data, combined with its flexibility and the development of big data, has made it a leading approach for predictive modelling.21 22 Machine learning has already been applied in the field of adult IFI. A study comparing the prediction performance of four models with logistic regression for adult Candidaemia patients found that all four demonstrated superior efficiency to logistic regression.23 We found that none of the included studies use machine learning to develop diagnostic prediction models for IFI in children, which may be related to the small number of children with IFI included. However, there have been a lot of studies that used machine learning to develop prediction models,24 and some methods to deal with the low incidence and small sample size. Banerjee et al reviewed the use of machine learning in small sample sets, specifically in rare diseases.25 In this study, they concluded that combining datasets to increase the number of samples, dimensionality-reduction methods to find the underlying structure in the data, combining with statistical techniques to reduce misinterpretation of model output, building on prior knowledge and indirectly related data, etc, may improve the usage of machine learning. Besides, for extremely low incidence events, Pickering et al26 have developed a scalable, model-agnostic, Bayesian-inspired DNO-BED framework, which also indicated the potential usage of machine learning in low incidence and small sample size events. Thus, given the complexity of clinical data, future research could explore the potential application of machine learning methods in developing diagnostic prediction models for IFI in children.

Modelling methods

All prediction models included in our research were at high risk of bias, mainly due to irrational data sources, the predictors not excluded from the outcome definition or the lack of blinding of predictors to outcome, and the low EPV.

For the data sources, the design with the lowest ROB for diagnostic model studies is a cross-sectional study, and cohort, randomised controlled trial, or nested case–control study could also be used.11 In cohort studies, a prospective cohort design is preferable; however, retrospective studies can be more practical and more feasible to conduct,16 which is consistent with our observation that most IFI prediction models are based on retrospective cohort data. Retrospective studies are often limited by poorer data quality and a lack of measured predictors, which makes appropriately conducted data analysis critically important.16 Challenges like inaccurate measurement of inclusion/exclusion criteria data and difficulties in adjusting baselines arise, potentially introducing bias risks due to data quality concerns.11 For example, potential temporal bias in case-control studies can lead to a reduced predictive effect of the predictors or even lead to prediction failure.27

For the predictors, failure to exclude predictors from the outcome definition and lack of blinding of predictors to outcome may increase the association between predictors and outcomes, leading to an overestimation of model performance and bias.11 Besides, our study also found that most of the models were developed based on all data collected during clinical encounters rather than data available at the time of diagnosis, which may limit the applicability of the prediction models. With a view to addressing this concern, a study, which focuses on invasive fungal disease in adult haematological patients undergoing chemotherapy, developed a prediction model based on all available predictors and excluded risk factors unavailable prior to chemotherapy from the model to improve its clinical applicability.28 Besides, the identification of each predictor varied among different studies, making it difficult to apply in different clinical settings. It is recommended to include predictors available at or prior to the time of diagnosis and define the predictors according to international guidelines or expert consensus.

An EPV of at least 10 has been widely adopted as a criterion to minimise overfitting; in our study, only one prediction model met the required patient numbers.18 Insufficient sample size may pose a risk of overfitting and overestimate the model’s performance, especially for models which typically require an EPV>200 to reduce the risk of overfitting.11 However, the PROBAST suggested higher EPVs of at least 20, and that the sample size needed to minimise overfitting is context-specific and depends on outcome prevalence, overall model performance (R2) and predictor distributions, which means EPV is no longer the most appropriate method for evaluating the sample size of prediction model studies. In our study, due to the low risk of IFI (4.3 to 19 per 10 000 children per year), it is difficult for researchers to recruit more children, and other methods should be used to deal with overfitting. Internal validation is one of the most commonly used methods to reduce overfitting, of which the simplest method is dividing the original data into training and validation sets, and k-fold cross-validation and Bootstrapping are more efficient.8 11 However, only 1 of the 10 prediction models conducted internal validation;18 thus, most of them failed to avoid potential overfitting issues. Statistical methods can also be used to reduce model overfitting. Shrinkage methods, such as penalised regression, Ridge regression and Lasso regression, can reduce the impact of overfitting by calculating the shrinkage factor.29 30 However, only one of the included studies used bootstrap and calculated the VIF;18 most of them failed to use these methods to reduce overfitting. It is recommended that studies use internal validation and statistical methods such as shrinkage methods to avoid overfitting.

Among the prediction models, five of them included statistically significant indicators from univariate analysis in multivariate modelling. However, in univariate analysis, some potential predictors may not have shown statistical significance due to the limitations of the sample data, whereas certain predictors may only become statistically significant after adjustment. Instead, non-statistical methods combined with prior knowledge and the current context should be adopted for the selection process.11 In the multivariate modelling process, five studies included statistically significant variables in multivariable regression, which are also not recommended.11

Only one of the included studies reported the missing data and excluded all patients with missing data,18 while the others did not report the missing data handling methods. Missing data are frequent in medical research, which is often not ‘missing completely at random’ nor ‘missing at random’. Complete case analysis can lead to a reduction in sample size and incorrect estimation of the predictor-outcome association. Multiple imputation is currently considered the best approach for handling missing data.31 Despite the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis standard published in 2015 stating that both in the development and validation of prediction models, it is essential to report missing data, describe the details of handling missing data, and discuss it in the limitations of the study,7 failure to report missing data is still common: it is prevalent in different disease areas such as postpartum haemorrhage.32

Nine of the 10 models were considered not to assess model performance correctly, as none of them performed calibration. Calibration refers to the consistency between model predictions and observed outcomes. Calibration plots display the direction and magnitude of errors between the model and the outcome over the entire prediction horizon, while the Hosmer-Lemeshow test does not provide such information. Therefore, calibration plots are preferred.11 However, most studies tend to report discrimination rather than calibration when evaluating the performance of predictive models.33 Besides, only one study used temporal validation as external validation, which may limit the applicability of the use of the models, and it is recommended to use external validation to improve the model performance in different study settings.

A study of supervised modelling methods found that the bias was mainly related to small sample sizes, not properly handling missing data and failure to adequately address overfitting,34 which is similar to our study. It is clear that the above problems are common biases in various modelling methods, and researchers should attach great importance to them in the subsequent research on developing predictive models.

Limitations

There are some limitations in our study. First of all, due to the high heterogeneity, a meta-analysis of the AUROC of the included studies was not performed. However, the AUROC of the included studies ranged from 0.76 to 0.95, indicating good performance of discrimination of the studies. Additionally, for the risk factors, the results of risk factors are mainly based on descriptive analysis and data extracted from the original studies; the validity of the synthesised evidence is ultimately constrained by the quality of the data reported in those primary sources. Differences in department and population of the included studies might lead to potential bias in risk factors for IFI. Furthermore, among the studies included in our research, the population, setting and department varied: four studies recruited children from the ICU, one recruited hospitalised children after liver transplantation, one included children with acute leukaemia and two studies focused on hospitalised children with tumours. Therefore, the reliability of comparisons between included studies was limited by the heterogeneity of the population, setting and department. Finally, we only included Chinese and English literature and did not include literature in other languages, which may introduce a certain bias.

Conclusions

This study found that the diagnostic prediction models for paediatric IFI were not only based on data available at the time of diagnosis but were also methodologically assessed as high risk of bias, both of which limit the clinical applicability of these models. There are some suggestions for improving the clinical applicability of prediction models in IFI. For variable selection, predictors should be limited to clinical data available at or before diagnosis (eg, medication history) and defined using international guidelines to ensure consistency across studies and clinical settings. During development, overfitting should be mitigated via shrinkage methods (eg, Ridge or Lasso regression) and internal validation (eg, k-fold cross-validation or bootstrapping). External validation (temporal or spatial) is essential, with reporting of model discrimination, calibration and clinical utility. Finally, given data complexity, future research may explore machine learning for paediatric IFI prediction models.
