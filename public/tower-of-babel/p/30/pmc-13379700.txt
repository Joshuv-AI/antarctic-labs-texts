The Impact of Induction of Insulin Secretion in MIN6 Cells on the Extent of Cytoophidia Formation and Their Structural Forms
Authors: Maryam Karimi, Razieh Yazdanparast, Kolsoom Shahdadnejad
Published: 2026

Insulin secretion depends on guanosine triphosphate (GTP) synthesized by Inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase (IMPDH). 1. Background

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic condition defined by persistently high blood glucose levels over an extended period ( 1 , 2 ). The main causes of this disease are insufficient insulin secretion by beta cells or resistance of cells to insulin ( 1 , 3 ). Studies have shown that guanosine triphosphate (GTP) plays a crucial role in insulin secretion and that the inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase (IMPDH), a key enzyme in the GTP biosynthetic pathway, is highly abundant in beta cells and can significantly impact insulin secretion ( 4 - 9 ).

IMPDH is a highly conserved enzyme involved in purine metabolism, catalyzing the initial step in the de novo synthesis of GTP by converting IMP to XMP. This enzyme is subject to tight regulation at multiple levels ( 10 , 11 ). Structurally, the IMPDH monomer comprises two main domains: a catalytic domain responsible for the conversion of IMP and NAD+ into XMP and NADH, and a regulatory subdomain known as the CBS or Bateman domain. The connection between the catalytic domain and the CBS domains is flexible, allowing variations in their relative orientation ( 10 , 12 ). IMPDH exists in solution as a homotetramer, but when nucleotides such as ATP or GTP bind to sites in the regulatory domains, they cause reversible dimerization of the regulatory domains, forming octamers. The effect of nucleotide binding is to cause the enzyme to form a filamentous ultrastructure known as cytoophidia ( 13 , 14 ). When cellular GTP levels decrease or demand increases, the binding of nucleotides (such as ATP) to the regulatory CBS domains triggers a conformational change that leads to the formation of reversible octamers. These octamers then assemble into larger filamentous structures called cytoophidia, which can be observed as rod- or ring-shaped filaments under immunofluorescence. Cytoophidia are functional metabolic compartments that increase the catalytic efficiency of IMPDH under GTP stress conditions. These structures enhance the local concentration of substrates by sequestering active enzymes into filaments, stabilize the active octameric state, and protect IMPDH from feedback inhibition, so facilitating GTP production ( 9 , 13 - 15 ).

IMPDH can form rod and ring-shaped cytoophidia when inhibited by its inhibitors, such as Mycophenolic acid (MPA) and Ribavirin (Riba), or when the cells’ need for GTP increases. Rod-shaped cytoophidia are typically 3 to 10 μm in length, while ring-shaped cytoophidia have a diameter of 2 to 5 μm. The thickness of both rod- and ring-shaped cytoophidia can be more than 500 nm. In most cells, rod-shaped cytoophidia are the main form ( 15 , 16 ). Recent studies have further elucidated the biophysical mechanisms underlying cytoophidium assembly, including the role of molecular crowding in facilitating IMPDH cytoophidia bundling under physiological stress ( 17 ), and have highlighted IMPDH dysregulation’s broader implications in metabolic diseases, including diabetes ( 18 ). These structures have been observed in cultured mammalian cells and mouse pancreatic beta cells ( 9 ). In pancreatic β-cells, GTP plays a crucial role in driving G-protein signaling, vesicle trafficking, and the exocytosis of insulin granules ( 4 - 8 , 19 ); therefore, cytoophidia are localized adjacent to insulin granules to supply GTP ( 20 ). However, in the pancreas, the detailed organization of IMPDH cytoophidia remains not fully elucidated.

Previous studies have shown that GTP is essential for insulin secretion ( 19 ). The increased consumption requires an increase in GTP produc-tion. Since IMPDH is the primary enzyme responsible for GTP synthesis, it is logical that the enzyme’s activity must also increase during insulin secretion to meet the elevated cellular demand for GTP ( 9 ). This increase in activity is likely facilitated by the formation of cytoophidia structures.

While recent studies have identified the presence of IMPDH cytoophidia in pancreatic beta cells ( 9 , 20 ), the dynamic structural changes of cytoophidia during insulin secretion and their response to pharmacological inhibition remain poorly understood. This study addresses this gap by systematically characterizing the morphological adaptations of cytoophidia in MIN6 cells under glucose stimulation and IMPDH inhibition, providing novel insights into their role in supporting GTP-dependent insulin secretion and their potential as a therapeutic target in diabetes.

2. Objective

Herein, we aimed to investigate the structural changes of IMPDH cytoophidia during insulin secretion in MIN6 cells, a mouse pancreatic beta cell line, in the presence and absence of enzyme inhibitors. Additionally, we aimed to evaluate the expression of the enzyme during insulin secretion, which underscores the importance of its role in providing GTP.

3. Materials and Methods

3.1. Materials

Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) from Gibco (Catalog No.: 31600083), MTT from Sigma (Catalog No.: M2128), MPA and Riba from Sigma (Catalog Nos.: M3536 and 36791-04-5, respectively), and GTP from Sigma (Catalog No.: 51120). For immunological analyses, primary antibodies against IMPDH (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; Catalog No.: SC-166551) and actin (Sigma; Catalog No.: A3853-100UL) were used. Secondary antibodies included anti-mouse IgG (H + L) from Biosource (Catalog No.: AL1104) and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse antibody from Abcam (Catalog No.: ab150113). Additionally, Hoechst stain from Acros Organics (Catalog No.: 33258) and a mouse insulin ELISA kit from Padgin Teb Co (Catalog No.: PTC-10062-M9648) were employed.

3.2. Cell Culture

MIN6 cells were cultured in DMEM medium (25 mM glucose), supplemented with 0.05 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 2 mM L-glutamine, 20 mM HEPES, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, along with 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The culture medium was changed every 24 h to ensure optimal growth and viability.

3.3. MTT Assay

The cytotoxic effects of MPA and Riba on MIN6 cells were evaluated using the MTT assay. Briefly, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 104 cells/well for 24 h. Subsequently, cells were treated with different concentrations of MPA for 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h, and Riba for 24 h. Following treatment, 20 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. After incubation, 100 μL of DMSO was added to each well and incubated in the dark for 15 min to solubilize the formazan crystals. Finally, the absorbance was measured using a microplate reader at a wavelength of 570 nm ( 21 ).

3.4. Insulin Secretion Assay

MIN6 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 4 × 104 cells/well for 24 h. Then, the cells were then washed twice with glucose-free Krebs buffer (115 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 24 mM NaHCO3, supplemented with 2.2 mM CaCl2 and 20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4) containing 0.02% BSA. Following, the cells were starved for 45 min in the same buffer at 37 °C. After starvation, they were washed again with glucose-free Krebs buffer and subsequently incubated for 2 h in Krebs buffer containing varying glucose concentrations (3.1 mM, 6.3 mM, 12.5 mM, and 25.0 mM). Insulin secretion was then measured using a mouse insulin ELISA kit.

3.5. Immunofluorescence Analysis

The cells were first washed twice with PBS after removing the supernatant and then fixed in methanol at -20 °C for 10 min to maintain cellular integrity. After fixation, the cells were washed three times with PBS and blocked for 1 h in PBS supplemented with 1% BSA. The cells were then incubated overnight with the IMPDH primary antibody, diluted in PBS containing 1% BSA, at 4 °C. Subsequently, the cells were washed three times with PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 and incubated for 1 h with a fluorescent secondary antibody in the dark at room temperature. Following this incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20. To visualize nuclear structures, the cells were stained with Hoechst for 15 min. Finally, after a triple wash with PBS, the cells were examined using an OPTIKA IM-3F HBO fluorescence microscope to observe the localization of IMPDH.

3.6. Western Blot Analysis

To evaluate protein expression, cells were rinsed with PBS and lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors from the Abcam kit (Catalog No.: ab206996). After lysis, the samples were centrifuged at 10,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentrations were determined using the Lowry method ( 22 ). Subsequently, 70 μg of protein from each sample was separated via SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (MILLIPORE; Catalog No.: IPVH00010). The membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature with TBST containing 5% nonfat milk to prevent nonspecific binding. Following blocking, they were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against IMPDH and β-actin. After incubation, the membranes were washed five times with TBST for 5 min each and then treated with anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were washed again five times with TBST for 5 min each. Finally, protein bands were visualized using ECL and exposed to X-ray film.

3.7. Image Analysis

Image analysis was conducted using Fiji software. “Particle Analysis” within the “BoneJ” plugin, located in the “Plugins” section, was used to measure the length and thickness of cytoophidia.

3.8. Quantitative Analysis

Quantitative analysis was performed using Fiji software. The data are presented as mean values ± standard deviation (SD) to provide a comprehensive overview of the variability within each data set. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA through GraphPad Prism software to assess significant differences among the experimental groups, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

4. Results

4.1. The Cytotoxic Effects of MPA and Riba On MIN6 Cells

MIN6 cells were treated with MPA at different concentrations (0-100 µM) for 24, 48, and 72 hrs, and with Riba at different concentrations (0-150 µM) for 24 h. The data demonstrate that neither drug exhibited significant cytotoxicity after 24 h of treatment, with more than 70% of the cells remaining viable at concentrations up to 50 µM of MPA and 150 µM of Riba (Fig. S1). Given that the cells typically showed cytotoxic effects within 24 h or 48 h of MPA treatment ( 23 - 25 ), the cells were subsequently treated with MPA for 72 h. After 72 h, it was observed that doses >1.5 μM resulted in cytotoxic effects. In subsequent experiments, the cells were treated with MPA and/or Riba at concentrations of 2 μM and 4 μM, respectively.

4.2. Glucose-Stimulated Insulin Secretion in the Presence and Absence of MPA and Riba

MIN6 cells were treated with different glucose concentrations (3.1, 6.3, 12.5, and 25.0 mM) and for various time intervals (20, 40, 60, and 120 min) to evaluate insulin secretion. As shown in Figure 1A, insulin secretion increased in a time- and dose-dependent manner. Peaking at 25.0 mM glucose for 60 min. This condition was used in subsequent experiments. Notably, treating cells with 25 mM glucose for 1 h significantly increased insulin secretion, but inhibition of IMPDH by MPA /or Riba significantly decreased insulin secretion, although it did not reach zero (Fig. 1B), indicating that despite this inhibition, the enzyme retained some level of activity and continued to supply GTP to the cell, probably via the formation of cytoophidia structures.

4.4. Effects of MPA and Riba on Cytoophidia Formation during Insulin Secretion in MIN6 Cells

To examine the morphology of cytoophidia structures during insulin secretion, MIN6 cells were starved with Krebs buffer for 45 min and then treated with 25 mM glucose (Glc) for 1 h. As shown in Figure 3A, Glc caused the formation of short and thick rod-shaped cytoophidia structures; whereas under control conditions (Fig. 2A), no distinct morphology among the cells. Following 1 h of Glc treatment, the cytoophidia structures were formed. This might be due to the fact that when the cells were exposed to Glc, they began to secrete insulin, which increased their need for GTP. Thus, the enzyme should become more active during insulin secretion, and this was achieved through the formation of cytoophidia structures. We also evaluated the structural shapes of cytoophidia when the cells were simultaneously exposed to Glc and each of the inhibitors. As shown in Figure 3B, 3C, when the cells were treated with Glc during inhibition by MPA and/or Riba, it was observed that the cytoophidia structures were thicker. In fact, it seemed that the presence of Glc caused the structures to change to become thicker with no change in the length. This might explain was why during insulin secretion, when the enzyme was inhibited by MPA or Riba, insulin secretion did not decline to zero (Fig. 1B) and the enzyme changed its structure to increase its activity to counteract the inhibition, providing the cell with the GTP it needed, but these changes were not enough and insulin secretion decreased by half or more.

4.5. Cytoophidia Structural Variations during Different Treatments

The changes in the length and thickness of the cytoophidia structures are shown in Figure 4.

Changes in the length and thickness of cytoophidia structures. A) Length and B) thickness of cytoophidia structures were examined during each treatment. **** (P < 0.0001). * (P < 0.01).

Figure 4A shows the changes in the length of the structures under six different conditions. In the control, the structures had a very short length, measuring at most 0.2 μm. When treated with Glc, their length increased to approximately 1 μm, with most structures measuring between 0.2 and 1 μm, while a few extended up to 2 μm. MPA treatment led to a more significant increase in length, with some structures reaching up to 6 μm, although most ranged between 1 and 2.5 μm. Simultaneous treatment with Glc and MPA slightly decreased the length; most measured between 0.9 and 2 μm, indicating a negligible effect of Glc on shortening the length. Riba resulted in longer structures compared to the control but shorter than those treated with MPA, with most measuring between 1 and 2 μm. In simultaneous treatment with Glc and Riba, the structures became longer, but most of them were between 0.5 and 2 μm, while during simultaneous treatment with Glc and MPA, very few structures had a length less than 0.9. In Figure 4B, the thicknesses of the structures were examined. When treated with Glc, their thickness increased to 0.6 μm, making them thicker. MPA resulted in thin and elongated structures, with a thickness between 0.3 and 0.5 μm. When cells were treated simultaneously with Glc and MPA, the structures became thicker, ranging from 0.5 to 0.8 μm. Riba led to thicker structures compared to MPA, with thicknesses between 0.3 and 0.6 μm. Overall, Riba seemed to induce shorter but slightly thicker structures compared to MPA. In simultaneous treatment with Glc and Riba, there was an increase in the thickness of the structures; however, the thickness ranged from 0.3 to 0.8 μm, with some structures exhibiting smaller thicknesses. Overall, it is likely that when the enzyme is inhibited by MPA and Riba during insulin secretion, it forms thicker structures to counteract the inhibition and, at the same time, to provide the GTP necessary for insulin secretion, allowing it to partially maintain its activity. Additionally, due to the increased thickness of the structures while maintaining their length during the inhibition of this process, there is a possibility that enzyme expression might increases.

4.6. IMPDH Expression during Insulin Secretion and Its Inhibition

Western blot results (Fig. 5) demonstrated that IMPDH expression was elevated under all experimental conditions compared to the control. MPA significantly increased enzyme expression, whereas Riba induced a comparatively smaller increase in expression than MPA. Moreover, enzyme expression significantly increased when cells were treated with 25 mM Glc for 1 h, indicating that during insulin secretion, IMPDH expression increases in response to elevated GTP demand. Interestingly, enzyme expression in the presence of Riba was comparable to its expression in the presence of Glc, despite that glucose treatment lasted only 1 h compared to 24 h for the inhibitors. Additionally, when cells were treated simultaneously with glucose and the inhibitors, there was an increase in expression. This observation is consistent with immunofluorescence results (Fig. 3), which demonstrated an increase in the thickness of cytoophidia structures during simultaneous treatment with glucose and the inhibitors. Notably, enzyme expression was greater when cells were treated simultaneously with Glc and Riba compared to their separate treatments. However, this effect was not evident with MPA combined with Glc. This suggests that MPA may sufficiently increase enzyme expression, potentially exceeding the cell’s need, which could explain why no further increase in expression occurred upon the addition of Glc. In other words, this indicates that the cellular capacity for enzyme production might have reached to its limit.

4.7. Effect of GTP on the Disassembly of Cytoophidia Structures

To confirm previous findings that cytoophidia are formed during insulin secretion to supply GTP (Fig. S2), structural changes were evaluated by adding GTP to all treatment groups. When cells were treated with GTP, no rod- or dot-shaped cytoophidia were detected (Fig. S2A). In contrast, treatment with GTP in combination with MPA or Riba led to a significant decrease in the number of cytoophidia (Fig. S2: B, C). The remaining structures also showed a decrease in length and thickness (Fig. S2: I, J). Additionally, during insulin secretion, in the presence of GTP, as well as simultaneous treatment with glucose, MPA, and GTP, resulted in the complete disassembly of cytoophidia structures (Fig. S2: D, E). However, when cells were treated simultaneously with glucose, Riba, and GTP, some cytoophidia structures remained (Fig. S2F). Yet, their number was significantly lower compared to conditions without GTP (Fig. 3C), and these structures also exhibited decreased length and thickness (Fig. S2: K, L).

5. Discussion

Diabetes mellitus is characterized by hyperglycemia due to defects in insulin production/secretion, as well as impaired responsiveness of target cells to insulin ( 26 ). Studies have shown that GTP plays a crucial role in insulin secretion, either directly or via G-proteins ( 6 , 27 ). Initial evidence for GTP’s role in glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) was provided by inhibitors of GTP synthesis ( 5 , 28 ). IMPDH is an enzyme that catalyzes the first step in the de novo synthesis of GTP ( 10 ), and its activity can affect insulin secretion ( 9 ). Recent research has expanded upon this concept to include diabetic vasculopathy, in which IMPDH1 has been found to promote endothelial pyroptosis in high-glucose conditions. This suggests that IMPDH1 may play a larger role in the development of diabetes-related complications ( 29 ). Studies have demonstrated that inhibiting IMPDH significantly reduces insulin secretion ( 19 ), which is also supported by our findings (Fig. 1B). Additionally, when IMPDH is inhibited, it can form rod- and ring-shaped structures called cytoophidia, mainly in the cytoplasm ( 30 ). Previous studies have shown the inhibitory effect of MPA on GSIS and guanine nucleotide levels ( 19 ), but its impact on cytoophidia formation during insulin secretion remains unresolved. In this study, we investigated in more detail the changes in cytoophidia structures during insulin secretion and when IMPDH is inhibited in this process. Cytoophidia structures were first studied in mouse pancreatic islet cells by Chang et al. in 2015, but their morphological changes were not reported ( 9 ). In 2021, Zhou et al.’s study showed the formation of these structures near insulin granules and their close association with insulin granules; however, the effects of enzyme inhibition and changes in these structures were not reported ( 20 ). Our study examines the cytoophidia structures during insulin secretion and IMPDH inhibition (Fig. 2,3) and demonstrates the importance of this enzyme in this process by significantly increasing its expression (Fig. 5). We also observed that cytoophidia structures formed during insulin secretion inhibition were thicker than those of the control group (Fig. 3), suggesting increased enzyme activity. Many studies have investigated the changes in the length and thickness of these structures under different physiological conditions ( 31 , 32 ), except under the induction of insulin secretion, which was the main goal of our investigation. Our results showed that during insulin secretion, the cytoophidia structures change from scattered and dot-like to rod-shaped forms, and their thickness significantly increased following enzyme inhibition (Fig. 3,4). A novel mechanism has been proposed, suggesting that IMP-dependent interactions between ARL13B and IMPDH may play a role in the induction of cytoophidia under conditions of nucleotide imbalance ( 33 ). But why do the cytoophidia structures increase their thickness when insulin secretion is inhibited? Regarding the role of G-proteins on insulin secretion ( 34 - 36 ) and their activation dependence on GTP on one hand ( 37 , 38 ), and the cell’s demand for GTP during insulin secretion on the other hand, it is anticipated that during insulin secretion, the demand for GTP rises. This factor leads to the maturation of cytoophidia structures to thicker forms to enhance their activity and GTP production under the enzyme-inhibitory condition. A study by Gooding et al. in 2015 shows that when IMPDH is inhibited, insulin secretion returns to normal levels by the exogenous GTP addition, parallel to our results (data not shown); however, their study did not report cytoophidia structural changes in the presence of GTP ( 19 ). Our findings also indicate that cytoophidia structures disassemble in the presence of GTP (Fig. S2).

6. Conclusion

In summary, our findings indicate that combined exposure of the cells to Glc + MPA and/or Glc + Riba is associated with distinct cytoophidia structural maturation to thicker and longer and more active forms relative to untreated cells to provide adequate GTP to support insulin secretion. The presented data provide the primary ground for considering the cytoophidia structural maturation, by small molecules, as a new approach to induce endogenous secretion of insulin in the relevant diabetes condition.
