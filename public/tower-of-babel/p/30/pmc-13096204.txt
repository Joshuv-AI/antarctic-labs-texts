Distilling population specific expertise into a unified model for generalizable brain tumor segmentation
Authors: Ahmed Elzayat, Nourhan Hanafy, Mariem Magdy, Hazem Zakaria, Mina Tayeh, Abdulkhalek Al-Fakih, Yeong Hyeon Gu, Mohammed A. Al-masni, Meena M. Makary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multimodal brain tumor segmentation models often struggle to generalize across diverse populations due to variations in tumor pathology, patient demographics, and imaging protocols. A common approach to mitigate these challenges involves training separate models per population, employing ensemble methods, fine-tuning pretrained networks, or adopting curriculum learning strategies. While these approaches may yield improvements within specific domains, they often suffer from limited scalability, increased inference cost, poor adaptability to heterogeneous populations, and susceptibility to overfitting or catastrophic forgetting. To address these challenges, we propose a novel Multi-Teacher Single-Student Knowledge Distillation framework (MTSS-KDNet), built on the specialized knowledge of individual teacher models and distilling their collective expertise into a unified student model. Our framework performs population-aware knowledge transfer, guiding the student to integrate the strengths of multiple specialized teachers through both latent- and output-level supervision. This enables effective and independent generalization across all tumor types. In this paper, we focus on five distinct tumor populations: Adult Gliomas, Pediatric Gliomas, Sub-Saharan African Gliomas—which, although pathologically similar to their adult counterparts, often suffer from degraded MRI image quality—Intracranial Meningiomas and Brain Metastases. These tumor types exhibit unique developmental, morphological, anatomical and imaging characteristics, introducing heterogeneity that poses significant challenges to the ability of models to generalize accurately. Our approach achieves superior performance across all five populations, with average dice scores (DSC) of 0.87, 0.84, and 0.77 in the whole tumor (WT), tumor core (TC) and enhancing tumor (ET) regions, respectively, outperforming both population-specific and strong benchmark models. These results highlight the robustness and versatility of our method, offering a promising solution for enhancing generalizability in brain tumor segmentation while facilitating seamless clinical deployment.

Introduction

Deep learning-based methods have demonstrated promising performance in medical image segmentation, particularly when models are trained on large-scale, carefully curated, and well-annotated datasets1,2. However, the construction of such datasets remains a challenging task, especially for underrepresented patient populations or rare medical conditions, due to the scarcity of data, labor-intensive nature of annotation, and the need for specialized clinical expertise. Moreover, segmentation models trained on heterogeneous datasets often fail to generalize to real-world clinical environments, characterized by diverse imaging protocols, tumor pathologies, and patient demographics3. This lack of robustness hampers their reliability and deployment in practical settings, where cross-population segmentation models are crucial for ensuring consistent performance across heterogeneous cases. Inconsistent segmentation across patient groups can lead to misdiagnosis or delayed treatment, disproportionately affecting underrepresented populations and intensifying disparities in medical treatment. Recently, a new generation of foundation models, such as the Segment Anything (SAM)4, has been introduced, demonstrating strong general-purpose segmentation capabilities. However, these models are inherently prompt-based rather than fully automatic, requiring spatial inputs such as points or bounding boxes to guide segmentation. Moreover, in complex clinical scenarios such as brain metastases, where multiple lesions may appear in different brain regions, a single prompt may not capture all tumor sites, reducing SAM’s effectiveness. These limitations underscore the need for specialized models capable of handling multi-lesion, domain-specific challenges while accommodating both disease heterogeneity and imaging complexity.

In recent years, the Brain Tumor Segmentation (BraTS) challenges have played a pivotal role in establishing community benchmarks through expert-annotated datasets and standardized evaluation protocols for adult glioma segmentation5–7. However, as segmentation performance on these datasets approaches saturation, the research focus is shifting towards more diverse and clinically complex datasets—such as BraTS-PED8, BraTS-Africa9,10, Intracranial Meningioma11, and Brain Metastases12, which are further detailed in section “BraTS datasets”. These datasets introduce additional variability stemming from age groups, anatomical differences, and image quality disparities, thereby presenting new challenges for algorithmic generalization. This shift shows a broader realization within the community: segmentation algorithms intended for clinical deployment must demonstrate robustness across a wide range of tumor types, imaging protocols, and patient populations representative of real-world clinical diversity.

Although many segmentation methods perform well within individual datasets, achieving robust generalization across different domains remains a persistent challenge due to variations in dataset size, tumor subtypes, imaging characteristics, and annotation standards. Fine-tuning approaches13,14 have been explored to address this issue by enabling knowledge transfer from large datasets to smaller ones, typically through pre-training followed by task-specific adaptation (see Fig. 1a). However, such approaches often suffer from performance degradation on non-target datasets due to overfitting and limited cross-domain robustness. Additionally, the need to train and maintain separate models for each dataset significantly increases computational cost and training, particularly as the number of datasets grows.

Ensemble-based methods15–17 offer an alternative by combining their predictions from multiple models, each trained independently on a specific dataset, often using averaging or majority voting (see Fig. 1c). While ensembles may improve generalization to some extent, they inherit many of the same limitations, requiring additional training, adding computational overhead, and struggling to scale as the number of datasets grows. Curriculum learning methods18,19 show promise by gradually increasing task complexity during training (see Fig. 1b), but they require careful design of training schedules and extensive hyperparameter tuning for each dataset, adding further complexity and reducing their practicality in real-world applications.

To address these limitations, we propose a novel multi-teacher, single-student knowledge distillation framework, MTSS-KDNet, designed to achieve robust cross-tumor generalization in brain tumor segmentation. Unlike prior methods that train separate models or rely on computationally expensive ensembles for each tumor type, our approach distills complementary knowledge from multiple population-specific expert models into a single, unified student network (see Fig. 1d). This strategy eliminates the need for dataset-specific fine-tuning and reduces reliance on large annotated datasets for each population, thereby addressing both scalability and generalizability challenges. Moreover, to counter the inherent imbalance in tumor prevalence and dataset sizes, we introduce a fairness-aware sampling strategy that ensures equal representation from each tumor population during training. By integrating expertise from diverse tumor cohorts, the student model is capable of delivering strong performance across heterogeneous and previously unseen clinical scenarios. Consequently, our method offers a practical and effective solution for lesion-agnostic segmentation in real-world clinical environments.

Related work

A variety of deep learning techniques have been applied to brain tumor segmentation in recent years, with U-Net1,2 and its numerous extensions, such as 3D U-Net20, nnU-Net21, V-Net22, UNet++ 23, Attention U-Net24, and TransUNet25 being among the most commonly used. While these architectures have demonstrated reasonable performance on standardized datasets like BraTS, they are mainly designed and trained for adult glioma segmentation, often under specific imaging protocols. However, their ability to generalize across different tumor types and heterogeneous clinical scenarios remains limited. For instance, although nnU-Net automated preprocessing and architecture adaptation, it still relied on dataset-specific annotations and lacked mechanisms to explicitly address inter-tumor variability21. More recent transformer-based architectures, such as TransBTS26 and SwinUNet27, introduced self-attention mechanisms to model long-range spatial dependencies, but they were still trained and evaluated on narrowly scoped datasets, limiting their generalization.

Recent research has explored knowledge distillation28–32 as a means of transferring semantic knowledge from larger, expert models (teachers) to lightweight students, enabling enhanced performance under limited data. Domain generalization techniques33,34 have also been introduced to improve model robustness across unseen distributions. Furthermore, the emergence of foundation models such as SAM4, MedSAM35, and UNesT36 highlights a shift toward general-purpose segmentation frameworks. However, these models are inherently prompt-based rather than fully automatic, requiring spatial inputs, such as points or boxes to guide segmentation, which may limit their utility in automated clinical pipelines. In contrast, MoME (Mixture of Modality Experts)37 was recently introduced as a fully automatic foundation model for brain lesion segmentation that adapts to various MRI modalities through expert subnetworks. While MoME demonstrated noticeable performance across multiple lesion types and datasets, it mainly focused on handling different MRI sequences, but did not explicitly account for variations across different tumor populations. These approaches, though effective within constrained settings, still struggle to adapt across tumor types, highlighting a critical gap in models capable of handling cross-tumor heterogeneity.

Generalizability

While an increasing number of deep learning methods have investigated generalization across domains and tasks, most of these efforts have been concentrated outside the medical imaging domain, where large and homogeneous datasets are more readily available. In contrast, generalization in medical imaging, particularly for brain tumor segmentation, poses unique and significant challenges. A primary limitation is the scarcity of expertly annotated brain MRI datasets, which are often small in size, institution-specific, and contain incomplete imaging modalities. Furthermore, brain tumors exhibit extensive heterogeneity in their morphology, anatomical location, and visibility across imaging sequences, adding additional complexity to developing models that can robustly generalize across different patients. Consequently, methods that show strong generalization performance in natural image tasks frequently underperform in the medical domain, emphasizing the necessity for specialized solutions tailored to the inherent variability of clinical data.

To address this, several studies have proposed strategies aimed at improving generalization across tumor populations. For example, Fu et al. (2024) addressed domain shift in brain tumor segmentation by proposing DA-nnUNet38, which adapted adult glioma models to pediatric data using a domain classifier with a gradient reversal layer. This enabled learning domain-invariant features without pediatric annotations, improving tumor core segmentation. However, it focuses only on closely related tumor types and may fail under larger structural variability across different lesions.

Zeineldin and Mathis-Ullrich (2024) proposed HT-CNNs13, a hybrid transformer-CNN model fine-tuned via transfer learning to improve cross-tumor segmentation. It showed better generalization across tumor types using domain-specific pretraining. However, it still depended on high-quality annotations and overlooks key variability sources like imaging sequences and lesion characteristics.

Jiang et al. (2024) addressed limited generalization in brain tumor segmentation by proposing an ensemble model with adaptive post-processing15. Evaluated on multiple tumor types, including gliomas, meningiomas, and pediatric tumors it improved robustness by combining outputs from several specialized models. However, it required training multiple networks, increasing computational cost and still may not fully handle all lesion and domain variations.

Similarly, Heras Rivera et al. (2024) introduced a multi-architecture ensemble for brain tumor segmentation that integrates heterogeneous models, including convolutional, transformer-based, and MedNeXt-style networks, to generate complementary predictions39 Although this approach improves robustness across diverse imaging settings, it is computationally expensive and focuses solely on output fusion rather than on cross-tumor knowledge transfer or population-specific generalization.

Within this setting, Fadillah et al. (2024) proposed a MedNeXt-based CNN ensemble with adaptive post-processing to improve generalization across different tumor populations16. While effective within the challenge setting, their method was designed around a limited set of tumor types and MRI modalities, and therefore did not fully address broader variability in lesion appearance, anatomy, or imaging conditions.

Capellán-Martín et al. (2024) proposed a region-wise ensemble of nnU-Net and Swin UNETR with adaptive thresholding to handle tumor heterogeneity across multiple types17. Their method leveraged complementary model strengths to address BraTS 2023 challenges. However, it depended on multiple networks and complex post-processing, increasing computational cost and sensitivity to anatomical variability.

Bengio et al. (2009) introduced curriculum learning18 to improve convergence by training on progressively harder samples, later extended by Jiang et al. (2015) with self-paced mechanisms19. These methods help with training stability but risk forgetting earlier samples if not revisited. Moreover, they overlook domain-specific variability, limiting their effectiveness in cross-tumor segmentation tasks.

Knowledge distillation

Knowledge distillation (KD) has emerged as a powerful strategy for transferring knowledge from a high-capacity teacher model to a more compact student model, enabling competitive performance with reduced computational cost. In medical image segmentation, KD is particularly valuable for enhancing generalization in data-scarce domains, such as brain tumor analysis, where acquiring large-scale, expert-annotated datasets is labor-intensive and costly.

Recent studies have explored KD to improve segmentation robustness across domains and modalities. For example, Wang et al. (2023) proposed a cross-modal KD framework using attention-based feature transfer from a full-modality teacher to a partial-modality student32. Choi et al. (2023) similarly introduced a single-stage KD model for glioma segmentation with reduced inputs40. Both approaches improve robustness under missing modalities but remain limited to single tumor types and predefined teacher-student modality pairings.

In the context of efficient medical image segmentation, Qin et al. (2021) proposed Region Affinity Distillation (RAD)28, a KD framework that transferred region-level semantic relations from teacher to student via similarity maps. This enabled the student model to learn inter-region interactions and improved segmentation accuracy. While effective for boundary refinement, it primarily targets local consistency and is not designed for cross-domain or cross-tumor generalization. Rahimpour et al. (2022) proposed a cross-modal KD framework where a multi-sequence teacher guided a student using only a single MRI sequence29. This allowed the student to mimic multi-modal representations and improve segmentation robustness under missing modalities. However, the approach is limited to single-sequence inference and doesn’t address tumor-type variability.

More recently, Ahmad et al. (2024) introduced a multi-teacher cross-modal KD framework with cooperative deep supervision to improve unimodal segmentation30. It fused semantic guidance from multiple teacher modalities, enhancing generalization with limited inputs. However, the method is modality-focused and does not explicitly address tumor-type or population-level variability.

Despite these advancements, existing KD approaches face several limitations: they often rely on well-annotated, homogeneous datasets28–30,32,40; they typically address only a narrow set of tumor types or modalities32,40; and they remain susceptible to knowledge degradation or catastrophic forgetting when applied to diverse, evolving datasets18,19. In contrast, our proposed method directly addresses these shortcomings by employing a multi-population KD framework that utilizes a diverse range of tumor datasets alongside multi-modal imaging data. Unlike prior work, we distill complementary expertise from multiple teacher models—each specialized on a different tumor population—into a single student model. This approach promotes generalization by maintaining knowledge retention across training phases, mitigating forgetting, and accounting for inter-tumor variability in morphology, location, and imaging characteristics. Our framework is evaluated across five distinct tumor populations, demonstrating its robustness, scalability, and potential for clinical feasibility in real-world scenarios.

Materials and methods

BraTS datasets

The datasets used in this study are derived from the BraTS challenges, comprising five distinct brain tumor populations. The Adult Gliomas (GLI), Sub-Saharan African Gliomas (SSA), Pediatric Gliomas (PED), and Intracranial Meningiomas (MEN) datasets were obtained from the BraTS 2023 challenges5–11, while Brain Metastases (MET) was sourced from the BraTS 2024 challenge12. An overview of the datasets and their distribution across population groups is illustrated in Fig. 2. While metastatic tumors represent a larger cohort than pediatric or African gliomas, all three populations are relatively scarce compared to adult gliomas and meningiomas. This multi-faceted data imbalance is addressed through a bias mitigation strategy described in section “Population-aware sampling for bias mitigation”.

As highlighted in Fig. 2, the five tumor populations exhibit substantial heterogeneity that poses significant challenges for developing a single robust segmentation model. Adult gliomas are typically deep-seated and centered within the brain parenchyma, whereas Sub-Saharan African (SSA) gliomas, although anatomically similar, are acquired using lower-quality imaging protocols, introducing modality-specific noise and variability. Pediatric gliomas often differ structurally, with altered spatial arrangements of subregions—for instance, necrosis frequently appears peripherally, in contrast to the central localization observed in adult gliomas. Intracranial meningiomas tend to be superficial, originating from the meninges and frequently abutting the skull. In contrast, brain metastases demonstrate high heterogeneity in morphology, often appearing as multifocal lesions with variable spatial distribution. These biological and anatomical variabilities emphasize the challenge of achieving population-wide generalization with a single segmentation model.

All datasets consist of four MRI modalities per subject: T1-weighted (T1w), T2-weighted (T2w), Fluid-Attenuated Inversion Recovery (FLAIR), and contrast-enhanced T1-weighted (T1ce). These scans were acquired using various clinical protocols and MRI systems. Each volume was preprocessed to a uniform volume size of 240 × 240 × 155 voxels and resampled to an isotropic resolution of 1 × 1 × 1 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{mm}^{3}$$\end{document}. Additionally, all scans were initially skull-stripped and co-registered to a standard anatomical space to ensure spatial consistency across populations. Ground truth segmentation masks were manually annotated by experienced neuroradiologists, with labels corresponding to intra-tumoral subregions. Specifically, the masks include labels for background (BG), enhancing tumor (ET), peritumoral edema (ED) and necrotic core (NC). For evaluation, these subregions are aggregated into broader categories, with the Tumor Core (TC) formed by merging the ET and NC regions, and the Whole Tumor (WT) encompassing all three labeled regions—NC, ED, and ET.

Data preprocessing

A standardized preprocessing and augmentation pipeline was applied uniformly to all datasets to ensure consistency and support robust model training. Initially, all 3D MRI scans were cropped around the brain foreground with a fixed center, focusing computational resources on the relevant brain region. To reduce memory and training cost while preserving anatomical context, we resized the volumes from 240 × 240 × 155 to 128 × 128 × 128 voxels, ensuring full brain coverage and maintaining the original aspect ratio. Intensity normalization was performed independently for each modality by standardizing voxel intensities to zero mean and unit standard deviation. To enhance model generalization and mitigate overfitting, real-time data augmentation techniques were employed during training. Specifically, random axial flipping was applied along the depth axis (axial plane) with a probability of 0.5 to introduce spatial variability. Additionally, contrast adjustment was performed using gamma correction with values sampled uniformly between 0.65 and 1.5 and a probability of 0.15, modifying image intensity distributions to simulate real-world variations in acquisition conditions. It is important to note that no augmentation was applied during the validation or testing phases. Finally, we concatenated all four preprocessed modalities (T1w, T2w, FLAIR, and T1ce) along the channel axis and used them as input to both the teacher and student networks.

Proposed MTSS-KDNet

The proposed multi-teacher, single-student knowledge distillation (MTSS-KDNet) framework is designed to enable robust cross-population generalization for brain tumor segmentation. An overview of the framework is illustrated in Fig. 3a. At its core lies a unified student model trained to assimilate specialized knowledge distilled from multiple population-specific teacher models. Each teacher model is independently pretrained on a distinct tumor population, enabling it to capture and learn domain-specific representations and lesion characteristics tailored to its corresponding dataset.

During student training, we freeze the weights of all teacher models and guide the student using a combination of intermediate and output-level supervision. For each training sample, the input is annotated with its corresponding population type and simultaneously passed through both the student and the population-matched teacher model. This distillation process proceeds at two levels: (i) intermediate feature alignment, where the student model learns to replicate internal representations of the teacher, and (ii) output-level supervision, where the student aligns its predictions with those of the teacher. This dual-level guidance enables the student to internalize rich, population-specific representations while learning to generalize across diverse tumor types. Additionally, the student is supervised by its segmentation loss using ground truth annotations, further refining its prediction capabilities.

Teacher models

We construct five specialized teacher models, each trained independently on one of the following tumor populations: (1) Adult Glioma, (2) Sub-Saharan African Glioma, (3) Pediatric Glioma, (4) Intracranial Meningioma, and (5) Brain Metastases. By training on a single population, each teacher model learns to extract discriminative features and segmentation priors tailored to the unique anatomical and morphological characteristics of its cohort. These pre-trained teachers serve as domain experts, and their collective knowledge is selectively transferred into the student model, as shown in Fig. 3a.

Each teacher model was implemented using the MONAI Dynamic U-Net architecture with 3D convolutions, configured with four input channels and four output channels corresponding to the three tumor subregions and the background class. Training was conducted using a composite loss that combined Dice loss and binary cross-entropy, applied independently to the WT, TC, and ET regions. The models were optimized with the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10− 3, weight decay coefficient of 1 × 10− 5, ε of 10− 4, and the learning rate was adaptively adjusted through a Reduce Learning Rate on Plateau scheduler with a reduction factor of 0.1 and minimum learning rate of 1 × 10− 6. A batch size of 2 was used for both training and validation, and mixed-precision training (PyTorch AMP) was used to accelerate convergence and reduce memory consumption. Training was continued until convergence based on the stabilization of validation loss.

Population-aware sampling for bias mitigation

Inspired by the approach proposed by Puyol-Antón et al. (2021)41, we implement a stratified batch sampling technique to maintain population balance during student learning. This technique ensures that each training batch is composed of five distinct samples, with each sample representing a different population, as shown in Fig. 3b. By constructing each batch with these diverse samples, the model is exposed to the entire spectrum of populations in every training step, effectively mitigating bias and preventing any particular population from dominating the learning process.

This fairness-aware strategy promotes balanced representation and learning across all tumor types, as the model receives a consistent and equitable loss contribution from all groups during each epoch. Our experimental results demonstrate that this approach significantly improves the generalization ability of the student model, as evidenced by its performance across diverse populations during evaluation.

Student model

To enable effective knowledge transfer and generalization across populations, the student model is trained using a combination of three complementary learning objectives. First, latent-level distillation, which encourages alignment of deep feature representations between the student and the population-specific teacher model. Second, logits-level distillation, which aligns the student’s output predictions with those of the corresponding teachers. Third, supervised segmentation loss, which drives task-specific learning grounded in the true labels, as shown in Fig. 4. This combination of multi-level supervision promotes the integration of population-specific knowledge while maintaining task-specific accuracy. To further clarify the importance and contribution of each objective, we provide a detailed explanation of the individual components and their integration within the overall training framework, as detailed below.

Loss function

Deep supervised learning

To enhance learning and preserve gradient flow, we employed deep supervision by computing losses at multiple decoder levels42, which improves convergence, especially for small or ambiguous tumor regions. In this study, we explored the application of deep supervision in three distinct scenarios; in the first two, losses are computed across the final three decoder levels using ground-truth reference segmentations for both teacher and student networks. In the third scenario, which is applied during knowledge distillation, a voxel-wise binary cross-entropy loss is calculated between the student and teacher outputs—without reliance on ground-truth supervision—thereby focusing solely on aligning the student’s predictions with those of the teacher. Outputs from intermediate decoders are upsampled via nearest-neighbor interpolation to match the original resolution (128 × 128 × 128) of ground-truth labels. Latent space distillation loss

To facilitate an effective knowledge transfer from teacher to student, we perform distillation at the intermediate feature level, specifically at the deepest part of the network where high-level semantic information is most concentrated. Before distillation, both the teacher’s and student’s feature maps at this stage are passed through a Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM)43. CBAM enhances the representational power of features by sequentially applying channel and spatial attention, allowing the student to focus on the most informative regions emphasized by the teacher. Following attention refinement, a softmax activation is applied to the teacher’s features to convert them into a probability distribution, while a log-softmax is applied to the student’s features to maintain numerical stability during training. We apply a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence loss as a distillation objective, which measures the difference between the teacher’s feature distribution and that of the student. This encourages the student to mimic the semantic emphasis encoded by the teacher. Logits level distillation loss

To enable fine-grained supervision at the output stage, we perform KD on the prediction (logits) level. Both the student and teacher logits are first scaled by a temperature factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document}, a common technique that softens the output distributions44. This smoothing helps the student better capture the nuanced confidence levels of the teacher, especially in uncertain or overlapping tumor regions45. After temperature scaling, the teacher’s logits are passed through a sigmoid activation and binarized. This step ensures that only confident predictions are used to guide the student, which promotes more decisive and accurate voxel-level learning. The student’s probabilities are then compared to these binarized teacher outputs using the Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss, calculated per channel (WT, TC, ET). We multiply the BCE loss by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\tau ^2}$$\end{document}, following established distillation practices44,46. This compensates for the effect of temperature scaling on the gradient magnitudes, ensuring that the softened distributions still provide effective supervisory signals during backpropagation. We also extend this distillation to intermediate decoder outputs via deep supervision, as outlined in section “Deep supervised learning”. Supervised student loss

In addition to the distillation provided by the teacher models, the student is also trained using its own direct loss with ground truth annotations. This component of training is critical, as it provides an independent learning signal that ensures the student does not solely mimic the teacher but also optimizes its segmentation performance. This has been shown to improve generalization and guards against propagating teacher errors. The student’s segmentation loss combines the Dice loss \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( {{\mathcal{L}_{{\mathrm{Dice}}}}} \right)$$\end{document} and Binary Cross-Entropy loss \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( {{\mathcal{L}_{{\mathrm{BCE}}}}} \right)$$\end{document}, both applied with deep supervision. These losses are computed separately for each tumor subregion (WT, TC, ET), ensuring that the model effectively learns to segment all structures. The Dice loss measures the overlap between predicted and label segmentations, while the BCE loss provides fine-grained voxel-wise supervision by maximizing the likelihood that the predicted probabilities match the ground truth labels. Inference

Due to the high memory demands of processing full 3D MRI volumes, we employ a sliding window inference approach. The input volume is partitioned into overlapping sub-volumes of size 128 × 128 × 128 voxels, which are processed sequentially in mini-batches of size 4. An overlap ratio of 0.25 is applied between adjacent windows to ensure smooth blending across boundaries. The outputs from overlapping regions are combined using a Gaussian weighting scheme to reduce edge artifacts21. This method enables efficient inference while preserving spatial detail. Importantly, inference is performed exclusively using the student model, which directly processes input data from any of the five populations, with no need for access to any teacher model. This ensures generalization to unseen populations without added computational overhead. The resulting prediction has the original input volume dimensions and yields a segmentation mask with discrete labels, where 1 corresponds to necrosis, 2 to edema, 3 to enhancing tumor, and zeros elsewhere for non-tumor regions.

Experiments and setup

We conducted extensive experiments to evaluate the performance and generalization capabilities of our proposed framework across five heterogeneous brain tumor populations. This section outlines the key components of our experimental design, including the backbone network, comparative benchmark models, the staged inclusion of teacher population, and the ablation study.

Backbone architecture

Our framework utilizes Dynamic UNet (DynUNet)47 from the MONAI library as the backbone architecture for both teacher and student models. DynUNet is a modular and adaptive 3D segmentation network, inspired by nnU-Net48, designed specifically for volumetric medical imaging. It features a flexible encoder–decoder architecture with residual skip connections to preserve spatial detail and supports efficient multi-scale feature extraction across varying input resolutions. We adopt a seven-stage encoder-decoder structure, following the design presented in40, and integrate deep supervision strategy within the knowledge distillation framework (see section “Deep supervised learning”). To ensure fairness and consistency across all experiments, we utilized the same network architecture for all models. For each population dataset, we randomly split the data into 70% training, 10% validation, and 20% testing.

Implementation details

Our framework was implemented using Python 3.10.12 with PyTorch 2.5.1 and MONAI 1.4.0. All teacher models were trained individually on NVIDIA Tesla P100 GPUs, requiring approximately 12 h to complete 25 epochs for large tumor populations and 35 epochs for smaller ones. All models were trained until convergence based on validation performance.

The knowledge distillation stage was executed on dual NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPUs, allocating one GPU to the student network and the other to the teacher network. The student model was trained for 60 epochs, with an average runtime of 12 h per 3 epochs. Due to GPU memory constraints, we employed a batch size of 5 with gradient accumulation. The hyperparameters were set as follows: α = 1, β = 10, and ζ = 0.1 were chosen to balance the contributions of the loss components with respect to each other, whereas the temperature parameter τ = 5.0 was empirically determined through preliminary experiments to achieve optimal performance across our evaluation metrics. We employed the AdamW (Adam with decoupled weight decay) optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times ~{10^{ - 3}}$$\end{document}, and weight decay 1 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times ~{10^{ - 5}}$$\end{document}. We adopted the ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler with patience = 10 epochs, reduction factor = 0.1 and a minimum lr of 1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$~ \times ~{10^{ - 6}}$$\end{document}, and dynamically adjusting based on validation loss. The scheduler incorporated a 1-epoch cooldown period and a 0.001 minimum loss improvement threshold for stability during plateaus. All network weights were initialized using He initialization.

Evaluation metrics

Following the standard evaluation protocols from previous BraTS challenges, we employ the Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) and the 95th percentile Hausdorff Distance (HD95) as our primary performance metrics. DSC quantifies volumetric overlap between predicted and ground truth segmentations, while HD95 measures boundary accuracy, capturing spatial discrepancies between the predicted and actual lesion contours. Together, these metrics provide a comprehensive assessment of segmentation performance across both tumor structure and boundary alignment.

Pediatric glioma population

The pediatric glioma population presents unique challenges for segmentation due to its high anatomical variability and limited representation in the training data. Despite these challenges, our proposed method achieved the highest average DSC (0.819) among all compared methods, as reported in Table 1. This surpasses the Vanilla model (0.805), Fine-tuned model (0.755), and all curriculum-based and ensemble-based strategies. It also achieved the highest WT (0.914) and ET (0.656) DSCs, with the ET score marking a substantial gain over the next best method, the Vanilla (0.582). In terms of boundary delineation, our Student’s average HD95 (21.81) exceeded that of the CL (18.37) and RCL (18.94), but remained significantly lower than ensemble-based models, as well as the rest of the competing methods.

The outcomes show the Student model’s effectiveness in segmenting tumors in a population with complex and less predictable anatomy. Its remarkable improvement in ET segmentation, where it outperformed all models by a margin of at least 4.7%, illustrates the success of our method in capturing critical localized features that other methods, including the teacher (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T_{Pediatric}})$$\end{document} and ensemble models, failed to extract. While our Student model did not achieve the lowest HD95, its superior Dice performance, especially in the most clinically challenging region (ET), demonstrates a practical trade-off in favor of accurate volumetric segmentation. The ensemble models, although theoretically more robust through aggregation, suffered in both segmentation accuracy and boundary precision, particularly in the ET subregion.

Importantly, despite the performance of the MTSS-KDNet in adult and Sub-Saharan African glioma populations not being significantly higher than most of the competing models, training on these populations clearly contributed to the strong generalization seen in the pediatric setting, despite having the smallest dataset. The proposed MTSS-KDNet’s ability to obtain knowledge from these prior domains enabled it to better segment pediatric gliomas, highlighting the benefit of cross-population knowledge transfer in distillation-based frameworks. These performance gains are further supported by paired t-test analyses, which confirmed that the improvements are statistically significant (p < 0.05) across most tumor subregions and evaluation metrics, indicating that the observed advantages are unlikely to be due to chance.

Teachers progression evaluation

We investigated the effect of progressively incorporating teacher networks, each trained on a distinct tumor population, into the distillation framework. This staged inclusion analysis allowed us to isolate and quantify the impact of domain heterogeneity, providing insights into how structurally and anatomically diverse tumor populations influence cross-domain generalization.

Figure 6 illustrates the average DSC as population-specific teacher models are incrementally added. Initially, using only the teacher trained on the adult glioma (GLI) dataset yields high in-domain performance (0.882 on GLI) but poor generalization to other populations—particularly metastases (MET: 0.541), pediatric gliomas (PED: 0.661), and meningiomas (MEN: 0.678). Introducing the Sub-Saharan African glioma (SSA) teacher improves accuracy on SSA (from 0.845 to 0.887) and moderately benefits MEN and MET. However, performance on GLI slightly decreases (from 0.882 to 0.871), likely due to the lower-quality imaging in SSA data introducing noisy representations that interfere with GLI-specific features. Adding the third teacher, trained on PED, results in a substantial performance gain in PED segmentation (from 0.667 to 0.789) but introduces a decline in accuracy across all other populations. This trade-off may stem from the structural and anatomical differences of pediatric tumors, which introduce conflicting patterns in the latent space during distillation. The inclusion of the MEN teacher results in a marked improvement in MEN segmentation (from 0.703 to 0.848) and yields additional gains for GLI, SSA, and MET, although PED performance drops slightly (from 0.789 to 0.778), likely due to the growing domain variance. Finally, incorporating the MET teacher leads to consistent performance gains across all populations, especially for MET (from 0.604 to 0.682), PED (rising to 0.819), and MEN (to 0.860), while also modestly restoring GLI performance to 0.878.

The addition of population-specific teacher models offers valuable insights into how domain heterogeneity affects knowledge distillation in segmentation tasks. Initially, the strong performance of the GLI dataset highlights the advantages of training with high-quality, homogeneous data, but also reveals its limited generalizability to other populations. The introduction of SSA teacher improves cross-population performance, likely due to anatomical similarities between adult and African gliomas; however, the concurrent drop in GLI accuracy highlights the influence of data quality mismatches. The sharp gain is observed with the inclusion of the PED teacher, suggesting that dedicated supervision is essential for anatomically divergent populations. However, the associated performance degradation across other domains indicates that distillation from structurally distinct sources may introduce feature conflicts, complicating the learning of shared representations. The subsequent addition of the MEN teacher mitigates these conflicts, resulting in consistent improvements across adult-related populations. This may be attributed to the intermediate anatomical and imaging characteristics of meningiomas, which serve as a stabilizing bridge between diverse domains. Finally, the MET teacher consolidates the model’s generalization ability, demonstrating that inclusion of diverse yet complementary populations leads to more robust and balanced representations. Overall, these findings emphasize that the cumulative addition of diverse teachers leads to clear and consistent gains in multi-domain generalization.

Overall, these embeddings provide qualitative evidence that our model effectively captures cross-population differences, reinforcing its generalization capability across anatomically and clinically heterogeneous tumor types.

Ablation study

We conducted an in-depth ablation study to assess the contribution of individual components within our framework. Beginning with a conventional segmentation baseline, we sequentially introduced the KL bottleneck loss to enforce compact and meaningful latent representations, followed by a CBAM attention module placed prior to the KL branch, guiding the model’s focus toward spatially relevant features. We further examine the effect of adding a BCE-based logits loss to enhance supervision at the output level. The complete model combines all these components, demonstrating the incremental performance gains associated with each addition.

Visualizing the effect of CBAM module

A key component of our framework is the CBAM module, which is applied at the deep bottleneck layer. We must clarify that a direct heatmap of the bottleneck spatial attention map would not be meaningful for identifying fine-grained features like “tumor boundaries,” as the spatial resolution at this extreme depth is too low. Instead, the module’s function here is to perform a different, more high-level task: a coarse, region-of-interest (ROI) weighting. It learns which of the quadrants of the feature volume are most relevant (via spatial attention), and which of the high-level features (via channel attention) to amplify. To provide a meaningful visualization that demonstrates the consequence of this coarse weighting, we conducted a new ablation study. Specifically, we trained two identical student models: one with our proposed bottleneck CBAM distillation and one without it (using only the standard KL divergence on the raw bottleneck features). We then used Grad-CAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping)53 on representative on a high-resolution decoder layer for both trained students. This approach allows us to visualize what the student models themselves learned to identify as key features when making their final segmentation predictions.

The results of this ablation study are presented in Fig. 8, which visualizes arbitrary samples from each of the five distinct populations. Column 3 (With CBAM) and Column 4 (Without CBAM) demonstrate the clear impact of our module. As can be seen, the heatmaps from the CBAM-trained student are consistently more precise and tightly focused on the true tumor boundaries. In contrast, the model trained without CBAM often produces diffuse, unfocused heatmaps that incorrectly highlight healthy tissue. This provides the concrete evidence that our bottleneck CBAM module, while spatially coarse, is successfully enhancing feature representativeness and guiding the student model to learn the true key features more effectively.

Computational cost analysis

We conducted a computational analysis to evaluate the inference-time cost of our proposed method relative to the benchmark models and ensemble strategies. It is critical to note that all single-model approaches, including our Proposed (Student), Teacher, Vanilla, Fine-tuned, CL, and RCL, are built upon the identical DynUNet backbone. The differences between these methods lie in their training schemes, which do not alter the architecture’s computational profile at inference.

While our proposed method involves a more complex and costly training process compared to simpler baselines (e.g., “Vanilla” or “Fine-tuned”), this is a deliberate design trade-off. Those simpler methods, despite their faster training, do not sufficiently solve the core generalization problem. Our method is designed to achieve the strong generalization performance of a high-cost ensemble, but—as this analysis will show—it retains the computational efficiency of a single model at inference time.

As a result, the metrics for all single-model methods are identical: 50.8 M (50,836,108) parameters, 26.65 Teraflops (T-FLOPs), a peak GPU VRAM usage of 6.25 GB, and an average inference time of approximately 19 s per volume. In contrast, the ensemble strategies require the aggregation of five separate models to produce a final prediction. This inherently multiplies the computational load, resulting in a 5-fold increase to ~ 254.2 M parameters and ~ 133.26 T-FLOPs. This increased complexity also translates to significantly slower performance and higher memory consumption, with ensembles requiring an average of ~ 85 s for inference and a peak GPU VRAM usage of ~ 7.5 GB. This analysis confirms that our proposed method retains the computational efficiency of a standard single-model baseline, avoiding the significant overhead associated with high-performing ensemble methods.

Limitations

Despite the notable performance of the proposed framework, several limitations remain. First, although the fairness strategy was effective in mitigating dominance bias, the adult glioma population remains the most represented in the dataset. This imbalance may still influence the learned feature representations, potentially biasing the model toward better performance on dominant populations at the expense of underrepresented ones. Second, the method requires substantial computational resources. This stems not only from the inherent time complexity and cost of the multi-stage training process but also from the large scale of the multi-population datasets. This demand is further amplified by the fairness strategy, which involves repeating underrepresented datasets to match the dominant population, increasing the effective batch size and thereby raising memory and processing demands. Third, the framework assumes access to high-quality, well-generalized teacher models for each population. However, these teachers are trained independently and may suffer from dataset-specific overfitting, especially in low-resource cohorts like pediatric or Sub-Saharan African gliomas. Since the teacher weights are frozen during knowledge distillation, any biases or suboptimal behaviors present in the teachers are inherited by the student without correction.

Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a novel Multi-Teacher Single-Student Knowledge Distillation (MTSS-KDNet) framework for generalizable brain tumor segmentation across five heterogeneous tumor populations. By leveraging specialized teacher models and enabling population-aware knowledge transfer through both latent- and output-level supervision, our unified MTSS-KDNet effectively integrates cross-domain expertise without requiring separate training for each population. Comprehensive experiments demonstrate that our approach consistently outperforms state-of-the-art strategies, including curriculum learning, ensemble methods, fine-tuning, and naive vanilla, in both segmentation accuracy and boundary precision. These results highlight the robustness and adaptability of our framework in handling the complex variability found across tumor types and imaging settings. Our work addresses a critical gap in the literature by offering a scalable and generalizable solution that unifies population-specific expertise with a single deployment-ready model, paving the way for more reliable and clinically applicable brain tumor segmentation foundation models. In future work, we plan to extend the evaluation of the proposed framework to additional public 3D medical segmentation datasets to further assess its robustness and generalizability across diverse imaging domains. Although this was not feasible within the current scope due to dataset and computational constraints, we acknowledge the importance of such validation and highlight it as a valuable direction for future studies.
