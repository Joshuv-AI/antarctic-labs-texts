Characteristic pathological findings of achalasia behind unresectable esophageal cancer with pathological complete response after chemotherapy: a case report and literature review
Authors: Koichi Okamoto, Takashi Miyata, Tetsuya Asakawa, Koki Furuse, Kaori Maruyama, Shota Motoyama, Taigo Nagayama, Hisashi Nishiki, Akifumi Hashimoto, Yasuto Tomita, Hideto Fujita, Noriyuki Inaki, Shinichi Kinami, Itasu Ninomiya, Hiroyuki Takamura

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEsophageal achalasia is a rare disease characterized by impaired peristalsis throughout the esophagus, marked esophageal dilation, and nonorganic stenosis of the lower esophagus due to degeneration of the Auerbach’s plexus, and it is a known risk factor for esophageal cancer. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of achalasia‑associated advanced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma successfully treated using a multidisciplinary approach.Case presentationA 69-year-old female presented to our department with a 10-yr history of dysphagia and vomiting, accompanied by worsening hematemesis and passage disturbance. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy revealed a protruding lesion with stricture in the mid-to-lower thoracic esophagus, and biopsy confirmed squamous cell carcinoma. Further evaluation revealed unresectable advanced esophageal cancer with multiple lymph node metastases involving the dorsal descending aorta and left supraclavicular region. Following induction chemotherapy, marked shrinkage and disappearance of the primary tumor and metastatic lymph node were observed. However, nonorganic narrowing of the esophagogastric junction, along with esophageal tortuosity and dilation, persisted, leading to a diagnosis of esophageal achalasia. The patient subsequently underwent conversion thoracoscopic esophagectomy with the intent of achieving radical resection. Histopathological examination of the resected specimen demonstrated pathological complete response of both the primary tumor and lymph nodes, resulting in curative resection. Additionally, a reduction in the Auerbach’s plexus and intramural nerve fibers was observed, consistent with achalasia. One year after surgery, paraaortic lymph node recurrence was detected and treated with local radiotherapy. Despite subsequent chemotherapy for recurrence, the patient ultimately died of cancer 25 months after surgery. This case represents a rare instance of conversion surgery achieving pathological complete response following induction chemotherapy for unresectable advanced esophageal cancer associated with achalasia.ConclusionWe report this case with a comparison of histopathological findings to those of other esophageal cancer cases with pathological complete response encountered at our institution, along with a review of the relevant literature.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04940-0.Keywords: Case report, Chemotherapy, Conversion surgery, Esophageal achalasia, Esophageal cancer

Background

Esophageal achalasia (EA) is a rare motility disorder characterized by impaired peristalsis throughout the esophagus, marked dilation, and nonorganic stenosis of the lower esophagus due to congenital or acquired degeneration or loss of myenteric neurons, including the Auerbach’s plexus, which coordinate esophageal peristalsis and relaxation of the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) [1]. Clinically, EA presents with dysphagia, regurgitation of food and digestive fluids, respiratory symptoms such as cough, aspiration, and pneumonia, chest pain, and weight loss resulting from LES spasm or failure of relaxation.

EA is a significant risk factor for the development of esophageal cancer. Chronic inflammation of the esophageal mucosa caused by food residue retention and stagnation of digestive fluids is thought to contribute to carcinogenesis [2]. Because esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) arising in the setting of EA is extremely rare, its management is particularly challenging owing to its unique pathological features, diagnostic difficulty, and limited treatment precedents. To the best of our knowledge, no previous reports have described successful treatment of highly advanced ESCC associated with EA using a multidisciplinary strategy that includes induction chemotherapy followed by conversion surgery. Therefore, we present a rare case in which pathological complete response (pCR) was achieved after induction chemotherapy. In addition, we describe the characteristic histopathological findings of EA in the tumor region that disappeared following chemotherapy, comparing them with specimens from other ESCC cases with pCR treated at our institution, and provide a review of the relevant literature.

Case presentation

Present medical history

A 68-year-old Japanese female with a history of hypertension and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) presented to her physician with a chief complaint of swallowing discomfort. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) performed approximately 30 year earlier had revealed a lower esophageal stricture; however, no subsequent EGDs had been conducted. About 10 year before presentation, the patient noticed progressive difficulty in food passage and frequent vomiting, although a definitive diagnosis of EA had not been established. The patient later visited a local hospital with complaints of vomiting, hematemesis, and body weight loss (− 3 kg over 1 month). EGD revealed a tumorous lesion located 35 cm from the incisors, accompanied by marked circumferential stricture that prevented passage of a 9.2-mm-diameter fiberscope. She was diagnosed with advanced lower esophageal cancer and referred to our department for further evaluation and treatment. Her alcohol consumption was approximately 20 g/day (non-flusher), and she had a smoking history of 10 cigarettes per day for 45 year. Her regular medications included diltiazem and lansoprazole.

Subsequent EGD demonstrated a large type 1 tumor with a wide base, extending over approximately five-sixths of the esophageal circumference and occupying the lumen, centered on the left wall of the lower esophagus, 33–42 cm from the incisors (Fig. 1A). Although the lumen was markedly narrowed, a thin endoscope with a diameter of 5 mm could barely pass through the lesion (Fig. 1B, C). Biopsy specimens from the tumor revealed poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinoma (SCC).

The esophageal lumen proximal to the tumor was markedly dilated, with substantial food residue and digestive fluid retention. In addition, nonorganic narrowing of the esophagogastric junction (EGJ) was observed distal to the tumor, suggesting EA (Fig. 1D). Diffuse roughening of the mucosal surface, consistent with chronic inflammation or dysplasia, was noted in the nontumorous portions of the esophagus (Fig. 1E). The EGD examination was performed without anticholinergic agents, yet no esophageal peristalsis was observed.

Fluoroscopic examination revealed a large elevated tumor occupying the esophageal lumen as well as marked dilation of the proximal esophagus with wall irregularities and absence of peristalsis, despite the lack of anticholinergic medication (Fig. 2A). Contrast‑enhanced computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a tumorous lesion in the lower esophagus and enlarged lymph nodes behind the descending aorta (No. 112aoP; Fig. 2B). Enlargement of the left supraclavicular lymph nodes was also observed (Fig. 2C).

18F‑fluorodeoxyglucose (18F‑FDG) positron emission tomography showed faint abnormal 18F-FDG uptake in the lower esophageal tumor and in the enlarged lymph nodes behind the descending aorta (Fig. 2D, E). Abnormal uptake was also detected in the right cervical and left supraclavicular lymph nodes. No distant organ metastases, pleural effusion, or ascites were identified.

Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with unresectable advanced esophageal cancer (LtMt, 10 cm, 5/6 circ, SCC, cType 1, cT3 N3 M1[LYM, 102upR, 104 L, 112aoP, 16a2], cStage IVb), suspected to be associated with EA, and a multidisciplinary treatment strategy was selected. Induction chemotherapy using the DCF regimen (docetaxel 60 mg/m2 on Day 1; cisplatin 60 mg/m2 on Day 1; and fluorouracil 800 mg/m2 on Days 1–5, every 3 weeks) was initiated. After the first cycle, the patient developed grade 2 nausea and vomiting, grade 4 hyponatremia, and grade 2 neutropenia. Consequently, the cisplatin dose was reduced by 20% from the second cycle onward.

After three cycles of DCF, EGD revealed marked shrinkage and flattering of the elevated lesion, and biopsy specimens showed no histological evidence of viable cancer cells (Fig. 1F, G). Nonorganic narrowing of the EGJ persisted despite tumor regression (Fig. 1H). Esophageal fluoroscopy demonstrated tortuosity, marked dilation, and a bird’s beak appearance at the EGJ, leading to a diagnosis of EA (retention dilation type, sigmoid type, Grade II; d = 6.0 cm; Fig. 3A). DCF therapy achieved a near-complete response (CR) in the primary tumor and significant shrinkage of almost all metastatic LNs (Fig. 3B–E). The patient was therefore diagnosed with resectable esophageal cancer (CT-cT0, CT-cN1 [106recR] M0, CT-cStage II) with a remarkable treatment response.

Surgical procedure

To evaluate the histopathological response and achieve curative resection, minimally invasive esophagectomy (MIE) as conversion surgery was performed 3 months after initiation of treatment. The procedure included esophagectomy with comprehensive mediastinal, abdominal, and cervical LN dissection. However, dissection of distant lymph nodes beyond the standard range (112aoP) was omitted to minimize invasiveness and reduce postoperative complications. Thoracoscopic esophagectomy and mediastinal lymphadenectomy were performed. Reconstruction was achieved via the posterior mediastinal route using a gastric conduit with esophagogastric anastomosis. The total operative time was 561 min, and intraoperative blood loss was 240 g.

Histological findings of the resected specimens

Macroscopically, the resected esophageal specimen showed a scar-like lesion with indistinct borders and heterogeneous whitish changes extending from the middle to lower esophagus, without visible residual tumor (Fig. 4A, B). The esophagus was markedly dilated along its entire length. Histological examination revealed no viable cells in the tumor area (Fig. 5A, B), along with irregular epithelial thickening and keratinization, submucosal and adventitial fibrosis, and scattered disruption of the muscular layer, consistent with pCR following chemotherapy (Figs. 4C and 5C and D). No lymph node metastases were detected in the dissected mediastinal or intra-abdominal lymph nodes (Fig. 5E).

The final pathological diagnosis was pCR of esophageal cancer (MtLt, CT-pT0, CT-pN0 [0/74], M0, CT-pStage 0, Ef. Grade 3, D2, Cur B). Additionally, the intermuscular nerve plexus was markedly reduced throughout the resected esophagus, with very few identifiable ganglion cells (Fig. 6A, B). In particular, S-100–positive nerve structures in the upper and lower esophagus were sparse, with evidence of degeneration and loss of ganglion cells and nerve fibers, consistent with the EA (Figs. 6C and 7B–D).

Comparison of histological findings between achalasia and non-achalasia cases

Histopathological findings, including S-100 immunostaining, were compared between the present case and two other cases of advanced esophageal cancer with pCR following chemotherapy which were resected around the same period. In the non-achalasia cases, nerve plexuses, nerve fibers, and ganglion cells were preserved between the inner circular and outer longitudinal muscle layers within the tumor scar area, indicating that these neural structures are not necessarily affected by tumor-related scarring and fibrosis. In contrast, the present case exhibited a marked reduction in nerve plexuses, nerve fibers, and ganglion cells, consistent with Grade II EA characterized by degeneration and loss of neural elements (Fig. 8).

Postoperative clinical course

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 23. She was followed without adjuvant chemotherapy due to the patient’s frailty. One year after surgery, regrowth of lymph nodes behind the descending aorta (112aoP) was detected. Local treatment with intensity-modulated radiation therapy (69 Gy/23 factions/ 32 days) achieved a clinical CR. At the patient’s request, additional chemotherapy was not administered.

At 24 months after surgery, CT imaging revealed re-enlargement of the paraaortic lymph node metastases, carcinomatous pleurisy, multiple liver metastases, and metastasis to the right supraspinatus muscle with invasion of the right scapular. Despite receiving three additional cycles of DCF followed by a cycle of FOLFOX as salvage chemotherapy, disease progression could not be controlled, and the patient died of recurrent ESCC 29 months after initiation of treatment.

Discussion

Characteristics of EA

This case report describes a patient with highly advanced ESCC complicated by EA, who achieved pCR after induction chemotherapy and subsequently underwent curative conversion surgery.

In the present case, although the clinical course suggested EA, typical imaging findings were masked by the presence of a large esophageal tumor before chemotherapy. Following significant tumor regression, characteristic findings such as a bird’s beak appearance and diffuse esophageal dilation became evident; however, a definitive diagnosis using high-resolution manometry (HRM) remained difficult. Instead of esophageal manometry, the final diagnosis was based on esophagography performed after chemotherapy, clinical symptoms, and pathological findings, clinical symptoms, and pathological findings of loss of Auerbach’s plexus in the resected specimen, which were considered consistent with EA.

Histological examinations performed following Heller’s myotomy, surgical esophagectomy, or autopsy have revealed characteristic pathological findings in EA, including degeneration of the Auerbach’s plexus and interstitial cells of Cajal, as well as fibrous tissue replacement of the esophageal muscularis [3]. Gockel et al. [4] reported that the esophageal wall in patients with EA not only showed a marked reduction in intramural ganglion cells but also commonly exhibited severe fibrosis of the smooth muscle layer and myopathy-like changes in smooth muscle cells. Inflammatory cell infiltration, including B and T lymphocytes in the mucosa and muscularis, as well as eosinophilic infiltration into the lamina propria of the muscularis, suggested that the loss of intramural ganglion cells may be attributable to inflammation caused by autoimmune mechanisms, chronic degenerative changes in the central and/or peripheral vagus nerve, or severe myopathy of smooth muscle cells.

Kjellin et al. [5] evaluated histological findings from endoscopic biopsy specimens obtained from the lower esophagus of patients diagnosed with EA using HRM. They reported that chronic inflammation was almost universally present in the esophageal mucosa of patients with advanced EA who underwent esophagectomy, whereas the esophageal mucosa of patients with mild achalasia was nearly pathologically normal. Histopathological findings of the present case revealed highly distinctive findings compared to esophageal tissue of two other cases of advanced esophageal cancer with pCR. Nerve plexuses, nerve fibers, and ganglion cells were observed between the inner circular and outer longitudinal muscle layers within the tumor scar area in the case without EA. This finding suggests that these neural structures are not necessarily affected by scarring and fibrosis resulting from tumor regression following chemotherapy (Fig. 8). However, in addition to the regression of nerve tissue and ganglion cells characteristic of EA, there is a possibility that the patient developed chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy due to repeated DCF therapy. It cannot be ruled out that the characteristic pathological findings in this patient may have been reproduced through mechanisms such as mitochondrial deoxyribonucleic acid damage, increased reactive oxygen, and axonal degeneration due to microtubule damage caused by taxane-based drugs or platinum-based agents [6].

Eosinophilic esophagitis is a disease characterized by reversible esophagitis and esophageal motility disorders (EMDs), thought to be caused by products released from eosinophils in the esophageal mucosa and muscularis propria. Furthermore, abnormal accumulation of eosinophils and/or their degranulation products has been reported in the muscularis propria of many patients with EA. In muscularis propria-dominant eosinophilic esophagitis, toxic proteins released from degranulated eosinophils can destroy nerve cells, potentially leading to various reversible and irreversible EMDs including EA [7].

Pseudoachalasia is a clinical entity that often presents with symptoms like those of EA. Several reports have suggested that pseudoachalasia may result from chronic inflammation and fibrosis caused by esophageal radiation exposure or malignant tumors [8–11]. In the present case, radiation therapy was not administered, and pseudoachalasia was excluded based on the 30-yr persistence of symptoms and the presence of nonorganic stenosis at the EGJ immediately adjacent to the tumor area.

Esophageal achalasia as a risk factor for malignancies

EA is a significant risk factor for esophageal cancer, with chronic inflammation of the esophageal mucosa caused by food retention and stagnation of digestive fluids playing an important role in carcinogenesis [2, 12, 13]. In a meta-analysis, Tustumi et al. [14] reported that 2.8% of patients with EA developed esophageal cancer. Unlike typical epidemiological patterns of ESCC, the cumulative incidence continued to increase beyond 10 year, suggesting the involvement of distinct pathogenic mechanisms. Notably, no increased incidence of Barrett’s esophagus or esophageal adenocarcinoma was observed. Similarly, Sato et al. [15] reported that ESCC occurred in 0.88% of 2,714 Japanese patients with EA or other EMDs, with disease duration, advanced age, and habitual alcohol consumption identified as significant risk factors. In the present case, the patient had a long history of EA and heavy alcohol consumption, and ESCC developed in the lower esophagus, where inflammatory changes are presumed to be most severe. In esophageal cancers with a background of chronic inflammation due to EA, the specific symptoms of esophageal cancer are often difficult to notice, leading to often discovery as advanced disease. Therefore, tight surveillance and early therapeutic intervention at the time of EA diagnosis are desirable.

Changes in multidisciplinary treatment for advanced ESCC

Induction chemotherapy for unresectable esophageal cancer has undergone substantial changes over time. As several reports have demonstrated the usefulness of DCF in resectable esophageal cancer, this regimen has also begun to be applied to unresectable cases [16–18]. In the present case, the recurrent disease was resistant to multiple anticancer treatments, including DCF and FOLFOX, and subsequently progressed with multiple distant organ metastases, raising concerns regarding the indication for conversion surgery. In the present patient, adjuvant chemotherapy after resection was not selected due to the patient’s postoperative frailty and the fact of pCR. If the patient’s overall condition is acceptable, the addition of adjuvant chemotherapy and strict postoperative surveillance can be a useful treatment strategy.

Further advances in multidisciplinary treatment strategies incorporating immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are anticipated. As of January 2026, chemotherapy combined with ICIs has become the standard first-line treatment for patients with unresectable esophageal cancer, based on large-scale clinical trials including KEYNOTE-590, CheckMate 648, and RATIONALE-306 [19–21].

Conversion therapy

Increasing evidence suggests that, in some patients with initially unresectable disease, systemic therapy can result in marked tumor regression, enabling subsequent surgical resection [22, 23]. Matsuda et al. [22] evaluated the safety and efficacy of conversion therapy including conversion surgery in the JCOG1314 cohort. The 3-yr overall survival (OS) rates were reported to be 52.4% in the conversion therapy group and 14.3% in the non‑conversion group. Sugimura et al. [23] reported that MIE can be safely performed as conversion surgery with curative intent in patients with stage IV esophageal cancer who respond to induction chemotherapy. In this case, the treatment strategy was to proceed with conversion surgery, considering that distant metastases were controlled by induction chemotherapy, curative resection by the primary tumor resection and thorough lymph node dissection was possible, and the concerns about the future continuation of DCF therapy due to its serious adverse events.

Tanaka et al. [24] reported that in conversion surgery in esophageal cancer patients with abdominal paraaortic lymph node (PALN) metastases, the frequency of metastases in the harvested PALN was approximately half, and that the omission of PALN dissection may be acceptable. On the other hand, they emphasized that cases with PALN metastases have a worse prognosis than those without PALN metastases, and that PAKN dissection is important in cases where clinical CR has not been achieved. In the present case, both 112aoP and 16a2 lymph node metastases achieved clinical CR based on imaging, and omission of lymph node dissection in these regions was therefore considered acceptable. However, considering the possibility of regrowth if metastatic lymph nodes that have shrunk due to chemotherapy are not dissected, as in this case, additional treatments such as radiation therapy aimed at thorough local control can also be a useful option. Nevertheless, further prospective studies are required to confirm the long-term oncological benefits of this approach.

Application of MIE

Numerous trials have demonstrated the usefulness of MIE. In the JCOG1409 trial, a multicenter randomized controlled trial conducted in Japan, Takeuchi et al. [25] reported that MIE was noninferior to open surgery with respect to safety and 3-yr OS (82.0% vs. 70.9%) and was superior in terms of 3-yr recurrence-free survival (72.9% vs. 61.9%). These results provide strong evidence that MIE with curative intent may also be applicable in conversion surgery when distant metastases have disappeared, as in the present case.

In recent years, the introduction of robotic-assisted MIE (RAMIE) has further improved surgical outcomes of esophageal cancer surgery [26]. The RAMIE may facilitate curative resection without residual disease and suggested that the application of robotic-assisted systems in conversion surgery may expand in the future.

Limitation

There are some limitations in the present case report. Firstly, the definitive diagnosis of EA relies solely on fluoroscopic examination and postoperative histopathological findings, lacking the necessary preoperative HRM examination. Secondly, differentiating between EA and paraneoplastic pseudoachalasia before tumor reduction is difficult. Thirdly, as this is a single-case report, its generalizability is limited. The low certainty of the diagnosis is a limitation of this case report.

Conclusion

We reported a very rare case in which radical treatment was possible through multidisciplinary therapy for highly advanced esophageal cancer complicated with EA. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that pathological findings characteristic of EA were confirmed in the resected esophagus in which EA-associated esophageal cancer disappeared.
