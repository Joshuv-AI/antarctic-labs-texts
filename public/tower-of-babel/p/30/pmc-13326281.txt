Purulent Pericarditis Presenting With Abnormal Electrocardiogram Consistent With STEMI
Authors: Mehrdad Zarghami, Natasha Garg, Tetyana Okan, Hina Wazir, Fadi Hana, Tarek Abdulhadi, Mohammed Hussein, Aditya Mangla, Zoran Lasic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPurulent pericarditis is a rare, life-threatening condition accounting for <1% of pericardial disease.Case SummaryA 57-year-old woman with active intravenous drug use and poorly controlled type 2 diabetes presented with chest pain and inferolateral ST-segment elevations. Emergent coronary angiography revealed nonobstructive coronary artery disease. Subsequent echocardiogram revealed a large pericardial effusion with tamponade physiology. Pericardiocentesis drained 600 mL of purulent fluid positive for methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus.DiscussionThis case illustrates the diagnostic challenge of “pseudo-STEMI” in the setting of purulent pericarditis and underscores the importance of a rigorous diagnostic approach when angiography fails to identify a culprit lesion, necessitating a broader differential to determine the true underlying pathology.Key words: echocardiography, myocardial infarction, pericardial effusion, tamponade

History of Presentation

A 57-year-old woman presented with generalized malaise, weakness, and substernal chest pain. On physical examination, she was hypotensive (87/52 mm Hg), and afebrile. Examination revealed multiple needle track marks on the upper extremities. Cardiac auscultation was initially unremarkable, without murmurs or friction rubs.

Past Medical History

Significant history included poorly controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus (hemoglobin A1c: 11.5%), active intravenous heroin use, hypertension, liver cirrhosis with esophageal varices, and hyperlipidemia.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), acute myopericarditis, aortic dissection, septic shock with demand ischemia, and metabolic-induced electrocardiographic changes.

Investigations

Electrocardiogram demonstrated ST-segment elevation in leads II, III, and aVF, with reciprocal changes in I and aVL and ST-segment depression in V1 and V2, prompting emergent “code STEMI” activation (Figure 1). Given the high clinical suspicion of acute coronary occlusion based on electrocardiographic findings, the patient was emergently transferred to the cardiac catheterization laboratory prior to the availability of laboratory results.

Electrocardiogram

Twelve-lead electrocardiogram demonstrating sinus tachycardia with ST-segment elevation in leads II, III, and aVF with reciprocal changes in I and aVL, and ST-segment depression in V1 and V2 mimicking acute inferoposterior myocardial infarction.

Coronary angiography revealed minimal, nonobstructive coronary artery disease. While the patient was in the cardiac catheterization laboratory, laboratory results from the initial emergency department presentation became available, revealing normal troponin I, leukocytosis (18.6 × 109/L), severe hyperglycemia (794 mg/dL), and metabolic acidosis consistent with diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA).

In the absence of an obstructive culprit lesion on coronary angiography, and worsening hypotension, an emergent transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) was performed. This revealed a large, circumferential pericardial effusion with fibrinous strands and early tamponade physiology (Figure 2, Video 1), leading to the deferral of right heart catheterization in favor of immediate intervention.

Management

Managing this patient required a multifaceted strategy to address concurrent multiorgan failure. After the exclusion of obstructive coronary artery disease, aggressive antiplatelet therapy was withheld to mitigate bleeding risks associated with hepatic cirrhosis and known esophageal varices. Furthermore, to prevent worsening end-organ dysfunction—specifically acute kidney injury—management focused on judicious fluid resuscitation and the initiation of an insulin infusion for DKA.

Urgent pericardiocentesis yielded 600 mL of cloudy, sanguinopurulent fluid (Figure 3, Video 2). Fluid analysis revealed 354,000 white blood cells/μL (89% neutrophils), protein 6.0 g/dL, and lactate dehydrogenase 5,497 U/L. Blood cultures were positive for methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA). The patient was managed for septic shock and DKA. Initial empiric vancomycin and piperacillin-tazobactam were de-escalated to intravenous cefazolin. Hemodynamics stabilized rapidly after source control. A repeat TTE confirmed resolution of the effusion, visualized in the apical 4-chamber view (Figure 4, Video 3) and the parasternal short-axis view (Figure 5, Video 4).

Outcome and Follow-Up

Hemodynamics stabilized rapidly after source control and vasopressors were discontinued within 24 hours. The pericardial drain was removed after output ceased. A repeat TTE showed resolution of the effusion. The patient was discharged after completing a 10-day course of intravenous cefazolin.

Discussion

Purulent pericarditis remains a “can't miss” diagnosis, with mortality exceeding 40%.1,2 While classically associated with pneumonia or thoracic surgery, the contemporary profile has shifted toward patients with intravenous drug abuse and bacteremia.3

The presentation of purulent pericarditis as a STEMI mimic is a rare but high-acuity diagnostic challenge. As noted in the literature, the inflammatory process of the epicardium can result in ST-segment elevation that precisely mirrors an acute coronary occlusion.4,5 While classic pericarditis typically presents with diffuse, concave ST-segment elevation and PR-segment depression, regional or purulent variations can manifest as focal ST-segment changes with reciprocal depressions, leading clinicians toward an initial diagnosis of coronary artery disease. In the present case, the localized inflammation of the visceral pericardium likely caused an “epicardial current of injury,” manifesting as inferior ST-segment elevations. This clinical mimicry is supported by other documented cases where STEMI was the initial diagnosis and can be associated with pericardial effusion such as pericarditis, esophageal perforation, malignancy which can form “pseudo-STEMI” patterns via mechanical epicardial compression or reduced subendocardial perfusion.5,6 The metabolic stress of DKA and concurrent septic shock further complicated the clinical picture, potentially masking the classic “stage 1” diffuse ST-segment elevation typically seen in viral pericarditis.7

This case aligns with current European Society of Cardiology guidelines, emphasizing that in cases of suspected pericarditis with hemodynamic instability, urgent echocardiography and drainage are Class I recommendations.8,9

Clinical Perspectives

This case demonstrates the diagnostic challenge posed by purulent pericarditis, which can closely mimic an acute STEMI. While traditionally associated with pneumonia or thoracic surgery, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion in contemporary high-risk populations, such as patients with intravenous drug abuse and bacteremia. Early recognition via bedside imaging is critical for initiating lifesaving source control. Furthermore, it avoids the significant hemorrhagic risks associated with unnecessary fibrinolytic or antiplatelet therapy—a vital consideration when infection mimics a STEMI and emergent coronary angiography is unavailable.

Conclusions

MSSA-associated purulent pericarditis represents a critical diagnostic pitfall that can closely simulate acute myocardial infarction. Clinicians must maintain a high index of clinical suspicion for this pathology in patients with bacteremic risk factors who present with ST-segment elevation in the absence of obstructive coronary disease.
