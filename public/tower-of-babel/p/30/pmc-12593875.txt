Chromosome-scale assembly of European flax (Linum usitatissimum L.) genotypes and pangenomic analysis provide genomic tools to improve breeding
Authors: Boris B Demenou, Adama Ndar, Christophe P Pineau, Damien D Hinsinger, William Marande, Delphine Hourcade, Patricia Faivre-Rampant
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDecoding the genome of cultivated species is one of the key starting points for supporting marker-assisted selection to accelerate breeding programmes. To date, five to six flax genotypes, including fiber flax, linseed and related wild flax, mainly from Canada, China and Russia, have been sequenced and assembled. But no genome assembly was available for Western European flax cultivar, although France is the world's leading producer of fiber flax (85% of world production). The Canadian oilseed flax cultivars CDC Bethune have been mainly used as reference for studies and breeding activities. The best assembly to date was the Chinese fiber Yiya5 assembly, which had the best metric of all the flax genome assemblies available. Recent analyses have shown that up to 30% of fiber flax reads from different origins do not map to the oilseed genome. Thus, much genetic information could be neglected using the oilseed genome as a reference. In this study, we sequenced, assembled and annotated the genomes of four European flax cultivars (two fiber Bolchoï and Idéo; two oilseed Marquise and Attila) and present the first flax pangenome. ONT Minion long reads sequence data were -assembled using Flye, -polished, validated and organised into hybrid scaffolds using optical map data (Bionano Genomics), and finally anchored into fifteen T2T pseudomolecules for each genotype using the fiber flax Yiya5 assembly. The final genome size reached 437, 441, 442 and 453 Mb for Idéo, Marquise, Attila and Bolchoï, respectively. Approximately 47,000 protein-coding genes were annotated for each genotype. For the first time, a pangenome graph were constructed for ten flax cultivars, including our four assemblies and six publicly available assemblies (CDC Bethune, Longya10 and Line 3896, Heiya-14, Yiya5 and Atlant). The total pangenome graph size was 835.77 Mbp with the core pangenome size of 172.2 Mbp. A total of 74,123 non-redundant orthologous proteins representing panproteome were identified. The pangenome results should be interpreted with caution given the quality of the publicly available genomes. This study with the resources generated, is a step forward for the development of genomic and genetic tools, useful for the improvement of Western European flax characterization and breeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12248-8.Keywords: Flax, Fiber, Oilseed, Genome assembly, Annotation, Pangenome, Idéo, Bolchoï, Attila, Marquise

Background

The cultivated flax (Linum usitatissimum L.) is an industrial crop grown for fiber and seeds since at least 8000 to 10,000 years ago [1] or even 30,000 years [2]. Domestication of flax by humans occurred in the Near-Middle East and then propagated to the Nile Valley, Europe, and finally to the rest of the world [1]. The cultivated Flax is divided into two major morphotypes with different morphologic, anatomic, physiologic, and agronomic traits: linseed flax and fiber flax. Linseed is grown mainly for the oil contained in its seeds and fiber flax for the fiber contained in its stems. Linseed is smaller, more branched with branches starting from the middle of the stem and produce many large seeds [3]; while fiber flax is taller, less branched with branches only in the upper part of the stem and produces longer fiber and comparatively fewer seeds [4].

The history of flax domestication from linseed to fiber flax or fiber flax to linseed, from the wild flax species pale flax (L. bienne Mill.) is less controversial, although multiple domestication processes are suggested to give rise to the cultivated flax. Most of the studies support that oil-use preceded the fiber-use [4] and therefore that flax was domesticated first for its seeds and later for its fibers [5–8]. Multiple independent domestication events [9, 10] suggested oil-fiber dual-use and were supported by archaeological records [11, 12].

The oil and other products from linseed is mainly used in industries like food, pharmaceutical and construction (paints and bio-based composite materials) [13, 14]. Flax seeds are rich in omega − 3 fatty acids and lignans essential for human and animal feeds [15, 16]. Today, although flax fiber accounts for less than 0.4% of the world's textile fibers [17], it is mainly used to produce high-quality clothings, bed linen, and household linen. More recently, other markets and uses have appeared and fiber (short and long) are also used for the automotive industries and for insulation in the construction industries [17], and for biofuel industries and pulp [18]. Over the last decade, long fiber production has grown spectacularly, driven by end demand, investment, collective promotion and the development of these new uses. Linen is also benefiting from growing demand for natural fibers and the perception of its low environmental impact by the end consumer [17]. The dynamics of demands and uses are forcing the global flax industry to redefine its production ambitions. For fiber flax, the ambition is to account for 1% of the textile fibers produced in the world in the next few years by increasing production area and flax productivity.

As other crops, flax faces problems of productivity, disease resistance, tolerance to abiotic stress and adaptation to climate change. Plant breeding is one way of increasing the productivity of this crop and achieving the objective of increasing production. Genetic resources such as reference genomes, gene annotation and genotyping tools have become essential [19] for genetic research and marker-assisted selection (MAS) of new cultivars, as they enable the decoding of the crop genome and the identification of genetic markers for breeding.

The first published flax genome—linseed cultivar CDC Bethune – sequenced with Illumina technology in 2012 using paired-end and mate-pair libraries; resulted in a contig-level reference genome assembly size of 302 Mb with scaffold N50 of about 700 kb. This reference genome was later assembled at the chromosome-level by [20] with addition of BioNano genome optical, BAC-based physical, and genetic mapping [20]. In 2020, using Illumina sequencing platform, genetic mapping and Hi-C technology, [4] produced three new scaffold-level assemblies of linseed cultivar Longya-10 (genome size of 306 Mb), fiber cultivar Heiya-14 (genome size of 303.7 Mb), and pale flax (genome size of 293.5 Mb). The Longya-10 assembly was then scaffolded into 15 pseudomolecules of 300.2 Mb. The development and widespread use of long-read sequencing (Oxford Nanopore and PacBio HiFi) has opened a new chapter in flax genome assembly. Two new assemblies of fiber flax cultivars were published in 2021. The genome of Russian fiber flax cultivar Atlant was sequenced and assembled at the contig scale, using both Oxford Nanopore and Illumina sequence data, with a genome size of 361.7 Mb [21]. Then, the genome of Chinese fiber flax cultivar Yiya5 was sequenced and assembled at the pseudomolecule scale with the combination of the PacBio HiFi and Hi-C data. This latter showed the largest genome size of all available assemblies reaching 454.95 Mb with the best assembly metrics [19]. The most recently published assembly was the Illumina sequence reads assembled genome of the Russian genotype Line3896 [22] however it did not reach the chromosome level.

The publication of the assembled genome from 2012 allowed further progress in molecular studies of fiber and oil flax [23–27] and most of them used the CDC Bethune assembly as a reference. However, the true size of the flax genome remains controversial. A comparative genomic study of oilseed and fiber flax genomes has shown that almost 30% of the reads of a Canadian fiber flax cultivar, Stormont Cirus, are missing from the CDC Bethune oilseed reference sequence [28]. This result supports many studies that have shown that different morphotypes of the same species could have large variations in their genomes [29, 30]. The assembly size of CDC Bethune accounts for 81% coverage of the flax genome estimated to 370 Mb [31], with a size one third smaller than the size of Yiya5. Thus, many data could have been neglected by using CDC Bethune as a reference for fiber and oil flax studies; or by using the foreign fiber flax genome assembly as a reference.

Despite the existence of multiple assemblies available to date, most of them are for non-European cultivars (Canada, China, Russia and others), while the leading fiber flax production area remains Europe (France, Belgium and the Netherlands for fiber flax), with more than 80% of world production of fiber flax (FAOSTAT 2022). It is therefore important to have genetic tools adapted to the characterisation of west European genetic resources to improve varietal selection. Deciphering the genome of European flax varieties could allow developing tools more closely adapted to the European genetic diversity; but also, could pave the way for more broader genetic studies such as the pangenomic approach. To meet the needs of breeding programmes, it is essential to focus on the most representative and high performing flax varieties. At present, 83% of the fiber flax market comes from France and the Benelux countries with 586 110 t of production (FAOSTAT, 2023) [17]. The dominant genetics in this region at the beginning of the study are represented by Idéo and Bolchoï, which are highly productive in terms of fiber yield and disease resistance [32]. These varieties now influence all European breeding programmes (French breeder Linéa, personal communication). In addition, oilseed varieties provide valuable genetic diversity. Marquise is known for its high seed yield, while Attila covers 90% of the winter oilseed flax area in France due to its excellent cold resistance and high yield. The genetics of these varieties will shape future breeding programmes as breeder look for traits to address climate change and broaden the genetic pool [33].

For many years, researchers have proposed the concept of “pangenome”, which allows the identification of the core and dispensable genomes for a species or for a specific genetic pool [34–36] and theoretically captures all the genetic variations within this pool. The pangenome represents a catalogue of sequences or genes within a given species, as it incorporates the genetic information from all the genomes sampled, resulting in a large and diverse set of genetic material [36]. The core genome is the part of the genome that is shared by most of the individuals included in the construction of the pangenome, and the dispensable genome is the part that is shared by only a few individuals. Pangenome can solve the shortcoming of previous studies based on a single reference genome that missed abundant genomic variation and failed to comprehensively detect most of the diversity [37]. For many cultivated species such as wheat, barley, rice, cotton, chickpea, tomato, soybeans and others [37–42], the construction and use of pangenomes have improved the understanding of genetic variation, species diversity, and key genes associated with agronomic traits [37]. To date, no pangenomic study is available for flax, as pangenomic construction requires a high-quality assembled genome at the chromosome level and for many accessions that are representative of the genetic diversity of the species.

The main aim of this study was to sequence, assemble and annotate the genome of four European flax genotypes (two fiber Bolchoï and Idéo; two oilseed Marquise and Attila). We then established the first flax pangenome based on the Idéo reference using the four high-quality assemblies from this study and the six publicly available genome assemblies of CDC Bethune, Longya10 and Line3896 (oilseed) and Heiya-14, Yiya5 and Atlant (fiber). These resources could be useful for genetic diversity studies, as well as for the development of genomic tools such as a genotyping tool for genotype characterization and breeding.

Methods

Plant material

Four flax (Linum usitatissimum) varieties Bolchoï, Idéo, Marquise and Attila from the French official catalog [43] were selected. In 2021, these varieties represented the highest yielding under European growing conditions. Each variety was propagated from the offspring of a single base seed plant to minimize contamination risks.

Linum usitatissimum var. Bolchoï (Bolchoï) is a spring-type fiber flax variety developed by Terre de Lin (France). It stands out as the first high-yielding variety with resistance to powdery mildew (Oidium lini), a major issue in northern France and was registered in 2014.

Linum usitatissimum var. Idéo (Idéo) is also a spring-type fiber flax variety, resistant to powdery mildew, added to the official catalog in 2019 by the GIE LINEA (France). This resistance was provided by its original ancestry including the Raja variety. Traces of this variety can be found in Canadian documents from the 1950 s (Bibliothèque et Archives nationales du Québec; [44]).

Linum usitatissimum var. Marquise (Marquise) is a spring-type oilseed flax, registered in 2012 by the GIE LINEA (France). With its high yield, Marquise dominated the market during the second half of the 2010s. Its lineage includes the Linda variety, which contributed to the short and highly branched morphology observed in all the currently cultivated varieties.

Linum usitatissimum var. Attila (Attila) is a winter-type oilseed flax, also registered by GIE LINEA (France) in 2020. This recent variety combines high yield with excellent cold resistance, making it a dominant player in the winter linseed market since its introduction. Attila’s genetic background merges elements from Linda and Oliver—the latter being the world’s first winter oilseed linseed variety, registered by INRA and SCA Lin 2000 in 1995.

Long read sequencing with Oxford Nanopores Technologies

High quality genomic DNA of four flax varieties (Bolchoï, Idéo, Attila and Marquise) were isolated from flash-frozen leaves with the Genomic-tip 500/G kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Deutchland) at INRAE CNRGV platform (Toulouse, France). DNA samples were purified with 1/1 ratio AMPure XP Beads (Beckman Coulter, Pasadena, Ca, USA), then quantified using a Qubit 3.0 Flurorometer (Invitrogen), with the Quant-iT dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Mass., USA). Integrity of DNA was assessed using the Femto Pulse system (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Prior to libraries constructions, shorts fragments (< 30 kb) were eliminated with the Circulomics SRE-XL kit (Pacifique Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA, USA).

For each variety, two libraries were built from 2 µg of purified genomic DNA following the SQK-LSK110 protocol (Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT), Oxford, UK), with modifications. DNA repair and end-prep incubation were performed for 20 min at 20 °C, followed by 20 min at 65 °C; each AMPure XP beads purification incubation and elution time were extended to 10 min at 21 °C and 30 min at 37 °C, respectively, with mixing every 5 min with wide bore pipette. Adapter ligation incubation was extended to 40 min at 25°C. For each sample, the two libraries were pooled after adapters ligation and before purification. Libraries were then loaded on a MinION (ONT) R9.4.1 flowcell, with at least one reload after a Nuclease Flush using the Flow Cell Wash Kit EXPWSH004 (ONT) according to the standard protocol.

Short read sequencing

Genomic DNA of the four flax accessions were isolated from young leaves following the Mag-Bind Protocol (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA). Genomic DNA was quantified using the Quant-iT dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Mass., USA) with the Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen). Short reads libraries were prepared from 800-1000ng of genomic DNA following the Kapa Hyper prep PCR free protocol with Illumina TruSeq DNA UD Indexes (Santa Clara, CA, USA). Libraries were then sequenced at EPGV (INRAE, Evry, France) on two lanes of an Illumina NovaSeq6000 – S4 flow cell, with PE 2 × 150 cycles.

Ultra high molecular weight (uHMW) DNA extraction and scaffolding with optical maps

uHMW DNA were purified from 0.5g of fresh etiolated young leaves according to the Bionano Prep Plant Tissue DNA Isolation Base Protocol (30,068—Bionano Genomics) with the following specifications and modifications. Briefly, the leaves were fixed in fixing buffer containing formaldehyde. After 3 washes, leaves were cut in 2 mm pieces and disrupted with rotor stator in homogenization buffer containing spermine, spermidine and beta-mercaptoethanol. Nuclei were washed, purified using a density gradient and then embedded in agarose plugs. After overnight proteinase K digestion (Qiagen) in the presence of Lysis Buffer and one hour treatment with RNase A (Qiagen), plugs were washed and solubilized with 2 µL of 0.5 U/µL AGARase enzyme (ThermoFisher Scientific). A dialysis step was performed in TE Buffer (ThermoFisher Scientific) to purify DNA from any residues. The DNA samples were quantified by using the Qubit dsDNA BR Assay (Invitrogen). The presence of mega base size DNA was visualized by pulsed field gel electrophoresis (PFGE).

Labelling and staining of the uHMW DNA were performed according to the Direct Label and Stain (DLS) protocol (30,206—Bionano Genomics). Briefly, labelling was performed by incubating 750 ng genomic DNA with 1 × DLE-1 Enzyme for 2 h in the presence of 1 × DL-Green and 1 × DLE-1 Buffer. Following proteinase K digestion and DL-Green clean-up, the DNA backbone was stained by mixing the labelled DNA with DNA Stain solution in the presence of 1 × Flow Buffer and 1 × DTT, and incubated overnight at room temperature. The DLS DNA concentration was measured with the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay kit (ref Q33231, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Labelled and stained DNA was loaded on 1 Saphyr chip and was run on the BNG Saphyr System according to the Saphyr System User Guide. Digitalized labelled DNA molecules were assembled to optical maps using the BNG Access software (solve version 3.5).

First genome assembly and polishing

We performed the initial assembly for Idéo and the three other genotypes with four different long-read assemblers with default parameters except for genome size (set at 400 Mbp): Flye [45], Necat [46], Smartdenovo [47] and Redbean [48]. For each assembler, two assemblies using (i) all ONT sequences data and (ii) a subset of 35x ONT sequence reads larger than 5 kb obtained with Filtlong [49] with parameters –min_length 5000 –keep_percent 90 were performed.

Assembly metrics were calculated using QUAST [50] with default parameters, and the best assembly for each genotype was selected based on the assembly size, the number of contigs, the contig max length and the N50. The assembly obtained with Flye using the longest reads was selected for all varieties.

Because nanopore reads contain systematic error in homopolymeric regions [51], we polished the selected assemblies with two rounds of Racon v1.4.3 [52] with -m 8 -x −6 -g −8 parameters and Medaka v1.6.1 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) with ONT reads; followed by a third round of polishing using polca [53] or HapoG [54] (using default parameters) with high quality Illumina short reads generated for each of the four genotypes.

Hybrids scaffolding and chromosome scale building

Hybrid scaffolding was performed between the sequence assembly and the optical genome maps with the hybridScaffold pipeline (Bionano Genomics, solve version 3.6 [55, 56]) and hybridScaffold.pl script with -B 2 -N 2 parameters, to align the contigs and the maps.

The locations where the nanopore contigs were overlapping identified as artefactual duplications were removed with the Bionano Scaffolding Correction tools BiSCoT v2.3.3 [57] with default parameters. Because Hybrids scaffolding generated sometimes gaps between neighboring NGS sequence contigs on hybrid scaffolds, we used TGS Gap-Closer software [58] with default parameters to fill N-gaps in the genome assembly and to reduce the total number of Ns in the hybrid scaffolds. To do this, we selected from the same ONT sequence data obtained with Filtlong above, the sequence larger than 10 kb with a quality score Q > 20 using Chopper v0.2.0 [59] with -q 20 -l 10,000 parameters. Finally, the hybrids scaffold for each genotype was then polished with Hapo-G [54] using the same high quality Illumina short reads as above; followed by a run of Purge Haplotigs [60] with parameters -l 15 -m 115 -h 190 for Idéo; -l 8 -m 57 -h 190 for Bolchoï; -l 19 -m 103 -h 190 for Attila and -l 18 -m 104 -h 190 for Marquise; to detect and remove some potential allelic contig.

The hybrid scaffolds were then ordered, oriented, and patched into chromosomes for each genotype using RagTag [61] with default parameters. First, ragtag.py scaffold was run using as reference the only published chromosome scale flax fiber genome, Yiya5 [19]. We then ran ragtag.py patch for each genotype using the assemblies of the three other genotypes and the assemblies of CDC Bethune and Longya 10.

Assembly completeness and quality evaluation

Assembly completeness was assessed using Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs, BUSCO v5.3.2 [62] in genome mode with the eudicots_odb10 gene set of 2326 genes and was compared to existing assemblies (CDC Bethune, Longya10 and Yiya5 ant Atlant). The quality of the assemblies was then assessed for completeness, heterozygosity and collapse of repetitive regions using the k-mer distribution toolkit KAT [63] with kmer size of 27 and Merqury [64].

Transposable elements and gene annotation

We annotated the four assemblies for transposable elements (TE) and for genes. EDTA [65] was used to generate a high-quality non-redundant transposable element library for centromere localisation and whole-genome TE annotations with parameters –overwrite 1 –sensitive 1 –anno 1 –evaluate 1. A library of TEs was also generated de novo from the Idéo assembly using RepeatModeler [66] (v2.0.1, parameters: BuildDatabase -name mydb;RepeatModeler -database mydb -pa 12) and used to detect TEs on the four new assemblies using RepeatMasker v. [67] (v4.1.2-p1, parameters: -lib mylib -xsmall -pa 12). Masked genome sequence was used for structural annotation.

Gene structural annotation was performed using the Eukaryote EuGene pipeline v2.1.0, the Nextflow/Singularity-based version of the EuGene tool [68]. The EuGene worflow combines ab initio prediction of coding sequences, with protein and transcript evidence. Eugene provides a set of protein databases ready to use. These databases are built upon publicly available databases (Uniprot/Swiss-Prot and Uniprot/TrEMBL) cleaned from identified repeated elements (match with Dfam consensus sequences < https://dfam.org > and Transposon-PSI transposon protein database < http://transposonpsi.sourceforge.net/ >) [68]. We used the Plant Domains protein database. For transcript datasets, we retrieved Illumina RNA-Seq data [69] for Atlant (SRA N° SRR11830028 to SRR11830032), CDC-Bethune (SRA N° SRR3314329 to SRR3314333), Heiya14 (SRA N° SRR8281912, SRR8281913, SRR8281916 and SRR8281917), Longya10 (SRA N° SRR8281918 to SRR8281921) and Yiya5 (SRA N° SRR14341308 to SRR14341312) from the SRA database and performed data cleaning and transcriptome assembly using Trinity v2.14.0 [70]. The transcriptome assembly was first cleaned for duplicated sequences using cd-hit [71, 72] and then for rDNA and polyX using the EuGene pipeline.

For functional annotation, we performed a blast search of protein-coding gene sequences against several public nucleotide and protein sequence database resources. We also used eggNOG-mapper v2 [73] for searching the eggNOG v5.0 [74], GO (Gene Ontology Consortium, 2021) and KEGG [75] databases; DIAMOND v2.0.9.147 [76] for searching the Swiss-Prot [77] and NR databases, and the InterProScan v5.50 [78] program for searching the Pfam v33.1 [79] database; all with default parameters.

Telomere detection and centromere localization

Telomeres were directly detected by searching the telomeric repeats (TTAGGG/CCCTAA) within 50 kb of each terminal chromosome sequence for all assemblies, using the tidk explore tool (v0.2.1) [80] and the python script FindTelomeres.py (available at https://github.com/JanaSperschneider/FindTelomeres/blob/master/FindTelomeres.py). The location of the centromere was detected for all genotypes using Centrominer from the Quartet toolkit [81] with default parameters, adding the TE annotation file from EDTA to increase detection accuracy.

Genome comparison and synteny analysis

Genome comparison was done between pairs of assembled genomes from this study, as well as between the Idéo genome assembly and CDC Bethune [20], Atlant [21], Longya 10 [4] and Yiya5 [19] assemblies. We analysed genome synteny and structural rearrangements between genomes using SyRI v 1.6.3 [82], and used plotsr v1.1.1 for the visualization of variations and genomic collinearity (with parameters –genomes –chrname -S 0.7 -W 15 -H 4 -f 8 -o out.png). We also used the D-genies (v1.5.0) online tool for dotplots generation [83]. We finally performed genome comparison between Yiya5 assembly and the four genome assemblies by: (i) mapping high quality trimming Illumina reads to Yiya5 assembly; (ii) mapping the whole genome against the genome of Yiya5 and finally (iii) by computing the metric (count and length) of different variation types (sequence and structural variations) between Yiya5 (reference) and the four assembled genomes.

Pangenomic analysis

We constructed a pangenome for flax using ten accessions, including the four assembled genomes in this study (Idéo, Bolchoï, Marquise and Attila) and six accessions with publicly available genome assemblies (CDC Bethune, Longya10 and Line 3896 (oilseed), and Heiya-14, Yiya5 and Atlant (fiber)). We used two approaches based on the protein coding genes and the whole genome assembly.

For protein coding genes approach, we used Proteinortho v6.3.0 [84, 85] to perform reciprocal best alignment [86] between the ten accessions protein-coding genes to identify the set of orthologous genes. Genome annotation unavailable for the six publicly available genomes was quickly performed using Helixer v0.3.1 [87] with the parameters –lineage land_plant –species linum_usitatissimum; as the aim is not to perform new annotation. Proteins with an identity score greater than 50% and sequence coverage greater than 70% (e-value less than 10–5) were considered as significant hits. Core genome and pangenome size curves were generated using proteinortho_curves [84] with 100 iterations.

For the whole genome approach, we applied the Minigraph-Cactus pipeline [88] to construct the flax pangenome variation graph based on Idéo assembly, with parameters “–gbz -giraffe full clip filter –gfa full clip filter –vcf –chrom-vg full clip filter –reference Idéo. We generated three graphs: the “full graph” that includes all sequences, whether aligned or unaligned; the “clip graph” that excludes unaligned sequences longer than 10 kb and the “filter graph” that removes nodes with support from less than 2 haplotypes. We used Panacus (v0.2.3) and the count-vg-hap-cov function of hal2vg v1.1.5 [88] to calculate pangenome size, pangenome growth, core genome size, and the non-reference sequence component supported by minimum haplotypes. We computed statistics for structural variants (SVs) from one of the resulting VCF file representing a pangenome graph as sites of variation along a reference Idéo; which has had nested variants and sequences greater than 100 kb removed using vcfbub.

Results

Genome sequencing

For short reads sequencing, an average of 39x coverage per sample was obtained. Data volume generated for each accession is shown in the Supplementary Table S1. A total of 22.7 Gb (N50 = 49.65 kb), 46.1 Gb (N50 = 43 kb), 38.1 GB (N50 = 56 kb) and 35.3 Gb (N50 = 50 kb) of Nanopore sequences were recovered for the varieties Bolchoï, Idéo, Attila and Marquise, respectively (Supplementary Table S1).

Optical maps assembly

A total of 197 Gb (Attila), 100 Gb (Idéo), 103 Gb (Marquise) and 196 Gb (Bolchoï) of molecules with a size larger than 150 kb, the threshold for map assembly, were generated representing 535x, 284x, 301x and 520x of genome coverage, respectively (Supplementary Table S1).

The assembly produced between 112 and 137 optical maps with an N50 ranging from 8,45 to 9,5 Mb for a total genome map length ranging from 345 to 377 Mbp (Supplementary Table S1) according to the considered cultivar.

Genome assembly and chromosome level construction

The pipeline used for the genome assembly is shown in detail in Supplementary Figure S1. We started assembly with Idéo and Bolchoï. We tested four of the most commonly used software packages at the time of our work (Supplementary Table S2). We obtained the best assembly metrics with Flye. Then, we only tested the Flye assembly for the Marquise and Attila genotypes, resulting in the selection of ONT sequences longer than 5 kb for both genotypes without restriction on the size of the Filtlong output (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S2). We obtained 478, 561, 389 and 366 contigs, reaching a genome size of 450.31 Mb, 439.45 Mb, 435.81 Mb and 438.18 Mb for Bolchoï, Idéo, Marquise and Attila, respectively (Supplementary Table S2). The Flye tool assembly was then polished using filtered ONT sequences for the first two rounds, followed by a third round using high-quality Illumina sequences to correct systematic errors in the homopolymeric regions of the nanopore sequences. The third round of polishing with polca and HapoG gave a comparable output and we chose the polca output for further analysis. This resulted in a final number of 431, 516, 322 and 303 contigs with a cumulative genome size of 449.71 Mb, 439.18 Mb, 435 Mb and 437.36 Mb for Bolchoï, Idéo, Marquise and Attila, respectively (Table 1).

Hybrid scaffolding of polished ONT contigs using 137, 112, 124 and 127 assembled Bionano optical maps (Supplementary Table S1) resulted in 67, 59, 54 and 59 hybrid scaffolds, respectively, giving a total length of 381.8 Mb, 378.2 Mb, 383.9 and 397.7 Mb for Bolchoï, Idéo, Marquise and Attila, respectively (Supplementary Table S2 and S3). The N50 length was improved for Idéo (from 8.96 to 10.51 Mb), slightly for Bolchoï (from 10.16 to 10.73 Mb) and Attila (from 11.04 to 11.22 Mb); but not for Marquise (10.38 Mb) (Supplementary Table S3). The contigs not scaffolded (unplaced ONT contigs) represented from 40 to 68 Mb depending on the genotype (Supplementary Table S3). The percentage of N in the hybrid scaffolds was low (< 0.54%) for all genotypes and was reduced to < 0.50% after gap bridging with TGS Gap-Closer. At this stage, hybrid scaffolding resulted in four hybrid scaffolds for each of the 15 chromosomes, with almost all chromosome arms reconstructed; a level of assembly that justified anchoring.

The hybrid scaffolds and the unscaffolded contigs were finally anchored in 15 pseudomolecules for each genotype, guided by the Yiya5 genome reference [19] using Ragtag, followed by a run of TGS Gap-Closer. We chose the Yiya5 genome as a reference because this pseudomolecule scale assembly showed the largest genome size of all available assemblies and the best assembly metrics. The final genome assembly was composed of 15 pseudomolecules with 229, 329, 192 and 168 additional sequences (concatenated in a “unknown” chromosome); reaching the final genome size of 453 Mb, 437 Mb, 441 and 442 Mb for Bolchoï, Idéo, Marquise and Attila, respectively. The percentage of N in the final assembly was < 0.78% for the four genotypes.

Detection of telomere and centromere locations in chromosomes

We detected out of 30 telomeres, 26 telomeres for all cultivars except for Marquise, for which 23 telomeres were detected (Fig. 1, Table 1 and Supplementary Table S4). This resulted in 11 intact chromosomes for Bolchoï, Idéo and Attila and 9 intact chromosomes for Marquise, at a T2T level among the 15 chromosomes (Table 1). At least one telomere was detected for each chromosome, except for Marquise where no telomere was detected on chromosome 4 (Lus04_marquise). One telomere was missing on four chromosomes for Bolchoï (Lus05_bolchoï, Lus03_bolchoï, Lus04_bolchoï and Lus15_bolchoï); Idéo (Lus07_idéo, Lus05_idéo, Lus09_idéo and Lus12_idéo) and Attila (Lus12_attila, Lus04_attila, Lus09_attila and Lus15_attila). For Marquise, in addition to the chromosome Lus04_marquise without a telomere, one telomere was missing for chromosomes Lus11_marquise, Lus12_marquise, Lus03_marquise, Lus09_marquise and Lus15_marquise (Supplementary Table S4).

Genomic comparison with existing assembling: synteny analysis

Synteny analysis was performed to uncover the extent of synteny and the presence and distribution of chromosomal rearrangements between the Idéo assembly used as a reference and the three other assemblies of this study (Fig. 4). We found a globally concordant macro-synteny, with several chromosomal rearrangements (inversion, translocation, duplication) within genomes and between assemblies. Genome translocations were found mainly in chromosome 5 between Idéo and Bolchoï, Marquise and Attila; in chromosome 9 between Idéo and Attila, Attila and Bolchoï, and between Attila and Marquise. Genome duplications of variable size were observed, e.g. between the beginning of chromosome 12 in Idéo and the end of chromosome 9 in Bolchoï. Genome inversions of variable size were also found between many chromosomes, for example on chromosome 6 between Idéo and Bolchoï and on chromosome 15 between Bolchoï and Attila.

Between Idéo and four other assemblies (CDC Bethune, Atlant, Longya10 and Yiya5); dotplot analysis generally showed good synteny, except for the Atlant assembly, for which only a few part of this genome aligned with the Idéo sequences (Supplementary Figure S2). For Longya10 and CDC Bethune, chromosome breaks (gaps) were observed in the centromeric regions of almost all chromosomes (Supplementary Figure S2). A large inversion was also observed in the entire arm of chromosome 7 between Idéo and Longya10, while a shorter inversion was observed in the centromeric region of chromosome 1. Chromosome 15 of Idéo had translocations compared to the Longya10 assembly, while translocations were observed on chromosome 7 of Idéo compared to chromosome 13 of CDC Bethune (Supplementary Figure S2).

Genomic comparison between Yiya5 and the four assembled genomes of this study exhibited some sequence and structural variations (Supplementary Figure S3). Indeed, only 3% of Illumina reads of the four genomes do not map to the Yiya5 assembly. From 567 K (Idéo) to 835 K (Marquise) SNP were identified (Supplementary Table S9) while from 142 (Idéo) to 232 (Attila) syntenic regions representing up to 83% genome length of each of the four genomes (362.6 Mbp for Idéo, 377.5 Mbp for Bolchoï, 364.6 Mbp for Attila and 363.6 Mbp for Marquise) were identified (Supplementary Table S9). For Idéo assembly, 21 structural inversions representing a total length of 2.9 Mbp; 181 structural translocations representing a total length of 5.4 Mbp and 485 duplications representing a total length of 2 Mbp were identified (see Supplementary Table S9 for more details for Idéo and the three other génome assemblies).

Panproteome analysis provides a catalogue of flax genes

We constructed a protein based pangenome by clustering the protein sets encoded in ten L. usitatissimum genomes, including the four genomes from this study and six other genomes publicly available, to approximate the complete repertoire of flax genes. The genome annotation performed for the six publicly available assemblies resulted in between 47,971 and 49,728 protein-coding genes (Supplementary Table S10). We used clustering parameters of > 50% identity and > 70% sequence coverage (e-value less than 10–5) to obtain the most significant homology clusters. The gene accumulation curves in Fig. 5 showed that the number of the core genes decreased continually with the addition of new genotypes, as expected when sampling more diverging genomes of a species. Inversely, the number of pangenome repertoire genes increased when the number of genotypes increased suggesting the open state of the pangenome of flax.

A total of 74,123 non-redundant orthologous proteins representing the pangenome were obtained; of which 21,003 (28%) representing the core genome were conserved in all 10 genomes (Fig. 5). The accessory-shell genome, a set of protein found in at least two genomes, accounted for 38,190 (52%) non-redundant predicted proteins while the singleton genome for 14,930 (20%) non-redundant predicted proteins. The number of singleton proteins ranged from 671 for CDC Bethune (oilseed flax) to 3594 for Atlant (fiber flax) (Fig. 5). Among the four European cultivars analysed in this study, the oilseed cultivars Attila (1198) and Marquise (1310) exhibited more singleton proteins than the fiber flax cultivars (681 singleton proteins for Bolchoï and 829 for Idéo). But among all cultivars included in the pangenome, the fiber flax Atlant and Heiya 14 contained the highest singleton proteins (with 3594 and 3135 singleton proteins, respectively); while the oilseed cultivar CDC Bethune and fiber cultivar Bolchoï showed the lowest number of singletons (with 671 and 681 singleton proteins, respectively).

Pangenome graph construction and structural variants analysis

For the whole genome approach, the Idéo assembly was used as a reference for the pangenome graph construction. The total size of the graph pangenome was 835.77 Mbp, which is approximately twice the size of a single flax genome. The core pangenome size was about 172.2 Mbp, representing 20.6% of the total pangenome size (Fig. 6). The dispensable genome shared by at least two cultivars was about 434.1 Mbp (52%) and the singleton genome ranged from 14.7 Mbp to 74.7 Mbp (27.4%). A total of 483,467 INDELS were identified from the graph pangenome VCF files, including 251,071 insertions and 232,396 deletions (Fig. 6). The length of insertions ranged from 50 to 91,919 bp (160,337 bp), with 100–500 bp insertions having the highest frequency (7%), using a minimum size cut-off of 50 bp. Discussion

Decoding the genome of cultivated species is one of the key starting points for supporting marker-assisted selection to accelerate breeding programmes. To date, five to six flax genotypes, including fiber flax, linseed and related wild flax (pale flax), mainly from Canada, China and Russia, have been sequenced and assembled. These authors used different sequencing technologies and data; Illumina then BAC-based physical map, simple sequence repeat (SSR), marker-based consensus genetic map and BioNano genome optical maps for CDC Bethune [20] and more recently PacBio HiFi and Hi-C for Yiya5 assembly [19]. The Canadian oilseed CDC Bethune genotype assembly is the most widely used reference for flax genetic studies and breeding, even in Western Europe. Western Europe, in particular France, Belgium and the Netherlands, is the main fiber producing regions (80% of world production; FAOSTAT 2022). However, no flax genomic tools (genome assembly, annotation, and genotyping tool) for the western European gene pool are currently available to characterise genetic resources available for breeding. Decoding the genome of western European flax genotypes could make it possible to develop genotyping tools that are closer to that particular germplasm but also to assess the main world gene pools for pangenomic studies.

In this study, we successfully assembled and annotated high-quality genomes for four French and Western European fiber and linseed flax cultivars. We then performed comparative genomic analysis and, for the first time, pangenomic analyses for ten cultivars, including the four assembled genomes in this study with six publicly available genome assemblies namely CDC Bethune, Longya 10 and Line 3896 (oilseed) and Heiya 14, Yiya5 and Atlant (fiber).

High quality assembled and annotated genomes and genomes comparison

High quality data (sequences and Bionano maps) generated was assembled to hybrid scaffold level with missing data accounting for less than 0.50% of the total genome length. These high-quality assemblies after hybrid scaffolding justified the next step of anchoring using the best available reference genome, without incorporating the reference genome errors. We chose the Yiya5 assembly as it appeared to be the most complete among the available assemblies and showed the best metrics, especially for repetitive regions [19]. The last assembly stage allowed reaching the chromosome level by using Yiya5 fiber flax assembly [19] as a reference. The assembly quality parameters of the four assemblies were comparable showing the high-quality assembly for those genotypes.

These high-quality assemblies were supported by many parameters such as the contig N50 value, the telomeric region search, the results of the Busco assessments, but also by many other analyses such as the k-mer distribution analysis. Indeed, the comparison of the contig N50 values showed a value of up to 11.04 Mbp for our four assemblies, a value higher than that obtained for other assemblies; 9.61 Mbp for Yiya5 [19] and less than 0.5 Mbp for the others [4, 20, 21, 31] (Fig. 2B). Between 76 and 86% of telomeres (out of a total of 30) were identified for the genotypes, with up to eleven T2T chromosomes among the fifteen flax chromosomes. This is the first time a T2T analysis and chromosomes were reported for flax assemblies, at the time of our work. A Genome Busco value of about 95% was obtained for the four cultivars, a value higher than 93.9% reported for CDC Bethune but similar to that obtained for Yiya5 (95.0%). The Busco analysis highlighted the high level of genome duplication of the flax genome in this study (up to 73%), supporting the value reported for the available genomes (Fig. 2A). This result is consistent with the very recent whole genome duplication (WGD) event of the flax genome reported by [20], as well as the TEs content [19]. The TEs content of our assemblies was about 55% of all genomes. This value was similar to that reported by [19] for Yiya5, but higher than the 23% and 38% obtained for CDC Bethune [20, 31] and Longya 10 [4]. This difference could be explained by the quality of these assemblies (CDC Bethune and Longya 10), as the sequencing technologies or assembly tools used by these authors did not fully capture all the repeated regions.

Subsequent analysis using the KAT tool (Fig. 3) revealed a unimodal graph indicating that most of the Illumina reads occurred once in the assembly, supporting the homozygous state of the flax genome. The black graph indicated that very few sequences were missing from the assembly, as almost all the Illumina Kmer were found in the ONT-based assembly, supporting a good recovery of kmers for this species. This result was supported by the Merqury QV score (> 30.37 for all assemblies), which achieved an accuracy of more than 99.9% for assembled bases. This average quality score of 32 across the four assemblies was similar to that obtained by [51] for bread wheat and is consistent with QV already reported for plant genomes sequenced by ONT [51, 89]. However, for [51] the per-base quality of ONT sequencing is still an issue, despite recent improvements in ONT technology.

Approximately 47k protein coding genes were obtained for each of the four cultivars; and the quality of genome annotation was evaluated with the Busco protein analysis; reaching to a Busco value of approximately 96%, one point higher than the Busco value of genome. This result supports the good quality of the genome annotation performed. A similar number of genes (49k) was obtained for Yiya5 [19]. For CDC Bethune and Longya 10, about 43k genes were annotated, while for Atlant almost twice as many (78k) were annotated. Indeed, the number of protein-coding genes in this study was comparable to that of the above genotypes, except for the Atlant genotype which had 77,522 gene models. This latter result could be related to a problem with the annotation parameter used for Atlant. The comparison of our four assemblies with the available CDC Bethune [20, 31], Longya10 [4], Yiya5 [19] and Atlant [21] genomes (Table 1) also allowed us to assess the quality of our assemblies. Indeed, for the cumulative genome size, we obtained from 437 to 452 Mbp, a size comparable to that of Yiya5 (455 Mbp; [19]); but up to 150 Mbp higher than that obtained for CDC Bethune [20, 31], Longya10 [4] and Atlant [21] (Fig. 7). This difference in size could be due to genotype, as many studies have shown that different morphotypes of the same species can have large variations in their genomes [30, 90]. However, it could also be due to the quality of the assemblies published by these authors, especially in the repetitive regions (especially for CDC Bethune and Longya10), as shown for example by the dotplot between Idéo and these assemblies (Supplementary Fig. S2). Indeed, we observed misalignment mainly in centromeric regions, with those assemblies lacking many sequences from the centromeric regions.

Comparison of cumulative genome size (in Mbp) of the four assemblies of this study with four others publicly assemblies

According to the missing data, our four assemblies had lower values (< 0.78) compared to CDC Bethune (15%) and Longya 10 (2%), but slightly higher than the 0.002% of Yiya5. Repetitive elements of about 55% of the flax genome were annotated in this study. This value is similar to that obtained for Yiya5 [19], but higher than that of the other flax genomes (less than 37%; [27]). This result for the other flax genomes is probably due to the size and/or quality of these assemblies, as mentioned above, and seems to support the assembly problems in the repetitive region, resulting in a smaller assembly size. It supports the fact that long-read technologies can detect a higher percentage of repeats, allowing better resolution and identification of structural variants than short-reads [91]. This level of flax TEs is lower than that observed in other crops; for example in Cotton (61%; [92]), maize and wheat (80%; [93, 94]).

The synteny analysis supports a good synteny between the four fiber and oilseed genomes with many structural rearrangements such as inversions, translocations, and duplications. The main rearrangements were a genome translocation, found on the chromosome 9 between Attila and other genomes; and on the chromosome 5 between Ideo and the three other genomes. Sequence divergence was also observed in the centromeric regions of many chromosomes between almost all pairs of genomes (Fig. 4B). No major syntenic patterns separating fiber and oilseed were observed, suggesting no major rearrangements between the cultigroup, considering the four elite genotypes. However, we observed a deletion of almost 10 Mbp on chromosome 9 of the Idéo genotype, which was not present in the second fiber genotype, Bolchoï, nor in the oilseed genotypes. This result needs to be confirmed by chromosome counting and sizing for Idéo. Comparing the Yiya5 genome to the four genomes of this study, we found that despite only 3% of WGS Illumina reads of the four genotypes do not map to the Yiya5 assembly, many structural variations were obtained when comparing assemblies' sequences. Indeed, up to 17% of the genome assembly sequences for the four genomes, including inversions (up to 0.67%), translocations (up to 2.13%), duplications (up to 0.67%), unaligned sequences (up to 11%); diverge from the Yiya5 assembly (Supplementary Table S9). This result supports the genetic variability and divergence between Yiya5 and these genomes; and the need for new genome assemblies for Western European genotypes; rather than using the Yiya5 assembly as a reference; thus allowing the first pangenomic analysis for flax. Using another genome assembly rather than Yiya5 is also supported by the fact that Western European flax breeders have no access to Chinese varieties such as Yiya5, so there is no added value in using this variety as a reference.

A catalogue of nearly 75,000 genes available for flax studies

The pangenomic analysis of ten fiber and oilseed flax genotypes support the open state of the flax pangenome, suggesting that the ten genotypes do not yet capture all the genetic diversity within cultivated flax species, although these genotypes come from the main flax producing continent. It also showed that L. usitatissimum contains a larger repertoire of genes, about twice the number of genes in the flax genome, that have not yet been described before. Only 21,003 (28%) non-redundant orthologous proteins characterized all genomes (core proteome). This value is comparable to the pangenome graph where 172.2 Mbp genome size, representing 21% of flax pangenome, were shared by the ten genotypes. The size of core genome varies between species. The flax core pangenome was smaller than that observed in other species such as diploid cotton (87%, [37]) with more stringent parameters; in tomato (74.2%; [95]), Arabidopsis thaliana (70%), bread wheat (64%, [96]), Brassica napus (62%; [90]) and soybean (36%; [97]); but higher than that observed for the pear pangenome (13%; [98]). This difference could be due to many parameters such as the number of samples used for their construction, the bioinformatic parameters and methods used, but also to the intra species genetic diversity.

In contrast, the accessory shell genome (shared by at least 2 genomes) was the most represented in the flax pangenome with 52% of the flax genome (434.1 Mbp and 38,190 non-redundant orthologous proteins); and the singleton genome represented 20% of the non-redundant orthologous proteins and 28% of the pangenome size (229.47 Mbp). The high proportion of accessory genomes (up to 80%) suggests that the pangenome of flax is of the open type and will require the inclusion of other assembled genomes likely to increase the size and gene repertoire. It indicates that the genotypes harbour a high level of [unique] diversity, which may reflect, among other, environmental adaptation [99]. Up to 3595 non-redundant orthologous proteins (14.7 Mb to 74.7 Mbp) were singletons. Indeed, the panel of ten genotypes was both fiber and oilseed, and originated from Western Europe (Idéo, Bolchoï, Attila and Marquise), Eastern Europe (Russia: Atlant), America (Canada: CDC Bethune) and Asia (China: Longya10, Heiya-14, Yiya5 and Line 3896), representing the main flax-growing continents. The genetic diversity analysis of flax species showed major genetic groups separating oilseed flax accessions from America or Europe and those from Asia or Middle-East and fiber flax accessions originating from Eastern Europe and those from Western Europe [26]; supporting a high genetic and geographical structure [100–102]. The genetic distance between genotypes which is the result of many factors such as cultigroup (fiber vs. oilseed), geographical origin, environmental adaptation, could explain the rate of singletons genome in the flax pangenome. Indeed, a general pattern based on cultigroup + geographical origin was observed for singletons, showing that the fiber accessions from Asia and Eastern Europe (Atlant and Heiya 14) had the higher level of singletons genome. In Western Europe, a cultigroup pattern was observed, with oilseed flax (Attila and Marquise) having the highest level of singletons. However, this pattern needs to be confirmed by adding more genotypes/diversity to the pangenomic analysis.

The sequences data generated in this study could be useful for analysing the genetic diversity of European and world flax collections, for characterizing new materials and for identifying the allelic version of important QTLs. For example, using the DNA sequences generated in this study for the four genotypes (Idéo, Bolchoï, Attila and Marquise) and those for the six other genotypes available in SRA archive, we visualised the genetic diversity for one QTL involved in seed yield (Marker770415, position in CDC Bethune Chr6: 11,929,857–11,930,253 bp; [103]). This QTL was identified by QTL mapping in an F2 population resulting from the cross between two homozygous inbred lines "NY17" and "DIANA". We observed a polymorphism for this Marker770415 with two allelic states, segregating fiber lines (Atlant, Bolchoï, Idéo, Line3896 and Yiya5) with genotype "G" at position 371 of the QTL, from oilseed lines (Attila, CDC Bethune, Longya10 and Marquise) with genotype "A" (Fig. 8). A third allelic state was observed in this position for the Heiya14 cultivar.

Limitations of the pangenomic analysis

In this study, we conducted a pangenomic and panproteomic analysis for ten genotypes. For the pangenomic analysis, we included a genome assembly that had been published in a peer-reviewed journal for six genotypes. For the panproteomic analysis, we annotated protein-coding genes using the Helixer annotation tool for those six genotypes. Pangenomic analysis required good quality assembly genomes. However, some assemblies among the six publicly available could be improved, given the genome size and certain assembly quality metrics. These include Atlant, Line 3896, CDC Bethune and others. However, (i) the size of some assemblies, smaller than that obtained in our study and for Yiya5, may be as much due to assembly problems in repeated regions as to biological realities; (ii) the genome completeness of most of these assemblies is very good for use in pangenomic analysis, with BUSCO scores of over 93.9%. Ideally, genome annotation for the panproteomic analysis should be performed using the same approach as for the four genotypes: Idéo, Bolchoï, Attila and Marquise. However, the aim of this study is not to re-annotate these genomes. Using two different approaches for genome annotation could produce different sets of gene models and/or missing genes, but not necessarily. However, we found that Helixer tool produced a similar number of genes to that obtained for our four accessions (approximatively 47k genes), with less than a 1% variation in the number of genes (results not shown). Given all these concerns, the pangenome results should be interpreted with caution.

Ongoing work and future perspectives

This study is the first step of a study program that aimed to develop a genomic tool to enhance genetic studies to help breeding programs develop adapted genotypes to western European climatic context. The quality of our assemblies and the genetic divergence between these assemblies and Yiya5 assembly, support the use of one of them as a reference for variants identification. On the ongoing study, we aimed to develop a genotyping tool for genotype characterization. To do this, a genotype panel representing mainly a genetic pool of western Europe pools was selected and was re-sequenced; and was used to identify variants (using one the assembly genome as a reference) and to select a SNP for genotyping tool design. This tool will be then used to analyse genetic diversity of flax accessions available in the Arvalis flax genetic resource with about 1600 accessions from around the world. The steps described above are prerequisites for deeper genetic analysis to identify genetic markers for breeding and for genotype characterisation.

Conclusion

In this study, we provided for the first time a high-quality genome assembly and annotation for four Western European fiber and oilseed flax genotypes. We used a combination of ONT and Illumina sequencing and Bionano maps. ONT sequencing, with the recent improvement in terms of error rates, facilitated the assembly, especially in the repetitive regions. We also performed for the first time pangenomic analysis for flax genomes originating from the major gene pools, using ten cultivars including the four genome assemblies from this study and the ones publicly available.

This study is a step forward for the development of genomic and genetic tools for the improvement of Western European flax breeding. It provides high-quality genomic resources that will be useful for subsequent analyses. This resource will be leveraged by ongoing resequencing of a panel of European flax accessions for variants identification, as well as the development of a genome-wide genotyping tool to study genetic diversity and characterise available genetic resources for breeding.

This study, and others to come, fills a gap in existing genomic and genetic tools and should allow a more detailed characterisation of the genetic diversity in the material available. These resources will provide breeders with both data and tools to empower genomic improvement programs in flax.
