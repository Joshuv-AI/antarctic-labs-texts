Fluid–structure interaction and thermal performance: a numerical study on crossflow heat exchangers with aerodynamically optimised splitter elements
Authors: Sachin Kaushik, Harvindra Singh, Alok Kumar, Chandra Kishore, Pravat Ranjan Pati, Sunil Chamoli, Jayant Giri, Mohammad Kanan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe thermohydraulic performance of backwards-facing splitter plates with different L/D ratios in a crossflow heat exchanger under fully developed turbulent flow at Reynolds numbers between 5500 and 14,500 is examined numerically in this study. While velocity streamlines, static pressure contours, and turbulent kinetic energy distributions show vortex formation, wake suppression, and enhanced near-wall mixing mechanisms, simulations using 3D and 2D models validate against experimental data with maximum deviations of 14% and 12% for Nusselt number (Nu) and friction factor (f), respectively. With optimal L/D ratios balancing heat transfer gains against pressure drop penalties, the splitter plates significantly enhance convective heat transfer through flow reattachment and secondary vortices, while maintaining performance evaluation criteria (PEC = (Nu/Nu₀)/(f/f₀)^{1/3}) greater than one across all configurations. Longer plates yield superior PEC at moderate Re because of effective boundary layer control. These results provide applicable design guidelines for compact heat exchangers in industrial thermal management applications that require improved heat dissipation within limited pumping power budgets, by establishing quantitative relationships between splitter geometry, flow regime, and thermohydraulic efficiency.Keywords: Heat transfer enhancement, Backward splitter plate, Pressure drop analysis, Crossflow heat exchanger, Performance evaluation criterionSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

Heat exchangers are essential components of many industrial processes, including electricity generation, chemical processing, refrigeration, air conditioning, & waste heat recovery. International energy standards have increased interest in heat exchanger design research to achieve environmentally friendly thermal performance optimisation and lower material and energy costs1,2. According to Kim and Cho3 & Hosseinnezhad et al.4, thermal systems rely heavily on heat exchanger efficiency as a critical component in addressing contemporary energy concerns, necessitating continuous development in this area of knowledge. The traditional industrial heat exchanger is limited in performance due to its high operational requirements and compact structure5,6. The design of heat exchangers has been faced with overwhelming competition due to the compromise between thermal effectiveness and pressure drop constraints7,8. The progression of manufacturing with additive manufacturing has provided designers with the opportunity to incorporate engineered defects that would be impossible through traditional manufacturing methods1,9. There have always been significant studies on heat transfer enhancement mechanisms, aiming to improve the performance of heat exchangers from both thermal and hydraulic perspectives1. These approaches are categorized as passive, active, and composite-based, in line with Zhang et al.5 and Awais & Bhuiyan7. Passive heat transfer methods encompass several techniques that enhance heat transfer without requiring supplementary energy, including surface treatments, extended surfaces, swirl flow devices, and fluid additives10,11. External power input is primarily responsible for enhancing heat transfer, either through mechanical assistance or via flow vibration in conjunction with fluid vibration12. Hybrid methods combine various passive and active methodologies to enhance the synergistic strength of heat exchangers7. Vortex generators for drag reduction are one of the most successful passive methods, as they disrupt the thermal boundary layer and create secondary flow paths or circular flows, which also help mix fluids2,7. Multiple VG configuration studies, including delta wings, rectangular wings, and delta winglets, have examined their effects on heat transfer enhancement in combination with pressure characteristics, as reported by Samadifar and Toghraie2. The VG performance can be optimised through the precise selection of angle of attack, height, and placement pattern to achieve its highest effectiveness without incurring excessive pressure penalties7. Over the last few years, scientists have investigated nanofluids for use in heat exchangers due to their promising prospects as working fluids4,11. The thermal transport properties of nanofluids are enhanced due to their combination of regular base fluids with dispersed nanoparticles. Numerous investigations are being conducted to examine the combination of improved heat exchanger designs and nanofluid technology, which has shown promising results in enhancing the thermal performance of heat exchangers. Computational fluid dynamics serves as an important tool, complementing experimental investigations, in the analysis and optimisation of heat exchanger geometries, as noted by Karimi and Afrand11 and Samadifar and Toghraie2. CFD helps show how fluid flows, how temperature is distributed, and how pressure drops, without needing an expensive experimental setup. Turbulence prediction in enhanced heat exchangers is performed using Reynolds-Averaged Navier–Stokes and Large Eddy Simulation. The present research investigates the heat transfer and flow characteristics of splitter plate crossflow heat exchangers when operated under various operational conditions. In industrial settings where improved heat transfer efficiency outweighs the drawback of higher pressure drop, the backward splitter plate configuration examined in this study is especially appropriate. Compact HVAC heat exchangers, air-cooled heat exchangers in power generation systems where increased thermal effectiveness directly impacts operational efficiency, and electronics cooling systems that require the effective removal of high heat fluxes under restricted airflow conditions are examples of such applications. The study addresses current research gaps regarding the effects of splitter plate orientation on heat transfer performance while adopting new approaches for the development of sustainable heat exchange systems. Thermal performance improvements, along with reduced pressure penalties, become achievable when the strategic combination of splitter plate position and inclination angle is applied relative to the tube banks. This document presents the research methodology, along with experimental data and practical implications for enhancing heat transfer. The current research aims to provide confirmed thermo-hydraulic trends and a physical understanding of splitter-plate-aided tube banks, as opposed to global optimum design parameters.

All in all, the current research indicates the efficiency of splitter plates in improving heat transfer; however, the vast majority of studies are performed in isolated systems or not for the purpose of validating the numerical analysis, and therefore, their direct application to the design of a real crossflow heat exchanger is limited.

Despite some works being done on splitter plates and flow-control devices in crossflow heat exchangers, most of the available literature focuses on restricted ranges of Reynolds numbers, single numerical experiments, or specific splitter shapes without systematic validation. The current research contributes to the existing literature by offering a comprehensive experimental–numerical analysis of backward splitter plates on a multi-row staggered tube bank with a turbulent Reynolds number of 550,014,500. In contrast to past research, the current work presents a simultaneous study of the effects of Reynolds number, splitter plate length (L/D), and surface roughness on the heat transfer coefficient, pressure drop, and the entire thermo-hydraulic behaviour. Additionally, the research highlights physically perceived design trends through a thorough investigation of wake stabilization, pressure recovery, and turbulence modulation, which is supported by performance evaluation criteria (PEC). These performances enable the application of validated design guidelines for splitter-plate-assisted crossflow heat exchangers, as opposed to isolated performance enhancements. Although considerable research has been conducted on flow-control devices and splitter plates in crossflow heat exchangers, several gaps remain. Most of the literature tends to use isolated numerical analyses, narrow ranges of Reynolds numbers, or a single geometry parameter, with few studies reporting experimental confirmation of numerical predictions for multi-row tube bank configurations. Furthermore, the interactive behaviour between the Reynolds number, the length of the splitter plate, and the surface roughness in relation to heat transfer and pressure drop has not been studied in a systematic framework of validity. These constraints hinder the immediate application of previous results in making design-oriented decisions.

The key contribution of the present research work includes the experimental verification of a 3D CFD model on the backward splitter plates in staggered tube bank configuration; a Quantitative study of the interactive effects of Reynolds number, splitter plate length and roughness of surfaces on Nusselt number and pressure drop; Design-based analysis on the thermo-hydraulic behaviour at intermediate Reynolds number through wake control.

Numerical methodology and boundary condition

Methodology of the model

This work conducts a numerical analysis of the thermal performance and pressure variation characteristics in a grid-like circular tube arrangement crossflow heat exchanger system. Each tube features trailing-edge splitter plates that control wake dynamics to achieve improved thermal performance in the model system. Figure 1 illustrates the computational domain, which consists of 13 circular tubes arranged in a predetermined order within a rectangular channel. The design parameters align with typical industrial applications, as they were selected in accordance with standard heat transfer literature dimensional ratios. Its primary design attributes include a diameter of 16.4 mm and 34.3 mm (SL/D ≈ 2.09), a longitudinal centre-to-centre spacing of 31.3 mm (ST/D ≈ 1.91), and a cross tube spacing of 37.7 mm (SD/D ≈ 2.30). The airflow duct attains its height value of 95.4 mm to maintain three tube rows across its transverse plane. The duct dimensions, extending from length to width, amount to 190.8 mm, which generates a 2:1 aspect ratio between these measurements. This ratio controls legitimate flow conditions through cost-efficient resource expenditure. A splitter plate is used as a rectangular plate that joins directly to the trailing border of every tube, while attached to its downstream surface. The flow-modifying elements match the size of one tube diameter (16.4 mm) and are 1.75 mm thick for orientation, which runs parallel to the flow direction.

(a) Three-dimensional view and (b) two-dimensional schematic of the computational domain used in the present study.

A computational domain consisting of 13 staggered circular tubes was arranged to achieve flow conditions that provide representative measurements throughout the tube bank. For proper flow development, the area goes 32.8 mm upstream from the primary row of tubes. To capture wakes and measure pressure recovery, the domain goes 229.6 mm downstream. The sideways area requires four split-tube setups so that the flow repeats, replicating what happens with endless tube rows. The Design Modeller from ANSYS was used to construct the geometry. At the same time, the mesh design implemented refined cell resolution in regions where flow structures were expected, around tubes and splitter plate edges, to achieve accurate results. The meshing process for the computational domain was performed using ANSYS Fluent Meshing, which produced an unstructured polyhedral mesh. The poly-hexcore mesh technique allocates polyhedral cells on the boundaries to enhance accuracy in the boundary layers.

In contrast, the use of hexahedral cells in the rest of the domain increases efficiency in the bulk flow region. This poly-hexcore technique yields improved near-wall resolutions, and the remaining unimportant areas, which are filled in with structured cells, contribute to enhanced stability and convergence in the simulations. In terms of the mesh independence check, an assessment was made between coarse and fine meshes, with 823,792 and 854,316 cells, respectively, as shown in Fig. 2. The inflation layers received specific implementation near both tube walls and the splitter plate to achieve precise boundary layer resolution.

Computational meshes used in the study: (a) coarse mesh and (b) fine mesh.

A poly-hexcore mesh approach was employed to achieve sufficient near-wall resolution while maintaining numerical stability and computational efficiency. The boundary layers near the tube and splitter plates were modelled with inflation layers to accurately represent boundary layer behaviour, which has a direct contribution to the local heat transfer coefficient and friction factor. It was established that mesh-independent solutions were obtained because the grid independence analysis showed that additional mesh refinement did not produce changes in the average Nusselt number of less than 1% or a pressure drop of less than 2%.

A mesh independence test (Fig. 3) reveals that the Nusselt number (Nu) exhibits a 0.9% difference and the pressure drop (ΔP) shows a 1.5% difference between the fine and coarse meshes, thus confirming that no significant change in results occurs with further mesh refinement. The Nusselt number determination from CFD analysis using a coarse mesh deviated from experimental values by up to 20%, despite being compared against experimental findings from13, highlighting the need for refined meshing. These results led to the selection of the fine poly-hexcore mesh (854,316 elements) for the current numerical analysis, aiming to strike the best possible balance between accuracy and computational cost.

Additionally, the mesh was created to meet the requirements for near-wall resolution (y⁺ within the appropriate range for normal wall functions). Numerical methods and boundary conditions

The flow and heat transfer characteristics are analyzed through the ANSYS Fluent CFD solver in the current investigation. The simulation domain consists of air as the primary fluid region, along with aluminium elements that represent the splitter plates (SPs). The fluid domain has boundaries that consist of a right-side velocity inlet with various Reynolds numbers (5,500, 8,500, 11,500, and 14,500), with the inlet air temperature set at 288 K. Simultaneously, a pressure outlet is established at the left boundary with a pressure of 0 bar. The tube surfaces have a no-slip wall boundary condition at a constant surface temperature of 363 K. The modelling domain contains tube surfaces characterised by three different roughness levels, including a smooth surface (k_s/D = 0) and two roughness intensities (k_s/D = 0.01 and k_s/D = 0.02). All SP bases that contact the tubes operate at a temperature of 363 K, with equal air surface roughness properties as the tube surfaces. The remaining SP surfaces function as adiabatic walls. In line with widely used parameters for internal turbulent flow simulations in heat exchanger applications, a turbulence intensity of 5% and a turbulence length scale equal to 0.07 times the hydraulic diameter were specified at the velocity inlet. To guarantee consistent comparison analysis, these parameters were maintained constant across all Reynolds values. To guarantee a consistent comparison of thermo-hydraulic performance, the inlet turbulence intensity, wall roughness, and thermal characteristics were kept constant throughout all simulated scenarios. Prior research shows that relative enhancement trends in turbulent crossflow configurations are weakly sensitive to moderate variations in these parameters, even though uncertainties in these boundary conditions may impact the absolute values of the Nusselt number. The simulation employs the Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) equations30 together. Three governing equations constitute the system. These are the principles of continuity, which is followed by momentum that includes turbulent stresses, and ends with energy, which considers both conductive and convective heat transfer. The Boussinesq approximation, still employed in the RNG k-ε model for stresses, is used for the transport equations of turbulent kinetic energy and the turbulent dissipation rate, which are applied to mean velocity gradients. Convergence for the model solutions is achieved by the application of the RMS criterion, against which a target residual of 10^-6 is obtained for each governing equation.

The continuity (1), momentum (2), energy (3), k (4), and Ψ (5) formulas make up the complete set of governing formulas.

where ρ and p stand for fluid density and pressure, respectively. T is the fluid temperature, u is the fluid’s velocity, Ψ is the rate of dissipation, and Gk is the kinetic energy produced by turbulence due to mean velocity gradients. µt represents the turbulent viscosity; σk represents the turbulent Prandtl number for k; µ and λ represent the fluid viscosity and thermal conductivity, respectively. The values of the model constants C1° and C2° are 1.44 and 1.92, respectively.

It is essential to select a suitable turbulence model to accurately predict heat transfer and pressure drop in crossflow tube banks with splitter plates, where the primary mechanisms governing thermo-hydraulic processes include flow separation, interaction with the wake, and vortex shedding. Reynolds-Averaged Navier Stokes (RANS) models and, in particular, the RNG k-ε model, have been successfully applied in the past to the study of tube banks and splitter-plate-assisted heat exchangers, mainly because they are more effective in the treatment of high strain rates and recirculating flows13,17,21. Moreover, recent comparisons between RANS and Large Eddy Simulation (LES) methods have demonstrated that the RNG k-ε model makes predictable forecasts of average trends in Nusselt number and pressure drop at a considerably low Reynolds number, and has much lower computational cost29,30. Based on these results, the RNG k-ε turbulence model was adopted in the current study, ensuring an optimal balance between numerical accuracy and computational efficiency.

The RNG k–ε model was used in conjunction with standard wall functions for near-wall treatment. To ensure compliance with the chosen wall treatment and provide a precise prediction of near-wall momentum and heat transfer, the mesh was designed to maintain y⁺ values primarily within the range of 30–100 along the tube and splitter plate surfaces.

Results and discussions

CFD validation

The 3D and 2D boundaries were simultaneously used in the validation of the computational fluid dynamics (CFD) model, and the results were compared against experimental data from13. The 2D simulation showed a maximum Nusselt number deviation of 14%, while the 3D simulation showed a 12% deviation. Although there was reasonable agreement between the 2D results and the experimental values, there were a few cases where the errors were slightly increased, particularly in the predictions of the Nusselt number and pressure. In contrast, while errors for most parameters were smaller percentages, 3D results were a closer fit to the experimental data. Similarly, pressure drop values in the 3D domain showed improved agreement with experimental data. Given its more accurate predictions and overall greater agreement with the experimental information, for increased dependability, all subsequent studies were conducted in the 3D domain. Throughout the examined Reynolds number range, the quantitative difference between numerical and experimental Nusselt numbers stays within ± 12–14%, whereas the corresponding pressure drop divergence is less than ± 10%. These levels of agreement are deemed appropriate for engineering-scale thermo-hydraulic analysis. They are similar to those documented in related CFD investigations of crossflow heat exchangers using RANS-based turbulence models. The high variation in the CFD and experimental results is primarily due to uncertainties in experimental heat loss estimation, simplifications in the numerical model, such as the assumption of constant thermo-physical properties, and the limitations of the RNG k–ε model in resolving instantaneous turbulent structures and vortex shedding. Furthermore, variations in near-wall heat transfer prediction could be caused by idealized inlet turbulence conditions and the use of wall functions. However, the splitter plate configuration-induced relative trends and performance differences are consistently replicated by the model.

The above observation further confirms that the chosen mesh resolution is sufficient for accurately forecasting thermo-hydraulic trends in the current configuration, as evidenced by the strong agreement between numerical and experimental results.

Thermal hydraulic performance

The heat transfer performance of a crossflow heat exchanger becomes more pronounced when using a backward splitter plate based on static temperature contours (Fig. 7), velocity magnitude (Fig. 8) and streamlines (Fig. 9). The installation of a splitter plate enhances convective heat transfer because the generated vortices improve mixing throughout the system and break down the thermal boundary layer. The Nusselt number grows progressively higher when the Reynolds number (Re) increases, according to Fig. 12. The Nusselt number rises 7.92% with a change in Re from 5500 to 8500 at L/D = 2 and grows 23.74% further when Re reaches 11,500. The Nusselt number achieves its maximum enhancement value of 42.56% when Re reaches 11,500 through L/D = 3. The heat transfer performance shows improvement because the splitter plate enhances turbulence generation and vortex shedding activity. The evaluation of heat transfer enhancement requires consideration of pressure drop and friction factor, as these parameters determine the associated penalties. The flow resistance characteristics become visible through the presented static pressure contours (Fig. 5) and turbulent kinetic energy distributions (Fig. 6). The friction factor measurements for various Reynolds numbers and different splitter plate lengths observe presentation in Fig. 13. This increase in Reynolds number from 5500 to 8500 and 11,500 decreases the friction factor by 12.66% and 14.05% when the L/D ratio equals 2. The reduction in the friction factor as Re increases follows predictions because viscous forces become less prominent as flow velocities increase. The results for L/D = 5 indicate that a 12.35% increase in the friction factor occurs when Re is set at 14,500. The performance evaluation criteria (PEC) simultaneously determine the tradeoff between heat transfer enhancement and pressure drop costs. The reference case of a plain tube bank without a splitter plate, assessed at the same Reynolds number and under the same boundary circumstances, is used to determine the performance evaluation criterion (PEC) in this work.

From an engineering design standpoint, the configuration is thermohydraulically advantageous if the PEC value is greater than unity, meaning that the improvement in heat transmission exceeds the associated rise in pressure drop. Consequently, PEC > 1 indicates that the splitter plate arrangement can provide improved thermal performance without imposing a disproportionate pumping power penalty—a crucial factor in applications using compact heat exchangers.

The conflicting effects of pressure loss and turbulence augmentation are seen in the fluctuation of PEC with Reynolds number and splitter plate length. Wake stabilization and delayed vortex shedding support pressure recovery at intermediate Reynolds numbers while preserving improved convective heat transfer, leading to higher PEC values. PEC gradually declines or saturates at higher Reynolds numbers due to increased turbulence production and flow resistance, which reduces the relative advantage. In a similar vein, the length of the splitter plate affects the extent of wake interaction; excessively long plates increase frictional losses without corresponding increases in heat transfer.

This performance evaluation criterion (PEC) provides a comparative thermo-hydraulic analysis by weighing the pressure drop penalty associated with steady operating conditions against the heat transfer enhancement. Lifecycle factors, such as cumulative pumping power consumption, maintenance requirements, or fouling behaviour during long-term operation, are not explicitly taken into account, even though PEC values greater than unity may offer a better thermal performance than flow resistance. Therefore, the first-order performance measure that can be used to predesign and compare the improvement plans is the current PEC-based assessment.

Both the effect of wake stabilisation and reduced viscous dominance can be considered as causes of the reduction in the factor of friction at intermediate Reynolds numbers. The backward splitter plate alters the wake structure by retarding the separation of the flows and facilitating the earlier reattachment of the flow downslope of the tubes, thereby enhancing pressure recovery and minimizing form drag. At intermediate Reynolds numbers, inertia forces are dominant over viscous forces, resulting in a lower value of the friction factor. However, the local turbulence intensity and mixing in the fluid are increased. A further increase in the Reynolds number causes the turbulence induced by the splitter plates to become dominant, resulting in a gradual rise in the pressure losses of the longer splitter plates.

According to studies by Mangrulkar et al.13, Choudhary et al.17, and Karali et al.21, wake modification and delayed flow separation result in PEC values exceeding unity, which enhances heat transfer with controlled pressure penalties. The backward splitter plate structure yields thermo-hydraulic performance enhancements that are both physically realistic and consistent with previous literature, as evidenced by the agreement between the PEC trends presented in these studies and those observed in the current work.

Although the backward splitter plate proves to be regularly effective in increasing heat transfer by modifying the wake and enhancing turbulence intensity, the optimum splitter plate length (L/D) and surface roughness cannot always be applied to all tube bank systems or fluids. It is hoped that the underlying mechanisms of enhancement, namely delayed flow separation and enhanced fluid mixing, will be generalizable to other crossflow designs used in similar regimes of turbulent Reynolds number. Nonetheless, the balance of heat transfer augmentation to pressure drop can be modified by differences in the arrangement of the tubes, pitch ratios, and the thermo-physical characteristics of the working fluid. Thus, it can be suggested that re-optimising the geometric parameters may be beneficial when scaling up the current configuration to alternative tube bank structures or fluids.

Conclusion

Numerical simulations were conducted to investigate the heat transfer and pressure drop in a cross-flow heat exchanger equipped with a backward splitter plate. In this investigation, the thermal and hydrodynamic qualities were evaluated in relation to the Reynolds coefficient and splitter plate length, and the results were compared with experimental measurements. The contour plots of static pressure with turbulent kinetic energy, as well as the ones of the velocity magnitude and temperature distribution, facilitated a better understanding of the formation of vortices and the dynamics of the flow patterns. Limitations and future research work perspective

It should be noted that the thermohydraulic superiority indicated by PEC > 1 is based on steady-state flow and heat transfer performance. Comprehensive lifecycle assessments that incorporate long-term pumping energy, fouling behaviour, and maintenance costs are recommended for future studies to fully evaluate the economic and operational feasibility of splitter-plate-assisted heat exchanger designs. Future research may include systematic sensitivity analyses of surface roughness, inlet turbulence intensity, and variations in thermo-physical properties on absolute heat transfer prediction.

By incorporating data-driven and optimisation techniques, the current CFD framework can be expanded to investigate more effective splitter plate designs methodically. Surrogate or machine-learning models that can predict Nusselt number, pressure drop, and performance evaluation criteria can be trained using CFD-generated datasets. These models can then be used in conjunction with optimisation algorithms to effectively search the design space of splitter plate geometry and surface characteristics, making it possible to find configurations that perform better than current designs at a lower computational cost.
