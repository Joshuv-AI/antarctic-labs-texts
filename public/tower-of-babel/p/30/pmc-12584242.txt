Clinical analysis of Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia in children with malignant disease
Authors: Haiyan Luo, Pan Wu, Haixia Yang, Benshan Zhang, Mincui Zheng, Wenyong Kuang, Wanli Li, Xiaoming Li, Xinping Zhang, Jiaotian Huang, Qianxiu Fan, Shan He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo characterize Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) in pediatric malignancy patients receiving chemotherapy or glucocorticoids, and provide guidance for timely diagnosis and effective treatment.MethodsA retrospective analysis of clinical features, laboratory findings, radiological characteristics, treatment approaches, and outcomes in pediatric malignancy patients who developed PJP.ResultsTen patients (1:1 male-to-female ratio; median age 6.50 years, range 2.83–14.58 years) were followed for a median of 14 months (range 6–53 months). Eight developed PJP during chemotherapy and two post-completion. Nine received glucocorticoids in their chemotherapy regimen. PJP prophylaxis was either absent (n = 4) or discontinued > 1 month prior (n = 5). Clinical presentations included fever, dyspnea, and wheezing, often without significant cough. Laboratory findings showed elevated (1,3)-β-D-glucan(BDG)in 7 cases. Chest CT typically revealed bilateral diffuse patchy infiltrates, ground-glass opacities, and nodular shadows. All cases had a positive metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) or targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) result for P. jirovecii from sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples.Treatment included Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole(TMP/SMZ) with/without caspofungin and adjunctive corticosteroids. Eight patients required PICU admission (median stay 7.5 days, range 0–45 days) for respiratory support. Nine patients survived PJP, with one PJP-related death and one death from underlying disease.ConclusionHigh clinical suspicion for PJP is warranted in pediatric malignancy patients presenting with fever, dyspnea, elevated BDG, and characteristic imaging findings, particularly in those with inadequate prophylaxis. Early pathogen detection and treatment initiation are crucial. While TMP/SMZ remains first-line therapy, combination with caspofungin may improve outcomes. Short-term adjunctive corticosteroids may benefit moderate to severe cases.Keywords: Malignant disease, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, Diagnosis, Treatment, Children

Background

Pneumocystis jirovecii (PJ), formerly known as Pneumocystis carinii, is an opportunistic fungal pathogen that causes Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP). Before the implementation of routine prophylaxis, PJP was the leading cause of infectious mortality in pediatric leukemia patients [1]. While prophylaxis has significantly reduced PJP mortality in children with cancer, new challenges have emerged due to the increasing prevalence of hematologic malignancies requiring immunosuppressive therapy and the development of novel biotherapies.

Multiple risk factors for PJP have been identified. The primary risk stems from T-cell deficiency or dysfunction, whether due to underlying malignancy or immunosuppressive therapy. Corticosteroid treatment has been established as an independent risk factor associated with mortality in both adults [2, 3] and children [1, 4]. Additional risk factors include respiratory cytomegalovirus (CMV) coinfection [5], myeloablative conditioning for hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT), high-grade graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), T-cell-specific therapy (e.g., alemtuzumab), craniospinal or chest irradiation, and myelosuppressive therapy [4, 6].

Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP/SMX) remains the gold standard for PJP prophylaxis in both HIV-positive and HIV-negative patients. While effective prophylaxis has substantially reduced PJP morbidity and mortality [7], a significant number of patients develop PJP despite available prophylaxis guidelines [8], with medication non-compliance emerging as a major contributing factor in pediatric cases. The clinical presentation of PJP typically includes fever, hypoxemic dyspnea, and nonproductive cough, accompanied by bilateral interstitial pneumonia with diffuse patchy consolidative and ground-glass opacities on chest imaging [9, 10]. However, early diagnosis remains challenging due to the absence of pathognomonic clinical or radiological findings at disease onset, and delayed diagnosis correlates with increased mortality. Prompt identification and treatment initiation are crucial for improving outcomes. While TMP/SMX is an effective first-line treatment [11], PJP remains a significant threat to immunocompromised children.

Although numerous studies have documented PJP in HIV-negative adults, research on pediatric HIV-negative PJP remains limited. This retrospective descriptive study characterizes PJP cases in children with malignant diseases at our institution. By analyzing clinical characteristics, diagnostic approaches, and treatment outcomes, we aim to enhance understanding of PJP in pediatric malignancies and provide guidance for early diagnosis and effective management.

Methods

Study design and settings

We conducted a retrospective observational study in the Department of Hematology at a Chinese children’s hospital between January 2018 and August 2024. During this period, the hospital recorded approximately 2,720 admissions for patients with malignant diseases.

Study population

The study included all patients with malignant diseases who received a diagnosis of PJP during the study period. Inclusion criteria for the participants were as follows: (1)inpatients with malignant diseases;⑵clinical manifestations like fever with/without cough or dyspnea, or progressive hypoxemia;⑶lung radiological signs—diffuse ground glass shadows, diffuse infiltration in the interstitium or consolidation et al. ⑷had a positive metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) or targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) result for P. jirovecii from sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples; Exclusion criteria for the participants were as follows: (1) age>18 years, (2) not undergoing mNGS or tNGS, and (3) incomplete medical records.

The standard for diagnosing PJP was a comprehensive clinical judgement by two senior pediatrics.This judgement was based on a combination of the host immune status, clinical, radiological, and microbiological criteria, the use of TMP-SMX for prophylaxis and treatment response.

Data collection

Hospital database records were analyzed to determine PJP incidence among inpatients with malignant diseases (including leukemia, lymphoma, neuroblastoma, nephroblastoma, hepatoblastoma, retinoblastoma, medulloblastoma, osteosarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, and hemophagocytic syndrome). Through chart review, we collected demographics (age and gender), clinical parameters (malignancy types, laboratory values: total lymphocyte count (LYM), total granulocyte count (NE), serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), plasma (1,3)-β-D-glucan (BDG), and inflammatory markers (PCT, CRP); radiological findings, coinfection, PJP prophylaxis history, chemotherapy details, time interval between last chemotherapy and PJP diagnosis, and corticosteroid usage), and treatment outcomes (PJP treatment regimen (drug choice and duration), PICU length of stay, respiratory support requirements, and 30-day mortality rate).

We noted that PJP diagnosis date was defined as the first documentation of relevant pulmonary changes on chest imaging. Laboratory data were collected on or within 7 days prior to diagnosis. Chest images were evaluated by at least two specialist radiologists. Chemotherapeutic agents included cyclophosphamide (CTX), methotrexate, daunorubicin, vincristine (VCR), pegaspargase, cytarabine, mercaptopurine (6-MP), dexamethasone (DEX), prednisone, actinomycin D (ACT-D), etoposide (VP-16), and doxorubicin (ADR). PJP prophylaxis consisted of oral TMP/SMZ (25 mg/kg daily) administered three consecutive days weekly during chemotherapy. Death was attributed to PJP if caused by progressive respiratory failure in affected patients.

Results

During the study period, 10 patients with a high clinical suspicion of PJP were diagnosed with PJP through the integration of radiological and laboratory investigations, and all had respiratory specimens test positive for PJP via mNGS or tNGS. One additional child with leukemia and suspected PJP was excluded due to death from respiratory failure before diagnostic confirmation. Among approximately 2,720 patient-years of malignant disease admissions during 6.5 years,10 (0.37%) of whom developed PJP.

Patient characteristics

Among the 10 confirmed PJP cases, demographics and clinical features were as follows (Table 1). The cohort had equal gender distribution with a median age of 6.50 years (range: 2.83–14.58 years). Regarding PJP prophylaxis, six patients received prophylactic treatment with TMP-SMX, but all of them discontinued the prophylaxis for various reasons before the onset of PJP, while four patients did not receive prophylactic treatment for Pneumocystis jirovecii from the very beginning due to various reasons.Primary malignancies included: acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL): n = 4 (40%; two low-risk, one intermediate-risk, one relapsed), lymphoblastic lymphoma (LBL): n = 4 (40%; two B-LBL, two T-LBL), hemophagocytic syndrome (HLH): n = 1 (10%), and nephroblastoma: n = 1 (10%). Nine patients (90%) received dexamethasone (continuous or intermittent) as part of their chemotherapy regimen. PJP developed during active chemotherapy in eight patients (80%) and post-chemotherapy completion in two patients (20%). The median interval between chemotherapy initiation and PJP diagnosis was 12 months (range: 1–27 months). Clinical characteristics of the PJP patients

At PJP diagnosis, all patients presented with fever, while cough and dyspnea were present in 40% and 50% of cases, respectively. Respiratory complications were common, with 40% developing respiratory insufficiency and 60% progressing to severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) or respiratory failure. Concurrent pulmonary infections were identified in 70% of cases. Radiological findings showed diffuse bilateral patchy infiltrates in 90% of patients on chest CT (Figs. 1, 2 and 3), characterized by ground-glass opacities, punctate lesions, nodular shadows, and areas of consolidation. Laboratory findings included: elevated BDG in 70% of patients, increased serum LDH in 60% of patients, universal lymphopenia, with 90% showing counts < 0.7 × 10⁹/L, neutropenia in 40% of cases, variable inflammatory markers: CRP (median 35.32 mg/L, range 2.11–162.9 mg/L), and PCT (median 0.71 ng/ml, range 0.03–29.18 ng/ml). Treatment of PJP

All patients received initial therapy with TMP-SMX (100%), with 9 patients (90%) also receiving caspofungin and one patient receiving TMP-SMX (100 mg/kg.d) monotherapy. Treatment duration was standardized at approximately 3 weeks. Adjunctive corticosteroids (new or increased dosage) were administered to 4 patients (40%). Respiratory support included: invasive mechanical ventilation: 4 patients (40%), ECMO: 1 patient (who subsequently died from respiratory failure), and blood purification: 2 patients (20%). The median PICU stay was 7.5 days (range: 0–45 days). Overall PJP-attributable mortality was 10% (1/10 patients) (Table 3).

Discussion

PJP is a significant threat to immunocompromised children, occurring through either environmental exposure or reactivation of latent infection [12]. The primary risk factors encompass impaired cell-mediated immunity and immunosuppressive therapy, particularly in patients undergoing treatment for hematologic malignancies, HSCT, or receiving high-dose corticosteroids [13, 14]. A recent multicenter study highlighted that patients with immune-mediated inflammatory diseases and solid tumors experienced more severe PJP manifestations and higher mortality rates, with prior corticosteroid therapy (prednisone ≥ 10 mg daily) emerging as the sole immunosuppressant associated with 90-day mortality [3]. Our cohort’s disease distribution - acute leukemia (50%), lymphoblastic lymphomas (30%), HLH (10%), and solid tumors (10%) - aligns with these established risk profiles, with 90% receiving high-dose corticosteroids.

PJP infections occur more frequently in patients lacking prophylaxis [15], with up to 25% of unprophylaxed pediatric oncology patients developing PJP during chemotherapy [1]. The European Conference on Infections in Leukemia (ECIL) guidelines recommend TMP/SMX as first-line prophylaxis in pediatric hematology patients throughout chemotherapy and for 6 weeks to 6 months after completing maintenance therapy. For HSCT recipients, prophylaxis should continue from neutrophil engraftment until achieving protective immune recovery [16]. In our institution, we recommend TMP/SMX prophylaxis (25 mg/kg daily for three consecutive days weekly) for all chemotherapy patients who can tolerate it. The reasons for non-use or discontinuation of TMP-SMX in our survey included high-dose methotrexate chemotherapy, severe liver damage, glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency, and poor adherence of the child or parent.But analysis of our PJP cases revealed that 40% had never received prophylaxis, 50% had discontinued prophylaxis more than one month before infection, and 10% received irregular prophylaxis. These findings suggest that prophylaxis non-adherence, rather than prophylaxis failure, drives infections.

Studies investigating reduced-dose or intermittent TMP/SMX regimens to minimize toxicity have shown varying results [17, 18]. Hughes et al. demonstrated in a prospective, double-blind randomized trial that three consecutive days weekly was as effective as daily administration for ALL patients while reducing toxicity (PJP rates: 0% vs. 21%) [19]. However, less frequent dosing schedules show higher breakthrough rates, with twice-weekly regimens yielding 1.2% breakthrough infections [20] and weekly prophylaxis proving insufficient during intensive chemotherapy or autologous stem cell transplantation [21]. These findings align with our observation that 60% of PJP occurred in patients receiving sub-optimal prophylaxis schedules. Optimizing prophylactic regimens to balance efficacy and tolerability remains an important research goal.

Since its recognition as an AIDS-defining illness in 1981 [22], PJP diagnostics have evolved significantly. While open lung biopsy remains the gold standard for detecting characteristic cysts and trophozoites [23], its invasive nature has led clinicians to rely on probable diagnosis based on host immune status, immunosuppression severity, and clinical, radiological, and microbiological criteria. Notably, non-HIV patients often present with more subtle but potentially more severe manifestations [24]. In our cohort, clinical presentations included universal fever (100%), dyspnea (50%), cough (40%), respiratory insufficiency (40%), and progression to severe ARDS or respiratory failure (60%). This pattern aligns with previous reports in pediatric cancer and HSCT populations, where fever, hypoxia, tachypnea, and nonproductive cough are typical [25, 26]. Radiologically, all patients demonstrated diffuse bilateral infiltrates extending from the perihilar region, a characteristic finding that should prompt PJP consideration in high-risk patients.

Laboratory markers have emerged as crucial diagnostic indicators. A comparative study of 40 PJP and 40 non-PJP cases identified decreased lymphocyte counts (particularly CD3+, CD4+, and CD8 + T cells) and elevated serum LDH as significant risk factors [27, 28]. Our data corroborated these findings, with 90% of patients showing lymphocyte counts < 0.7 × 10⁹/L and 60% demonstrating elevated LDH. Notably, all four patients requiring mechanical ventilation had markedly elevated LDH (>1000U/L), suggesting its potential role as a severity indicator. The AIEOP-BFM ALL 2009 trial similarly reported universal lymphopenia (< 1000/µL) at PJP onset [29], and Huang et al. confirmed lymphocyte counts < 0.7 × 10⁹/L as a risk factor in non-HIV immunocompromised patients [30].

Regarding biomarkers, BDG elevation was observed in 70% of our cases, with all patients showing positive P. jirovecii detection in BALF via mNGS (80%) or tNGS (20%). Recent evidence suggests that while BDG alone may be more suitable for exclusion than diagnosis [31], its combination with molecular methods (mNGS or tNGS) enhances diagnostic accuracy [32–38]. Mappin-Kasirer et al. developed a promising clinical risk score incorporating serum LDH, radiological patterns, and prophylaxis history [39]. However, meta-analyses indicate that BDG’s pooled sensitivity remains insufficient for definitive diagnosis [40], with varying cut-off values in non-HIV patients [41] and confounding factors including age, comorbidities, prior prophylaxis, and detection methods [42].It is important to note that the diagnostic threshold for mNGS/tNGS in PJP is not universally standardized and may vary depending on the specific laboratory and clinical context. Clinicians should interpret mNGS/tNGS results in conjunction with clinical symptoms, imaging findings, and other laboratory tests to make an accurate diagnosis.Currently, there is no unified optimal threshold for mNGS to distinguish between PJP infection and colonization. According to literature reports, the thresholds vary, with some studies suggesting 10 reads [43] and others proposing 6 reads [33]. In our case series, all mNGS read counts were above 30, tNGS read counts were above 2400, which can effectively assist in the diagnosis of PJP by integrating clinical manifestations with other laboratory examinations.The unification of specific thresholds requires further research with larger datasets in the future. These limitations underscore the need for more sensitive, non-invasive diagnostic indicators and predictive models.

Current treatment guidelines from both ECIL and the American Society of Transplantation recommend TMP/SMX as first-line therapy (trimethoprim 15–20 mg/kg and sulfamethoxazole 75–100 mg/kg) for ≥ 14 days, with a preference for 21 days in most cases [11, 44]. Important precautions include avoiding concurrent methotrexate administration and screening for G6PD deficiency.The target peak concentration of TMP-SMX is suggested to be 100–200 mg/L [11], but we are currently unable to monitor this concentration to confirm therapeutic efficacy.While echinocandins target only the cyst form of P. jirovecii, combination therapy with TMP/SMX and caspofungin has shown promise. In our cohort, to ensure therapeutic efficacy, 90% received this combination therapy, consistent with recent evidence suggesting superior outcomes compared to TMP/SMX monotherapy [45, 46].However, studies on caspofungin in combination with TMP-SMX as first-line therapy are currently limited to retrospective analyses and case reports, and further prospective and large-scale studies are needed to confirm its efficacy.

The role of adjunctive corticosteroids remains controversial in non-HIV PJP. While some studies demonstrate reduced mortality in patients with respiratory failure [47, 48] and improved clinical response rates with combination therapy [49], others show no benefit in 30-day or 1-year mortality [50, 51]. In our study, 40% of patients with rapidly progressive disease, such as case1 with persistent high fever that is difficult to control and case 2, case 7, case 10 both with rapid deterioration of respiratory function, received adjunctive corticosteroids with favorable outcomes. Notably, the single mortality case, who developed ARDS requiring mechanical ventilation, ECMO, and blood purification, did not receive early corticosteroid therapy. This observation, while not definitive, underscores the potential importance of early intervention, as prompt treatment initiation may reduce progression to invasive ventilation and death [52].

Several limitations warrant consideration in our analysis. Initially, it is important to note that this study is a single-center investigation with a limited sample size. Specifically, one patient who was highly suspected of having PJP did not undergo confirmatory testing prior to death. This circumstance may potentially influence the analysis of the results. It underscores the critical importance of early diagnosis and timely initiation of treatment for effective disease control. Through a comprehensive review of the 10 cases presented herein, we have identified additional biological markers that may be indicative of PJP. Furthermore, we propose that systemic anti-PJP treatment should be proactively initiated in cases where PJP is highly suspected, even before the results of etiological tests are available. To further substantiate the perspectives presented in this paper, additional multicenter studies with larger datasets are warranted.Second, despite guideline recommendations, we lacked standardized alternative prophylaxis protocols for patients intolerant to TMP/SMX. Third, serial BDG monitoring was not performed in patients with initially normal levels, potentially missing dynamic changes in this biomarker. Nevertheless, our findings clearly demonstrate that PJP infection in pediatric malignancy patients primarily results from inadequate prophylaxis coverage or adherence. Future research should focus on developing rapid diagnostic strategies and establishing optimal timing for combination therapy, including both antifungal agents and adjunctive corticosteroids. Such advances could potentially improve outcomes in this vulnerable patient population.
