Medical students’ perspectives on climate change, climate-health education, and professional identity: a qualitative study
Authors: Martin Wei-Pin Loh, Mattias Wei Ren Kon, Akshaya Krishna, Frederick Hui Fei Chan, Konstadina Griva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClimate change is a global health emergency that affects both physical and mental health due to causes including heat stress, spread of vector-borne diseases, and nutritional deficiencies. As healthcare systems face growing demands, future healthcare professionals must be prepared to respond, adapt and support mitigation. This research aims to explore medical students’ perspectives on climate change, perceived climate-health education gaps in medical curricula, and how climate action manifests in their professional identity as future physicians.MethodsA qualitative study using semi-structured interviews exploring the aforementioned topics of interest was conducted on 20 medical students in Singapore. Participants were recruited across all cohorts of existing students via convenience sampling. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed, and analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsSeven themes were identified: (1) awareness of climate change as a health determinant; (2) psychological responses towards climate change such as climate-related anxiety; (3) perceptions of climate change as important yet distant; (4) marginalisation and absence within medical education; (5) healthcare’s dual role as contributor and responder; (6) tensions in professional identity and responsibility; and (7) calls for experiential, clinically integrated climate competence training. Collectively, these themes reflected strong recognition of climate–health links alongside uncertainty about professional roles and limited curricular integration.ConclusionMedical students view climate change as clinically relevant but underrepresented in training. Embedding applied climate-health education and defining professional responsibilities within the curriculum may better prepare physicians for the evolving health challenges of a warming world.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09775-7.

Introduction

Climate change is a major determinant of health with accumulating evidence linking rising temperature and environmental degradation to heat-related illness, respiratory and vector-borne diseases, and worsening of mental health [1–3]. Healthcare systems are already experiencing high demand and burden from climate-sensitive diseases [4, 5], especially due to temperature [1–8] and infectious diseases [9, 10]. The healthcare sector itself also contributes to greenhouse emissions and waste, raising concerns about sustainability. Climate-health impacts are expected to intensify globally, particularly in coastal, densely populated and ageing societies, such as Singapore, increasing strain on healthcare systems [11–13]. Future health professionals must therefore be equipped to respond to these challenges through medical curricula that prepare clinicians to manage and prevent climate-sensitive diseases and to deliver care in a changing environment.

Yet medical education treats climate as a peripheral topic, undermining its relevance to professional identity and clinical practice. Medical education is more than knowledge and skills acquisition; it is also the process of professional identity formation [14]. Through the formal curriculum, assessment processes and clinical rotations, medical students internalise not only biomedical knowledge but also the responsibilities, values and professional norms/standards that define legitimate and ethical medical practice [15]. When climate change and planetary health are treated as peripheral, climate-health practice may be seen as outside the remit of medicine, creating a disconnect between future clinical practice and current training.

Bell’s concept of ‘eco-medical literacy’ [16], frames climate change in context of clinical competencies advocating for early integration of climate-related knowledge into medical reasoning and clinical care rather than a standalone topic. More than a decade later, institutions have begun to respond with revisions on medical curricula [17–19]. Initiatives such as the Planetary Health Report Card (PHRC), a student-led, metric-based benchmarking tool and the United Kingdom’s General Medical Council guidance on sustainable healthcare education [20, 21] highlight progress, but adoption across medical schools remains uneven and fragmented.

The Society of General Internal Medicine (SGIM) Environmental Health Interest Group identified persisting gaps and deficiencies in climate and planetary health curricula, including limited systematic approaches to climate and planetary health medical education; minimal emphasis on learner-driven approaches; limited focus on physician and learner well-being; and weak integration of health equity and climate justice [22]. This lack of a proper, defined structure has been voiced by educators and students alike, who have reported dissatisfaction with content, volume, and assessments of climate content [23, 24]. Globally, incorporation of climate-health education remains inadequate [25, 26]. Gupta et al. in a 2022 study revealed that only 1.8% of students reported receiving sustainable healthcare education, despite 89% desiring more integration [27].

Prior work has mainly focused on student knowledge and/or climate perception with limited attention on how medical education influences professional role and the legitimacy of climate sensitive health practice [28]. Healthcare professionals are trusted messengers and care coordinators, positioning them to promote climate advocacy. Medical societies, including the American College of Physicians and American Academy of Paediatrics, have called for clinicians to leverage this influence to educate patients about climate-related health risks [29, 30]. However, climate advocacy expectations are layered atop numerous clinical responsibilities, creating time scarcity barriers [31]. This reflects a broader health system-level concern. Since medical education does not exist in silo, health systems must provide institutional support, resources, and formal leadership pathways to enable climate advocacy without overburdening clinical workload [32].

This study aims to explore medical students’ perspectives on climate change and planetary health, identify curricular gaps, and examine how climate-health education could be more effectively integrated. The study will also examine students’ perspectives on climate in the context of professional identity such as the disjunction between the role of a doctor and a climate advocate. Findings will inform the co-creation of feasible and contextually relevant climate-health educational initiatives within undergraduate medical education around the world, especially in the authors’ own institution to aid in ongoing curriculum development at Lee Kong Chian School of Medicine, Nanyang Technological University.

Methods

Study design

The study is reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist (Appendix A) [33]. A qualitative study design was employed, using reflective thematic analysis as the methodological framework to explore medical students’ perspectives on climate change and planetary health. Semi-structured interviews were conducted to allow for flexible, in-depth discussions, whereby participants could elaborate on personal views while ensuring that key topics were consistently addressed across interviews. Interviews were conducted individually.

Interview structure

An interview guide was developed from existing literature, piloted, and revised for clarity (Appendix B). The interview guide was informed by professional identity formation theory [34], recognising medical education’s influence on what medical students perceive as legitimate clinical practice, and by curriculum theory that views curriculum as the framework for professional norms and values [35].

The interview guide covered the following topics:

Participant recruitment

Participants were recruited between February 2025 and September 2025 through institutional mailing lists, posters and in-class announcements distributed across all cohorts of medical students at the Lee Kong Chian School of Medicine, Nanyang Technological University. The medical school offers a five year undergraduate Bachelor of Medicine, Bachelor of Surgery (MBBS) programme. The first two years comprise foundational or pre-clinical content, while the last three years comprise purely clinical teaching with clinical rotations across all specialties.

All students were invited to participate with recruitment materials circulated broadly to ensure access across different years of study. A convenience sampling approach was used with participants being self-selected. Interested students completed an online form expressing willingness to participate and were provided with study information and an informed consent form prior to scheduling an interview. Of 30 students who expressed interest online, N = 20 consented and participated (response rate 66%). Recruitment and analysis proceeded iteratively, and recruitment stopped when no new patterns relevant to the research questions were identified, indicating sufficient depth and richness of the dataset.

Data collection

One-to-one interviews were conducted individually via Zoom by three interviewers (MWL, AK, FHFC) and were audio-recorded with participants’ consent. Interviews were conducted separately by the three interviewers using the same semi-structured interview guide. Regular debriefing meetings were held throughout data collection to support consistency in interviewing approach and depth of topic exploration. The interviews lasted between 30 and 45 min. Automated transcripts were generated using Microsoft OneDrive’s transcription function and subsequently reviewed against the audio recordings and manually corrected where necessary to ensure transcription accuracy before anonymisation by the research team.

Data analysis

Data was analysed using reflective thematic analysis [36] via an interpretive approach described by Terry and Hayfield that recognises the researchers’ active role in knowledge production and theme development, rather than prioritising coder agreement. The process included the following stages: (1) familiarisation with transcripts, (2) generation of initial codes, (3) development and refinement of candidate themes, and (4) final definition and naming of themes supported by illustrative quotations.

Three members of the research team (MWL, MWRK, AK) independently engaged with the transcripts and contributed to coding and interpretation. The research team reconvened to discuss coding patterns, any differing interpretations, and collaboratively refined the developing thematic structure. These discussions were used to deepen analytic interpretation and reflexivity, and refine theme development, rather than to achieve consensus or establish inter-coder reliability. Taguette software was used to support coding and data organisation [37]. Thematic saturation was reached after 15 interviews, with no novel themes or subthemes generated after by each coder.

The research team included medical students, a student from School of Biological Sciences and academic staff with interests in climate-health education, health behaviour, and qualitative research. The team acknowledged that prior engagement with planetary health and assumptions about climate change and medical education could influence data interpretation, and discussions were conducted throughout analysis to examine assumptions and inform theme development.

Results

A total of 20 participants took part in the interview (Table 1). The majority were female (50%), Chinese (80%) and in their 4th year of study (40%). A majority also fell within the age range of 20–22 years (75%). Thematic analysis identified seven themes describing students’ understanding of climate-health links, emotional responses, perceived curricular gaps and tensions in professional identity (Fig. 1). Theme 1: Awareness of climate change as health determinant

Participants had a good understanding of the connections between climate change and health. They viewed climate change as a driver of poor health, increasing risks for heat-related, respiratory and vector-borne infection and/or environmental contamination. Their understanding extended to indirect effects such as exacerbating poverty and inequalities with vulnerable populations disproportionately affected. Although students did not frame climate change as a health emergency yet overall, they viewed climate change as clinically relevant and connected to patient care.

Subtheme 1: Effects of environmental threats on health

Participants discussed extensively the direct effects of the altered environment on health outcomes due to climate change. Rising temperatures and increased pollution were the driving factors for negative outcomes on human health, as mentioned by Participant 14, with “pollution and how it affects the air quality that we breathe in and that would in turn lead to health conditions like lung disease.”1 Common negative impacts raised were heat-related illness and nutritional deficiencies.

Subtheme 2: Effects of climate sensitive diseases

This subtheme explores the participants’ perspectives of the global increase in incidence of infectious and non-infectious disease due to climate change. The increased spread of vector-borne diseases such as malaria and dengue and seasonal viruses such as influenza were noted by participants. Participant 15 raised this, stating that “diseases spread more so because… you have the malaria belt increasing in size. It used to only be around the equator and it’s going up and it’s going down because now the temperature is good enough to sustain them. And that is where our viruses also come in.“2 The effects of increased temperature and pollution on increasing heat-related illnesses and respiratory disease were also significant.

Subtheme 3: Disproportionately adverse effects for vulnerable populations

Participants recognised certain populations (elderly, those in poverty, etc) experience greater consequences of climate change than the average individual. Vulnerability was attributed to physical limitations, geographic disadvantage, and social constraints. Participant 10 captured this sentiment, stating that “…climate change and increasing temperatures have led to a lot of problems for especially the vulnerable, the young and the old when they are unable to regulate their heat well and disproportionately attacks the poor.“3 However, few participants linked these vulnerabilities to the delivery of healthcare, despite recognising that climate-related disease burdens may increase while access to care becomes more difficult for these populations.

Theme 2: Psychological responses towards climate change

Participants discussed their own emotional responses to climate change during the interview. Some students described emotional and existential responses to climate change, including climate anxiety, uncertainty about the future, and concerns about the wellbeing of future generations. Participant 15 stated they felt fear for “… our future generations, because (they’re) someone who wants to become a parent in the future. And… it is kind of a consideration whether (they’re) even going to bother bringing life into this world where it’s…. very hard and tough to live.”4 Although the intensity of these responses varied, participants frequently expressed feelings of futility, helplessness, and limited personal agency in the face of escalating environmental change. These accounts reflected an underlying tension between awareness of climate threats and uncertainty regarding how individuals, including future physicians, could meaningfully respond.

Theme 3: Climate change: Important yet distant

Despite acknowledging the importance of climate, students viewed climate change as distant, both geographically and temporally, which appear to reduce urgency and engagement. Limited direct exposure, particularly in Singapore which was viewed as relatively protected and sheltered from climate impacts because of geography, infrastructure, and environmental policies, led them to view climate change as abstract and distant or disconnected to daily life despite awareness of its health implications.

Subtheme 1: Limited direct exposure to climate impacts in Singapore

Participants perceived Singapore as insulated due to its geography, economic resources and strong infrastructure and technology. In their account, the main focus was on the present with limited reflection on how existing measures might hold under escalating global climate change scenarios. Participant 19 stated that “ … because… living in this part of the world, especially in this country, (they) are not really affected, exposed to the adverse effects of climate change.”5

Subtheme 2: Abstract knowledge of climate change without experiential foundation

Participants’ narratives indicated a disconnect between awareness of climate change and preparedness for action, suggesting a dissonance between knowledge and lived experience. Many reported limited engagement in community-level environmental activities citing academic demands and time constraints. Individual behaviours ranged from recycling or reusing materials to no reported environmentally conscious practices in daily life. This gap appeared to reflect competing priorities and convenience, which often took precedence over engagement with climate-related actions. Participant 17 mentioned that they felt “quite sheltered from…climate change and its effect” and that it seemed “like a very abstract topic”, resulting in a difficulty to gain an appreciation for climate efforts.6

Theme 4: Marginalisation and absence within medical education

Participants reported limited acknowledgement of climate change within their experiences as medical students, both in formal curriculum content and in their day-to-day academic and personal contexts. The emphasis on ‘becoming a good doctor’ through mastery of biomedical content was perceived by some students to leave limited space for engagement with climate change in medicine, noting lack of such topics in their training.

Subtheme 1: Gaps in medical education

Participants reported a lack of formal teaching on climate change within their medical education. Identified gaps included the climate–health relationship and public health perspectives on climate change. For some participants, limited curricular integration reinforced the perception that climate change is peripheral to medical training. Some also viewed it as a broad, systems-level issue, with limited relevance to clinically focused teaching and placements. Participant 18 aligned with this sentiment, stating that they thought the incorporation of climate change into medical education was “not very well” done and that they did not think that “the medical curriculum emphasises climate change at all.”7

Subtheme 2: Competing priorities in medical education

Some participants deprioritised climate change–related content due to the volume of existing medical topics and its perceived limited relevance to examinations and future clinical practice. Many described an immediate focus on core biomedical content, including physiology, pathology, and anatomy, as their primary academic priority. This emphasis on exam-oriented, biomedical learning was recurrent. Participant 5 felt that “… it’s not something that can be integrated as of now” and that there were “more serious things that more directly affect whether a patient lives or dies.“8 Participants also suggested that didactic teaching of climate change may be less well received, especially as a non-examinable and abstract topic.

Theme 5: Healthcare’s dual role as contributor and responder

Participants described the dual role of healthcare in climate change, involving both responding to climate-sensitive health conditions and contributing substantially to climate change through resource use and waste generation, particularly single-use plastics. They perceived a tension between healthcare’s responsibility to respond to climate-sensitive conditions and the environmental burden associated with healthcare resource use and waste generation, particularly single-use plastic materials. Participant 19 best summarises this as working “bidirectionally” - that “healthcare is going to worsen climate change, which will then have an increased effect on how we deal with people suffering from climate related injuries”, in turn causing a “vicious cycle” or a “positive feedback loop that’s just going to worsen as the climate changes.”9

However, these issues were not linked to their daily priorities or anticipated professional role and did not feature prominently in their thinking. Many expressed ambivalence or rationalised waste as necessary for patient safety, emphasising sterility and immediate clinical concerns. A minority suggested that sustainability might be considered only after patient outcomes were secured. Participant 6 describes this priority with: “but if you do want to cut things within medicine, you have to draw a very fine line to make sure you’re not compromising patient safety by cutting that particular aspect.”10

Subtheme 1: Environmental impact of healthcare waste and resource usage

Awareness of the environmental impact of healthcare varied. Some participants were surprised by the extent of healthcare’s contribution to climate change, while others reported limited awareness or did not view it as a pressing concern. Participants in clinical placement years were more likely to recognise the scale of single-use waste and resource use in clinical settings. Participant 20 demonstrated this recognition, believing that “the healthcare sector does consume a lot of goods and energy” and stated that “it makes sense in the amount of carbon emissions that’s being produced.”11

Subtheme 2: Tension between infection control and sustainability

Participants described the conflict between infection control requirements and their own desire to reduce waste through more sustainable practices. Many believed that reusing disposable items could compromise infection control and in turn patient safety. As future health care professionals, they prioritised patient safety with sustainable healthcare practices considered acceptable only when they did not compromise clinical care. Participant 20 thought that “generally the consensus would be (to) take care of… current patients first” and that they would not “compromise patient safety… for wastage.”12

Theme 6: Tensions in professional identity and responsibilities

Participants were uncertain about whether climate action fell within the scope of a physician’s role, reflecting tension between immediate clinical responsibilities and broader roles in prevention, advocacy, and systems change. Participants broadly fell into three groups: bystanders, integrators, and advocates. Bystanders were generally unwilling to advocate or discuss climate change in the future as physicians, viewing this as outside the clinician remit. Participant 5 ascribed to this role, stating that they did not think they had to “actively promote… because again, (their) primary job and role is to take care of sick people.”13

Integrators viewed climate change as a discussion point with patients to improve health outcomes, but not to implement systemic change. Participant 8 for instance mentions how “it’s very important to educate them on how climate change does affect their lives”14, yet participant 16 adds that healthcare in general is “quite bureaucratic” and despite efforts from healthcare staff it will still be “rather hard to see very much change from the ground up.”15

Advocates saw the physician role as a platform for broader action including research and policy to support sustainable healthcare practices. Some students reported mentorship in clinical rotations which reinforced their intention to emulate these practices. Participant 1 described their experience in a clinical posting where the chief of surgery was an advocate for climate change and enacted programmes around the hospital and operating theatres, encouraging hospital staff to “use hand sanitiser instead of paper towels”, and it was inspiring as the participant felt it was important to first “embody it for myself” before trying to motivate others.16 Across all groups, there was general uncertainty about the scope of a physicians’ responsibility in relation to climate change.

Theme 7: Building climate competence in medical training

Participants advocated for early, interactive, and clinically relevant climate education focused on developing competence rather than adding content, including communication training and service-based learning. They emphasised applied and clinically relevant approaches, over didactic teaching and noted that climate change needed to be integrated in the medical curriculum. Participant 6 suggested “starting a service program around climate change… (being) far more valuable than having raw course content about it” due to their belief that “social responsibility… is lacking more than knowledge.“17

Some participants highlighted the role of personal behaviours in building climate competence with the medical schools well-positioned to support habit formation through reinforcement strategies. They noted everyday actions such as recycling items and reducing wastage. Views on climate competence varied, with some considering integration of knowledge into (own) clinical practice as sufficient, while others emphasised the ability to communicate and educate others as an essential skill.

Discussion

This study examined how medical students understand the climate–health nexus and how they situate climate change within their emerging professional identities. Participants demonstrated high awareness of climate-related health risks, including heat-related illness, respiratory and infectious diseases, and widening health inequities. Notably, several participants reported substantial awareness of climate–health issues despite limited formal curricular exposure, suggesting additional influences such as extracurricular activities, self-directed learning, social media, and broader societal discourse [38, 39].

However, this knowledge was not consistently integrated into anticipated clinical roles. Climate change was recognised by participants as relevant to health, yet remained ambiguously positioned within the scope of physician responsibility. While health professionals are increasingly called upon to advocate for sustainability and serve as informed, trusted change agents in addressing climate change [40, 41], this uncertainty about professional boundaries may limit preparedness for climate-responsive practice and development of a climate-ready workforce [32].

Although participants were cognitively and emotionally aware of the clinical implications and scale of the threat, including anxiety and concern for future generations or a sense of overwhelm [30], it did not consistently translate into professional and personal commitment. Participants expressed uncertainty regarding how climate considerations should inform clinical decision, patient communication, and professional priorities. Climate change was relevant but not always framed as a core professional responsibility, suggesting that climate competence is not yet embedded within their developing sense of professional identity.

The structure of medical training reinforced this uncertainty. Climate-health content was described as limited, inconsistently delivered, or absent from formal curricula [31]. In assessment-driven environments, what is taught or examined may signal what matters [42]. The absence or low visibility of climate change and climate-health content may position these topics as supplementary rather than integral to medical work. Medical education shapes norms, priorities, and the perceived scope of practice [43]. Without this integration, medical training may reinforce a narrow view of medicine that overlooks environmental determinants despite their increasing clinical relevance [44].

Perceived distance further limited engagement. Although students acknowledged the seriousness of climate change, many described its effects as geographically or temporally remote and distant [45]. In high-resource settings, especially when not affected by extreme climate events, robust infrastructure and governance may reinforce perceptions of resilience, reducing urgency and deprioritising climate considerations in clinical work [46]. Such perceptions may delay proactive adaptation in both medical education and health systems, even as evidence indicates increasing risks from heat stress, infectious disease shifts, supply chain instability, and climate-related migration [9, 11].

Healthcare’s dual role, as both contributor and responder to climate-health risks was either overlooked or surprising for many students. Some students framed sustainability and patient safety as competing priorities, prioritising immediate clinical outcomes over environmental considerations. Yet, sustainable healthcare is defined by the delivery of high-quality care while minimising environmental harm [32]. This perceived tension may reflect limited exposure to operational models in which clinical excellence and environmental stewardship are aligned. Although medical students did not always recognise their potential role in advancing sustainable healthcare and contributing to systems-level change, such a role is well established in the literature [47], including growing hospital sustainability initiatives worldwide.

Participants also expressed divergent views regarding the physician’s role: some favoured inaction (‘bystanders’), others prioritised integrating climate considerations into patient care (‘integrators’), and a third group advocated for engagement in education and policy (‘advocates’) [48, 49] as per theme 6. These perspectives underscore variability in how climate responsibility is incorporated into professional identity and uncertainty as to whether climate advocacy and sustainable practice fall within the physician’s professional remit.

The ‘integrators’ and ‘advocates’ pushed for longitudinal curricular integration with multiple touch points across curriculum such as adding climate content in core topics including respiratory medicine, infectious diseases, and epidemiology, or the communication skills components [50, 51]. Experiential learning, including engagement with at-risk communities and experts with lived experience, was seen as essential for developing clinical skills and fostering a meaningful emotional connection to climate change [50]. Hands-on exercises and interactive lessons that emphasise the bidirectional climate-health relationship can reinforce knowledge while encouraging sustainable practices in clinical settings [51]. Though some students, especially the ‘bystanders’ who feel that climate change falls outside of their scope of practice, may feel that such content has limited value, studies exhibit that education can conversely shape perspectives [43, 44]. Should the importance of climate-health be conveyed at a foundational stage of students’ education via the appropriate mode of teaching, these ‘bystanders’ could grow to adopt a different perspective towards the issue.

On the topic of the mode of teaching, students emphasised that climate competence requires more than content delivery in a single shot [50, 51]. Foundational knowledge is necessary but insufficient, competence develops through reinforcing influences: integrating climate considerations across clinical disciplines, applying environmental awareness in patient care, and internalising environmental stewardship as part of professional identity. Clinical rotations that expose students to hospital sustainability initiatives, mentorship by role-model physicians, and community engagement were highlighted as critical for reinforcing learning. The Climate Health Curriculum Tool (CHCT) [52] supports this approach, with domains spanning Knowledge for Practice, Systems-Based Practice, and Professionalism. Together, these elements link knowledge, skills, and professional norms, fostering applied competence rather than isolated awareness.

Implications

Synthesis of the themes informed the conceptual framework presented in Fig. 2, which illustrates how climate competence may develop through the interaction of curriculum design, experiential learning, and institutional culture in shaping professional identity formation. This interpretation is consistent with emerging curricular frameworks for planetary health education that emphasise structured and longitudinal integration across training stages [53].

Conceptual framework illustrating how climate competence develops through the interaction of curriculum design, experiential learning, and institutional culture in shaping medical students’ professional identity formation

Participants’ accounts revealed a consistent disconnect between awareness of climate-health issues and their translation into clinical relevance and perceived professional responsibility. Although climate change was recognised as important, it was frequently described as distant and peripheral within medical training because of its limited and inconsistent presence in the formal curriculum and competing academic demands. Participants further emphasised that meaningful engagement with climate-health requires more than knowledge acquisition alone, highlighting the importance of ongoing exposure in clinical settings, role-modelling by senior clinicians and mentors, and reinforcement through institutional practices that normalise sustainability within healthcare environments.

Beyond the curriculum, students advocated for community activities to better reinforce the real-life impacts of climate change on health. Interacting and helping vulnerable communities can help to develop a unified culture amongst medical students through collaborative initiatives [52]. Institutionally, medical schools can develop a culture promoting sustainability and climate awareness to develop student habits through sustainability activities and extracurricular lectures to reinforce curricular learning in daily life. Through developing a parallel student-led and organisational culture, student’s curricular learning can be transformed into a real-life experiential foundation.

In addition to this framework, the authors make the following recommendations for undergraduate medical curricula. Firstly, exposure to physician-led sustainability activities during clinical rotations can help students recognise how clinical care can align with environmental stewardship, reinforcing responsibility and agency in sustainable healthcare practices. Moreover, observation of sustainable healthcare practices in medical institutions through attachments to different stakeholders such as sustainability offices would help to integrate policy-making into the professional roles of future physicians [52]. Furthermore, ethical aspects of climate-health education, such as climate justice or the professional roles and responsibilities of health professionals towards climate change, should ideally be explicitly raised and discussed in the curriculum to encourage students to reflect and foster a deeper understanding of the ethics of planetary health. Developing holistic student professionalism would cement future physicians’ role and identity in combating climate change, allowing the foundational knowledge and experience to effect change on a larger scale to improve population outcomes.

Limitations & future work

Study limitations should be considered. First, this study used online semi-structured interviews, which may have limited rapport-building and reduced access to non-verbal and interactional cues, although the format enabled participation across diverse schedules and facilitated recruitment. Second, self-selection bias is possible, as participation was voluntary and students with interest in climate and health may have been more likely to come forward. Third, although thematic saturation was reached, further research across diverse training settings is needed to examine how these patterns manifest in different institutional and cultural contexts. Fourth, the study was conducted at a single medical school in Singapore. Thus, findings may have limited transferability given variation in curricular structures, health systems, and cultural contexts that may shape perceptions of climate risk and professional responsibility.

Conclusion

This study aimed to understand the perceptions and knowledge of medical students towards climate change and their preparedness to deal with its impact on healthcare in the future as doctors. While students understood climate change and its impacts on health, many felt unprepared to deal with it in the future due to a weak knowledge base from the medical curriculum. Issues in medical students’ beliefs that sustainable healthcare practices oppose patient outcomes and the marginalisation of climate change in the medical curriculum contributed to medical students’ lack of a unified understanding of sustainable healthcare practices and an unwillingness to act for some. Advancing climate competence requires alignment of curriculum, experiential training, and institutional culture in school and at health care settings. Medical schools can reinforce climate responsibility through longitudinal integration of climate–health content, exposure to sustainable clinical practices, mentorship, and visibility of physician-led initiatives, along with the establishment of environmental and sustainability practices in the medical school. Embedding sustainability into hospital operations and clinical rotations signals institutional priorities and normalises climate-responsive practices. Possible future explorations could be in the evaluation of the outcomes of integrating climate change into the medical curriculum and other facets of medical school.
