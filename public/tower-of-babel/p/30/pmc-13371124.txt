Blended community nursing care: towards a new normal? Process evaluation of a large-scale implementation
Authors: N. Kruithof, R. Verwey, E. Duijnstee, M. Breemen, R. de Ritter, L. M. J. Hochstenbach, E. van Rossum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBlended care, combining in-person and digital support through various care technology devices, represents a promising yet underutilized approach in community nursing. Its successful adoption requires major workflow adaptations. Previous studies have highlighted the need to evaluate the usability, feasibility, and effectiveness of implementation strategies for blended care to ensure their successful integration. The aim of this study was to examine the implementation of a comprehensive blended care package, offering around 40 devices across care domains via a single supplier, within a large Dutch community care organization, and explored what healthcare professionals (HCPs) and clients need to optimize its use.MethodsData were collected through observations of implementation activities (four training sessions, five counselling sessions, and four care needs assessments) and semi-structured interviews with eleven HCPs and five clients. Training covered information on blended care and demonstrations of ten devices. Counselling focused on identifying suitable clients and clarifying device use. Data were thematically analysed, guided by the Medical Research Council- and Capability-Opportunity-Motivation model.ResultsHCPs were introduced to various devices, with no opportunity to test devices. In three care needs assessments, nurses and clients showed uncertainty about device functionality. HCPs valued the introductory training and counselling sessions and acknowledged the benefits of blended care, including increased client autonomy. HCPs expressed a preference for testing care technologies before introducing them to clients. Some reported concerns about job loss or unwanted changes in their roles. In general, clients were initially hesitant but ultimately appreciated its effectiveness.ConclusionBlended care was implemented per the organization’s strategy, ensuring HCPs were informed, able to ask questions, and encouraged to engage in discussions. However, insufficient product knowledge and concerns about integrating blended care might have influenced adoption, highlighting the need for improved knowledge transfer and implementation support. Guidelines may support healthcare organizations and HCPs in facilitating successful implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04794-7.

Introduction/background

Due to the ageing population and the Dutch policy to prevent or postpone hospital or nursing home admission as much as possible, the demand for community care is increasing. However, the availability of nursing staff is declining. The Dutch Comprehensive Care Agreement highlights the use of eHealth to maintain high-quality and affordable care [1]. Blended care, which combines traditional face-to-face care with home care technology, is promising. Commonly used care technologies include video calling, medicine dispensers, remote monitoring systems, and tools for supporting daily structure (2).

Despite its potential and the wide range of available home care technologies, their adoption remains limited within community nursing practices [2]. The transition to blended care, also known as hybrid care, requires significant adjustments in community nursing care practices and impacts collaboration within the care chain [3]. It demands organizational and procedural changes, as well as an appropriate ICT infrastructure and funding [4]. Embedding blended care in daily practice requires a cultural shift in both attitudes and behaviors among healthcare organizations, healthcare professionals (HCPs), and clients [5].

Prior to implementing blended care, it is crucial to assess the compatibility of care technologies with: (1) the living environment and clients’ self-management abilities; (2) their impact on relationships with HCPs, informal caregivers, and relatives; and (3) the influence of monitoring functions on all stakeholders involved [6]. Ross et al. (2016) highlight the importance of carefully selecting digital applications, taking into account factors such as complexity, user adaptability, system compatibility, and costs. They stress the importance of thorough implementation planning, which should involve organizational preparation, the appointment of ambassadors, and adequate training. Furthermore, continuous monitoring, evaluation, and post-implementation adoption are considered essential to ensuring the achievement of the intended outcomes [3].

Evaluating the implementation process of care technology applications, along with the intended outcomes of these applications, is crucial. In their knowledge synthesis, Vis et al. conclude that there is a lack of empirical evidence regarding the usability, feasibility and effectiveness of implementation strategies. Therefore, they recommend detailed reporting of these strategies and work plans to address this gap [4].

Most studies to date have focused on single care technology applications, whereas blended care encompasses the integration of multiple care technologies. Additionally, there is a lack of comprehensive understanding regarding how clients, informal caregivers/relatives, and HCPs adapt to a reduction in physical care interactions.

In the Netherlands, a limited number of community care organizations, in collaboration with a large health insurer, have implemented Virtual Home Care© (VirtueleThuiszorg©, VHC) [5], including MeanderGroep Zuid-Limburg (MGZL). Virtual Home Care© incorporates approximately 40 care technology applications designed for monitoring bodily functions (e.g., weight, blood pressure, and saturation), alarm systems/supervision (e.g., infrared fall detection), support of Activities of Daily Living (ADL) (e.g., electric donning aid for compression stockings), lifestyle management/well-being (e.g., online exercise and welfare coaching), and support for daily structure (e.g., digital agenda with reminders and medicine dispenser). These applications aim to maintain client self-management and autonomy, thereby facilitating prolonged independent living at home. For HCPs, VHC aims to alleviate workload in a responsible manner while ensuring that the quality of care remains uncompromised.

In parallel with this implementation, this study was initiated to establish a ‘learning practice’ involving all participating partners. The aim of this study was to systematically examine the innovation process, in order to support the transformation of blended care into a standard practice within community care. By exploring the evolution of the VHC implementation within a large community care organization, this study aimed to identify experienced challenges and knowledge gaps, as well as the needs of HCPs and clients to optimize the use of blended care.

Methods

From April until November 2024, a process evaluation, using multiple qualitative methods, was conducted on the implementation of blended care at MGZL, a large community care organisation in the Netherlands. Thirteen non-participating observations, eleven semi-structured interviews with HCPs, and five semi-structured interviews with clients were conducted.

Setting

In the Netherlands, GP practices and community care organizations constitute the primary teams responsible for providing care to dependent individuals living at home [7]. Dutch community care encompasses personal care (e.g., assistance with ADL, such as washing and dressing), nursing care (e.g., medical tasks like wound care), and domestic support (e.g., assistance, such as laundry and cleaning) [8, 9]. Personal and nursing care are delivered by community care organizations via community care teams [9, 10]. The community nurse (usually with a bachelor’s degree) leads the team, coordinating and organizing the care provided by its members, who may include bachelor- or vocational-trained registered nurses, certified nurse assistants, and helping aides. Additionally, the community nurse conducts care needs assessments to determine the most appropriate care for clients [11].

Setting of data collection

VHC provides a structured and integrated approach as a single provider of care technology, facilitating the efficient delivery of all requested technologies. In addition to supplying these technologies, VHC oversees their installation, performs regular maintenance, and addresses any technical issues to ensure sustained functionality and reliability.

Blended care was implemented in 84 community care teams of MGZL (including 12–15 members per team) from November 2021 to August 2024 (with a lead time of three months per team). Implementation steps per community care team are described in Fig. 1 [12]. All community team members participated in the implementation activities. Each member was expected to be able to apply blended care to both existing and new clients after completing the implementation activities.

This study examined the implementation activities primarily developed by the community care organization [12], including mandatory introductory training sessions, counselling sessions, and care needs assessments. Implementation activities were analyzed in four district teams within a single region. The three-hour introductory training session was mandatory for each community care team member. The training was conducted by a specially appointed nurse from MGZL, followed by a demonstration of a selection of 15 devices by a representative of Virtual Home Care©. Shortly after this introductory training session, designated key users from each community care team, along with a trainer from MGZL, conducted a 1.5-hour counselling session with their own team members to discuss all clients of the team who could potentially benefit from blended care. During the subsequent care needs assessments, HCPs shared and discussed the advice formulated during these counselling sessions with their clients. During the implementation of blended care, the community care organization later added a dashboard, success sessions, and booster trainings for the community care teams (see Fig. 1). These additional implementation activities were not included in this study.

Participant selection

Community team members (i.e., nurses, nurse assistants, and helping aids) of MGZL, as well as members of a GP practice frequently collaborating with MGZL, were eligible to participate. For the semi-structured interviews, community team members, members of the GP practice and clients were recruited by project staff of MGZL and the GP practice. Project staff were instructed to select team members ranging from those with limited to extensive digital skills and/or experiences with blended care, and new clients and clients who had already been receiving care from MGZL. Participants were recruited from various teams. In certain teams, blended care had been implemented some time earlier, whereas in other teams, blended care was in the process of being implemented during the study period. All participants had sufficient knowledge of the Dutch language.

Ethical approval and informed consent

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee Zuyderland Medical Center - Zuyd (the Netherlands), project number METCZ20240006. All participants provided written informed consent.

Data collection observations and interviews

Observations and interviews were conducted by researchers with backgrounds in health sciences (NK, RV, ED, MB, PE). None of the researchers were part of the participating community care teams or involved in the direct care delivery of the community care teams. To gain a comprehensive insight into the implementation activities, non-participating observations were conducted. For this purpose, semi-structured observation forms based on the Medical Research Council (MRC) model and the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation (COM-B) model were used. The MRC-model maps the implementation, contextual factors (facilitators and barriers) and changing mechanisms [13]. The COM-B model proposes three necessary components for any behavior to occur, i.e., capability (C), opportunity (O), and motivation (M) [14]. During the four 3 h introductory training sessions and the five 1,5 h counselling sessions, the following aspects were observed: 1) content and type of trainers’ information (e.g., clinical reasoning processes, cooperation with other HCPs, facilitating and limiting factors of the use of blended care), and 2) HCPs’ reactions and input. Each introductory training session and counselling session was observed by two independent researchers (NK, RV, ED, MB and PE). During the four care needs assessments, the following aspects were observed: 1) HCPs’ clinical reasoning process, 2) HCPs’ provided information about blended care, 3) HCP’ interaction with the patient, and 4) patients’ reaction and input. Care needs assessments were observed by one researcher (RV, NK, and ED).

Eleven semi-structured interviews with HCPs, each lasting 30–45 min, were conducted by RV, MB, ED, and NK. To increase the inclusion rate, HCPs were able to choose whether the interview was conducted face-to-face or online (Microsoft Teams). Interviews with clients were face-to-face. Audio recordings were made, and to guarantee the confidentiality of the participants, identifying features from the transcripts were removed. Two topic guides were developed, one guide for HCPs and one guide for clients. The interview guides (see Supplementary file 1) used in this study were specifically developed by the authors (RV and NK) for the purpose of this research. Topics discussed with HCPs included: (1) ‘How digital-minded are you?’, (2) ‘Why and how do you decide whether blended care is indicated for your client?’, (3) ‘What are your experiences with blended care?’, (4) ‘Which implementation strategies are useful to increase the use of blended care?’, and (5) ‘Describe the collaboration with other healthcare organizations while using blended care’. Topics discussed with the five included clients included: (1) ‘How digital-minded are you?’, (2) ‘Which technical device(s) was/were suggested by your nurse? Can you explain why this/these device(s) was/were suggested?’, (3) ‘How did the community nurse convince you (or not) to use blended care?’, (4) ‘What is your experience with blended care?’, and (5) ‘Which type of support do clients need when their nurse proposes blended care?’

Data analysis

Data analysis followed an iterative and interpretative thematic approach. Data from the observations and semi-structured interviews were analyzed using thematic analysis in Atlas.ti (version 24) [15]. Data was coded and thematized based on predefined themes of the MRC and COM-B models. There was also room for new themes that emerged from the data.

Observations: RV, NK, ED, and PE read and analyzed the data. Summaries were written (PE, RV) and checked by a counter-reader (NK, ED) and verified by three nurses of MGZL in charge of the implementation activities.

Interviews: Audio-recorded data were transcribed verbatim. Prior to the data analysis, transcripts were sent to the participants to check data accuracy [16]. Deductive analysis [17] was conducted. ED & NK and MB & RV read and analyzed the data from the HCPs and clients, respectively. The authors manually coded the transcripts. By using the ‘AI summaries’ tool in Atlas.ti, summaries of the codes were made. These AI summaries were manually checked for correctness and completeness. Codes were manually combined into themes. Any discrepancies in codes or themes were resolved through discussion with a third researcher: NK for the HCPs’ data and RV for the clients’ data. Finally, to support the identified codes and themes, representative quotes from the semi-structured interviews were manually selected.

Results

This section first presents the characteristics of the participants. Next, the results of the observations are described, followed by the findings from the semi-structured interviews. The identified themes and deductive codes can be found in the Supplementary file 2.

Characteristics of the participants

Thirteen observations of implementation activities involving various community care HCPs were conducted. Eleven HCPs from the community care organization and the GP practice were interviewed. Among them were eight community care workers and three professionals from the GP practice, including one general practitioner and two nurse specialists. Five clients were interviewed. The median age (IQR) of the HCPs (82% female) and clients (80% female) was, respectively, 39 (IQR: 30–56) and 85 (IQR: 71–86).

Results of the observations

Mandatory introductory training sessions

Introductory training sessions were observed in which 7–23 members of the community care organization participated. The value of blended care for both client and HCP were discussed, supported by illustrative examples. HCPs were unable to test the devices themselves. It was emphasized that physical and digital care can complement each other. Blended care initially requires a time investment from HCPs and saves time later. HCPs received instructions regarding: (1) the insurance compensation of the care technology devices, (2) the procedure for requesting a care technology device (HCPs had to complete a questionnaire within the electronic health record of the client), and (3) the steps to incorporate the use of blended care into the care plan. HCPs responded to blended care in a mixed way, with some recognizing its potential benefits in enhancing client self-management and reducing administrative burdens for HCPs. Several HCPs expressed concerns that blended care might replace traditional care (impairment of work/work experience).

Counselling sessions

Five counselling sessions only for the community team members of MGZL and one counselling session with community team members and nurse specialists working in a GP practice were observed. In the counselling sessions, 5–8 team members per team participated. It was observed that the HCPs were encouraged to think aloud together regarding the potential use of blended care for each client. There was a considerable variation in the aspects addressed by the HCPs during this selection process. The client was discussed based on the clinical characteristics, the care problems, the provided care, the personal characteristics (e.g., digital skills) and the context, including the presence of informal caregivers.

Significant confusion persisted regarding the various care technology devices. The counselling sessions were also utilized by the trainer to provide further clarification and detailed explanations about the devices, supplemented by examples and the sharing of both successful and less successful experiences with blended care. HCPs saw no added value of blended care in the following situations: (1) for clients with serious psychosocial problems, (2) in situations where physical care cannot be phased out (e.g., combination of showering and weighing), (3) when there is no digital option for physical care (e.g., wound care) and (4) when care for the client will stop in the foreseeable future (e.g., admission to a nursing home). When the use of blended care would result in a reduction of physical care, some HCPs expected to miss crucial contact moments, particularly with care avoiders, clients in highly complex situations, or those who were highly valued by them. Supporting informal caregivers through blended care was hardly discussed during the counselling sessions. Collaboration with other disciplines was also scarcely addressed, except for the counselling session involving community team members and nurse specialists working in the GP practice. During the counselling session between the community team members and the nurse specialists, the reimbursements for the care technologies were discussed.

Care needs assessments

In all four of the observed care needs assessments, the client had already been receiving care for an extended period. At two care needs assessments, an informal caregiver was also present, and it was observed that the nurses primarily directed their attention towards the informal caregiver. The following care technology devices were discussed: medication reminders, tools for supporting daily structure, systems for monitoring vital signs, and devices for ensuring safety and fall detection. During two care needs assessments, the devices were requested; in one care needs assessment, no device was requested; and following another care needs assessment, the client was considering using the device. Arguments for choosing the care technology device primarily focused on enhancing client safety by providing increased supervision. Reasons for not choosing the care technology device included, among others, general distrust of technology by the client and their informal caregivers, concerns about potential privacy infringements, an increased burden on informal caregivers (in the case of social alarms), aesthetic considerations (wearing an alarm device), and the preference for a more attractive consumer eHealth alternative (Apple Watch) instead of the care watch from the VHC range.

In three out of four care needs assessments, clients and nurses still appeared to be unclear about operating the device. In one care needs assessment, the supplier’s website was consulted. Nurses did not adhere to a standardized structure when discussing their recommendations for utilizing a care technology device (explanation of how digital intervention could fit within the care plan). In two care needs assessments, it was observed that nurses attempted to persuade their clients by highlighting positive experiences reported by other clients. Nurses also reassured their clients that the care technology device could be safely returned after a trial period.

Several findings from the observations were corroborated by the results of the semi-structured interviews. Concerns were expressed by HCPs regarding potential job loss (n = 2) and unwanted job changes (n = 2). HCPs emphasized the high value of the counselling sessions, which clarified the rationale and application of blended care. They appreciated the trainers’ concrete examples and shared experiences. The results of the semi-structured interviews also confirmed that assessing the suitability of blended care requires consideration of client-specific factors such as diseases, sensory impairments, and preferences of the client and the informal caregiver.

Facilitating factors of the implementation and use of blended care: All HCPs reported generally positive experiences with the mandatory introductory training session. Three of the eight HCPs (that followed the training session) indicated that this training session significantly enhanced their digital skills in using care technology. Colleague key users played a crucial role during the implementation of blended care. HCPs emphasized the importance of staying well-informed about new care technologies. Several HCPs also noted the need for (more) refresher sessions and e-learnings.

HCPs and clients reported various benefits of blended care, such as increased clients’ autonomy and self-management, and a reduction in HCPs’ physical demands and administrative burden (e.g., by the use of a medicine dispenser or glucose meter):…. with a medicine dispenser, we don’t need to visit (a client) five times a day, but instead, we can visit once a week. HCPs agreed that it is essential to acknowledge and discuss the challenges of blended care among colleagues. Almost all clients (n = 4) were satisfied with the information provided during the care needs assessments. Two clients suggested a paper manual for the device. Additionally, clients would like to receive a demonstration of the care technology and would like to utilize peer support from clients already using the proposed care technology. When introducing blended care to a client, it is important that HCPs clearly highlight its benefits:(…) maintaining the client’s independence is crucial. … I strongly advocate for clients to try to do as much as possible independently for as long as possible. This is definitely one of the advantages of blended care. HCP05.

Four HCPs reported that clients are often initially hesitant about blended care but are generally pleasantly surprised by its effectiveness. According to them, informing informal caregivers and relatives about blended care is crucial, as they can persuade clients to use it. Blended care can relieve informal caregivers (e.g., by using a fall detection system) and provide them with more insight into the client’s health status (e.g., glucose monitoring). However, informal caregivers or relatives also need to support the client with the device (e.g., charging a device or serving as a backup in case of a detected fall):(…) devices often need to be operated by family members, and the responsibility for backup frequently falls on family members or clients’ social network. Hindering factors of the implementation and use of blended care: HCPs indicated that hesitation to adopt blended care, often due to limited digital skills or insufficient knowledge of care technology, makes it less likely that they will introduce it to clients. Some HCPs stated that some clients did not perceive the value of blended care or were afraid to engage with the technology:They (i.e., clients) don’t want it. Often, …. they feel that it doesn’t provide any added value, is too much hassle, and they are not comfortable with technology. HCP02.

HCPs reported an increase in workload during the implementation of blended care due to additional activities such as extra care needs assessments, requesting care technologies, and providing instructions about the care technology to clients (and informal caregivers).

When blended care was initiated for a client, it was not always clear which HCP should request the care technology. Blended care depended on the client’s insurance coverage, as not all health insurance plans reimbursed the care technologies. Besides, there was often ambiguity regarding whom HCPs should report device malfunctions. Last, there was uncertainty about how HCPs within the network should keep each other informed regarding the use of blended care for a specific client.

Although four out of five clients were satisfied with the use of blended care in their daily life, all clients experienced hindering factors, such as becoming dependent on care technology (n = 3), losing human contact (n = 3), reduction or absence of (according to clients) necessary physical care moments (n = 3), fear or incorrect use of care technology (n = 2) and privacy concerns (n = 2). Several clients (n = 3) mentioned that the care technology was implemented at the insistence of their HCP or their informal caregivers.

Both HCPs and clients indicated that it is sometimes difficult to completely trust care technology. Concerns arose from both groups about what happens if the care technology fails (e.g., when a table obstructs the infrared line of a fall detection system, preventing an alarm from being triggered).

Discussion

This process evaluation provided insights into a large-scale implementation strategy of blended care within a large, Dutch community care organization and examined the needs of HCPs and clients to enhance the effective implementation and utilization of blended care. An important finding from the observations was the absence of a standardized procedure for introducing blended care to clients. The semi-structured interviews revealed that blended care has the potential to enhance clients’ autonomy and alleviate HCPs’ workload.

Blended care was implemented in line with the community care organization’s implementation strategy. HCPs were informed about the purpose and necessity of blended care, given opportunities to ask questions, and encouraged to engage in collaborative discussions about its application. While HCPs generally expressed satisfaction with the implementation activities, they highlighted the lack of hands-on testing of the devices. Insufficient product knowledge posed a significant challenge during implementation, as introducing 40 care technologies within the three-hour timeframe of the introductory session proved impractical. Furthermore, some HCPs faced challenges in integrating blended care into their daily practice due to concerns, such as technological complexity. Consequently, the knowledge transfer was suboptimal, indicating room for improvement in the implementation strategy.

Karnehed et al. stress the importance of evaluating the impact of specific care technology services on the work of HCPs, focusing on how these services influence actions and priorities at both individual and organizational levels [18]. To understand how the implementation strategy facilitated behavioral change, it is essential to examine HCPs’ capability (consisting of knowledge and skills), opportunity and motivation, as outlined in the COM-B model [16]. Although most of the HCPs expressed a positive opinion about blended care, altering their behavior often proved challenging. A willingness to change could have been hindered by concerns about job displacement, role shifts, or negative expectations related to blended care. This aligns with previous findings that highlight the difficulties associated with adopting care technologies [19–21].

Counselling sessions played a pivotal role by emphasizing the practical aspects of blended care, such as practical examples and tips, sharing experiences, think-aloud discussions, and sharing success stories. Learning through examples, feedback, and reflection has been recognized as an effective approach to promoting behavior change [22, 23]. During care needs assessments, it became apparent that integrating knowledge regarding the devices into practice posed difficulties. Consequently, the counselling sessions largely facilitated the implementation of blended care.

The implementation of blended care primarily emphasized technological advancements, often placing less direct attention on the integration and optimization of care processes that these innovations are designed to enhance. While technology has significant potential in healthcare, its value ultimately depends on achieving an optimal balance between technological innovation and the embedding of these tools within nursing practice. Results showed that HCPs and clients expressed uncertainty regarding their motivation to engage with blended care, regardless of their prior training or experience with blended care. The analyses of both the observations and the interviews revealed that key aspects of blended care implementation, such as structured introductions to clients and shared decision-making regarding care technologies, were scarcely addressed. Furthermore, the results of this study underscore that the decision to use blended care depends on the unique characteristics and individual needs of each client. Blended care is not universally suitable for all clients, such as those experiencing severe psychological issues. This finding is in line with previous research, which also reported that not all clients are suitable candidates for blended care [24]. During the evaluation of whether blended care was appropriate for specific clients, HCPs in our study demonstrated limited use of clinical reasoning processes. These findings highlight critical areas for improvement in achieving the effective integration of blended care into clinical practice.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. By using the MRC and the COM-B models, it provides a thorough understanding of the implementation process, mechanisms of impact, and contextual factors influencing behavior changes. Different data collection methods were used to verify findings; interview data confirmed the observation data, leading to data triangulation [25]. All data were analyzed by two independent researchers, which enhanced the trustworthiness of the findings [25]. However, there are limitations, such as selection bias in participant recruitment: digitally skilled HCPs and motivated clients were probably overrepresented in the interviews. This may have influenced perceptions of the implementation strategy and the use of blended care in daily practice. Furthermore, another limitation is the limited number of observations, which may not fully encompass the entire implementation context. As a result, the findings derived from the observations should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, many of the patterns identified in the observations were corroborated through the semi-structured interviews with HCPs. Finally, care needs assessments were observed by a single researcher. This was a deliberate choice since the presence of a second researcher could be perceived as too intrusive for clients. We believe that this approach did not significantly influence the results.

Implications for practice

The implementation strategy of multiple care technologies differs significantly from that of a single care technology due to factors such as complexity, scaling, and integration. Incorporating multiple technologies into existing care processes and IT systems poses greater challenges, requiring a well-structured demand-driven approach. Insights gained from this study highlight key areas for improvement in the large-scale integration of care technologies. First, HCPs must be well-informed about the available range of care technologies and should have the opportunity to test care technologies before introducing them to clients. Decision-support tools can facilitate this process. Second, sufficient time must be allocated for HCPs to integrate blended care into their daily practices, as implementing blended care demands additional time and effort. Third, resistance from HCPs towards blended care should be addressed, as its implementation may raise concerns about job displacement or unwelcome changes in work roles. Fourth, HCPs should be equipped with effective communication strategies to introduce blended care to clients and actively involve informal caregivers, whose support can improve acceptance and utilization of blended care.

For a successful implementation, future research should focus on better understanding of the motivational factors influencing HCPs’ and clients’ engagement with blended care. Additionally, it is essential to develop a practical guideline that serves as a valuable tool in addressing identified challenges and providing support for their solution. This guideline should incorporate essential competencies, including communication techniques, shared decision-making and clinical reasoning skills specific to blended care. Additionally, this guideline should provide clear instructions at both the organizational and HCP level, along with practical tools tailored to the specific needs of HCPs.

Conclusion

The process evaluation highlights key insights into the implementation of blended care within a large Dutch community care organization, revealing both successes and areas for improvement. The findings demonstrate that blended care was successfully introduced; however, the implementation was characterized by insufficient product knowledge, uncertainty regarding (changing) roles, and challenges related to HCPs and clients’ motivation using blended care. These challenges should be addressed in future implementations to enhance the adoption of blended care in other health care organizations. To facilitate this, a comprehensive guideline might be useful to support healthcare organizations and HCPs in the effective utilization of blended care.
