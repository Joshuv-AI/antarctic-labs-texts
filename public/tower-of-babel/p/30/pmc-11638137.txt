Association of Combined Autoreactivity to Sm/RNP Common Motif and U1 RNP With Mixed Connective Tissue Disease and Systemic Lupus Erythematosus
Authors: Ruoning Ni, Aleksander Lenert, Petar Lenert
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the clinical features in patients with suspected connective tissue disease who tested positive for anti‐Sm/RNP common motif antibody with or without associated anti‐RNP antibody.MethodsThe titers of anti‐Sm/RNP and anti‐RNP antibodies were measured by the multiplex solid‐phase bioassays (Bio‐Rad). Clinical manifestations were compared among the three subgroups (RNP only, Sm/RNP only, and double positive for RNP and Sm/RNP). Patients were further evaluated for the diagnosis of mixed connective tissue disease (MCTD) and/or systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) using accepted classification criteria.ResultsA total of 133 patients were included in this study. The rates of inflammatory arthritis and Raynaud phenomenon were significantly higher in patients testing positive for both anti‐RNP and anti‐Sm/RNP antibodies compared to anti‐RNP only or anti‐Sm/RNP only (69.1% vs 28.8% vs 25.0%, P < 0.0001 for arthritis and 59.5% vs 23.3% vs 37.5%, P = 0.0005 for Raynaud phenomenon). Area under the curve (AUC) values were 0.68 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.59–0.77, P < 0.0001) for anti‐Sm/RNP titers and 0.65 (95% CI 0.55–0.74, P = 0.0039) for anti‐RNP titers with inflammatory arthritis. AUC values were 0.67 (95% CI 0.58–0.77, P = 0.0002) for anti‐Sm/RNP titers and 0.59 (95% CI 0.49–0.69, P = 0.0352) for anti‐RNP titers with Raynaud phenomenon. The odds ratios for the diagnosis of MCTD and SLE were significantly higher in patients with double positivity compared to those testing solely positive for anti‐RNP antibody.ConclusionAnti‐Sm/RNP common motif autoreactivity when combined with anti‐RNP antibody positivity identifies those patients who are closely related with certain clinical manifestations and who are associated with well‐defined connective tissue disease such as MCTD or SLE.

INTRODUCTION

The Sm was the first extractable nuclear autoantigen characterized in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). 1 This was followed by the discovery of antibodies reacting with the U1 RNP complex as a target. 2 High titers of U1 RNP antibodies in patients who tested negative for the Sm antibody were initially linked to a clinical syndrome characterized by a combination of features seen in SLE, scleroderma, and polymyositis. These patients showed excellent response to glucocorticoid therapy. Sharp et al 2 proposed the term mixed connective tissue disease (MCTD). It was initially believed that these patients rarely develop renal involvement and have a favorable prognosis. [Correction added on 09 October 2024, after first online publication: The Sm RNP has been corrected to The Sm.]. However, this assumption was later challenged by several groups. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Despite some common features between RNP and Sm/RNP antigens, they also carry significant structural differences. The Sm antigen consists of an immunoreactive 14‐kd (D) protein. The Sm/RNP complex, however, contains both the 14‐kd protein and a 68‐kd reactive protein along with its possible degradation fragments. 9 Anti‐Sm antibodies frequently target B/B′, D1, and D3 proteins, whereas anti‐RNP antibodies are directed toward 70‐kd, A, and C proteins. 10

In addition to MCTD, anti‐RNP antibodies and antibodies targeting the Sm/RNP common motif have also been identified in patients with SLE and occasionally in other connective tissue diseases. 11 When anti‐RNP antibodies present alone in patients with MCTD, their presence tends to correlate with the Raynaud phenomenon and with relatively rare renal involvement. 12 However, the presence of anti‐RNP antibodies does not reliably predict the full spectrum of disease manifestations or its severity. 13 Furthermore, epitope spreading can occur in MCTD and may lead to changes in clinical phenotype over time. This phenomenon can complicate the clinical picture and make it more difficult to predict disease progression based on the initial antibody profile. 7 , 14 The clinical course of patients with anti‐Sm/RNP and anti‐RNP antibodies could be highly variable. At initial presentation, a portion of patients may present with high titers of these antibodies without fulfilling the criteria for a definite connective tissue disease, and their disease may evolve over time. 15 This variability underscores the limitations of using antibody presence alone as a prognostic tool.

Rheumatologists occasionally encounter patients with positive anti‐Sm/RNP common motif antibodies with or without associated anti‐RNP or anti‐Sm antibodies in different clinical scenarios. The clinical phenotype of patients presenting solely with anti‐Sm/RNP antibodies or in association with anti‐RNP antibodies remains largely unknown. The primary objective of our study was to evaluate the clinical significance of anti‐Sm/RNP antibodies with or without anti‐RNP positivity in patients with suspected connective tissue disease.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study patients

This was a cross‐sectional unmatched comparison study. This study included all patients with suspected connective tissue disease referred to the University of Iowa Rheumatology Clinic from January 1, 1995, to August 8, 2023, who tested positive for anti‐Sm/RNP common motif and/or anti‐RNP antibodies. All referred patients underwent detailed clinical evaluation, with documented clinical manifestations, including presence or absence of skin rash, mucositis, sicca symptoms, inflammatory arthritis, pleuritis, pericarditis, serositis, Raynaud phenomenon, sclerodactyly, myositis, interstitial lung disease, pulmonary hypertension, renal involvement, neurologic involvement, thrombosis, and miscarriage. Clinical manifestations are defined in Supplemental Table 1. The total symptom score per patient was defined as all the aforementioned manifestations divided by the number of patients. All cumulative data were retrieved from the existing medical records available through the electronic medical record database. Data analysis included demographic characteristics, clinical manifestations, organ involvement, disease duration, and antibody reactivity. This retrospective cross‐sectional study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Iowa Hospitals and Clinics (approval no. 202003670). Consent was waived because this was a retrospective chart review without intervention.

Quantification of anti‐Sm/RNP common motif antibody and anti‐RNP antibody

Blood samples were obtained from patients, and the antibody profile was analyzed at the time of diagnosis. The anti‐Sm/RNP common motif antibody, anti‐RNP antibody, and anti‐Sm antibody were measured by multiplex assays (BioPlex 2200 System, BioRad Laboratories). Per the manufacturer's instructions, the Sm/RNP common motif antibody (BioPlex) detects antibodies against Sm and RNP epitopes at the junction between Sm and RNP antigens. This antibody can be seen in patients with MCTD and SLE. The Sm/RNP assay is reactive to homogenous and rim, speckled patterns, U1 RNP, and Sm antigen in the control serum. The RNP assay shows reactivity to speckled pattern, U1 RNP, and Sm antigen. Lastly, the Sm assay is reactive to serum with homogenous and rim, speckled patterns, and Sm antigen.

Diagnostic and classification criteria for MCTD and SLE

Four sets of diagnostic criteria were used for diagnosing MCTD. Those criteria were from Sharp, 16 Kasukawa et al, 17 Alarcon‐Segovia and Villarreal, 18 and Kahn and Appelboom. 19 For SLE, we used the most recent 2019 SLE classification criteria from the EULAR and American College of Rheumatology (ACR). 20

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism version 10.2.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, www.graphpad.com). Continuous variables were expressed as means ± SD, and categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Differences between groups were evaluated with Student's t‐test or the Mann‐Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. We determined unadjusted odds ratios (ORs) and calculated sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and likelihood ratios (with 95% confidence intervals [CIs]) to assess the association between antibody profile and specific connective tissue disease diagnosis. Subsequently, an adjusted OR was calculated from multivariate logistic regression adjusting for key variables of interest (age, sex, and self‐reported ancestry or ethnicity). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were created to evaluate the power of detecting inflammatory arthritis and Raynaud phenomenon using anti‐Sm/RNP antibody titers and anti‐RNP antibody titers. Area under the curve (AUC) values with 95% CIs were calculated. A P value less than 0.05 was considered significant.

RESULTS

Study participants

One hundred thirty‐nine patients in total were tested for anti‐Sm/RNP common motif, anti‐RNP, and anti‐Sm antibodies. Among them, 133 of 139 (95.7%) were included in this study, and 6 patients (4.3%) who tested positive for anti‐Sm antibody were excluded. Among the patients with positive anti‐Sm antibody, four of six fulfilled the classification criteria of SLE. Of the 133 patients included, all were negative for anti‐Sm antibodies (Supplementary Figure 1).

Table 1 shows the demographic and clinical characteristics of the 133 patients. The mean ± SD age of the patients at diagnosis was 41.7 ± 17.7 years, and 91.7% were female. Overwhelmingly, the patients self‐identified as White (81.2%), 9.0% self‐identified as Black, 3.8% self‐identified as Asian, and 3.8% self‐identified as having Hispanic ethnicity. Interestingly, the White populations accounted for only 68.2% (30 of 44) of patients who tested positive for both anti‐Sm/RNP common motif antibody and anti‐RNP antibody (double‐positive group), which was a significantly lower percentage compared to the isolated anti‐Sm/RNP‐positive subgroup (100%, 16 of 16, P = 0.0126) and the isolated anti‐RNP‐positive subgroup (84.9%, 62 of 73, P = 0.0384). The Black population represented 22.7% (10 of 44) in the double positivity subgroup, which was a significantly higher prevalence compared with the isolated anti‐Sm/RNP‐positive subgroup (0%, P = 0.0495) and the isolated anti‐RNP‐positive subgroup (2.7%, P = 0.0009). There were no racial differences between the isolated anti‐Sm/RNP‐positive and isolated anti‐RNP‐positive subgroups. The disease duration was not significantly different between the double‐positive group and the anti‐Sm/RNP‐positive subgroup (mean ± SD 2,959 ± 2,119 days vs 2,454 ± 1,646 days, P = 0.9334); nor was it significantly different between the double‐positive group and the anti‐RNP‐positive subgroup (mean ± SD 2,959 ± 2,119 days vs 2,193 ± 1,689 days, P = 0.1088).

Clinical characteristics

Among all different subgroups, the most common clinical manifestation was inflammatory arthritis in 42.1% (56 of 133) of all patients, followed by Raynaud phenomenon in 36.8% (49 of 133), sicca symptoms in 33.8% (45 of 133), and skin rash in 19.5% (26 of 133). Compared to the isolated anti‐Sm/RNP or anti‐RNP antibody subgroup, patients with double positivity presented with statistically significant inflammatory arthritis (25.0% vs 28.8% vs 70.5%, P < 0.0001) and Raynaud phenomenon (37.5% vs 23.3% vs 59.1%, P = 0.0005).

Patients who tested positive for anti‐Sm/RNP (with or without anti‐RNP; Supplementary Table 2.1) were more likely to have inflammatory arthritis if they were double positive compared to patients with isolated Sm/RNP positivity (70.5% vs 25%, P = 0.0042). However, these two subgroups showed no statistical difference in incidence of Raynaud phenomenon (59.1% vs 37.5%, P = 0.1569), skin rash, mucositis, sicca symptoms, serositis, sclerodactyly, myositis, interstitial lung disease, pulmonary hypertension, renal involvement, neurologic disease, thrombosis, or miscarriages. The total symptom score per patient was higher in the double‐positive subgroup (3.3 vs 2.2, P = 0.0224) (Supplementary Table 2.1).

Among patients with anti‐RNP antibody positivity, the double‐positive subgroup compared to the isolated RNP‐only subgroup demonstrated higher rates of skin rash (29.5% vs 12.3%, P = 0.0281), sicca symptoms (45.5% vs 28.8%, P = 0.0465), inflammatory arthritis (70.5% vs 28.8%, P < 0.0001), and Raynaud phenomenon (59.1% vs 23.3%, P = 0.0001). The total symptom score per patient was also statistically higher in the double‐positive subgroup than in the isolated anti‐RNP‐positive subgroup (3.3 vs 1.5, P < 0.0001) (Supplementary Table 2.2). Because of a relatively small number of patients testing positive for isolated anti‐Sm/RNP common motif antibody, when isolated anti‐Sm/RNP‐ and anti‐RNP‐positive subgroups were compared, no significant difference in clinical manifestations was observed, although there was a trend toward a higher total symptom score per patient in the isolated anti‐Sm/RNP subgroup (2.2 vs 1.5, P = 0.0616) (Supplementary Table 2.3).

Comparing patients testing positive for anti‐Sm/RNP (with or without positive anti‐RNP reactivity) to those who tested only positive for the anti‐RNP antibody, racial difference was observed between the subgroups. The Black population was more prevalent in the anti‐Sm/RNP subgroup (16.7% vs 2.7%, P = 0.0063). Patients in this subgroup also presented with higher prevalence of skin rash (28.3% vs 12.3%, P = 0.0206), inflammatory arthritis (58.3% vs 28.8%, P = 0.0006), Raynaud phenomenon (53.3% vs 23.3%, P = 0.0004), and renal involvement (15.0% vs 2.7%, P = 0.0227) and had a higher total symptom score per patient (3.0 vs 1.5, P < 0.0001) (Supplementary Table 2.4).

Association of antibody profiles with MCTD and SLE

The four different sets of diagnostic criteria for MCTD were compared between the double positive and isolated anti‐RNP‐positive subgroups (Table 2). As shown in Table 3, double positivity compared with isolated anti‐RNP positivity was associated with increased odds of MCTD when the criteria by either Sharp, 16 Kasukawa et al, 17 or Kahn and Appelboom 19 were used (unadjusted ORs were 5.1, P = 0.0053; 3.54, P = 0.0186; and 7.86, P < 0.0001, respectively). When Alarcon‐Segovia and Villarreal's18 criteria were used, the unadjusted OR was not significant (P = 0.1961). The statistical significance remained after step‐up false discovery rate control 21 (Supplementary Table 4.1). In multivariate logistic regression analyses, the adjusted OR for the diagnosis of MCTD (by any criteria) was 7.05 (P < 0.0001) in the double‐positive group compared with the isolated anti‐RNP‐positive group (Table 4 and Supplementary Table 3.1).

The 2019 EULAR/ACR classification criteria 20 for SLE were then used to compare patients with double positivity, isolated anti‐Sm/RNP positivity, or isolated anti‐RNP positivity (Table 5). Notably, there was no significant statistical difference in the positivity of anti–double‐stranded DNA (anti‐dsDNA) or low C3 or C4 levels among the three groups. Quantification of anti‐Sm/RNP antibody and anti‐RNP antibody

Supplementary Figure 2 compares the ROC curves of anti‐Sm/RNP and anti‐RNP antibodies in relation to inflammatory arthritis and Raynaud phenomenon. The AUC values were as follows: 0.68 (95% CI 0.59–0.77, P < 0.0001) for anti‐Sm/RNP titers with inflammatory arthritis and 0.67 (95% CI 0.58–0.77, P = 0.0002) for Raynaud phenomenon. For anti‐RNP titers, the AUC values were 0.65 (95% CI 0.55–0.74, P = 0.0039) with inflammatory arthritis and 0.59 (95% CI 0.49–0.69, P = 0.0352) for Raynaud phenomenon.

DISCUSSION

MCTD is a rare autoimmune disease characterized by features of other connective tissue diseases, such as systemic sclerosis (SSc), SLE, and polymyositis. It is unique in that it presents with antibodies targeting the U1 small nuclear RNP particle (U1 snRNP), which is not typically seen in other connective tissue diseases. 22 However, there is ongoing debate about its classification, diagnosis, and standard of care due to the overlap of symptoms with other connective tissue diseases and the fact that patients with U1 snRNP may meet the classification criteria of other “defined” connective tissue diseases over time. 22 The controversy surrounding undifferentiated connective tissue disease (UCTD) lies in its broad encapsulation of conditions, including incomplete forms of SLE and SSc, some of which progress to a formal clinical diagnosis over time. 23 The progression of UCTD to SLE or SSc is not definite, and the factors that predict this progression are still under investigation. For instance, younger age, serositis, and the presence of anti‐dsDNA antibodies have been associated with progression to SLE, 24 whereas puffy fingers, abnormal nailfold changes, and anti–topoisomerase I have been associated with progression to SSc. 23 However, the heterogeneous nature of UCTD and the risk of bias in most studies limit the ability to apply these results in routine clinical practice. 23

Various autoantibodies are associated with distinct clinical manifestations and autoimmune diseases. 2 Anti‐Sm antibodies specifically target Sm proteins and frequently target B/B′, D1, and D3 proteins involved in RNA splicing. 10 These antibodies are highly specific for SLE, although they are found in only about 30% of patients with SLE. 25 , 26 Meanwhile, anti‐RNP antibodies react with multiple components of the U1 snRNP, such as RNP‐A, RNP‐C, and RNP‐68 specific antigens, which are also involved in RNA splicing. These antibodies are found in a variety of connective tissue diseases but are particularly associated with MCTD. In SLE, anti‐RNP antibodies are less specific than anti‐Sm antibodies but are more frequently present. 27 , 28 These antibodies are associated with a high specificity for MCTD, supporting the hypothesis that MCTD is a distinct clinical entity associated with a specific serologic marker. 28 In UCTD, the presence of anti‐RNP antibodies may be indicative of a potential evolution toward a defined connective tissue disease, such as SLE or MCTD. 27 Moreover, anti‐Sm/RNP common motif antibodies detect Sm and RNP epitopes at the junction between Sm and RNP antigens. The Sm antigen targets an immunoreactive 14‐kd (D) protein. The Sm/RNP complex, however, contains both the 14‐kd protein and a 68‐kd reactive protein along with its possible degradation fragments. 9 The distinguished target antigen fragments of antibodies may explain the various combinations of antibody positivity observed in our study, including double positivity of anti‐Sm/RNP and anti‐RNP, solo anti‐Sm/RNP positivity and solo anti‐RNP positivity. This may further establish our understanding of different clinical manifestations with various antibody profiles.

Our study results are significant in at least two major aspects. First, patients with double positivity of anti‐Sm/RNP and anti‐RNP antibodies manifest more commonly identified clinical symptoms, particularly inflammatory arthritis and Raynaud phenomenon, compared to those who are anti‐Sm/RNP positive only or anti‐RNP positive only. The prevalence of clinical manifestations correlated statistically with the titers of anti‐Sm/RNP antibody and anti‐RNP antibody. The adjunction testing of anti‐Sm/RNP antibody could contribute valuable diagnostic information to the uncertain clinical importance of isolated anti‐RNP antibody positivity.

Another finding that stands out is that patients exhibiting double positivity meet the diagnostic and classification criteria for MCTD or SLE more frequently than those with only one antibody positivity. Comparing the four existing diagnostic criteria stands as a significant strength of the study. It was considered that the criteria by Kasukawa et al 17 equipped the highest sensitivity of 77.5% (95% CI 68.4–84.6), and Kahn and Appelboom's19 and Alarcon‐Segovia and Villarreal's18 criteria were the most specific with a specificity of 99.4% (95% CI 96.5–99.9). 29 There were patients diagnosed with both MCTD and SLE per classification criteria, which may explain the overlapping clinical features of MCTD with SLE. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 This intriguing finding may be related to epitope spreading in the autoimmune diseases, a process in which the immune response initially directed against a small number of specific epitopes expands to target additional epitopes, often within the same or related proteins. This phenomenon contributes to the pathogenesis of SLE and MCTD by broadening the immune response and potentially leading to increased tissue damage and diverse clinical manifestations. In our study, we found no correlation between the duration of the disease among three antibody groups, suggesting epitope spreading might not relate to disease duration. In SLE, a longitudinal study has shown that antibodies initially targeting RNP‐A and RNP‐C proteins may then spread to other epitopes, such as those in Sm antigen. 30 In the context of MCTD, epitope spreading has been observed with patients initially presenting with anti–U1 RNP antibodies expanding their immune response to include antibodies against Sm proteins, which are spatially related to U1 snRNP. This expansion of autoimmunity can occur early after the diagnosis of MCTD and is associated with changes in clinical manifestations. For instance, patients with epitope spreading in MCTD have been found to have a lower prevalence of skin sclerosis and a higher prevalence of interstitial lung disease compared to those without epitope spreading. 14

These findings must be interpreted with caution because this was a single‐center cross‐sectional study. Further prospective longitudinal studies are required to determine the evolution of disease with positive anti‐Sm/RNP common motif antibody and assess the prognosis of different antibody profiles, the results of other myositis‐ or scleroderma‐specific antibodies, the therapeutic management of different patient populations, and multiomics and cytometric efforts to understand the serological and clinical heterogeneity across autoimmune connective tissue diseases. 31 This helps gain a better understanding of the clinical significance of anti‐Sm/RNP antibody on disease progression and explore personalized treatment plans. Moreover, it is important to bear in mind the possible underestimation of patients with SLE with the exclusion of positive anti‐Sm antibody due to the limited population in our study. The predominant population in our study is White, and only individuals of White race exhibit solo anti‐Sm/RNP positivity. This requires further validation by the other autoimmune centers with interest in SLE and MCTD. Anti‐Sm/RNP common motif antibodies when present together with RNP antibodies identify patients with higher prevalence of arthritis and Raynaud phenomenon who are also more likely to meet the classification criteria for MCTD and/or SLE.
