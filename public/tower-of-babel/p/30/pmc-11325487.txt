Reaction Characteristics of Calcination and Decomposition of Phosphogypsum in a Bubbling Fluidized Bed
Authors: Pengxing Yuan, Meng Li, Jun Hu, Lin Li, Shiyi Chen, Wenguo Xiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA bubbling fluidized bed reactor is designed to investigate the high-temperature calcination and decomposition characteristics of phosphogypsum (PG). The reactor employs electric heating, and a lifting discharge tube is installed in the middle of the air distributor to allow flexible switching between batch and continuous feeding modes. The batch test results show that the solid-phase bed materials with a PG particle size of 0.27–0.55 mm and a coal particle size of 0.55–0.83 mm are found to have uniformly mixed at a PG/coal mass ratio of 5:1 and calcined at a high temperature of 1100 °C for 30 min. PG completely decomposes to yield mainly CaS and a small amount of Ca2(SiO4) as the solid-phase products. For the above-mentioned optimal process parameters, a 120 min thermal-state continuous test is conducted. Feeding and discharging are performed at intervals of 30 min to maintain the stability of the static bed height inside the reactor. The results indicate that the PG decomposition rate is higher than 92% in the batch and continuous tests. However, the continuous decomposition of PG has significant process advantages, such as a higher CaS yield (71.20%) compared to that in the batch mode (64.49%). Furthermore, PG undergoes agglomeration and bonding within the particles when heated, intensifying the formation of a Ca2(SiO4) eutectic.

Introduction

Phosphogypsum (PG) is an industrial byproduct produced by the wet phosphate acid process. Its main component is CaSO4·2H2O, which is generated by the reaction of phosphate with sulfuric acid1,2:

It is estimated that 4.5–6.0 t PG is generated for every 1.0 t of phosphoric acid produced.1,3 The total deposit of PG in China currently exceeds 7.0 × 108 t, and it is increasing at a rate of 7.5 × 107 t per year.4 The open storage of PG not only causes wastage of land resources but also poses the risk of environmental hazards. Therefore, realizing the safe disposal and resource reutilization of PG has become a research hotspot in the field of energy and chemical engineering.

Traditionally, PG absorption pathways were mainly explored in industries such as the cement, construction, and agricultural industries.5,6 PG can be used as a retarder and activator in cement production, as it allows modifying the setting time of cement and improves the resistance strength of cement.6 Meanwhile, the gypsum-like substance formed by the transformation of CaSO4·2H2O at different temperatures and pressures can be used as building materials.7 In addition, PG is rich in Ca and S and trace elements such as Fe, Si, and P, which can be used to produce ecological fertilizers and improve the yield of cash crops.8 However, the comprehensive utilization rate of the above methods is only 30–40%,4,9 and balancing PG production and consumption remains a challenge.

High-temperature reduction decomposition is considered an effective means of efficiently utilizing PG. The thermal decomposition of PG provides an opportunity to use the main components, i.e., SO3 and CaO, to produce sulfuric acid and Ca-based materials, respectively, while reducing the pressure on the environment. Theoretically, the thermal decomposition temperature of PG in an inert atmosphere can reach 1700 °C,9,10 indicating an energy intensive process. In this situation, the introduction of an appropriate amount of a reducing agent as a catalyst can cause CaSO4 decomposition into SO2 and CaO at lower temperatures. Müler and Kühne11 pioneered the utilization of coke for the reductive decomposition of PG and found that coke effectively reduces the PG decomposition temperature and accelerates its decomposition.12,13 In addition, reducing gases such as CO,2 H2,14 and H2S15 and solid reducing agents such as sulfur,13 coal gangue10,16 and coal17,18 were tested. When using coal, PG may undergo the following main decomposition reactions during high-temperature calcination:

In principle, reactions 2, 3 and 5 are the reactions expected in PG calcination and decomposition to prepare sulfuric acid cogeneration Ca-based materials. However, thermodynamic results indicate that reaction 4 may occur preferentially.5,6 If so, the desulfurization rate of PG will be reduced. Meanwhile, the reaction between CaSO4 and CaS depends on high-temperature conditions of 1200–1500 °C.18,19 These requirements clearly do not meet the expectations of the researchers. Therefore, reaction 4 has always been suppressed as a side reaction of the PG decomposition process and is usually not considered.

Many recent studies indicated that CaS is inevitably produced during PG decomposition. Sun et al.6 obtained a PG conversion rate of 97% in the catalytic decomposition of PG using iron phosphorus slag, and reported that CaS is a key intermediate product in PG decomposition. Liu et al.16 adopted a thermogravimetric method to study the effect of composite reducing agents prepared from coal gangue and coke on PG decomposition reaction characteristics. Their results showed that when the coal gangue/coke/PG mass ratio was 1:2:10, the decomposition rate of PG reached as high as 99%, and the solid-phase products were mainly CaS and Ca2Al2SiO7. Fang et al.20 investigated the high-temperature decomposition behavior of PG and carbon with iron additives using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) methods. They found that the decomposition temperature of CaSO4 and the quantity of CaS products decreased as the additive iron dosage increased. Jia et al.21 studied the sulfur conversion behavior of coal and CaSO4 mixtures during thermal decomposition in a fixed-bed reactor and found that the sulfide sulfur content increases rapidly at temperatures higher than 600 °C due to the decomposition of CaSO4 to form CaS.

Reaction 4 plays a significant role in the PG decomposition technology used to produce sulfuric acid cogeneration Ca-based materials. First, CaS can be oxidized to form CaO under appropriate O222 or CO25 partial pressures, as shown in reactions 6 and 7, though this step requires harsh reaction conditions. Reactions 8 and 9 occur at high O2 or CO2 concentrations,5,23 leading to a decrease in the PG decomposition rate. Previous studies4,22 exploring the high conversion rate of PG focused on the one-time conversion of CaSO4 to CaO. That is, an oxidizing atmosphere was introduced in the initial stage of the high-temperature reduction decomposition of PG, making the process more difficult to control. Then, reactions 2 and 9 occur simultaneously in the same reaction system. In an O2 oxidizing atmosphere, the O2 and reducing agent consume each other, slowing the reaction. Meanwhile, the high cost of obtaining pure O2 should also be considered.24 Therefore, to ensure excellent PG decomposition and desulfurization performance, a cyclic switching of reducing and oxidizing atmospheres is necessary. Specifically, PG is first decomposed in a reducing atmosphere to complete the conversion of CaSO4 to the intermediate product CaS; then, most of the CaS is converted to the target product CaO in an oxidizing atmosphere for further desulfurization. In addition, ensuring a uniform particle mixing quality and efficient gas–solid contact efficiency of the fluidized bed reactor helps provide sufficient reaction space and time for PG calcination.4,24

Second, CaSO4 and C can react to form CaS at a low temperature,5,18 while the CO2 flue gas produced by the reaction is rich in high thermal. Therefore, PG drying in the early stage and calcination in the later stage can be integrated to achieve the advantages of process integration, shortened process flow, and improved thermal recovery rate of the system. Finally, CaS, an important chemical raw material,16,19 can be leached by the H2S solution to obtain the CaH2S2 solution, which can be further carbonized to extract H2S and CaCO3; finally, H2S can be used to prepare sulfuric acid and thiourea. Therefore, clarifying the generation mechanism and reaction patterns of CaS during the PG decomposition process has multiple advantages and insights for the energy and chemical industry.

This study investigated the high-temperature calcination and decomposition characteristics of PG and coal in a CO2 atmosphere using a bubbling fluidized bed reactor designed by the research group. The effects of operational variables such as bed material particle size, reaction temperature, reaction time, and PG/coal mass ratio on the phase of PG decomposition products were discussed. A thermal state continuous feeding experiment of PG calcination and decomposition was conducted. The optimal process parameters and performance of PG corresponding to the maximum decomposition were determined, and the feasibility of the PG calcination and decomposition process in a bubbling fluidized bed was objectively evaluated. The experimental samples were qualitatively analyzed, quantitatively calculated and morphologically characterized using XRD, X-ray fluorescence spectrometry (XRF), and SEM techniques, respectively. The results provide a theoretical basis and data for the unit development and subsequent scale-up experiments of PG decomposition for processes related to sulfuric acid cogeneration calcium-based materials.

Bubbling Fluidized Bed Reaction Unit

Figure 2 shows a schematic diagram of the bubbling fluidized bed reaction unit. The experimental system includes a reactor, gas supply, temperature control, feeding, and blow-off units. The dimensions of the stainless steel reactor were as follows: a height of 150 cm, an outer diameter of 6 cm, and an inner diameter of 5 cm. Electric heating was applied during the experiment. The air distributor was located 60 cm away from the bottom of the reactor, and a height adjustable discharge tube was installed in its center. The discharge tube was 70 cm long and had outer and inner diameters of 1.20 and 0.95 cm, respectively. During the PG decomposition batch experiment, the discharge tube was fixed ∼10 cm above the air distributor. During the continuous feeding experiment, the discharge tube and air distributor were set on the same plane. All experiments were conducted in an inert CO2 atmosphere with a minimum fluidization rate (μmf) of 0.033 m/s and an operating gas velocity of 3 μmf. A ramp-up program was set up, and the thermocouples monitored the real-time temperature of the bed, which was directly inserted into the reaction zone in the bed from above the reactor. After the reactor temperature rose to the preset value, the MFC volume flowmeter was used to control the CO2 volume flow to 2556 mL/min. The reaction was initiated by feeding PG–coal mixed bed into the reactor through the feeding tube. Finally, the sulfur-containing flue gas generated during PG calcination and decomposition was discharged into the atmosphere after alkaline washing. After completion of the reaction, the sample residue was collected for characterization and analysis.

Results and Discussion

Batch Feeding Experiment

Effect of Particle Size Distribution

PG samples with particle size distributions of 0.27–0.55 and 0.55–0.83 mm were obtained after granulating the PG powder. Table 3 summarizes the initial conditions of the experiments described in this section. Figure 3I shows the phase results of decomposition products calcined at high temperatures for obtaining PG with two particle size distributions. From Figure 3I, the product phases of PG for both particle sizes include CaSO4, CaS, and Ca2(SiO4). Meanwhile, as the PG particle size increased, the CaSO4 diffraction peak strengthened, the CaS diffraction peak diminished, and a higher Ca2(SiO4) eutectic content was obtained. This result is explained as follows: as the particle size of PG increases, the gap rate between the particles also increases, and a part of the gas escapes from the gaps among the particles or overflows in advance, weakening the contact efficiency of the effective reducing gas produced by coal pyrolysis and gasification18 with PG. Meanwhile, the formation of Ca2(SiO4) eutectic reduces the PG decomposition rate and the target product yields (CaO and SO2).4,25 Therefore, the optimal PG particle size range in this study is 0.27–0.55 mm; the undecomposed CaSO4 and Ca2(SiO4) eutectic contents are the least for this range.

Figure 3II shows the analysis of the effect of particle size of the solid reducing agent coal on the phase of PG decomposition products. The figure shows that the product phases of the coal particles of four different sizes contain a small amount of Ca2(SiO4) eutectic. Meanwhile, with an increase in the coal particle size, the peak intensity of the CaSO4 diffraction peak diminishes while that of the CaS diffraction peak becomes stronger, indicating a higher PG decomposition rate. This result can be explained as follows: after the fluidized gas was heated and expanded, some fine coal particles were carried out of the reactor,26 weakening the contact and reaction process of coal with PG. Furthermore, small particles tended to adhere to the wall or generate static electricity, leading to particle agglomeration between particles and reducing the fluidization quality of the bed. Therefore, the larger the coal particle size, the more conducive is the situation to enhancing the uniform mixing of solid–solid phases and realizing the full contact between gas–solid phases. In summary, for a coal particle size of 0.55–0.83 mm, the PG was completely decomposed, and the main decomposition product was CaS; CaSO4 was almost absent.

Effect of Reaction Temperature

From Table 4, about 60 g of the solid-phase bed material was taken according to the PG/coal mass ratio of 5:1. After full and uniform mixing, the material was calcined for 30 min at different reaction temperatures (the temperature was increased from 800 to 1000 °C in steps of 100 °C). The effect of PG calcination temperature on the phase of its decomposition products is shown in Figure 4I. From this figure, at reaction temperatures of 800, 900, and 1000 °C, the decomposition products all show CaSO4 diffraction peaks, indicating that PG did not decompose at these temperatures. At a higher reaction temperature of 1100 °C, the product phases are CaS and Ca2(SiO4), and no CaSO4 diffraction peaks are found. That is, the PG–coal solid–solid coupling decomposition reaction occurs specifically at high temperatures because the diffusion process of solid-phase reactions is limited by kinetics.17 The chemical reaction rate is determined by the process in which atoms or ions are activated by heat and undergo migration. Therefore, a low temperature cannot provide sufficient energy to activate the reaction, and an extremely high temperature can facilitate side reactions, leading to a decrease in the yield of the target product. Therefore, 1100 °C is regarded as the characteristic temperature for the complete decomposition of PG.

Effect of Reaction Time

Table 4 summarizes the investigation of the phases of PG decomposition products under the conditions of a reaction temperature of 1100 °C and a PG/coal mass ratio of 5:1. The effect of different reaction times (time increased from 10 to 30 min in steps of 10 min) were examined. The results are shown in Figure 4II: the diffraction peak intensity of CaSO4 gradually weakens with reaction time, indicating that a longer residence time of the sample in the reactor is more favorable for PG decomposition. At a reaction time of 30 min, PG is completely decomposed, and the main components of the reaction products are CaS, along with a small amount of Ca2(SiO4). Maintaining an appropriate residence time is conducive to enhancing the full mixing and contact between PG and the reducing atmosphere (coal), obtaining excellent bubbling fluidization quality, and improving the decomposition and desulfurization performance of PG. Therefore, the optimal reaction time for PG calcination decomposition is taken as 30 min.

Effect of PG/Coal Mass Ratio

From the data in Table 4, PG samples (∼50 g) and different amounts of the reducing atmosphere, i.e., coal (i.e., 5, 10, and 20 g), were uniformly mixed and calcined at 1100 °C for 30 min. The effect of the PG/coal mass ratio on the phase of PG decomposition products is shown in Figure 4III. As seen from Figure 4III, when no coal particles are added, only CaSO4 diffraction peaks are present in the product spectrum, indicating that the PG sample does not decompose independently at 1100 °C. This is because the theoretical thermal decomposition temperature of PG is higher than 1200 °C in the absence of a reducing atmosphere.2,9 With the introduction of a solid reducing agent coal, the diffraction peak intensity of CaSO4 in the reaction products diminishes gradually, while that of CaS gradually increases. At a PG/coal mass ratio of 5:1, almost all PG decomposes, and the reaction products are CaS and a small amount of Ca2(SiO4). This indicates that adding coal promotes PG decomposition as the coke formed by coal pyrolysis and combustible gases such as CO provides a reducing atmosphere for PG decomposition, establishing a high-temperature reduction decomposition system for the solid–solid phases. Meanwhile, the mineral components in coal and coal ash have a promoting effect on the decomposition of PG.10,16 There is no significant change in the produce phases on adding more coal until the PG/coal mass ratio reaches 2.5:1. Thus, the amount of reducing agent is an important factor affecting the high temperature calcination and decomposition of PG and coal. If an insufficient amount of coal is added, the reaction is not thorough, and PG is not completely decomposed. If the coal consumption is excessive, more raw materials will be consumed, reducing the technical and economic benefits. Therefore, the optimum PG/coal mass ratio is 5:1, at which point PG is completely decomposed.

Reliability Verification of Batch Experiments

As shown in Table 4, based on the optimized process conditions, ∼ 50 g of a PG sample with a particle size of 0.27–0.55 mm and ∼10 g of coal with a particle size of 0.55–0.83 mm were taken with a PG/coal mass ratio of 5:1. The PG and coal samples were uniformly mixed and calcined at 1100 °C for 30 min. As shown in Figure 4IV, the product phases obtained by experimenting thrice were CaS and Ca2(SiO4). The slight difference in the diffraction peak intensity of the product components may be related to the homogeneity of PG particles after granulation. In summary, the results indicate that the XRD characterization method is feasible and that the batch test data obtained simultaneously are accurate and reliable.

Continuous Feeding Experiment

Determination of Static Bed Height

According to the design structure of the bubbling fluidized bed, the key factor determining the success of the thermal continuous experiment is maintaining the stability of the static bed height. Therefore, first, the appropriate static bed height must be determined. For a PG/coal mass ratio of 5:1, different masses of PG and coal were mixed evenly and calcined at 1100 °C for 30 min (Table 5). According to the data for Tests 5, 15, and 16 in Table 5, the total mass of PG and coal is directly proportional to the bed height. In other words, the greater the amount of solid-phase bed material that enters the reactor, the higher the bed height. Figure 5I shows the effect of bed height on the phase of PG decomposition products. From Figure 5I, the CaSO4 diffraction peak in the decomposition products gradually becomes more intense as the bed height increases, indicating the presence of more undecomposed PG in the reaction products. The main reason for this result is that the density of PG (1.21 g/cm3) is much higher than that of coal (0.79 g/cm3), and remarkable stratification occurs between the two during bubbling fluidization. The higher the bed height, the more obvious the stratification and the less sufficient the contact and reaction between the reducing gas and coke produced by the pyrolysis of the upper coal and the lower PG.

From the results of the cold state continuous experiment, the total mass of the solid phase bed material entering the reactor each time should be maintained between 100 and 200 g to ensure that the bed material completely covers the air distributor and forms a material seal in the bed. During continuous feeding, some of the bed material will fall into the discharge tube. In the cold state, ∼40 g of the PG-coal mixture is required to fill the discharge tube. Since most of the materials entering the discharge tube are not located in the insulation area of the electric heater, these materials may be discarded as waste. Meanwhile, since the main chemical component of PG is CaSO4, with the prolongation of PG calcination time in the reactor, sulfur-containing gases such as SO2, H2S, and COS will be removed.27 The broken particles will also be transported out of the bed by the fluidized gas. At this point, the mass of the bed material is reduced accordingly, causing a decrease in the bed height. Finally, considering that the air distributor is only 15 cm away from the feed inlet of the reactor tube wall, the high temperature and fluidized environment of PG calcination may lead to the expansion of gas and bed particles. To prevent blockage of the tube wall inlet, Hsb should not exceed two-thirds of the vertical distance between the air distributor and the tube wall inlet. Therefore, combining the bed height and the results of the cold state experiments, the initial bed material amount was determined to be ∼180 g (see the data of Test 16 in Table 5).

Thermal State Continuous Experiment Results

As seen from the data of Tests 17–20 in Table 5, the PG calcination and decomposition experiment in the bubbling fluidized bed was performed with four consecutive feedings for a total reaction of 120 min. For a PG/coal mass ratio of 5:1, the amount of initial bed material was 176.36 g (Test 17); the theoretical discharge amount of discharge tube is not included); the amounts for the second, third, and fourth feedings were 140.21 (Test 18), 104.99 (Test 19), and 68.80 g (Test 20), respectively. Calcination was performed at 1100 °C for 30 min after each feeding. Each continuous feeding amounted to ∼36 g less than the previous one this is assumed to be the value of the product mass at the end of the PG decomposition reaction. In principle, the mass of the reaction product is variable, and it should depend on the amount of feed at each time of the thermal-state experiment, the decomposition desulfurization efficiency of PG, and the actual discharge amount of the discharge tube. However, it is difficult to accurately grasp the actual conversion rate of PG after each feeding in continuous experiments. Meanwhile, as shown in Table 5, the discharge tube in Tests 17–20 discharged 30.13, 7.93, 5.24, and 33.26 g of bed material, respectively. This is significantly different from the theoretical discharge amount of the discharge tube, and the four discharge amounts did not follow any significant pattern. There are three possibilities leading to the above differences. First, the high temperature of 1100 °C causes the expansion and deformation of the discharge tube, reducing its internal volume. Second, the chemical composition of PG is diverse, and substances such as SiO2 and Al2O3 can easily combine with the calcium in CaSO4 at high temperatures to form eutectic substances that adhere and aggregate on the reactor wall.16,28 Third, PG particles have complex fluidization characteristics and are prone to agglomeration in the horizontal direction affecting the fluidization quality.4 Therefore, the mass of the reaction product (36.32 g) in Test 16 in Table 5 is assumed to be the residual amount of the sample for continuous experiments. In summary, the thermal state continuous experiment maximizes the stability of the bed material mass during the initial reaction stage and static bed height. According to Tests 17–20 in Table 5, the initial bed material mass was maintained at 180 ± 4 g, and the static bed height was maintained at 8.50 ± 0.03 cm.

Reliability Verification of Continuous Experiments

Figure 5II shows the phase results of the bed material discharged through the discharge tube four times in the thermal-state continuous feeding experiment. From Figure 5II, the phase components of the four discharged bed materials contain CaSO4, CaS, and Ca2(SiO4). Meanwhile, the intensity of the CaSO4 diffraction peak weakens gradually, while that of the CaS diffraction peak strengthens gradually because the fresh bed material pushes PG particles that are reacting or have reacted in the reactor into the discharge tube. In addition, with more residence time for the mixed bed material in the reactor, sufficient contact and reaction between PG and coal are realized. After the thermal-state continuous experiment is completed, the sample residue in the reactor is removed for repeated characterization, and the results are marked as Tests 21–23, as shown in Figure 5III. Figure 5III shows that the CaSO4 diffraction peaks are all very weak, indicating that PG was completely converted to the intermediate product CaS; thus, the continuous feeding process yielded results similar to those of batch experiments.

Evaluation of PG Calcination and Decomposition Process

As mentioned above, the main component of the fresh PG sample is CaSO4, and the main component of the sample residue after calcination and decomposition is CaS. Table 1 compares the changes in the contents of each component of PG samples before and after batch and continuous experiments. The forms of calcium and sulfur in the prereaction PG sample (CaSO4) and the postreaction decomposition product (CaS) are both CaO and SO3. Therefore, the decomposition rate of PG and CaS yield in batch and continuous experiments can be quantified according to eqs 10 and 11, respectively, and the results are shown in Figure 5IV. From Figure 5IV, the decomposition rates of PG in batch and continuous experiments are almost equal (92.32 and 93.26%, respectively). In contrast, CaS yields varied significantly, with a value of 71.20% in the continuous experiment, which is higher than that in the batch experiment (64.49%) by 6.71 percentage points. This difference can be explained as follows: the operating conditions of continuous experiments are the best working conditions optimized according to the batch process. Meanwhile, in the continuous experiment, the accumulated undecomposed PG in the reactor may continue the continuous conversion of CaSO4 to CaS. Further, CaS can be oxidized to form CaO and SO2. The solid-phase product (CaO) can be used as a feedstock for producing calcium-based materials such as cement, while the gas-phase product SO2 can be used for preparing sulfuric acid. Compared to the batch experimental processes, continuous PG decomposition has significant technological advantages. Designing an efficient and stable PG calcination reaction unit will be a key factor in promoting its commercial operation.

Conclusions

This study investigated the high temperature calcination and decomposition behavior of PG and coal in a bubbling fluidized bed. The main results are summarized as follows:

The optimal particle size distribution ranges of PG and coal are 0.27–0.55 and 0.55–0.83 mm, respectively. For these sizes, the solid–solid phase mixing is more uniform, and the gas–solid phase contact is satisfactory. The energy transfer for solid–solid phases is limited by kinetics, and low-temperature conditions cannot provide sufficient energy to activate the reduction and decomposition reaction of PG and coal. Hence, the optimal reaction temperature is 1100 °C. Introducing a reducing atmosphere and maintaining a suitable residence time effectively improve the decomposition and desulfurization performance of PG and promote the establishment of a high-temperature reduction and decomposition system. The optimum PG/coal mass ratio is 5:1, and the reaction time is 30 min.

The bed material mass and static bed height are positively correlated. However, the density difference between PG and coal tends to stratify the particles inside the reactor. The key to the successful operation of a thermal-state continuous feeding experiment is that the bed material forms a material seal inside the bed, and a certain static bed height is maintained. Therefore, the initial bed material amount is set at ∼180 g, and the static bed height is ∼8.50 cm. A continuous experiment was performed with four feeding for a reaction time of 120 min. The main components of the solid phase product were CaS and a small amount of Ca2(SiO4), indicating that PG was completely converted into the intermediate product CaS.

The batch and continuous experiments provided similar PG decomposition: both greater than 92%. Notably, the CaS yield in the continuous experiment (71.20%) was significantly better than that in the batch experiment (64.49%) because the undecomposed PG accumulated during the continuous feeding process underwent continuous conversion to CaS with prolonged residence time in the reactor. In addition, PG was susceptible to agglomeration and adhesion within the particles when heated, promoting the production of eutectic substances such as Ca2(SiO4). Further studies should continue optimizing PG decomposition performance and elucidating the continuous decomposition behavior of PG for a stable running bed height.
