Novel hybrid machine learning-based prediction of building space heating load: a comprehensive study
Authors: Ahmet Emir, Halil Ibrahim Topal, Suat Ozturk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Space heating load is the main energy consumption agent for residential buildings. Its prediction is crucial for the efficient and cost-effective management of energy suppliers. In this study, nine machine learning methods, including a novel hybrid natural gradient boosting (NGBoost) and residual-based mixture density network (MDN) model, are evaluated to predict the space heating load of a building using a dataset composed of seven independent input parameters. The evaluation of the methods is carried out based on the performance criteria of mean absolute error, mean squared error, root mean squared error, mean absolute percentage error, and the coefficient of determination. The results show that the smallest mean absolute percentage error, with a value of 0.05, belongs to the novel hybrid NGBoost + MDN model, which is more effective and competitive compared to histogram-based gradient boosting, elastic net, light gradient boosting machine, and extreme learning machine approaches. The best model in prediction accuracy is NGBoost + MDN because it has the lowest MAPE. All the methods achieve values above 0.99 for the coefficient of determination, however the decision tree has the highest score of 0.9989. Moreover, extreme gradient boosting and elastic net techniques perform smaller squared errors with 0.063 and 0.065. In addition, it has been determined from the uncertainty analysis that the newly developed hybrid model demonstrates better prediction stability, as evidenced by its consistently maintaining prediction residuals within a ‘Low Error Region’ area of the residual plot. The Residual Prediction Deviation (RPD) index measurement indicates the model’s very high degree of robustness and reliability for managing stochastic variations in heating load at an RPD score of 25.845. These results are relevant to the particular case studied and should be analyzed considering the choice of the building, as well as the settings used for validation purposes.

Introduction

The rapid growth of population has significantly increased sheltering needs in recent decades and caused a rise in energy demand for constructions and heating, ventilation, and air-conditioning (HVAC) processes of residential and commercial buildings1. Energy consumption in the European Union (EU) similar to Canada, China, Japan, etc. is around 33% of total for present buildings and construction sector as USA realizes 39%2–4. Buildings also account for approximately 36% of global greenhouse gas emissions according to the United Nations Climate Change Assessment Report5. The EU has brought out serious regulations for energy performance and emissions of all the recently erected buildings by 2030 in answer6.

The depletion of energy sources with raising demand brings the issues of energy management, efficiency and sustainability forward under future carbon footprint objectives of countries nowadays7. Building energy management system (BEMS) productively provides the energy use for instant HVAC demands in addition to lighting and room equipment of building according to inner and outer ambient parameters and can run the installed few systems in a combined way in district heating systems (DHS)8. Its computer-aided central control system driven by software is intervened with an inner control panel or online by users as well9. The more sophisticated ones possess machine learning based software that automatically adjusts the system parameters by forecasting the future from momentarily previous (historical) data of the changing environment conditions, consumption rate, energy load and even bill costs10,11. Under these circumstances, proper prediction of the heating load of buildings becomes important to facilitate the optimization of energy consumption and operational efficiency of BEMS, as well as its capability to manage real-time operations.

A machine learning technique is a data-driven approach in which algorithms learn relationships between inputs and outputs describing mathematical correlations between inputs and outputs that are constituted from quantified dataset of a physical problem12. It is used to handle with vast quantity of data, describes complicated patterns, and adapts to changing conditions. There are four types of machine learning algorithms as supervised, unsupervised, semi-supervised, and reinforcement learning13. Supervised learning learns from labelled data with known input-output relation as unsupervised one uses unlabelled data to describe groups and patterns. Semi-supervised learning is a hybrid model of supervised and unsupervised algorithms. Reinforcement learning learns by getting feedback data in relation with environment14. Dataset is divided into two pieces, training data as 80% of all the data and testing data as the rest. The prediction model of machine learning is assessed on testing data after it is trained using training data15. Moreover, hybrid models entail a technique that integrates several models into one model. The techniques used may include machine learning models or statistics3,16–18. Hybrid modeling refers to the integration of multiple predictive models rather than optimization-based approaches. The advantages discussed above make machine learning models very useful to incorporate within BEMS systems because proper forecasting of the heating load is necessary for efficient operation of BEMS.

Software engineers have begun to develop different types of machine learning methods lately and mechanical engineers have been trying to operate these methods on BEMS systems of buildings for HVAC as well. Actually, there is no the best machine learning technique that is valid to forecast energy need for heating and cooling loads of all the buildings19. These trainable and self-learning techniques can give several performances on the implemented systems for a given data type across different scenarios20. Machine learning models mostly have to be improved by parameter tuning, optimization, model selection, ensemble, feature engineering, etc. to obtain better performance for forward prediction in terms of a certain historical data21,22. Therefore, it is very crucial to determine the method making the best prediction in the shortest time with respect to performance criteria or metrics as Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Mean Squared Error (MSE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), R-Squared (R2)23–25.

Recent studies have focused on several machine learning approaches and hybrid models for energy consumption and load predictions of buildings. For instance, Dalipi et al. used Support Vector Regression (SVR), Partial Least Square (PLS), and Random Forest (RF) algorithms to determine heat load of buildings and concluded SVR to have the best performance26. Yun et al. carried out hourly thermal load predictions of small and medium office buildings, a midrise apartment, and a high-rise apartment with Indexed Autoregressive with Exogenous (ARX) model27. Roy et al. forecasted the heating load and cooling load of residential buildings by Multivariate Adaptive Regression Splines (MARS), Extreme Learning Machine (ELM) and a hybrid model of MARS and ELM techniques, and found hybrid model to be better28. Catalina et al. studied a multiple regression prediction model to get the prediction of heating energy need with measured data of 17 block-building29. Kurek et al. focused on 72-hours heat demand forecasting methods for the Warsaw District Heating Network with Ridge Regression, Auto-regression with Exogenous input, and Deep Artificial Neural Networks (DANN) at high accuracy30. Jovanovic et al. utilized Feed Forward Backpropagation Neural Network (FFNN), Radial Basis Function Network (RBFN) and Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Interference System (ANFIS) to forecast heat energy need of a university campus and showed all the models to be at good accuracy31. Lin et al. introduced a novel prediction model, Hybrid Spatial-Temporal Attention Long Short-Term Memory (STALSTM) to forecast heat load and compared it with a state-of-the-art algorithm for three heat exchange station in a heating system32. Geysen et al. predicted thermal load in a district heating system located in Rottne, Sweden by data-driven methods of Linear Regression (LR), Extremely Randomized Trees Regression (ERTR), Feed-Forward Neural Network (FFNN) and Support Vector Machine (SVM) and indicated ANN and ERTR models to be better33. Li and Yao determined annual residential heating and cooling loads along with resident behaviour for five different machine learning methods and proved Gaussian radial basis function kernel support vector regression to be the best-performing model34. Zhao and Liu studied a hybrid model of wavelet transform, support vector machines (SVM), and partial least squares regression (PLS) to forecast the cooling and heating loads of office buildings for several time horizons35. Song et al. predicted hourly heating load by Temporal Convolutional Neural Network (TCNN) indicating high performance according to RFR, ETR, SVR, SGD, RNN, LSTM, etc36. Moradzadeh et al. evaluated Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) and SVR methods for prediction of heating and cooling loads of residential buildings and expressed MLP to be better37. Gong et al. estimated heat load of residential buildings with hybrid models of Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT), Two Tree-Based Ensemble Learning Algorithms, Extremely Randomized Trees Regression (ERTR), and Gradient Boosting Decision Tree methods and found ERTR model based on DWT to give a better prediction38. Ghasemkhani et al. determined the heating and cooling loads of Internet-of-Things-Based Smart Buildings with new models using Tri-Layered Neural Network (TNN) and Maximum Relevance Minimum Redundancy (MRMR) algorithms and compared to the state-of-the-art-methods39. Moayedi et al. used several machine learning methods to predict heating load of energy efficient buildings and founded Random Forest (RF) to have the better performance with respect to Multi-Layer Perceptron Regressor (MLPR), Lazy Locally Weighted Learning (LLWL), Alternating Model Tree (AMT), Elastic Net (ENet), and Radial Basis Function Regression (RBFR)40. Even though these studies provide useful information, they tend to be specific about particular models or dataset and do not use a common framework for comparison. As recent research findings, the application of deep learning along with optimization approaches increases the efficiency of predictions and sustainability. Various forms of artificial intelligence approaches, including ensemble methods and neural network models like Back Propagation Neural Network (BPNN), prove efficient in detecting nonlinearities and predicting compressive strength41–44.

Futhermore, studies have shown the success of hybrid models in solving complex engineering problems through the use of soft computing and machine learning algorithms. For example, reliability-based design and estimation of pile bearing capacity have greatly benefited from hybrid models like GA-XGBoost and ANFIS, which have succeeded in addressing uncertainty and enhancing predictive efficiency45,46. Soft computing methods have been applied in other geotechnical and structural engineering problems like slope stability analysis with generalized failure criteria47. Moreover, hybrid and evolutionary machine learning models, including deep learning and genetic programming, have achieved remarkable results in complex behavior predictions like concrete and mortar characteristics48,49. These studies reveal the emerging trend of integrating machine learning algorithms with optimization and hybridization techniques for better accuracy and efficiency.

Most literature works analyze only a small set of models while reporting only the accuracy-related figures. Moreover, some other challenges like generalizability, robustness, and error-oriented assessment techniques are often overlooked. The hybrid method involving probabilistic learning and residual modeling has seldom been used for the purpose of building heating load predictions. This means that there is a requirement for more effective modeling. In this regard, this paper proposes the use of NGBoost + MDN as a solution. Specifically, through NGBoost, it becomes possible to generate probabilistic estimates via the estimation of the complete distribution of the target variable; nevertheless, such a procedure may be associated with certain systematic biases in case of the occurrence of nonlinear dependencies. For that reason, the combination of both methods becomes possible due to the use of MDNs for the modeling of the residuals generated by the use of NGBoost predictions, which represents the key rationale behind the choice of NGBoost + MDN architecture.

Nevertheless, although many recent works have been devoted to reliability analysis and prediction modeling individually, they still consider data from specific domains and rarely explore probabilistic modeling combined with residual learning-based hybrid models in building energy estimation applications. The lack of probabilistic modeling with residual learning-based hybrid models in building energy estimation applications is the motivation for this study’s NGBoost + MDN framework.

The main issue seen in the existing literature in which studies that have been conducted for the purposes of estimating building heating loads can be found is that many of the researches use only either deterministic methods or ML-based models. Even though probabilistic methods (e.g., NGBoost) have more strengths in modeling uncertainty compared to other methods, they cannot consider possible systematic errors caused by complicated nonlinear relations. To solve this problem in the proposed model, the model that integrates NGBoost and MDN is proposed. The NGBoost is used for estimating the probabilistic distribution of the target variable, whereas the MDN makes the model perform better through training residuals based on the probabilistic distribution estimated by the NGBoost model. With regards to this matter, the NGBoost + MDN model is intended to solve these limitations through the integration of both probabilistic and residual learning techniques. Here, the difference between the present study and past researches is the combination of probabilistic prediction and residual learning compared to only using deterministic or independent machine learning algorithms.

In contrast with hybrid models based on the combination of traditional deterministic boosting methods and neural networks, the novel approach employs probabilistic boosting, i.e., NGBoost along with the residual modeling by means of MDN. Therefore, it is possible to account for both the distribution of the target and residual relationships simultaneously. Several gaps in the current literature motivate the current study. In the first place, many studies tend to use only a few algorithms, which may not be sufficient enough to consider the complexity of data associated with the field of building energy. Many prior works have already shown successful applications of machine learning methods to predict building energy consumption and heating loads, either based on measurement data or simulations. Nonetheless, there are some limitations in the current state of research; existing work tends to use only deterministic methods for forecasting purposes or applies machine learning models separately. The combination of probabilistic learning with residual nonlinear modeling has rarely been explored in heating load prediction studies. Thus, the unique contribution of this paper is the developed NGBoost + MDN approach.

In order to tackle the above problems, this paper aims to create a hybrid machine learning model for heating load prediction, which may be used directly in BEMS applications. The study adopts the NGBoost + MDN model to conduct probabilistic learning and residual modeling simultaneously. The goal of such research design is to increase prediction performance while maintaining flexibility.Although existing works prove the success of applying different machine learning and deep learning models to building energy prediction, several drawbacks could still be pointed out.As an answer to the above problems, a probabilistic hybrid NGBoost + MDN model is proposed to apply in a dataset based on dynamic simulation. On a practical level, the current research tries to make dynamic heating load predictions more accurate and reliable.The application of MDN in the present study is justified based on its ability to model complex residuals that cannot be modeled using simple regression neural networks. This makes it possible to effectively model nonlinear error patterns through MDN.In addition, the selection of the NGBoost + MDN framework is based on its capability to model uncertainty together with the complex nonlinear residual structure. While this research does not create an actual real-time BEMS system, the developed predictive models may be implemented in such systems.

The proposed framework is developed to improve the accuracy and reliability of heating load prediction by integrating probabilistic learning and residual modeling. In this context, this paper investigates the prediction of space heating load for a building located in Türkiye with several machine learning techniques, including Decision Tree (DT), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBOOST), Light Gradient-Boosting Machine (LGBM), Elastic Net (ENet), Extreme Learning Machine (ELM), Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), Histogram-Based Gradient Boosting (HGB), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and novel Hybrid Natural Gradient Boosting (NGBoost) and residual based Mixture Density Network (MDN) based on available historical data of the buildings as time, ambient temperature, space heating, and solar irradiation. Weather data, including ambient temperature and solar irradiation, was obtained from the METEONORM file in the TRNSYS software, and the space heating load was calculated using TRNSYS 18.0.

Dataset description

Weather data

This section explains the dataset used in the study. The weather data, such as ambient temperature and solar irradiation, was generated from the METEONORM files in TRNSYS 18.0. To achieve this, Type 15 − 6 was selected from the TRNSYS components in the Simulation Studio environment, and TR-Istanbul-Ataturk-Ab-170,600 was chosen as the external file. The weather data are not based on actual measurements; instead, they consist of typical meteorological year (TMY) data generated by METEONORM, representing statistically synthesized climate data. Figure 1 shows the ambient temperature obtained, and Fig. 2 illustrates the solar irradiation rate generated by the software for Istanbul7. As a result of statistical analysis of TMY data, some weather extremes like intense cold fronts or heat waves could even get averaged out in the database. In such cases, predictions based on the suggested models must be considered taking into account possible difficulties in capturing real-world scenarios.

Building description and simulation

A typical multi-story building was selected as the reference for calculating the space heating load. The building has three floors, each 3 m high, giving a total height of 9 m. Each floor has a space heating area of 300 m². This building represents a typical residence for six families, with a total of 24 occupants. The building’s columns and ceiling are made entirely of reinforced concrete, while the spaces between the columns are filled with bricks. This is the most commonly preferred building type in Türkiye. The building has 720 m² of outdoor surface area, of which 180 m² (25%) is windows and the rest is external walls. The area of the columns is included in the external wall area since their overall heat transfer coefficient is identical to that of the external wall. Extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost)

Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), is composed of gradient-boosted decision trees, and increases significant popularity. It has high predictive performance in supervised learning tasks. This method supports effective modeling complex patterns within data. It was widely used for both classification and regression problems.

At its core structure, XGBoost provides a weak ensemble learning based on decision trees, in a sequential manner. Each new tree is trained to decrease the errors caused by the preceding trees, gradually improving the overall prediction accuracy. Model’s parameters are updated by minimizing a loss function with applying gradient descent techniques.

XGboost optimizes predictive accuracy by sequentially adding trees that correct previous errors. Extreme learning machine (ELM)

Input weights and hidden-layer biases are assigned randomly. Output weights are specified analytically with a generalized inverse operation. With so little time spent on training, good performance is achieved. ELM has been used in various regression, classification and function approximation tasks. It provides simplicity, high speed, and ease of implementation. ELM has theoretical foundation that ensures universal approximation capability under certain conditions so it is a practical alternative to conventional neural network training methods.

Light gradient boosting machine (LGBM)

Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGBM) is a gradient boosting framework introduced by52 to address the computational and memory limitations of traditional Gradient Boosting Decision Trees (GBDT).To implement the method, several innovations are combined. So, LGBM is suitable for large-scale and high-dimensional data.

The first contribution is based on Gradient-based One-Side Sampling (GOSS). GOSS prioritizes training samples with larger gradients while randomly discarding part of the samples with smaller gradients, which reduces computational cost without sacrificing significant predictive information.

Second key is Exclusive Feature Bundling (EFB). Many high-dimensional datasets contain sparse and mutually exclusive features (such as one-hot encoded variables). EFB merges these features into a smaller number of combined features, by reducing the effective dimensionality while preserving the information content.

Finally, LGBM provides a leaf-wise tree growth strategy beyond the conventional level-wise approach. LGBM expands the leaf, thereby it yields the maximum loss reduction at each step. LGBM avoids overfitting, achieves deeper trees and often higher accuracy, though with the need for careful regularization to avoid overfitting.

Decision tree (DT)

DT is widely used type of machine learning technique for predicting energy performance, both in early design phases and during building retrofits. It is modeled as a hierarchical tree. Tree expands from a single root node to multiple leaves. DT is composed of the root, internal nodes, and terminal leaves. Nodes are present in it, and each node represents a test on an attribute, while branches define the possible outcomes of these tests, guiding the path toward the leaves. The leaves determine the final decisions or predicted outcomes. In this hierarchical structure, the root node is located at the top, followed by intermediate branching nodes, and the leaves are present at the bottom. A decision tree involves formulating questions about the training data and progressively narrowing down the possibilities based on the answers. At every node, a condition related to an input variable is implemented, and the tree continues to divide until a terminal node is reached. Therefore, each leaf is connected to the root. Root represents a distinct rule for prediction12.

Long short term memory (LSTM)

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks are a specialized type of recurrent neural network (RNN). It is designed to effectively find long term dependencies in sequences. Present RNNs have difficulties with long-term dependencies. It is difficult to learn patterns over extended sequences due to the vanishing and exploding gradient problems, LSTMs address this issue by using a memory cell structure and gates that adjust the flow of information.

An LSTM cell consists of input, forget and output gates. The input gate determines how much new information enters the cell. The forget gate discards unnecessary information. Output gate sends information to the next state. By selectively updating, forgetting, and outputting information, LSTMs are able to maintain relevant context across long sequences.

As for the model training phase, the validation loss is watched for signs of overfitting. No overfitting is observed, and thus the same number of epochs are used to train all networks. Stability of the results from training is noted since no variations in model performance are witnessed. A fixed number of epochs ensures consistency of comparisons between various models.

LSTM model is built by piling up layers that can help recognize temporal patterns, utilizing rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation functions. The training process takes place for a pre-defined number of epochs (3000), providing baseline model consistency. Dropout technique is not used since there was no evident overfitting problem detected throughout the training phase.

As a result, LSTMs are able to maintain relevant context across long sequences with selectively updating, forgetting and outputting information, and LSTMs especially are useful for tasks involving time series, natural language processing, and speech recognition53.

Here, rather than applying the sliding window technique, dependency with respect to time will be introduced by means of lagged features such as t-5 min and t-24 h. Thus, historical data is directly incorporated in the feature space. The inputs to the LSTM model are the time-lagged features extracted from the original data.

Convolutional neural network (CNN)

Automated feature learning makes convolutional neural networks (CNNs) one of the most sought-after deep learning architectures. Like other neural networks, a CNN’s architecture is comprised of multiple convolutional and pooling layers. During the convolutional phase of a CNN, several kernels (or filters) are positioned on the input, and each captures different local patterns, which are then represented as feature maps.

Feature maps from the convolutional layers go through pooling layers, which help reduce dimensionality without discarding essential information. In max pooling, each region of the feature map is filtered, and only the highest value is passed to the next layer. This operation makes the network focus on the most salient features and filters out weak features. With average pooling, each region is represented with the mean value, which makes the representation of features smoother.

With the integration of average pooling layers, CNNs gain a degree of translation invariance, with the added benefit of reduced overfitting risk and lowered computational cost from a reduced parameter set. The result of this over-simplification is a model that still consistently retains powerful features, while the internal data representation is dramatically simplified54. Two dimensional representation of input data that is fed into the CNN architecture has been transformed such that the temporal aspect and features can be captured by the network at the same time. The network can therefore learn temporal relations across various features.

Similarly, the CNN model takes in lag-based inputs instead of sequential windows, which enables the learning of temporal trends using the explicitly input past data points.

The CNN architecture comprises convolution and fully connected layers using ReLU activation functions. The input dataset is designed such that it considers temporal dependencies as well as feature dependencies. The model undergoes training for 3000 iterations as per the benchmark experiment framework. No dropout mechanism is implemented, considering there are no signs of overfitting behavior observed.

Histogram–based gradient boosting (HGB)

Histogram-Based Gradient Boosting (HGB) is a tree-based ensemble technique which enhances efficiency while performing gradient boosting by binning continuous feature values of a vector into a finite number of discrete values. Instead of requiring every possible split point for analysis, the algorithm using the histograms approximates split candidates and thus, significantly reduces training time and memory.

This approach is composed of multiple decision trees. Each consequent tree eliminates the errors of its predecessors. HGB is capable of maintaining competitive accuracy on large datasets by merging the histogram technique with boosting strategy55.

Elastic net (ENet)

ENet is a linear regression like regularization technique. It is a combination of both L1 penalty (Lasso) and the L2 penalty (Ridge). The key to this being that we want the variable selection of L1. It has been shown to correct the stability issue of L2 in presence of highly collinear predictors40.

Hybrid NGBoost + simplified MDN model

The hybrid modeling approach includes two steps. The first step involves training the NGBoost model to obtain base predictions. In the second step, the residual is calculated and modeled by an MDN. MDN modeling is performed with a validation ratio of 10% (validation split = 0.1), with early stopping criterion based on validation loss. The MDN modeling is done with MSE as the loss function and Adam as the optimization function. Whereas the baseline DL models are configured with fixed parameters for comparison, the hybrid model utilizes a validation-based model training technique.

This approach makes it possible for the model to learn complicated nonlinearities not being captured during the initial stage.

The simplified architecture for MDN used in this study aims at capturing the residual error pattern derived from the NGBoost models., which is different from conventional Mixture Density Networks architecture. The model is trained to predict the residual error produced from the NGBoost model with respect to the input variables. The model architecture is composed of multi-layer feed-forward neural network architecture, and the ReLU activation function is applied, single layer of 32 neurons is used. The output layer contains one neuron to produce a point estimate of the residual error. In this research, as opposed to the use of mixture components and parameters of the probability distribution in classical MDN models, the training of the model is done based on the mean square error (MSE) loss function. The predicted value is calculated by summing the residual error produced from the MDN and the output of the NGBoost model. In contrast to conventional MDN networks, there is no modeling of the mixture component and the probability distribution parameters. Instead, an MSE loss function is used for training the network. Consequently, the learning task for the MDN module is defined as a regression problem rather than probability estimation based on likelihood.

The main contribution of the suggested model is that it is a combination of NGBoost (a probabilistic machine learning model) and MDN (deep learning). Although the model based on the NGBoost algorithm makes estimations of the conditional probability of the target variable, there still can be some systematic errors in the resulting estimations. In this case, MDN was used for modeling such residuals. In fact, MDN helps improve the output of the model by taking into account the nonlinear connections between variables. Unlike an optimization algorithm, the suggested hybrid system is the integration of two predictive models where MDN is used to model the residuals generated from the NGBoost method.

In the suggested hybrid model, residual learning can be implemented through a two-stage process where the NGBoost algorithm predicts the target variable initially. Residuals are then calculated as the difference between the real value and predicted values from NGBoost. These residuals are further modeled using the MDN model. The predicted residuals are combined with the initial NGBoost prediction to get the final prediction.

The use of the simplified MDN architecture as the residual model is due to its capability of modeling the highly nonlinear structures of the prediction error. Although MLP models output deterministic results. The proposed simplified MDN model is quite adaptable when capturing non-linear residual relationships obtained through NGBoost algorithms. In the present study, even though a simplified form of MDN is employed, MDN still has the inherent strength in modeling complex residual structures better than the standard regression models.

In the hybrid model, the base predictor was NGBoost, and the addition of MDN helped to enhance the last output by learning the residuals of the NGBoost model. Unlike classical gradient boosting methods, NGBoost not only produces point estimates but also estimates the probability distribution of the target variable. NGBoost optimizes the distribution parameters using the natural gradient approach56.

Loss functions are minimized for the model training process according to the type of the model. In the case of deep learning models, MSE is chosen as the loss function for which the Adam optimizer is employed to minimize it. The entire model building process includes data preparation, construction of features such as lags, training of models using the training dataset, and finally testing of the model on a testing dataset. All of the processes are followed for all models for consistent comparisons. NGBoost models the conditional distribution of the output variable by maximizing the loss function that is based on the negative log-likelihood.

However, the training scheme for the suggested NGBoost + MDN framework differs from the other approaches in that it involves the use of early stopping to address overfitting issues through validation loss. This shows how the training method varies between conventional deep learning models and the innovative hybrid model design.

In this study, the target variable y is modeled as having a Normal distribution:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:y\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mu\:\left(x\right),\sigma\:\left(x\right)\right)$$\end{document}

Here, µ(x) is the conditional mean (expected value); σ(x) is the conditional standard deviation, x is the input feature vector.

The NGBoost model learns the parameters µ(x) and σ(x) through the minimized negative log-likelihood (NLL) function:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{L}}_{{NGBoost}} = - \mathop \sum \limits_{{i = 1}}^{N} \log p\left( {y_{i} |~\mu \left( {x_{i} } \right),\sigma \left( {x_{i} } \right)} \right)$$\end{document}

In this study, the average parameter of the NGBoost model µ(x) is used for the final prediction:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:{\widehat{y}}_{NGB}=\mu\:\left(x\right)$$\end{document}

While NGBoost has the ability to predict both conditional mean µ(x) and conditional standard deviation σ(x), only the conditional mean predictions are taken into consideration for this analysis. This decision was made since other models included in this analysis only predict the mean and no other statistics.

Although the NGBoost model is a powerful predictor, it can produce systematic errors in complex and non-linear patterns. To model these errors, a multi-layer dense neural network was used in the second stage.

The residuals for the NGBoost predictions are calculated as:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:r=y-{\widehat{y}}_{NGB}$$\end{document}

The MDN model learns these residuals by taking vector x as input57:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{r}={f}_{MDN}(\mathrm{x};{\uptheta\:})$$\end{document}

Here, fMDN(.) denotes the parameters learned by MDN (θ).

In this hybrid model, the ultimate output is computed by merging the output from NGBoost with the residual output from MDN:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{y}}_{Hybrid}={\widehat{y}}_{NGB}+\widehat{r}$$\end{document}

In this way, the overall predictive ability of the NGBoost model is maintained, and at the same time, MDN is able to model both local and nonlinear error components. This helps to minimize both bias and variance.

The suggested hybrid approach adopts a sequential learning method. During the first phase, training is performed for the NGBoost algorithm based on the input variables to obtain an initial estimation of the output variable. During the second phase, the residual errors are calculated as the differences between the actual values and NGBoost estimations. These residual errors are employed as target values in order to train the MDN model with respect to the input variables. The final output value is estimated through the combination of NGBoost estimation and the residual estimation from the MDN algorithm. The two algorithms undergo separate learning in a sequential manner.

Evaluation metrics

The models’ performance was evaluated by using the metrics Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Mean Squared Error (MSE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE), and the coefficient of determination (R²). MAPE was adopted as the main criterion in this study for comparing different models. Other criteria (MAE, MSE, RMSE, and R²) were included for additional evaluation purposes.

MAPE determines model accuracy by averaging the absolute percentage differences between estimated and true values. Considering the dataset outputs for Heating Load (HL), the actual values are denoted as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:{y}_{i}$$\end{document}, while the predicted outputs are also represented as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{y}}_{i}$$\end{document}, with the index ranging from 0 to N, MAPE is calculated as13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$MAPE = 100\% \mathop \sum \limits_{{j = 1}}^{N} \frac{{\left| {y_{i} - \hat{y}_{i} } \right|}}{{y_{i} }}$$\end{document}

The MAPE performance is given here in fractional form (between 0 and 1). For the computation of MAPE, the problem of dividing by zero was solved by adding a small constant number to those instances where the target value equals zero. In the MAPE calculation, values equal to zero were assigned the value of 1. Since the heating load is zero at temperatures above 15.5 °C, the value of MAPE cannot be computed since the denominator is zero. Thus, all samples whose actual values are zero were not included in the computation of MAPE. MAPE computation involved only samples with non-zero target values. This is a widely practiced technique used in literature to avoid dividing by zero problems in percentage computation.

MSE is defined as the mean of the squared differences between predicted values and the actual outcomes and defined as14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$MSE = \frac{1}{N}\mathop \sum \limits_{{i = 1}}^{N} \left( {y_{i} - \hat{y}_{i} } \right)^{2}$$\end{document}

RMSE corresponds to the square root of the mean squared errors, reflecting the standard deviation of prediction errors and is calculated as15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$RMSE = \sqrt {\frac{1}{N}\mathop \sum \limits_{{i = 1}}^{N} \left( {y_{i} - \hat{y}_{i} } \right)^{2} }$$\end{document}

MAE indicates how much the predicted values deviate from the actual ones. It is computed as the mean of the absolute differences between predictions and true values and is evaluated as16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$MAE = \frac{1}{N}\mathop \sum \limits_{{i = 1}}^{N} \left| {y_{i} - \hat{y}_{i} } \right|$$\end{document}

Each of the error metrics captures the extent of deviation between predictions and observed data. Hence, reduced error values signify improved model effectiveness.

R² indicates the goodness of fit of the model to the data. Its value ranges from 0 to 1, and a value closer to 1 shows stronger model performance. Here, the mean of the predicted outputs is denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\stackrel{-}{y}}_{i}$$\end{document}, then R² is calculated as17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^{2} = 1 - \frac{{\mathop \sum \nolimits_{{i = 1}}^{N} \left( {y_{i} - \hat{y}_{i} } \right)^{2} }}{{\mathop \sum \nolimits_{{i = 1}}^{N} \left( {y_{i} - \bar{y}_{i} } \right)^{2} }}$$\end{document}

The various error measures consider the accuracy of the model under consideration from different angles. When it comes to MAPE, which considers the error rate in percentage form, there is also MAE and RMSE, which consider the error rate in terms of its size. Especially when the target value is lower, MAPE shows even small error rates as high percentage rates.

Hyperparameter optimization

The models population has been tuned with appropriate strategies to allow fair comparison and reproducibility. For conventional machine learning models (XGBoost, LGBM, HGB, DT, ENet, ELM and Hybrid NGBoost + MDN), grid search was employed to find the best hyperparameters. The search ranges were derived from previous works & piece-wise observations through iterative trials of a few prescriptions and the best performing parameter configuration can be elected by validation based decision making.

In the case of deep learning methods (LSTM and CNN), the method of grid search was not performed due to the high computational burden required for extensive hyperparameters’ optimization in deep neural networks. Unlike traditional machine learning algorithms, deep learning algorithms have a large number of possible hyperparameters, including the number of layers, number of neurons, batch size, number of epochs, etc. Therefore, tuning for LSTM and CNN models was done manually considering results from previous research and experimentation. A set of model settings was tested, and finally, the selected models were chosen by minimal validation error. Since the performance of all models is compared using the same metric, namely MAPE, in order to provide equal optimization criteria for all models, different optimization techniques can be used without affecting their performance evaluation.

The high computational intensity of exhaustive search in deep architectures was the reason for choosing manual hyperparameter tuning. Therefore, hyperparameters had been tuned iteratively, on the advice of literature and experience, in search of a balance of the models accuracy and efficiency at full computational potential.

Results and discussion

This section provides the report of the experimental results in the heating load dataset. Predictive accuracy and not computational cost is at the center of this study; hence no time comparisons have been made. The training data was utilized in only model training whereas predictive accuracy was analyzed using a different independent test data to guarantee objective performance evaluation. Nine predictive models, namely, HGB, ENet, DT, LGBM, ELM, CNN, LSTM, XGBoost and Hybrid NGBoost + MDN models, were created and compared systematically. The selection of models is mainly dependent on the MAPE values from the test dataset. Experiments were run on Windows system with 16 GB RAM, four core processor and the models were written in Python with scikit-learn and Keras libraries. To evaluate the performance, MAPE was chosen as the key indicator due to its scale-independence as well as reflecting the relative errors in a manner which can be directly used to interpret the data in the context of the energy management. Other measures of error including MAE, RMSE, and R2 were also computed to contribute additional information to the analysis but the focus on MAPE enabled a more dependable comparison among diverse predictive methods.

ENet, LGBM and HGB also performed well with competitive results with a relatively low MAPE and consistent values of RMSE and can be considered good substitutes among other traditional regression and boosting models. Deep learning-based methods like CNN and LSTM were useful in catching intricate temporal and nonlinear patterns, however, their MAPE values were a bit higher than those of ensemble approaches, whereas ELM provided a computationally efficient approach with reasonable predictive quality. Interpretability is another feature provided by DT and XGBoost, which allows understanding the importance of input variables in the model with significantly higher error rates than Hybrid NGBoost + MDN. Overall, these findings prove that the use of predictive models assessed on the basis of MAPE, supplemented by MAE, RMSE, and R2 facilitates the effective and balanced interpretation of the predictive model strength and weaknesses to be able to identify the most effective ways to forecast heating loads in energy-efficient buildings. Additionally, the stability of the models is confirmed through cross-validation experiments discussed in Section “Statistical insights into R²-based model evaluation”, where we observe consistently high R² values with very small standard deviations. All experiments were conducted using the same computer configurations. This paper aims at analyzing the models’ ability to predict; nonetheless, it was also noted that inference times were adequate enough for real-time BEMS systems. The main objective of this research paper is to compare various models for their effectiveness rather than to prove their statistical significance. It is important to point out that no significance tests were carried out, hence the results should only serve as indicative and not conclusive. So the results presented should not be taken as evidence of statistical significance, only indicative. Since the differences between models are quite small, it might be necessary to conduct some form of statistical test to prove superiority conclusively. The results presented in this paper refer to a particular building and its environment.

Comparative performance study of models on heating load

Table 3 presents the results of the performance measures of the considered models in a variety of error measures. Concerning MAPE, hybrid NGBoost + MDN method (0.05) had the lowest value, which implies greater accuracy than the other methods. HGB method (0.257) and ELM (0.272), XGBoost and LGBM models were also found to perform competitively with MAPE values amounting to 0.293.

Taking into consideration that MAE, NGBoost + MDN (0.14), ENet (0.106) and LGBM (0.113) gave the lowest errors, it could be argued that their models give more consistent predictions in terms of absolute deviations. In comparison, LSTM (0.312) and HGB (0.350) obtained rather high MAE.

In terms of MSE and RMSE, XGBoost (0.063, 0.251) and ENet (0.065, 0.256) performed better, as they exhibited lower squared errors, whereas DT and LSTM showed greater deviations.

When R 2 Score is taken, all the models attain values greater than 0.99, meaning that there is a high level of explanatory power. Of these, DT (0.9989) performed marginally better than the others, but the differences were of minor significance. The high R² values obtained for all the models are not because of the simplicity of the problem; instead, they arise because of the dependence that is present in the data itself.

It was shown that Hybrid NGBoost + MDN recorded the smallest MAPE with a high R2, showing better accuracy and strength in representing the nonlinear dynamics of heating load.

On the whole, the comparative analysis shows that the NGBoost + MDN approach have the advantage of reducing percentage-based error (MAPE), whereas XGBoost and ENet can minimize the squared error measures. The large values of R 2 in all the models will indicate that each of the methodologies can model the underlying trends of the heating load data with great precision.

The higher effectiveness of the NGBoost + MDN method is due to its hybrid nature based on probabilistic prediction combined with residual learning. On the one hand, NGBoost learns the conditional probability density function of the dependent variable, while on the other hand, the MDN part takes into account complicated nonlinear residual relationships, which cannot be captured by any traditional approaches. Such a two-layered process helps to decrease variance and bias, thus increasing the predictive quality.

Another successful category includes Tree algorithms like XGBoost, LGBM, and HGB, since these methods have the power to detect any nonlinear relationship between the features. Nevertheless, it is quite difficult to model complicated residual dependencies for these algorithms. Simple linear regression models, including ENet Regression, have very similar performances to other models when evaluated on the MAE metric. However, they cannot find non-linear dependencies, which is why they perform less efficiently than other models. When considering deep learning techniques such as CNN and LSTM, one can note that they do not outperform ensemble models. The reason for this could lie in the nature of the data, which already contains lagged variables that reflect its time dependence.

Moreover, the data used for this study contains significant time dependency and nonlinear correlations between the input variables and the heat load. The inclusion of lagged-based features somewhat reduces the complexity of the learning process, which is an advantage to tree-based and hybrid models rather than deep learning models. This accounts for the superior performance of the ensemble and hybrid models relative to the LSTM and CNN models.It should also be mentioned that the quality of work performed by models largely depends on the characteristics of the dataset and capabilities of the models used, which highlights the importance of choosing proper models.

From the list of inputs, it is seen that ambient temperature and its lagged versions (t − 5 min and t − 24 h) have the strongest influence on predicting the heating load. This makes sense because the main reason for heating demand is based on the ambient temperatures. The lagged versions of the heating load itself are also strongly influential predictors. This can be interpreted as the strong dependence of the heating demand time-series data on past demands. The effect of solar radiation on heating demand is less strong than that of ambient temperature and acts in an indirect way, which means that heating demand depends indirectly on solar radiation through passive solar heating.

The first aspect is the existence of obvious seasonality in the heating load data where extremely low or even zero heating loads occur in the warm seasons, whereas higher heating loads appear in the cold seasons. As should be noted, the value of MAPE is calculated with respect to non-zero heating loads in the utilized data sets. With regard to evaluating the model performance practically, one should take into consideration the possibility of the model’s different behavior when working with other sets of data with various loads. In general terms, it may be said that models tend to show stability in forecasting smaller and medium levels of heating loads and may exhibit some deviations when forecasting higher heating loads due to considerable changes in the environment.

Error-based analysis

In this part, the recorded values indicate the mean size of the prediction errors calculated as the absolute value of the difference between the actual and estimated values. The recorded values are used to assess the pattern of errors in each model.

In the study, the success of the hybrid NGBoost + MDN model developed for building heating load estimation was validated by applying nine different machine learning methods for error-based analysis. Error-based analysis is focused on the residual distributions of the prediction processes of the models, and it reflects the robustness of the models against the stochastic nature of the system.

For the Error-based analysis in this study, it was done using the prediction errors of the models. The difference between the predicted and actual value was determined for each of the models, and the distribution of the errors was evaluated. When performing the evaluation, the model reliability was assessed based on both the size and the distribution of error values. This implies that when there is a smaller distribution of errors, the model produces more reliable results. The Residual Prediction Deviation (RPD) was one of the tools used to assess the model performance in terms of uncertainty. It was calculated as the ratio of the target variable to the error of the model, and the larger the RPD values, the better the performance of the model.

When considering the analysis result presented in Fig. 12, it is determined that these methods are organized in a special hierarchy concerning the Error-Based Analysis. The recorded values (for example, 0.1069, 0.3125) show the mean size of prediction errors for each model. The methods such as Elastic Net (0.1069), LightGBM (0.1140), XGBoost (0.1155), CNN (0.1278), and the proposed NGBoost + MDN (0.1404) methods are classified as the most reliable methods in which the uncertainty is minimized. The DT (0.1601) and ELM (0.1754) methods are located in this region with increased residual values. It has been determined that the stability of these methods is reduced during variable parameters such as building internal heat gains. It was observed that the LSTM and HGB models had the highest prediction errors in comparison with other models. In particular, the LSTM models based on time-series analysis using deep learning techniques showed higher error boundaries for dynamic data behavior, possibly related to gradient variations during training.

As a matter of fact, the error-based analysis in this problem demonstrates that the proposed hybrid model not only acts as a predicting tool but also turns out to be the suggested NGBoost + MDN model showed comparable and consistent performance in terms of stability and prediction accuracy when compared to other models considered in error tolerance critical processes like the prediction of a heating load.Results pertaining to RPD are presented numerically without categorization to avoid threshold-based interpretation without proper supporting evidence.

Conclusions

We performed a comparison of nine well-known machine learning and deep learning models (NGBoost + MDN, HGB, ENet, DT, LGBM for ML while ELM, CNN, LSTM, XGBoost were selected as the DL model) used in predicting building heating load. The data used in this study is TMY data generated from Meteonorm, and not real measured data. This may limit the generality of the models designed as the weather variability and building characteristics may differ from those of the statistical TMY data sets. Results from this research have been based on one building model and one set of climate data. It is thus essential that future research evaluates the performance of the model using other building models and other climate data sets. The hybrid approach for combining NGBoost with MDN creates a unique method by integrating probabilistic forecasting with an MDN model that uses residual nonlinear error to improve predictions. This is what differentiates our approach from conventional ML and existing hybrid methods in the literature. Such an approach differs from other hybrid approaches to boosting and neural networks that use deterministic boosting techniques and do not model residuals. MAPE is a core validation metric in the literature and was used as leading selection criterion while selecting models, so to make sure our comparative analysis leans complementary for accuracy and confidence.

In this research, the best MAPE result is obtained using the novel NGBoost + MDN hybrid approach, suggesting that the hybrid method performs better than other methods with respect to reducing errors. Further proof that this model is efficient comes from the high R² values together with strong performance in different tests conducted to show how competitive this model is against others like ENet (Predict Regression), whose performance was equally commendable but with a few wider errors. CNN and LSTM models, being deep learning techniques, were able to identify nonlinear patterns in the data set; nevertheless, under the current data set configuration, their performance could not surpass that of the ensemble and hybrid models. While DT and XGBoost also provided some new perspective on feature importance and but the trade-off between simplicity, computability as well predictive power became more visible. Along with the success demonstrated, an analysis of errors performed by the hybrid model shows that the algorithm exhibits a higher degree of prediction stability because its predicted values are clustered within a region of low errors, i.e., the prediction residuals are clustered together. It is possible to undertake an uncertainty analysis (i.e., prediction intervals and calibration metrics) in future research. Supplementary analysis was performed using the RPD method, while the main analysis was done using the error metric approach. The RPD index score of 25.845 also supports this finding by indicating the ability of the hybrid model to handle stochastic variations in the space heating load with great confidence and stability.Together these results demonstrate that through a judicious combination of conventional and advanced data-driven methods, it is possible to achieve accurate long term dependable projections for heating loads. The results may help understand how to improve prediction accuracy in building energy management systems under similar circumstances.

While the current analysis concentrates primarily on numerical distinctions in performance, it is demonstrated that the efficiency of the models in heating load prediction is heavily dependent on the match between the model architecture and data properties. Regarding practical applications, it becomes clear that the concept of hybridization can be efficiently implemented within building energy management systems, which may greatly benefit from more accurate predictions of future energy demand. On a final note, despite the encouraging outcomes, the study is based exclusively on a simulated dataset and a concrete building layout. Further research might consider applying the method to real-world data sets and developing uncertainty-sensitive prediction procedures. Additionally, the study shows that including these predictive models into efficient building energy management systems can help in decision making get good results and reduce operational costs alongside minimizing their emissions, emphasizing bridging between computational modelling & real-world applications hence serving as useful recommendations for both researchers and professionals of building integrated energy analytics. Although this research produced some positive outcomes, there are still some limitations that have to be mentioned. Firstly, the data applied to this research was simulated, which does not ensure its applicability to the real world. Secondly, this investigation was carried out under particular climatic conditions with one type of buildings. In other words, it does not consider other types of buildings and climates. What is more, occupant behavior could also contribute to the heating demand but was not taken into consideration. The limitations mentioned above imply the necessity to conduct further research with real-world data.

In addition, this paper also attempts to fill the research gap stated in the Introduction section. For example, in contrast to most of the prior research using only a few algorithms, a wide range of machine learning and deep learning models was analyzed. Moreover, the use of data from the simulation-based approach allows for a more accurate characterization of building energy performance compared to the current research literature. Therefore, through the application of multiple modeling strategies together with data obtained from simulations, this research makes an effort towards closing the research gap. Further research could include exploring prediction mechanisms that factor in uncertainties and creating models that adapt according to varying environmental and occupant situations.

It must also be mentioned that the MDN architecture used is a simple form of residual learning and lacks probabilistic distribution modeling using mixture components like conventional Mixture Density Networks. Future research can consider fully probabilistic MDN architectures combined with NGBoost architecture. In particular, it is important to mention that the developed MDN architecture can be seen as a residual learning architecture and does not incorporate mixture components for probabilistic distribution modeling like in classical Mixture Density Networks. Moreover, the applied dataset includes TMY data calculated via METEONORM software, not the actual data from operations. The usage of statistical models like TMYs may imply the smoothing of extremely climatic phenomena such as heavy cold or heat waves; therefore, the performance of the developed model may be lower under extreme climate conditions. Possible future research topics may involve validation of the introduced approach using actual measured data from building operations. It is important to note that the results presented herein relate to one particular building and specific climatic conditions; thus, they cannot be extrapolated to other buildings and environments without additional verification.
