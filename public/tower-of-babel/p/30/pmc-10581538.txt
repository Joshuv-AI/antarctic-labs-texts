IRplus: An Augmented Tool to Detect Inverted Repeats in Plastid Genomes
Authors: Carmen Díez Menéndez, Peter Poczai, Bernardo Williams, Leena Myllys, Ali Amiryousefi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-throughput sequencing methods have increased the accessibility of plastid genomes, which are crucial for clarifying phylogenetic relationships. Current large sequencing efforts require software tools for routine display of their distinctive quadripartite structure, which is denoted by four junction sites. By concentrating on these junctions and their close vicinity, IRscope has emerged as the standard tool for detection of this structure and creating simplified comparative graphical maps of plastid genomes. Here, we provide an augmented version (IRplus) that encompasses a novel set of functions such as integrated error detection, flexible color schemes, and an upgraded method to detect inverted repeats in genomic sequences. Spanning across the plant tree of life, IRplus allows the quick visualization of various sets of plastid genomes and features, next to smooth interoperability with other widely used annotation file formats and platforms. The IRplus can be accessed at https://irscope.shinyapps.io/IRplus/, and source codes are freely available at https://github.com/AmiryousefiLab/IRplus.

Significance

The expansion and contraction of inverted repeats and bordering regions is principally responsible for the size variation of plastid genomes and conveys significant evolutionary signals. As shown by multiple relevant studies, plastid genome structure analysis has led to novel insights into this organelle's biology aided by individually created IR maps. To further supports this process, IRplus, the upgraded version of its predecessor enables for adaptive identification and scaled visualization of the genetic structure of plastid genomes in vicinity of their junction sites.

Introduction

Plastids found in photosynthetic organisms are endosymbiotic organelles with their own genomes (plastome). Although the origin and timing of the primary endosymbiosis is debated (Irisarri et al. 2021; de Vries and de Vries 2022), this event stands as an important turning point in the evolution of eukaryotic life. It is presumed that ∼1.9 billion years ago in the Paleoproterozoic, a cyanobiont (a symbiotic cyanobacterium) initiated a symbiogenetic event with a heterotrophic eukaryotic host leading to the formation of primary plastids in the monophyletic supergroup called Archaeplastida (or Plantae sensu lato), which includes land plants, glaucophytes, and red and green algae (see Sánchez-Baracaldo et al. 2017; Strassert et al. 2021). The endosymbiosis occurred repeatedly, and plastid-bearing lineages spread across the eukaryotic tree of life (Sibbald and Archibald 2020). Plastids were lost and reduced several times among photosynthetic lineages; they moved horizontally on the phylogenetic tree and can be obtained through kleptoplasty by heterotrophic organisms such as Elysia sea slugs (Havurinne and Tyystjärvi 2020).

The explosive increase in complete plastid genome sequences due to high-throughput sequencing technologies (HTS) challenged the traditional static view of plastid DNA (ptDNA) (Gonçalves et al. 2020; Doyle 2022). It was also revealed that plastid genomes of several green plant lineages underwent significant structural changes and gene or intron losses and demonstrated rate heterogeneity in protein-coding sequences (Wicke et al. 2011, Ruhlman and Jansen 2018; Gonçalves et al. 2019). This rather dynamic nature of plastid genomes is responsible for heritable variation both within and between populations through heteroplasmy driven by recombination separate from biparental inheritance paradigms (Woloszynska 2010; Mower and Vickrey 2018; Gonçalves et al. 2020).

Despite such complex mechanisms, plastid genomes maintain a quadripartite structure across all major green plant lineages, which consists of a large and small single copy (LSC and SSC) region separated by two identical copies of inverted repeats (IRs) creating four separate junction sites (JS) (Goulding et al. 1996). The junctions connecting the IRs with the LSC are called JLA (IRa/LSC) and JLB (IRb/LSC), whereas the endpoints meeting the SSC are labeled as JSA (SSC/IRa) and JSB (IRb/SSC). Although the LSC and SSC regions appear in an inverted orientation and equimolar state due to “flip-flopping” (Ruhlman et al. 2017), the two IRs share the same sequence because of their important function in genome stability (Palmer and Thompson 1982) and replication initiation (Wolfe et al. 1987). However, the presence of IRs is not essential for chloroplast functionality (Mower and Vickrey 2018); IR-lacking genomes accumulate a set of novel repeats around the endpoint of the JS (e.g., inverted repeat-lacking clade of legumes (Sabir et al. 2014)), which generate further structural diversity through repeat-mediated recombination (Ruhlman and Jansen 2018; Gonçalves et al. 2020). Moreover, the IR structure is dynamic, and the quadripartite plastome organization can be regained after ancestral loss in some seed plant lineages (Jost et al. 2022). The two copies also undergo frequent intramolecular recombination, which produces isomeric forms resulting in expansion and contraction of the JS. These identical sequences are then maintained through the continuous operation of gene conversion driven by recombination-dependent replication (RDR) and not through “flip-flop” recombination as previously thought (Kolodner and Tewari 1979; Zampini et al. 2017). Thus, nucleotide substitution is 3-fold lower in the IRs than, for example, in the SSC region (Wolfe et al. 1987; Zhu et al. 2016). The fascinating consequence of this copy-dependent repair mechanism is the phenomenon known as rate heterotachy (Lockhart et al. 2006; Abdullah et al. 2020). Theoretically, genes that moved from the IR to the LSC or SSC should experience a rate acceleration corresponding to their new position, whereas genes that moved from the LSC or SSC to the IR should undergo a deceleration similar to that of other IR genes, known as the IR rate effect. Recent studies provided strong evidence for the existence of this phenomena (Zhu et al. 2016), although some exceptions do exist (Weng et al. 2017). This suggests that there are differences in both the rate and type of mutations throughout the plastid genome. Robbins and Kelly (2023) have recently shown that the evolutionary rate of a plastid gene is contingent upon its positional proximity to the IR within the plastid genome. They found that angiosperm plastid genes that are situated farther away from the IR boundaries have exhibited a greater capacity to explore a wider range of sequence space on which natural selection can act. As a result, the location of a gene and the overall structure of the plastid genome have limited the capacity for adaptive modifications in the chloroplast.

Nonidentical IR sequences escaping homogenization are rare; expansion and contraction create further genome rearrangements that resemble large inversions (Knox 2014). For example, if genes from one end of the SSC are moved to the IR through an expansion and then duplicated in both copies during a contraction, this will look like a large inversion has occurred across most of the SSC, with the new IR boundary as one endpoint. Such structural diversity is rare among most land plants, and the phylogenetic distribution of lineage-specific JS changes shows a conservative evolutionary trajectory. In contrast to angiosperms and nonvascular land plants, streptophytic green algae (Turmel et al. 2017), lycophytes (Smith 2009), ferns (Robinson et al. 2018), and gymnosperms (Chaw et al. 2018) exhibit remarkable structural variation. Despite the explosive growth of whole-genome sequences, many branches of the Archaeplastida phylogenetic tree remain unclear. Results from species that have not previously been explored may reveal fascinating structural variation, highlighting the need to continue investigating the evolutionary dynamics of plastid genomes.

The essence of this exploration is based on comparative genomics through direct comparison of large-scale genetic material of different organisms between each other to gain a better understanding of species evolution and gene functions among plastid genomic regions. Detection of IR expansion/contraction and the correct identification of IRs and possible sequence errors are crucial for the study of structural diversity in plastid genomes (Amiryousefi et al 2018a). In recent years, IRscope (Amiryousefi et al. 2018b) became a widely applied tool to explore the evolutionary dynamics of IRs and to depict JS changes. Here, with the incorporation of a novel set of functions, we present an augmented version of this software (IRplus) that significantly facilitates the detection of the IR regions and deeper comparative analysis of more diverse plastid genomes.

Program Description

We provide downloadable source codes in an open-access format by expanding the functionality of the program beyond what we presented previously. The entire R source code, including all libraries and dependencies, is deposited at https://github.com/AmiryousefiLab/IRplus. The online interactive version of the code is also accessible through https://irscope.shinyapps.io/IRplus/. For enhanced clarity and more control on the program's utility, the updated code functions are formatted and organized in separate files, which require the installation of the RStudio package. All functions have been wrapped together in an R repository, allowing users to download and change the program according to their needs. In general, the main three updates of IRplus are the 1) enhanced ability to detect inverted repeats in the presence of minimal mismatches between these regions among a wider range of species; 2) updated coloring themes, which allow a more versatile depiction of the outputs; and 3) overall finetuning of the existing functions and their parameters based on our experience and user's feedbacks.

Novel Inverted Repeat Detection

By default, built-in methods for automated IR recognition in currently available software (e.g., Lohse et al. 2013) operate under the assumption that all IR sequences are identical and single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP) can terminate IR detection (Zheng et al. 2020). Nonidentical IR sequences may arise from sequencing errors or from mutations escaping copy correction mechanisms. Recently, we developed a novel method to detect mismatches in IR regions (Zheng et al. 2020), and we adopted this algorithm in IRplus, resulting in a faster and more versatile IR detection. In brief, IRplus extracts corresponding genome sequences from the uploaded files and initiates subgenome formation and then maps reverse complement sequences by adjusting the start and endpoints of the IR regions. The optimum radius is determined by an internal function setting its parameters based on the positions of the genes in relation to the various junctions. Therefore, if the first neighboring gene to the JS is located x bp away, that distance will be used as the radius for that particular species and junction. Afterward, the program will repeat the process for the subsequent species with this learned parameter. If this value does not contain any gene marked in the sequence, a new longer distance is determined where the first neighboring gene of that species is detected and then recursively carried over to the previous species. This allows the scalable representation of genes present in the vicinity of the JS in a comparative manner between species under study. In the next step, the whole IR regions are shifted in a sliding window to detect mismatches among them. Finally, the differences between the IRs are highlighted if their sequences are nonidentical. If insertions, SNPs, and deletions are present among IRs, they will be marked with green, orange, and red dots, respectively. If mismatches are located near the JS, they are shown in the specific place where they occur. Otherwise, a text with the number of mismatches of each kind will appear in the middle of the resulting plot (fig. 1 and Supplementary Data S1, Supplementary Material online).

Versatile Coloring Schemes

We outfitted IRplus with a new interactive interface and output themes that provide a considerably easier control and seamless regulation of the output. An option to analyze up to 20 species was added. The user interface changes dynamically according to the chosen number, and a validator section was added to track the number of species included in the analysis. IRplus first attempts to use external libraries, such as Biostrings (Pagés et al. 2022), coRdon (Elek et al. 2022), and genbankr (Becker and Lawrence 2022) to read compatible GenBank (.gb) files. If this is not possible, IRplus uses its own generic functions to extract necessary information regarding the structure of the genomes. One of the major changes in the updated version of the program is its compatibility with files exported from the popular software Geneious Prime (Kearse et al. 2012).

Given the increasing quantity of deposited plastid genomes, it is important to have drawing tools with flexible coloring schemes that can render visual representations of genomes in a user-specified manner (Zheng et al. 2020). We outfitted IRplus with tools and coloring schemes to provide a foundation for more accurate IR expansion/contraction data display. It allows for sequential, divergent, and qualitative schemes that are readily comprehensible, including those with color vision deficiency. Users can choose from different predefined themes (e.g., cherry tree, sea, and sunset) to create visual representations of IR JS and corresponding genes. The resulting plots can be customized using random coloring options. A sample plot with only two species is also shown in the same tab, ensuring comparison of various plotting themes. The updated version of the program allows downloading of results in various file formats (e.g., pdf, png, jpeg, bmp, and tiff), thus simplifying downstream image processing.

Updated Features

Several bugs were fixed to extract species names from different file types. Following submission, the software will start a search for IR regions in the input files. The interactive timing meter provides insight into the calculation work and output variation, and the real-time interactive coloring theme may be utilized to simply generate the required output. Note that the species chosen will result in the identification of a consensus radius for each junction point of the to-be-plotted genes. This implies that the algorithm may not produce optimal results if the input files do not reflect closely related species. When this occurs, the application will alert the user so that the evolutionary distance of the underlying input species can be reduced. Note that the input files may consist of handwritten annotations, GenBank accession numbers, or associated GB files of chloroplast genomes. If no IR is found for some of the species, to avoid errors, the user is advised to run the program again without those species. It is important to note that IRplus is only a plotting tool and does not correct apparent annotation errors of plastid genomes present in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), for example, unexpected truncation of genes, misannotated exon–intron boundaries, or shifted replication of origin (Tillich et al. 2017; Amiryousefi et al. 2018a).

In the Frequently Asked Question (FAQ) section, a new tab is added which is directly connected with GitHub issues section. Based on our experience, this may serve as an interactive forum where users can report bugs or ask relevant questions. Our experience showed number of issues with the use of IRscope in last years communicated to us by the users. These issues ranging from misidentification of the IR regions (due to aberrant dissimilarity of the IRs) to misplaced features of the genome on the resulting plots were addressed in the updated IRplus. Thanks to its improved detection method, also the scope of the input species is expanded considerably to encompass all IR-containing plastid genomes (supplementary Table S1, Supplementary Material online). IRplus was also improved in handling of the SSC reversion function as in a number of reported cases, IRscope failed to conserve the genomic structure at IR JS. Low memory space and its consequential disconnection from servers in IRscope was also bypassed by deploying IRplus on considerably enhanced machines leading to continuous analysis of up to 20 species at once.

Conclusions

IRplus is the update of the widely appreciated online IR detection and mapping software (IRscope) which is created to represent a more accurate genetic architecture of IR JS in plastid genomes. Due to its novel algorithm, IRplus determines the correct location of IRs despite the mismatches introduced in de novo plastid genomes due to sequencing and assembly error. Although the quality of the input files is still of paramount importance, IRplus offers an adaptive capability to partially overcome this issue in existence of the minimal anomalies in the genome sequences. Also, this freeware exhibits a high level of compatibility to annotation and assembly outputs of major tools in this space, such as Geneious and DOGMA. In addition to its many coloring schemes, IRplus allows a versatile interactive adjustment of the output. The free availability of the software’s source code provides yet an additional level of flexibility that allows more expert R users to adopt their results as necessary.
