Introduction

Penetrating coronary artery ulcer (PCAU) is an uncommon and often under-recognized form of coronary artery pathology characterized by disruption of the intima and identified angiographically by contrast extending beyond the vessel lumen [1, 2]. Although arterial ulcers are more frequently described in the aorta, PCAU is rare and poses a diagnostic challenge due to its nonspecific clinical presentation and overlapping features with other acute coronary syndromes.

The sequence of atherogenic processes parallels the development of other arterial ulcerations, beginning with plaque rupture and vascular wall disruption [3]. The pathological significance of PCAU remains unclear due to limited studies [3]. Evidence suggests they may form a nidus for inflammation and destabilize distal plaques [4]. PCAUs likely result from ruptured coronary plaques and are key markers of complex coronary lesions, which predict cardiovascular events [1]. The American Heart Association (AHA) consensus group categorizes coronary artery ulcers as complicated plaques, alongside hemorrhage, thrombosis, and hematoma [5].

High-resolution coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) and intravascular imaging techniques—such as intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) and optical coherence tomography (OCT)—are increasingly revealing PCAU. Yet, its natural history and optimal management remain unclear.

Because PCAU can rapidly lead to life-threatening complications—including intramural hematoma, dissection, thrombosis, and acute myocardial ischemia—timely recognition is critical. Yet, the current literature offers only isolated case reports and small series, resulting in wide variability in diagnostic criteria and treatment strategies and a lack of consensus guidelines for optimal care.

We present a penetrating coronary artery ulcer case with recurrent chest pain, managed conservatively to a successful outcome. We highlight key diagnostic challenges, therapeutic decisions, and clinical implications.

Case summary

64-year-old man with multiple co-morbidities - hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease (CAD), previous percutaneous coronary intervention with a stent in the left anterior descending (LAD) artery, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and peripheral arterial disease (PAD). He presented with recurrent, exertional, left-sided chest pain of 3-months duration.

His medications include metoprolol 25 mg twice daily, atorvastatin 40 mg daily, aspirin 81 mg daily, and losartan/HCTZ 100/25 mg daily. Physical examination and vitals were normal.

He is married with two children; he has no history of cigarette smoking and does not drink alcohol. He has a family history of hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus in both parents but has no known family history of coronary artery disease or chronic kidney disease.

Cardiac workup included an electrocardiogram showing a normal sinus rhythm, and serial Troponin I levels were undetectable. Echocardiogram revealed mild left ventricular systolic dysfunction with ejection fraction (EF) of 45%, grade 1 diastolic function and trace pulmonary regurgitation.

A pharmacologic single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) myocardial perfusion test, ordered for further CAD risk stratification, was abnormal, showing a moderate reversible inferior wall defect of moderate size.

A diagnosis of persistent chest pain with an abnormal nuclear stress test and mild cardiomyopathy was made. A cardiac catheterization was scheduled for further evaluation.

Cardiac catheterization was performed via trans femoral access, under moderate conscious sedation, following hydration with normal saline to prevent contrast nephropathy. A 6-Fr Judkins left 4 (JL4) catheter was used to engage the left main coronary artery and perform left coronary angiography in multiple orthogonal views. A 6-Fr AL-1 catheter was used to engage the right coronary artery due to its anterior superior take-off. Right coronary angiography was performed in multiple orthogonal views.

Left ventriculogram was not performed due to moderate CKD with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 38 ml/1.73/m2. At the end of the procedure, the arteriotomy was sealed with a MynxGrip™ Vascular Closure Device (Access Closure, Inc., Mountain View, CA). The procedure was well tolerated with no adverse event.

Coronary angiography revealed a right-dominant system with an angiographically normal left main coronary artery (LMCA). The left anterior descending (LAD) artery had a widely patent stent in the proximal segment with mild-moderate diffuse disease. The left circumflex (LCX) artery had moderate proximal and distal disease (Figs. 1 and 2). The right coronary artery (RCA) had an anterior superior take off with a grade 2 penetrating central ulcer in the proximal segment along with diffuse moderate disease (Figs. 3).

Outcome and follow up

The patient was managed medically due to the non-obstructive nature of the coronary lesions. He was continued on antiplatelet, statins, beta blockers and angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEI) therapy. He remained chest pain free at 2-week and 3-month follow-up visits.

Discussion

Penetrating coronary artery ulcer (PCAU) remains a sparsely documented clinical entity, with limited case reports and literature available to date. Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease is a major global burden with significant morbidity and mortality and has been shown to have a multifactorial etiology [6]. Atherosclerosis is characterized by the accumulation of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol within the arterial intima, initiating a cascade of pathological events. These include infiltration of macrophages, concomitant activation of inflammatory pathways, proliferation of smooth muscle cells and connective tissue elements, ultimately resulting in plaque formation and arterial narrowing [7]. Atherosclerotic plaques are typically stabilized by a fibrous cap; however, rupture of this cap exposes the highly thrombogenic core to the circulating blood, precipitating acute coronary syndromes [8, 9]. In other instances, an ulcer may form when a plaque rupture [8], which is likely the etiology of PCAU in our case. Ulcerated plaques can either reseal and progress to form a large plaque [10] or persist as chronic ulcers without re-endothelialization [11].

Although studies on coronary artery ulcers are scarce, extensive literature exists on arterial ulcers in other vascular territories, such as the carotid arteries, in which atherosclerosis is the central pathogenic mechanism. These insights offer a more effective approach to understanding PCAU [12]. The presence of a carotid artery ulcer predicts stroke, and a similar thromboembolic prognosis is hypothesized for PCAU [3]. Furthermore, the presence of ulcers in the carotid arteries is considered a strong predictor of plaque instability, and a similar pattern has also been observed in the coronary arteries, with associated adverse outcomes [3]. While plaque morphology is more important than the degree of stenosis in the etiology of acute events in the coronary circulation, stenosis is a significantly important factor in the carotid circulation, as hypoperfusion is often associated with clinical symptoms [3].

As in previous reports [8, 13], a coronary ulcer in the RCA was the significant pathology found during angiography. Although there was no significant stenosis, a spontaneously resolved thrombosis after plaque rupture is a probable mechanism for the recurrent angina in this patient. Because the management of PCAU remains unclear and there are no treatment guidelines, previous cases—including ours—have been managed conservatively [8, 13]. Therefore, more studies on PCAU are needed to clarify its pathologic significance and to develop the most effective management approach, particularly in the era of intravascular imaging. In our case, an intravascular imaging with intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) or optical coherence tomography (OCT) would have provided a three-dimensional image of the ulcer with better characterization, however, this was not available.

Conclusion

Penetrating coronary artery ulcers (PCAUs) are rare angiographic findings that often result from plaque rupture. The lack of established treatment guidelines and the conservative management of previous cases highlight a significant gap in clinical practice. Targeted research is imperative to define evidence-based strategies and optimize management in the era of advanced intravascular imaging.

Patient’s perspective

Living with high blood pressure, diabetes and chronic kidney disease has always required a high level of vigilance, but the recurring chest pain introduced a new layer of fear into my daily life. Each episode felt like a reminder of my vulnerability, and not knowing the exact cause was perhaps the hardest part. When I was told I have a penetrating coronary artery ulcer, it was a diagnosis I had never heard of, which was initially quite daunting. However, the medical team took time to explain that, in my specific case, a conservative approach-focusing on medication and close monitoring rather than immediate intervention was the safest path forward. Since my discharge, I feel more empowered to manage my health. While I remain cautions, knowing there is a clear management plan in place has provided me with a much-needed sense of stability and hope for my long-term recovery.

Take home message

Penetrating coronary artery ulcer is a rarecause of recurrent chest pain which can be managed conservatively whenidentified. It is uncommonly encountered in clinical practice, and currentknowledge remains limited to a small number of case reports and series.Because of its overlapping presentation with other acute coronary syndromes, failure ofrecognition may delay diagnosis and lead to adverse complications such as acutecoronary occlusion. Further studies are needed to determine best clinical practices.
