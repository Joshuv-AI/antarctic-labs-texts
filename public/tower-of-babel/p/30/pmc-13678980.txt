Engineering a bi-functional nanoplatform containing FH peptide for preferential cardiac delivery to counteract doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity
Authors: Jun Li, Zheng-long Zhong, Jing Tang, Yi Chen, Chang-pei Xiang, Jing Li, Ju-long Tian, Xiao-ming Luo, Jian-jie Zou, Yu-qing Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

DOX is a widely used anthracycline antibiotic that plays a crucial role in treating various solid tumors and blood cancers in clinical oncology. Its strong ability to fight tumors comes from its action of intercalating DNA and inhibiting topoisomerase II, which has made DOX a primary treatment option for conditions such as breast cancer, liver cancer, colorectal cancer, and leukemia [1, 2]. However, the effectiveness of this chemotherapy drug is often limited by its dose-dependent side effects, with cumulative cardiotoxicity being the most serious concern. Research indicates that around 10% of patients who receive a total dose greater than 450 mg/m² may experience irreversible heart damage, which can lead to severe complications like congestive heart failure [3, 4].

The pathophysiological mechanisms responsible for DOX-induced cardiotoxicity are complex and involve several interrelated processes. Firstly, the generation of ROS occurs through iron-mediated redox cycling, which can lead to oxidative stress and damage to cardiac cells. Secondly, mitochondrial dysfunction is a significant factor, as DOX disrupts the electron transport chain complexes, impairing energy production and contributing to cell injury. Lastly, the activation of apoptotic pathways, particularly through p53-dependent mechanisms, plays a crucial role in promoting programmed cell death in cardiac tissues. Together, these mechanisms illustrate the multifaceted nature of DOX-induced cardiotoxicity and highlight the need for further research to develop protective strategies against this adverse effect [5, 6]. Despite the understanding of the underlying mechanisms, the available therapeutic options for cardioprotection are still quite limited. Dex is currently the only cardioprotectant approved by the FDA. It provides protective effects primarily through two mechanisms: it chelates iron, thereby reducing oxidative stress, and it stabilizes topoisomerase IIβ, which plays a crucial role in DNA repair and cellular function [7, 8]. Clinical evidence suggests that Dex may reduce the effectiveness of chemotherapy by interfering with the antitumor mechanisms of DOX, highlighting the need for innovative cardioprotective strategies that maintain the efficacy of cancer treatments [9, 10].

Recent advancements in nanomedicine have underscored the potential of PDA, a biopolymer inspired by mussels, which is synthesized through the oxidative polymerization of dopamine in alkaline conditions [11]. Distinguished by its remarkable biocompatibility, pH-responsive drug release capabilities, and versatile surface functionalization potential, PDA has emerged as a highly promising platform for drug delivery [12]. Of particular interest is the FH peptide (FHKHKSPALSPV), a novel cardiac-targeting ligand that specifically binds to Tenascin-C (TN-C), an extracellular matrix glycoprotein that is markedly upregulated in cardiac tissue following DOX-induced injury [13, 14]. This TN-C-mediated targeting mechanism enables the peptide to facilitate selective accumulation of the nanoplatform at sites of myocardial damage.

Our investigation systematically evaluates several key aspects: first, we analyze the physicochemical characteristics and drug release kinetics of PDA-Dex/FH; second, we assess its in vitro biosafety and cardiac targeting efficiency; third, we examine its cardioprotective efficacy against DOX-induced toxicity in both cellular and murine models; and finally, we explore the preservation of DOX’s antitumor activity in tumor-bearing mice. Through these multidisciplinary approaches, we demonstrate that PDA-Dex/FH not only significantly reduces myocardial damage but also preserves the chemotherapeutic effectiveness of DOX, thereby addressing the critical clinical challenge of managing DOX-associated cardiotoxicity.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Dopamine hydrochloride was procured from Sigma Company, Dextroimine was obtained from MCE Company, ammonia was sourced from Aladdin Company, ethanol was acquired from Sinopharm Holdings Co., Ltd., and the targeting peptide was supplied by Gill Biochemistry Co., Ltd.

Synthesis of PDA

Clean a 250-mL beaker to serve as the reaction vessel, then add 90 mL of ultrapure water, 40 mL of absolute ethanol, and 3 mL of ammonia, stirring for 30 min. Subsequently, introduce 10 mL of a 50 mg/mL DA solution and maintain the reaction at 300 rpm for 24 h. After approximately 12 h of reaction time, dilute 50 µL of the reaction mixture into 700–1000 µL of water to monitor particle size. Transfer the reacted solution into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes and centrifuge using a refrigerated centrifuge to remove unreacted DA, ethanol, and medium. Finally, based on the amount of precipitate obtained, add an appropriate volume of pure water to each tube for ultrasonic dispersion and resuspension before conducting particle size measurement and ultraviolet quantification.

PDA-Dex synthesis

To synthesize PDA-Dex, load Dex at a mass ratio of 1:1. Combine 1 mL of a mixed solution containing 1 mg of Dex with 1 mL of a 1 mg/mL PDA solution and stir at 400 rpm for 24 h to facilitate Dex loading via π-π conjugation, hydrophobic interactions, and other mechanisms. Following the reaction, centrifuge the mixture at 13,000 rpm for 10 min to remove unreacted Dex, washing twice to obtain the final product PDA-Dex.

PDA-Dex/FH synthesis

To synthesize PDA-Dex/FH, load FH at a mass ratio of 10:1. Introduce 100 µg of EDC aqueous solution into 1 mL of a 1 mg/mL PDA-Dex solution and stir at 400 rpm for 12 h. Subsequently, add 100 µg of NHS and allow the reaction to proceed for an additional 12 h, followed by the addition of 100 µg of FH and a further reaction period of 24 h to facilitate FH loading via π-π conjugation, amide reactions, and other mechanisms. After completion of the reaction, centrifuge the mixture at 13,000 rpm for 10 min to remove unreacted reagents, washing twice to obtain the final product PDA-Dex/FH.

Material characterization testing

Particle Size and Zeta Potential: Dilute the sample appropriately with ultrapure water, then utilize the particle size and zeta potential model in a Zetasizer NanoPRO particle sizer to assess the particle size and zeta potential of the sample. Sample Morphology: After diluting the sample to a specific ratio, deposit onto a specialized copper mesh, followed by adding 2% uranium acetate for negative staining. Allow it to dry naturally, and observe the morphology of the sample using a TEM. Ultraviolet/Infrared Spectroscopy: Dilute the test sample before employing an ultraviolet spectrophotometer for UV spectrum analysis. All samples were prepared as dry powders for infrared spectroscopy conducted with an infrared spectrometer. Encapsulation Efficiency, Loading Rate, and Release Curve of Drug: Seal 2 mL of the drug-carrying system in dialysis bags with a molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) of 3500 Da and place them into separate bottles containing 10 mL of pH 7.4 PBS buffer containing 0.5% (v/v) Tween-80 to maintain sink conditions; continuously shake at 37 °C and 150 rpm every two hours. Collect 2 mL from each solution for centrifugation. The drug concentration at various time points is analyzed via LC-MS to evaluate drug release behavior from the nano-drug-loaded system by calculating cumulative release amounts over time.

Cell culture and biocompatibility testing

The myocardial cells utilized in the experiment were primary cells isolated from the hearts of neonatal mice and subsequently cultured in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. The culture medium consisted of MEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. A 96-well cell culture plate was utilized to inoculate cells for a duration of 24 h before incubation with nanocomposites of varying compositions. At time points of 0, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h, the supernatant was discarded; CCK-8 detection solution was added. After incubation, the absorbance at a wavelength of 450 nm from each well was measured using a microplate reader.

Detection of the viability of DOX-treated cells and reactive oxygen species by nanocomposites

A 96-well cell culture plate was utilized to inoculate cells for 24 h, followed by the addition of 2 µM DOX and nanocomposites with varying compositions for a subsequent incubation period of 48 h. For further analysis, a separate 12-well cell culture plate was used to inoculate cells for 24 h; subsequently, cells were treated with 2 µM DOX and different nanocomposite formulations for an incubation period of 6 h before rinsing with PBS. Calcein AM (a live cell indicator) and PI stock solution (a dead cell indicator) was introduced for staining imaging under an inverted fluorescent microscope. In another experiment using a new set of wells on a separate 12-well plate, cells were again inoculated for 24 h prior to treatment with either 2 µM DOX or various nanocomposites over an incubation duration of eight hours followed by PBS rinsing. Add TUNEL detection solution and anti-fluorescence quenching DAPI mounting solution was applied prior to imaging under fluorescence microscopy in order to observe and quantify fluorescence intensity. Lastly, another set of experiments involved treating cells in a fresh batch on a new twelve-well culture plate that had been inoculated previously as described above but treated instead with DCFH-DA and Hoechst33342 staining occurred which fluorescence levels were detected via CLSM.

Cell phagocytosis experiment

The cells were plated in a 12-well plate and cultured for 24 h. Following cell adhesion, the supernatant was discarded, and various FITC-labeled nanocomposites were introduced to incubate with the cells for durations of 0 h, 2 h, 4 h, and 8 h. Upon completion of the incubation periods, the cells were washed three times with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, and subsequently stained with DAPI for five minutes. Finally, fluorescence images of the cells were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope to assess fluorescence expression.

Construction of a mice myocardial injury model

C57BL/6 mice were obtained from Weitonglihua, Beijing (male, 5 weeks old). Following a 3-day acclimatization period, an acute myocardial injury model was established through intraperitoneal injection of DOX (total dosage: 2.5 mg/kg/day for five consecutive days). Myocardial injury in the mice was assessed using cardiac ultrasound both prior to and following induction. Upon successful establishment of the models, they were randomly assigned into six groups: blank group, DOX group, DOX + Dex (150 mg/kg) group, DOX + PDA-Dex (Dex: 150 mg/kg) group, DOX + PDA-Dex/FH (Dex: 150 mg/kg) group, DOX + PDA-Dex/FH (Dex: 100 mg/kg) group, and DOX + PDA-Dex/FH (Dex: 50 mg/kg) group. The treatments were administered according to these specified groups, and the animals were sacrificed one week later for subsequent experiments.

Animal imaging

A myocardial injury model was established, followed by the labeling of the nanocomposite with Cy5 dye and administration of the drug via nebulization. In vivo imaging was conducted at 2, 4, 8, and 12 h post-drug administration. At the 12-hour mark, each organ of the mouse (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, brain) was harvested for ex vivo imaging.

Establishment of a mice model for tumor-bearing studies

The triple-negative breast cancer cell line 4T1 cells were used to establish tumor-bearing mouse models. These cells were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Committee of Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. C57BL/6 mice were procured from Weitonglihua, Beijing (male, 5 weeks old). After a 3-day adaptation period, 1.5 × 10^6 cancer cells () were injected into the lower right abdomen of each mouse. Treatment commenced once the tumors reached a volume of 200 mm³. DOX was administered for antitumor therapy at a total dosage of 4 mg/kg, given every two days at a rate of 1 mg/kg per administration for four doses in total. Additionally, Dex intervention was applied at a total dosage of 150 mg/kg. The experimental groups included PBS group, DOX group, DOX + PDA group, DOX + Dex inh. group, DOX + PDA-Dex group, DOX + PDA-Dex/FH group, and DOX + Dex iv..

Biochemical detection

Cells, cell supernatant, mouse heart tissue, and serum should be collected and processed according to the instructions provided in the MDA, cTnI, CK-MB, and LDH assay kits.

RT-PCR

Cultured cells and mouse heart tissue were harvested for total RNA extraction and its concentration and purity were assessed with an ultraviolet spectrophotometer. The reverse transcription reaction should be performed promptly after measurement completion. Primers were designed and synthesized before conducting a qRT-PCR amplification reaction using cDNA as a template with specific primers. Fluorescent signals were detected using a qRT-PCR instrument to derive Ct values. β-actin served as the internal reference gene, allowing calculation of each gene’s expression level via the 2-ΔΔCT method.

Westernblot

Cultured cells and mouse heart tissue were harvested for total protein extraction. The protein concentration of each group was determined by BCA protein quantitative kit, and the sample amount of each group was normalized to 30 µL (the total sample volume was 20 ~ 40 µg). A polyacrylamide gel with a concentration of 10% to 15% was prepared according to the molecular weight of the protein, electrophoretic under the condition of 80 V for 15 min, and then the voltage was changed to 100 V for another 50 min. After electrophoresis, the protein was transferred to the PVDF membrane and sealed with 5% skim milk powder. Then the first antibody was incubated at 4℃ overnight, and the second antibody was incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Soak the PVDF film in ECL luminescent solution for 1 min, then put it into the fluorescence imaging system for exposure and imaging. Scan the grayscale value with Image J software. The expression of protein to be tested = the gray value of the tested band / the gray value of the corresponding internal reference.

Results

Detection and identification of PDA-Dex/FH

Transmission electron microscopy and particle size analysis revealed that the particle size of PDA-Dex/FH was approximately 100 nm, exhibiting uniformity. The dimensions and morphology of the nanoparticles remained unchanged before and after loading, indicating that the loading process had minimal impact on both the size and morphology of PDA-Dex/FH, which demonstrated excellent stability (Fig. 1A and B). The potential measurements indicate that the surface potential of PDA prior to drug loading was − 36.84 mV, which shifted to -43.19 mV post-loading, signifying a substantial alteration in the nanoparticles’ surface potential and supporting the successful incorporation of both PDA and Dex, thereby effectively demonstrating the successful synthesis of PDA-Dex. Furthermore, after loading with the FH, the potential changed to -35.75 mV, reflecting another significant variation; additionally, this change is accompanied by the emergence of FH’s characteristic absorption peak in the ultraviolet spectrum (Fig. 1C and D). Furthermore, the infrared spectrum analysis reveals that PDA exhibits stretching vibration absorption peaks corresponding to C-H at 2962 cm− 1, characteristic skeletal vibration peaks of aromatic rings at 1617 cm− 1, and phenolic C − O tensile vibration absorption peaks at 1285 cm− 1. Dex displays a stretching vibration peak associated with carbonyl groups around 1705 cm− 1, a deformation vibration absorption peak for N-H near 1442 cm− 1, a stretching vibration absorption peak for C-N close to 1024 cm− 1, and a characteristic C-H absorption peak appearing at 937 cm− 1. The FH infrared spectrum presents absorption peaks at 1666 cm− 1 and 1543 cm− 1, which correspond to the vibrational absorptions of the amide I and amide II bands of FH. The infrared spectrum of the synthesized nanocomposite PDA-Dex shows characteristic absorption peaks of PDA at approximately 2910 cm− 1 and notably shifts the skeletal vibration peak attributed to the aromatic ring from 1617 cm− 1 to a blue-shifted position at 1604 cm− 1 due to the interaction between PDA and Dex. Additionally, the infrared spectrum of PDA-Dex indicates characteristic absorption peaks related to Dex near 1709 cm− 1 and 1019 cm− 1; however, their intensities are significantly diminished. A red shift is observed in the Dex absorption peak around this region as it migrates from 937 cm− 1 to an altered position at approximately951cm− 1 with reduced intensity. Lastly, the infrared spectrum of nanocomposite PDA-Dex/FH demonstrates characteristic absorption peaks attributable to both PDA and Dex located at approximately2910cm− 1, 1604 cm− 1, 1019 cm− 1, and951cm− 1, these indicate the successful synthesis of PDA-Dex and PDA-Dex/FH (Fig. 1E). The drug loading rate, as measured by LC-MS, was found to be 29.63%, with an encapsulation efficiency of 42.14%. The release rate of PDA-Dex within 24 h reached 94.64%, while the release rate for PDA-Dex/FH during the same period was recorded at 78.36%. These results indicate that the synthesized materials exhibit a favorable sustained-release effect and confirm the successful synthesis of PDA-Dex. Furthermore, compared to PDA-Dex alone, the drug release from PDA-Dex/FH was significantly slowed down, further supporting its successful synthesis (Fig. 1F and G).

Evaluation of the safety profile of nanocomposites

Following the incubation of PDA and PDA-FH nanocomposites with cells, cell viability was assessed at time points of 0, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h. The experimental results indicated that cell viability did not decrease significantly, suggesting that the Dex-loaded nanocomposites exhibited no toxicity (Fig. 2A). Employing a DOX-induced injury model in vitro, CCK-8 assays revealed a significant reduction in cell viability. Subsequent intervention with PDA resulted in a partial restoration of cell viability. Moreover, treatment with PDA-Dex/FH effectively mitigated DOX-induced apoptosis, leading to a recovery of cell viability exceeding 90% (Fig. 2B).The results of live-dead staining demonstrated that DOX effectively induced significant cell death. Following the administration of PDA, a marked reduction in red fluorescence was observed, indicating a substantial decrease in the proportion of dead cells and suggesting that PDA mitigated the damage caused by DOX. Subsequent intervention with PDA-Dex revealed that Dex attenuated the toxic effects of DOX, leading to a significant and effective restoration of cell viability. When employing PDA-Dex/FH, nearly no red signal was detected, indicating that this nanocomposites substantially alleviated DOX toxicity and restored cells to their normal state (Fig. 2C). To investigate the effect of DOX on the apoptosis levels of cardiomyocytes and the potential ameliorative effects of nanocomposites, TUNEL assays were employed to assess cardiomyocyte apoptosis. The results indicated a significant increase in green fluorescence following DOX treatment, reflecting an elevated level of apoptosis in cardiomyocytes. Conversely, treatment with nanocomposites resulted in a decrease in green fluorescence, with the PDA-Dex/FH group exhibiting the lowest fluorescence intensity. This suggests that DOX-induced apoptosis in cardiomyocytes is significantly inhibited after treatment with PDA-Dex/FH. Notably, due to its lack of targeted delivery, the improvement observed in the PDA-Dex group was less pronounced than that seen with PDA-Dex/FH; however, PDA also demonstrated some capacity to attenuate cardiomyocyte apoptosis owing to its antioxidant properties (Fig. 2D).

Functional validation of nanocomposites in vitro

To investigate the uptake of nanocomposites by cardiomyocytes, the nanocomposites were labeled with FITC, and fluorescence within the cells was assessed using a fluorescence microscope after incubation for varying durations. The results indicated that as time progressed, green fluorescence in the cells increased progressively, demonstrating that cellular uptake of nanocomposites gradually enhanced over time. No significant difference in fluorescence values was observed between the PDA and PDA-Dex groups, suggesting equivalent uptake efficiency by cardiomyocytes. The PDA-FH group showed higher intracellular fluorescence intensity than both PDA and PDA-Dex groups, indicating that the FH peptide alone could promote cellular internalization of nanoparticles. Notably, PDA-Dex/FH exhibited the strongest fluorescence intensity at 8 h, which was significantly higher than that of the other three groups, indicating that incorporation of the targeting peptide augmented nanocomposites uptake by cardiomyocytes (Fig. 3A and E). We employed a model of DOX-induced myocardial damage in cardiomyocytes to conduct functional validation of the nanocomposites. Following DOX induction, cardiomyocytes exhibited oxidative stress damage and fibrosis. Subsequent analysis revealed significant increases in the levels of myocardial injury markers CK-MB, cTnI, LDH, and MDA post-DOX treatment. Given that PDA possesses antioxidant properties, cell damage in the PDA group was reduced compared to the untreated group. However, due to the combined antioxidant and protective effects of both PDA and Dex, all damage markers— CK-MB, cTnI, LDH, and MDA—showed significant differences when compared with the untreated group after treatment. Additionally, owing to the influence of targeting peptides, PDA-Dex/FH demonstrated superior therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 4A). Experiments indicated that following DOX induction, ROS levels in cardiomyocytes significantly increased relative to those in the untreated group, however, ROS levels were downregulated after treatment with various nanocomposite groups. The decrease in ROS levels was particularly notable in the PDA group due to its antioxidant effect. Furthermore, Dex’s ability to mitigate myocardial injury led to a substantial reduction in ROS levels within Dex-loaded PDA formulations. After conjugation with targeting peptides—which enhance phagocytic capacity—the ROS level observed in the PDA-Dex/FH group was significantly lower than that seen across other groups, indeed it represented the lowest level overall, confirming that PDA-Dex/FH exhibits pronounced antioxidant activity (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, to assess the therapeutic effects of nanocomposites at the molecular level, we employed RT-PCR to evaluate the expression levels of apoptosis and fibrosis-related genes, including CTGF, α-SMA, COL-1, FN, BAX, and Caspase-3. The results indicated that following DOX treatment, there was a significant increase in the expression levels of both apoptotic and fibrotic genes in cardiomyocytes, suggesting substantial cellular damage. Post-treatment analysis revealed a slight decrease in gene expression within the PDA group; and the PDA-Dex group exhibited a more pronounced reduction. This indicates that both PDA and PDA-Dex possess therapeutic potential for mitigating DOX-induced apoptosis and damage in cardiomyocytes; however, the superior efficacy observed with PDA-Dex/FH can be attributed to the targeting peptide’s influence (Fig. 4C). In summary, PDA-Dex/FH demonstrates notable protective effects against DOX-induced myocardial injury and apoptosis primarily through antioxidant mechanisms, inhibition of apoptosis pathways, and alleviation of fibrosis.

Functional validation of nanocomposites in vivo

To assess the consistency of the in vivo role of this nanocompound with in vitro findings, we established a mouse model of acute myocardial injury using DOX and evaluated various parameters including cardiac function, apoptosis, oxidative stress, and fibrosis. Our results indicated that following the establishment of the DOX-induced acute myocardial injury model, both EF and FS values significantly decreased in the model group. Treatment with Dex injection resulted in a clear increase in EF and FS values. PDA-Dex treatment also improved cardiac function to a certain extent, but its efficacy was weaker than that of free Dex. Furthermore, inhalation of PDA-Dex/FH at doses of 150 mg/kg, 100 mg/kg, and 50 mg/kg led to dose-dependent improvement in EF and FS values; notably, recovery was most pronounced at 150 mg/kg while it was least effective at 50 mg/kg. The cardiac function recovery in the 150 mg/kg PDA-Dex/FH group was comparable to that achieved with Dex injection, and superior to the PDA-Dex group (Fig. 5A). Heart tissue samples from mice were processed into paraffin sections and subsequently stained with H&E as well as TUNEL assays. H&E staining revealed significant myocardial structural damage and disorganized arrangement of cardiomyocytes following DOX induction; all treatment groups showed varying degrees of structural recovery, with the PDA-Dex group showing moderate amelioration that was inferior to the free Dex group (Fig. 5B). The results of the TUNEL assay demonstrated pronounced positive staining in the model group after DOX induction, indicating extensive cardiomyocyte apoptosis. Following treatment, the apoptotic rate was reduced in the Dex group, and the PDA-Dex group also showed a decreased number of TUNEL-positive cells but with less efficacy than free Dex. Furthermore, inhalation of PDA-Dex/FH at doses of 150 mg/kg, 100 mg/kg, and 50 mg/kg effectively mitigated cardiomyocyte apoptosis in a dose-dependent manner; notably, the anti-apoptotic effect was optimal at 150 mg/kg while it was least effective at 50 mg/kg (Fig. 5B). Following the establishment of the DOX-induced acute myocardial injury model and administration, mouse hearts were harvested and homogenized to assess biochemical markers including MDA, cTnI, CK-MB, and LDH. The results revealed a significant increase in these markers within the model group, indicating substantial myocardial injury following DOX induction. Subsequent treatment with Dex resulted in a marked reduction of myocardial injury indicators, suggesting a robust protective effect. PDA-Dex treatment also lowered the levels of these injury markers, but the magnitude of reduction was smaller than that in the free Dex group. Inhalation of PDA-Dex/FH at doses of 150 mg/kg, 100 mg/kg, and 50 mg/kg also led to dose-dependent improvement in these indicators; notably, recovery was most pronounced at 150 mg/kg while it was least effective at 50 mg/kg. The biomarker recovery in the 150 mg/kg PDA-Dex/FH group was comparable to that achieved with Dex injection, and better than the PDA-Dex group (Fig. 5C). Additionally, we assessed the expression levels of genes associated with fibrosis and apoptosis, including CTGF, α-SMA, COL-1, FN, Caspase-3, and Bax. Our findings indicated a significant increase in the expression levels of these genes within the model group, confirming substantial myocardial damage following DOX induction. Post-treatment analysis revealed a decrease in the expression levels of CTGF, α-SMA, COL-1, FN, Caspase-3, and Bax across all treatment groups. The PDA-Dex group showed moderate downregulation of these genes, but the effect was weaker than that of free Dex. Inhalation of PDA-Dex/FH at doses of 150 mg/kg, 100 mg/kg, and 50 mg/kg resulted in dose-dependent downregulation for each gene; notably, the downregulation effect was most pronounced at 150 mg/kg while it was least effective at 50 mg/kg. The gene expression modulation in the 150 mg/kg PDA-Dex/FH group was comparable to that achieved with Dex injection (Fig. 5D). Beyond evaluating these indicators at the gene level, we also examined their protein expression profiles. The results demonstrated that changes in protein expression were largely consistent with those observed at the gene level. Following DOX-induced acute myocardial injury, protein expression levels for CTGF, α-SMA, COL-1, FN, Caspase-3, and Bax significantly increased, and were downregulated following different drug treatments. The PDA-Dex group showed moderate reduction of these fibrosis- and apoptosis-related proteins, but the efficacy was inferior to that of free Dex. Inhalation of PDA-Dex/FH at doses of 150 mg/kg, 100 mg/kg, and 50 mg/kg led to concentration-dependent downregulation of these proteins; specifically, 150 mg/kg PDA-Dex/FH restored protein expressions close to normal levels, and the effect was similar to that of Dex injection. Although 50 mg/kg PDA-Dex/FH also resulted in some downregulation of related proteins, the efficacy was limited (Fig. 6A). To further assess the targeting efficacy of the FH peptide-modified nanocomposites, we labeled four nanoparticle formulations (PDA, PDA-FH, PDA-Dex, PDA-Dex/FH) with Cy5 dye and administered them via nebulization. In vivo imaging was performed at 2, 4, 8, and 12 h post-administration, followed by ex vivo imaging of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and brain) harvested from mice after a 12-hour period to evaluate targeting accuracy.

The in vivo imaging results indicated that following nebulized administration, fluorescent signals were strongly distributed in the nasal cavities, throats, and lungs at the 2-hour mark for all groups, suggesting substantial initial retention of nanoparticles in the respiratory tract. Over time, fluorescence gradually concentrated within the cardiac region. The unmodified PDA group showed the weakest cardiac fluorescence, while the PDA-Dex group had slightly higher cardiac accumulation. The PDA-FH group exhibited significantly higher fluorescence intensity in the heart than PDA and PDA-Dex groups, proving the cardiac targeting capacity of the FH peptide alone. Notably, PDA-Dex/FH showed the strongest cardiac fluorescence signal among the four groups, with considerable fluorescence retained in the heart at 12 h post-treatment. Ex vivo organ imaging was consistent with the in vivo results: PDA-Dex/FH had the highest accumulation and strongest fluorescence signal in the heart, followed by the PDA-FH group. Collectively, these findings suggest that FH modification effectively enhances the cardiac targeting capability of nanoparticles, and PDA-Dex/FH can efficiently accumulate at the injured myocardial sites (Fig. 6B). After verification, the experimental results in vivo are consistent with the experimental results in vitro.PDA-Dex/FH has shown significant protection against DOX-induced myocardial damage and cell death mainly through antioxidant mechanisms, inhibition of cell death pathways and alleviation of fibrosis, and PDA-Dex/FH has enhanced targeting ability and can effectively target damaged myocardial sites.

Impact of nanocomposites on the antitumor efficacy of DOX

DOX is an anthracycline-class antitumor agent known for its broad spectrum of activity and significant therapeutic efficacy. However, the most serious adverse effect associated with this class of drugs is cardiotoxicity, which limits their clinical application. Dex has been shown to reduce both the incidence and severity of doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity, making it suitable for patients receiving high doses of DOX and thereby enhancing its utility in cancer treatment. In our study, we utilized PDA to formulate nanocomposites incorporating Dex and FH, aiming to optimize the targeting capability of Dex to improve its efficacy in mitigating cardiotoxicity. Nevertheless, it remains uncertain whether this formulation will impact the antitumor activity of DOX; thus, we employed a tumor-bearing mouse model to investigate this question. Analysis of tumor gross morphology revealed that the control group exhibited the largest tumor volume; however, following treatment, a significant reduction in tumor volume was observed across all groups, with minimal differences among them. This suggests that various nanoparticles and administration methods do not significantly influence the anticancer efficacy of DOX (Fig. 7A). H&E staining corroborated this trend, demonstrating a high density of tumor cells in solid tumors from control mice, which decreased post-treatment without substantial variation between treatment groups. Immunohistochemical analysis utilizing the proliferation marker Ki67 and apoptosis marker Caspase-3 indicated elevated expression levels of Ki67 in paraffin sections of solid tumors while Caspase-3 exhibited low expression. Post-treatment, Ki67 expression was downregulated in treated groups whereas Caspase-3 displayed upregulation; however, intergroup differences remained minimal (Fig. 7B and C). The TUNEL assay for detecting apoptotic levels revealed pronounced fluorescence across all groups after treatment, indicating substantial apoptosis within tumor cells irrespective of group assignment—consistent with immunohistochemical findings for Caspase-3 (Fig. 7D). Furthermore, RT-PCR and Western blot analyses assessed mRNA and protein expression levels for Ki67, Caspase-3, Bcl-2, and Bax. Results demonstrated a significant decrease in both Ki67 and Bcl-2 expressions post-treatment alongside marked increases in Caspase-3 and Bax expressions—indicating notable inhibition of tumor cell proliferation coupled with enhanced apoptosis; yet again no significant intergroup differences were noted (Fig. 8A and B). Collectively, these findings suggest that different nanoparticles and administration strategies do not impact DOX’s anticancer activity.

Discussion

In this study, we successfully developed and characterized PDA-Dex/FH, a novel nanocomposite designed to mitigate DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. Our comprehensive analysis, including spectroscopic methods and physicochemical characterization, confirmed the successful integration of Dex and FH onto the PDA platform. Both in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that PDA-Dex/FH effectively treats DOX-induced cardiotoxicity, with FH incorporation enhancing cardiac targeting while preserving DOX’s antitumor efficacy.

Polydopamine has emerged as a versatile coating platform, offering a straightforward and efficient method for surface modification [15, 16]. Its ability to form coatings on various substrates, including metal nanoparticles, polymers, and proteins, enables broad functionalization possibilities [17]. Recent discoveries regarding PDA’s covalent and non-covalent interactions with biomolecules have expanded its applications beyond simple coating to include nanoparticle and membrane formations [18]. The formation mechanism, analogous to biological melanin synthesis, proceeds through covalent polymerization [19]. PDA’s surface chemistry, characterized by catechol and amine groups alongside π-π conjugations, facilitates interactions with diverse substances [20]. The incorporation of Dex occurs through π-π conjugation and hydrophobic interactions, while FH attachment is mediated by amino and carboxyl group interactions. We selected PDA as our carrier system due to its exceptional properties, including simple preparation, cost-effectiveness, biocompatibility, multi-drug loading capacity, and stimulus responsiveness [21].

Physicochemical characterization revealed consistent particle morphology (~ 100 nm) throughout the drug loading process, demonstrating PDA’s structural stability. Sequential zeta potential changes and characteristic spectroscopic signatures confirmed successful synthesis of PDA-Dex/FH. The system achieved 29.63% Dex loading efficiency with 42.14% encapsulation rate and demonstrated controlled release properties (78.36% cumulative release over 24 h).

The biocompatibility assessment showed that neither PDA nor PDA-FH affected cardiomyocyte viability. The FH peptide (FHKHKSPALSPV) specifically targets Tenascin-C (TN-C) [13], which is upregulated during DOX-induced cardiac toxicity [14]. This targeting mechanism enables selective accumulation in injured cardiac tissue [22]. Cellular uptake studies using FITC-labeled nanocomposites demonstrated enhanced internalization of PDA-Dex/FH compared to non-targeted formulations. In vivo biodistribution studies using Cy5-labeled compounds showed initial broad distribution followed by cardiac-specific accumulation, with sustained presence even after 12 h.

Using a 2 µM DOX-induced injury model, we observed that DOX administration significantly reduced cell viability through ROS-mediated damage. PDA-Dex/FH demonstrated superior protective effects, attributed to PDA’s antioxidant properties and Dex’s cardioprotective actions, restoring cell viability to over 90%. ROS level assessments confirmed these findings, with PDA-Dex/FH showing the most pronounced antioxidant effects.

Cardiac function markers (CK-MB, cTnI, LDH, MDA) [23, 24] showed significant improvement with PDA-Dex/FH treatment. In vivo studies demonstrated enhanced cardiac function parameters (EF, FS) following treatment. DOX-induced cardiotoxicity involves multiple mechanisms, including free radical production, apoptosis, and inflammation [25–28]. Histological and molecular analyses revealed that PDA-Dex/FH effectively reduced cardiac fibrosis markers (CTGF [29, 30], α-SMA [31], COL1 [32], FN [33]) and modulated apoptotic pathways [34, 35]. Importantly, while providing cardioprotection, PDA-Dex/FH did not compromise DOX’s antitumor efficacy. Tumor studies showed maintained anticancer activity, evidenced by consistent Ki67 expression [36] and apoptotic pathway activation [37] across treatment groups.

In conclusion, PDA-Dex/FH represents a promising therapeutic strategy for managing DOX-induced cardiotoxicity. Its success stems from the synergistic combination of PDA’s versatile chemistry, Dex’s cardioprotective effects, and FH’s targeting capabilities, offering an effective solution without compromising cancer treatment efficacy.
