Successful Surgical Repair of Large Extracranial Internal Carotid Artery Pseudoaneurysms Causing Cervical Aerodigestive Compression
Authors: Uoc Huu Nguyen, Thang Ngoc Duong, Hien Thi Thu Do
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionExtracranial internal carotid artery pseudoaneurysms are well recognised; however, those large enough to cause airway or oesophageal compression are exceedingly rare. Surgical management of complicated lesions remains difficult and is often accompanied by significant potential for adverse events.ReportTwo patients were diagnosed with extracranial internal carotid artery pseudoaneurysm presenting with airway and oesophageal compression. Both were successfully treated by surgical excision and revascularisation of the internal carotid artery. Post-operative recovery was uneventful in both patients, with no complications observed.DiscussionExtracranial internal carotid artery pseudoaneurysm should be detected and treated early in cases of rapid progression or when complications are present. Surgical excision of the pseudoaneurysm combined with revascularisation provides a definitive solution to the lesion and represents a relatively safe approach.Keywords: Extracranial, Internal carotid artery, Pseudoaneurysm, Vascular trauma

INTRODUCTION

Extracranial internal carotid artery (ICA) pseudoaneurysm is a recognised vascular pathology that may lead to severe complications, including rupture, ischaemic stroke, or death. Common aetiologies include trauma, penetrating vascular injury, infection, and iatrogenic vessel damage.1,2 Endovascular stent graft placement is a good therapeutic option due to its high success rate and low complication rate of approximately 0.9%.3 In contrast, open surgical repair carries a reported mortality rate of up to 22%.4,5 Nevertheless, surgery sometimes becomes the first line treatment in selected cases, depending on institutional resources and patient specific factors. Open repair is technically demanding, requiring experienced vascular surgeons and well equipped facilities.

In this article, two extracranial ICA pseudoaneurysm cases successfully treated by surgical excision of the pseudoaneurysm and revascularisation using reversed great saphenous vein grafts are presented.

REPORT

Case 1

A 34 year old man presented with a history of blunt trauma to the left side of the neck after a motor vehicle accident one year earlier. At that time, he was diagnosed with left ICA injury but declined treatment. One year later, he was admitted with a pulsatile left cervical mass, measuring approximately 5 cm in diameter, associated with pain and dysphagia. Angiography revealed a large pseudoaneurysm arising from the left ICA in the first cervical (C1) segment, located 35 mm distal to the carotid bifurcation. The pseudoaneurysm measured 30 × 43 × 53 mm, projecting medially and anteriorly (Fig. 1A).

Case 2

A 21 year old woman, at 33 weeks gestation, presented with a history of a small left ICA aneurysm measuring 0.5 cm, which had been detected one year earlier during evaluation for headache and neck pain following dental extraction. At that time, conservative management with surveillance was recommended. During her current pregnancy, she was admitted to the hospital with rapid enlargement of a pulsatile left cervical mass, accompanied by headache and dysphagia. Angiography demonstrated a large pseudoaneurysm of the left ICA measuring 35 × 42 × 51 mm (Fig. 1B). The pseudoaneurysm displaced and compressed the oropharyngeal and nasopharyngeal spaces towards the right, while simultaneously invading and displacing adjacent cervical muscles and soft tissues towards the left (Fig. 2). At 34 weeks of gestation, the patient underwent elective caesarean delivery. Three days later, definitive surgical repair of the carotid artery lesion was performed.

Surgical technique

Both patients underwent surgery following a similar protocol. General anaesthesia with endotracheal intubation was administered, and the patient was positioned supine with the head rotated to the right. A skin incision was made along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle to access the carotid sheath (Fig. 2). The common carotid artery, external carotid artery, and proximal segment of the ICA were carefully controlled. The pseudoaneurysm was maximally exposed (Fig. 3A). A 5 cm segment of the great saphenous vein was harvested and mounted on a 3 F Fogarty catheter (Fig. 3B). Systemic anticoagulation was achieved with intravenous heparin at a dose of 50 IU/kg body weight. Systolic blood pressure was maintained at 140 mmHg throughout the procedure.

(A) Exposure of pseudoaneurysm and carotid arteries (white arrow: pseudoaneurysm, white arrowhead: ICA). (B) Distal anastomosis between great saphenous vein and ICA (white arrow: segment of great saphenous vein, white arrowhead: orifice of ICA). ICA = internal carotid artery.

The common and external carotid arteries were clamped, and the pseudoaneurysm sac was opened longitudinally. The Fogarty catheter with the prepared saphenous vein graft was introduced into the distal ICA, and the balloon was inflated to achieve temporary haemostasis. End to end anastomosis between the reversed saphenous vein graft and the distal ICA was performed using a continuous 6-0 Prolene suture. After completion of the distal anastomosis, the Fogarty catheter was withdrawn, vascular clamps were applied to the graft, and the proximal anastomosis between the graft and the common carotid artery was completed. All thrombi within the pseudoaneurysm sac were evacuated, and the collateral branches were ligated for haemostasis. A negative pressure drain was placed, and the wound was closed in anatomic layers. Carotid clamping times were 42 minutes and 40 minutes in the two cases, respectively. Intra-operative blood loss was <200 mL.

Post-operative outcomes

Both patients were extubated immediately after surgery without neurological complications. No hoarseness or dysphagia was observed. Doppler ultrasound of the carotid arteries performed on post-operative day four demonstrated good patency of the saphenous vein grafts, with normal flow velocities and no evidence of anastomotic stenosis. Surgical drains were removed, and both patients were discharged on post-operative day five.

At one month follow up, clinical examination and Doppler ultrasound confirmed stable findings with satisfactory graft function.

DISCUSSION

Extracranial ICA pseudoaneurysm is a rare vascular lesion, accounting for approximately 0.4–7% of all peripheral pseudoaneurysms.5 In this series, one patient had a clear aetiology related to direct cervical trauma, whereas the other had no history of trauma; in the latter case, the pseudoaneurysm was presumed to be associated with infection after a dental procedure one year earlier. Both mechanisms are consistent with those reported by other authors.1,5 In direct trauma cases, disruption of the arterial wall leads to loss of elasticity and weakening of the vessel, which may subsequently progress to aneurysm or pseudoaneurysm formation over time. In both patients, the vascular lesion was detected one year before presentation; however, hospital admission occurred only after rapid enlargement of the pseudoaneurysm, resulting in compression of the airway and oesophagus and the onset of clinical symptoms. These findings underscore the importance of close surveillance and timely intervention for carotid artery lesions, even in asymptomatic patients, to prevent life threatening complications.

According to current guidelines for the management of cerebrovascular trauma, angiography of the carotid system is the first line modality for definitive diagnosis of carotid artery injuries.6 Beyond establishing the diagnosis, angiography provides essential anatomic information for surgical planning, including the location and size of the pseudoaneurysm, as well as the length of healthy proximal and distal arterial segments. In this report, the distal diameters of the ICA were 1.5 mm and 1.0 mm, respectively. Although covered stent placement in the ICA is considered the gold standard for treatment, open surgical repair was chosen for several reasons. Firstly, the large cervical pseudoaneurysms in both patients caused significant compression of the airway and oesophagus; stent placement alone would not have relieved this mass effect, whereas surgery allowed complete excision of the pseudoaneurysm and surrounding tissue. Secondly, in both cases the distal ICA segment was located >2 cm below the skull base, permitting safe vascular anastomosis. Finally, both patients were young; endovascular stenting would have required dual antiplatelet therapy for at least three months, followed by long term single antiplatelet therapy, with the attendant risk of future stent thrombosis. In contrast, revascularisation using autologous saphenous vein grafts enabled restoration of normal flow without the need for prolonged adjunctive therapy. Several studies have demonstrated that revascularisation with vascular grafts significantly reduces the risk of neurological complications and death compared with simple pseudoaneurysm ligation or excision.1,5,6,7 The authors’ findings are consistent with this evidence, supporting the role of open surgical reconstruction as a safe and effective alternative in selected patients.

Because the pseudoaneurysms extended close to the skull base, extensive dissection of the lesions was deliberately avoided to minimise the risk of injury to cranial nerves XII, V, and VII, which traverse the parotid region. Consequently, the proximal ICA could not be fully exposed or controlled with an external vascular clamp. Although this represents a limitation of the technique, it also offers advantages by significantly reducing operating time and limiting blood loss during dissection. To achieve distal control of the ICA, an endoclamp technique was employed using a 3 F Fogarty catheter. After opening the pseudoaneurysm sac, the Fogarty catheter was advanced intraluminally and the balloon inflated. This manoeuvre provided secure distal control and facilitated clear visualisation of the ICA orifice, allowing safe and precise anastomosis with the reversed saphenous vein graft.

A unique aspect of the second case was that the patient was 33 weeks pregnant. Given the anticipated prolonged carotid surgery and the need for systemic intra-operative heparinisation, which could adversely affect the foetus, the authors consulted with obstetric specialists and opted for conservative management until 34 weeks of gestation. Elective caesarean delivery was performed, and three days later, once uterine involution was satisfactory and the risk of haemorrhage had subsided, definitive carotid surgery was undertaken.

Follow up results demonstrated that both patients remained clinically stable after surgery, with resolution of airway and oesophageal compression symptoms. No neurological complications were observed, and Doppler ultrasound confirmed graft patency. These findings suggest that the surgical strategy employed was appropriate and effective in these challenging cases.

In conclusion, extracranial ICA pseudoaneurysm is a rare and challenging vascular lesion. Although endovascular interventions have advanced considerably and are widely regarded as the standard of care, open surgical excision of the pseudoaneurysm combined with revascularisation using an appropriate strategy remains a safe and definitive treatment option. This approach not only restores vascular continuity but also allows complete removal of the pathological mass, thereby addressing both the haemodynamic and compressive effects of the lesion.
