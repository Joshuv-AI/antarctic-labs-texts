Clarifying the Obesity Paradox in Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement
Authors: Sant Kumar, Hursh Naik, Nezar Falluji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigher body mass index (BMI) has been associated with better outcomes after transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR), but whether this “obesity paradox” varies by age is unclear.ObjectivesThe objective of the study was to determine whether age modifies the association between BMI and 1-year outcomes after TAVR.MethodsThe authors performed a retrospective analysis of the Society of Thoracic Surgeons/American College of Cardiology Transcatheter Valve Therapy Registry, including patients who underwent TAVR between January 2021 and February 2023. Patients were categorized by age (<65 or ≥65 years) and BMI (<25 or ≥25 kg/m2). The primary outcome was 1-year all-cause mortality. Secondary outcomes included major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events: stroke, cardiac mortality, myocardial infarction, and readmission. Inverse probability of treatment weighting regression models were used to assess outcomes.ResultsAmong the 6,639 patients (mean age 76.7 ± 11.6 years; mean BMI 29.7 ± 9.9 kg/m2), 1-year all-cause mortality occurred in 427 (6.4%). Among patients <65 years, outcomes did not differ between BMI groups (P > 0.05). Among patients ≥65 years, BMI <25 kg/m2 was associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality (adjusted HR [aHR]: 1.29; 95% CI: 1.05 to 1.57; P = 0.013) and major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (aHR: 1.20; 95% CI: 1.08-1.34; P = 0.001) vs those with BMI ≥25 kg/m2. The association between higher BMI and lower mortality strengthened with advancing age and was significant in patients 75 to 84 years (aHR: 0.60; 95% CI: 0.44-0.82; P = 0.001) and ≥85 years (aHR: 0.36; 95% CI: 0.25-0.51; P < 0.001), but not in younger strata.ConclusionsHigher BMI was not uniformly protective. Among older adults, lower BMI was associated with a higher-risk phenotype and worse 1-year outcomes.

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has become an increasingly important treatment option for severe symptomatic aortic stenosis and is now widely used in contemporary practice. This expansion has been concentrated in older adults. Carroll et al1 reported a steady rise of TAVR volume in the United States, with a median treated age of 80 years. Attinger-Toller et al2 showed that most TAVR candidates in SwissTAVI were 70 to 90 years of age and that more than two-thirds were older than 80 years, whereas Sharma et al3 demonstrated that by 2019, TAVR had become the treatment of choice in more than 97% of patients older than 80 years and was increasingly used across the 65- to 80-year range as well. As TAVR is increasingly performed in patients older than 70 years, there has been growing interest in whether body mass index (BMI) broadly modifies postprocedural risk. The obesity paradox refers to the counterintuitive observation that overweight or obese patients may experience better outcomes than normal-weight or underweight patients despite obesity being a traditional cardiovascular risk factor.4

The existing literature on TAVR and BMI is mixed.5, 6, 7, 8, 9 Sharma et al7 observed higher 30-day and 1-year mortality among underweight patients and lower 1-year mortality among overweight and class I/II obese patients. Conversely, McInerney et al8 found similar 2-year survival rates in morbid obesity and nonobesity patients. More recently, Reichl et al9 found no survival advantage for obesity itself. Together, these data highlight several gaps, including heterogeneous BMI definitions and the absence of direct evaluation of age as an effect modifier of the BMI-outcome relationship after TAVR.

Because contemporary TAVR practice is increasingly concentrated in older adults, an age-neutral interpretation of BMI may obscure clinically important heterogeneity and may misattribute risk to obesity rather than to a lower-BMI, frailty-prone phenotype. We therefore sought to determine whether age modifies the association between BMI and 1-year outcomes after TAVR in a contemporary registry cohort from a large integrated health system, with specific emphasis on all-cause mortality and other major adverse clinical events.

Methods

Data source

This study utilized data from the Society of Thoracic Surgeons/American College of Cardiology Transcatheter Valve Therapy (STS/ACC TVT) registry, which captures clinical information on patients undergoing commercial TAVR in the United States. The registry includes demographic, imaging, and procedural data, with outcomes assessed during hospitalization and at 30 days and 1 year. Participation is mandated by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services as part of its coverage determination for all U.S. Food and Drug Administration–approved TAVR devices, and the TVT registry is the only registry designated to meet this requirement.10 Data integrity is maintained through standardized data definitions, routine quality checks, and independent yearly audits.11 The registry functions with approval from a central institutional review board and has a waiver of informed consent, as per federal regulations.

The present analysis included patients within the TVT registry who underwent TAVR at hospitals affiliated with the CommonSpirit Health system between January 2021 and February 2023 and had both age and BMI data available. The CommonSpirit Health Cardiovascular Service Line and the CommonSpirit Research Institute Institutional Review Board, which acts as the Institutional Review Board of record, oversaw the management of the CommonSpirit TAVR research database, including data use and ethical oversight for this study.

Outcomes and definitions

The primary outcome of interest was all-cause mortality at 1 year. This endpoint was selected because the study objective was to evaluate whether BMI reflects an age-dependent risk phenotype after TAVR, rather than early periprocedural risk. Although 30-day outcomes are available in the TVT registry, 1-year mortality was considered more clinically relevant for assessing the obesity paradox in an older TAVR population. Secondary outcomes included major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCE), defined as a composite of cardiac mortality, myocardial infarction (MI), stroke, or all-cause readmission. Cardiac mortality was classified as mortality due to MI, heart failure, arrhythmia, structural cardiac issues, or other cardiovascular causes, as determined within the registry. MI and stroke definitions adhered to the standardized TVT registry criteria aligned with Valve Academic Research Consortium standards used during the study.12,13 Readmission was defined as any rehospitalization due to any cause, as this measure is likely to be recorded more objectively and consistently than cardiovascular-specific readmission in registry- or claims-based follow-up.14,15 Clinical outcomes in the TVT Registry were documented during the initial hospitalization, as well as at 30 days and 1 year after the procedure. To ensure thorough detection of postdischarge events, all outcomes were additionally linked with the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services claims database for Medicare beneficiaries.

Major bleeding was identified based on registry-reported criteria aligned with standardized TVT registry definitions.13 It included overt bleeding accompanied by a hemoglobin decrease, bleeding necessitating transfusion or procedural intervention, or bleeding leading to hemodynamic instability. The evaluation of procedural complications covered cases of permanent pacemaker implantation during the initial hospitalization, emergency surgery, and significant vascular complications.13

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as mean ± SD and compared using the independent sample t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as absolute counts and percentages and compared using the chi-square or Fisher exact test, as appropriate.

The primary outcome was 1-year all-cause mortality. Time-to-event analyses were conducted from the date of TAVR to mortality or censoring at 1 year or the last available follow-up, whichever occurred first. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to estimate HRs with 95% CIs.

The primary analysis categorized patients into 4 prespecified groups according to age and BMI: BMI <25 kg/m2 and age <65 years, BMI ≥25 kg/m2 and age <65 years, BMI <25 kg/m2 and age ≥65 years, and BMI ≥25 kg/m2 and age ≥65 years. Baseline characteristics, procedural features, and clinical outcomes were compared across these groups. To account for baseline differences between BMI groups within each age stratum, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was performed using propensity scores derived from logistic regression models that included baseline clinical and procedural covariates. Covariate balance after weighting was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with residual imbalance addressed using doubly adjusted Cox proportional hazards models that included covariates with postweighting SMD >0.10. Pairwise unadjusted and IPTW-adjusted HRs for clinical outcomes across the 4 age/BMI groups were then calculated.

We assessed the potential impact of missing data using multiple imputation by chained equations under a missing-at-random assumption. The imputation model included age, BMI, sex, STS risk score, left ventricular ejection fraction, baseline creatinine, comorbidities, procedural characteristics, access site, valve type, procedure status, clinical outcome indicators, follow-up time, and the Nelson-Aalen cumulative hazard for time-to-event analyses. Twenty imputed data sets were generated. Continuous variables were imputed using predictive mean matching, binary variables using logistic regression, and categorical variables using multinomial regression. Propensity-score weighting and Cox proportional hazards models were then re-estimated in each imputed data set and pooled using Rubin’s rules.

Because the primary objective of the study was to determine whether age modified the association between BMI and 1-year all-cause mortality, formal interaction analyses were performed using IPTW-adjusted Cox proportional hazards models. For the categorical interaction analysis, age was modeled using prespecified categories of <65, 65 to 74, 75 to 84, and ≥85 years, and BMI was modeled as <25 vs ≥25 kg/m2. HRs for BMI ≥25 kg/m2 vs BMI <25 kg/m2 within each age category were estimated as model-based contrasts from a Cox model saturated with respect to BMI category and age group, including main effects for BMI category and age group and their multiplicative interaction. The global BMI × age-group interaction was assessed using likelihood-ratio (LR) testing comparing models with and without the interaction terms. An ordinal interaction trend was also tested to evaluate whether the association between BMI ≥25 kg/m2 and mortality changed progressively across increasing age categories. In addition, BMI and age were modeled as continuous variables using restricted cubic splines with a BMI × age interaction term to assess nonlinear effect modification; nonlinearity and the overall continuous BMI × age interaction were evaluated using LR tests. For the restricted cubic spline analysis, 4 knots were placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the observed analytic cohort distribution for both BMI and age. Predicted 1-year mortality was plotted across the 1st to 95th percentile range of BMI at representative ages of 60, 70, 80, and 85 years, and 95% CIs for the predicted curves were derived from the model-based variance-covariance matrix.

As part of a sensitivity analysis, BMI was reclassified into conventional categories: underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), healthy weight (18.5-24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25.0-29.9 kg/m2), and obesity (≥30.0 kg/m2). These categories were examined overall and within age strata of <65 years and ≥65 years. Similar to the primary analysis, IPTW-adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression was used to calculate the HRs with 95% CIs associated with the primary outcome of 1-year all-cause mortality.

In an additional sensitivity analysis, patients were categorized according to extreme BMI values as BMI <16 kg/m2, BMI 16 to 40 kg/m2, or BMI >40 kg/m2 and examined within age strata of <65 and ≥ 65 years. One-year all-cause mortality was compared using BMI 16 to 40 kg/m2 as the reference group.

A P value ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.2; R Core Team) and MedCalc Statistical Software (version 12.7.7; MedCalc Software).

Results

Study cohort

Of the 6,663 patients in the study cohort, 24 (0.4%) were excluded from the analysis because they lacked either age or BMI data. Of the 6,639 patients included in the analytic cohort, 165 (2.5%) had BMI <25 kg/m2 and were <65 years of age, 523 (7.9%) had BMI ≥25 kg/m2 and were <65 years of age, 1,599 (24.1%) had BMI <25 kg/m2 and were ≥65 years of age, and 4,352 (65.6%) had BMI ≥25 kg/m2 and were ≥65 years of age. The overall cohort had a mean age of 76.7 ± 11.6 years and a mean BMI of 29.7 ± 9.9 kg/m2.

Among patients ≥65 years of age, those with BMI <25 kg/m2 were older (82.2 ± 7.0 vs 78.8 ± 6.8 years; P < 0.001), more often females (45.6% vs 39.9%; P < 0.001), and had higher STS risk scores (4.9 ± 4.0 vs 3.8 ± 3.2; P < 0.001) than those with BMI ≥25 kg/m2. Older patients with BMI ≥25 kg/m2 more frequently had hypertension (92.1% vs 85.0%; P < 0.001) and diabetes mellitus (41.7% vs 22.6%; P < 0.001), whereas prior MI (17.5% vs 14.9%; P = 0.004) and peripheral arterial disease (PAD) (24.6% vs 21.3%; P = 0.003) were more common in older patients with BMI <25 kg/m2.

Among patients ≥65 years of age, procedural differences were more pronounced. Age-dependent association between body mass index and mortality

When age was examined in 4 categories, adjusted 1-year mortality estimates for BMI <25 kg/m2 and BMI ≥25 kg/m2 were nearly identical among patients <65 years of age but progressively diverged with advancing age (Figure 1). Adjusted mortality was 2.4% vs 2.3% among patients <65 years old, 7.1% vs 5.2% among those 65 to 74 years old, 8.8% vs 5.5% among those 75 to 84 years old, and 15.8% vs 6.4% among those ≥85 years old, respectively. Age-stratified Cox models showed a similar pattern (Figure 2). In the saturated categorical interaction model, the association between BMI ≥25 kg/m2 and 1-year all-cause mortality differed significantly across age strata. The global BMI × age-group interaction was assessed using LR testing comparing models with and without the interaction terms. Age-stratified interaction analyses showed that the association between BMI and 1-year mortality differed significantly across age strata (LR chi-square = 7.85, df = 3, P = 0.039), with a significant ordinal interaction trend indicating progressively stronger protection associated with BMI ≥25 kg/m2 as age increased (P for interaction trend = 0.010). Consistent with these findings, spline-based modeling demonstrated evidence of nonlinearity for both BMI (LR chi-square = 8.94, df = 2, P = 0.011) and age (LR chi-square = 12.67, df = 2, P = 0.002), as well as a significant BMI × age interaction (overall LR chi-square = 19.82, df = 9, P = 0.019).

In a sensitivity analysis, BMI was reclassified into standard categories—underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), healthy weight (18.5-24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25.0-29.9 kg/m2), and obesity (≥30.0 kg/m2)—and examined within age groups (<65 and ≥ 65 years) (Supplemental Figure 3). The overall pattern was similar to the primary analysis. Compared with a healthy weight, obesity was associated with a significantly lower adjusted hazard of mortality (HR: 0.72; 95% CI: 0.55-0.95; P = 0.019). By contrast, the higher hazard observed in underweight patients (HR: 1.46; 95% CI: 0.75-2.87; P = 0.272) and the lower hazard observed in overweight patients (HR: 0.87; 95% CI: 0.67-1.13; P = 0.294) were not statistically significant. Among patients younger than 65 years, compared with healthy weight, underweight, overweight, and obesity were not significantly associated with adjusted hazard of mortality (P = 0.101, P = 0.516, and P = 0.070, respectively). Among patients aged ≥65 years, the categorical BMI analysis demonstrated a protective association for obesity compared with healthy weight (HR: 0.56; 95% CI: 0.37-0.84; P = 0.005 at 75-84 years; HR: 0.30; 95% CI: 0.18-0.51; P < 0.001 at ≥ 85 years), with effect sizes increasing and statistical significance emerging in the 75 to 84 and ≥85 age strata (Supplemental Figure 4).

In an additional sensitivity analysis evaluating BMI extremes, patients were categorized as BMI <16 kg/m2, BMI 16 to 40 kg/m2, or BMI >40 kg/m2 and examined within age strata of <65 and ≥ 65 years. Extreme BMI values were not significantly associated with 1-year mortality in either age stratum. Among patients <65 years, mortality was 3.8% in those with BMI >40 kg/m2 vs 2.5% in those with BMI 16 to 40 kg/m2 (HR: 1.58; 95% CI: 0.45-5.60; P = 0.446), whereas among patients ≥65 years, mortality was 7.7% vs 6.9%, respectively (HR: 1.13; 95% CI: 0.76-1.66; P = 0.536). BMI <16 kg/m2 was rare and had no observed mortality, precluding stable estimation.

In the multiple-imputation analysis to account for the potential impact of missing data, results were unchanged from the primary analysis: among patients aged ≥65 years, BMI <25 kg/m2 remained associated with higher 1-year all-cause mortality, MACCE, and readmission compared with BMI ≥25 kg/m2, and the age-dependent BMI-mortality interaction remained statistically significant (Supplemental Table 2, Supplemental Table 3).

Discussion

This contemporary analysis of a large integrated health system cohort within the STS/ACC TVT registry provides important insight into the age-dependent relationship between BMI and clinical outcomes after TAVR. The primary findings are: 1) the apparent obesity paradox after TAVR is not uniform across the overall study population, but is significantly modified by age; 2) among patients ≥65 years of age, those with BMI <25 kg/m2 had higher STS risk scores (4.9% vs 3.8%) than those with BMI ≥25 kg/m2; 3) in this older cohort, BMI <25 kg/m2 was associated with significantly worse 1-year outcomes, including higher all-cause mortality (P = 0.006), MACCE (P < 0.001), stroke (P = 0.028), cardiac mortality (P = 0.010), MI (P = 0.042), and readmission (P = 0.002), whereas no significant differences were observed between BMI groups among patients <65 years old; 4) after IPTW adjustment, the adverse association of lower BMI persisted among older adults, with BMI <25 kg/m2 associated with higher risk of all-cause mortality (P = 0.013), MACCE (P = 0.001), and readmission (P = 0.008) compared with BMI ≥25 kg/m2; and 5) age-stratified and spline-based analyses demonstrated that this relationship strengthened progressively with advancing age, with BMI ≥25 kg/m2 not significantly associated with lower mortality in patients <65 or 65 to 74 years of age, but associated with significantly lower mortality in patients 75 to 84 years of age (aHR: 0.60; 95% CI: 0.44-0.82; P = 0.001) and ≥85 years old (aHR: 0.36; 95% CI: 0.25-0.51; P < 0.001). Taken together, these findings suggest that the TAVR obesity paradox is predominantly an age-dependent phenomenon, most evident in older adults rather than across all patients undergoing TAVR (Central Illustration). These findings should be interpreted in the context of prior work. Sharma et al7 analyzed 31,929 patients and used 6 BMI categories: underweight <18.5 kg/m2, normal weight 18.5 to 24.9 kg/m2, overweight 25.0 to 29.9 kg/m2, class I obesity 30.0 to 34.9 kg/m2, class II obesity 35.0 to 39.9 kg/m2, and class III obesity ≥40 kg/m2. They found higher 30-day and 1-year mortality in underweight patients and lower 1-year mortality in overweight patients and in patients with class I or II obesity. They did not specifically evaluate age-dependent effect modification. Reichl et al9 studied 6,156 patients from 2 high-volume centers and defined 4 BMI groups: underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5-24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25.0-29.9 kg/m2), and obesity (≥30.0 kg/m2). They found no survival advantage for obesity compared with normal weight, whereas underweight predicted higher 1-year and 5-year mortality. They likewise did not test age as a modifier. McInerney et al8 addressed a different question. They compared nonobese patients with BMI 18.5 to 29.9 kg/m2 with patients with morbid obesity, defined as BMI ≥40 kg/m2 or BMI≥35 kg/m2 with obesity-related comorbidities. They did not examine age interaction. They found similar matched 2-year survival between groups, but higher major vascular complications and lower device success in the morbid obesity group. In contrast, our study used a pragmatic BMI threshold of <25 vs ≥25 kg/m2 in the primary analysis. This approach grouped overweight and obesity together and therefore tested a higher-BMI phenotype rather than obesity alone. We then examined whether the prognostic significance of that threshold changed across age strata (<65, 65-74, 75-84, and ≥85 years) and in a continuous BMI × age spline model. Our findings are similar to prior studies in showing excess risk at lower BMI. Our findings differ in that the apparent benefit of BMI ≥25 kg/m2 was not present across the whole cohort. It was concentrated in older adults and was strongest in patients aged 75 years or older. Furthermore, our findings were also consistent in sensitivity analyses using conventional BMI categories, with lower adjusted mortality among patients aged ≥65 years with BMI ≥30.0 kg/m2 than among those with BMI 18.5 to 24.9 kg/m2. These differences suggest that the TAVR obesity paradox may reflect the risk associated with older, low-BMI patients rather than a universal survival benefit of higher BMI.

The clearest message from our study is that age is a key driver of post-TAVR risk, but the meaning of BMI changes with age. In the crude cohort, BMI did not meaningfully separate outcomes in patients younger than 65 years. In contrast, among patients aged 65 years or older, lower BMI was associated with higher all-cause mortality, MACCE, and readmission. That signal remained after weighting. Age also remained an independent factor associated with 1-year all-cause mortality in the adjusted model, whereas BMI did not. By contrast, BMI is better understood here as a modifier of risk rather than as a uniform 1-directional risk factor across the whole cohort. The age-stratified HRs and the spline interaction both support that interpretation. The apparent benefit of higher BMI was minimal in younger patients. It became progressively stronger with advancing age.

Clinically, these findings suggest that BMI <25 kg/m2 in older TAVR patients is less likely to represent a healthy lean phenotype and more likely to represent vulnerability. In elderly patients with severe aortic stenosis, BMI <25 kg/m2 may reflect frailty, sarcopenia, undernutrition, chronic inflammatory burden, or reduced physiologic reserve. That interpretation fits the structure of our results. The strongest and most consistent signals were seen for all-cause mortality and readmission. The differences in stroke and MI weakened after adjustment. This pattern points more toward impaired resilience and recovery capacity than toward protection from specific ischemic events. Similarly, although bleeding complications after TAVR can contribute to prolonged hospitalization, readmission, and mortality,16, 17, 18 major bleeding was infrequent and did not differ significantly between BMI groups in either age stratum; therefore, bleeding is less likely to be the primary driver of the observed association between lower BMI and worse 1-year outcomes among older patients.

Another potential contributor to this association is vascular-access suitability. In our cohort, older patients with BMI <25 kg/m2 had a higher prevalence of PAD and were less likely to undergo femoral access, whereas those with BMI ≥25 kg/m2 more often underwent transfemoral TAVR. This may suggest that older patients with higher BMI had a more favorable vascular access profile, which could partly contribute to their better outcomes. However, both PAD and access site were included in the weighting and adjusted models, and the association between lower BMI and worse outcomes among older patients persisted after adjustment. Thus, differences in PAD burden and access-site feasibility may partially explain the observed findings but do not fully account for the age-dependent BMI-mortality relationship. In addition, valve sizing may also have contributed to the procedural risk profile of lower-BMI older patients. Smaller prosthesis size, particularly ≤23 mm, has been associated with prosthesis-patient mismatch and worse postprocedural outcomes,19,20 although the relationship is complex and may vary by valve type, oversizing, and conduction-risk considerations. In our cohort, patients aged ≥65 years with BMI <25 kg/m2 more frequently received smaller valves than those aged ≥65 years with BMI ≥25 kg/m2, including 20- or 23-mm devices in 36.4% vs 27.0% of patients, respectively, suggesting that annular size and device-selection factors may have partly contributed to their higher-risk phenotype.

These observations may assist bedside risk stratification. An elderly patient with BMI <25 kg/m2, especially one aged 75 years or older and particularly one aged 85 years or older, should likely be viewed as having an added layer of biologic risk. That patient may warrant more deliberate frailty testing. That patient may also benefit from a nutritional assessment, sarcopenia screening, rehabilitation planning, more thorough discharge planning, and greater attention to readmission prevention. At the same time, these data should not be interpreted to mean that obesity is benign. Higher-BMI patients often carried more hypertension and diabetes in prior studies.21 Higher-BMI cohorts have also shown more vascular and bleeding complications in some procedural analyses. The most practical bedside interpretation is therefore not that obesity protects. It is that low BMI in an older TAVR candidate may be a warning sign.

This study clarifies the obesity paradox in TAVR, showing that it is a real but nonuniversal phenomenon. The paradox is age-dependent and likely phenotype-dependent, being weak or absent in younger patients and most evident in the oldest. It mainly affects broad outcomes like mortality and readmission. These results help clarify the paradox, suggesting that the increased risk is primarily attributable to leaner older adults rather than to a protective effect of adiposity alone. Consequently, the study shifts the focus from obesity itself to the significance of low BMI in elderly TAVR patients.

Study limitations

This study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, as a retrospective observational analysis of registry data, it cannot establish causality and remains subject to residual confounding despite the use of IPTW and doubly adjusted Cox modeling. Second, BMI is an imperfect surrogate for body composition and does not distinguish adiposity from lean mass, central obesity, frailty, sarcopenia, nutritional status, or unintentional weight loss. Likewise, active or metastatic malignancy and other potential contributors to cachexia or low BMI, including occult chronic illness, formal frailty measures, sarcopenia indices, nutritional markers, functional status, and unintentional weight loss, were not available in sufficient granularity for adjustment; therefore, residual confounding by non-TAVR-related comorbidities cannot be excluded, and the observed association should be interpreted as identifying a vulnerable low-BMI phenotype rather than proving a causal protective effect of higher BMI. Accordingly, the observed association may reflect a vulnerable low-BMI phenotype rather than a protective effect of adiposity itself. Third, the primary BMI threshold (<25 vs ≥ 25 kg/m2) was intentionally pragmatic but grouped overweight and obesity together and may have obscured heterogeneity across finer BMI categories, particularly at the extremes, although the spline analysis and the sensitivity analysis partially addressed this issue. Fourth, the subgroup of patients younger than 65 years was relatively small and had few clinical events, limiting precision and reducing power to detect age-specific associations in younger patients. Fifth, several clinically relevant variables that may influence outcomes after TAVR, including formal frailty measures, sarcopenia indices, nutritional markers, functional status, and weight trajectory before the procedure, were not available in sufficient granularity for this analysis. Sixth, this study was derived from CommonSpirit Health hospitals participating in the STS/ACC TVT registry and evaluated outcomes through 1 year; therefore, generalizability to other health systems, nonregistry populations, and longer-term follow-up may be limited. Because postdischarge event ascertainment was supplemented by Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services claims linkage for Medicare beneficiaries, completeness of follow-up event capture may not have been uniform across all patients. Finally, although the TVT Registry uses standardized definitions, routine quality checks, and independent audits, procedural decision-making was not centrally adjudicated across centers. Therefore, center-level variation in valve selection, device sizing, access planning, and operator practice could not be fully assessed and may have contributed to residual confounding.

Conclusions

In this contemporary STS/ACC TVT registry cohort, the apparent obesity paradox after TAVR was significantly modified by age. Higher BMI was not uniformly associated with better outcomes across the overall population; rather, lower BMI identified a higher-risk phenotype among older adults, with the association most evident for mortality in patients aged 75 years or older. Among patients aged 65 years or older, BMI <25 kg/m2 was also associated with a higher risk of MACCE and readmission. These findings suggest that the obesity paradox in TAVR is driven less by a universal protective effect of adiposity and more by the adverse prognostic significance of lower BMI in elderly patients, likely reflecting reduced physiologic reserve.
