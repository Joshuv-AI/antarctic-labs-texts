Long-term outcomes following atrioventricular septal defect in adult patients
Authors: Han Zhang, Gang Li, Jiashu Sun, Yansong Zuo, Qiang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the results after repair of atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD) in adult patients in our centre.MethodsWe assessed 168 patients who received AVSD repair at a single institution from 2013 to 2023. Relevant data were obtained from medical and surgical records, and focused on the early and long-term results.ResultsMedian age at surgery was 36.4 (27.0–47.8) years. One hundred and forty-seven patients (87.5%) with partial AVSD, 15 patients (8.9%) with intermediate AVSD and six patients (3.6%) with complete AVSD. Left atrioventricular valve (LAVV) regurgitation was present in 160 patients; the LAVV clefts were closed completely in 155 patients (92.3%), of which 36 (21.4%) underwent mitral valve ring annuloplasty and 18 (10.7%) patients underwent additional implantation of an incomplete flexible band into the LAVV. There were two early deaths, with two deaths over a median follow-up of 36.0 (14.0–60.0) months. According to the follow-up outcomes, the heart measurement, pulmonary artery pressure and valve regurgitations recovered significantly. A total of 34 patients underwent 37 reoperations. The majority of subsequent reoperations were performed because of residual LAVV pathology (n = 6, 16.2%), and arrhythmias (n = 31, 91.2%). Competing risks analysis revealed that 10 years after the initial operation, 2.6% had died without reintervention, 23.3% had received reintervention and 74.7% were alive without reintervention.ConclusionsAVSD in adult patients can be done safely with low early mortality and good long-term out-comes. While the reoperation rate is minimal, it is important to closely monitor the significant occurrence of arrhythmias.

Introduction

Atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD) represents a spectrum of congenital heart defects, ranging from partial AVSD (PAVSD), intermediate AVSD (IAVSD) and complete AVSD (CAVSD), and represents 7% of all congenital heart diseases (CHDs) [1]. With the advancement of paediatric cardiology, the population of adult patients with AVSD expands continuously year after year. We are facing new challenges in the treatment of AVSD in adults with unclear results [2,3]. There were many reports about the surgery outcomes of patients in young age with good surgical results, but there are a few reports involving surgery outcomes in adult patients. In this study, we want to evaluate experience in adult patients undergoing AVSD repair, including surgery procedures, pathological characteristics, early and long-term outcomes.

Methods

A retrospective cohort study at Beijing Anzhen Hospital investigated cardiac surgeries for patients who underwent AVSD repair in adults from January 2013 to December 2023. The study received approval from the Beijing Anzhen Hospital Research Ethics Board (Number: 2024173X). Data were collected through a review of medical records until the last cardiology follow-up. Demographic and perioperative data were obtained, including age, weight, sex, type of AVSD, surgical procedure, preoperative degree of valve regurgitation, cardiac measurement, associated pathologies, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) times, aortic cross-clamp times, follow-up years, reoperation, history of cardiac arrhythmias, most recent echocardiographic findings and survival status. The severity of left atrioventricular valve (LAVV) or right atrioventricular valve (RAVV) regurgitation was assessed based on standard echocardiographic guidelines [4], and described by the ultrasonologists who distinguished the regurgitations into none/trace, mild, moderate and severe four levels. The left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO) refers to the restriction of blood flow out of the left ventricle, and the level of obstruction can be valvular, sub-valvular or supravalvular. Early mortality was defined as death occurring during the same hospitalization, or within 30 days of surgery for discharged patients, and late death occurred after this period of time.

Technique of operation

The surgery was conducted using a standard median sternotomy approach, with aortic and bicaval cannulation. Following cardioplegic arrest, a right atriotomy was performed. The choice of particular surgical technique was based on the intraoperative transoeso-phageal echocardiography and anatomical findings. The LAVV ‘cleft’ was closed using multiple interrupted polypropylene sutures and the LAVV was tested with instillation of saline into the left ventricle, and the need for an additional ring annuloplasty or band implantation was then determined. If double orifice LAVV was identified, the regurgitant orifice (typically the smaller one) was closed with a pericardial patch. The RAVV was then evaluated and any associated RAVV ‘cleft’ was also closed as needed. A DeVega annuloplasty was performed more commonly and in the more recent era we preferred additional band implantation, particularly when the RAVV annulus was much dilated. A pericardial patch was then used to close the atrial septal defect. In addition, surgical repair of PAVSD or CAVSD was performed with either the modified single patch (MSP) or double patch (DP) technique depending on surgeon preference. Patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) who underwent MAZE procedure at the same time.

Statistical analysis

The study employed the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) 25.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) and GraphPad Prism 6 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA) for statistical analysis. Continuous data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (range), and skewed continuous data are presented as median (p25–p75), depending on the variable type. Categorical variables were assessed using χ2, while numeric variables were analysed using Mann–Whitney’s U-tests. To assess the effect of LAVV and RAVV repair on echocardiographic outcomes, the Wilcoxon signed rank test was used to compare pre-operative and most-recent measurements in patients for whom paired data were available. For continuous variables, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used. Estimates of overall survival, freedom from LAVV reintervention and freedom from poor outcome were obtained by means of the Kaplan–Meier method, with the difference among subgroups being tested by the log-rank test. If a time-related endpoint was subjected to a competing risk (i.e. death), then the competing risk framework was examined. Competing risks analysis of death and reoperation were based on the decomposition of the hazard function into multiple, additive overlapping phases of hazard, as described by Blackstone et al. [5]. A significance level of p < .05 was considered for all statistical analyses. Note that 95% confidence intervals were not adjusted for multiple comparisons and inferences drawn from them may not be reproducible.

Results

During the study period, there were finally 168 patients enrolled in the study, of which 62 (36.9%) were male patients. One hundred and forty-seven patients (87.5%) had PAVSD, 15 patients (8.9%) had IAVSD and six patients (3.6%) had type A CAVSD. Median age at repair was 36.4 (27.0–47.8) years, and median weight was 60.0 (51.0–69.0) kg. The characteristics of all the patients are summarized in Table 1. There were 160 patients with LAVV regurgitation, among which 35 (20.8%) were mild, 71 (42.3%) were moderate and 54 (32.1%) were severe. The occurrence rate of double orifice LAVV (DOLAVV) was 1.9% (n = 3). Overall, 66 patients (39.3%) were found to have pulmonary hypertension, with a mean pulmonary artery pressure (PAP) of 35.0 (29.8–48.0) mmHg, and the mean pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) was 2.6 (1.6–3.5) WU, and in the CAVSD group, the mean PVR was 4.3 (2.9–6.9) WU.

Perioperative outcomes

There were two patients (1.2%) who died within 30 days after surgery. One patient with PAVSD and AF developed low cardiac output syndrome after surgery and died due to heart failure and multiple infectious episodes. The other patient with PAVSD and pulmonary hypertension also died after surgery due to malignant arrhythmia.

Preoperatively, 13 patients (7.7%) had AF, four (2.4%) had first-degree atrioventricular block and three (1.9%) had third-degree atrioventricular block. Three patients (1.9%) underwent planned permanent pacemaker insertion for pre-existing heart block in the immediate postoperative period, while two patients (1.2%) developed new heart block and required permanent pacemaker insertion after surgery. All patients with AF underwent MAZE procedure, but three patients (23.1%) still experienced AF/flutter after surgery, and 10 patients (76.9%) maintain sinus rhythm in the 30-day postoperative period.

The LAVV clefts were closed completely in 155 patients (92.3%), of which 36 (21.4%) underwent ring annuloplasty and 18 patients (10.7%) underwent additional implantation of an incomplete flexible band into the LAVV. The reasons for leaving the cleft completely open included a small LAVV in eight, a tiny LAVV cleft in five cases. And two (1.2%) patients with double orifice LAVV, the regurgitant orifice was closed with a pericardial patch. One patient (1.2%) required LAVV replacement after attempts at valve repair resulted in more than moderate regurgitation, attributed to valve thickening and shrinkage (Table 2). In addition, 27 patients (16.1%) underwent De Vega valvuloplasty for tricuspid valve regurgitation while 38 patients (22.6%) received additional band implantation. Two patients (1.2%) underwent LVOTO relief and two patients (1.2%) underwent pulmonary valvotomy during PAVSD repair (Table 2).

After the surgery, the pre-discharge echocardiogram showed that 61 patients (36.7%) had mild LAVV regurgitation, nine (5.4%) had moderate regurgitation and three (1.8%) had severe regurgitation (Figure 1(A)).

Long-term outcomes

At a median follow-up of 36.0 (14.0–60.0) months, the completeness of the study was 97.0%. There were two (1.2%) late deaths, including one patient diagnosed with IAVSD died due to malign tumours, one patient diagnosed with PAVSD died due to cerebral vascular disease. The survival rate was 95.8% at both the 5-year and 10-year following the initial repair (Figure 2). The findings of the competing risks analysis indicated that at the 10-year mark following the initial operation, 2.6% of the individuals had passed away without intervention, 23.3% had undergone reintervention and 74.7% remained alive without requiring any additional intervention (Figure 3).

Competing risks analysis showed that at the 10-year mark following the initial operation, 2.6% of the individuals had passed away without intervention, 23.3% had undergone reintervention and 74.7% remained alive without requiring any additional intervention.

During the follow-up period, a total of 34 patients underwent 37 reoperations. The subsequent reoperations were conducted due to residual LAVV pathology (n = 6, 16.2%), and arrhythmias (n = 31, 91.2%, AF = 24, atrioventricular block = 4, sinus node dysfunction = 3). Median time to any reoperation was 12.0 (6.0–12.0) months, and to LAVV reoperation was 49.0 (34.8–58.5) months. The individual procedures included LAVV repairs (n = 2/37, 5.4%), LAVV replacement (n = 4/37, 10.8%), PPM placement (n = 7/37, 18.9%), radiofrequency ablation treatment (n = 19/37, 48.6%) and maze procedure during LAVV procedures (n = 5/37, 13.5%).

The subjects were categorized into three groups based on the AVSD classification. Among the PAVSD, IAVSD and CAVSD groups, the rates of re-intervention for LAVV pathology or arrhythmias showed no significant differences (p = .461) (Figure 4). In the PAVSD group, the percentage of patients not requiring any additional surgery was 73.8% at 10 years after the first repair. In the IAVSD group, this rate remained constant at 40.0% at 10 years following the first repair. Similarly, in the CAVSD group, there was an 83.3% rate of freedom from any subsequent operations at 10 years post the first repair (p = .587).

Kaplan–Meier’s curves comparing the freedom from re-intervention in patients with different diagnosis.

Most recent echocardiographic findings about LAVV revealed that there were 69 patients (43.4%) with mild regurgitation, 41patients (25.8%) with moderate regurgitation and eight patients (5.0%) with severe regurgitation (Figure 1(A)). A repeated measures analysis of the 159 patients demonstrated the LAVV regurgitation grades showed significant improvement over follow-up (Z = −0.817, p = .000) (Figure 5). We observed similar improvement in RAVV regurgitation during follow-up (Z = −11.065, p = .000) (Figures 1(B) and 6). Additional, the subjects were categorized into two groups based on whether underwent additional band implantation. During follow-up, the freedom from moderate or severe LAVV regurgitation was 44.4% in the additional band implantation group and 27.3% in the isolated cleft closure group (p = .134). And the freedom from moderate or severe RAVV regurgitation was 10.3% in the additional band implantation group and 7.5% in the isolated cleft closure group (p = .585).

It was found during follow-up, the mean diameter of diastolic left ventricles relatively dilated from 42.0 (39.0–46.0) mm to 45.0 (42.0–48.0) mm (p = .001), the mean diameter of diastolic left atriums decreased from 45.0 (34.0–60.0) mm to 40.0 (33.0–53.0) mm (p = .038), the mean diastolic right ventricles (RVs) decreased from 32.0 (25.0–38.0) mm to 21.0 (19.0–24.0) mm (p = .000), and the mean diastolic right atriums decreased from 53.0 (45.0–60.0) mm to 21.0 (19.0–24.0) mm (p = .000) (Figure 7). AF was documented in 24 (14.5%) patients, 19 patients (79.2%) had PAVSD, four patients (17.4%) had IAVSD and one patient (4.3%) had CAVSD at the initial surgery. The time interval from the initial surgery to the interventional treatment for AF was 12.0 (6.0–12.0) months. Previous MAZE operation (p = .000, HR: 5.990, CI: 2.345–15.306), moderate or severe LAVV regurgitation after initial surgery (p = .000, HR: 6.284, CI: 2.721–14.514) and moderate or severe LAVV regurgitation in the latest follow-up examination (p = .027, HR: 2.406, CI: 1.106–5.232) were identified as risk factors for AF reintervention on multivariable analysis.

Preoperative and most-recent heart measurements.

Compared with preoperative NYHA functional classes, there were 41 patients (25.8%) in class I, which improved significantly (p = .000). Additionally, the mPAP declined significantly from 35.0 (29.8–48.0) mmHg preoperatively to 20.0 (19.3–25.8) mmHg in a subset of 56 patients (p < .001). Among them, there were 44 patients underwent right heart catheterization, and the PVR was 2.6 (1.6–3.5) WU. After the initial surgery, eight out of 56 patients expressed dissatisfaction with the reduction in PAP. Among these patients, three (37.5%) had PAVSD, two (25.0%) had IAVSD and three (37.5%) had CAVSD, and the most recent follow-up showed a PVR of 4.1 (3.7–4.8) WU.

Analysis of the CAVSD group data revealed that there was no late mortality, two patients (33.3%) with severe regurgitation, and one patient underwent radiofrequency ablation treatment for AF.

Discussion

The outcomes of surgical treatment of AVSD have markedly improved over the last decades.

Currently, most centres believed that the optimal time for repair is during the early childhood [6–8], perioperative mortality is consistently reported to be approximately 1%, with a range from 0% to 4.5% [9,10]. Long‑term results from earlier studies had demonstrated a 15 years survival of 86–89% [11]. But, some patients still only received treatment in adulthood due to unclear symptoms or economic reasons. There is very limited contemporary data on the outcome of AVSD repair in adults. Earlier data was suggestive of poor survival for patients operated after 20 years of age [7], PAVSD repair in adults reported an early mortality of 3–6% [12,13]. While, Patlolla et al. showed patients with pAVSD older than 18 years of age had a lower early mortality of 2.0% [14], Carlos et al. also showed there were no adult deaths with PAVSD or IAVSD [15].

We found that most studies on AVSD repair were focused either on specific AVSD type (PAVSD or IAVSD), we analysed the entire cohort of AVSD in adult patients. And in this study, the early mortality was found to be 1.2%. Improved outcomes in recent times can be attributed to advancements in perioperative medical management, such as techniques for CPB, myocardial protection and anaesthetic approaches, especially in the management of pulmonary hypertension during the perioperative period. Pulmonary hypertension and children repaired were older at the time of surgery may have contributed to risk for mortality in previous studies [16].

The improved outcomes highlight the significance of targeted PH drug therapy in managing pulmonary hypertension, as it efficiently decreases PVR in affected patients [17]. Our team strongly believes that adopting a more assertive technique to targeted PH therapy, such as implementing ‘triple’ therapy involving parenteral prostanoids or other agents, along with an accurate preoperative evaluation of PH including PAP and PVR, has the potential to expand the scope of surgical indications and decrease PAP both pre- and post-surgery. Typically, the targeted PH drug therapy includes bosentan or sildenafil. For reducing ventricular preload and regulating pulmonary circulation, digoxin and oral diuretic were given. During follow-up, additional cardiac catheterization or echocardiographic examinations were performed, targeted PH drug therapy was withdrawn when mPAP decreased to below 20 mmHg.

We found that the long-term mortality and need for LAVV reoperation were very positive. It was previously believed that dealing with both long-term RV volume overload and pulmonary hypertension made repairing AVSD in adults more difficult, with uncertain long-term outcomes [18]. Song et al. reported no late death within 2 years after surgery [19], and another research including 179 patients resulted 61 deaths in long-term follow-up [14]. In our study, there were two late deaths, we believed that close follow-up and regular medication after surgery are beneficial for reducing long-term mortality, especially for patients with pulmonary hypertension.

In contrast to patients operated on during childhood, those who underwent AVSD repair in adults mainly required reoperation due to recurrent LAVV regurgitations and arrhythmias [20–22]. Patlolla et al. discovered that 53% of patients experienced ≥ moderate regurgitation after 5 years, while Zhou et al. reported that 15% (10 out of 66) of patients experienced late recurrence of moderate/severe LAVV regurgitation in their study [14,20]. In this study, a repeated measures analysis revealed a significant reduction in LAVV regurgitation following surgery. There were only six patients underwent reoperation due to LAVV pathology. The Mayo Clinic’s 40-year experience [7] and the findings from Aubert et al. [23] both indicate that undergoing the operation at a later age may be advantageous in reducing the re-operation rate of LAVV. Prior studies have shown that abnormal LAVV such as LAVV dysplasia, double outlet LAVV and pseudoparachute LAVV, as well as residual cleft, rupture of cleft sutures, and annular dilation were independent predictors of LAVV reoperation [24–26]. Contrary to other series, we did not a significant predictor of LAVV reoperation, this observation may be due to low numbers.

When it comes to surgical technique, prioritizing LAVVP during the initial surgery is crucial. It’s clear that LAVVP is better than LAVVR due to the preserved left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction and the absence of haemorrhagic, thromboembolic and infectious complications [26–28], although, the long-term durability of LAVVP remains its fatal weakness. We closed the LAVV ‘cleft’ as a matter of routine, except for those cases in which closure would lead to LAVV stenosis. Murashita et al. also reported that cleft closure in combination with annuloplasty may improve valvular function during the follow-up period [29]. We also performed annuloplasty procedure for patients with moderate/severe LAVV regurgitation or severe annular dilation, including ring annuloplasty or additional band implantation. Tricuspid regurgitation may arise with different congenital diagnoses, and there have been limited reports on RAVV regurgitation following AVSD repair. Surgical treatment for tricuspid regurgitation has shown to be successful in averting the onset of heart failure [30]. Our follow-up results showed that there were 146 patients had none or wild regurgitation. While there was no statistical different, we thought the tricuspid annuloplasty and/or additional band implantation were effective in adult patients.

It had known that AVSD association with subsequent development of LVOTO and recurrent LVOTO may also adversely impact late survival. The structure of the LVOT in AVSDs has been thoroughly documented. The abnormal position of the aortic root, inadequate muscular interventricular septum, short inlet with a long outlet septum, and augmented encroachment by atrioventricular valve tissue on an already constricted LVOT could potentially lead to heightened LVOT obstruction in patients [31]. Chowdhury et al. found a 3.0% prevalence of LVOTO in their series who underwent septal myectomy, and no reoperation in their series for LVOTO [32], As well as, Sojak et al. reported seven patients (10.1%) underwent reoperation for LVOTO [24]. In our case, there were two patients with concomitant LVOTO at the initial surgery, and no LV outflow tract stenosis developed. A more careful long-term follow-up is required, but based on our experience, we believe that for patients who need to resolving LVOTO, it is imperative to remove fibrous tissue from the LVOT along the ventricular septum, the anterior leaflet of the LAVV, and fibrous tissue on the aortic cusps is crucial for eliminating the gradient. There has long been debate about whether the MSP technique predisposes to LVOTO. Theoretically, the ventricular septal defect (VSD) was closed directly, which may push the LAAV down toward the ventricular septal crest. This may result in LV outflow tract stenosis [33,34]. In our group of cases, there were a total of 21 patients with IAVSD or CAVSD, the surgeon chose DP or MSP technique for surgery based on the patient’s pathology. And our research verified that there were no occurrences of LVOTO following MSP repair. Careful technical were necessary, positioning the interrupted VSD sutures carefully on the right side of the ventricular septal crest and transferring bridging leaflet tissue from the RAVV to support the LAVV [34,35].

Arrhythmia especially AF were common among these patients. Older patients with a significant left-to-right shunt from an ASD are at higher risk of developing atrial arrhythmias [36]. According to our research findings, MAZE surgery proved effective in maintaining sinus rhythm, reducing the risk of stroke, and avoiding the need for long-term anticoagulation. But during the follow-up, AF was documented in 23 (14.5%) patients. Previous studies have found that patients with left atrial dilatation, right atrial dilatation, moderate or severe LAVV regurgitation and pulmonary hypertension had more frequent AF. And the type of AVSD and the age at repair were not associated with AF [37]. In our study, the values for heart measurements were significant improvement during the follow-up period, but moderate or severe LAVV regurgitation was also a risk factor for AF. All approaches to minimize the need for reinterventions and LAVV regurgitation should be explored in order to ultimately reduce the burden of arrhythmias in these patients.

Limitations

Like any retrospective study, our study has certain limitations, including the lack of a control group, the relatively small sample size. Some patients missed some data about PAP, PVR and arrhythmias reported during follow-up. This study is limited by lack of data on the functional quality of life that the patients may have at late follow-up including data on medication use. It can be challenging to assess the outcomes due to the diverse patient population and multitude of factors that contribute to a lack of progress. We believe that randomized clinical studies and multicentre studies are needed.

Conclusions

Adult patients with AVSD can safely undergo surgery with low early mortality and favourable long-term outcomes. For those with pulmonary hypertension, a combination of drug therapy can also yield positive results. Although the reoperation rate is minimal, it is important to carefully monitor the significant occurrence of arrhythmias.
