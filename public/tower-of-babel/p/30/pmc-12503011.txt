Development of a Small‐Scale Continuous Virus Filtration Model That Incorporates Inline Spiking and Mixing
Authors: Ioana Pintescu, Julie Kozaili, Daniel Strauss
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRecent efforts have focused on shifting biomanufacturing from traditional batch mode processing to continuous processing as these technologies have the potential to increase productivity and product quality. However, the lack of a unified methodology for viral clearance validation of virus filtration (VF) in continuous processing has presented a hurdle to its implementation. Through the use of spiking a concentrated virus stock into a continuous filtration system, product flow with a stable virus level can be supplied to the filter over a long duration to allow uninterrupted flow and achieve viral clearance validation conditions comparable to those of traditional batch spiking for batch processing. In this study, we developed a small‐scale inline virus spiking and mixing model as a proposed validation strategy for a connected, continuous chromatography and VF system operated in constant flow mode over long durations and with high throughputs. The small‐scale model was successfully operated for upwards of 72 h and produced the expected virus‐spiked product feed solutions with no deviation in virus, protein and buffer concentrations throughout the runs. In addition, laboratory‐scale Planova BioEX filters showed complete removal of porcine parvovirus with a logarithmic reduction value greater than 5.5 when run continuously for 72 h.Keywords: continuous virus removal filtration, inline spiking and mixing, viral clearance

Introduction

Biomanufacturing has undergone many transformations in recent decades to increase production while reducing costs and environmental footprint (Fisher et al. 2019; Schofield 2018; Gottschalk et al. 2012). Implementation of continuous bioprocessing both in upstream and downstream purification is one strategy for driving efficiencies in the production of therapeutics. Several unit operations, such as continuous perfusion cell culture in upstream processing and continuous virus inactivation in downstream processing, have already been successfully incorporated into the large‐scale production of biologics (Zydney 2015; Godawat et al. 2015). However, a majority of biomanufacturing has yet to fully transition to continuous processing in part due to the lack of research and models at the small‐scale. Thus, the push to transform downstream purification from its long‐established, traditional batch mode processing to a continuous mode of processing remains a relatively new phenomenon.

Virus filtration (VF), also known as nanofiltration, is one of the final steps in downstream purification and is implemented to remove small and large size virus particles from a product solution (PDA Virus Filter Task Force 2008). Using a size exclusion mechanism, VF has been shown to be very robust and effective in ensuring the viral safety of the final product, often achieving a virus logarithmic reduction value (LRV) of 4 or greater. In traditional manufacturing settings, virus filtration steps are operated under single‐use, batch mode processing. Implementation of continuous mode VF would allow for the filter to operate under extended processing times using constant flow mode and low flux to achieve a much higher throughput (Johnson et al. 2022; Lute et al. 2020; David et al. 2019). To date, VF has been successfully included in continuous processes in a laboratory setting but has not yet been implemented into continuous processes at large‐scale (Coolbaugh et al. 2021; Shirataki et al. 2023).

When implementing VF in a continuous process, there are multiple compatibilities and constraints to consider. VF has regulatory requirements for integrity testing of the virus filter and traceability to batch segregation. In batch production, large hold tanks are used between downstream purification steps to allow for in‐process sampling and testing of the product. In continuous operations, however, the product is expected to move through unit operations without pause. In fully continuous downstream trains, product flow into the VF step is driven by the preceding column chromatography step in a connected operation (Chiang et al. 2019; Angelo et al. 2019). Virus filters need to withstand the challenges associated with connecting these operational steps. These include variation in product feed following chromatography, such as elution peaks, which have the potential to increase system pressure and/or overload/foul the virus filter over time (Shirataki et al. 2021). In some cases, surge vessels are used in continuous processes, and their use before VF can help attenuate fluctuations in feed characteristics, including pH and conductivity variations as well as product concentration (Johnson et al. 2016). However, depending on their size, surge tanks can take up floor space in manufacturing rooms and increase residence time distribution of the final product (Sencar et al. 2020). Therefore, minimizing or foregoing surge tanks can contribute to increased operational flexibility and maximize efficiency for continuous processes. To better understand how this challenging process can be evaluated at small scale, we chose to forgo the addition of a surge vessel and directly connected the VF step to a flow‐through chromatography step.

In viral clearance validation studies, batch mode spiking is typically performed by adding a virus spike to the product, performing a prefiltration, and then operating the virus filter under constant pressure with high flux until the expected throughput and run time are reached. The filtrate is subsequently tested for virus removal. When testing viral clearance of a connected, continuous chromatography to VF operation, batch spiking upstream of the chromatography step can be challenging as virus would be removed in the chromatography step before reaching the virus filter. This is often circumvented by decoupling the two unit operations for viral clearance validation studies. Alternatively, by spiking a concentrated virus stock directly into the chromatography output, the system proceeds in continuous flow mode while a stable level of virus is supplied to the filter over a long duration with uninterrupted flow. The addition of a static inline mixer immediately following the spiked feed stream and upstream of the virus filter ensures proper mixing. Variations of these types of setups have been evaluated and shown to work successfully in several recently published studies. Lutz et al. (2010) were one of the first groups to investigate the use of inline spiking of virus as a way to avoid the prefiltration of spiked feeds as it has been shown that prefilters can unintentionally trap virus and thus lower the concentration of virus before VF. In their study, however, filtration was operated under constant pressure mode, similar to batch VF, and conditions for continuous processing were not tested. Bohonak et al. (2020) used inline spiking with daily swaps of virus‐loaded syringes for low flux filtration of highly concentrated protein feed resulting from continuous upstream processing and polishing chromatography steps. In this study, batch spiking was compared to inline spiking for a range of low and high monoclonal antibody (mAb) concentrations for runs lasting in duration of 24 to 72 h to optimize the conditions for VF in continuous processing. In our study, we chose to connect polishing chromatography and VF unit operations to assess a more complete downstream continuous processing model. Malakian et al. (2022) investigated viral clearance validation and residence time distribution for their inline spiking setup and compared continuous and batch virus filtration. One limitation of this study is that in a VF run of 1 h, a relatively short virus spike lasting 1 min was injected into the system via a syringe pump and pushed through an inline mixer before reaching the virus filter. Shirataki et al. (2023) explored end‐to‐end continuous processing of a monoclonal antibody that included perfusion, capture, low pH virus inactivation, polishing steps AEX and CEX flow‐through chromatography attached to an AKTA Pure 25 system and VF using Planova BioEX filters. Virus was spiked at a 1% ratio downstream of the chromatography step via the AKTA sample pump with mixing by the AKTA inline mixer. However, this mixer is known to perform less precisely and cause fluctuations in the system pressure likely due to induced aggregation of more sensitive molecules.

The purpose of this study is to demonstrate a feasible small‐scale viral clearance validation model for continuous VF. This was evaluated through the implementation of inline spiking in constant flow mode downstream of a flow‐through chromatography step to introduce virus to the product feed and to ensure that a continuous load of virus consistently reaches the virus filter (Lutz et al. 2010; Bohonak et al. 2020; Malakian et al. 2022; Hwang et al. 2023; Li et al. 2020). The model used flow‐through AEX and CEX chromatography columns fully connected to the virus filter and an AKTA Pure 25 pump to drive the product flow. Additionally, we separately tested the ability of the static inline mixer to consistently mix virus‐spiked solutions injected into the system via a syringe pump over several days under varying solution conditions. Continuous virus filtrations using the AKTA system were successfully run to achieve high throughputs and long durations without process pauses and over pressurizations, and all filtrations achieved complete viral clearance with virus LRV of 5.5 or greater.

Materials and Methods

Virus Removal Filters

Planova BioEX filters (0.001 and 0.0003 m2 effective surface area) from Asahi Kasei Life Science Corporation (Tokyo, Japan) were used for the inline spiking filtrations. NalgeneTM Rapid‐FlowTM Sterile Single Use Vacuum Filter Units with PES filters (0.2 μm) bottles from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA) were used as prefilters. All filtrations with virus‐spiked feed included prefiltration.

Proteins, Buffers, and Reagents

Bovine serum albumin (BSA) and corresponding buffer, 1X phosphate buffered saline (PBS), 15 mS/cm, pH 7.4, were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). mAb expressed by a CHO‐MK cell line in perfusion cell culture and purified through Protein A and polishing chromatography steps was concentrated to a 10 g/L stock solution as previously published (Shirataki et al. 2023) and provided with buffer (20 mM Tris‐acetic acid, 10 mS/cm, pH 6.5) from Asahi Kasei Life Science Corporation (Tokyo, Japan).

Virus Stock and Infectivity Assay

Porcine parvovirus (PPV), a small (18–24 nm), nonenveloped single‐stranded DNA virus, was used in this study. PPV strain NADL‐2 (ATCC VR742, ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) was propagated in PK13 cells (ATCC CRL‐6489) in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA) and supplemented with a 3% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and serum‐free media for media exchanges (Strauss et al. 2017). PPV virus stocks were prepared from infected PK13 cell culture supernatants at approximately 6 to 7 days postinfection by centrifugation at 1200xg for 20 min at 4°C and filtered through a 0.45 μm prefilter (Hongo‐Hirasaki et al. 2010). PPV titer was determined using the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) method using 1:10 serial dilutions on 96‐well plates with incubation at 37°C for 10 days and a media exchange on day 2. Infected cell culture wells were detected by hemagglutination assay (HA). PPV titer for load and permeate samples was determined using the Spearman‐Karber method and used to calculate PPV LRV.

Equipment and Setup

All runs were conducted with a pump in constant flow mode. For runs without a filter, product feed was supplied using a Masterflex Easy‐Load II peristaltic pump (Masterflex, Gelsenkirchen, Germany) with dual pump heads to minimize pressure fluctuations and PharMed BPT size 13 tubing (Masterflex, Vernon Hills, IL, USA), and inline spiking was conducted by supplying the spike solution through a Legato 200 series push/pull syringe pump (KD Scientific, Holliston, MA, USA) with a 20‐mL syringe followed by mixing with a static inline mixer (Koflo Corporation, Cary, IL, USA) as shown in Figure 1A. Sampling of mixed solution was conducted after the static mixer.

Pump‐driven setups with inline spiking and mixing. A: Supply of product feed through peristaltic pump with inline spiking and mixing. B: Flow‐through Cellufine MAX IB and MAX DexS chromatography operated by AKTA Pure 25 System with inline spiking and mixing input to a virus filter. P: Pendotech PressureMAT pressure sensor.

To test inline spiking of virus in continuous processing with VF, a flow‐through chromatography step was produced by connecting Cellufine MAX IB and MAX DexS columns (both 5 mL total volume, JNC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) in series to the column valve port of an AKTA PureTM 25 chromatography system (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) programmed with Unicorn 7.8 software. A VF step with a lab‐scale Planova BioEX filter was added after the inline static mixer as well as a sampling line operated by the push/pull syringe pump (Figure 1B). The product stream and spike flows were introduced through a 3‐way valve connected to the AKTA outlet line and syringe pump, immediately preceding the static inline mixer. An empty syringe for sampling spiked and mixed solution was similarly set on the pull side of the syringe pump between the pressure monitor and the virus filter; both syringes were used for the duration of each run. Filtration was driven by the AKTA system pump and pressure profiles were monitored by the AKTA system and Pendotech pressure monitors (Pendotech, Princeton, NJ, USA) placed before and after the static mixer.

Before each run, the settings of the peristaltic pump in Figure 1A and AKTA pump in Figure 1B as well as the syringe pump for both setups were confirmed to produce the respective target constant flow rate.

Inline Mixing Evaluation With Various Protein Concentrations and Buffer Compositions

Inline mixing and the stability of virus in mixed solutions were examined for the worse‐case scenarios of higher protein concentrations (1, 5, and 10 g/L BSA in 1X PBS buffer) and 1 g/L BSA protein with high/low pH and conductivity buffer solutions using the setup shown in Figure 1A. For both higher protein concentration runs and high/low pH and conductivity buffer conditions, a 14‐mL aliquot of each respective BSA solution was spiked with PPV to a target load titer of 6.0 log TCID50/mL with an overall 0.1% spike ratio. The spiked protein solutions were prefiltered with a 0.2 μm Nalgene Rapid‐Flow PES filter bottle before loading in the push/pull syringe pump. All runs were performed in duplicate. Virus titer was assayed by TCID50 assay.

For the higher protein concentration runs with 1, 5, and 10 g/L BSA in 1X PBS buffer, stock PPV with a titer of 8.1 log TCID50/mL was used to prepare the 14 mL spiked aliquot and the syringe pump speed was set to 4.9 μL/min. The peristaltic pump speed was set at 0.6 mL/min for collection of a total volume of 1742 mL tested in each run, which is the expected throughput over 48 h based on the speeds of the two pumps. Two samples were taken at the start of each run (t = 0) and either immediately stored at −80°C or held at room temperature for the duration of the run and then stored at −80°C until virus titer assay along with pooled samples of the mixed solution collected at 5, 21.5, 29, and 48 h.

To test the effect of buffer solution conditions on inline mixing, a 1 g/L BSA protein solution was prepared in four distinct buffers (Table 1): high and low pH and high and low conductivity. Stock PPV with a titer of 7.8 log TCID50/mL was used to prepare the 14 mL spiked aliquot and the syringe pump was set to 3.2 μL/min. The peristaltic pump speed was set at 0.2 mL/min for collection of a total volume of 878 mL, which is the expected throughput over 72 h based on the two pump speeds. Two samples were taken at the start of each run (t = 0) and either immediately stored at −80°C or held at room temperature for the duration of the run and then stored at −80°C until virus titer assay along with pooled samples of the mixed solution collected at 20, 25, 44, and 72 h.

For the virus‐spiked BSA filtrations, unprocessed protein was spiked with PPV to 8.0 log TCID50/mL with a 0.1% spike ratio to produce a target load titer of 6.0 log TCID50/mL to the virus filter. For mAb filtrations, the protein was similarly spiked with PPV to 7.7 log TCID50/mL. Given the small amount of protein used in the syringe relative to the total filtration volume, there was little concern about filter plugging due to using protein that was not processed on the chromatography columns.

Throughout the runs, pressure was maintained below 0.3 MPa to remain below the lowest maximum pressure of the system components (AKTA, column system, and virus filter) using the AKTA “Pre‐Column” and “Delta Column” pressure alarms. Aliquots of product feed stored at 4°C were warmed to room temperature before placing in the system and permeate collection was switched out daily. Before placement in the experimental setup, the filter was subjected to the pre‐use flush with the respective buffer offline. With the chromatography columns and the filter in place within the system, 40 mL of buffer was primed through the columns and directed to waste followed by an additional 10 mL of buffer that was directed to the virus filter for priming before switching to product feed. The mixed sample collected at the pull side of the syringe pump throughout the filtration duration and permeate samples collected daily were stored at ‐80°C until virus titer assay by the TCID50 method.

Results and Discussion

Effect of Protein Concentration and Buffer Composition on Inline Mixing

Virus stability in feed solutions can be impacted by high and low pH or conductivity as well as high protein concentration. Further, shifts in virus titer over long filtration run times could affect virus LRV calculations. As a first step, inline spiking was evaluated to confirm the feasibility of using a static inline mixer and to confirm virus stability over runs as long as 72 h. Inline spiking of PPV and mixing of BSA solutions at different concentrations showed stable average titers (average of duplicate runs for each condition) for samples collected for 1, 5, and 10 g/L in 1X PBS buffer over 48 h (Tables 3) and 1 g/L BSA in four buffer conditions of high and low pH or conductivity for 72 h (Table 4). Titers in Table 3 show an increasing trend, but PPV titers remain within the acceptable range of ±1 log of the target PPV spike of 6.0 logTCID50/mL for the mixed solution. These results confirm the stability of PPV as well as the effectiveness of inline mixing even for worse‐case filtration conditions with extended run times of 48 and 72 h.

Connected, Continuous Chromatography and Virus Filtration With Inline Mixing

After confirming sufficient inline spiking, mixing, and virus stability for extended durations, runs more representative of downstream processes were tested. Virus removal filtrations were conducted under a constant flow mode with spiking by syringe pump for both BSA and mAb proteins. The AKTA system was used in place of the peristaltic pump to connect flow‐through AEX and CEX columns and virus filtrations to simulate a more representative model of a connected downstream processing unit operation. This connected chromatography to VF model was able to process both BSA and mAb protein solutions without disruptions and pressure excursions over 72 h. Volumetric throughput and run time were selected based on material availability, target throughput ( > 1000 L/m2) and desired filtration time ( ≥ 48 h). Flow from the AKTA pumps and syringes pumps was supplied continuously and uninterrupted throughout the run times.

Operating multiple unit operations of downstream bioprocessing in a continuous manner requires simultaneously accommodating multiple sets of operating parameters and constraints. The individual components of the connected, continuous chromatography and virus filtration with inline mixing using the AKTA were developed in stages. First, the overall maximum operating pressure was set at 0.3 MPa (43.5 psi) based on the pressure parameters of the AKTA (max 2900 psi, 20 MPa), the column system (max 43.5 psi, 0.30 MPa for each column), and the virus filter (max 49.7 psi, 0.34 MPa). For all AKTA driven filtrations, no back pressure issues were experienced due to the virus filter.

Use of the peristaltic and syringe push/pull pumps at flow rates of 0.300 mL/min and 3.70 μL/min, respectively, resulted in a starting Planova BioEX filtration pressure of 10 psi, and for filtration of 0.2 g/L BSA alone, the pressure increased over 72 h to 25 psi (Figure 2A), which is well within the maximum operating pressure of the filter. As this flow rate was also suitable for the column chromatography process, the flow rate of 0.300 mL/min was set on the AKTA system and was used for the subsequent continuous runs.

(A) Filtration of 0.2 g/L BSA by peristaltic pump (protein only; pre‐AKTA BSA), following column chromatography on the AKTA (protein only; unspiked BSA) and following column chromatography on the AKTA with inline virus spiking (spiked BSA) and (B) Filtration of 2 g/L mAb following column chromatography on the AKTA with inline virus spiking (duplicate runs).

Next, the BSA solution was run through the Cellufine MAX IB and MAX Dex S columns and filtered through the Planova BioEX filter without virus spike using the AKTA. The virus filter pressure started at around 9 psi and increased to 9.5 psi (Figure 2A). The markedly smaller pressure increase compared to the first filtration alone can be attributed to the purification of BSA through the columns before reaching the virus filter, and this phenomenon was observed in previous work, where aggregate levels were reduced post chromatography (Shirataki et al. 2023). The virus‐spiked BSA filtration had conditions comparable to the non‐spiked filtration, with a starting pressure at 10 psi and increasing to 15 psi after 72 h. The larger pressure increase observed in this filtration compared to the non‐spiked filtration may be attributable to the impurities associated with the virus preparation spiked into the BSA loaded into the syringe. Four samples of the filtrate were collected at different timepoints over the course of the virus‐spiked filtrations and evaluated for viral clearance. Pressure profiles for all BSA filtrations including non virus‐spiked pump‐driven runs, non virus‐spiked and virus‐spiked AKTA runs are shown in Figure 2A.

Based on the system developed for BSA, mAb protein runs were developed and run in duplicate on the AKTA system, as shown in Figure 2B. Both filtrations displayed similar pressure profiles over 72 h: run 1 had a starting pressure at 13 psi and ended at 25 psi and run 2 had a starting pressure at 15 psi and ended at 27 psi. The higher protein concentration and virus spike, when compared to the BSA filtrations, contributed to higher pressure increases while remaining within pressure limits. Viral Clearance

The viral clearance of BSA and mAb runs with inline spiking of PPV are summarized in Table 5 and plotted against throughput in Figure 3. The PPV titer of the mixed feed stream collected by the pull syringe and PPV LRV show that all filtrations were conducted with PPV titer around the target of 6.0 logTCID50/mL, and complete clearance was achieved at all timepoints and in the pooled sample. For the spiked BSA filtration, the virus load titer was 6.38 logTCID50/mL. The run achieved complete viral clearance in samples 1–4 with PPV LRV ≥ 5.7. For mAb runs 1 and 2, the virus load titers were measured at 6.25 and 6.38 logTCID50/mL, respectively. Both runs achieved complete viral clearance in samples 1–4 with PPV LRV for run 1 at ≥ 5.5 and for run 2 at ≥ 5.7. Overall, these results demonstrate that extended filtration run times and high throughput volumes posed no problem for the virus filter in this setup. The filtrations ran without disruptions over the course of 72 h and no over pressurizations were observed on the system at any point. PPV LRV greater than 5 was achieved for every run and the Planova BioEX filters provided robust viral clearance under the selected conditions.

Conclusion

With recent efforts to shift biomanufacturing from traditional batch mode processing to continuous processing, there has been an increased push to connect downstream processing steps into one fully continuous system. In this study, we created a small‐scale model that incorporated both flow‐through chromatography and virus filtration into one continuous system without a surge vessel between the steps. For processes in which chromatography and virus filtration are connected unit operations, conducting the viral clearance validation in a coupled setup with inline virus spiking is more representative for continuous applications.

Here, we showcased the successful implementation of inline virus spiking for continuous filtrations operated under challenging solution conditions for extended times and high throughputs. Future studies are needed to test additional worse‐case conditions. Adapting this method for virus filtration coupled to a chromatography step operated in bind and elute mode will require accommodating variable feed applied to the filter. Other challenging conditions include testing different molecules and viruses that are known to cause aggregation and decreased throughput over the course of several days. Effective validation and better understanding of these factors will help facilitate the implementation of continuous bioprocessing and successful scale‐up transformations.
