Life in the slow lane: reflective practice in postgraduate Emergency Medicine training in South Africa
Authors: Sian Geraty, Jana Müller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmergency Medicine in South Africa faces multiple challenges from insufficient human and physical resources, high patient load, the rapid pace of the profession and the impact of trauma, vicarious trauma and moral injury on physician and trainee wellness. Recently, the College of Emergency Medicine of South Africa introduced wellness as a core competency for an Emergency Medicine specialist. In the context of the existing South African public healthcare sector and the literature on the challenges emergency medicine healthcare workers face, this may seem like an unrealistic expectation. The authors suggest possible strategies learned during engagement with health professions education to foster Emergency Medicine physician and trainee wellness. Slow medicine, transformative learning theory, and trauma-informed care are explored in the context of the South African Emergency Medicine postgraduate training platform, and suggestions for their incorporation into the workplace are presented. The authors do not offer a silver bullet for the complex challenges, but they open a dialogue to realistically explore ways to support this crucial workforce.Keywords: Emergency medicine, Health professions education, Transformative learning, Trauma-informed care

Introduction

Emergency Medicine (EM) postgraduate training in South Africa (SA) is in crisis. EM healthcare providers (HCPs) are overworked, understaffed, under-resourced, under-supported, and abused [[1], [2], [3]]. Faculty and trainees are at risk of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and burnout, potentially leading to chronic stress, depression, impaired patient safety, and even suicide [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. SA EM staff report an icidence of over 90% of verbal assault, and over 65% have been physically assaulted by patients [3]. Episodes of murder, rape, kidnapping, and armed robbery of HCPs are on the rise throughout SA [8]. The incidence of violence is likely highest in the pre-hospital sector (part of the EM training platform), where Emergency Medical Services (EMS) providers work in fear of armed robbery, assault, rape, and murder [9]. PTSD in HCPs is also often associated with secondary traumatic stress or vicarious trauma, arising from witnessing traumatic events in others [7,10]. However, burnout and PTSD may still not encompass the full psychological distress experienced by the violation of the EM provider’s moral code. Moral injury was first described in military personnel as “lasting psychological, biological, spiritual, behavioural, and social impact of perpetrating, failing to prevent, or bearing witness to acts that transgress deeply held moral beliefs and expectations” [11]. We recognise the parallels in African EM HCPs, who witness deep inequalities in the under-resourced African public healthcare system [2,9,12,13]. The increasing patient workload and deepening healthcare resource constraints affect healthcare worker agency, [2,12]. and all three increase the likelihood of medical errors [14,15]. These factors interact in cycles of trauma, PTSD and burnout in EM. HCPs struggle to access available support services, experience disturbed circadian rhythms, and have inadequate recovery [4,12,16]. EM HCPs are leaving the healthcare sector in droves, [4,17] leaving the rest battle-weary and working in a “warzone.” [1].

In facilities providing postgraduate EM specialist training, leaders have been asked to tackle the seemingly impossible: implementing workplace-based assessment (WBA). In the midst of the staffing crisis in the National Department of Health, [2] the additional workload on faculty and potentially “crushing” assessment burden on registrars seems to add to the woes [[18], [19], [20]]. As part of this curriculum renewal, the College of Emergency Medicine of South Africa has recently included “well-being” as part of the competency framework for the SA EM Specialist [21] Improved health, managing emotions, improved empathy, better patient care, teamwork, fewer errors, and a culture of positivity are all potential benefits to well-being in physicians [22,23]. Resilience, an individual’s ability to bounce back and become stronger during stressors, is complex and multifactorial [24]. Senior EM staff and faculty strive to improve resilience by advocating for strengthening the healthcare system through reduced workplace workload; reducing job-demand mismatch, improving staffing, adjusting working hours, and reducing system inefficiencies, which are undoubtedly key to addressing the pandemic of healthcare worker burnout [4,7,12]. However, no end is in sight, and it feels as though the broken system is winning.

Within the warzone, we see glimmers of hope – increased critical consciousness, social responsiveness, a strong sense of professional identity, and resilience in HCPs because of, not in spite of, the difficult experiences [[17], [25], [26], [27], [28]]. If resilience is not inherent, [24,29] how can EM faculty develop the tools for resilience and thriving in ourselves and those we lead and teach, while waiting on a resilient healthcare system?

We explore “slow medicine,” transformative learning theory, trauma-informed care, and their potential applications to EM.

Slow emergency medicine

“Slow medicine” has its roots in the Slow Food movement, which originated in Italy in the 1980s. “Fast” suggests pressured, high-output, high-volume – whether referring to food or to patient care. In contrast, slow medicine has been described as increased engagement and shared decision-making with patients, as well as intentional communication when making bedside supervision decisions – creating space and time for the patient and the task at hand in front of the provider [30]. Slow medicine describes patient care that is:

At first, “Slow Emergency Medicine” seems an oxymoron – who has time to “slow down” amidst long waiting times and fast-paced resuscitations? On closer examination, Slow EM is not about how quickly things are done – in the words of Jenny Watermeyer, “It is not just the pace. It is the posture.” For the full poem, see Poem. EM specialists warn against multitasking, recommend minimising interruptions, and prioritise taking breaks [32]. Close the door. Sit down if you can. Take no interruptions or have a system for managing them. Write notes in private. As a junior EM doctor recently noted, “Sometimes I just have to trust that someone else is taking care of the drama for a few minutes.” One Emergency Department (ED) posted a short “meditation suggestion” on the blood gas machine, prompting trainees to pause for the minute it takes to run its tests. Despite the short staff, most EDs defend academic training and regularly “huddle” to debrief, teach, laugh, and connect – no matter how many patients are waiting, these moments are important and ultimately lead to better patient care [33].

Undoubtedly, the death of a patient is one of the more emotion-laden, disorienting dilemmas and, in many cases, traumatic for the healthcare workers. The practice of a “pause” to honour the patient’s life and the team’s effort is being used more frequently worldwide. This pause has been shown to reduce internal conflict and improve short-term transitions to the next ED task, as well as overall feelings of connection, purpose, and resilience [34].

Implementing work-based observations and stimulated feedback sessions may create a pause in the bustle of the ED. A registrar taking time to engage in a learning conversation contrasts with the pace pressures to push queues of patients, [35] and the “think-aloud” debriefing process between learner and teacher encourages reflection and resulting expertise [36]. Although the time challenges of incorporating WBA are not lost on us, [18] these workplace-based teaching and assessment moments have the power to bring pause into the busy work weeks. Bernard [33] reminds us that reflecting itself may reduce burnout and improve resilience in EM trainees, and goes on to provide a beautiful example of “slow” and reflective emergency physicians (EPs) at work:

“During the shift, the physician is able to focus, is fully engaged with his or her patients, and is aware of his or her feelings and behavior at all times. Constant self-monitoring, expecting the unexpected, and quick thinking help the reflective EP minimize mistakes and lapses in professionalism” [[33], p.981].

However, what are we reflecting on, and how? Transformative learning theory and trauma-informed care may align with slow medicine to foster reflective, resilient, and relational EM faculty and registrars (Fig. 1).

Transformative learning

Transformative learning (TL) is described as engaging in critical reflection and dialogue to challenge one's frame of reference, resulting in action and future behaviour change [26,37]. Mezirow [26] contrasted the concept of maturity with the conscious recognition of self, in contrast to the frustration of repeated failures. TL can be summarised as a choice: to remain unchanged in the face of a challenge or to engage in critical reflection, dialogue, and personal transformation in response to a “disorienting dilemma” [17,38]. Described as events that disrupt our ways of previously seeing the world, disorienting dilemmas occur ubiquitously in HPE [27] and especially in emergency care settings [17]. Examples of these dilemmas include life changes both within and outside the profession, [25] exposure to death and grief, [17] and immersion in inequitable healthcare systems [27]. Negative emotions associated with these events are common and, if well managed, may lead to TL through critical reflection and dialogue [27,38]. Minor [23] draws parallels between mindfulness, which she defines as being present and aware without judgment, and reflection as described by Mezirow—critical, differentiating, and open to change. “At the heart of mindfulness is the essence of self-care” [[26], p.311]. TL has also been described in self-actualisation, a sense of belonging, professional identity formation, appreciation of relationships, and, ultimately, in the development of resilience [17] TL represents a fundamental change, one that changes our ways of being in the world [17,39].

For TL to occur, slowing down to create time and space for reflection and dialogue is essential. The key to operationalising this may be for the EM community to move towards quality, patient-centred engagements, intentional deliberation around decision-making, and junior-focused bedside teaching encounters that encourage slowing down and critical reflection. In particular, working through moral injury may require multiple conversations, deep reflection, collective dialogue and consideration of ethics and beliefs [40]. Within EM academic days, faculty and peer groups (“communities of practice”) could go a long way toward encouraging focused and critical reflection on disorienting dilemmas. Mezirow and Taylor [41] suggest using a life narrative, in whatever form (for example, poetry), to allow meaning to arise from experience, enabling students to rewrite their life narrative by replacing trauma with meaning. Building on the work of Yacek and Ijaz, [42] registrars may be provided with opportunities to reflect on others' narratives similar to their own, which can lead to an aspiration to make a difference. They thereby emphasise the need to be part of a community that seeks to process disorientations and even trauma to heal and grow, as a strategy to facilitate students’ TL. Fig. 2 summarises two potential outcomes from a disorienting dilemma and highlights the importance of self-reflection and dialogue with others for growth, resilience, and connection. Without these, disorienting dilemmas such as trauma may spiral down into PTSD and burnout, with its resultant negative effects on patients and teams.

Transformative learning as applied to trauma, resilience and mental health. When critical reflection and dialogue are utilised after a disorientating dilemma, this is more likely to lead to improved resilience and wellness. In the absence of these, disorientating dilemmas may lead to increased post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) with it’s subsequent adverse effects.

Of course, an important question to consider is whether TL was designed to facilitate the process of trauma. Many authors have researched and expanded on TL theory, and its role in processing disorientations [[42], [43], [44], [45]]. Some authors consider trauma, such as medical error or patient death, as a disorienting dilemma [45]. However, the disorienting dilemma that Mezirow refers to in TL theory concerns questioning one’s identity and worldview, a disorientation that challenges our assumptions and expectations about the world [26,38]. He based his philosophy partly on the humanising and critical consciousness work of Paulo Freire, who described true reflection leading to action, and authentic action stemming from critical reflection [46]. Simply put, TL should be a tool for emancipation [26] rather than a means of surviving trauma. Can TL allow for the trauma, moral injury, and stressors of SA EM to change us into change advocates for society? Is a shared goal of TL towards resilience and preventing burnout in these “helping professionals” as important as the established goal of TL towards critical consciousness for social action? [26,27,39,46]. As black and women’s rights activist Audre Lorde noted, "Caring for myself is not self-indulgence, it is self-preservation, and that is an act of political warfare" [47, p.130]. What are we fighting for, if not for the traumatised and vulnerable? Trauma-informed care centres patients and students and gives practice to the TL mission by creating spaces that facilitate support and open dialogue.

Trauma-informed care

Trauma–informed care (TIC) applies a set of strategies to patient care that assumes the potential for prior trauma and aims to prevent re-traumatisation. Its utility is rapidly growing worldwide in EM, and improvements in patient care include collaboration, physical safety assessments for patients, enhanced emotional safety for patients, and staff safety [48]. The six principles of TIC are outlined in Fig. 1. Embracing a TIC pedagogy overlaps with care for the vulnerable and patient empowerment, and may also remind HCPs of why we do EM.

While predominantly described in clinical settings, TIC has potential benefits for EM trainees as well - slowing down, improving patient connection and empathy, and the reminder to care for themselves in the face of trauma may have positive impacts on physician well-being [49]. It may be particularly applicable to registrars at risk for PTSD due to direct or indirect trauma [50]. When applied to health professions education, one key principle of TIC is to establish supportive learning environments in which hierarchies and power dynamics are recognised and made explicit. While slow EM encourages intentionally creating space for support and TL considers the importance of critical dialogue, TIC insists on trustworthiness as a pillar supporting both. TIC encourages clear and transparent communication and facilitates mutual peer support during group discussions. When TIC is applied to training, it encourages registrars to take ownership of their learning, including the ability to raise concerns about triggering situations. Implementation of WBA also creates opportunities for empowerment, as registrars choose assessment and feedback opportunities rather than the lack of agency associated with single-moment-in-time, high-stakes examinations. Together with TL and slow medicine, TIC holds social justice at its heart – towards access to learning for all, reducing power differentials, and empowering registrars who have experienced trauma to reflect on and build resilience.

Limitations

Improving resilience in EM trainees centres on creating safe spaces to work and learn – while not the focus of this paper, we do not stop our call to the National Department of Health and Higher Education Institutions in SA for improved working conditions, staffing, and the resources to reduce the healthcare inequalities in the public health sector.

Implementing slow EM, TL, and TIC into EM training calls for faculty development towards these ends. Faculty development is dependent on adequate institutional and systemic support, including protected time for faculty to learn and develop their own approaches [51]. Without adequate staffing and a systematic commitment, implementing these strategies may be uneven and dependent on already exhausted faculty members suffering the consequences of the very same broken system.

The context of this paper and the corresponding author’s lived experience are based in SA postgraduate EM training, and not all concepts may be applicable to other African readers. “Slowing down” is inherently context-specific, and EM processes, systems, and academic institutions may look different across African contexts. We hope that this piece can open up conversations about time in different cultural contexts.

This paper focuses largely on the “why” of slow EM, and its application to postgraduate EM training, and further work is needed on the practical applications in the SA public health context. Considerations for research include faculty support and training in TL and reflective practice, formal TIC training, and practical applications of “slow EM.”

Conclusion

“Do EM consultants reach the top because they have been the fittest, the strongest, and the most resilient? Alternatively, do the doctors with illness or difficult life situations filter out along the way?” [[32], p.102]. In the SA healthcare context, high patient workload, patient trauma, and lack of resources contribute to healthcare worker burnout, PTSD and suicide. In EM, one can argue that the system is creating these ‘doctors with illness’. We propose a culture of “Slow EM” toward relational, resilient, and reflective practice to mitigate some of the damage being done. Transformative learning and TIC are proposed as two pillars to encourage perspective shifts that foster increased resilience in the EM community. However, these require vulnerable conversations, critical reflection, commitment to self-care, and time and space for this transformation to occur [17,25]. The call for transformation in the chaos is a call for the entire team – starting with faculty and leadership – to slow down and reflect, build systems of support and demonstrate vulnerability.

Practically, we encourage starting “slowly.” One conversation, one debrief, one role-modelled pause, or one mindful moment of feedback at a time. There is, of course, no one single bullet towards thriving (and some days, surviving) African EM. There is no one single theory or easy way to navigate the messiness and challenges of being the face of the broken healthcare system. Our hope, however, is to open the conversation and consider that these counter-cultural theories of “slow” EM plant seeds of pause, critical reflection, transformation and relationships, with the potential to foster hope.
