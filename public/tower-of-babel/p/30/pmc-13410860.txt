Understanding residential socioeconomic and behavioral patterns for home air radon testing: a population-based intervention study in New Hampshire and Vermont (USA)
Authors: Rian M Hasson, Kayla A Fay, Julie E Weiss, Lynne M Clement, Michelle E Thompson, Jenna E Schiffelbein, P Jack Hoopes, Tamutenda Chidawanyika, Anna N A Tosteson, Judy R Rees
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRadon is a known environmental carcinogen associated with thousands of lung cancers each year. Understanding characteristics associated with testing is critical to design interventions to increase acceptance and promote mitigation. The objective of this study was to evaluate socioeconomic factors and cancer beliefs associated with previous home air radon testing, mitigation, and—for those who had not yet tested—acceptance of a free test.MethodsA population-based survey was administered to adults residing in New Hampshire and Vermont to understand previous home air radon testing and mitigation patterns, and among those who had not previously tested, acceptance of information on how to request a free test. The proportions of test kits requested and returned are reported.ResultsOf 1,717 survey respondents, 513 (29.7%) had previously tested for radon, 767 (44.7%) had not, and 437 (25.5%) did not know. Those who had not previously tested (767/1,280; 60%) had lower educational attainment, were less likely to be married, were less likely to be homeowners, and were more likely to express negative attitudes towards cancer prevention than those who had. Of 108 respondents whose previous test had shown levels ≥2.0 pCi/L, only 59 (55%) had installed a new mitigation system. Of those with a steady place to live, 996 had not previously tested for radon or did not know. Those 996 were offered information on how to obtain a free test kit and 66% accepted (659/996); 49% of those (324/659) used the information to request a kit, and 38% of those (124/324) returned the kit for testing. In multivariable models, not previously testing the home for radon was significantly associated with being divorced/separated, household income <$150,000, and lack of employment-based health insurance. Among those without a prior test, declining information for a free radon test was significantly associated with educational attainment of high school or less, and inversely associated with being divorced/separated.ConclusionsResident differences in socioeconomic factors and cancer beliefs regarding home air radon testing may offer potential opportunities for targeted interventions designed to decrease radon-associated lung cancer deaths.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12940-025-01260-w.Keywords: Radon, Radon testing, Socioeconomic factors

Background

Radon is a naturally occurring, colorless, odorless gas, and exposure has been linked to the development of lung cancer [1]. The American Cancer Society estimates there will be over 124,000 lung cancer deaths in the United States in 2025, and more than 21,000 of these deaths will be related to radon [2, 3]. While smoking still accounts for the most lung cancer related deaths, it is estimated that over 10% of radon related lung cancer deaths occur in non-smokers [4]. Among those who do smoke, exposure to radon is an even greater issue, as the combination of radon and smoking portends a greater risk of developing lung cancer [5]. Average concentrations of radon in home indoor air are increasing over time as a result of energy-saving changes in building construction including reduced use of natural ventilation, and motivating factors for testing and mitigation are not well known [6]. These factors make it increasingly important for individuals to understand their level of radon exposure and its implications for health, and for public health researchers to understand the socioeconomic and behavioral barriers to radon testing and mitigation in the home.

Radon exposure most commonly occurs through inhalation of radon gas which infiltrates the home through cracks in building foundations. It also occurs through radon gas released into the air when water is used and less commonly through ingestion via residential well water [1]. Research indicates that exposure at home contributes to 90% of radon-induced lung cancer risk [7], and residents experience linearly increasing risk of lung cancer with increasing radon exposures. The World Health Organization’s action threshold for mitigation is 2.7 picocuries per liter (pCi/L) [1], which is more conservative than the Environmental Protection Agency’s action level of 4 pCi/L) that was based on technological limitations in reducing elevated radon levels within the home) [8]. To address this, in 1984, home air radon testing was introduced to detect levels and inform mitigation needs for applicable home residents [9]. Approximately 1 in 15 homes in the United States exceed the EPA radon action level of 4 pCi/L, and the EPA recommends testing homes after any renovations and every two years as part of routine home maintenance [10].

Few studies have investigated the attributes of those who test their homes for radon and those who do not, and even fewer discuss mitigation efforts. Of the few studies completed, researchers have noted many different resident characteristics, beliefs, and knowledge levels that represent barriers to testing including lack of awareness of radon as a risk factor, age, educational attainment, economic stress, perceived severity, having a family/children, social influence and current smoking, house value, lack of home ownership, potential difficulty selling the house, cost of mitigation, and stigma (Fig. 1) [11–16]. In addition to these barriers, we know that community, health system, state, and national components of the resident ecosystem can also produce barriers that deter residents from participation (Fig. 1) [17–22].

Barriers to radon screening point to multiple points for targeted intervention

Approximately 100 people in New Hampshire (NH) and 50 people in Vermont (VT) die every year because of lung cancer attributable to radon [23, 24]. The purposes of this study were to understand demographic, social, cancer belief, and behavioral factors associated with radon testing and radon mitigation in NH and VT, which comprise the catchment area of Dartmouth Cancer Center, and—for those who had not yet tested—to evaluate the factors associated with acceptance of the offer of a free test. These factors may serve as targets for interventions designed to decrease radon-associated lung cancer deaths.

Methods

The Dartmouth Cancer Center’s Community Outreach and Engagement (COE) team worked with the University of New Hampshire (UNH) Survey Center to conduct a population-based survey in February and March 2022, using methods that have been described previously to represent the populations of New Hampshire and Vermont [25]. Briefly, we invited 6,309 members of the population-based Granite State (NH) and Green Mountain State (VT) panels to participate in a web-based health survey (Fig. 2). These panels consist of adult residents of NH and VT who had previously become panel members through recruitment processes including those randomly selected via phone calls and texting, as well as invitations from previous participation in surveys. Since these panels are assembled from simple random sampling of adults, and a weighting analytic process (described further below) further ensures representativeness of the general population, the panels are designed to collectively serve as representative views of the two states. The use of human subjects for this project was approved by the UNH Institutional Review Board for the Protection of Human Subjects in Research and the Dartmouth College Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects.

Flow diagram of included survey responses

Previously published survey questions [25] addressed demographic characteristics, cancer experiences and beliefs, social determinants of health, multiple home risk factors for cancer, smoking history, lung screening, and information about previous home air radon testing. Participants were asked if they had a steady place to live, and those who did not were excluded from the radon portion of the survey. Participants who reported having previously tested or not previously tested their home for radon were analyzed for factors relating to prior testing (N = 1,280 after excluding 437 who did not know, could not remember, or did not respond). Participants who had not definitely reported a prior home air radon test were offered information on how to order a free radon test from their state radon programs (N = 1,717, after excluding 513 who knew they had already tested). To understand their baseline radon knowledge, participants who were offered the test were not provided any educational information about radon itself. Those who chose to receive the information were sent a separate text or email explaining how to request a free test kit from the state radon program in the state where they lived. The request process in both states included a question about where the person requesting the test had heard about the free test; for participants who named the study as the source of their referral, both state radon programs provided aggregate data on the radon test results in June 2023, 15 months after the survey. At the time of the study, both states offered free radon testing but had no legal requirements for testing during property sales or property rentals.

There are several major differences between the two neighboring states’ radon programs, and participation in each state’s program depends on residence within that state. (i) Test kits are requested through a web-based form in NH, but by telephone or email in VT. (ii) Short term test kits are provided in NH (2–3 day tests, with advice to conduct confirmatory testing if a high or borderline result is obtained), but long term tests are provided in VT (at least 90 days, with advice to test during a winter season). (iii) The NH Radon Program sends reminders at 45 and 75 days to individuals who have not yet returned the kit to the laboratory, and the VT Radon Program sends reminders at 11 and 12 months. [26, 27]

Statistical analysis

To optimize generalizability to the populations of NH and VT, weighting was applied to the survey data in order to give descriptive population estimates of the parameters of interest. Data were weighted by age, sex, educational attainment, and region within the state of residence, using methods described previously [25]. Unweighted data are used when presenting the radon test results provided by the state radon programs.

Survey analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.). Data were analyzed using frequency and cross tabulation tables, including estimates of population totals, population proportions, and the Rao-Scott chi-square test of independence or no association between comparison groups. Analysis of subpopulations was addressed using the domain analysis, accounting for variability of the entire sample to estimate the variance of the subpopulation or subgroup(s). Due to rounding or when response instructions for survey questions asked respondents to select all that apply (noted in tables), percentages (%) may not sum to 100%.

Overall characteristics for study survey participants are presented. After excluding those who did not know whether they had tested their homes for radon, we compared those who had previously tested with those who had not previously tested, investigating associations by demographic characteristics, social determinants of health, risk factors within the home, lung cancer risk factors, and cancer experiences and beliefs. Of those who had not previously tested, we compared the characteristics of those who accepted or did not accept an offer of information to obtain a free air radon test. Of those who had previously tested for radon, we examined whether a radon mitigation system was installed for high radon test results, followed by the reasons for decisions not to take radon-related action. Lastly, we report aggregate radon test results from NH and VT state radon programs among survey participants who had not previously tested for radon and who accepted the offer of and returned a radon test kit.

Multivariable models used the SAS procedure SURVEYLOGISTIC and the results presented in Table 5 are odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). The procedure fits linear logistic regression models for binary response survey weighted data using the method of maximum likelihood. Two models were developed. The first modeled the effect of participants’ characteristics on their response to whether or not they had a previous home radon test. The second model was among those without a previous known radon test, and it described the characteristics of those who, during the survey, declined information about ordering a free radon test versus those who accepted it.

Results

Of the 1,717 survey participants (Fig. 2) representing the adult NH and VT population, weighted data showed that 55% lived in rural areas (Table 1). Our study of the associations between characteristics of those who did and did not previously test for radon in their homes included 1,280 participants (Fig. 2; Table 2). Compared to those who had previously tested their homes for radon (513; 40%), those who had not (767; 60%) included more participants with lower educational attainment (39% versus 23%), fewer married participants (55% versus 78%), and relatively more renters than homeowners (23% versus 11%). Among 655 participants with a private well, those who had not tested their well water for arsenic (another common, local, environmental hazard), comprised 39% of those who had not previously tested their home air for radon, but only 8% of those who had. Those with lung cancer risk factors including smoking within the past 15 years, and those eligible for lung screening, were also less likely to previously test for radon. Regarding social determinants of health, those that had not tested for radon included those with household incomes below $100,000 (68% versus 47% among 1–2 person households and 72% versus 51% among larger households); fewer with employer-based health insurance (49% versus 72%); and more Medicaid recipients (21% versus 5%). Participants who had not previously tested were more likely to agree that “there’s not much you can do to lower your chances of getting cancer” (24% versus 13%) and also that “there are so many different recommendations about preventing cancer, it’s hard to know which ones to follow” (72% versus 56%).

Of the 513 (40%) participants who previously tested their homes for radon (Fig. 2), 82 (16%) reported that their homes had tested high (≥ 4 pCi/L); 316 (62%) reported normal results (< 4 pCi/L), including 26 who reported their test result as having been high normal (2–4 pCi/L); and 115 (22%) did not specify (Table 3). Of 108 who reported that their homes had tested high (≥ 4 pCi/L) or high normal (2–4 pCi/L) for radon, 59 (55%) had installed a new mitigation system in their home, 31 (29%) confirmed that their home still had no mitigation system, and 10 (9%) reported that their existing mitigation system had not been modified after the test result. Key barriers to mitigation included lack of concern about the test result (16; 40%), lack of knowledge about how to mitigate (12; 30%), and cost (3; 8%).

The same demographic and socioeconomic factors were then assessed with respect to acceptance or refusal of information about how to order a free radon test; this information was offered during the survey to participants with a steady place to live who had not previously tested for radon (N = 767), or who did not know (N = 437) (N = 1,172, weighted data, Table 4). Just over half of the 1,172 participants who responded to the offer of information to obtain a free radon test (658/1,172; 56%) accepted the offer. Compared to those who accepted the information, participants who declined were more likely to have lower educational attainment (55% versus 33%) and to have never married (42% versus 26%), and they were less likely to think it was important to discuss home radon testing with a healthcare provider (25% versus 60%). However, participants who were eligible for lung cancer screening were more likely to accept the free radon test (p = 0.03), and among those eligible for lung screening, those who had received a CT scan to check for lung cancer were also more likely to accept the radon test (p < 0.0001).

In both states combined, 996 participants with a steady place to live who did not report having tested previously were offered information to obtain a free radon test during the survey (Fig. 3, unweighted data). Of those, 659 (66%) accepted this offer, and 324/659 (49%) used the information to request a free test kit from their state’s radon program (50% in NH and 47% in VT). Only 124/324 (38%) returned the kit to the laboratory for analysis within 15 months (40% in NH and 35% in VT). Overall, only 124/996 (12%) participants who were offered the information to obtain a free test actually returned the completed test kit to the laboratory for analysis (13% in NH and 11% in VT). The EPA action limit of 4 pCi/L was exceeded in 25/83 (30%) homes in NH and 5/41 (12%) in VT. Homes within the 2–4 pCi/L range, at which remediation should be considered, comprised a further 23/83 (28%) in NH and 1/41 (2%) in VT.

Logistic regression was then used to build age- and sex- adjusted multivariable models to assess factors for not previously testing the home for radon and for declining a free radon test (Table 5). Interestingly, those who did not previously test their home were significantly more likely to be divorced/separated (OR 2.18, 95% CI (1.14–4.18)), have a household income less than $150,000 (OR 4.13, 95% CI (1.97–8.64) and have health insurance other than provided by their employer (OR 2.80, 95% CI (1.61–4.86)). Among participants who had not previously tested for radon, those who were more likely to decline information for a free radon test were significantly less likely to be divorced/separated (OR 0.43, 95% CI (0.19–0.96)) and significantly more likely to have less than a high school education (OR 2.73, 95% CI (1.33–5.63)).

Discussion

Radon is an important environmental and public health concern, affecting an estimated one in 15 homes in the US, one in seven in VT, and one in three in NH.[10, 26] Given that radon is the second leading cause of lung cancer and demographics, attitudes, and behaviors can drive motivations to participate in environmental testing, [28, 29] it is vital to understand associated factors for home air radon testing and mitigation to increase participation. We found many differences in the characteristics of those who had previously tested for radon and those who had not, most of them reflecting social or economic disadvantage: educational attainment, marital status, household income, health insurance, home ownership, smoking in the past 15 years, and attitudes towards cancer prevention. In particular, our finding that renters are less likely to have tested their homes has important policy implications; for example, Maine’s legal requirement for landlords to provide tenants with their radon test result is linked to important contractual protections for the tenant [30]. This study contributes to the current body of radon testing literature, as it is one of few that explores the association of socioeconomic and behavioral patterns regarding prior home testing and mitigation, in addition to direct observation of testing practices in those who had not previously participated.

Our findings are in line with prior research that has demonstrated a number of factors associated with home air testing for radon including education, age, gender, perceived severity, social influence, current smoking, previously hearing of radon-related health issues or understanding how they affect the home-buying process, caring for family, higher median home value, rurality, and increased radon risk potential. [13, 14, 16, 17, 31, 32, 33] Khan et al. found that understanding the risk of radon, knowing someone who had lung cancer, having a family/children, and better financial resources were all facilitators for home radon testing [12, 13]. In our study, having children in the household was not significantly associated with prior radon testing, nor with accepting information about ordering a free radon test. This may indicate an opportunity for education in pediatric primary care or even during prenatal care so that parents understand the importance of reducing exposure to ionizing radiation during childhood. Khan et al. also noted several other reported obstacles to testing, or taking preventive action including a lack of homeownership, potential difficulty selling the house, cost of mitigation, and stigma [12, 13]. Taken together, these studies have identified potentially important resident characteristics that could be targeted when designing strategies to increase participation in radon screening.

Among our study participants who had previously tested their home for radon and found a high or high/normal radon value, almost half noted that they either still had no mitigation or the existing mitigation system had not been updated. These participants cited multiple barriers, including a lack of knowledge of how to mitigate and a lack of concern over the result and expense. These findings are consistent with those of Khan et al., who noted that barriers to taking action after a high-test result included lack of homeownership, potential difficulty selling the house, cost of mitigation, and stigma [12, 13].

In our survey, the free radon test was offered without accompanying education about the risks of radon. We did not measure knowledge about radon, and one possible explanation for our findings is lack of knowledge or interest among those who did not accept the offer of a free test, a group with disproportionally lower educational attainment and less likely to have heard of a test to screen for lung cancer, and less likely to think it was important to discuss radon with a healthcare provider. Other studies have demonstrated that lack of information and/or misinformation is a major barrier to testing [12–14, 34]. A systematic review by Vogeltanz-Homes reported that of those who had heard of radon, the majority did not know it causes cancer, and misinformation regarding radon risk was very common, especially in those aged less than thirty and/or with lower educational attainment [34]. Khan et al. also reported that one of the top barriers to testing was lack of awareness of radon as a risk factor [12–14].

Several studies have looked at potential interventions to increase home radon testing. Cholowsky et al. reported that only 20% of people were prompted to test after one encounter with information to increase awareness about radon; 65% needed 2–5 encounters, and 15% needed 6 or more encounters [33]. Wu et al. conducted a study to compare outreach strategies for increasing radon testing. They found that participants who were recruited by telephone were more likely to complete the home test compared to those who were recruited in-person [35]. In a randomized study, Kim et al. found that those who used a smartphone app designed to provide information and the ability to request a radon test showed significantly greater knowledge of radon and were three times more likely to request a radon test kit than those who received information via a brochure [36]. These studies highlight the importance of not only education in promoting radon testing, but finding the right messaging and strategy to encourage follow-through in the testing process.

Previous reports in the literature have shown a wide range of return rates for radon testing. Wu et al. reported a 62.1% and 29.4% radon test return rate after their phone based and in-person based recruitment, respectively, while Kim et al. reported only 3% test return rate after receipt of either app-based information or a brochure [35, 36]. Interestingly, only 12% of our study participants who were offered information about obtaining a free test actually returned one for analysis, and each step of the process proved to be a barrier. Just over one-third of those offered information about the free test ordered one, and the proportions were similar in NH (33%) where tests are ordered online and VT (31%) where tests are ordered by email or phone. The process to return the completed test for analysis was also a barrier; only 38% of those who ordered a test kit returned it for analysis, and the proportions were slightly lower in VT (35%), which offers a long-term test, than in NH (40%), which offers a short-term test. VT’s long-term test is recommended to be set up over a winter season, so the timing of our survey in late February and early March may have affected the proportion returned.

Ultimately, a better understanding of testing and post-test behavior offers many avenues for improving participation in home air radon testing. We identified many demographic and belief facilitators to home radon testing and mitigation, and better understanding their motivations will improve future efforts. In this study we observed patterns in both resident characteristics and beliefs to try to elucidate associations with radon testing and mitigation. To increase the chance of successful interventions, we are currently conducting qualitative exploration of these factors. A future manuscript will be forthcoming reporting the findings from this exploration in addition to resident knowledge levels, community, health system, state, and national barriers that will be needed to evaluate the radon testing landscape with a wider lens to have a more far-reaching and longer-lasting impact.

There were several limitations to this study. First, because the test kit was offered without providing information about what radon is and why one should test, our findings reflect a combination of effects that include pre-existing knowledge and participant motivation to do additional research if they had not heard of radon before. Second, VT’s radon program recommends testing during winter months which may have decreased the testing completion rate, as our survey was administered in late February and early March. Of note, NH offers a short-term test kit which can be completed within a few days, while VT provides a long-term test kit, which takes at least 90 days to complete. Coupled with the recommendation that the testing be completed in the winter months, this longer timeframe could pose a potential barrier for testing. Third, the state radon programs could only provide aggregate data on tests returned to their laboratory from participants who said they had been referred from this study; this limited our individual-level analysis to those who accepted information about how to obtain a free test, and correctly stated that they had been referred from this study. We could not associate individual level testing with risk factors such as smoking or lung cancer screening eligibility. Additionally, it is possible that some individuals would be more likely to return a completed test if they have a financial stake in it i.e. if they are required to pay a nominal fee for the test; we were unable to assess this hypothesis in our study. Further, while our study population had a diverse sample regarding age, gender, education, marital status, household size, and income, our population is largely white rural; this limits our ability to investigate differences by race and ethnicity and in urban settings. Lastly, participation in any phone/text-based survey runs the risk of selection bias, although our statistical analyses were weighted to better approximate the general population, as described previously [32].

Conclusions

There are several demographic, cancer belief, and socioeconomic differences between residents who test their homes for radon and those who do not, which offer possible targets for intervention. These differences will be important in customizing educational interventions to promote radon testing. When offered information about a free radon test through state radon programs, each step in the testing process – deciding to accept a free test, ordering the test, and returning it to the laboratory - represented a potential barrier to completing the process. Even those who had previously tested for radon did not necessarily take the recommended action to protect their homes when the test result was high.
