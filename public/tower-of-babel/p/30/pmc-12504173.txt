Reduced ALDH1A1 expression in multiple myeloma cells increases resistance to daratumumab via downregulation of retinoic acid
Authors: Sujie Zheng, Jing He, Xiaolu Song, Ye Peng, Wufeng Yuan, Xiangmin Tong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Multiple myeloma (MM) remains challenging in relapsed/refractory cases due to resistance to therapies like the proteosome inhibitors or anti-CD38 antibody daratumumab (Dara). This study investigates the dual role of ALDH1A1, an aldehyde dehydrogenase implicated in drug resistance, especially in modulating daratumumab efficacy. Clinical samples from newly diagnosed multiple myeloma (NDMM) patients and in vitro studies demonstrated that ALDH1A1 expression increases consistently with the development of drug resistance to proteasome inhibitor-based chemotherapy. However, paradoxically, daratumumab-resistant non-responder relapsed/refractory MM (nrRRMM) patients showed lower ALDH1A1 expression compared to responder RRMM (rRRMM) patients. Mechanistically, ALDH1A1 enhanced CD38 expression by upregulating retinoic acid (RA), which activated the retinoid acid receptor (RAR) signaling pathway. Knockdown of ALDH1A1 in MM cell lines (H929, RPMI 8226) reduced CD38 levels, impaired daratumumab-mediated antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), and suppressed NK (natural killer) cell activity (perforin/granzyme B secretion). In contrast, RA supplement reversed declined CD38 expression caused by knocking down ALDH1A1, thereby restoring antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) efficacy. In vivo, ALDH1A1 inhibition diminished daratumumab’s antitumor effects in xenograft models, while RA co-administration reversed this resistance. These findings highlight ALDH1A1 as a context-dependent regulator: promoting chemotherapy resistance but enhancing daratumumab sensitivity via RA-CD38 axis activation. This study identifies ALDH1A1 as a predictive biomarker and proposes therapeutic strategies combining RA or ALDH1A1 modulation to overcome daratumumab resistance in RRMM.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a hematological malignancy characterized by the clonal proliferation of malignant plasma cells in the bone marrow. These abnormal plasma cells produce excessive amounts of monoclonal immunoglobulins, leading to various clinical manifestations and complications [1]. MM accounts for approximately 1% of all cancers and about 10% of all hematologic malignancies, predominantly affecting older adults, with a median age at diagnosis of around 70 years [1, 2]. The disease is often associated with a range of symptoms, including bone pain, anemia, renal impairment, and increased susceptibility to infections due to compromised immune function [3, 4]. Treatment options for MM have evolved significantly over the past two decades, incorporating novel agents such as proteasome inhibitors and immunomodulatory drugs, leading to improved survival rates. However, the disease progression of relapsed/refractory MM (RRMM) patients is still a big obstacle for treatment of MM [5, 6]. Recently, anti-CD38 antibody, Daratumumab (Dara), has been one of the major breakthroughs in improving treatment response and clinical outcomes for relapsed/refractory MM (RRMM) patients [7, 8]. It triggers direct cytotoxic effects and activates immune-mediated mechanisms through binding to CD38 on malignant plasma cells, most were antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC) [9]. Despite these advancements, many patients still experience the emergence of early drug resistance and relapse [10, 11], and the underlying mechanisms behind this clinical behavior remain largely unknown.

Aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) is a group of NAD(P)-dependent intracellular enzymes involved in retinoic acid metabolism, whose activity is increased in both hematopoietic stem cells and cancer stem cells (CSCs) in a variety of tumor types including MM, and ALDH1A1 (aldehyde dehydrogenase 1 family member A1), is most abundantly expressed isoenzyme in MM [12]. In multiple myeloma, ALDH1A1 is considered a marker for tumor-initiating cells, and it is highly expressed in cancer stem cells within the myeloma, and its presence is associated with increased tumor growth and poor prognosis [13]; essentially, high ALDH1A1 levels also have been reported to be associated with drug resistance, mostly are proteosome inhibitor, but whether its overexpression is also associated with CD38 antibody resistance is not investigated thoroughly. Multiple myeloma stem cells (MMSCs) have also been considered as the major cause resulting in relapse [14], but the role of ALDH1A1 in maintenance of stemness of MM is not clarified so far. Besides keeping the stemness of MM, ALDH1A1 was also reported to up-regulated PI3K-AKT and Wnt/catenin pathways to promote tumor proliferation and drug resistance [15, 16].

Currently, there is a big lack of knowledge about the role of ALDH1A1 in MM sensitivity to anti-CD38 antibody-based treatment, specifically Dara [17]. Previous studies demonstrated that upregulation of CD38 expression on multiple myeloma cells by retinoic acid improves the efficacy of Dara, and ALDH1A1 is a key enzyme to produce retinoic acid [18]. Therefore, ALDH1A1 might be helpful to enhance the efficacy of Dara. It seems that ALDH1A1 may play a dual edged role in MM treatment, based on different treatment strategy selection. In the current study, based on MM clinical samples from our hospital, we first try to reconfirm the role of ALDH1A1 in first-line chemotherapy combination treatment which proteosome inhibitor is dominant; after that, continue to investigate its role in MM sensitivity to Dara, further using in vitro and in vivo tools, to clarify its underlying mechanisms in regulating Dara drug resistance.

Materials and methods

Patient cohort

This study was approved by the institutional review board at the Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. Fifty-eight new diagnosed MM (NDMM) patients were enrolled after obtaining informed consent. The inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) The diagnosis of MM must comply with the 2014 or 2016 International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) criteria for diagnosis, staging, and treatment response assessment [19–21]. (2) According to the diagnostic criteria for MM [21], patients must have clonal plasma cells greater than 10% in the bone marrow, accompanied by at least one of the CRAB symptoms: elevated calcium (Ca > 11mg/dL), elevated creatinine (Cr > 2mg/dL), reduced hemoglobin (Hb < 10g/dL), or bone lesions (lytic bone destruction or pathological fractures). The NDMM patients received VRd and KRd regimen. Their chemotherapy strategies included VRd: Bortezomib + Lenalidomide + Dexamethasone, or KRd: Carfilzomib + Lenalidomide + Dexamethasone. The 20 age and sex matched healthy control (HC) subjects were enrolled to obtain the bone marrow cells after patient consent.

Another 61 RRMM patients were recruited and treated with Dara-Rd (daratumumab + lenalidomide + dexamethasone) therapy. The relapsed/refractory MM refers to patients who experience disease progression after achieving at least partial response (PR) and show no response to salvage therapy (failing to achieve at least minimal response (MR). Enrolled RRMM patients must have received at least 2–4 cycles of treatment with VRd and KRd regimens. The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) Patients who have received fewer than 2 cycles of chemotherapy regimens; (2) Patients diagnosed with plasma cell leukemia or primary amyloidosis.

RRMM patients were grouped based on their response to Dara treatment as follows: (1) The responsive RRMM (rRRMM) group: Patients who achieved a treatment response of PR or better (≥ PR) after Dara-Rd regimens, including stringent complete response (sCR), complete response (CR), very good partial response (VGPR), and PR; (2) The non-response (nrRRMM) group was defined as patients who achieved a treatment response below PR (< PR) after 2–4 cycles of Dara-Rd regimens, including minimal response (MR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD). A summary of key clinical and treatment data of included patients is available in Table1. No sex or gender analysis was carried out.

Characteristics of the patients recruited at baseline

Primary MM cells collection from bone marrow of patients

Bone marrow (BM) aspirates were obtained from patients before they received and after 2–4 cycles of VRd, Krd or Dara-Rd therapy. The CD138-positive cells were collected from BM of diagnostic samples. Briefly, BM plasma cells were enriched using anti-CD138-coated magnetic microbeads and AutoMACS Pro Separator (130-097-614, Miltenyi Biotec GmbH, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) as previously described by Bansal, R et al. [22]. The MM cells collected were used to isolate mRNA and total protein, for reverse transcription qPCR, and western blot.

The study protocol was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committees of the Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital (Ethics number: QT2025029). All patients provided written informed consent for their bone marrow tissue samples to be used for research.

Gene expression profiling

The Gene expression profiling (GEP) cohorts were collected from GEO database, and GSE276561 and GSE47552 were employed. All the patients in both two data sets were newly diagnosed MM subjects. GSE276561 containing 67 healthy donors, monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS), 120 smoldering MM (SMM), 689 newly diagnosed MM (NDMM). GSE47552 analyzed gene expression profiling in 5 bone marrow healthy donors, 20 patients with MGUS, 33 with high-risk SMM and 41 with bone marrow MM. The two datasets were downloaded to explore the gene expression of ALDH1A1 in NDMM patients.

Cell lines and culture

Human MM cell lines NCI-H929 (H929), RPMI 8226 (8226) were purchased from Chinese academy of medical sciences (Beijing, China). The H929 and 8226 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Invitrogen, CA, USA). All media supplemented with inactive 10% fetal bovine serum (Hyclone, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 mg/mL streptomycin (60162ES76, Yeasen Biotech, CHN). Nature killing (NK) cells were separated from PMBCs of healthy donor by cell sorting magnetic bead CD56+ (130-092-661, Miltenyi Biotec GmbH) and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, recombinant human interleukin IL-2 (I2644-10UG, Sigma-Aldrich), and IL-15 (SRP3077, Sigma-Aldrich). The activation and expansion of NK cells followed the methods reported by Thangaraj et al. [23]. All cells were cultured in humidified 5% CO2 incubators at 37℃. Dara (HY-P9915, MCE, USA) was obtained to explore its effects on MM cells in vitro and in vivo. For in vitro analysis, Dara with concentration of 100 µg/mL were added in medium and cultured with cells (1 × 106) at 37 °C for 1 h. Human IgG (100 µg/ml, 14506-50MG, Sigma-Aldrich) was used as control (CTRL). For in vitro studies, cell samples were prepared in triplicate for each group. All in vitro measurements were performed in triplicate.

Lenalidomide-resistant cell line induction

Stable lenalidomide -resistant cells were constructed following the methods reported previously [24]. The gradually increasing concentration of lenalidomide in culture medium ranges from 1 µM to 30 µM for every 5 days. A stable passaged cell line in medium containing 30 µM of lenalidomide was final obtained and cultured.

Lentiviral gene transfection and gene knockdown

The ALDH1A1 was knockdown (KD) by transfected with shRNA. The adopted short hairpin RNA (shRNA) for ALDH1A1 was used to interfere with its expression, and the sequence was 5’- 3’: shALDH1A1-1, GGCACTCAATGGTGGG AAATTCAAGAGATTTCCCACCATTGAGTGCC [25]. shALDH1A1-2, GCCAAATCATTCCTTGGAATT; shALDH1A1-3, CGGGCTAAAGAAGTATATCCTT. The negative sequence for knockdown is 5-TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT-3. The sequences were packaged by super silencing vector (C06001, GenePharma, Shanghai, China) and selected by ampicillin treatment 72 h after transfection. The over expression (OE) of ALDH1A1 was introduced to ALDH1A1-KD cells to confirm its role in MM cells. Lentiviruses containing the human cDNA of ALDH1A1 sequence (NM_000689.3) and negative control sequence (CTRL) were purchased from Sino Biological Ltd (HG11388-ACGLN, Beijing, China). MM cells were transfected with lentivirus vector to yield ALDH1A1- over-expressed (OE) MM cells. The titer of the infected lentivirus was 5 × 107 TU/mL and the multiplicity of infection (MOI) was 20. Transfected cells were selected with ampicillin treatment 72 h after transfection. The transduction efficiency was determined by western blotting.

ADCC and cytotoxicity assay

Dara-mediated ADCC and cytotoxicity were measured in a 96-well plate. Fifty microliters of MM cells (1 × 10⁴ cells) were treated with 100 µg/ml of Dara for 1 h after cells were labeled by carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE, 65-0850-84, Invitrogen), and 50 µL NK cells were loaded in wells with effector-to-target (E : T) ratio of 10:1 and then co-cultured at 37 °C for 4 h in a 5% CO2 incubator. One microliter of propidium iodide (PI, Life Technologies, USA) was added in culture medium to label dead cells. Cells were analyzed by a BD FACSCanto™ II (BD Biosciences, NJ, USA). The ADCC efficiency was calculated as the relative dead cells among the CFSE positive cells after deducting spontaneously died cells.

Extracellular perforin, granzyme B and INF-gamma measurement

Commercial enzyme-linked immunoabsorbent assay (ELISA) kits (NBP3-11726, DGZB00, and DIF50C, Novus Biologicals) were used measuring above granule contents, following protocols reported previously [26, 27]. The Supernatants were analyzed by ELISA for the contents released into the culture medium following the protocols provided by the manufacturer.

Flow cytometry analysis

The positive CD38 cells were labeled by APC anti-human CD38 antibody (E-AB-F1058E, Elabscience) or APC anti-mouse CD38 antibody (E-AB-F1193E, Elabscience) and analyzed by FACSCanto™ II flow cytometer. The concentration of the obtained cells was adjusted to 1 × 106 cells/mL and then flow cytometry was performed following reported methods [28]. At least 1 × 104 target cell events were obtained and the median fluorescence intensity (MFI) of CD38 + cells were compared after cells were analyzed by FlowJo™ software.

mRNA sequencing

Cells with or without ALDH1A1 knockdown (N = 3) were prepared into single-cell suspensions and then resuspended in low-temperature phase buffered saline (PBS) for sequencing analysis. BioMiao Biological Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd., China completed the cell library’s construction and sequencing. They were using the HISAT2 system to integrate data and align with Homo_ Sapiens GRCh38_ The release95.genome.fa genome. The successfully aligned data was assembled using StringTie. Subsequently, FPKM was used as a quantitative indicator to obtain differentially expressed genes between the two groups. Further screening was performed using p-value < 0.05, logFC<−1, or logFC > 1 to obtain significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The list of significant DEGs was uploaded to DAVID (https://david.Ncifcrf.gov/) to perform the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis.

Gene expression analysis

Human ALDH1A1 (NM_000689, MD, USA) and CD38 (NM_001775) qRT-PCR primer were obtained from OriGene. Briefly, the total RNA was extracted using a Trizol reagent kit (R1100, Solarbio Lifescience, Beijing, China). The solution was extracted by chloroform. The concentration and purity of RNA were measured by a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). For RT-qPCR, 5 µg RNA was added to perform reverse transcription and qPCR was performed. GAPDH was used as endogenous control to compare expressions of ALDH1A1 and CD38 by ΔΔCt assay.

Western blot

The RIPA lysis solution was added to lysate cells or tissues. The fully lysed solution was placed in 4 ℃ and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min. The concentration of total protein in supernatant was measured by a BCA kit (PC0020, Solarbio Lifescience). A total of 30 µg protein was loaded in separating gel for sodium dodecyl-sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (YA1701, Solarbio) and blocked by 5% skimmed milk for 1 h at 37 °C. The primary antibody for ALDH1A1 (12035, 1:1000 dilution, Cell signaling), CD38 (1:1000 dilution, Cell signaling), RAR (62294, 1:1000 dilution, Cell signaling) and internal reference β-actin (MA5-15739, 1:1000, Invitrogen) were loaded on the membrane and cultured overnight. A secondary HRP-conjugated secondary antibody was further loaded in membrane and cultured 2 h before the bands were developed by ELC reagent. The images of bands were analyzed by the Image Lab Software (V 6.0, Bio-Rad Laboratories).

Rescue assay

For rescue assay, retinoic acid (RA, HY-14649, MCE, USA) 5nM was adopted [29] to verify the effect of ALDH1A1. Cells in control group (CTRL) were treated by solvent (DMSO).

Human NK-reconstituted murine model of MM

The NSG male mice, with age of 5-6-week-old was adopted to construct subcutaneous tumor [30]. The experimental procedures and protocol (No: 03–043) were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). All mice were housed in a pathogen-free environment and were handled in strict accordance with Good animal Practice. 2 × 106 H929 cells were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of 6-week-old male NCG mice (Biocytogen LTD, Beijing, China). Tumor burden was serially monitored every four days. After tumor engraftment reached to 100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into 4 groups (vehicle control, which received PBS injection; Dara treatment; Dara + ALDH1A1 inhibitor (673 A, 20 mg/kg); Dara + ALDH1A1 inhibitor + RA (20 mg/kg body weight by oral gavage). Dara was given intraperitoneally for a total of 3 administrations (8 mg/kg per mouse weekly) starting on treatment day 1 following randomization. Human primary NK cells (5 × 106) were injected intravenously the day after the Dara injection once per week for 3 weeks. Tumor sizes were measured using Vernier caliper every four days. The mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide before the tumors exceeded 2.0 cm in any dimension. Each mouse was free to access drinking water and pellet diet during the study. Their body weight was weighed weekly, and the tumor size were measured with a vernier caliper every 3 days.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data were presented and analyzed by GraphPad 10.4.2 software. For all in vitro assays, experiments were performed with triplicate biological replicates, and the measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For clinical sample analysis, The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to confirm the normal data distribution. Student’s t-test was adopted for two group comparisons with normally distributed pairwise data and the Mann-Witney test for abnormally distributed data. Student t test was adopted for two-group analysis. For multi-group comparison, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for multi-group comparisons followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. p < 0.05 was considered statistically different.

ALDH1A1 directly regulates CD38 expression

To validate whether ALDH1A1 regulates CD38 expression, we generated ALDH1A1 knock-down (KD) MM cells in H929 using three different shRNA sequences (Fig. 4. A) and selected the most effective shRNA (shALDH1A1-2) for further analysis (labeled as shALDH1A1 subsequently). As shown in Figure. 4. B, the loss of ALDH1A1 was indeed associated with CD38 downregulation on the mRNA levels. We next sought to confirm whether the overexpression (OE) of ALDH1A1 could restore the expression of CD38 in KD MM cells. By introducing ALDH1A1-OE via plasmid (Fig. 4. C), we proved that CD38 mRNA level was significantly restored in MM KD cells (Fig. 4. D). Moreover, we confirmed that the cell-surface expression of CD38, which is important for Dara binding, was significantly decreased in KD MM cells but increased after introducing ALDH1A1 by flow analysis (Fig. 4. E). Discussion

CD38 monoclonal antibodies, represented by Dara and isatuximab, have shown significant efficacy in the treatment of MM [7, 38–40], but the development of drug resistance has limited their long-term use. CD38 is the core target of targeted therapy, and its downregulation or loss of expression is one of the important reasons for drug resistance in targeted therapy [41]. Downregulation or deletion of CD38 expression is a multifactorial dynamic process in which tumor cells evade targeted therapy. This process involves mechanisms such as trogocytosis [42], gene regulation [43], protein stability [44], and CD38 gene mutation [45]. Overcoming drug resistance through the aforementioned mechanisms has become an important entry point for improving CD38 efficacy. Conventional chemotherapy regimen VRd and KRd might lead to upregulation of ALDH1A1 expression in MM. This study reveals for the first time a new mechanism by which ALDH1A1 enhances CD38 expression by upregulating the generation of retinoic acid (RA), thereby reducing resistance to Dara. This discovery not only provides a new perspective for understanding the dual role of ALDH1A1 in MM treatment, but also offers potential targets for overcoming drug resistance.

Aldehyde dehydrogenases (ALDHs) is a group of isozymes catalyzing detoxification of specific aldehyde substrates. The upregulated ALDH1A1 is related to chemotherapy resistance in multiple tumors [46–48]. ALDH1A1 has been reported to promote drug resistance in multiple myeloma through the NEK2 and Hedgehog signaling pathways [12, 13]. However, this study showed that the role of ALDH1A1 was reversed in treatment with Dara: its high expression upregulated CD38 expression by promoting RA synthesis, thereby enhancing NK cell-mediated ADCC. This finding suggests that the function of ALDH1A1 may be highly dependent on the therapeutic context - it appears as pro-drug resistance in chemotherapy, while enhancing sensitivity in CD38 targeted immunotherapy. This duality provides a theoretical basis for the design of individualized treatment strategies: interventions targeting ALDH1A1 need to be dynamically adjusted according to the treatment plan (chemotherapy or immunotherapy).

This study validated the mechanism by which ALDH1A1 regulates CD38 expression through the RA-RAR signaling pathway using clinical samples, cell models, and animal experimental systems. Specifically, ALDH1A1 knockdown leads to a significant decrease in CD38 transcription and protein expression, and weakens the ADCC effect mediated by Dara; Overexpression of exogenous RA or ALDH1A1 can reverse this phenomenon. This is consistent with previous studies that RA activates the CD38 promoter [18, 36] by binding to RAR/RXR receptors. It is worth noting that ALDH1A1 inhibitors (such as 673 A) can inhibit CD38 expression and weaken the efficacy of Dara both in vitro and in vivo, but the combination of RA can partially restore efficacy, further confirming the key role of this axis. In addition, transcriptome analysis showed that knocking down ALDH1A1 resulted in significant downregulation of RAR pathway genes, suggesting that ALDH1A1 may affect the CD38 regulatory network through epigenetic modifications or transcription factor interactions. Wang et al. reported that ALDH1A1 induces ZBTB7B-glycolysis and thus promotes immune escape in tumor cells [49], and as a NAD + glycohydrolase and ADPR cyclase, the enzymatic function of CD38 may be affected by glycosylation status. We found that ALDH1A1 reduces the resistance of Dara against MM via up-regulating production of RA. However, the specific mechanism by which ALDH1A1 promotes RA still needs further clarification.

The clinical sample analysis of this study showed that ALDH1A1 expression was significantly higher in RRMM patients (rRRMM) who were effectively treated with Dara than in the ineffective group (nrRRMM), and ALDH1A1 expression was positively correlated with CD38 expression. This result suggests that ALDH1A1 may serve as a biomarker for predicting the efficacy of Dara. In addition, the combination of RA or drugs that inhibit ALDH1A1 degradation (such as deubiquitinase inhibitors) may enhance the sensitivity of CD38 targeted therapy. In patients with low expression of ALDH1A1, exogenous RA may improve prognosis through a “drug sensitization” strategy; For chemotherapy resistant patients with high expression of ALDH1A1, targeted inhibition of ALDH1A1 may delay disease progression.

Although this study revealed a new mechanism of ALDH1A1 in the resistance to Dara, there are still limitations: firstly, the clinical sample size is small (n = 61), and the association between ALDH1A1 expression and long-term survival of patients has not been analyzed; Secondly, the experiment is mainly based on H929 and RPMI 8226 cell lines, and needs to be validated in more MM cell models and primary cells; Thirldy, the clinical application of RA may be limited by its metabolic instability and toxicity, and safer RAR/RXR agonists need to be developed. In addition, connection between ALDH1A1 and lenalidomide resistance is correlative as presented and that future work is warranted.

Conclusion

In summary, ALDH1A1 upregulates CD38 expression through the RA/RAR signaling pathway, thereby enhancing the efficacy of Dara. This discovery not only reveals the complex role of ALDH1A1 in MM treatment, but also provides new ideas for overcoming CD38 targeted therapy resistance. Future research needs to further optimize the regulatory strategies of the ALDH1A1-RA/RAR axis to promote the development of personalized therapies.

Limitation of the study

The limitations of this article are mainly including the relatively small (n = 61) clinical sample size, and absence the correlation between ALDH1A1 expression level and long-term survival rate of patients, which limits the universality and clinical guidance value of the research results. Secondly, research has not fully considered the heterogeneity of patient populations, such as the impact of age, gender, disease stage, or genetic background differences on ALDH1A1 function, which may introduce selection bias. Thirdly, the experiment was mainly based on H929 and RPMI 8226 cell lines, and the conclusions were not validated in more multiple myeloma (MM) cell models and primary cells, which may affect the comprehensiveness of mechanism research. In addition, in vivo experiments were conducted using immunodeficient mouse models, whose tumor microenvironment differs from that of humans, which may weaken the clinical translational significance of the results. In the future, it is necessary to expand the sample size, incorporate diverse models, and deeply analyze molecular mechanisms to optimize treatment strategies.
