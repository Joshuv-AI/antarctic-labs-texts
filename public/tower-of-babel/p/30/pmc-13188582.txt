Enterococcus thailandicus as an uncommon pathogen in severe lower extremity trauma: a case report
Authors: Nian-Jhen Wu, Chia-Jui Yang, Chun-Hung Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnterococcus thailandicus is a recently described enterococcal species with uncertain clinical significance. While Enterococcus faecium and Enterococcus faecalis are well recognized as nosocomial pathogens, clinical reports of E. thailandicus remain exceedingly rare.Case presentationWe report the case of a 46-year-old Thai male construction worker in Taiwan who sustained a severe forklift-related lower extremity injury in January 2025, including extensive degloving, tibialis anterior tendon rupture, and fibula fracture. Despite multiple surgical debridements and soft tissue reconstruction, he developed septic shock three days after admission. Tissue cultures from repeated debridements consistently yielded E. thailandicus. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed susceptibility to penicillin, vancomycin, tigecycline, and levofloxacin. The patient ultimately underwent eight debridements followed by definitive soft tissue reconstruction with latissimus dorsi free flap and skin grafting. He recovered and was discharged 54 days after the initial trauma.ConclusionsThis case highlights the possible clinical relevance of E. thailandicus in severe polymicrobial traumatic wound infections. Further studies are needed to clarify its virulence traits and antimicrobial resistance profile.

Background

Enterococcus spp. are Gram-positive, facultative anaerobic cocci that inhabit the intestinal microbiota [1, 2]. Enterococcus faecalis and Enterococcus faecium are established causes of both community and healthcare-associated infections, but the role of Enterococcus thailandicus remains poorly understood since its initial description in 2008 [3]. Previous studies focused on its probiotic or anticancer potential [4–6], and only one human case has linked it to intra-abdominal infection [7]. Here, we present a case of a 46-year-old Thai male construction worker in Taiwan who sustained a severe lower extremity injury due to a forklift accident. The patient suffered an extensive degloving injury of the left leg, tibialis anterior tendon rupture, and left fibula fracture, with an additional laceration wound on the right ankle. He underwent multiple surgical debridements and soft tissue reconstructions, with subsequent tissue cultures revealed Enterococcus thailandicus. This case highlights the possible clinical relevance of E. thailandicus in traumatic wound infections and underscores the need for further studies on its clinical relevance and antimicrobial susceptibility profile.

Case presentation

A 46-year-old Thai male construction worker in Taiwan without known medical history or family history was sent to our department where he was crushed by a rolled over forklift while working in January 2025. The patient was found under the forklift in a construction yard with his bilateral lower limb incarcerated. Extensive physical examination and plain radiograph confirmed massive degloving injury of the left leg, with a skin defect measuring approximately 30 × 20 cm, rupture of the tibialis anterior tendon, and a comminuted fracture of the left distal fibula (Gustilo–Anderson Grade IIIB). Additionally, he had a deep laceration wound over the right leg lateral aspect (Fig. 1).

He was hemodynamically stable upon arrival, and underwent urgent debridement, tibialis anterior tendon repair and application of negative pressure wound therapy. Prophylactic antibiotic therapy with intravenous ampicillin-sulbactam 3,000 mg every 6 h was established. However, three days after admission, the patient developed fever and chills, with a peak body temperature of 40.4 °C, tachypnea with a respiratory rate of 39 breaths per minute, tachycardia with a heart rate of 119 beats per minute, and hypotension with a blood pressure of 84/51 mmHg. Laboratory findings revealed leukocytosis (WBC, 15.77 × 10³/mm³), elevated C-reactive protein (CRP, 28.380 mg/dL), and metabolic acidosis with hyperlactatemia (lactate 5.02 mmol/L). These findings necessitated fluid resuscitation, initiation of inotropic support, escalation of antibiotic therapy to piperacillin–tazobactam 4,500 mg every 6 h, and emergent fasciotomy with surgical debridement. Intraoperatively, a substantial accumulation of purulent material with foul odor was observed within the wound. The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit after the surgery.

Blood cultures and tissue samples collected from the debridement were inoculated using the BD Kiestra™ Work Cell Automation system (BD Life Sciences—Integrated Diagnostic Solutions, Sparks, MD, USA) and incubated with imaging on the BD Kiestra™ ReadA system (BD Life Sciences—Integrated Diagnostic Solutions, Sparks, MD, USA). Isolated colonies were identified by the Bruker Microflex LT system (Bruker Diagnostics, Germany). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted with the bioMérieux VITEK®2 system (bioMérieux, France). Blood cultures were negative for bacterial growth, and tissue culture collected from the debridement revealed heavy growth for Enterococcus thailandicus, as well as Aeromonas hydrophila and Enterobacter cloacae. Antibiotic susceptibility testing indicated that the isolated E. thailandicus was susceptible to penicillin, vancomycin, tigecycline and levofloxacin (Table 1).

Antibiotic treatment was then switched to intravenous cefepime 2,000 mg every 8 h and teicoplanin 600 mg every day. Detailed description of the total duration of antibiotic therapy is presented in Table 2. After adjustment of the antibiotic regimen, the fever curve gradually flattened, and no further signs of sepsis were observed during hospitalization. The patient underwent a total of 8 surgical debridements with negative pressure wound treatment, and tissue samples from the first 3 debridements were all positive for Enterococcus thailandicus. He received definite reconstruction surgery for his soft tissue defect, including free latissimus dorsi muscle flap with split-thickness skin graft 37 days after the initial trauma after stabilizing his infection status. The patient was discharged 54 days after the initial trauma. The patient received intravenous antibiotics for a total of 54 days, followed by oral therapy for 14 days. He was followed up in our outpatient clinic for six months before returning to Thailand. During this follow-up period, no specific adverse events were observed. At the last outpatient follow-up, the patient achieved full weight-bearing and was able to ambulate without assistance. Discussion and conclusion

We report a case of uncommon infection due to E. thailandicus and emphasize its possible pathogenicity, especially in individuals with traumatic soft tissue injury.

First isolated from fermented sausage in Thailand on 2008 [3], the role of E. thailandicus in clinical disease remains poorly understood. Few studies on E. thailandicus exist, with previous study mainly focused on its probiotic activity [4, 5] and in vitro anticancer activity [6, 8]. On the other hand, very few studies focused on its role as a human pathogen. Current study reported its prevalence in animal intestinal villi and crypts [9, 10], animal feces [11], food [6] and sewage [12]. In addition, it has also been detected in human fecal samples [13].

Though the role of Enterococcus spp. in traumatic wound infection was extensively studied [14, 15], to date, there is only one report regarding E. thailandicus as a potential pathogen in human, which was identified in peritoneal drain fluid culture in an immunosuppressed patient with intra-abdominal infection [7]. In our patient, given the highly contaminated construction-site injury with extensive soft tissue exposure, environmental inoculation from soil, debris, or organic material appears to be a plausible route of acquisition. Nevertheless, endogenous colonization cannot be entirely excluded. In our case, its heavy growth in 3 separate tissue samples isolation from a severely contaminated traumatic wound suggests its potential pathogenicity. However, although E. thailandicus was repeatedly isolated, the septic shock in this patient was more likely multifactorial and may have been primarily driven by other organisms such as Aeromonas hydrophila and Enterobacter cloacae. Therefore, the precise pathogenic contribution of E. thailandicus remains uncertain.

In our case, intravenous ampicillin–sulbactam was initiated according to institutional protocols for open fractures. However, for highly contaminated grade IIIB open fracture, broader initial coverage might be considered [16]. Antibiotic therapy was escalated promptly once septic shock developed. Whether earlier broader-spectrum therapy could have modified the infectious course remains uncertain.

In summary, this observation underscores the need for further investigation into the clinical relevance and pathogenic potential of E. thailandicus, particularly in trauma-associated wound infections. As novel enterococcal species continue to emerge, clinicians should be vigilant in identifying and characterizing their clinical impact, particularly in high-risk infections.

This case report has several limitations. First, as a single-patient observation, the findings cannot be generalized to broader clinical settings. Second, given the highly contaminated nature of the traumatic wound and the isolation of multiple organisms, it is not possible to definitively establish Enterococcus thailandicus as the sole causative pathogen of septic shock in this patient. Repeated isolation suggests persistence; however, colonization cannot be excluded in the context of polymicrobial contamination. Additionally, the initial empiric antimicrobial regimen may not have fully covered all environmental pathogens typically encountered in severe open fractures, which may have influenced the early infectious course. Further studies are warranted to determine the antibiotic susceptibility patterns and virulence factors of E. thailandicus.
