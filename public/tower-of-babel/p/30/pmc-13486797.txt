Ascending, arch, and cerebrovascular disease: Best of 2025 in the Journal of Vascular Surgery: Cases, Innovations, and Techniques Volume 11
Authors: Nolan C. Cirillo-Penn
Published: 2026

It is my honor to summarize selected articles on ascending aortic, aortic arch, and cerebrovascular disease published in the Journal of Vascular Surgery: Cases, Innovations, and Techniques during 2025. Certainly, the task of selecting the best articles in such a wide breath of pathology became daunting with the number of high-quality submissions in each of these domains. This collection of manuscripts represents clinical highlights from the year, in my opinion. These articles also point to clinical gaps in our current practice, and opportunities for future innovation and ongoing exploration in our rapidly evolving field.

Best of 2025 (in no particular order!)

The ascending aorta represents a harsh environment with a dearth of commercially-available endovascular options in the United States. Baghbani-Oskouei et al1 present an excellent case to correct ascending aorta-stent graft length mismatch in thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) of type A aortic dissection (TAAD). This article highlights the clinical challenges many of us frequently face when our cardiac surgery colleagues have patients who are considered prohibitive or very high-risk for open repair of TAAD. The authors highlight anatomic limitations in the ascending aorta to achieve adequate seal of the entry tear, as well as procedural sizing for endovascular ascending aortic repair where greater curve distance from sinotubular junction (STJ) to innominate artery is a key determination for stent graft feasibility. From bilateral percutaneous femoral and right radial access, Baghbani-Oskouei et al1 demonstrate correction of a 100 mm length Gore conformable TAG thoracic stent graft (W.L. Gore & Associates, Inc) deployed intentionally over the innominate origin by withdrawal of an angioplasty balloon from zone 0 to the innominate to allow flaring of the aortic stent and innominate artery stenting both in the manuscript and well-illustrated video. Although this may not be applicable in every case, it certainly provides an option for patients who may otherwise lack conventional, commercially-available options, whereas purpose-build devices work through the regulatory pathways.

In similar fashion, Ko et al2 report two cases of single-branched TEVAR with the Gore TAG Thoracic Branch Endoprosthesis (TBE, W.L. Gore & Associates, Inc) to treat acute aortic syndromes of the arch and distal ascending aorta. Both cases highlight the feasibility and utility of hybrid surgery in urgent proximal aortic pathologies with involvement of the proximal arch. These patients were deemed unfit for open repair and failed initial attempts are medical management, given the isolated nature of the lesions which were a sufficient length from the STJ in elongated ascending aorta. The authors also use temporary balloon occlusion of the inferior vena cava to decrease preload and decrease systolic impulse for accurate graft deployment rather than rapid ventricular pacing.2 Cerebral debranching strategy also remains a critical decision-making component of urgent repair of the aortic arch with single-branched devices. This balances expeditious procedural times and perfusion based on specific-patient anatomy. The construction of the TBE’s retrograde portal in relation to the main aortic graft allows for treatment of shorter lengths of ascending aorta than commercially available stent grafts.

Schwartz et al3 report an alternate strategy in zone 0 TEVAR in the setting of isolated lesions using the TBE proximal aortic extender. As mentioned several times in this editorial, length from STJ to innominate artery remains a limiting factor in endovascular repair of the ascending aorta. The authors describe the limited number of options for short, large diameter grafts. They use the aortic extender to obtain seal in two cases of pseudoaneurysm, both from previous open repair sites. Rapid ventricular pacing was used for blood pressure control during deployment. Of note, the short cover length allowed coverage of the coronary arteries or innominate, however, did require three cuffs in one case.3 Given the limitations of the current devices, treating surgeons must be facile with multiple different options and bail out maneuvers in this high-risk patient population. Special care must be taken, particularly when landing in the mid-ascending aorta due to the risk of retrograde TAAD.

Ujihira et al4 present bail out of this devastating complication after endovascular arch repair with a physician modified stent graft. Endovascular arch repair was performed with a modified Relay Plus stent graft (Terumo Aortic) with a single antegrade branch for the innominate using an Endurant limb (Medtronic) after cervical debranching. The landing zone was in the mid-ascending aorta with no intimal defects on completion imaging. The authors note retrograde TAAD with protrusion of the bare metal stents into the false lumen on postoperative day 24. Again, in the setting of limited commercially-available options, repair was performed with a sandwich technique. A shortened Zenith Dissection Endovascular Graft (Cook Medical) was deployed proximally at the STJ while avoiding coverage of the antegrade innominate branch. Subsequently the branch was extended into the proximal stent graft and sandwiched with a Relay Pro NBS stent graft (Terumo Aortic). The gutter leaks ultimately resolved.4 This case transparently highlights the risks of proximal endovascular aortic repair extending to native ascending aorta, with retrograde TAAD being a key risk. The relative hostility of the mid-ascending aorta compared with the sinotubular junction as a proximal landing zone is an important point in zone 0 TEVAR, as well as the risks of bare metal stents in this location. This will require close monitoring despite resolution of gutter leaks radiographically, but the author’s solution was effective for emergent repair in the absence of purpose-built ascending and arch devices.

Shifting gears away from the high medical risk patients, Soler et al5 report an excellent three-staged repair of challenging multifocal aneurysmal disease in a patient with a variant of unknown significance in MYH11. The team shows excellent utilization of supraclavicular extension of sternotomy to repair a large ascending, arch, and right subclavian aneurysm with total arch replacement, bypass to the subclavian, and vertebral transposition. They deferred classic and frozen elephant trunks due to the caliber of the proximal descending thoracic aorta and performed open thoracoabdominal repair with visceral reconstruction and a c-loop intercostal patch. The third stage repair of the infrarenal aorta and iliac aneurysms was performed preserving the native, normal caliber tissue around the inferior mesenteric artery, as well as another segment in the thoracic aorta during the previous stages.5 Soler et al5 describe a thoughtful approach to protection of the spinal cord and collateral circulation, as well as intentional creation of surgical graft landing zones that would allow for future endovascular repair of native segments of aorta should degeneration be noted in future surveillance. The use of the cannulation arm of the four-branch arch graft for subclavian aneurysm repair is a useful technique in arch repair. The authors also highlight the role of surgeons in genetic evaluation of patient with suspected heritable aortopathies.

MacArthur et al6 present a beautiful clinical video demonstrating technical aspects of vertebral transposition to facilitate treatment of a saccular distal arch aneurysm with TBE with a dominant left vertebral artery arising from the aortic arch at the origin of the left subclavian artery. The authors illustrate exposure of the vertebral artery, placement of the anastomosis to the left carotid artery on the posterolateral wall to minimize kink and tortuosity of the transposed artery, and use of a coronary punch in creation of the arteriotomy. MacArthur et al6 postulate increase in applicability of TBE with adoption of vertebral reconstruction in the setting of aberrant anatomy. This case succinctly demonstrates surgical technique for one of the less common vascular surgical operations with many technical points. The placement of clamps with subsequent rotation of the artery for a posterolateral anastomosis is well demonstrated and shows a clear “How I do it” that can easily be replicated. More data will certainly be needed on the outcomes of vertebral reconstructions in the setting of endovascular aortic repair but this is an excellent option for expanding patient applicability.

There is no shortage of rare cases in cardiac and vascular surgery, as evidenced by Shimoji et al7 presentation of intimal sarcoma of the aorta resulting in acute hoarseness due to pseudoaneurysmal degeneration of the distal arch with compression of the recurrent laryngeal nerve. Repair of the pseudoaneurysm was performed through total arch replacement under hypothermic circulatory arrest. This required ligation of the proximal subclavian artery with bypass to the left axillary artery. The patient recovered well from this, however, pathologic examination of the aortic wall demonstrated changes consistent with a primary, undifferentiated sarcoma of the aorta. The patient was noted to have metastasis to the femur and rib. He subsequently survived rupture of a cerebral aneurysm, ultimately surviving over a year beyond arch replacement.7 This case highlights unusual pathology with an atypical presentation and reaffirms the need for a high index of suspicion when treating patients who are off the beaten path. As the world experience in oncovascular surgery continues to grow, vascular involvement in tumor management and resection margins will become a critical skill.

Across the world, multivessel endoarch repair is gaining traction in treatment of complex aortic pathologies. The article by Takagi et al8 demonstrates a role of a patient-specific custom arch graft with three fenestrations for a bicarotid trunk, left subclavian artery, and aberrant right subclavian artery in the setting of type B dissection with postdissection aneurysm using the Najuta fenestrated stent graft (Kawasumi Laboratories Inc). The authors used a three-dimensional printed plaster model for preoperative planning and assurance that the planned device would be appropriate in the patient’s aberrant arch anatomy. The patient tolerated the procedure well and dismissed on the third postoperative day. Six-month follow-up demonstrated false lumen thrombosis to the level of the superior mesenteric artery with no evidence of retrograde TAAD or supra-aortic trunk occlusion.8 The case highlights innovation in treatment happening across the world over the last decade, as well as the utility of three-dimensional printing in preoperative planning. I have personally also found this useful in select cases, particularly for the arch.

Observations by Saouti et al9 demonstrate the utility of two stage hybrid repair of type B aortic dissection with involvement of the ascending aorta. The authors highlight lack of adequate landing zone for endovascular repair with coverage of the entry tear, as well as utility of protection from complication related to ascending aortic involvement in dissection, particularly at the aortic root. Ascending aortic and zone 2 aortic arch replacement was performed with special attention to create a long, >3 cm landing zone for future endovascular repairs. In early follow-up, the patient had expansion of the aortic diameter and was planned for TEVAR with carotid-subclavian bypass and ligation of the vertebral artery arising from the arch. Adequate circle of Willis was identified preoperatively. The patient underwent TEVAR with knickerbocker disruption of the distal septum to allow for seal of the false lumen entry flow. At the 3-month follow-up, the patient had complete remodeling of the descending thoracic aorta in the stented segment.9 This approach represents an alternative approach to total arch replacement with frozen elephant trunk, particularly in those with large entry tears in the distal thoracic aorta. This approach protects the ascending aorta, while minimizing thoracic coverage and risk of SCI in the early recovery phase from emergent arch replacement when there are more substantial hemodynamic fluctuations than during long-segment TEVAR. The emphasis on creation of the distal anastomosis with sufficient length of Dacron for TEVAR is a key point that allows for endovascular repair of distal disease with TEVAR and hybrid debranching, or single-branched TEVAR later down the line, while expediting the index operation.

Although the aortic section of this editorial highlights many aspects of supra-aortic trunk and cerebrovascular management during aortic operations: the isolated cerebrovascular disease section highlights a myriad of rare cases. Amatya and Mukherjee10 present an incredible imaging case of a patient with real-time ophthalmic imaging during Amaurosis fugax. The patient was an ophthalmologist with access to his equipment at the time of his event and captured multiple images of emboli within the ophthalmic artery. This series of images captures the embolus in transit from the central retina with ischemia of the optic nerve to the periphery. Ultrasound guidance and computed tomography angiography confirmed high-grade stenosis. The patient underwent awake carotid endarterectomy (CEA) without recurrence of symptoms over the next 2 years.10 This cases shows rarely captured imaging of a relatively frequent pathology in vascular surgery.

Nelson et al11 present a difficult case of CEA for highly calcified carotid stenosis in the setting of Eagle syndrome. The authors highlight anatomic planning and intraoperative relationships of the neurovascular structures, musculature, and elongated styloid process during multidisciplinary styloidectomy and CEA. The patient had no neurologic sequalae from the case and long-term resolution of his symptomatic facial pain.11 The high-quality operative images and this case demonstrate anatomy that may be encountered, as well as, the role of collaboration with other specialties in the care of complex vascular patients.

The report from Wong et al12 highlights the important phenomenon of plaque protrusion during carotid stenting. Given the rarity of this entity, scattered reports of identification and treatment exist. The authors present a 75-year-old man with symptomatic high-grade carotid stenosis who was treated with transcervical carotid artery revascularization. Immediate completion angiogram demonstrated a mobile, protruding plaque. A second stent was placed in with high amounts of debris captured in the filter. The patient did well, with no neurologic complications.12 This case brings awareness to a rare and often feared issue in carotid stenting which can lead to severe neurologic complications. As carotid stenting continues to gain traction, identifying plaque protrusion and techniques for treatment will become more important. As newer stents become more available, attention to neuroprotection and particle size will remain a consideration.

Rare aberrant anatomy can present in any aspect of vascular surgery. Although CEA remains a surgeon favorite based on the classic anatomy, Leonard et al13 present an extremely rare case of persistent hypoglossal artery (PHA) in the setting of high-grade stenosis in the common internal carotid artery/PHA trunk. The authors demonstrate shunting of the PHA given its dominant role in the posterior circulation. Intraoperative angiogram was also used to assure intracranial patency and absence of complication when encountering atypical anatomy.13 This observation highlights thorough preoperative evaluation of anatomy and a high index of suspicion even in “bread and butter” operations.

Conclusions

These selections from Volume 11 of the Journal of Vascular Surgery: Cases, Innovations, and Techniques highlight the innovation by our colleagues in vascular surgery, and variations on classic anatomy and technique that make the specialty invigorating. Each paper highlights important considerations in preoperative evaluation, technical conduct, or current challenges yet to be overcome in 2026. Although many other fantastic articles could easily have been included, I hope readers enjoy these as much as I did while selecting them.
