Depressive symptom profiles across pregnant, postpartum, and nonperipartum women: measurement invariance and differential item functioning analyses of 2005–2021 NHANES data
Authors: Manit Srisurapanont, Chawisa Suradom, Narong Maneeton, Awiruth Oon-arom, Benchalak Maneeton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe DSM-5-TR classifies peripartum depression as symptomatically indistinguishable from major depressive disorder (MDD). However, evidence suggests that depressive symptom profiles may differ among pregnant, postpartum, and nonperipartum (NPP) women. This study used 2005–2021 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data to evaluate Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) depressive symptoms and assessed measurement equivalence across these groups.MethodsMeasurement invariance (MI) and differential item functioning (DIF) analyses included 632 pregnant, 385 postpartum, and 6,503 NPP women (aged 15–49 years). After confirming the PHQ-9 factor structure, we evaluated MI using multigroup confirmatory factor analysis (MGCFA) and assessed DIF using ordinal logistic regression and item response theory. In MGCFA, model changes supported invariance (measurement equivalence between groups) if ΔCFI ≥ -0.010 together with ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015 and ΔSRMR ≤ 0.030 for metric invariance and ΔCFI ≥ -0.010 together with ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015 and ΔSRMR ≤ 0.015 for scalar and strict invariance. DIF, defined as differential item responses between groups, was statistically significant at p < 0.01 (likelihood-ratio χ2 test) and clinically significant if the pseudo-R2 change was ≥ 0.035.ResultsA two-factor PHQ-9 model was used. The cognitive-affective factor included anhedonia, depressed mood, guilt/worthlessness, concentration difficulty, psychomotor changes, and suicidal ideation. The somatic factor included sleep disturbance, fatigue, and appetite changes. MGCFA supported metric (ΔCFI = -0.0005, ΔRMSEA = -0.0001, ΔSRMR = 0.0027), scalar (ΔCFI = 0.0004 and ΔSRMR = -0.0024), and strict invariance (ΔCFI and ΔSRMR < 0.0001). Statistically significant DIF emerged between pregnant and NPP women (guilt/worthlessness, psychomotor changes, fatigue, and appetite changes), postpartum and NPP women (sleep disturbance and fatigue), and pregnant and postpartum women (sleep disturbance). However, all statistically significant DIF effects were modest and of minimal clinical relevance (total DIF pseudo-R2 difference ≤ 0.012).ConclusionThe PHQ-9 assesses depressive symptoms equivalently in pregnant, postpartum, and NPP women, allowing meaningful comparisons across groups. At equivalent depression severity, differences in PHQ-9 item responses across these groups are modest and of minimal clinical relevance. The absence of symptom bias does not support a distinction between peripartum depression and DSM-5-TR MDD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28575-8.

Introduction

Peripartum depression is a major health concern affecting approximately 11.9% of peripartum women [1]. This condition is associated with adverse outcomes, including preterm birth, low birth weight, and an increased maternal suicide risk [2]. Beyond causing functional impairment, peripartum depression disrupts mother-infant bonding and may impair long-term childhood cognitive, psychosocial, and motor development [3].

Peripartum depression shares core features with major depressive disorder (MDD). The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR) requires full MDD criteria for diagnosis: persistent depressive symptoms and significant impairment for at least two weeks [4]. Consequently, the “peripartum onset” specifier is defined by timing, not by distinct symptom patterns. Antenatal depression (AND) and postpartum depression (PPD) fall under this single specifier, reflecting limited evidence for a clinical distinction between these stages. Accordingly, the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), developed for the general population, is recommended without modification to screen for peripartum depression [5].

Recent evidence challenges the DSM-5-TR classification of peripartum depression. First, 2007–2018 NHANES data showed different prevalence rates of probable depression (PHQ-9 ≥ 10): 11.1% in the general population, 6.5% in pregnant women, and 6.4% in postpartum women [6]. Second, postpartum women report less sadness and anhedonia than nonperipartum (NPP) women [7]. Third, somatic symptoms—including sleep disturbance, fatigue, and appetite changes—occur more frequently in pregnant women than in NPP women [8, 9]. Because these somatic features may increase false-positive results, using the PHQ-9 in peripartum populations remains debated [10, 11]. Fourth, a review identified anxiety, obsessive thoughts, psychomotor changes, and impaired concentration as features that may distinguish peripartum depression from standard MDD [12]. This review also noted clinical discrepancies between AND and PPD, supporting separate classifications for pregnancy-onset and postpartum-onset MDD. Cumulatively, these findings raise questions about PHQ-9 measurement equivalence across these groups and suggest that depressive symptom profiles differ across pregnant, postpartum, and NPP women.

Measurement invariance (MI) evaluates whether an instrument assesses the same underlying construct across groups [13], a prerequisite for ensuring valid cross-group score comparisons. One MI study found that the PHQ-9 performed adequately only during pregnancy, demonstrating inconsistent factor loadings and poor model fit in the postpartum group [14]. Conversely, longtitudinal MI analyses showed that PHQ-9 scores were comparable across pregnancy trimesters [15], and the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) scores were invariant across antenatal and postpartum periods [16, 17].

Differential item functioning (DIF) analysis evaluates “item bias” between psychological groups [18]. Unlike direct group comparisons, DIF analysis tests whether individuals with the same level of a latent trait—such as depression severity—respond differently to specific items based on group membership. Previous studies identified item bias in DSM-IV major depressive episode (MDE) criteria between patients with MDD and bipolar disorder [19, 20] and between those with and without multiple sclerosis [21].

PHQ-9 measurement noninvariance and item bias remain poorly understood in pregnant, postpartum, and NPP women. This gap limits the clinical utility of this instrument for peripartum depression screening, depressive disorder classification, and specifier applications. Therefore, this secondary analysis evaluated the MI and DIF of PHQ-9 items—which correspond to DSM-5-TR depressive symptoms—across these three groups. Because current evidence suggests that depression may manifest differently across these populations, we anticipated potential measurement variation or item bias in the PHQ-9 scores.

Methods

Study design and setting

This analysis used data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), which assesses the health and nutritional status of a representative, noninstitutionalized U.S. population. The NHANES survey was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Research Ethics Review Board. Because the data are deidentified and publicly available, this secondary analysis was exempt from institutional review board (IRB) approval. This article complies with the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Participants

We categorized NHANES respondents as pregnant, postpartum, or NPP. To ensure age comparability, the sample was restricted to women aged 15–49 years to align with the World Health Organization definition of reproductive age [22].

The postpartum period was defined as 12 months after the last live birth [23], rather than the 4-week DSM-5-TR specifier. This broader timeframe accounts for evidence that depressive symptoms often remain elevated throughout the first postpartum year [24].

Variables, data sources, and data processing

We accessed and imported NHANES data using the nhanesdata R package [25], extracting variables from the Demographic Variables (DEMO), Reproductive Health (RHD), and Mental Health – Depression Screener (DPQ). The DPQ module contains the PHQ-9, a validated 9-item depression screening instrument [26]. Respondent sequence numbers (SEQN) served as unique identifiers to link records across modules. Figure 1 shows the filtering and aggregation of 2005–2021 NHANES data to derive the final analytical sample of women with complete PHQ-9 data. We used the DEMO module (n = 113,249) to identify pregnant and NPP participants. Excluding 94,257 individuals with missing or unreported pregnancy status (RIDEXPRG) left a primary sample for classification. Participants were categorized as pregnant based on a positive laboratory test or self-reported pregnancy (n = 1,763). From the remaining 17,229 participants, we excluded 454 individuals whose 12-month postpartum period was identified via the RHD module, yielding a final sample of 16,775 nonpregnant-NPP women.

Postpartum status was determined using variables RHQ197 (“How many months ago did you have your baby?”) and RHD143 (“Are you pregnant now?”) from the RHD module (n = 39,362). We excluded participants with missing birth interval data (n = 38,509) or those exceeding 12 months postpartum (n = 342). Individuals currently pregnant, identified via RHD143 (n = 20) and the DEMO variable RIDEXPRG (n = 12), were also excluded. This filtering resulted in 479 nonpregant postpartum respondents.

Of the 41,098 DPQ module participants, we excluded 4,306 with incomplete PHQ-9 data and 212 who refused or answered “don’t know”, leaving 36,580 respondents with complete scores. We then linked these complete PHQ-9 records with the three previously defined cohorts, restricting the sample to women aged 15–49 years identified via the DEMO module.

Measurement

The PHQ-9 is a self-administered instrument based on DSM-IV criteria for MDE [26]. Nine items assess depressive symptom frequency over the preceding two weeks on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), yielding total scores from 0 to 27. We used a PHQ-9 total score ≥ 10 to define moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms, or probable depression. At this cutoff, the instrument exhibits 84% sensitivity and 81% specificity for peripartum depression [27].

Study size and bias

The final analytic sample comprised 606 pregnant, 385 postpartum, and 6,091 NPP women. We combined all three cohorts for MGCFA but paired the data into three comparative datasets for DIF analysis: pregnant vs. NPP (n = 6,697), postpartum vs. NPP (n = 6,476), and pregnant vs. postpartum (n = 991). Because a standard sample size threshold to detect MI is lacking [28], we applied no minimum threshold for MI analysis. However, for a DIF study evaluating a short scale, a sample size exceeding 300 per group is sufficient to maintain Type I error rates below 5% and statistical power above 80%. Our sample size was therefore adequate for DIF analysis.

Excluding women without complete PHQ-9 data introduced selection, information, and complete-case biases. Consequently, the estimated prevalence of probable depression does not represent the broader US population of pregnant, postpartum, and NPP women.

Descriptive analysis

We summarized and compared demographic characteristics and PHQ-9 item scores across the three cohorts. Categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson χ2 test, and continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Internal consistency and factor structure assessment

We pooled PHQ-9 data from all three cohorts to evaluate internal consistency and factorability. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha (α) and McDonald’s hierarchical omega (ωh), with values between 0.70 and 0.90 indicating high consistency [29]. Bartlett’s test of sphericity confirmed significant item intercorrelation (χ2 P < 0.05). Factorability was established if the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) Measure of Sampling Adequacy (MSA) exceeded 0.80 [30].

Factor structure was assessed using established goodness-of-fit criteria. An acceptable fit required a Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) ≥ 0.90 and a Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) ≤ 0.08 [31]. We first evaluated a unidimensional model using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). If the fit was inadequate, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was applied using minimal residual extraction with promax rotation to specify a two-factor solution. Factor loadings ≥ 0.30 guided factor designation. If the two-factor model was inadequate, we iteratively increased the number of factors until achieving a satisfactory structure.

Measurement invariance analysis

We evaluated MI across the pregnant, postpartum, and NPP cohorts simultaneously using multigroup confirmatory factor analysis (MGCFA) within a structural equation modeling framework. We tested a hierarchical sequence of increasingly restrictive models: configural, metric, scalar, and strict invariance [32]. Because the items were ordinal, they were specified as ordered categorical indicators and estimated using the weighted least squares mean and variance adjusted (WLSMV) estimator. For ordinal data, invariance testing requires sequential equality constraints on factor loadings, item thresholds, and residual variances.

The MI evaluation followed four sequential steps [13]. First, the configural model assessed whether the same factor structure was present across groups (equivalence of model form). Second, the metric model constrained factor loadings to equality across groups (equivalence of factor loadings). Third, the scalar model additionally item thresholds to equality (equivalence of item thresholds), which allows for meaningful comparisons of latent means. Fourth, the strict model constrained residual variances to equality across groups (equivalence of item residuals).

Model fit was evaluated using the CFI, RMSEA, and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). Invariance was supported if changes in fit between nested models met Chen’s criteria [33]: ΔCFI ≥ -0.010, ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015, and ΔSRMR ≤ 0.030 for metric invariance; and ΔCFI ≥ -0.010, ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015, and ΔSRMR ≤ 0.015 for scalar and strict invariance.

Differential item functioning analysis

We conducted DIF analysis to determine whether peripartum status influenced specific PHQ-9 item responses. In the first two comparisons, the pregnant and postpartum cohorts served as focal groups (coded 1), and the NPP cohort served as the reference group (coded 0). The NPP group was selected as the reference because clinical depression models derive primarily from the NPP population. In the third comparison, the pregnant cohort was the focal group (coded 1), and the postpartum cohort was the reference group (coded 0), reflecting the more established literature on postpartum depression compared to antenatal depression.

We used a hybrid ordinal logistic regression (OLR) and item response theory (IRT) framework for DIF analysis [34]. A graded response model (GRM) iteratively estimated the latent trait representing depression or its syndrome (factor) severity [35]. Three nested OLR models were compared for each item: Model 1 (latent trait); Model 2 (latent trait and peripartum status); and Model 3 (latent trait, peripartum status, and their interaction).

We determined statistically significant DIF (item bias) using likelihood-ratio (LR) χ2 tests at α < 0.01. DIF magnitude was evaluated using McFadden’s pseudo-R² difference for total DIF (Model 1 vs. Model 3), calculated as the sum of R² differences between Model 1 vs. Model 2 and Model 2 vs. Model 3. Total DIF R² differences were categorized as modest (< 0.035), moderate (0.035–0.070), or large (> 0.070) [36]. A total DIF R² difference ≥ 0.035 was considered clinically significant (symptom bias), indicating a practical difference [37, 38]. Uniform and nonuniform DIF were identified by the absence or presence of intersections between item true score curves along the latent trait continuum. To ensure model convergence and stability, each response category required at least five observations.

Statistical software

Descriptive analyses were performed using jamovi (version 2.6) [39]. All data processing and other statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5). Specifically, we used the psych R package for internal consistency and factor structure assessments [40], the lavaan R package (version 0.6) for MI analysis [41], and the lofdif R package (version 0.4) for DIF analysis [42].

Results

Participants, descriptive, and outcome data

Table 1 summarizes the demographic and clinical characteristics of the 7,520 participants, comprising 632 pregnant, 385 postpartum, and 6,503 NPP women. The mean ages of the pregnant (27.53 years) and postpartum (28.47 years) groups were significantly lower than that of the NPP group (32.58 years; P < 0.001). Marital status differed significantly between both peripartum groups and the NPP group (P < 0.001), with NPP participants more likely to be divorced (7.7%) or never married (34.2%). Ethnicity distribution varied significantly across all comparisons; Mexican participants comprised a higher proportion of the pregnant group (25.8%) than the NPP (16.6%) or postpartum (21.6%) groups.

Probable depression (PHQ-9 total score ≥ 10) was significantly less prevalent in the pregnant (8.2%) and postpartum (7.5%) groups than in the NPP group (12.2%; P = 0.003 and 0.006, respectively). Although both peripartum groups had lower mean total scores than the NPP group, only the postpartum mean (3.3) was significantly lower than the NPP mean (3.9; P = 0.012). Both pregnant and postpartum groups reported significantly lower severity for anhedonia, guilt/worthlessness, and concentration difficulty than the NPP group (P ≤ 0.030). Conversely, the pregnant group reported significantly higher severity for sleep disturbance and fatigue than both the postpartum and NPP groups (P ≤ 0.013). Additionally, the postpartum group reported significantly lower sleep disturbance severity than the NPP group (P = 0.010).

Internal consistency and factor structure

Pooled PHQ-9 data showed good internal consistency (α = 0.86; ωh = 0.78). Bartlett’s test of sphericity confirmed significant item intercorrelation (χ2 = 23,619.53, df = 36, P < 0.001). The KMO values confirmed data suitability for factor analysis, with individual MSA scores ranging from 0.88 to 0.93 and an overall MSA of 0.91.

A unidimensional model yielded an inadequate fit (CFI = 0.93, TLI = 0.91, RMSEA = 0.09). An EFA then evaluated a two-factor structure (see Table 2). Factor 1 (cognitive-affective) comprised six items (factor loadings): anhedonia (0.46), depressed mood (0.69), guilt/worthlessness (0.74), concentration difficulty (0.43), psychomotor changes (0.52), and suicidal ideation (0.61). Factor 2 (somatic) included sleep disturbance (0.71), fatigue (0.88), and appetite changes (0.45). The interfactor correlation (r) was 0.74. This two-factor model showed an acceptable fit (CFI = 0.98, TLI = 0.96, RMSE = 0.06). Factor 1 mean score of the NPP group (mean = 1.79, SD = 2.81) was significantly higher than those of the pregnant (mean = 1.31, SD = 2.31) and the postpartum (mean = 1.31, SD = 2.33) groups (P ≤ 0.001) (see Table 1). Factor 2 mean score of the pregnant group (mean = 2.39, SD = 2.16) was significantly lower than those of NPP (mean = 2.11, SD = 2.22) and postpartum (mean = 1.98, SD = 2.06) groups (P ≤ 0.004). Subsequent MI and DIF analyses of PHQ-9 responses were based on this two-factor model.

Differential item functioning results

Table 4 presents DIF parameters for the nine PHQ-9 items across three group comparisons. Based on the two-factor structure, seven items exhibited statistically significant DIF across the six pairwise tests (LR χ2, P < 0.01; see Supplementary Figs. 1–7). In the pregnant-versus-NPP comparison, four items showed statistically significant DIF: guilt/worthlessness (nonuniform), psychomotor changes (nonuniform), fatigue (uniform), and appetite changes (uniform). In the postpartum-versus-NPP comparison, two items showed statistically significant DIF: sleep disturbance (nonuniform) and fatigue (uniform). In the pregnant-versus-postpartum comparison, only sleep disturbance showed statistically significant DIF (uniform). Notably, all DIF magnitudes (total DIF R2 difference) were ≤ 0.012, remaining well below the threshold for moderate DIF (total DIF R2 difference ≥ 0.035).

Discussion

Main findings

Based on the two-factor PHQ-9 model, the MI analysis of 2005–2021 NHANES data from 7,520 pregnant, postpartum, and NPP women supported full invariance across all tested levels. These findings indicate that the PHQ-9 assessed depressive symptoms equivalently across these groups. Although DIF analysis identified several items with statistically significant DIF (item bias) across peripartum groups, all DIF magnitudes remained below the threshold for clinical significance. Therefore, these modest item biases do not translate into a clinically meaningful symptom bias across groups.

Internal consistency and factor structure

The PHQ-9 showed good internal consistency (α = 0.86; ωh = 0.78), consistent with recent meta-analyses across diverse populations and cultural contexts (α = 0.85–0.86) [43, 44]. Using 2007–2018 NHANES data, Patel et al. reported a two-factor model with good fit (CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.99, RMSEA = 0.03), which exceeded the present fit (CFI = 0.98, TLI = 0.96, RMSEA = 0.06) [45]. Despite this discrepancy in fit magnitude, both studies identified the identical two-factor structure and item composition. This specific two-factor structure has also been validated in clinical populations [14]. However, previous research has identified one- or two-factor models depending on the statistical methods used [46].

Measurement invariance

The PHQ-9 demonstrated configural, metric, and scalar invariance across peripartum stages. These findings suggested that the questionnaire maintains a consistent latent structure across groups. Furthermore, the items function similarly relative to the underlying construct, allowing for meaningful score comparisons across stages.

The MI of the PHQ-9 observed in this study aligns with previous research. Prior studies demonstrated the MI of the PHQ-9 across sociodemographic and medical-condition groups [47], among working-age and older adults seeking treatment for common mental disorders [48], between people with and without diabetes [49], and across ethnic groups [50].

The present results contrast with previous findings indicating that the PHQ-9 showed adequate performance only in pregnant women, alongside inconsistent factor loadings and poor model fit in postpartum women [14]. That study reported a scalar invariance violation between pregnant and postpartum women, suggesting that depressive symptom scores cannot be meaningfully compared across those groups. Although both studies used MGCFA to evaluate the PHQ-9 two-factor model, Stafana et al. (2023) examined only pregnant versus postpartum groups, whereas the present study simultaneously compared pregnant, postpartum, and NPP groups [14]. Additionally, the previous study evaluated the Italian version of PHQ-9, which may introduce cultural or language nuances. Finally, that study utilized data from healthcare settings and restricted the postpartum group to within 6 months after childbirth.

Differential item functioning

The NPP group exhibited greater overall depression severity than the postpartum group, as measured by total PHQ-9 scores. This difference likely explains the higher severity of anhedonia, sleep disturbance, guilt/worthlessness, and psychomotor changes observed in the NPP cohort. However, this study identified DIF between the groups only for sleep disturbance and fatigue. This discrepancy arises from the distinct statistical methodologies underlying raw mean comparisons versus DIF analysis.

DIF analysis evaluates whether groups exhibit different item responses after adjusting the laten construct estimate. Direct item mean comparisons are less robust than DIF modeling because observed differences can be confounded by variations in overall depression severity. Consequently, DIF assessments offer greater internal validity than direct mean comparisons.

For DIF, statistical significance is sensitive to sample size; thus, large samples may detect trivial differences. Conversely, DIF magnitude quantifies the strength of item bias, independent of sample size. Therefore, this study incorporated both statistical significance and the magnitude of DIF to determine meaningful item bias.

Due to the large sample size, this study identified several item biases among the pregnant, postpartum, and NPP women. However, no items reached the clinically significant threshold or demonstrated symptom bias.

Our DIF findings, which revealed no symptom bias for PHQ-9 among pregnant, postpartum, and NPP women, contrast with a prior study that examined DIF in DSM-IV MDE criteria using the 2004–2005 NESARC sample (n = 11,342) [20]. Although both studies identified item biases, only the previous study found a symptom bias for suicidality endorsement between pregnant and NPP women. This discrepancy regarding suicidality may reflect different measures and statistical methods. First, the previous study used a structured clinical interview yielding dichotomous data (yes/no), whereas our study analyzed PHQ-9 ordinal data (scores 0–3). Consequently, that study evaluated item endorsement bias, while our study assessed item severity bias. Second, that study was based on an IRT likelyhood ratio framework, whereas ours utilized an OLR/IRT framework. Third, because the statistical methods differed, the clinical significance thresholds were defined differently. Despite these differences, both studies found that—except for the suicidality criterion—the DIF of most DSM-IV MDE criteria and all PHQ-9 items fell below the clinical significant thresholds.

Fatigue was a prominent somatic feature among pregnant and postpartum women, but its elevated severity did not meet the threshold for clinical significance. Specifically, its high factor loading on the somatic factor aligns with reports that somatic symptoms are prominent during pregnancy and the postpartum period [8, 9]. Statistically significant uniform DIF revealed that fatigue severity was higher in pregnant and postpartum women than in NPP women after adjusting for overall somatic severity. However, the DIF magnitude did not reach the clinical threshold (total DIF R2 difference = 0.001 and 0.003), indicating that this greater severity was modest and of minimal clinical relevance. This absence of symptom bias suggests that fatigue is of minimal clinical utility for differentiating peripartum depression from MDD—a finding that aligns with the DSM-5-TR classification of peripartum depression as a subtype of MDD.

Implication for practice and research

Measurement invariance across pregnant, postpartum, and NPP women suggests that the PHQ-9 assesses depressive symptoms equivalently across these groups. This equivalence confirms that the PHQ-9 is valid and comparable across these populations. Consequently, healthcare workers and policymakers can trust that elevated scores reflect true psychological distress rather than the physiological somatic changes typical of pregnancy and the postpartum period.

The present findings align with the current DSM framework. Specifically, the absence of symptom bias between peripartum and NPP women does not support a distinction between peripartum depression and MDD. Likewise, the lack of symptom bias between pregnant and postpartum women does not support a separation between antenatal- and postpartum-onset depression.

This study evaluated the factor structure, measurement invariance, and DIF of the PHQ-9 in a US sample of women, where approximately 10% met the moderate-to-severe depression threshold. Replication studies are needed in women diagnosed with MDD across peripartum stages. Replication in non-U.S. samples—especially in low- and middle-income countries—is also warranted given the higher prevalence of peripartum depression [51]. Because the transition to motherhood involves complex physiological, psychological, and social changes [52], future invariance and item bias analyses should incorporate other peripartum-relevant variables, such as pregnancy trimester, breastfeeding status, and delivery mode.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, sample, selection, and measurement biases cannot be excluded. Because this study did not apply NHANES sampling weights to adjust for oversampling, survey nonresponse, and post-stratification adjustments, these findings preclude population-level inference [53]. Consequently, the descriptive statistics and reported proportions of probable depression (PHQ-9 ≥ 10) do not represent population-level and prevalence estimates, respectively. Excluding incomplete PHQ-9 cases may have introduced selection bias, particularly among those with greater depression severity. Furthermore, using the self-reported PHQ-9 may have introduced measurement bias, as self-reported instruments are often less accurate than structured clinical interviews in assessing depressive symptoms. Second, small sample sizes prevented informative MI and DIF analyses among women with moderate-to-severe depression. Only 29 postpartum participants met this threshold, which is insufficient for these analyses. Third, because only 7%–13% of participants had moderate-to-severe depression, these findings may not generalize to this clinical population. Fourth, the peripartum status data of the DEMO module relied partly on self-report rather than laboratory confirmation, potentially introducing misclassification. However, relying solely on laboratory tests could also cause misclassification. Fifth, the postpartum group included women up to 12 months postpartum—well beyond the DSM-5-TR four-week specifier—potentially increasing heterogeneity in symptom profiles. Because approximately 60% of women with postpartum depression at 9 to 10 months did not report those symptoms at 2 to 6 months [54], the wide postpartum range investigated here could confound result interpretation and limit comparability with prior literature. Sixth, the significance threshold of P < 0.01 for the likelihood ratio (LR) χ2 test used in this study was exploratory, notwithstanding its common use in DIF analysis [38]. Although this threshold is more stringent than the standard threshold of P < 0.05. the risk of type I errors (false-positive results) remains elevated due to multiple comparisons. Finally, the sample was limited to community U.S. women, which restricts generalizability to other countries, healthcare settings, and high-risk populations. In addition, the findings reflected subthreshold symptoms of depression, which need replication in clinical populations.

Conclusions

The PHQ-9 assesses depressive symptoms equivalently across pregnant, postpartum, and NPP women and permits meaningful comparisons among these groups. At equivalent depression severity, the differences in PHQ-9 item responses across these groups are modest and of minimal clinical relevance. The absence of symptom bias does not support a distinction between peripartum depression and DSM-5-TR MDD. Future studies should evaluate peripartum women with MDD, using larger samples, across different countries, and under diverse clinical conditions.
