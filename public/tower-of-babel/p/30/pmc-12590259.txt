Ultrasound-assisted enzyme extraction of Citri Exocarpium Rubrum (Juhong) polysaccharides and its structural characteristics and evaluation of improving Parkinson’s disease through HDAC6/NLRP3 pathway
Authors: Xianqun Meng, Sundong Lin, Mingtao Zhu, Siyi Wang, Haixue Kuang, Qiuhong Wang, Tian Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Citri Exocarpium Rubrum (CER) has been utilized in food and medicine for over 2000 years both domestically and internationally. This study investigated the polysaccharide extraction process, structural characteristics, and biological activity of CER using ultrasonic-assisted enzyme extraction (UAE). The sugar yield of UCERP extracted via UAE, was 14.23 ± 0.12 %, with optimal extraction conditions of 70°C, 50 min of ultrasonic enzyme hydrolysis, 4.0 % cellulase, a solid-to-liquid ratio of 20: 1 mL/g, and ultrasonic power of 240 W. The monosaccharide composition of UCERP1-1, purified by isolation, includes rhamnose, arabinose, galactose, glucose, and galacturonic acid (ratio of 0.042: 0.202: 0.242: 0.06: 0.454). Moreover, the structural analysis revealed that UCERP1-1′s fractions are primarily linked by a repeating unit:→[4)-α-D-GalAp-(1]6 → [4)-α-D-GalAp-(1]5 → [4)-β-D-Galp-(1]3 → 3)-α-L-Rhap-(1 → 4)-β-D-Galp-(1 → 4)-β-D-Galp-(1 → . In vivo, a Parkinson’s disease (PD) model was established in mice using MPTP to assess the therapeutic effects of UCERP1-1. It significantly alleviated PD symptoms, improved behavioral deficits, increased the number of TH-positive cells, reduced dopaminergic neuron loss in MPTP-treated mice, and decreased SH-SY5Y cell death induced by MPP+. Further analysis via ELISA, Western blot and Cellular thermal shift assay revealed that UCERP1-1 reduced inflammatory markers and alleviated oxidative stress in PD mice. UCERP1-1 also regulated the HDAC6/NLRP3 pathway in PD mice. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for the potential use of UCERP1-1 as a functional food for PD or related diseases.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD), the second most common neurodegenerative disorder after Alzheimer’s disease. The main pathological feature of PD is the loss of dopaminergic (DA) neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc) and deposition of Lewy bodies [1]. According to research estimates, the number of PD patients will increase from approximately 7 million in 2015 to about 1.3 million by 2040 [2]. The pathophysiology of PD results from a complex interaction of abnormal α-synuclein (α-Syn) aggregation, mitochondrial dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and other factors [3]. Although levodopa remains the primary treatment for PD, prolonged use may lead to adverse reactions in some patients [4]. Therefore, the development of new drugs with fewer side effects is urgently needed. Additionally, the extensive inflammatory response plays a central role in PD progression [5,6], but the mechanisms remain unclear. Histone deacetylases (HDACs) are categorized into four major classes, with HDAC6, a class IIb member, primarily expressed in the cytoplasm [7]. Pharmacological inhibition of HDAC6 reduces the NLRP3-mediated inflammatory response in a PD model [8]. HDAC6 may serve as a promising target for modulating the NLRP3-mediated inflammatory response in patients with PD [9].

Citri Exocarpium Rubrum (CER), known as Juhong in China, is the dried exocarp of Citrus reticulata Blanco and its cultivars (Fam. Rutaceae). It is both a traditional medicinal and edible plant. Current research on CER primarily focuses on flavonoid, while the structural characterization and biological activity of polysaccharides remain underexplored [10]. Due to their high efficiency and low toxicity, polysaccharides are considered one of the most promising medicinal agents. CER has been reported to possess antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anticancer, and other therapeutic activities [11,12]. The main area of polysaccharide research continues to be its structural characterization and pharmacological activity. However, the structural features of CER polysaccharides and their protective potential against PD, along with the underlying mechanisms, have not yet been reported.

Ultrasonic-assisted extraction (UAE) has been successfully applied to isolate polysaccharides from various plants [13]. This technology facilitates the penetration of the medicinal surface, causing polysaccharide particles to vibrate intensely and releasing the polysaccharides [[14], [15], [16]]. However, ultrasonic conditions must be carefully controlled to prevent the degradation of soluble polysaccharides [16]. Zhou et al. reported that the application of UAE did not disrupt the structure–activity relationship and increased the extraction rate of Dendrobium polysaccharides [17]. Similarly, Liu et al. demonstrated that ultrasonic waves accelerated the migration of phenolic compounds in grape skins and seeds, thereby enhancing the quality of sweet wine [18]. In contrast, the high viscosity of polysaccharides hampers their subsequent release during traditional high-temperature water extraction, leading to incomplete extraction and resource wastage [19]. Therefore, exploring more efficient methods for CERP extraction is crucial. Ultrasound-assisted enzymatic extraction combines ultrasound-induced cell disruption with enzymatic hydrolysis, facilitating the diffusion and release of polysaccharides [19]. This method not only enhances extraction efficiency but also reduces the structural damage to polysaccharides to some extent. Wang et al. further found that the polysaccharides extracted using this technique exhibited better antioxidant activity [20]. UAE/enzymatic extraction could further enhance the productivity and biological activity of polysaccharides [20]. So, we opted for ultrasound-assisted enzymatic extraction to extract CERP.

In this study, UAE/enzymatic extraction was employed to obtain unstudied CER polysaccharides. The optimal extraction process of CERP was investigated using single-factor and response surface methods. For the first time, the monosaccharide composition, molecular weight (Mw), and structural characteristics of CER polysaccharides (UCERP1-1) were thoroughly analyzed. Additionally, in vivo and in vitro experiments were conducted to evaluate the potential of UCERP1-1 in preventing and treating PD. This study fills the gaps in the extraction, characterization, and biological activity of UCERP1-1 and provides a scientific foundation for its further development.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Dry CER was produced from the Sankeshu Traditional Chinese Medicine Market in Harbin, China, subsequently determined. MPTP (1-Methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine, Sigma, China). MPP+ was produced from Sigma (China), while L-DOPA (Med Chem Express LLC, USA) served as the positive control. Elisa kits for tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) were obtained from the Jian-cheng Institute of Biotechnology. Superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-PX), malondialdehyde (MDA) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) of kits were obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Antibodies targeting tyrosine hydroxylase (TH), α-Syn, apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing CARD (ASC), cysteine aspartic acid specific protease-1 (caspase-1), NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3), and histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6) were purchased from Abtec Biotech Co., Ltd (Harbin, China). Additional chemicals were premier.

Pretreatment

CER was first soaked in anhydrous ethanol for 1 h at 25–27 °C. Then, reflux at 60 °C for 2 h to remove fats and color. After filtration, the material was dried at 55 °C for 5 h until its weight stabilized, ensuring uniformity, and was then stored for later use.

Optimization and obtained UCERP1-1

For enzyme extraction, single-factor optimization includes enzyme type, enzyme amount, ultrasonic enzyme digestion time, extraction temperature, and material-to-solvent ratio. 5 kg of herbal powder was extracted by ultrasonication under papain, pectinase, and cellulase, enzyme concentration (2 %, 3 %, 4 %, 5 %, 6 %), extraction time (30, 40, 50, 60, 70 min), temperature (50, 60, 70, 80, 90 ℃), liquid-to-solid ratio (10: 1, 15: 1, 20: 1, 25: 1, 30: 1 mL/g) and ultrasonic power (240 W). After filtration, the extract was centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. To concentrate the extract, five volumes of anhydrous ethanol were added, and the mixture was refrigerated at 4 ℃ overnight. In addition, RSM (response surface methodology) and BBD (Box-Behnken design) were subsequently applied to analyze enzyme amount, enzyme digestion time, and extraction temperature using Design Expert 10.0 software. Ultrasonic power was set to 240 W. After ultrasonic treatment, the polysaccharide (UCERP) was extracted and filtered.

UCERP1-1 was obtained through separation and purification. Briefly, proteins from UCERP were removed using the Sevag method, and the polysaccharides were eluted using DEAE-52 with water (UCERP1), 0.3 M sodium chloride, and 0.5 M sodium chloride. The UCERP1 fraction, eluted with water, exhibited a high content. Elution fractions with different concentrations were determined by phenol–sulfuric acid method and then enriched and freeze-dried. UCERP1 was further purified using Sephadex G-100 (2.6 cm × 80 cm) with water as the eluent, resulting in UCERP1-1. The purified polysaccharide was monitored by phenol–sulfuric acid method and lyophilized into powder, which named UCERP1.

Characterization of UCERP1-1 polysaccharide

Molecular weight (Mw) analysis

UCERP1-1 was identified using Li’s method [21], and its Mw was determined through High-Performance Gel Permeation Chromatography (HPGPC). The analysis was conducted using a BRT105-103–101 tandem gel column (8 mm × 300 mm) and differential detector RID-20A. 0.2 M NaCl liquor was used as the mobile phase. The Mw of UCERP1-1 was determined by dextran standards (Mw P5, P10, P50, P100, P400, P800 Da) (Showa Denko Materials Co., Ltd).

Monosaccharide of UCERP1-1

UCERP1-1 was hydrolyzed according to Li’s method. UCERP1-1 was analyzed via high-performance anion exchange chromatography with pulsed amperometric detection (HPAEC-PAD) (Dionex Carbopac™ PA20 column, 150 mm × 3.0 mm). The mobile phases: A:H2O, B:15 mM NaOH, C: 15 mM NaOH & 100 mM NaAc, by flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. Standard monosaccharide mixture containing fucose; galactosamine Hydrochloride, rhamnose, arabinose, glucosamine hydrochloride, galactose, glucose, xylose, mannose, fructose, ribose, galacturonic acid, Guluronic acid, glucuronic acid, mannuronic acid (BoRui Saccharide Biotech Co., Ltd.) were derivatized by a previously described method [21].

Uv–visible spectroscopy and Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectral

Ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectroscopy was employed to assess the absorbance of UCERP1-1 in the range of 200–400 nm. FT-IR spectroscopy of 2 mg UCERP1-1 was performed in KBr and measured over the range of 400–4000 cm−1 using a Shimadzu FT-IR650 spectrometer.

Methylation of UCERP1-1

Following Li’s research [21], UCERP1-1 was analyzed by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) (QP-2010ULTRA, Shimadzu, Japan). The methylated products were extracted with CHCl3 (DCM), and the solvent was removed by evaporation. The sample was hydrolyzed by 1 mL of 2 M CF3COOH (TFA) at 120 ℃ for 90 min and was reduced with NaBH4 for 2.5 h. The reduced monosaccharides were neutralized using acetic acid and acetylated with acetic anhydride. After acetylation, the resulting partial methylation alditol acetates (PMAAs) were analyzed by comparing with the BRT standard mass spectrum [21].

NMR spectroscopy of UCERP1-1

The 1H, 13C, and 2D NMR spectra were obtained by dissolving 70 mg of UCERP1-1 in 0.5 mL of D2O. The data were recorded on a Bruker DRX-500 spectrometer (Bruker, Rheinstetten, Germany). NMR data were analyzed using Topspin 4.1.3 software (Bruker, Germany).

Animals

Experimental animals

C57BL/6 mice (fifty-five male, 8 weeks of age, weighing 18–22 g) were sourced from Beijing Weitonglihua Laboratory Animal. The mice were acclimatized for 1 week under conditions of 20–25 °C and 40 %-60 % humidity before the experiment. All animal experiments were approved by the Experimental Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Heilongjiang University of Chinese Medicine, China (No. 21000430150119).

Acute toxicity assay

The acute toxicity test was performed according to the OECD Test Guidelines 425 (Up and Down Procedure). Mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 6): a normal control (NC) group and a UCERP1-1 treatment (1500 mg/kg UCERP1-1–1500). The NC group received normal saline by gavage, whereas the treatment group was administered UCERP1-1 at a maximum concentration of 1500 mg/kg. After two weeks, the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney of histopathology of mice were observed and photographed under light microscope.

Animal grouping and modeling

The mice were randomly divided into 5 groups: normal control (NC), model group (MPTP, 30 mg/kg), positive drug group (L-DOPA, 100 mg/kg), high-dose UCERP1-1 (200 mg/kg, UCERP1-1H), and low-dose UCERP1-1 (50 mg/kg, UCERP1-1L). The treatment was administered for 14 days, with NC and MPTP groups receiving an equal amount of physiological saline. From the 10th day of treatment, mice were intraperitoneally injected with MPTP for 5 consecutive days. After modeling, the mice exhibited typical PD symptoms, including tremors, vertical hair, arched back, straightened back feet, and salivation.

Histological analysis

Nissl staining: Paraffin sections (5 μm thickness) were treated by dehydration with anhydrous ethanol, cleared in dimethylbenzene, and sealed with resin before being observed under a microscope. The average number of intact cells was calculated after counting four random, non-overlapping fields (200x) per tissue section. All histopathological observations were conducted by a blinded investigator.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC): The paraffin sections were roasted, dewaxed, and hydrated using standard procedures. Afterward, the sections were sealed with a neutral adhesive, photographed under an optical microscope, and analyzed using statistical software. At a magnification of 200X, non-overlapping fields from each section were analyzed.

Rotarod and climbing pole test

Behavioral assessments included the rotarod test. Mice underwent a one-week pre-training period prior to the experiment. In the third week after MPTP management, the animals were tested on the rotarod device. In climbing rod test, mice were placed on a rubber ball with their heads up, and the time it took for them to climb from the rod to the ground with their heads down was recorded. After completing the rotarod and climbing rod tests, the animals were killed, and brain tissues were obtained.

SH-SY5Y cells test

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

SHSY5Y cells (Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd.) were seeded in plates and cultivateed overnight. Initially, the function of various concentrations of UCERP1, Fr.B, Fr.C and UCERP1-1 (0, 25, 50, 100, 200, and 400 μg/mL, n = 6) on SHSY5Y cell activity was assessed by CCK-8 kit (Beyotime, China). After 24 h, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added. After the fully mixed cell solution was incubated for 2 h, the OD450 nm value was detected using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA).

CCK-8 also was used to detect the effect of different concentrations UCERP1-1 on MPP+-induced SHSY5Y cells injury. The cells were then divided into five groups: NC, MPP+, UCERP1, Fr.B, Fr.C and UCERP1-1 (50, 100, 200 μg/mL, n = 6). A solution containing 1 mM MPP+ iodide (diluted in DMSO) was added to the cells and incubated for 24 h. After modeling, the cells were treated for an additional 24 h before further experiments. The absorbance value at 450 nm were determined.

Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) assay

SHSY5Y cells were seeded into 96 well plates (Corning, USA) at the density of 5 × 104 cells/well. 10 μL of LDH reagent (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) was added. Following the treatment of cells with MPP+, the supernatant was collected and transferred to a new 96-well plate. After adding and mixing with 60 μL of LDH solution, the plate was incubated in the dark for 30 min. Subsequently, the OD490 nm value was detected using a microplate reader.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The ELISA method was used to assess inflammatory factors and oxidative stress in the substantia nigra tissue mice. The levels of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, SOD, GSH-PX, MDA and ROS were detected respectively according to the reagent instructions.

Western blotting (WB) analysis

Substantia nigra tissue of the mouse brain was homogenized with ice-cold lysate, vortexed, and centrifuged to collect the supernatant. Protein was denatured using the BCA method in boiling water. The protein was separated from 10 % (w/v) sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to a polyvinyl difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, USA). The membrane was blocked with 5 % skim milk for 2 h, then incubated overnight with primary antibodies against β-actin, TH, α-Syn, HDAC6, NLRP3, ASC and caspase-1. The membrane was washed three times with Tris buffer (TBS, Beyotime, China) containing 0.1 % Tween-20 (Beyotime, China) on the next day. The membranes were then incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, the proteins were determined by enhanced chemiluminescence kit (ECL, Millipore, USA) by Chemiluminescence gel imaging system (Tanon, Shanghai, China).

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

The cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) was performed using the method described by Liu et al. [22]. The supernatant was subjected to thermal treatment in a PCR apparatus at temperatures ranging from 43 to 70 ℃, then cooled to room temperature for 3 min. The samples were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm and 4 ℃ for 20 min to separate the supernatant from the solid residue. The resulting samples were then prepared for analysis. HDAC6 protein expression in SH-SY5Y cells was assessed using western blotting.

Data analysis

Experimental data were analyzed through SPSS 22.0 and GraphPad Prism 8 software. Experimental data are showed as mean ± standard. One-way ANOVA and Duncan’s novel multiple-range tests were used to determine statistical significance, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Crude polysaccharides from CER were extracted using three different enzymes under constant conditions. Fig. 1E showed the changes in sugar yield for each enzyme. The yield of CERP extracted with cellulase was notably higher, leading to the selection of cellulase for further extraction. When the enzyme concentration was increased to 4.0 %, the polysaccharide yield reached 14.08 %. Further increases in enzyme concentration did not significantly affect the CERP yield, likely due to approaching saturation, which prevented further increases in polysaccharide yield [23] (Fig. 1A). As shown in Fig. 1B, increasing the ultrasonic enzyme digestion time led to higher CERP yields. But, a decline in yield was observed when the time exceeded 50 min, likely because prolonged ultrasonic treatment disrupted the macromolecular structure of the polysaccharides [24]. In Fig. 1C, CERP yield increased rapidly when the temperature rose (50°C-70°C), with yield of 13.96 % at 70°C. However, no significant increase was observed beyond this temperature, suggesting that higher temperatures not only consumed more energy but also damaged the polysaccharides, reducing their quality. As seen in Fig. 1D, the CERP yield peaked at a liquid-to-feed ratio of 20: 1 mL/g. Excessive solvent reduced the interaction between material molecules, lowering the dissolution rate. This could be attributed to an increase in water volume, which extracted other soluble substances and decreased the solubility of the CERP [25]. Therefore, the optimal single-factor conditions for extracting UAE-CERP crude polysaccharides were as follows: cellulase as the auxiliary enzyme, an enzyme dosage of 4 %, ultrasonic enzymatic time of 50 min, extracted temperature of 70°C, and material: liquid of 20: 1 mL/g.

These results suggest that the single-factor conditions are closely correlated with the CERP extraction rate. Additionally, when the factors exceed certain critical values, the extraction rate significantly decreases. In single-factor experiments, it is assumed that factors are independent, but in reality, most factors are interrelated. Further analysis using the results from the single-factor experiments is required for a more comprehensive understanding.

Response surface analysis

The optimal liquid-to-material ratio was determined to be 20: 1 mL/g, as CERP yield did not increase significantly beyond this ratio. A higher liquid-to-material ratio would result in solvent waste during the alcohol precipitation step. Thus, this factor was fixed at 20: 1 mL/g and not considered as a variable. Based on the single-factor experiment, a BBD with three factors and three levels was used to further optimize the extraction process [26]. The independent variables were enzyme dosage (A), ultrasonic time (B), and extraction temperature (C), while the total sugar yield was the response variable (R1). Extraction and purification

In this study, CER polysaccharides were extracted using ultrasound-assisted cellulase extraction (UAE), and UCERP polysaccharides were obtained. The optimal process conditions were determined through response surface optimization. Protein removal from UCERP was performed using the Sevag method. Subsequently, it was separated using DEAE-52, and the main fraction was collected. UCERP1 (Fr.A) was obtained by eluting with distilled water and accounted for 61.9 % of the crude polysaccharide, while 0.3 mol/L NaCl (Fr.B) was only 16.8 %, and the lost polysaccharide (Fr.C, 0.5 mol/L NaCl) was about 13.7 %. CCK8 assay was used to detect the cytotoxicity of UCERP1, Fr.B, and Fr.C on SHSY5Y cells, and the results showed that UCERP1, Fr.B, and Fr.C treatment led to a remarkable inhibitory effect at 400 μg/mL (Fig. 3C). We chosed 50, 100, and 200 μg/mL of UCERP1, Fr.B, and Fr.C for subsequent experiments on SHSY5Y cells. Moreover, CCK8 results showed that UCERP1 activated SHSY5Y activity damage caused by MPP+ in a concentration-dependent (Fig. 3D). The Fr.B and Fr.C groups had activation effects, but they were not significant and were inferior to the UCERP1 group (Fig. 3D). Therefore, the UCERP1 was selected for further purification and structure analyzed. Further purification of UCERP1 was achieved through Gel chromatography column, yielding UCERP1-1. BCA protein detection and UV spectroscopy suggested that UCERP1-1 was free from protein and nucleinic acid (Fig. 3B). As shown in Fig. 3E, UCERP1-1 exhibited a single symmetrical peak, indicating that it is a homogeneous polysaccharide. The Mp of UCERP1-1 was 2170 Da, Mw was 2152 Da, Mn was 2142 Da (Fig. S1 and Table S1).

Characterization of UCERP1-1

Monosaccharide of UCERP1-1

Monosaccharide composition analysis (Fig. 3F and G) indicated that UCERP1-1 contains rhamnose, arabinose, galactose, glucose, and galacturonic acid in a ratio of 0.042: 0.202: 0.242: 0.06: 0.454. Based on the data in Fig. 3G, UCERP1-1 is likely classified as an acidic polysaccharide.

FT-IR spectroscopy

FT-IR spectroscopy was used to examine the types and vibrations of function groups of polysaccharides (Fig. 3H). A peak around 3390 cm−1 was associated with O–H stretching in the UCERP1-1 [28]. The weak band 2937 cm−1 was related to C–H stretching. Absorption peaks at 1741 cm−1, 1423 cm−1, 1145 cm−1, and 1101 cm−1 can be attributed to C-O stretching vibrations. An absorption peak at 1614 cm−1 corresponds to C=O stretching vibrations. The peak at 1330 cm−1 was indicative of C=O symmetric stretching vibrations [29]. Peaks at 1241 cm−1, 1049 cm−1, and 1016 cm−1 were associated with O–H bending vibrations. The peak at 962 cm−1 was likely due to the rocking vibration of the terminal methyl group [30]. An absorption peak at 916 cm−1 corresponds to asymmetric ring stretching vibrations. The peak at 892 cm−1 can be attributed to C–H bending vibrations of the β-terminal group cis–trans isomerism [31].

Toxicity tests

HE staining revealed no obvious injuries in the heart, liver, spleen, lung, or kidney of mice in either the NC group or the UCERP1-1–1500 group. As for heart, we found that myocardial cells in UCERP1-1–1500 were neatly arranged and myofilaments were clear. The hepatocytes in the livers of mice with UCERP1-1–1500 exhibited intact lobular architecture, clear nuclei, and no evidence of fatty degeneration or fibrosis. In UCERP1-1-1500′s spleen, we found that the distinction between white and red pulp was distinct, and cell morphology was normal. As for lung, alveolar wall thickness in group UCERP1-1–1500 was normal and capillaries were intact. We also detect the effect of UCERP1-1–1500 on the kidney, histological analysis revealed that the glomerular cells had regular morphology, clear and complete structure. These findings suggest that UCERP1-1 has minimal side effects (Fig. 6 A and B).

Discussion

Ultrasound-assisted enzyme extraction method

Compared with traditional methods, ultrasound-assisted enzyme extraction does not require chemical reagents, thereby helping to preserve the structural integrity of polysaccharides. It offers several advantages, including high extraction efficiency, mild reaction, broad applicability, and environmental sustainability. This method enables the rapid extraction of large amount of target active products while retaining their original structural properties [38,39]. Similarly, Chen et al. [40] applied ultrasonic/enzymatic extraction to Akebia fruit polysaccharides, which not only improved the extraction yield but also showed potential antioxidant activity. Consequently, this technique has become a widely used approach for extracting plant polysaccharides. In this study, we extracted polysaccharides from Citri Exocarpium Rubrum (UCERP) using cellulase-assisted ultrasound in combination with response surface experimental design.

Structure-PD relationship analysis

UCERP1-1, isolated and purified from UCERP, is a homogeneous polysaccharide. Homogeneous polysaccharides form the basis for studying the structure and activity of polysaccharides. The biological activity of polysaccharides is closely associated with its molecular weight, monosaccharide composition, and glycosidic structure. PFP50-1 from the leaves of Perilla frutescens, mainly composed of galp and araf, showed anti-neuroinflammatory effect [41]. However, the structure and activity of Citri Exocarpium Rubrum polysaccharides are rarely researched. Here, a new water-soluble polysaccharide UCERP1-1 was obtained from Citri Exocarpium Rubrum, and was composed of arabinose, galactose, and galacturonic acid. Heteropolysaccharides containing arabinose, galactose, and galacturonic acid can modulate neurological disorders by exerting anti-neuroinflammatory and antioxidant effects [42]. Additionally, UCERP1-1 consists of α-D-GalAp-(1, 4)-α-D-GalAp-(1, 4)-β-D-Galp-(1, α-L-Araf-(1→, 5)-α-L-Araf-(1 → residues. The presence of these glycosidic linkages in the polysaccharide backbone is critical for its neuroinflammatory effects [35,[43], [44], [35], [45]], suggesting that UCERP1-1 may improve PD.

UCERP1-1 protected against MPTP-induced PD in mice

We found that UCERP1-1 alleviated dyskinesia in MPTP-induced mice. UCERP1-1 also protected against MPTP-induced PD in mice by increasing the number of Nissl-stained and TH-positive neurons. In addition, UCERP1-1 regulated the expression of α-Syn and TH proteins. α-Syn is a key amyloid protein contributing to Lewy body formation in PD-affected DA neurons [3]. TH, a marker for DA neurons [46], reflects the functional status of these neurons. The findings suggest that UCERP1-1 may be effective in rodent PD models or potentially in humans. SH-SY5Y is a widely used cell line in PD research [47]. Since these cells cannot metabolize MPTP into its active form, MPP+, a toxic metabolite of MPTP, was used instead [48]. CCK-8 and LDH assays showed that UCERP1-1 exerted an indirect protective effect on DA neurons. These results provided further evidence that UCERP1-1 may have an ameliorating effect on PD. Moreover, MPTP induces significant damage to DA neurons in mice, leading to neuron loss, microglial activation, and discharge of inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β) [49]. Notably, increased levels of these cytokines have also been detected in the sera of patients with PD [50]. Oxidative stress is another key pathophysiological factor in PD, resulting from the excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in response to harmful stimuli. Overaccumulation of ROS during PD progression can trigger α-Syn protein misfolding, thereby contributing to neuronal death and neuroinflammation [51,52]. ROS levels are normally regulated by endogenous antioxidants such as SOD, GSH-PX, and MDA. In our study, UCERP1-1 suppressed the levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β in MPTP-induced PD mice, increased SOD and GSH levels, and reduced the concentrations of MDA and ROS. These findings indicate that UCERP1-1 exerts potential anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects in MPTP-induced PD mice.

HDAC6/NLRP3 pathway in PD

Furthermore, HDAC6 plays a central role in the pathogenesis of Parkinson’s disease by regulating protein acetylation levels, thereby promoting neuroinflammation and α-Syn aggregation [53]. Accordingly, inhibition of HDAC6 can alleviate PD and neuroinflammation [54,55]. Recent studies reported that pharmacological inhibition of HDAC6 attenuated the NLRP3-mediated inflammatory response in a PD model [56]. Mechanistically, HDAC6 may indirectly influence the assembly and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome by altering microtubule stability and transport functions [57,58]. NLRP3 is a pattern recognition receptor that cooperates with ASC and caspase-1 to mediate the inflammatory response. Through epigenetic target research, it was found that that rhamnose, galactose, and glucose consistently bind to HDAC6 [59]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that UCERP1-1 suppresses MPTP-induced Parkinson’s disease in mice by modulating the HDAC6/NLRP3 pathway. Our western blotting results confirmed that UCERP1-1 significantly decreased the expression levels of HDAC6/NLRP3 signaling pathway markers. Moreover, CETSA analysis indicated that UCERP1-1 may directly bind to HDAC6 and enhance its thermal stability. Overall, these findings suggested that the protective role of UCERP1-1 is mediated through the HDAC6/NLRP3 signaling pathway.

Functional food

UCERP1-1, a newly identified polysaccharide from Citri Exocarpium Rubrum, showed low toxicity and protective effects against PD. Polysaccharides have emerged as important functional components [60]. For example, fenugreek gum (GM) polysaccharides have been linked to potential health benefits in humans, including prebiotic activity, improving glycemic balance, and reducing blood lipid concentrations in type 2 diabetes [61]. Beyond basic nutrition, functional foods enriched with polysaccharides can improve health, reduce overall disease risk, and even contribute to disease management. Polysaccharides from Lycium barbarum and Astragalus have already been developed into health products with demonstrated efficacy in preventing and treating Parkinson’s disease [62,63]. This research provides a scientific basis for the development of UCERP1-1 as a functional food ingredient with potential health-promoting and disease-preventive applications.

However, this study also has limitations. Polysaccharide from Citri Exocarpium Rubrum can also be extracted using pure water, and further research is needed to elucidate the antioxidant and anti-neuroapoptosis mechanisms of UCERP1-1 in PD protection.

Conclusions

This study optimized the ultrasonic/enzymatic extraction conditions for polysaccharides from CER. UCERP1-1 was successfully isolated and purified from Citri Exocarpium Rubrum polysaccharides. The main chain structure of UCERP1-1 is as follows:

→[4)-α-D-GalAp-(1]6 → [4)-α-D-GalAp-(1]5 → [4)-β-D-Galp-(1]3 → 3)-α-L-Rhap-(1 → 4)-β-D-Galp-(1 → 4)-β-D-Galp-(1 → . Besides, our findings supply preliminary proof that UCERP1-1 improved MPTP-induced PD by alleviating inflammation, oxidative stress and HDAC6/NLRP3 pathways. This work further highlights the potential of UCERP1-1 as an alternative therapy for treating PD.
