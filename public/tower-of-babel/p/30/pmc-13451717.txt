Prevalence, associated factors, and antibiotic susceptibility of bacterial isolates in suspected ear infections at University of Gondar Hospital, Northwest Ethiopia: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Anmut Amare, Mitkie Tigabie, Getnet Ayalew, Wondwossen Abebe, Yitayih Wondimeneh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEar infections affect millions worldwide, causing significant hearing loss, particularly in developing countries. Bacterial infections play a major role, necessitating studies on causative agents, risk factors, and antibiotic resistance.ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess the prevalence, associated factors, and antibiotic susceptibility of the bacterial isolates among patients in suspected ear infections at the University of Gondar Hospital, Ethiopia.MethodsA hospital-based prospective cross-sectional study was conducted from May to July 2023, involving 303 participants with clinical diagnosis of having ear infections. Ear discharge samples were cultured on blood agar, chocolate agar, and MacConkey agar. Antibiotic susceptibility was determined using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method. Cefoxitin disc and double-disc synergy tests detected methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) production in Enterobacterales respectively. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27, with logistic regression to identify associations. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsBacterial culture was positive in 48.5% (147/303; 95% CI: 42.8–54.3) of cases, yielding 172 isolates with Proteus mirabilis 30.2 % (52/172), Pseudomonas aeruginosa 13.9% (24/172) and S. aureus 13.9% (24/172) as the common isolated pathogens. Multidrug resistance was observed in 64.5% (111/172) of isolates, while 29.2% (7/24) of S. aureus exhibited methicillin resistance. ESBL production was detected in 19.1% (14/82) of Enterobacterales, particularly Klebsiella pneumoniae 27.3% (3/11). Associated factors included age under five (AOR = 1.19, p = 0.013) and upper respiratory tract infections (URTI) (AOR = 3.27, p < 0.001) in children. Among adults, diabetes mellitus (AOR = 2.73, p = 0.032), URTI (AOR = 1.97, p < 0.001), and frequent earphone use (AOR = 11.4, p < 0.001) were significantly associated.ConclusionP. mirabilis, P. aeruginosa, and S. aureus were predominant and exhibited high antibiotic resistance. Targeted antimicrobial stewardship, culture-based therapy, and public health interventions are essential, particularly for high-risk groups.

Introduction

Ear infection, affecting 65–330 million people globally, 1 is common in all ages but particularly in children due to their shorter, horizontal eustachian tubes, which facilitate pathogen entry to the middle ear. 2 It includes otitis externa, media, and interna, with middle ear inflammation being the most prevalent, 3 occurring as acute suppurative otitis media (ASOM), chronic suppurative otitis media (CSOM), or otitis media with effusion (OME). 4

Chronic suppurative otitis media is a persistent middle ear infection with a perforated tympanic membrane and draining exudate, often associated with cholesteatoma, with P. aeruginosa being the most common cause.5,6 The World Health Organization classifies CSOM prevalence into categories: highest (>4%), high (2-4%), low (1-2%), and lowest (<1%). Its prevalence remains high in developing nations (2-4%), with approximately 164 million cases of hearing loss attributed to CSOM in these regions. 7

Acute suppurative otitis media is characterized by fluid in the middle ear and acute infection symptoms, often recurring in children and leading to frequent antibiotic prescriptions. 8 Bacteria such as Streptococcus pneumoniae (S. pneumoniae), Haemophilus influenzae, (H. influenzae), and Moraxella catarrhalis commonly cause ASOM. 9 In Ethiopia, bacterial ear infections are prevalent, ranging from 48.5% to 95.2%, with P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, H. influenzae, Proteus spp., Klebsiella spp., and E. coli are the leading causes.3,5,10–21 Otitis media is particularly prevalent among children and is commonly treated with antibiotics including, amoxicillin and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid and ciprofloxacin. However, misuse or overuse of these antibiotics has led to rising global antibiotic resistance. 22

Currently, there is an increasing trend of antibiotic resistance rates to ear infections caused by bacteria reported worldwide. 23 Literatures showed that, bacterial isolates from ear discharge samples were resistant to amoxicillin, tetracycline, and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole.20,24,25 In Gram-negative bacteria (GNB), resistance mechanisms include the production of beta-lactamase enzymes, such as extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs), which degrade β-lactam antibiotics like penicillins, cephalosporins, and aztreonam, though not cephamycins or carbapenems. Extended-spectrum beta-lactamases are inhibited by β-lactamase inhibitors, such as clavulanic acid, with common producers including E. coli, Klebsiella, P. mirabilis, and P. aeruginosa.24,26

Many GNB isolated from ear infections exhibit multidrug resistance (MDR), complicating treatment options. 23 Genes encoding ESBL production are often located on plasmids that also carry resistance genes for fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. 27

Common factors associated with otitis media include recurrent upper respiratory infections, smoking, frequent earphone use, tonsillitis, allergies, infected house hold contact, and lower education levels.28–31 Understanding the prevalence and risk factors of bacterial ear infections is crucial for addressing antibiotic resistance. Although previous studies in Ethiopia, including in the current study area, have provided useful insights, many were retrospective in nature and limited by small sample sizes and lack of clinical correlation. For instance, earlier retrospective studies from 2009–2012 12 and 2013–2018 3 reported bacterial ear infection rates of 89.5% and 76.7%, respectively. Additionally, a prospective study in 2017 with 62 participants reported a prevalence of 95.2%. 5 A more recent retrospective study conducted in this setting added to the existing evidence; however, its retrospective design similarly lacked detailed clinical and microbiological context. In contrast, the current study employed a prospective approach with a larger sample size and comprehensive clinical and diagnostic data, which strengthens the reliability of the findings and provides updated evidence to inform local treatment guidelines.

This study provided updated data on bacterial causes of ear infections with their antibiotic susceptibility patterns. Thus, the study aimed to determine the prevalence, factors associated with bacterial ear infections and antibiotic susceptibility of the bacterial isolates among patients clinically suspected of having ear infections at the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Northwest Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Study area

This study was conducted at the Ear, Nose, and Throat (ENT) clinic of the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (UoGCSH) in Gondar city, Amhara, Northwest Ethiopia. Gondar is located 747 km from the country’s capital Addis Ababa. It is a major teaching hospital with 977 beds and 29 wards, serving around 13 million people in the region. The hospital provides a wide range of medical services, including internal medicine, surgery, obstetrics and gynecology, pediatrics, ENT care, physiotherapy, dental care, psychiatry, and dermatology, along with specialized units for tuberculosis, cancer, fistula surgery, and palliative care. Gondar town had an estimated population of 487,224 in 2023.

Study design and period

A hospital-based prospective cross-sectional study was conducted from May 2023 to July 2023 at the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital ENT clinic.

Operational definition

Clinically suspected ear infections are characterized by ear pain, hearing loss, ear discharge, and a feeling of fullness or pressure, with fever and irritability. Severe cases, especially inner ear infections, may involve dizziness, balance issues, and tinnitus.32,33 In this study, ear infections include middle and external ear infections.

Acute Suppurative Otitis Media is defined as a rapid onset of signs and symptoms of inflammation in the middle ear, typically with ear pain, fever, and purulent discharge if the tympanic membrane is perforated.

Chronic Suppurative Otitis Media is defined as persistent ear discharge through a perforated tympanic membrane lasting for 2-6 weeks, often accompanied by hearing loss.

Otitis externa is defined as inflammation of the external auditory canal, typically characterized by ear pain, itching, swelling, and discharge, with no perforation of the tympanic membrane.

Frequent earphone use was defined as the use of earphones for more than 1 hour per day on at least 4 days per week. 34

History of recent hospitalization refers to a study participant’s admission to a hospital within the past month of data collection. 35

Chronic tonsillitis is defined as a condition in which a study participant experiences seven or more episodes of acute tonsillitis within a year. 36

Recurrent upper respiratory tract infection defined as having two or more infections affecting the upper respiratory system (like the nose, throat, and sinuses) within six months. 37

To define the upper age limit for children as 14 years based on clinical and epidemiological research, especially for infectious diseases like ear infections; the age range 0–14 years is commonly used as the operational definition of “children.” The reasons include38,39:1. Disease epidemiology: The burden of diseases like otitis media, respiratory infections, and some vaccine-preventable illnesses is significantly higher in those 0–14 years. After this age, the risk profile changes. 38 2. Data consistency: using 0–14 years as a cutoff aligns with international reporting systems (e.g., The World Health Organization and demographic health surveys), allowing for comparability across studies. 39

Population

The source populations are all patients attending the ENT clinic, while the study participants are those with clinical diagnosis of ear infections (external and middle ear infections).

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion and exclusion criteria: all participants clinically suspected of having middle and external ear infections and who volunteer to participate in the study were included; however, Participants who had received antibiotic treatment within the 14 days preceding enrollment were excluded.

Sampling technique

A systematic random sampling method was used to enroll 303 participants. Based on information obtained from the team leader of the ENT clinic, approximately 15 patients clinically suspected of having ear infections visit the clinic daily. Over three months, this totals about 900 patients (15 patients x 20 days x 3 months). The sampling interval was set at 900/303 = 2.97, rounded to 3. The first patient on the initial sampling day was chosen by lottery a random number generator was used, and then every third patient was asked to participate and provide informed consent until the target sample size was reached.

Data collection and laboratory methods

Data collection

Sociodemographic variables such as age, sex, residence, educational status, marital status, and occupational status, along with behavior-related factors like cigarette smoking, family history of smoking, frequent use of earphones, and antibiotic use without a prescription, were collected using a structured questionnaire. The questionnaire was prepared in English, translated into Amharic (the local language), and then back-translated into English by language experts. A pre-test was conducted on five percent of the sample size (15 participants) at Gondar Ayira General Hospital before undertaking the main study to assess the feasibility of the questionnaire. The data for this study were collected by a nurse who received approximately two days of training and had prior experience.

Clinical data including types of ear infections, diabetes mellitus (DM) status, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) status, history of hospitalization, recurrent upper respiratory tract infections (URTI), and history of chronic tonsillitis, was recorded using data collection sheet.

Sample collection and transportation

Ear discharge swab samples were collected aseptically by an experienced nurse and ENT Specialist at the ENT clinic of UOGCSH using a sterile, pre-moistened, cotton-tipped applicator. Swabs were obtained as part of the standard clinical examination, ensuring proper technique based on the patient’s clinical presentation. For patients with tympanic membrane perforation and otitis externa, swabs were taken directly from the ear discharge in the ear canal. For patients with an intact tympanic membrane, a sample was obtained via tympanocentesis by ENT Specialist. Tympanocentesis was performed in 17 patients (5.6%) during the study period. The procedure was selectively undertaken in patients presenting with severe symptoms or those with a documented history of failure of prior antibiotic therapy occurring more than 14 days before enrollment, as determined by the treating clinician. Sterile gloves were worn throughout the procedure, and strict aseptic techniques were followed to prevent contamination. To minimize contamination in cases of otitis externa, the external ear was first cleaned with normal saline (0.85% NaCl) to remove surface debris. 17 During swab collection for otitis externa, the applicator was carefully inserted into the external auditory canal to obtain a sample while avoiding contact with the outer ear structures. All samples were immediately placed in Amie’s transport medium and transported within two hours, 17 to the Medical Microbiology Laboratory at the School of Biomedical and Laboratory Sciences for further analysis.

Isolation and identification of bacterial isolates

Upon arrival at the Microbiology laboratory, samples were inoculated in parallel onto MacConkey agar, 5% sheep Blood agar, and Chocolate agar plates (all sourced from Himedia Company, India). The samples were first streaked onto one quadrant and then spread to other quadrants using a sterile wire loop to obtain pure colonies. To selectively isolate Gram-negative bacilli, MacConkey plates were incubated under ambient aerobic conditions. To ensure the optimal recovery of both fastidious pathogens and Gram-positive bacteria, chocolate and blood agar plates were incubated in a candle jar which can generate about 3–5% carbon dioxide. All media were maintained at 35–37 °C for 18–24 hours. Negative plates for chocolate and blood agar plates were incubated for an additional 18–24 hours. 40

Bacterial isolates were identified through conventional methods including observation of colony morphology, pigment production, hemolysis type on blood agar, then Gram staining and biochemical tests were performed. Identification of Gram-positive bacteria was carried out using catalase testing, coagulase testing, the optochin sensitivity test, the bile solubility test, the bile-esculin test, bacitracin susceptibility testing with 0.04 U. H. influenzae was identified with susceptibility testing 10 U bacitracin discs, oxidase testing, and satellitism testing (SF 2).41,42

For Gram-negative bacilli, colonies from MacConkey agar plates underwent a series of biochemical tests on various media, including triple sugar iron agar, sulfur indole motility medium, Simon’s citrate agar, urease agar, and lysine iron agar. Tests assessed characteristics such as gas production, lactose fermentation, indole production, citrate utilization, urease activity, lysine decarboxylation, oxidase activity, and motility (SF 2). 43

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

Antibiotic susceptibility testing (AST) was performed according to the Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) 2022 guidelines. 44 The Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method was used for non-fastidious isolates on Mueller-Hinton agar plates, while a modified Mueller-Hinton agar with 5% sheep blood was used for fastidious isolates, particularly heated (chocolatized) 5% sheep blood for H. influenzae. A standardized suspension of confirmed isolates was prepared and adjusted to a 0.5% McFarland standard. Using a sterile cotton swab, the suspension was evenly spread on Mueller-Hinton agar for non-fastidious isolates and modified Mueller-Hinton agar for fastidious isolates.

Antibiotic disks were placed on the agar plates, with Mueller-Hinton plates incubated under aerobic conditions and modified Mueller-Hinton plates incubated in a candle jar. All media were maintained at 35–37 °C for 18–24 hours. 44 Zones of inhibition were measured using ruler and categorized as susceptible, intermediate, or resistant. For analysis, intermediate results were grouped with resistant results. Bacterial isolates that were resistant to at least one antibiotic agent in three or more antibiotic classes were considered MDR isolates. 45

The antibiotics tested, as recommended by CLSI, included penicillin (10U), ampicillin (10 μg), oxacillin (1 μg), amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (20/10 μg), ceftriaxone (30 μg), ceftazidime (30 μg), cefotaxime(30 μg), cefoxitin (30 μg)), tetracycline (30 μg), ciprofloxacin (5 μg), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (1.25 + 23.75 μg) gentamicin (10 μg)), erythromycin (15 μg), clindamycin (2 μg), meropenem (10 μg), vancomycin (30 μg) and chloramphenicol (30 μg). 44 All antibiotics were obtained from Abtek Biologicals, Ltd., Liverpool, UK.

Determination of penicillin and methicillin resistance

Penicillin non-susceptibility in S. pneumoniae was determined using oxacillin (1 μg) with a zone of inhibition ≤19 mm. Methicillin resistance in S. aureus and coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS) isolates was assessed using cefoxitin (30 μg), with resistance defined as a zone of inhibition ≤21 mm for S. aureus and ≤24 mm for CoNS. 44

Detection of extended-spectrum beta-lactamase producing-GNB

Double-disc synergy test was applied to detect the ESBL production. A disk of amoxicillin-clavulanate and 3rd generation cephalosporin, namely ceftazidime, cefotaxime and ceftriaxone was used. Briefly, Amoxicillin-clavulanate (30μg) disk was placed at the center of the inoculated Mueller-Hinton agar media, and disks of ceftriaxone (30μg), cefotaxime (30μg) and ceftazidime (30μg) each were placed 15 mm apart from amoxicillin-clavulanate (30μg) followed by incubation at 37°C for 18-24 hours. Any distortion or enhanced in the zone of inhibition of the ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, or ceftazidime discs towards the amoxicillin-clavulanate disc was considered as confirmatory for ESBLs production. 46

Data management and quality control

Data collection was conducted using a pre-tested questionnaire and data collection sheet, with training provided for data collectors. The investigator and supervisors managed the data collection and ensured strict adherence to pre-analytical, analytical, and post-analytical quality assurance measures. Reagents and media were checked for expiry dates and quality. Culture media were prepared following manufacturer instructions, sterilized at 121°C for 15 minutes, and dispensed aseptically. Media sterility was verified by incubating 5% of the batch at 35–37°C overnight to check for contamination.

The performance of the media was confirmed by testing with American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) strains, including P. aeruginosa (ATCC 27853), E. coli (ATCC 25922), S. aureus (ATCC 25923), H. influenzae (ATCC 49247), and S. pneumoniae (ATCC 49619). The potency of antibiotics was monitored using control strains of P. aeruginosa, E. coli, and S. aureus. For the ESBL confirmatory test, K. pneumoniae ATCC 700603 (ESBL-positive) and E. coli ATCC 25922 (ESBL-negative) were used. For MRSA detection, S. aureus (ATCC 25923) was used to check the quality of the commercially purchased antibiotic disks. 44 All laboratory results were analyzed only after ensuring quality control.

Data analysis and interpretation

After being checked for completeness and coding, the data was entered into EPI-data version 4.6. The data was exported to the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27 for further analysis. Bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to evaluate possible factors associated with the prevalence of bacterial ear infections. A p value ≤ 0.2 in bivariate logistic regression was considered for multivariate logistic regression analyses. All variables with p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant in multivariate analyses.

Ethical approval

The Research and Ethical Review Committee of the School of Biomedical and Laboratory Sciences, College of Medicine and Health Sciences, University of Gondar, approved the proposed research (Reference No. SBMLS/487, dated April 4, 2023). Permission was also obtained from the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital. Eligible participants were informed about the study’s purpose, potential benefits, and risks. Written informed consent was obtained from participants, and assent was provided by the children’s guardians. All procedures in this study were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1975), as revised in 2024. 47 The reporting of this study conforms to the STROBE Statement guidelines. 48 All culture-positive and AST results were forwarded to the ENT clinic for treatment and management. Participant information was coded and kept strictly confidential.

Of these, 125 (41.3%) were below 15 years of age, and the remaining 178 (58.7%) were 15 years or older. The majority of the study participants, 77 (25.4%), were between the ages of 5 and 14 years. Prevalence of bacterial ear infection

Out of the 303 specimens analyzed, the overall prevalence of bacterial growth was 48.5% (147/303; 95% CI: 42.8–54.3).

A total of 172 otopathogens were isolated among 147 culture-positive specimens, with 82.9% (n = 122) yielded a single bacterial isolate, whereas 17.1% (n = 25) exhibited bi-bacterial growth. Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacterial (GPB) isolates represented 65.7% (113/172) and 34.3% (59/172) of all bacterial isolates, respectively (Table 3). Discussion

Bacterial ear infections are a common, treatable health issue worldwide. However, if left untreated, they can lead to serious complications such as disabling hearing loss, speech disorders, reduced quality of life, and financial strain on healthcare systems. 49 The burden and prevalence of ear infections are higher in developing countries due to low living standards, poor hygiene, comorbidities, and inadequate nutrition. 50 Therefore, identifying bacterial causes of ear disease and associated factors with reporting AST results are essential for preventing the wide-ranging impacts of ear infections and guiding effective treatment in resource-limited areas like Ethiopia.

In this study, out of 303 samples, 48.5% (147/303) showed bacterial growth, with 82.9% (n=122) being mono-bacterial and 17.1% (n=25) bi-bacterial. This finding aligns with other research indicating that ear infections often involve polymicrobial co-infections.5,15,16,18 However, 51.5% (156/303) of samples showed no bacterial growth, likely due to the absence of anaerobic cultures in this study. Additionally, ear infections are commonly caused not only by bacteria but also by fungi and viruses. Otomycosis is a prevalent fungal infection especially in humid tropical and subtropical regions. 51

In the present study, the overall bacterial culture positivity rate was 48.5% (95% CI: 42.8–54.3). This result is consistent with findings reported in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia (48.5%), 10 Hawassa, Ethiopia (52.1%), 11 and Tanzania (53.3%). 52 However, it is lower than previous studies in Ethiopia, such as those conducted in Gondar (76.7%), 3 (89.5%), 12 and (92.2%) 21 ; Bahir Dar (78.2%) 13 and (80.4%) 14 ; Dessie (82%) 15 ; Mekelle (98.2%) 16 ; Jimma (92.5%) 20 ; Hawassa (75.6%) 17 ; Arba Minch (67.3%) 18 ; and Nekemte (87.6%). 19 Furthermore, the finding of this study is lower than studies conducted elsewhere, in Somalia (88.9%), 25 Nigeria (91%), 53 in Iraq (85.8%), 54 and in Pakistan (80.7%). 55 Possible reasons for this variation could be differences in study participants, study period, climate, and geographical variation.

In our study, GNB were the predominant species isolated from ear infections, accounting for 65.7% (113/172) of cases, while GPB represented 34.3% (59/172). This finding aligns with previous retrospective studies from the same study area, conducted between 2009 and 2012 12 and from 2013 to 2018, 3 which reported GNB as the leading cause of ear infections at rates of 56.4% and 59.1%, respectively.

This result is also consistent with findings from other regions in Ethiopia, including Bahir Dar (58.8%), 14 Dessie (74.2%), 15 Mekelle (56.0%), 16 Hawassa (79.5%), 11 Arba Minch (59.8%), 18 Jimma (56.4%), 20 and Nekemte (53.1%), 19 where GNB were similarly identified as the predominant species isolated from ear discharge. Additionally, this study concurs with reports from other countries, including Somalia, Nigeria, and Malaysia, where GNB predominated in ear infections, with frequencies of 77.3%, 71.6%, and 75.3%, respectively.25,56,57 The high prevalence of GNB infections may stem from their chronic nature, as these bacteria from external sources often access the auditory canal and become dominant, especially in developing countries where poor living standards, hygiene, and inadequate nutrition are common. 50

The predominance of P. mirabilis, P. aeruginosa, and S. aureus in ear infections observed in our study is consistent with previous reports from Ethiopia.5,14,16,17,20 Similar results have been observed in other countries, including Somaliland, 58 Kenya, 59 Angola, 60 Malawi, 61 and Iraq, 54 where P. mirabilis, S. aureus, or P. aeruginosa were the primary isolates. This similarity may be attributed to comparable environmental conditions, hygiene practices, and healthcare settings across these regions.

The presence of other Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, although at lower frequencies, aligns with earlier findings from both local3,17,19,20 and international studies.2,52,58,62–65 These variations in bacterial profiles could be due to differences in study design, sample size, seasonal factors, and geographic location.

Although global reports identify H. influenzae and S. pneumoniae as the most prevalent bacteria responsible for otitis media, 66 our study observed these pathogens at low rates. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in geographic location, study year, patient types, the coverage of pneumococcal conjugate vaccine and H. influenzae type b vaccine, overuse of antimicrobials that may eliminate sensitive organisms while favoring drug-resistant ones, and the biofilm-forming properties of Gram-negative bacilli and S. aureus.9,67

The high levels of antibiotic resistance observed among Gram-positive bacteria in this study are consistent with findings from previous studies in Ethiopia, such as those conducted Mekele, 16 and Arba Minch, 18 as well as reports from other countries including Bangladesh, 68 India, 69 and Côte d'Ivoire. 70 This pattern may be attributed to the widespread use and often inappropriate prescription of antibiotics like tetracycline and penicillin in both healthcare and community settings. The relatively lower resistance rates to antibiotics such as chloramphenicol and clindamycin might be due to their more restricted use, which reduces selective pressure and slows the emergence of resistance. Variations across studies could also reflect differences in local prescribing habits, antibiotic availability, and infection control practices.

Notably, most S. aureus isolates showed resistance to tetracycline (20/24; 83%), penicillin (19/24; 79%), and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (16/24; 67%). This finding is comparable to results from Arba Minch, Ethiopia, 18 where 34.6%, 84.5%, and 73% of S. aureus isolates were resistant to tetracycline, penicillin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, respectively. Similarly, in Nekemte, Ethiopia, 19 77.2%, 93.5%, and 51.1% of S. aureus isolates were resistant to tetracycline, penicillin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, respectively. Additionally, in Hawassa, Ethiopia, 17 85.4% and 73.2% of S. aureus isolates were resistant to penicillin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, respectively.

Notably, most S. aureus isolates showed resistance to tetracycline. This finding is comparable to results from Arba Minch, Ethiopia, 18 where 34.6%, 84.5%, and 73% of S. aureus isolates were resistant to tetracycline, penicillin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, respectively. Similarly, in Nekemte, Ethiopia, 19 77.2%, 93.5%, and 51.1% of S. aureus isolates were resistant to tetracycline, penicillin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, respectively. Additionally, in Hawassa, Ethiopia, 17 85.4% and 73.2% of S. aureus isolates were resistant to penicillin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, respectively.

In this study, S. aureus isolates were susceptible to clindamycin and chloramphenicol, respectively. This is similar to the outcomes reported by Henok et al., 18 where S. aureus isolates were susceptible to clindamycin (88.4%) and chloramphenicol (69.2%); Taddese et al., 17 where they were susceptible to clindamycin (85.4%) and chloramphenicol (85.4%); Hailu et al., 14 where they were susceptible to clindamycin (92.1%) and chloramphenicol (93.6%); and Guteta et al., 19 where they were susceptible to clindamycin (81.5%) and chloramphenicol (68.5%).

This is may be due to in many low- and middle-income countries such as Ethiopia, people often buy inexpensive, easily accessible antibiotics without a prescription, leading to ineffective treatment, resistance, and treatment failure, allowing antibiotic-resistant S. aureus isolates to survive and spread. Contributing factors include poor infection control, inadequate antimicrobial stewardship, and high bacterial loads in hospitals, which discharge resistant bacteria into communities. 71

The high resistance of Gram-negative bacterial isolates to commonly used antibiotics such as tetracycline, ampicillin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, cefoxitin, and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid observed in this study aligns with findings from various regions of Ethiopia, including Gondar, 21 Bahir Dar, 14 Jimma, 20 and Arba Minch, 18 as well as with a broader meta-analysis conducted in sub-Saharan Africa. 50

This widespread resistance likely reflects the extensive and often empirical use of these antibiotics in clinical practice, which facilitates the selection and persistence of resistant strains. Contributing factors may include inappropriate prescribing, over-the-counter availability, poor infection control practices, and limited antimicrobial stewardship programs. The observed regional and temporal differences in resistance rates could be due to variations in antibiotic usage patterns, diagnostic capacities, and local healthcare policies.

In this study, P. mirabilis, the most prevalent Gram-negative isolate, demonstrated high resistance to commonly used antibiotics such as tetracycline, ampicillin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and cefoxitin, while retaining notable susceptibility to meropenem, gentamicin, and cefotaxime. Similarly, P. aeruginosa exhibited substantial resistance to cefoxitin, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, and ciprofloxacin but remained relatively susceptible to meropenem and gentamicin. These resistance patterns are consistent with findings from previous studies in Ethiopia including those from Gondar, 5 Addis Ababa, 10 Bahir Dar,24,72 and Jimma, 73 as well as in other countries such as Iraq,54,74 India, 75 Nigeria, 76 Pakistan, 65 Angola, 60 Saudi Arabia, 2 Ghana,62,63 and Tanzania. 52 This resistance is likely due to the acquisition of antibiotic resistance genes through plasmids and transposons that encode various enzymes and efflux pumps, along with mutations in proteins involved in β-lactamase activity, efflux systems, outer membrane structure, gene replication, protein synthesis, transcription, and biofilm formation. 77 The resistance exhibited by these pathogens can contribute to complications such as hearing loss, chronic otitis media, and extended hospital stays, which increase treatment costs and place a substantial burden on healthcare systems.7,78 Nonetheless, the continued efficacy of meropenem and gentamicin offers valuable therapeutic options for managing these infections.

The overall prevalence of MDR in this study was 64.5% (95% CI; 57.35–71.65). This result aligns with earlier studies conducted in Ethiopia, where MDR rates were reported as 67.0% in Jimma, 20 69.9% in Hawassa, 17 61.5% in Arba Minch 18 and 66.2% in Nekemte. 19 However, it is lower than other studies in Ethiopia that reported rates of 88.3% in Gondar, 21 and 74.5% in Mekele, 16 also, 80% in Tanzania. 79 Conversely, the MDR rate in this study is higher than some other reports from Ethiopia, including Gondar at 35%, 5 45.72%, 3 Bahir Dar at 43%, 24 and Wollo at 50%. 80

The variation in MDR rates across studies may be due to differences in operational definitions of MDR strains, as well as differences in study settings and locations. Misuse of antibiotics is a major factor contributing to the selection of resistant mutants. Additionally, patients with ear infections in the study area are often treated without culture isolation and antibiotic susceptibility testing. Self-prescribing and empirical antibiotic treatment, without consideration for laboratory culture and sensitivity results, could explain the higher prevalence of MDR observed.22,23

In this study, MDR was notably high among K. pneumoniae, S. aureus, and P. aeruginosa isolates. These findings are consistent with global reports highlighting these species as part of the “ESKAPE” group Enterococcus spp., S. aureus, K. pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, P. aeruginosa, and Enterobacter spp., which are recognized for their ability to evade the effects of multiple antibiotics. The high MDR rates observed may be attributed to their intrinsic resistance mechanisms, frequent acquisition of resistance genes, and their adaptability in both hospital and community environments. These pathogens pose a serious public health threat and underscore the urgent need for novel antimicrobial agents and robust antimicrobial stewardship programs to limit further spread and preserve the efficacy of existing treatments. 67

Methicillin resistance was observed in 7 of 24 (29.2%, 95% CI: 11.01–47.39) S. aureus isolates and 3 of 21 (14.3%) CoNS isolates. Our findings are consistent with previous studies from various cities in Ethiopia, which reported MRSA rates of 34.5% in Jimma, 20 17.0% in Hawassa, 17 and 38.4% in Arba Minch. 18 These results are also comparable to international studies, including rates of 18.9% in China, 81 41% and 44.4% in Tanzania,52,82 and 17.0%, 29%, and 30.56% in India.83–85 However, our findings are lower than other studies in Tanzania (59.3%) 79 and India (58.3%, 52.94%)86,87 but higher than a study in India that reported 8.25%. 75 In our study, methicillin-resistant CoNS (3/21, 14.3%) was also detected, aligning with a study by Henok et al., which found that 17.8% (5/28) of CoNS isolates were methicillin-resistant.

The high prevalence of MRSA can be attributed to its widespread presence in both hospital and community settings. As a resident of the skin and mucous membranes, S. aureus is capable of adhering to medical equipment and persisting as a respiratory pathogen due to its adaptability and metabolic flexibility.41,42 It can invade and spread through the eustachian tubes to the middle ear, causing severe infections.75,79

In this study, 17.1% (95% CI; 8.95–25.25) of the 82 GNB isolates were positive for ESBL production. This finding is consistent with prior studies by Henok et al. (19.7%) 18 Endaylalu et al. (7.4%), 24 Dayie et al. (8%), 62 and Khatoon et al. (18.3%). 85 Conversely, our result is lower than reported rates from Nigeria (41%), 88 Tanzania (42%, 34.4%),52,82 and India (62.5%, 74.22%).87,89 This variation may be due to differences in sample size, study year, patient types, and ESBL detection methods. Phenotypic ESBL detection methods follow different guidelines, and the use of varying guidelines could lead to differences in the observed rates of ESBL production across studies. 90

Extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing pathogens identified in this study included K. pneumoniae, E. coli, and P. mirabilis with K. pneumoniae being the most prevalent. These findings are consistent with global concerns, as these species are recognized for their rapid acquisition and dissemination of ESBL-encoding plasmids, making them highly efficient in developing resistance to broad-spectrum β-lactams. Due to their critical role in the spread of antimicrobial resistance and their association with treatment failures, K. pneumoniae, and E. coli have been classified as “priority pathogens” by the World Health Organization. 67 Their presence in ear infections highlights the growing challenge of managing common infections with limited treatment options and emphasizes the importance of early detection and appropriate antibiotic stewardship. The high level of resistance to commonly used antibiotics may be attributed to the widespread local practice of empirical treatment. Therefore, antibiotics with retained activity against predominant isolates should be prioritized, and culture-based therapy should be promoted.

In this study, being under 5 years of age was significantly associated with higher odds of bacterial ear infections (AOR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.01–2.28, p = 0.013).

This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Ethiopia by Wasihun et al. 16 and Guteta et al. 19 The higher prevalence of bacterial ear infections in this age group could be attributed to factors such as low immune status, the shorter and more horizontal structure of their Eustachian tubes, frequent exposure to upper respiratory tract infections, and malnutrition. 2

Furthermore, the current study found that recurrent URTI was significantly associated with higher odds of bacterial ear infections among children (AOR = 3.27, 95% CI: 1.74–11.22, p < 0.001) and adults (AOR = 1.97, 95% CI: 1.16–3.36, p < 0.001). This finding aligns with previous studies conducted in Ethiopia, including in Addis Ababa, 10 Hawassa, 17 and internationally in Somalia 91 and Egypt. 92 The main reason for this association is that bacteria commonly found in ear infections, such as S. pneumoniae and H. influenzae, are also known to cause URTI. Additionally, high asymptomatic carriage rates of bacteria, including S. pneumoniae, S. aureus, H. influenzae, and K. pneumoniae, have been reported in the study area 41 and other parts of Ethiopia. 42 These bacteria reside in the nasopharynx and can reach the middle ear via the Eustachian tube. URTIs disrupt the muco-ciliary defenses of the Eustachian tube, increasing susceptibility to middle ear infections. 93

Moreover, among adults, diabetes mellitus was significantly associated with higher odds of bacterial ear infections (AOR = 2.73, 95% CI: 1.04–4.31, p = 0.032). This strong association is primarily attributed to diabetes being associated with poor circulation and a weakened immune system, resulting from a reduced ability of white blood cells to combat infections in diabetic patients. 94

This study also found that frequent earphone use was significantly associated with bacterial ear infections (AOR = 11.4, 95% CI: 4.6–18.1, p < 0.001). This association may be explained by several mechanisms, including inadequate earphone hygiene, repeated insertion of earphones causing irritation or microtrauma to the external auditory canal, and exposure to bacteria-contaminated surfaces. Earphones may also serve as fomites that facilitate the transfer of microorganisms to the ear canal. 95

However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because the data were self-reported and may be subject to recall bias. In addition, residual confounding from unmeasured behavioral and environmental factors cannot be excluded.

Limitation of the study

Bacterial identification was performed using conventional culture and biochemical methods, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing relied on the disk diffusion technique; serotyping, biofilm detection, and molecular methods for resistance determinants such as MRSA and ESBL were not performed due to limited resources. Anaerobic pathogens were not included because of the challenges associated with their cultivation. In addition, minimum inhibitory concentration testing was not conducted to confirm vancomycin resistance in S. aureus or penicillin non-susceptibility in S. pneumoniae.

As a single-center, hospital-based study, the findings may be subject to referral bias and may not be generalizable to the wider population. Furthermore, reliance on self-reported data introduces potential recall bias. The relatively short study period and possible seasonal variation, as well as regional antibiotic prescribing patterns, may also have influenced the results. The classification of intermediate isolates as resistant may have resulted in a slight overestimation of resistance and MDR prevalence; however, this conservative approach was adopted for epidemiological surveillance and comparative purposes.

Recommendations

These findings highlight the need for targeted antimicrobial stewardship and management of ear infections based on culture and sensitivity results. Policies focused on reducing unnecessary antibiotic use and promoting antimicrobial stewardship are crucial to combatting resistance, especially in the context of ear infections. Further research is also recommended to explore the mechanisms behind the resistance patterns observed, the impact of earphone use on infection rates, and the effectiveness of early interventions for high-risk populations, such as young children and individuals with diabetes mellitus. Future studies incorporating multicenter designs, longer follow-up periods, anaerobic culture techniques, and advanced molecular methods are recommended to provide a more comprehensive understanding.

Conclusion

A substantial proportion of ear discharge samples yielded positive bacterial cultures, with P. mirabilis, P. aeruginosa, and S. aureus identified as the predominant pathogens. High multidrug resistance, methicillin resistance in S. aureus, and extended-spectrum beta-lactamase production in K. pneumoniae, E. coli, and P. mirabilis were observed. Key factors strongly associated with bacterial ear infection included age under 5, diabetes mellitus, frequent earphone use, and recurrent upper respiratory tract infections.
