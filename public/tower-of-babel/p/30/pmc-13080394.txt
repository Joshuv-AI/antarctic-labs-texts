A Novel Mixed-Reality System Has Comparable Accuracy to a Robotic System in Total Knee Arthroplasty
Authors: Catelyn A. Woelfle, Nana O. Sarpong, Alexander L. Neuwirth, Carl L. Herndon, H. John Cooper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMixed-reality total knee arthroplasty (MR-TKA) allows for simultaneous viewing of the surgical field and overlaid data to potentially match the accuracy of robotic-assisted TKA (RA-TKA) but with minimal footprint and open platform compatibility. This study compared the in vivo accuracy of this novel MR-TKA system to a well-established RA-TKA system.MethodsData was collected from 40 TKA cases utilizing an FDA-cleared MR-TKA system and 40 modern imageless RA-TKA cases from June 2024 to February 2025. Deviations from intraoperative planned coronal femoral, tibial, and hip-knee-ankle alignment to actual alignment, measured on postoperative radiographs, were compared. Mean variance values between the thickness of planned and caliper-measured resections were also compared. Operative times and postoperative complications were collected.ResultsMean angular variance in femoral (0.9 vs 0.8°, P = .525), tibial (0.8 vs 0.7°, P = .458), and hip-knee-ankle (1.1 vs 0.8°, P = .078) coronal alignment were not significant between RA-TKA and MR-TKA cohorts, respectively. Mean depth variance in distal femoral (medial 0.6 vs 0.8 mm, P = .083; lateral 0.6 vs 0.8 mm, P = .078), posterior femoral (medial 0.6 vs 0.8 mm, P = .246; lateral 0.7 vs 0.6 mm, P = .464), and tibial (medial 0.6 vs 0.7 mm, P = .482; lateral 0.7 vs 0.7 mm, P = 1.00) resections were not significant. Mean operative time was longer with MR-TKA (107 vs 95 min, P = .002).ConclusionsA novel MR-TKA system provides similar accuracy in both resection depths and component alignment compared to an imageless RA-TKA platform.

Introduction

There has been extensive research exploring ways to improve implant alignment and bony resections to achieve optimal outcomes in total knee arthroplasty (TKA). Recent literature has demonstrated robotic-assisted TKA (RA-TKA) enhances precision in planning, cutting, and positioning implants through live intraoperative data, including gap balancing [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. RA-TKA has shown to better restore knee function and improve component survivorship and patient satisfaction [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. While there is continued debate on ideal alignment philosophy [[9], [10], [11]], it remains important for the surgeon to be able to accurately achieve the desired alignment. From an economic standpoint, some studies suggest RA-TKA may be associated with improvements in select short-term episode-of-care metrics, such as hospital length of stay, readmission and revision rates, and likelihood of an ambulatory procedure; however, existing evidence remains heterogeneous, and definitive long-term cost-effectiveness and clinical superiority over conventional TKA have not been established [12]. According to the 2024 American Academy of Orthopedic Surgeons American Joint Replacement Annual Report, RA-TKA popularity has steadily increased over the last decade and grown to over 15.9% of all TKA procedures as of 2023 [13] and is projected to continue to increase in coming years.

Extended reality is an umbrella term that includes all immersive technologies that blend the digital and physical worlds, including virtual, augmented, and mixed reality, each of which offer different ways and levels of world interaction. While virtual reality fully replaces the real world with a digital experience, augmented reality overlays digital content onto the real world that cannot be interacted with. Mixed reality is a middle ground between the 2, offering a combination of the real and digital worlds where digital content is overlaid onto the real world but can also be interacted with in real time [14].

Extended reality has been recently discussed as an enhanced version of RA-TKA. The application and potential advantages of extended reality in hip and knee arthroplasty are actively being investigated. Extended reality offers economic benefits by reducing the amount of equipment, imaging, and machinery, lowering the upfront costs for hospitals. This can lead to potentially reducing turnover and preparation times and equips the surgeon with the ability to maintain focus on the surgical field [14].

Augmented reality, specifically, allows environmental information to become digitally overlaid onto the real world, such as intraoperative gap assessment of the knee onto the surgical field. While data is limited, literature on augmented-reality TKA (AR-TKA) has demonstrated consistent accuracy and precision in bony resection and coronal component alignment, suggesting it may outperform conventional techniques [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. However, while AR-TKA can provide the surgeon real-time resection visualization, it lacks the ability RA-TKA has of providing live intraoperative assessments and adjustments to the surgical plan and lacks the ability to track both the femur and the tibia in real time.

Mixed reality, on the other hand, blends the virtual and physical environments and also allows surgeons to interact and manipulate the digital field while actively assessing the surgical field [14]. Mixed-reality TKA (MR-TKA), similar to RA-TKA, allows the surgeon to alter their surgical plan intraoperatively based on live measurements, including gap balancing, and computed data. There are several early studies on mixed-reality that have been published in orthopedics [20], particularly total hip arthroplasty [[21], [22], [23]]. MR-TKA offers simultaneous viewing of the surgical field and overlaid data to potentially match the precision and accuracy of RA-TKA but with minimal footprint and an open platform, allowing surgeons their choice of TKA component manufacturer. There has only been one case study on MR-TKA to date, which described the successful use of the technology in a single patient [24]. The present study investigates the accuracy of a novel MR-TKA platform and compares it to a well-studied imageless RA-TKA system. The objective of our study is to compare the concordance between planned vs actual bony resection measurements and postoperative alignment from both systems and hypothesize they will demonstrate comparable accuracy.

Material and methods

Study population

This study focused on our institution's initial experience utilizing a MR-TKA system (STELLAR Knee, Polaris, Miami, FL) from 2 fellowship-trained arthroplasty surgeons. The surgeons were proficiently trained in a series of simulated labs on how to utilize the MR system, and the first 10 MR-TKA cases of each surgeon were excluded from the study to compensate for a potential early learning curve period. Regarding the RA-TKA platform, both surgeons were well beyond their learning curves having performed hundreds of RA-TKA procedures and having developed a mature workflow prior to the start of the study. All MR-TKA cases from June 2024 to February 2025 were included in the study. The most recent cohort of patients within the same time period (September 2024-February 2025) who underwent RA-TKA using a well-established commercially available system (ROSA, Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN) was chosen for comparison. Baseline demographics, including age, gender, and body mass index (BMI), were analyzed to ensure the cohorts were well-matched. This study was approved by our institutional review board (IRB-AAAV7263).

Implants were standardized in all cases with the same knee implant from a single manufacturer (Persona Knee System, Zimmer Biomet). All procedures in both cohorts were performed through a midline incision and a medial parapatellar approach. Prior to attaching either system's navigation trackers, the anterior and posterior cruciate ligaments, medial and lateral menisci, and all osteophytes were removed.

Robotic system

The ROSA Knee (Zimmer Biomet) RA-TKA technique has been well-described in previous literature [25]. ROSA is a collaborative robotic navigational system, typically used in the imageless planning option, and consists of 2 main components. The robotic unit includes a touchscreen and a robotic arm, which are positioned on the same side of the operating knee and opposite the optical unit that consists of an infra-red camera and screen (Fig. 1a).Figure 1Robotic-assisted knee arthroplasty (a) setup and (b) planning screen from the robot's monitor.

Robotic-assisted knee arthroplasty (a) setup and (b) planning screen from the robot's monitor.

Once 2 navigational trackers are attached to the femur and tibia, there are 16 distinct landmarking points captured, beginning with the femoral head center by hip circumduction. The ROSA Knee registration pointer is then used to register the femoral and tibial articular surfaces and the medial and lateral distal malleoli. Gap laxity is assessed in live values by applying varus and valgus stress in both extension and flexion. After the preoperative knee state is determined, the surgeon can begin planning and adjusting several parameters (Fig. 2a). Once the plan is finalized, the robotic arm automatically positions itself in the surgical field to prepare for resections. The robotic arm then switches to collaborative mode, allowing the surgeon to guide and secure the cutting jig to the desired position while maintaining correct alignment and depth for resection. A conventional bone saw is used to make the distal femur, proximal tibia, and femoral 4-in-1 cuts. Each cut can be validated using the validation tool. Lastly, the knee's final state of range of motion and gap balance are assessed with trial components before the definitive components are implanted.Figure 2Mixed-reality total knee arthroplasty (a) setup with the surgeon wearing the headset and the 2 navigational trackers attached to the femur and tibia, and (b) planning screen seen by the surgeon through the system's headset.

Mixed-reality system

The STELLAR Knee is an FDA-cleared MR-TKA platform and includes a small tray of sterilizable reusable instrumentation, disposable screws, 4 disposable tracking instruments, and the head-mounted device (HoloLens2, Microsoft: Redmond, WA) (Fig. 1b). There are 2 navigational femoral and tibial arrays that allow for simultaneous tracking: a “Stylus” for point-based data collection, and a “Fin” for plane-based data collection. The platform provides 3D intraoperative guidance by showing measured and computed data on a holographic panel overlaid directly over the surgical field which can be moved freely using built-in hand tracking. The surgeon can control the workflow with their choice of voice commands and pressing holographic buttons on the panel, maintain sterility without the need for additional draping or assistance.

There are 14 distinct points captured during landmarking. Femoral head center is assessed by the femoral array, capturing multiple points during hip circumduction. The Stylus can “paint” the distal femur which records a series of points in space as the Stylus is moved over the articular surface and captures the most distal point for planning. The rest of the femoral and tibial landmarks are point-captured using the Stylus, through a very similar workflow as the RA-TKA system described earlier. After landmarking is complete, preoperative alignment and gap laxity are evaluated through live measurements displayed on a holographic panel directly over the joint as the surgeon takes the knee through a range of motions and applies varus and valgus stresses. The surgeon can then move to planning, which can be optimized on a per-user basis with pre-set preferences such as femoral flexion, tibial slope, and alignment angles. From there, the surgeon can adjust case-specific values to achieve optimal resection depth and alignment (Fig. 2b).

Once the plan is finalized, a proprietary Universal Resection Guide (URG) is attached to the bone with the cut block. The Fin's metal feet are inserted into the cut block's bone saw slot, which displays real-time alignment and resection depth values. Three precision dials on the URG are adjusted, navigating the URG until the Fin values align with the planned values (Fig. 3). The cut block is then pinned into place, and the Fin is used again to validate the pinned values. The distal femoral and proximal tibial cuts are performed, respectively, and postresection values can be validated with the Fin. The MR-TKA is an open platform system, and thus, the femur can be sized with the chosen implant manufacturers' instruments and validated using the Fin before the posterior femoral cut. Primary outcome

Our primary outcome was the absolute value of the difference between planned and measured coronal alignment. Operative reports included planned femoral, tibial, and overall hip-knee-ankle (HKA) alignment, which was devised from intraoperative bony landmarking, soft-tissue evaluation, and surgeon modification of desired implant alignment.

Actual alignment was measured on a HKA lower-extremity standing postoperative radiograph by an independent, blinded observer to limit potential bias. To measure HKA alignment, a plumb line was drawn from the center of the femoral head to the center of the knee joint and from the center of the knee joint to the center of the ankle joint. HKA alignment was defined as the angle between the 2 lines, and neutral alignment was defined as 0°. Femoral component alignment was calculated from the lateral distal femoral angles and medial proximal tibial angles, with 90° defined as neutral. Varus and valgus for all 3 alignments were determined as the degrees deviated from neutral and were rounded to the nearest 0.5°.

The RA-TKA system used in the current study tracks live cut values during each resection. If the live value for coronal alignment exceeds a value over 2 degrees from the planned value, the system reports this as an error and prompts the surgeon to reposition to ensure all values fall within the acceptable range [26]. Thus, we defined our acceptable measured deviation from planned alignment as ≤2.0°. The percentage of outliers outside of the acceptable deviation was calculated.

Secondary outcomes

Our secondary outcome was the absolute value of the difference between planned and measured bony resections. Planned resections were devised and recorded similarly to alignment. Actual resections were collected from intraoperative datasheets, which reported intraoperative caliper measurements of medial and lateral distal femoral, proximal tibial, and posterior condylar resections to the nearest 0.1 mm.

Similarly, as described earlier, the RA-TKA system will report an error if the live cut value for a resection depth exceeds a value over 2 mm from the planned value [26]. The acceptable measured deviation from the planned value was ≤2.0 mm. The percentage of outliers outside of the acceptable deviation was calculated.

Additional secondary outcomes were postoperative complications collected from office records as well as operative time, which was collected from intraoperative anesthesia reports. Operative time was defined as the time from case start to case finished (wound closed).

Statistical analyses

The primary outcome was mean variance in planned vs actual postoperative coronal alignment. In a recent literature review and meta-analysis from 2024, Zaidi et al. reported an average variance in intraoperative planned vs postoperative radiographic alignment to be within 0.61-1.87° (0.97 ± 1.34°) from the same RA-TKA system used in the current study [27]. Assuming a maximum standard deviation of 1.34°, a power analysis using the T statistic revealed needing a total population of 80 patients, 40 per technology group, to achieve 90% power with a 0.05 significance level to detect a 1° difference in coronal alignment.

Mean planned vs measured difference in femoral, tibial, and HKA alignment were compared between MR-TKA and RA-TKA cohorts using independent samples t-tests. Mean planned vs measured difference in medial and lateral bony resections for the distal femoral, proximal tibial, and posterior condylar cuts were also compared between cohorts using independent samples t-tests. Percentage of outliers for both alignment and resections were compared using Chi-squared tests. Operative time was also compared between cohorts using independent samples t-tests. All analyses were conducted in IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) Statistics software version 29.0.1.1.

Operative time

There was a statistically significant difference in operative time between MR-TKA (mean = 107 min, SD = 16 min) and RA-TKA (Mean = 95 min, SD = 17 min), t(78) = 3.25, P = .002. The minimum and maximum times were 75 and 143 min for MR-TKA and 64 and 128 min for RA-TKA.

Postoperative complications

We observed few postoperative complications in both cohorts. Two patients, one in each cohort, experienced postoperative wound drainage and were treated nonoperatively with local wound care and oral antibiotics. There was one patient in the RA-TKA cohort with superficial surgical site infection from a suture abscess 4.5 weeks postoperatively, who was also successfully treated nonoperatively with wound care and oral antibiotics.

One patient in the RA-TKA cohort experienced traumatic wound dehiscence from a mechanical fall, seemingly unrelated to the surgical technique, which required irrigation and re-closure in the emergency department. One patient in the MR-TKA cohort required a manipulation under anesthesia for arthrofibrosis approximately 7 weeks following primary TKA. No patients in either cohort underwent readmission or reoperation.

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that a novel MR-TKA platform has comparable accuracy and precision for both resection depth and coronal alignment when compared to a contemporary imageless RA-TKA system. The average planned vs actual measured femoral, tibial, and HKA coronal alignment were in 0.1° of difference between the RA-TKA and MR-TKA cohorts, with results also similar to literature with the same RA-TKA system. One study reported an average difference in planned vs measured HKA alignment of 1.2 ± 1.1° [28], whereas our study found HKA alignment differences of 1.1° in the RA-TKA and 0.8° in the MR-TKA cohorts, highlighting the prospective promise of the technology in future development. Likewise, the average planned vs actual measured distal and posterior condylar, as well as proximal tibial, resections between the 2 cohorts were within 0.1-0.2 mm of difference between the cohorts both medially and laterally. The same RA-TKA system has shown to have high accuracy in executing planned vs actual caliper measured resection depths, with results very similar to the results of both RA-TKA and MR-TKA systems found in the current study. For example, Gamie et al. found average medial and lateral distal femoral resection depths of 0.7 ± 0.6 mm and 0.9 ± 1.2 mm with RA-TKA, respectively [29], whereas our averages were 0.6 mm medially and 0.8 mm laterally for both RA-TKA and MR-TKA cohorts. This study is the first known study to compare both resection depth and coronal alignment accuracy in MR-TKA to RA-TKA and presents groundwork for the future of MR-TKA. These findings should be interpreted as demonstrating technical equivalence rather than clinical superiority, as neither MR-TKA nor RA-TKA has been definitively proven to improve long-term outcomes compared with conventional TKA.

The novel MR-TKA system studied here is most similar to RA-TKA than any other computer-assisted or AR system due to its ability to simultaneously track the femur and tibia and provides live intraoperative assessment of data that requires input from both bones including alignment, range of motion, and soft-tissue laxity. The compartment gap measurements of soft-tissue laxity that can be measured during the pre-resection assessment phase afford the opportunity to make precise modifications to the planned resection depths and angles. Gap balancing has shown to improve outcomes such as coronal alignment, pain, instability, and patient-related outcome measures [[30], [31], [32], [33], [34]]. Furthermore, RA-TKA has shown to provide balanced gaps in both extension and flexion in 94% of cases, whereas only 80% of gaps were balanced in conventional TKA [4], illustrating the importance of technology to improving gap balancing and overall outcomes. In addition, RA-TKA has shown to improve resection accuracy, clinical outcomes, and survivorship [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]], making the comparison to MR-TKA essential given their similarities.

MR-TKA provides all the advantages of RA-TKA while addressing cost, footprint, efficiency, and vendor concerns that remain unaddressed with RA-TKA. While these logistical and economic considerations may influence adoption, high-level comparative outcomes data between advanced technologies and conventional TKA remain limited. The significant capital investment and recurring annual maintenance costs of RA-TKA technology are well-established but are often weighed against the net positive gain for both the hospital and the patient from improved clinical outcomes and fewer reoperations in support of RA-TKA. Furthermore, from an operating room (OR) standpoint, the size and availability of the technology, in addition to the numerous trays, increase clutter and turnover time between cases. Prior literature reported the RA-TKA system in the present study to cost $700,000, with a spatial footprint of >1 m2 [35].

The MR-TKA system used in the current study has a single small headset, approximated to have a footprint of 0.4 m2 [36], which is easily portable and replaceable if it malfunctions, and comprises a single instrumentation tray, with only 4 disposable trackers. Although the applicability to specific OR settings will vary among institutions, Goldberg et al. found that single-use instruments could save $994 per case, due to the cost savings from tray sterilizations, and could accommodate saving time for an additional full procedure on 51% of OR days due to the reduced turnover time [37]. There is also no capital investment or maintenance cost for the MR-TKA headset, as the headset remains owned by the manufacturer and has a per-case cost model. Furthermore, the MR-TKA system is open-platform and compatible with any implant manufacturer, removing the burden of having to commit to shifting vendors, and affords the ability for the surgeon to focus on the surgical field by having the hologram panel overlaid onto the knee. Therefore, if MR-TKA can optimize these concerns while also matching benefits and outcomes, then it has strong potential for success in emerging surgical technological innovation.

While MR-TKA and RA-TKA are more alike than different regarding their platforms, there are a few key mechanical differences that are important to describe. As described previously, the RA-TKA system used in the current study has a robotic arm with a cutting guide that automatically moves to the surgical field in correct alignment after planning. In contrast, the MR-TKA system relies on manually pinning a URG. While the MR-TKA system can accurately align the resection guide through live values on the panel hologram while adjusting the guide, the guide is subsequently pinned into place, and the stability of the pinned guides may not be as robust as the mechanical stability the robotic arm provides, allowing room for error especially when pin fixation is poor or when using bone saws that offer greater maneuverability. We also found the MR-TKA system increased operative time by a mean of 12 min, although this may be confounded by a persistent learning curve. While we excluded the first 10 MR-TKA cases performed by each surgeon, it remains a novel technology that had been utilized much less than the RA-TKA system, so the inherent learning curve to proficiently utilize the platform remains a confounding factor on surgical time when compared to the mature workflows of RA-TKA.

A strength of the current study is the direct comparison on the performance of 2 cutting-edge technologies in TKA: one that has been well-established showing improved outcomes [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]] and another that is a novel technological innovation with some notable potential advantages in cost and portability. Our study also evaluated both resection depth accuracy and coronal alignment, which provided comprehensive comparison of each platform's ability to achieve optimal outcomes and their workflows. Being a preliminary study of a new system, our study affords the ability to provide real-time insight to the advantages and limitations of system.

The authors recognize the limitations of the study. Although our power analysis indicated our sample size was acceptable to report statistical significance, the sample size was limited given these were within the first 150 cases done following FDA clearance of the technology. Likewise, we were unable to extensively evaluate long-term software system stability and consistency or surgeon interface and ergonomics. We were also unable to evaluate the true cost-effectiveness or conduct a cost-analysis given the limited information available. All measurements taken by calipers or radiographs are also subject to human error, although this error should be similar between groups. Larger population studies where the MR-TKA system is used by more surgeons or, compared to other RA-TKA platforms, would make our results more generalizable. In addition, our study focused on the technical abilities of the systems and did not assess longer-term outcomes such as patient-related outcome measures and component survivorship. As a comparative evaluation of 2 advanced, emerging technologies, this study does not address whether either system improves outcomes relative to conventional TKA, and conclusions should be limited to comparative accuracy and workflow characteristics rather than clinical superiority.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the novel MR-TKA system demonstrates promising preliminary results in the accuracy and precision of resection depth and alignment when compared to a well-established RA-TKA system. Future, larger studies should continue to evaluate the accuracy of the MR-TKA as the technology involves and compare it to other computer and robotic systems available.
