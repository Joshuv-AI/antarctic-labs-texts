Surveillance is not safety: a response to Dewa and colleagues’ paper about passive remote monitoring technology (Oxevision)
Authors: Hat Porter, Bethan Mair Edwards
Published: 2026

Introduction

Technological advances are shifting the landscape of healthcare provision, and mental healthcare is no exception. Whilst technology can improve patient care, it is vital that consideration is given to the perspectives of staff and patients about its implementation in real-world contexts. As mental health service survivors, we therefore welcome Dewa et al.’s research, ‘A service evaluation of passive remote monitoring technology for patients in a high-secure forensic psychiatric hospital: a qualitative study’ published in BMC Psychiatry 23:946 [1]. As Dewa et al. note, it is the first paper examining the perspectives of staff and patients about remote monitoring technology in mental health settings – an important and timely subject matter. On reading the paper we were however surprised to note significant omissions relating to claims about the safety and legality of Oxevision – the product name for the ‘passive remote monitoring technology’ being researched. We take the opportunity in this Matters Arising piece to outline these omissions, underpinning these concerns with relevant evidence.

Background: oxevision’s functionality

Oxevision is a device developed by Oxehealth which incorporates a camera and infrared sensor [2]. The equipment is located in patients’ bedrooms or seclusion rooms and visually records the patient at all times. It provides staff the ability to remotely view an image of the patient and manually request spot checks of their pulse and respiration rate [2]. Oxevision does not alert staff to abnormal pulse readings and can only take vital signs when the patient is not moving [3].

Oxevision’s safety profile

Firstly, we wish to highlight that Dewa et al. have not adequately addressed the potential harms of Oxevision as a bedroom-based surveillance technology [4], instead portraying it as a safe device which supports patient safety. For example, when introducing Oxevision, they describe it as a “medical device that supports staff to monitor patient activity remotely to improve patient safety”. Since the article's publication, a systematic review has identified a lack of robust evidence investigating the impact of Oxevision on patient safety, including unintended harms and adverse effects and events [5, 6] in addition to notable methodological limitations [6].

Significantly, Dewa et al. do highlight an incident whereby Oxevision appears to have failed to detect deterioration in a patient’s condition prior to their death: “a few patients described how a person had died in their beds despite having Oxehealth, and that Oxehealth had not detected the patient deterioration or prevented their death”. This is an important finding and greater consideration could have been given to this in their discussion, particularly in light of other concerns associated with Oxevision, described in a Prevention of Future Deaths (PFD) report [7]. This PFD report outlines that patient safety was compromised by staff’s over reliance on Oxevision, including failures to respond to alerts and the use of Oxevision “not just as an adjunct to face-to-face observations, but instead of them”. Surprisingly, Dewa et al. actively recommend a trial of Oxevision that replaces in person checks – which even the manufacturers state the technology should not be used for [8]. We suggest this conclusion is both unsupported by the data and potentially harmful.

Whilst cursory reference is made to the role of Oxevision in exacerbating paranoia and anxiety; a patient’s attempts to cover the camera; and patients feeling that the camera was intrusive, Dewa et al. do not provide sufficient discussion of these as potential and possibly unanticipated psychological harms. This is consistent with a tendency for mental health research, policy and practice to implement restrictions (such as surveillance technologies) on patients in the name of ‘safety’, despite the psychological harms and distress these may cause [9]. By neglecting this aspect of patient safety, Dewa et al.’s notion of patient safety appears to be one-dimensional, relating to physical safety, as opposed to a more holistic approach inclusive of psychological harms.

Transparency about Oxevision’s camera

Dewa et al.’s research reveals that many patients were either unaware of the presence of a camera in their bedrooms or may have been provided with incorrect or incomplete information about its functionality by staff. For example, the authors state, “most staff reported that they understood why patients felt they had no privacy, and they did their best to maintain it […] [by] trying to reassure the patients that the camera is not recording them all the time”. This is a significant misrepresentation of the functions of Oxevision which does indeed record at all times and allows video footage to be saved and used as part of investigations or legal proceedings [10]. A failure from staff (knowingly or unknowingly) to be transparent about the presence of a continuously recording camera in patients’ bedrooms has significant ethical and data protection implications [11]. Although Dewa et al. do highlight that there was a lack of knowledge and understanding about Oxevision amongst both staff and patients, they do not discuss the legal and ethical implications of staff providing incorrect or incomplete information to patients, or harms this may have posed.

Oxevision’s legality

Whilst a brief overview of the legal basis for Oxevision is provided by Dewa et al. in their introduction, this makes broad and unreferenced claims about the “rigorous consideration and assessment of data protection and privacy in line with legislation”. We were surprised that Dewa et al. did not take this opportunity to acknowledge the legal issues surrounding the use of vision-based monitoring technologies. For example, the British Institute of Human Rights [12] outline concerns that the use of such technologies may pose an infringement to patients’ human rights under multiple Articles, including Article 8, the right to respect for private and family life, home and correspondence; and Article 3, the right to be free from torture and inhuman and degrading treatment. Dewa et al. did not seek ethical approval for their research, describing it as a service evaluation, however this does not absolve their responsibility to consider the ethical and legal implications of their research and findings.

In 2023, NHS England wrote to NHS mental health trusts to request that they review their use of Oxevision [13, 14]. This followed campaigning from mental health service users and survivors [15] who identified that a number of NHS mental health trusts (including Dewa et al.’s study site [10]) were using Oxevision without the explicit, informed consent of patients. The letter, dated 7th September 2023, emphasises the need for Oxevision to be used with patient consent and questions the legality of its use if patients have objected [13, 14].

In this context it is surprising that the issue of consent, and associated data processing, was not discussed further by Dewa et al., despite their data identifying that some patients “had a problem with Oxehealth and would not want it if they had a choice”. Whilst consent was obtained from participants to participate in Dewa et al’s study, their own data and analysis raises concerns about the consent process for the use of Oxevision at the investigation site as part of routine care. The legal and ethical implications of this for Dewa et al’s study (i.e. by exploring experiences of a technology that some patients do not appear to have provided explicit informed consent for) are not adequately acknowledged or discussed.

Minimising patient concerns about Oxevision

Despite stating that their research takes a phenomenological approach, which must be guided by the meanings participants ascribe to their experiences [16], this approach does not appear to have guided Dewa et al.’s conclusions. For example, the article’s abstract states, “Oxehealth technology was considered acceptable to both staff and patients if the technology was used to detect deterioration and improve patient’s safety providing patient’s privacy was not invaded”. However, data extracts reported appear to show that many participants had significant concerns about the technology and did describe Oxevision as invading their privacy, including by allowing them to be viewed by staff in a state of undress or when engaged in prayer. Therefore, we suggest that this conclusion is not a true representation of participant experiences, as evidenced by the data reported, and minimises participants’ concerns.

Conclusion

In our response to Dewa et al., we argue that in the absence of robust evidence to suggest Oxevision benefits patient safety, their paper lacks adequate consideration of the device’s potential harms and safety implications. We suggest that Dewa et al. do not sufficiently address the legal issues of recording patients without their informed consent or knowledge. Finally, we suggest that the conclusions drawn are misaligned with the data presented and appear to minimise and understate participants’ concerns about Oxevision. Whilst we welcome future consideration of patient and staff perspectives of technological innovations, this must be conducted with responsibility, rigour and awareness of ethical and legal issues.
