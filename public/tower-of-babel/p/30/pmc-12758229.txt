Aggregate fragmentation: the ticket to aggrephagy
Authors: Mario Mauthe, Harm Kampinga, Fulvio Reggiori
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOur recent study identifies a previously unrecognized requirement for protein aggregate fragmentation as a prerequisite for autophagic clearance of amorphous aggregates, a process that has been termed aggrephagy. We show that aggregate fragmentation depends on two distinct but cooperative components: the DNAJB6 (DnaJ heat shock protein family (Hsp40) member B6)-HSPA/HSP70 (heat shock protein family A (Hsp70))-HSPH1/HSP110 chaperone module and the 19S regulatory particles (RPs) of the proteasome. These factors act together to not only to fragment protein aggregates but also to compact them, enabling clustering of selective autophagy receptors (SARs) and subsequent local phagophore formation. Our results show that this fragmentase activity plays a role in the aggrephagic clearance of different aggregate species, including disease-related HTT (huntingtin) aggregates.Abbreviations: CLPB-caseinolytic peptidase B protein homolog; DNAJB6-DnaJ heat shock protein family (Hsp40) member B6; dualPIM-dual-particles induced by multimerization; ER-endoplasmic reticulum; HSPA/HSP70-heat shock protein family A (Hsp70); HTT-polyQ119-huntingtin with an expanded polyglutamine stretch of 119 units; RP-regulatory particle; SAR-selective autophagy receptor

Using a chemically inducible dual-particles induced by multimerization (dualPIM) aggregation system, we demonstrated that large cytoplasmic aggregates cannot be engulfed as whole by lysosomes. Instead, live-cell imaging and immune-electron tomography revealed a piecemeal process where fragments bud off from aggregates before being individually delivered for lysosomal degradation. Pharmacological inhibition or siRNA-mediated depletion of HSPA/HSP70, HSPH1/HSP110, or DNAJB6 block fragmentation, causing aggregates to persist and coalesce into large cytoplasmic inclusions [1]. Surprisingly, the 19S RPs, which contain the ubiquitin binding, the deubiquitinase and the AAA+ ATPase activity of the proteasome, but not the proteolytic 20S core particles of the proteasome, are also essential for this process of fragmentation. Knockdown of 19S RP subunits impairs fragmentation and autophagic clearance without relying on proteasomal proteolysis or ubiquitin-binding activity. Immuno-electron tomography reveals that loss of either DNAJB6 or 19S RP results in less compact inclusions and, despite SAR recruitment, phagophore initiation is absent. This suggests that aggregate compaction and SAR clustering, rather than SAR recruitment per se, are required to trigger autophagosome biogenesis (Figure 1). Thus, we showed for the first time that a chaperone module, proteasomal component and autophagy act in concert to clear protein aggregates. Puromycin-induced aggregates, which contain defective ribosomal products, and disease-related mutant HTT (huntingtin) with an expanded polyglutamine stretch of 119 units (HTT-polyQ119) inclusions also rely on the DNAJB6-HSPA/HSP70-HSPH1/HSP110 module and the 19S RPs for efficient clearance. Like for the dualPIM reporter, life imaging revealed how puromycin-induced aggregates undergo fragmentation before lysosomal delivery, a process that is also inhibited upon blocking the fragmentase machinery. In HTT-polyQ119-expressing cells, depletion of fragmentase components increases both the number and size of inclusions, as well as sodium dodecyl sulphate/SDS-insoluble aggregate material. HTT-polyQ proteins form pre-amyloid, gel-like structures before maturing into amyloids. Because HTT-polyQ amyloids cannot be cleared by autophagy, we speculate that the fragmentase does not act on the amyloid part of the HTT-polyQ inclusions but rather on the gel-like pre-amyloid precursors, promoting their autophagic degradation and thus delaying the formation of amyloid inclusions. Our hypothesis is that the fragmentase, which we have described, is a general fragmentase for amorphous aggregates and thus is distinct from, yet analogous to, other DNAJ-HSPA/HSP70-based machines that can fragment amyloids in vitro without the involvement of other AAA+ ATPases, but rather by HSPA/HSP70-mediated entropic pulling.

Interestingly, in yeast and bacteria/mitochondria, their respective HSPA/HSP70 modules also synergize with pulling forces generated by AAA+ ATPases such as Hsp104 and CLPB (caseinolytic peptidase B protein homolog) respectively, to form active disaggregases. Based on this, we infer that the major contribution of the 19S RPs in aggregate fragmentation is the force provided by its AAA+ ATPase activity. It is important to note that Hsp104 and CLPB extract single proteins from aggregates, which differs from what we detect here, i.e., the breaking of aggregates into smaller pieces. The precise mechanism of how 19S RPs facilitate the fragmentation remains to be determined. A possible explanation is that the 19S RPs are pulling on single proteins within aggregates and thereby weakening the structure of the aggregates that, together with the chaperone module action, facilitates the fragmentation. However, other scenarios are possible, and additional studies are required to solve this mystery.

Moreover, it is also unknown how the 19S RPs are recruited onto aggregates. In fact, we showed 19S RP association with the aggregate is DNAJB6-independent. Ubiquitination and SARs are also very likely not involved in this recruitment because inhibition of E1 enzymes does not block aggregate fragmentation. Therefore, future studies are needed to identify which factors, proteins and/or post-translational modifications, plays a role in 19S RP association with aggregates.

A very intriguing observation is that without fragmentation, aggregates coalesce into inclusions that are not degraded even if autophagy is functional. In the absence of the ATG machinery or the SARs, however, the fragmented aggregates do not form inclusions, which might be due to differences in the biophysical properties of the aggregates after fragmentation. It might also suggest that the fragmentase components are not only required for breaking and compacting aggregates into smaller pieces but also keep them in a “ready-to-be-degraded” state, unable to coalesce. Future studies on this difference may provide insights into to the molecular mechanism of cargo sequestration during selective types of autophagy.

Conceptually, our results shift the aggrephagy model from a simple “tag and engulf” paradigm to a multistep process, involving mechanical compaction and fragmentation of aggregates before their being conveyed for degradation. The fragmentation or fission, SAR clustering and piecemealing of large substrates before cargo sequestration within autophagosomes and autophagic degradation appears to be a conserved mechanism, as it has also been reported for selective types of autophagy such as mitophagy and ERphagy/reticulophagy (Figure 1). Similar to organellophagy processes, the piecemealing of aggregates or inclusions may favor a more efficient removal and it would prevent dysfunctional and potentially harmful material dispersal within the cell causing more damage.

Therapeutically, our results suggest that the exclusive induction of autophagy may be insufficient for clearing disease-associated inclusions. That is, activation of the fragmentase may be necessary to prime aggregates to make them a better substrate for aggrephagy. Even though we showed that overexpression of components of the fragmentase before aggregate formation can delay the formation of HTT-polyQ inclusions, it remains to be determined whether their expression or activation in tissues that already display inclusions, which is the most frequent situation in patients, can have a similar beneficial effect.
