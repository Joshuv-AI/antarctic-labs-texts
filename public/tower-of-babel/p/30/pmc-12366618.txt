Comparative performance of deep learning architectures for diabetic peripheral neuropathy detection using corneal confocal microscopy: a retrospective single-centre study
Authors: Wenqu Chen, Yuyang Deng, Weihuang Xu, Jianzhang Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aims to develop a deep learning algorithm (DLA) using the InceptionV3 architecture for effective diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) screening via corneal confocal microscopy (CCM) images.DesignRetrospective study.SettingOphthalmology Centre of General Hospital.Participants127 participants enrolled: 33 healthy participants, 57 diabetic patients with DPN (DPN+) and 37 diabetic patients without DPN (DPN−).InterventionsNot applicable.Main outcome measuresThe CCM image dataset, which was collected from participants (with five images per eye), was randomly divided into training, validation and test subsets in a 7:1:2 ratio. Introduction

Diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN), a late-stage complication of both type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus,1 2 significantly contributes to lower-limb amputations and imposes severe neuropathic pain on individuals with diabetes.3 It significantly affects the well-being and longevity of individuals affected by diabetes.4 Therefore, early diagnosis of DPN is crucial to reduce mortality rates and improve patients’ survival experience.

Currently, there are numerous early screening methods available for DPN, with representative approaches being the 10 g monofilament test and skin epidermal biopsy. The former is used to assess clinically significant large fibre neuropathy, while the latter primarily detects small nerve damage through invasive means.5,7 In terms of early non-invasive diagnosis of small fibre neuropathy, corneal confocal microscopy (CCM) effectively fills this gap.8,10 The key characteristics of CCM include high sensitivity and reproducibility, making it suitable for quantifying nerve fibre damage and repair.11 12 CCM demonstrates distinct alterations within the subbasal nerve plexus in DPN. These alterations encompass reduced nerve fibre density, morphometric abnormalities characterised by increased fibre thinness and tortuosity and diminished nerve branching.13 It is capable of detecting very early stages of small fibre injury.14

Today, the integration of computer science technology with medical image analysis to achieve more effective feature extraction in medical imaging has become a new trend.15 16 Petropoulos et al12 conducted a clinical evaluation demonstrating that automated analysis of CCM images offers enhanced efficiency in contrast to manual methods.17 Dabbah et al18 have devised an automated image analysis system for detecting nerve fibres within CCM images. This system uses random forests and neural networks for classification, thereby alleviating the labour-intensive nature of manual image analysis and diminishing the subjective impact of human researchers.19 In recent times, advanced convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have emerged as an efficient subset of deep learning algorithms (DLAs) and gained prominence in machine learning for the purpose of image classification.5 20 21 Salahouddin et al22 employed DLAs and adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference systems to quantify corneal nerves fully automatically. This integration enabled swift distinction between individuals diagnosed with DPN (DPN+) and healthy control subjects without DPN (DPN−).

Compared with manual analysis, the application of artificial intelligence (AI) in image detection offers better objectivity, higher efficiency and greater repeatability.20 To better highlight this advantage, we adopted the InceptionV323 architecture as the backbone network, using factorised convolutions and multiscale processing to extract crucial features from images. Diverging from conventional CNN architectures, InceptionV3 uniquely employs parallel convolutional kernels of varied dimensions. This design enables the simultaneous apprehension of both intricate local details and comprehensive structural insights within images, thereby augmenting the model’s versatility and its proficiency in recognition tasks. This approach enhances accuracy while concurrently reducing manual workload and analysis errors, thus simplifying the workflow. Ultimately, this aids in predicting whether DPN is present or absent.

In this study, we used a DLA based on InceptionV3 for a three-classification task to detect DPN by CCM images without nerve segmentation. We aimed to demonstrate the superior performance of the InceptionV3 model in CCM image classification by comparing it with traditional CNN models.

Methods

Participants

127 participants took part in the study. Within this cohort, 33 individuals were identified as healthy participants (HP), 57 as DPN+, and the remaining 37 as DPN−. Participants were patients from Fujian Medical University Union Hospital from January to June 2024. Diabetes and non-diabetes were defined according to the 2016 American Diabetes Association diagnostic criteria.24 The presence or absence of DPN was determined based on the updated Toronto Consensus Criteria.25 Participants with a history of neuropathy other than DPN, diabetic foot ulcers, vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, corneal diseases or surgeries, or contact lens wear were excluded from the study. These details have been incorporated into the Methods section. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University Union Hospital prior to its initiation (no. 2024KY112).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the study design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Image dataset

Images of the corneal subbasal nerve plexus in the central cornea were obtained from participants using a confocal laser microscope (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). During the procedure, participants’ heads were stabilised with a chin rest, and they maintained steady fixation on a target light to keep the laser reflection centred on the corneal apex. The images were saved as 400×400 µm JPEG files. During the data collection phase, the number of JPEG format images per eye for each participant ranged from 5 to 40. Experts meticulously excluded images that were either blurry or deemed unsuitable for the study, culminating in the selection of five representative images per eye for experimental purposes. Images were selected based on three strict principles: (1) high quality with good contrast and clearly visible nerves, (2) exclusive acquisition from the central cornea and (3) representation of the full spectrum of corneal nerve density, where images depicting minimal to maximal densities were intentionally chosen.26 Consequently, the dataset that was ultimately employed in the experimental analysis comprised 330 images classified as HP, 570 images classified as DPN+ and 370 images classified as DPN−.

Dataset preparation

The data were divided based on participants at a ratio of 7:1:2 for training, validation and test subsets, respectively. The allocation of the data adheres to the initial distribution ratios, resulting in 890 images allocated to the training set (DPN+, 400; DPN−, 260; and HP, 230), 120 images allocated for the validation set (DPN+, 50; DPN−, 40; and HP, 30) and 260 images allocated to the test set (DPN+, 120; DPN−, 70; and HP, 70). The training subset facilitates learning and optimisation of parameters in the model. Concurrently, the validation set serves a pivotal role in assessing the model’s efficacy throughout the training phase, allowing for fine-tuning to enhance performance. The test set is employed to appraise the model’s ultimate efficacy, offering a robust measure of its predictive accuracy and generalisability to unseen data.

Prior to the initiation of model training, essential image preprocessing steps were implemented. This included resizing the images from an original resolution of 384×384 pixels to 299×299 pixels, using bilinear interpolation to maintain image quality. Furthermore, to optimise training efficiency of the model, the images underwent normalisation, whereby the mean value of the pixels across the three-colour channels was adjusted to 0.5 and the SD to 0.5. This normalisation process scaled the pixel values to a range of (−1, 1), thereby facilitating more effective training outcomes.

Network architecture and different backbone

Our predictive model used the InceptionV3 architecture as its backbone network, leveraging factorised convolution and multiscale processing to extract profound features (figure 1). After extracting 1024-dimensional features from the network, we added a dense layer with 512 neurons, followed by a dropout layer27 with a rate of 0.7. Finally, it passes through a ternary dense layer, using the softmax activation function to output a three-category result.

In parallel, the study also incorporated a comparative analysis using several backbone models renowned for their efficacy. Specifically, ResNet30 and DenseNet,31 both grounded in CNN architectures, were chosen for their distinguished performance histories. Additionally, the Swin Transformer,32 a model predicated on the transformer architecture33 and recognised for its novelty and effectiveness, was included for comparison. To accommodate the input specifications of these networks, the preprocessing size for images was adjusted to 224×224 pixels. For DenseNet, both structures consist of four DenseBlocks and three Transition Layers, with a growth rate of 32 and bottleneck layer channels set to four times the growth rate. Specifically, in DenseNet-121, the number of bottleneck structures in each DenseBlock is 6, 12, 24 and 16, while in DenseNet-169, the numbers are 6, 12, 32 and 32. For ResNet, both configurations contain four Residual Block Groups, followed by global average pooling and a fully connected layer. In ResNet-50, the number of bottleneck structures in each residual block is 3, 4, 6 and 3, whereas in ResNet-152, the numbers are 3, 8, 36 and 3. For the Swin Transformer, which is a neural network based on the transformer architecture, the internal window size is set to 7, the patch size is 4 and the depths of each stage are 2, 2, 6 and 2. All other experimental parameters were maintained consistent with the original setup.

The experimental results were produced using a computer equipped with an Intel Core i7-13700K processor, 32 GB of RAM and an Nvidia GeForce RTX 4070 Ti graphics card. The development and training of the model were conducted using Python V.3.9 and PyTorch V.2.0.0.

Performance evaluation

The evaluation of the model’s effectiveness is conducted through the application of the test set data. For each model under consideration, a corresponding confusion matrix is generated. This matrix vividly illustrates the discrepancies between the actual classifications of the images and the predictions made by the model.

Combining the above definitions, for a single category A, there are several metrics as follows. Accuracy is defined as the proportion of correct outcomes among all classifications. Recall, also referred to as sensitivity or the true positive rate, represents the portion of samples predicted as A from those that are genuinely A. Precision is defined as the proportion of the sample in which the prediction is A that is also actually A. F1 score is the harmonic mean of recall and precision, which is a combination of the above two indicators. Therefore, for each classification, there are four evaluating indicators. The area under the curve)–receiver operating characteristics curve serves as a performance metric for multi-category classification tasks. It is necessary to average each class as the final composite judging indicator. Here, we choose the micro-average method, where each category is obtained by averaging the weights calculated according to the proportion in the original category.

Considering that each patient contributes five images per eye, the model generates a ternary probability output for each image, indicating the likelihood of each of the three possible classifications. To assess the model’s performance on a per-patient basis, a weighted average of the probabilistic outputs from all images pertaining to the same patient is computed. This aggregated output serves as the decisive criterion for classifying the overall health status or condition of the patient, thereby transitioning from image-level to patient-level evaluation.

Additionally, gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) technology34 is used to visually interpret the model’s decision-making process. Grad-CAM highlights the specific areas within the images that have the most significant impact on the model’s classification decisions. This visualisation technique not only provides insights into which features the model deems important but also aids in verifying the model’s focus on relevant anatomical or pathological features within the images. By employing Grad-CAM, researchers and clinicians can better understand the model’s evaluation process, potentially increasing trust in the model’s predictions and facilitating its clinical adoption.

Discussion

In our research, we used a DLA model based on the InceptionV3 backbone to perform a three-class classification task for detecting DPN without nerve segmentation. Compared with models based on CNN architectures, such as ResNet and DenseNet, and the Swin Transformer model, InceptionV3 performed the best in the three-class classification task without nerve segmentation. It achieved the highest accuracy (0.9231), recall (0.8846), F1 score (0.9020) and AUC (0.9534). Our study indicates that the model trained on InceptionV3 has high accuracy and sensitivity in the three-class classification task for DPN without nerve segmentation. This finding is of significant reference value for future use of AI algorithms in evaluating CCM images for DPN screening.

CNN-based DLAs perform excellently in analysing CCM images. In 2018, Oakley et al35 used a DLA to analyse CCM images of macaques, which proved easier than previously used filter-based analysis methods. In contrast with conventional machine learning methods, deep learning methods alleviate the necessity for manual feature selection and extraction, streamline complexity and empower computers to autonomously discern features during segmentation tasks. In the study by Williams et al,20 deep learning techniques were employed, specifically using a CNN enhanced with data augmentation, for the analysis and quantification of corneal nerve fibres. This approach aimed to support the diagnosis and management of DPN. The model achieved an AUC of 0.87, a specificity of 0.68 and a sensitivity of 90.132 in distinguishing DPN+ patients. Preston et al,5 Meng et al36 and Salahouddin et al22 used ResNet-50, ResNet-50 and U-Net-based CNN, respectively, to analyse CCM images for evaluating DPN and all achieved good results.

Similar to previous studies, we used a CNN-based DLA for the CCM classification task. However, our analysis revealed that, in comparison with methodologies that incorporate additional complexities—such as ResNet, DenseNet and even those using the transformer architecture like Swin transformer—the performance of InceptionV3, which was used in our study, proved superior. This superiority might be attributed to the effectiveness of parallel processing of features across varying scales, which InceptionV3 facilitates, as opposed to the deeper layer stacking approaches, such as skip connections found in the models.

The observations derived from Grad-CAM images suggest that the Swin Transformer architecture exhibits a more detailed capability in information extraction, providing nuanced insights into the focal areas crucial for making accurate classifications. However, the effectiveness of the Swin Transformer, and transformer models in general, is inherently dependent on the availability of substantial training data. The limited dataset size in our project has constrained the model’s potential, leading to outcomes that did not fully capitalise on the advanced capabilities of transformer architectures.

Analysing the outcomes, it was observed that when the prediction results of multiple images were aggregated and classified, the overall composite metrics across all models exhibited an improvement. This finding indicates that a collective assessment of multiple images yields more accurate results than the evaluation of individual images. This might be because each corneal image covers a limited area, and a single image may not reflect the overall condition of the cornea. Scarpa et al37 support this viewpoint in their study on CCM. Such an approach aligns more closely with real-world scenarios, where it is often challenging for medical practitioners to derive a diagnosis from a single image. A comprehensive evaluation, leveraging multiple images, inherently enhances diagnostic accuracy.

Our study suggests that the InceptionV3-based DLA has strong potential for future use in DPN screening using CCM. However, several limitations should be noted. First, the single-centre retrospective design may limit participant diversity and introduce selection bias, potentially reducing generalisability to broader populations. Second, the modest sample size (n=127 participants) restricts statistical power for subgroup analyses and increases overfitting risk in complex models such as Swin Transformer. Finally, the lack of external validation with independent cohorts hinders robust assessment of model performance across diverse clinical settings.

Despite these limitations, our research achieved reasonable classification results, providing significant reference value for clinical DPN screening plans using CCM. Currently, AI-based DPN screening has not been reported for clinical use. Based on past experiences with AI in diabetic retinopathy screening,38 AI algorithms often perform better in laboratory settings than in real-world applications. Therefore, more extensive and larger-scale studies of AI algorithms based on CCM images are inevitable.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a DLA based on the InceptionV3 architecture to classify CCM images for detecting DPN without nerve segmentation. Our model achieved superior performance compared with traditional CNN architectures like ResNet and DenseNet, and the Swin transformer model, highlighting its potential for effective DPN screening.
