Staged whole lung lavage in severe pulmonary alveolar proteinosis—Anaesthetic strategies for one-lung ventilation: A case report
Authors: Ashish Khanna, Nishant Chaudhary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pulmonary alveolar proteinosis causes severe hypoxaemia. Whole lung lavage is standard therapy, but anaesthetic management of staged sequential bilateral lavage in patients with partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2)/fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) <150 is challenging. We describe a 51-year-old female with autoimmune pulmonary alveolar proteinosis and respiratory failure who underwent staged lavage 3 days apart under general anaesthesia. A 35-Fr left-sided double-lumen tube (DLT) was placed with fibreoptic confirmation. Lung-protective one-lung ventilation was used with a tidal volume of 300 mL, a respiratory rate of 18/min, a positive end expiratory pressure of 6–8 cmH2O and FiO2 1.0. 10 L saline was used in right lung lavage with 9.2 L recovered over 185 minutes. Post-lavage PaO2 improved from 68 to 142 mmHg. 8 L saline was utilised in left lung lavage, with 7.5 L recovered over 160 minutes. Post-lavage PaO2 was 198 mmHg. The patient was extubated on the table after both procedures and discharged on room air on day 7. Sequential lavage with meticulous DLT positioning, lung-protective ventilation, serial arterial blood gas monitoring, and multidisciplinary coordination enabled safe management.

INTRODUCTION

Pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (PAP) is a rare disease (prevalence 0.37/1,00,000), characterised by surfactant accumulation due to impaired alveolar macrophage clearance.[1] This causes progressive hypoxaemia mimicking acute respiratory distress syndrome. Autoimmune PAP accounts for 90% of cases and is mediated by anti-granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) antibodies.[2] Rarely, PAP can be seen in patients with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS).[3]

Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) revealing periodic acid-Schiff (PAS)-positive proteinaceous material is diagnostic.[4] Whole lung lavage (WLL) is standard care for symptomatic PAP.[5] It involves instillation and drainage of large volumes of warmed saline under general anaesthesia with lung isolation to remove excess alveolar lipoproteinaceous material.[6] While simultaneous bilateral WLL is performed in some centres, staged sequential WLL is preferred in severe hypoxaemia to reduce catastrophic desaturation risk. Detailed anaesthetic management of sequential WLL in patients with partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2)/fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) ratio <150 is limited to isolated reports.

The novelty of this report is the explicit description of quantitative ventilatory parameters, serial arterial blood gas (ABG) trends, and decision points that enabled safe sequential WLL in a patient deemed too hypoxaemic for simultaneous bilateral lavage.

CASE REPORT

A 51-year-old female weighing 62 kg, with type 2 diabetes mellitus, presented with 6-month progressive exertional dyspnoea and dry cough. On admission, she was tachypnoeic, with a respiratory rate (RR) of 28/minute and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂) of 92–94% on a non-rebreather mask at 12 L/minute. ABG analysis revealed Type 1 respiratory failure and PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio <150.

High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the chest showed bilateral ground-glass opacities with interlobular septal thickening, the classic ‘crazy-paving’ pattern [Figure 1]. Chest radiograph revealed bilateral alveolar infiltrates. Echocardiography revealed normal ventricular function with no pulmonary hypertension. Flexible bronchoscopy with BAL demonstrated milky fluid; cytology confirmed PAS-positive lipoproteinaceous material. An anti-GM-CSF antibody suggested autoimmune PAP. Pulmonary function testing (PFT) showed a moderate restrictive defect [forced expiratory volume at one second (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC) 70%; FEV1 68% predicted; FVC 69% predicted]. Preoperative laboratory investigations were normal, and an infectious workup was negative.

After high-risk informed consent and multidisciplinary discussion, sequential bilateral WLL was planned. Steroids and bronchodilators were continued till the morning of surgery. Two units of packed red blood cells were arranged. Fasting guidelines of 6 hours for solids and 2 hours for clear fluids were followed. Alprazolam 0.25 mg and pantoprazole 40 mg were administered. No sedative premedication was given.

Standard American Society of Anesthesiologists monitors and invasive arterial and central lines were secured. Preoxygenation was done with FiO₂ 1.0 for 5 minutes. The patient was induced with propofol 100 mg and fentanyl 100 µg, and rocuronium 50 mg was administered to achieve neuromuscular blockade. A 35-Fr left-sided double-lumen tube (DLT) was inserted, and its position was confirmed by auscultation and fibreoptic bronchoscopy. Methylprednisolone 40 mg was administered post-induction intravenously. Anaesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane in an oxygen–air mixture and intermittent rocuronium. A lung-protective strategy was used: tidal volume (TV) 300 mL, RR 18/minute, and positive end expiratory pressure (PEEP) 5–8 cmH2O.

One-lung ventilation (OLV) of the left lung was commenced, and the right lung was lavaged first. This included repetitive cycles of 500–1000 mL warmed 0.9% saline solution from a height of 30 cm, followed by gravity drainage. During instillation, the lavaged lung was made dependent; alternate Trendelenburg/reverse Trendelenburg position was used. Manual chest percussion by a physiotherapist was followed by drainage.

Intraoperative nebulisation, steroids, and recruitment manoeuvres were used. Intravenous furosemide 20 mg was given when ABGs showed haemodilution. Frequent suctioning was performed.

A total of 10 L of saline was instilled in the right lung, with recovery of 9.2 L. The effluent cleared from milky to clear. The patient remained haemodynamically stable. The desaturation was managed by confirming the correct DLT position with fibreoptic bronchoscopy, suctioning, and continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) applied to the non-ventilated lung. Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) was kept on standby. After bronchoscopic suction, the patient was reversed with intravenous sugammadex 400 mg once the criteria for extubation were met. The patient was monitored in the intensive care unit for 24 h.

Contralateral lavage was done after 3 days using an identical protocol. A volume of 8 L of warmed saline was instilled in the left lung, and 7.5 L was retrieved. Following the second session, the patient demonstrated dramatic respiratory recovery. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 7 on room air and asymptomatic with SpO2 97% [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

WLL involves instillation/drainage of large volumes of warmed saline under general anaesthesia with lung isolation. DLT allows lung isolation and OLV and prevents spillage into the ventilated lung.[7] The risk of dislodgement with percussion exists with the bronchial blocker. Baseline hypoxaemia and lavage cause transient worsening. The dependent lung may have poor reserve, so FiO2 1.0 and PEEP should be applied.

Prone/lateral decubitus positions with the lavaged lung uppermost, along with reverse Trendelenburg positioning, improve drainage.[8] Proper drainage and suction are required for post-procedure oxygenation. Steroids prevent post-lavage pulmonary oedema due to fluid absorption and suppress inflammation in autoimmune PAP. They also reduce post-procedure bronchospasm. Warmed saline prevents hypothermia.[9] Manual percussion increases lipoprotein clearance. The optical density of recovered fluid is greater with manual percussion than with hand-held devices or no percussion.[10]

Serial ABG monitoring guides oxygenation, ventilation, fluid absorption, and the decision to abandon the procedure. Desaturation management includes DLT check, suctioning, CPAP, intermittent two-lung ventilation, and recruitment manoeuvres. Complications include pneumothorax, pleural effusion, hydropneumothorax, and pulmonary oedema. Intravenous fluids must be restricted, and post-procedure ventilation is often required until PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio >250 with minimal cuff leak. Most patients need post-procedure ventilation.[11]

Recently, some authors[12] reported anaesthetic considerations for whole-lung lavage in a case of acquired pulmonary alveolar silico-proteinosis. Similar to their experience, our case highlights the challenges of hypoxaemia management during one-lung ventilation and the need for lung-protective strategies. In contrast to their experience, our patient had autoimmune PAP and underwent staged bilateral lavage. DLT-based OLV, serial ABG analysis, and manual percussion resulted in >90% fluid recovery in our patient. Staged bilateral WLL reduces risk versus simultaneous bilateral lavage by maintaining one functional lung.

CONCLUSION

Staged sequential WLL can be performed safely in severe PAP with PaO₂/FiO₂ <150 using DLT-based lung isolation, lung-protective OLV, serial ABG monitoring, and multidisciplinary coordination. While this report cannot establish a definitive protocol, sequential WLL is a viable, lower-risk alternative to simultaneous bilateral WLL in severely hypoxaemic patients. Experienced centres with CPB on standby should be preferred for the procedure.
