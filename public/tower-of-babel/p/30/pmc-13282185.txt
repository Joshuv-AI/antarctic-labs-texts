Perceived Discrimination and Healthcare System Trust as Barriers to Survivorship Care in Endometrial Cancer
Authors: Jordyn A. Brown, Mya L. Roberson, Jennifer Richmond, Chantel L. Martin, Ganga S. Bey, Marc Peterson, Jamie Hunter, Shawn Smith, Stephenie Black‐Grant, Victoria L. Bae‐Jump, Andrew F. Olshan, Hazel B. Nichols
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPerceived discrimination can undermine healthcare system trust and contribute to medical mistrust, creating barriers to effective survivorship care. Endometrial cancer survivors, who experience intersecting social, reproductive, and aging‐related challenges, remain understudied, and population‐level evidence on the relationship between discrimination and medical mistrust in this group is limited.MethodsWe analyzed enrollment survey data from the population‐based Carolina Endometrial Cancer Study. Perceived discrimination was measured using the 9‐item Everyday Discrimination Scale, categorized into tertiles (low, moderate, high). Medical mistrust was assessed using the 7‐item Medical Mistrust Index (MMI), dichotomized as ≥ 1 item endorsed. Modified Poisson regression models estimated adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), adjusting for age at diagnosis, region, education, and caregiving support. Analyses were conducted overall and stratified by race, education, and geography, with sensitivity analyses of frequency‐ and chronicity‐based discrimination measures.ResultsAmong 907 survivors (35% Black, mean age 60.9 years), nearly half reported moderate or high perceived discrimination, and more than 80% endorsed ≥ 1 MMI item. Compared with low discrimination, moderate (aPR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.12–1.30) and high discrimination (aPR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.17–1.34) were associated with a higher prevalence of mistrust. Associations were consistent across racial, educational, and geographic subgroups. Sensitivity analyses confirmed that both the frequency and intensity of discrimination were positively associated with mistrust.ConclusionPerceived discrimination was common and strongly associated with mistrust among endometrial cancer survivors. Our findings highlight the need for culturally responsive survivorship care and equity‐focused interventions to strengthen trust and engagement.

Introduction

Endometrial cancer is the most common gynecologic malignancy in the United States, with an estimated 68,000 new cases in 2026 [1]. Five‐year survival exceeds 80% across all stages combined, producing a rapidly growing survivor population projected to increase from approximately 946,000 in 2025 to more than 1.2 million by 2035 [2, 3]. Despite overall improvements in survival, substantial racial disparities persist: Black women experience a 5‐year survival rate of 63%, compared with 85% among White women—a 20 percentage point gap that has remained largely unchanged for over two decades [1, 4, 5, 6]. As the survivor population grows, identifying factors that influence cancer care delivery and healthcare engagement is critical to addressing these inequities.

Racial disparities in endometrial cancer outcomes arise from a complex interplay of structural, social, and individual‐level factors. Structural inequities in healthcare access, insurance coverage, and treatment facility availability can affect timely diagnosis and receipt of guideline‐concordant care [7, 8, 9, 10]. Social factors, including socioeconomic status, cultural beliefs, and experiences of discrimination, further influence healthcare engagement across the cancer care continuum, while comorbidities and patient‐provider communication contribute at the individual level [11, 12, 13]. Characterizing these multilevel factors is essential to improving equity in survivorship care.

Perceived discrimination and medical mistrust are increasingly recognized as key, potentially modifiable determinants of healthcare engagement. Discrimination reflects unfair or unjust treatment based on characteristics such as race, ethnicity, or socioeconomic status, arising from interpersonal interactions or institutional policies and practices that perpetuate inequities [14, 15]. Medical mistrust denotes a generalized concern that healthcare providers or institutions may not act in a patient's best interest and has been associated with delayed care, reduced treatment adherence, and poorer health outcomes [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. Such challenges are common among individuals from marginalized communities and are shaped by historical and contemporary experiences of discrimination and injustice [16, 19].

Evidence from other cancer and chronic disease populations suggests that everyday discrimination may foster mistrust and undermine consistent healthcare engagement [16, 21, 24, 25]. Prior studies have documented associations between perceived discrimination and medical mistrust [21, 26, 27, 28], which in turn lead to delayed care and lower adherence to recommended treatments [18, 21, 29, 30, 31]. Although these relationships have been examined in cancer screening and among breast and other gynecologic cancer populations [32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39], they have not been examined in endometrial cancer survivors, who require ongoing healthcare engagement for treatment monitoring and comorbidity management. To address this gap, we examined the association between perceived discrimination and medical mistrust among endometrial cancer survivors in the Carolina Endometrial Cancer Study, a population‐based cohort in North Carolina.

Methods

Study Design and Population

The Carolina Endometrial Cancer Study (CECS) is a prospective, population‐based cohort enrolling women newly diagnosed with invasive endometrial cancer across North Carolina since January 2021. Eligible cases are identified using Rapid Case Ascertainment (RCA), a partnership between the UNC Lineberger Comprehensive Cancer Center and the North Carolina Central Cancer Registry. RCA identifies cases from pathology and surgical reports (ICD‐O‐3 codes C54.0‐C54.9 and C55.9) submitted directly by North Carolina hospital cancer registrars to the RCA team, with a median enrollment time of ~30 weeks post‐diagnosis.

All women aged 20–80 years who do not identify as White are invited to participate. White survivors are sampled 1:1 to Black survivors by age (< 60 and ≥ 60 years) and region (Mountains, Piedmont, Inner Coastal Plain, and Tidewater). The sampling approach was used to ensure adequate representation of Black survivors and to enhance comparability across racial groups with respect to age and geography. Treating physicians are notified by mail before potential participants are contacted. If no objection is raised, participants receive an introductory letter and brochure, followed by a phone call from trained staff to explain the study and obtain informed consent by mail or online. Separate consent is obtained for medical record and tumor sample release [40].

Participants complete an approximate 60‐minute enrollment survey by phone, mail, or online, covering sociodemographics, residential and occupational histories, social experiences, medical history, and quality of life. Medical records are reviewed to obtain diagnostic and treatment information, including tumor stage, grade, and histology (endometrioid and nonendometrioid). For this analysis, participants missing complete responses on the Everyday Discrimination Scale (EDS; n = 7), Medical Mistrust Index (MMI; n = 13), or both scales (n = 9) were excluded, yielding an analytic sample of 907 survivors. This research was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill (IRB # 24‐1435).

Exposure Ascertainment

Perceived discrimination was measured with the 9‐item Everyday Discrimination Scale (EDS), capturing routine, subtle experiences of unfair treatment in daily life (e.g., “You are treated with less courtesy or respect than other people”) [41]. Survivors reported frequency on a 6‐point scale ranging from “never” to “almost every day.” Given variability in EDS scoring across prior studies, we applied three complementary coding strategies [42]. The primary analysis used a situation‐based approach, dichotomizing items (“never” = 0; all other responses = 1) and summing endorsed situations (range 0–9) to capture exposure breadth based on the 9 core EDS items, excluding the optional attribution item used in some versions of the scale. This dichotomization captures the breadth of discrimination experiences across multiple contexts, complementing frequency‐ and chronicity‐based approaches that emphasize intensity. Sensitivity analyses used two coding strategies. First, a frequency‐based approach summing Likert responses (theoretical range 9–54, reflecting 9 items each scored 1–6) to preserve intensity. However, because participants were included if they completed at least one item and scores were summed across available responses only, observed scores in the analytic sample ranged from 6–54. Second, a chronicity‐based approach converting Likert responses to annual frequencies (range 0–2340) using established conversion methods to estimate cumulative exposure over time [42]. All EDS measures were categorized into tertiles (low, moderate, high) for comparison and to evaluate potential nonlinear dose–response relationships while preserving interpretability (Table S1).

Outcome Ascertainment

The primary outcome, medical mistrust, was assessed using the 7‐item Medical Mistrust Index (MMI), capturing survivors' trust in the healthcare system [43]. Survivors rated agreement on a 4‐point scale (1 = “Strongly Disagree” to 4 = “Strongly Agree”). Although the MMI is often analyzed continuously, responses were dichotomized (“Agree” or “Strongly Agree” = 1; otherwise = 0) to capture endorsement of medical mistrust beliefs. This approach prioritizes the presence versus absence of medical mistrust across multiple domains rather than the intensity of endorsement of any single item. Items were summed (range 0–7) and categorized as none versus ≥ 1 MMI item endorsed. We selected a threshold of ≥ 1 MMI item endorsed to distinguish participants with any pattern of medical mistrust‐related beliefs from those reporting none. This categorization improves interpretability by defining a clear contrast between absence versus presence of mistrust and supporting prevalence ratio estimation for this common outcome.

The secondary outcome, perceived racism in healthcare, was measured using the 4‐item Perceived Racism Index (PRI) [44]. Responses were dichotomized (1 = endorsement, 0 = nonendorsement). The item “Racial discrimination in a doctor's office is common” was coded consistently with the MMI; remaining items were reverse‐coded, so higher scores indicated greater perceived racism in healthcare. Items were summed (range 0–4) and categorized as none versus ≥ 1 PRI item endorsed.

Covariates

Sociodemographic characteristics were obtained from the enrollment survey and included race, health insurance type, educational attainment, household income, and receipt of caregiving support from family or friends during cancer treatment. Race was operationalized as a social construct reflecting survivors' self‐classification into racialized groups [45]. Clinical characteristics, including age at diagnosis, FIGO stage, tumor grade, and histology, were abstracted from medical records. While medical records abstraction provided complete information for 698 (77%) of the CECS cohort, all analyses relied solely on enrollment survey data, which fully captured the variables included in analytic models. Residential addresses were classified as urban [Rural–Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes 1–3] or rural (RUCA codes 4–10) per 2020 US Department of Agriculture guidelines [46]. Geographic region was assigned by county and categorized as Mountains, Piedmont, Inner Coastal Plain, or Tidewater (Figure S1).

Statistical Analysis

Survivor characteristics and MMI and PRI items were summarized using means and standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables and proportions for categorical variables. Chi‐square tests compared sociodemographic and clinical characteristics by mistrust status (0 versus ≥ 1 MMI item endorsed).

Modified Poisson regression models with robust standard errors estimated prevalence ratios (PRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for associations between situation‐based perceived discrimination tertiles and endorsement of ≥ 1 MMI item [47]. Analyses were conducted among overall and stratified by race (Black versus White), education (some college or less versus college graduate and beyond), urban–rural residence, and geographic region (Mountains and Tidewater combined; region‐stratified analyses limited to Piedmont and Inner Coastal Plain) to examine social and contextual differences and maintain adequate sample size. Secondary analyses examined associations with ≥ 1 PRI item overall and by race and education. Sensitivity analyses used frequency‐ and chronicity‐based EDS coding, with agreement across coding approaches assessed via Goodman‐Kruskal's gamma [48], weighted Cohen's kappa [49], and Pearson correlation coefficients to evaluate robustness.

To assess the robustness of the primary dichotomized approach, the MMI was analyzed as a continuous prorated sum score (range 7–28), with higher scores indicating greater medical mistrust. The prorated score was computed by multiplying the mean of available items by 7 to yield comparable scores across participants. Participants answering fewer than 5 of 7 items (n = 33, 4.0%) were excluded, yielding an analytic sample of 798 participants. Linear regression models with robust standard errors estimated unadjusted and adjusted mean differences in prorated MMI scores by perceived tertile, adjusting for the same covariates as primary analyses.

Models, informed by a directed acyclic graph and prior literature [50, 51], adjusted for age at diagnosis, geographic region, education, and receipt of caregiving support during cancer treatment. All statistical tests were two‐sided with α = 0.05 and performed in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

Among 907 CECS participants (mean age: 60.9 years, SD = 10.3), 59% were racialized as White, 45% had at least a college education, and 77% lived in urban areas. Most presented with endometrioid histology (77%), stage I disease (79%), and low‐grade tumors (55%; Table 1). Based on the situation‐based coding scheme, 51% of survivors reported low, 21% moderate, and 28% high discrimination. More than 80% indicated the presence of medical mistrust, defined as endorsement of ≥ 1 MMI item. Survivors reporting no mistrust were predominantly in the low tertile of situation‐based discrimination (76%). In contrast, survivors endorsing ≥ 1 MMI item were distributed across low (46%), moderate (23%), and high (31%) tertiles (p < 0.0001). Participants from other racial and ethnic groups were included in the analytic sample but were not reported separately due to small cell sizes.

Mistrust was similar across most sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. However, mistrust varied by race: Black survivors were disproportionately represented among those endorsing ≥ 1 MMI item (p < 0.0001). Mistrust also differed by household income (p = 0.0002), although the pattern was not monotonic across income categories.

The mean MMI was 3.5 (range 0–7). The item most frequently endorsed was being cautious with healthcare organizations (61%), while the least endorsed was confidence in healthcare organization competence (43%). The mean PRI score was 1.2 (range 0–4). Item‐level responses indicated variability in perceptions of racial bias in healthcare, with 44% agreeing that care is provided equally to Black and White patients, and 32% agreeing that racial discrimination is common in doctors' offices (Table 2).

In 831 survivors with complete covariate data, moderate (aPR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.12–1.30) and high (aPR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.17–1.34) discrimination were associated with a higher prevalence of medical mistrust compared with low discrimination (Figure 1). This pattern was consistent across race, education, and geographic subgroups. Among Black survivors, moderate (aPR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.05–1.30) and high (aPR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.11–1.32) discrimination were associated with a higher prevalence of mistrust compared with low discrimination. Similar estimates were observed for White survivors reporting high versus low discrimination, indicating generally consistent associations by race.

In analyses stratified by education, moderate discrimination was associated with a 22% higher prevalence of mistrust among survivors with some college or less and a 17% higher prevalence among college graduates. High discrimination was associated with a 28% and 24% higher prevalence of mistrust in these groups, respectively, with similar magnitudes and overlapping confidence intervals, suggesting no meaningful differences by education level.

Among urban survivors, moderate and high discrimination were associated with 20% (aPR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.10–1.31) and 26% (aPR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.17–1.36) higher prevalence of medical mistrust, respectively. Rural survivors demonstrated comparable increases of 20%–24%. In Piedmont and Inner Coastal Plain regions, moderate and high discrimination were associated with a 16%–32% higher prevalence of mistrust.

To assess the robustness of the dichotomized approach, we repeated analyses using the MMI as a continuous prorated sum score. Results were consistent with primary findings across all subgroups (Table S2). Compared with the lower perceived discrimination tertile, adjusted mean MMI scores were 2.0 points higher (95% CI: 1.3–2.7) in the moderate tertile and 3.1 points higher (95% CI: 2.5–3.8) in the high tertile overall, supporting the validity of the primary analytic approach.

In secondary analyses of the 792 survivors who completed the PRI, moderate (aPR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.16–1.66) and high (aPR = 1.58, 95% CI: 1.36–1.84) discrimination were associated with a higher prevalence of perceived racism in healthcare, with a pattern generally consistent across race and education subgroups (Figure 2). Among White survivors, moderate (aPR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.06–1.79) and high (aPR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.14–1.88) discrimination were associated with a higher prevalence of perceived racism in healthcare compared with low discrimination. For Black survivors, only high discrimination was associated with a statistically significant increase in perceived racism in healthcare, indicating that the strongest effects occur at the highest discrimination levels. Among survivors with some college education, moderate discrimination was associated with a 47% higher prevalence of perceived racism in healthcare, compared with 32% higher among college graduates. High discrimination was associated with a 78% higher prevalence among those with some college education versus 46% higher among college graduates, indicating stronger associations in survivors with lower educational attainment.

Findings were robust across all three EDS coding schemes (situation‐, frequency‐, and chronicity‐based), with high discrimination consistently associated with endorsement of ≥ 1 MMI and ≥ 1 PRI item (Figures S2–S7). Moderate discrimination was inconsistently associated with mistrust among college graduates and Inner Coastal Plain survivors, and with perceived racism in healthcare among Black and college graduate survivors. Agreement between EDS coding schemes was generally high, strongest between situation‐ and frequency‐based approaches, and lowest between situation‐ and chronicity‐based approaches (Table S3).

Discussion

In this population‐based cohort of Black and White endometrial cancer survivors, perceived discrimination was strongly associated with higher medical mistrust. These associations were consistent across race, education, and geographic contexts, indicating that mistrust is not limited to traditionally marginalized or disadvantaged groups. Even relatively common experiences of discrimination can meaningfully undermine trust in the healthcare system and may affect healthcare engagement in recommended endometrial cancer survivorship care.

Medical mistrust was highly prevalent in our North Carolina cohort, with more than 80% endorsing ≥ 1 MMI item, exceeding prior estimates in US cancer populations. For comparison, only 23% of cancer patients at a comprehensive cancer center and safety net hospital in Philadelphia reported mistrust [52]. Although the Philadelphia‐based sample used a different response scale, both studies used the MMI, allowing meaningful comparison. Similarly, 54% of adults with a personal history of cancer reported low or moderate trust in the US healthcare system [26]. This elevated prevalence of mistrust in our cohort may reflect cumulative discrimination, the demands of ongoing survivorship care, and regional context.

Mistrust remains a persistent concern in communities shaped by historical and contemporary inequities [20, 53, 54, 55]. Prior studies document higher mistrust among non‐White survivors [56, 57, 58, 59], especially Black women with breast cancer [59, 60, 61, 62] and Black men with prostate cancer [63, 64]. In our cohort, both Black and White endometrial cancer survivors reporting moderate or high discrimination were more likely to endorse ≥ 1 MMI item. Both personal and vicarious experiences, along with broader healthcare system factors, contribute to mistrust, with the type and context of discrimination shaping its magnitude and intensity across racialized groups [65, 66, 67].

Perceived discrimination was also strongly associated with perceived racism in healthcare, with the highest discrimination levels showing the strongest associations. Patterns were generally consistent across race and education, although moderate discrimination was not significantly associated with perceived racism in healthcare among Black survivors. These findings underscore that cumulative and context‐specific experiences can meaningfully influence how discrimination is recognized and reported, with more overt experiences more likely to register [68]. Perceptions of racial bias in healthcare are further shaped by social and subgroup contexts, highlighting broader factors that affect patient trust and engagement.

Features unique to endometrial cancer survivorship may further amplify mistrust. Survivors often report suboptimal care experiences [69, 70], including obesity‐related stigma [71, 72] and the dismissal or misattribution of abnormal vaginal bleeding to benign gynecologic conditions [73, 74, 75]. These experiences may compound cumulative discrimination and structural inequities, elevating the risk of disengagement from care. Shared narratives of dismissal and poor communication, amplified and circulated through social media, may also reflect historical and cultural patterns, reinforcing expectations about healthcare experiences [76, 77, 78, 79, 80, 81, 82]. Given the substantial psychosocial burden of care, strong patient‐provider relationships remain critical for treatment adherence and long‐term survivorship outcomes [83, 84, 85, 86]. Understanding how endometrial cancer survivorship influences experiences of discrimination and mistrust is essential for developing interventions that strengthen trust and engagement.

Our findings emphasize the need to address discrimination and mistrust in gynecologic oncology practice. Interventions promoting transparent communication, acknowledging patients' social experiences, and delivering culturally responsive care may reduce mistrust and improve healthcare engagement. Routine screening for discrimination and mistrust, similar to assessments for financial toxicity or social needs [87, 88], could identify survivors at risk for healthcare disengagement and suboptimal treatment adherence—a critical step in addressing persistent disparities in endometrial cancer survivorship.

Limitations of this work include reliance on self‐reported discrimination, mistrust, and perceived racism in healthcare perceptions, which may be subject to recall or social desirability bias. Survivors with marginalized identities may underreport these experiences, while those with greater awareness may more readily recognize and report them [89, 90]. We assessed overall discrimination experiences rather than specific types (e.g., race, ethnicity, gender, weight), which may differ in their respective associations with mistrust and perceived racism in healthcare. The cross‐sectional survey design precludes assessment of temporality, such that mistrust may both shape and result from discrimination. Despite adjustment for key sociodemographic characteristics, residual confounding from unmeasured social, clinical, or healthcare system factors is possible. This study draws on a population‐based cohort from a single southeastern US state, which may limit generalizability to other US states or international settings with differing demographic compositions and healthcare systems. Overall, these associations likely reflect interrelated experiences of discrimination and mistrust in endometrial cancer survivorship.

Strengths include data from the Carolina Endometrial Cancer Study, a contemporary, population‐based cohort spanning urban and rural settings. Validated instruments captured discrimination, mistrust, and perceived racism in healthcare, perceptions rarely documented in clinical records [91]. Consistent associations across discrimination coding schemes support the robustness of our findings. Primary analyses emphasized the presence of discrimination, while supplemental analyses examined its frequency and intensity in relation to mistrust. Future work should refine discrimination measures, explore temporal dynamics, and evaluate interventions to enhance trust and promote equity in survivorship care.

Perceived discrimination may be a key driver of mistrust and perceived racial bias in healthcare among endometrial cancer survivors. Addressing mistrust requires multilevel strategies that extend beyond individual clinical encounters, targeting structural inequities within healthcare systems. Replication in additional endometrial cancer survivor cohorts is needed to inform equity‐focused interventions that strengthen patient‐centered care and reduce disparities in endometrial cancer survivorship.

Conclusion

Perceived discrimination was consistently associated with higher medical mistrust among endometrial cancer survivors across race, education, and geographic contexts. Our findings highlight the profound influence of social and systemic factors on survivors' trust in healthcare systems and emphasize the need to address psychosocial challenges during survivorship. Interventions that reduce discrimination, deliver culturally responsive care, and strengthen patient‐provider relationships are essential for improving healthcare engagement, treatment adherence, and long‐term outcomes. Future research should prioritize actionable strategies to build trust and advance equity across the endometrial cancer care continuum.
