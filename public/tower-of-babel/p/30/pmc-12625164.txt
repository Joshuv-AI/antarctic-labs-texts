Introduction

“Drug loss” in Japan refers to drugs that have been approved and marketed overseas but haven't been developed, submitted, or approved in Japan. A comprehensive Model‐Informed Drug Development (MIDD) strategy, enhanced with artificial intelligence/machine learning, can minimize drug loss. Continued pharmaceutical industry and regulatory commitment and collaboration in applying MIDD will facilitate Japanese patients' access to essential medicines and solidify Japan's role in global pharmaceutical advancement.

Drug loss is a barrier for Japanese patients being able to access innovative therapies

“Drug loss” in Japan is a growing issue that demands attention. This phenomenon refers to drugs that have been approved and marketed overseas but haven't been developed, submitted, or approved in Japan. The FDA and EMA approved 243 new drugs between 2016 and 2020. Approximately 70% of these drugs aren't available in Japan (Figure 1) [1]. Between 2019 and 2023, the US FDA approved 98 new drugs submitted by emerging biopharma companies. Drug‐loss products in Japan accounted for 52.0% of these US‐approved drugs, significantly higher than the 19.4% seen in Europe [2].

Funnel plots of proportion of “Japanese Drug loss” products show that 70% of new therapies are unavailable in Japan.

Mitigating drug loss in Japan is crucial for ensuring that patients have access to innovative therapies. In Japan, patients who have not benefitted from existing medications or treatments often cannot access the latest innovative therapies. The drug loss problem limits further promising treatment options. Addressing this problem is especially critical for patients with rare diseases and pediatric cancers. Various industry‐government‐academia collaborations are working to minimize drug loss [3].

Since 2015, the Japan Agency for Medical Research and Development's GAPFREE/GAPFREE2 project has been advancing drug development. This initiative aims to integrate drug discovery, clinical research, and pharmaceutical manufacturing by fostering collaboration between academia and industry. Its goal is to facilitate the application of Japanese drug discoveries.

Likewise, the Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare (MHLW) and the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) have streamlined approval‐related regulations. These include easing the requirements for international joint clinical trials and accepting application documents in English. They have set a target for starting clinical trials for 86 existing drug loss products by 2026. Another goal is to formulate 50 development plans by 2028.

In July 2024, the Japanese government launched a five‐year plan to reduce drug loss and nurture the pharmaceutical industry. The initiative promotes collaboration between academia and industry to share drug discovery technologies.

Additionally, projects like the GHIT Fund have emerged as international platforms for drug discovery, particularly in combating infectious diseases. However, these efforts are still in their infancy. Also, the clinical applications remain limited and unclear. Thus, further research and development in this area are needed.

Confronting Drug Loss: Key Factors Hindering Drug Development Innovation in Japan

The Policy Research Institute of the Japan Pharmaceutical Manufacturers Association cites five causes of drug loss [4].

Of the drugs approved by the FDA or EMA between 2016 and 2022 that were not approved in Japan, approximately 85% did not include Japanese subjects in clinical trials. These factors impede Western pharmaceutical companies from entering the Japanese market. They also deter Japanese pharmaceutical companies from competing globally.

Furthermore, many of the target diseases for drug loss are pediatric cancers and rare diseases. These patients would benefit from the development of emerging therapeutic modalities such as multi‐specific antibodies, nucleic acids, gene therapies, and regenerative medicine.

European and U.S. pharmaceuticals and biotech firms have made significantly greater investments in patenting these treatment modalities compared to Japanese companies. Thus, Japanese pharmaceutical companies often lose out on opportunities to develop emerging treatment modalities. A February 2024 Boston Consulting Group report highlights that up to 75% of these innovations may become unavailable in Japan. This could lead to shortages of essential therapies [5].

Strategies to Overcome Drug Loss in Pediatric Oncology Using MIDD Approaches

A comprehensive MIDD strategy can increase the efficiency of drug development [6] for pediatric cancer and rare diseases. Preclinical scientists can leverage Quantitative Systems Pharmacology (QSP) models to evaluate disease mechanisms, target responsiveness, and interactions with biomarkers. Integration of large‐scale Japanese patient datasets—including omics and clinical biomarker data—can enhance the predictive performance and regulatory acceptance of these models. This approach also helps narrow down potential candidates for indications and dosages.

The use of MIDD then continues into Phase I/First‐in‐Human (FIH). Physiologically Based Pharmacokinetics (PBPK) models are useful for assessing drug–drug interaction (DDI) risk, the impact of genetic polymorphisms, and pharmacokinetics (PK) variability associated with growth. Simulation performed using these models can enable identifying initial doses for pediatric and rare diseases.

Even in Phase I/Ph1b where study sizes are still limited, Population Pharmacokinetic and Exposure–Response (PPK/ER) models can establish exposure‐response relationships and quantify efficacy/safety evaluations. For Phase II/III, pharmacological target response predictions and biomarker selection via QSP can help inform critical decisions. These include optimizing the indication patient populations and study designs, clarifying population variability and covariate effects using PPK models, and establishing dose adjustment strategies for target patients and adjacent populations. Researchers can also use MBMA to compare outcomes from multiple trials to strengthen the evidence base for clinical trials.

Here are some best practices for overseas companies preparing for Japanese regulatory submission. Sponsors should conduct pre‐submission meetings via the FDA's PDUFA VII MIDD Paired Meeting Program on potential PBPK, PPK, QSP, and MBMA strategies. These meetings enable pharmaceutical companies to reach an agreement with the agency on the validity of their MIDD strategy.

Alternatively, sponsors can obtain Scientific Advice or Parallel Scientific Advice (PSA) from the EMA. As a result, sponsors can align their MIDD strategy with both regulatory authorities from an early stage.

Furthermore, for post‐marketing and additional indications, drug developers can enhance the evidence for treatments by leveraging real‐world evidence using MBMA. In addition, PPK can help refine drug label claims. Some examples of drug claims include dose adjustment for genotypes, pediatric/elderly patients, and patients with renal/hepatic impairment.

Pediatric cancer and rare disease drug developers find obtaining sufficient information using conventional methods difficult. This is due to data constraints, ethical issues, and the small number of patients. To compensate for this, a comprehensive MIDD strategy would be a valuable strategy for eliminating drug loss [7, 8].

The Impact of AI/ML on MIDD Approaches

Developing a robust MIDD strategy requires collaboration between regulatory, clinical, pharmacometrics, and data science teams. The introduction of AI/ML technology to MIDD [9] (Figure 2) can improve development quality, efficiency, and automation [10]. Potential benefits include model optimization and dimension reduction for nonlinear and high‐dimensional data, automatic model generation and simulation, optimal dose estimation using Bayesian optimization and reinforcement learning, optimization of clinical trials through development scenario exploration and hypothesis generation, and integration and curation of external metadata.

Synergy between MIDD and AI [10].

Using an MIDD strategy, powered by AI/ML, can minimize drug loss. Additionally, it can help improve development efficiency and the likelihood of successful clinical trials, reducing the number of subjects and costs, increasing the accuracy of modeling complex diseases (e.g., cancer and immune diseases), and accelerating personalized medicine [9].

Conclusion

Many currently approved drugs in Western countries aren't available for Japanese patients. To address this urgent issue, a multifaceted strategy involving MIDD is essential. By harnessing AI and machine learning, stakeholders can optimize clinical trial designs and enhance patient recruitment, thereby expediting the development and approval processes.

In addition, collaboration among government entities, academia, and the pharmaceutical industry is vital to removing barriers to entry for international firms and supporting local innovation. Policies that encourage including Japanese subjects in clinical trials can help reduce drug loss. For example, they could offer financial incentives for developing therapies for underserved populations.

While recent Japanese government initiatives signal progress, continued commitment and dialogue among all stakeholders are necessary to evaluate and refine strategies to tackle this problem. By fostering collaboration and leveraging advanced scientific approaches, Japan can effectively mitigate drug loss. These efforts will increase patients' access to essential medications and enhance Japan's global position in pharmaceutical innovation.
