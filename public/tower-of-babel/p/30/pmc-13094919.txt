Examining the effectiveness of group-based interpersonal and social rhythm therapy (IPSRT) as an adjunctive treatment for patients with bipolar disorder: A quasi-experimental study
Authors: Maryam Mazaheri, Mohammadreza Pirmoradi, Banafsheh Gharraee, Hojjatollah Farahani, Kazem Malakouti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Bipolar disorder is fundamentally linked to a range of interpersonal issues, which significantly contribute to symptom exacerbation, recurrence, and even the risk of suicide. The present study aimed to investigate the effectiveness of group-based Interpersonal and Social Rhythm Therapy (IPSRT) on mania, suicidal behaviors, and social adjustment in patients with bipolar disorder.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This quasi-experimental study included a pre-test, post-test, and a 3-month follow-up period, with a control group conducted in 2023–2024. Participants received two individual therapy sessions, six group sessions, and 12 weeks of telephone-based therapy. The sample consisted of 20 patients with bipolar disorder and a history of suicide attempts who were randomly assigned to either the experimental (n = 10) or control group (n = 10). The instruments used included the Young Mania Rating Scale, the Suicidal Behaviors Questionnaire-Revised (SBQ-R), and the Social Adjustment Scale. Data were then analyzed using SPSS-25. In descriptive statistics, the section reported frequency, standard deviation, and mean. The inferential statistics section reported an analysis of variance with repeated measures.RESULTS:The findings indicated that IPSRT led to significant improvements in suicidal ideation and social adjustment among the participants (P < 0.01). The between-group effect sizes for suicidal thoughts and social adjustment were 0.61 and 0.29, respectively. Furthermore, while the therapy resulted in improvements in mania symptoms in the experimental group, the between-group effect size for mania was 0.14, which was not statistically significant (P = 0.11).CONCLUSIONS:The results of this study support the positive effect of IPSRT in improving suicidal thoughts and social adjustment in patients with bipolar disorder and a history of suicide attempts.

Introduction

Bipolar disorder is a chronic condition characterized by alternating manic or hypomanic episodes, accompanied by periods of depression.[1] Because of the negative emotional, behavioral, and mood-related changes it causes, this disorder requires therapeutic attention and research.[2] Among psychiatric disorders, bipolar disorder is notably associated with a high rate of suicide, with 30 to 50 percent of patients having attempted suicide, and around 15 to 20 percent dying as a result.[3] Individuals with bipolar disorder are 30 to 60 times more likely to be at risk of suicide compared to the general population.[4] Furthermore, the more severe the mania of this disorder and the more frequent its recurrence, the higher the risk of suicide and self-harm behaviors in these patients.[3] As symptoms recur, individuals’ ability to control impulsive behaviors and self-harm significantly decreases, leading to a marked increase in suicide.[5] Additionally, there is a bidirectional relationship between this disorder and social adjustment, with weakened social functioning in various aspects — interpersonal, occupational, and familial — being one of the most significant risk factors for suicide in these patients.[6] This risk is especially pronounced in patients with a history of suicide attempts. Research has shown that bipolar patients who face more social difficulties experience more frequent symptom relapses, and have lower social adjustment,[7] however, are at significantly higher risk of suicide.[6] Additionally, bipolar patients with social problems often face various interpersonal challenges with family,[8] friends,[9] and relationships, which are accompanied by feelings of being a burden and a lack of belonging — key social reasons for suicide attempts.[10] These findings highlight the critical role of interpersonal problems in bipolar patients. One of the most important treatments used for bipolar patients is medication therapy, which is employed to treat the acute phase, along with stabilizers to manage symptoms. However, these treatments come with challenges such as insufficient effectiveness for some symptoms, side effects, and a lack of attention to the functional issues of these patients.[11] In addition, despite the widespread use of pharmacological treatments, many randomized controlled trials have not fully supported their efficacy for long-term management.[12] These challenges have shifted attention toward psychological interventions as a means of improving patient outcomes. Many clinical guidelines also recommend that effective recovery in these patients is achievable through a combination of pharmacotherapy and non-pharmacological interventions.[13] Non-pharmacological interventions are associated with reduced symptom recurrence, fewer side effects, lower costs, and increased family cooperation.[2] Therefore, therapeutic research aimed at investigating interventions that can be used alongside pharmacotherapy for long-term improvements is of particular importance.

One of the treatments specifically developed for bipolar patients is Interpersonal and Social Rhythm Therapy (IPSRT). This therapy targets adherence to medical treatment, social stressors, and daily routines.[14] An underlying hypothesis regarding the development of bipolar disorders is that the disruption of daily routines can destabilize the body’s biochemical system, which regulates sleep, mood, and energy, thereby increasing the risk of mood episodes in susceptible individuals.[14] Social stressors, such as grief, role transitions, and interpersonal conflicts can disrupt circadian and social rhythms, adversely affecting mood. By establishing daily routines for sleep, wakefulness, meals, and purposeful activities, and addressing interpersonal problems, this therapy stabilizes social rhythms, improves mood, and enhances functioning. It also teaches patients skills to protect themselves against the onset and recurrence of new emotional episodes.[15]

Various studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of IPSRT in bipolar patients. However, these studies have several limitations, including the lack of a control group and difficulty distinguishing the effects of pharmacotherapy from psychotherapy in some cases. Additionally, the small sample sizes in many studies limit the generalizability of the findings. The effectiveness of this treatment on manic symptoms—a key feature of bipolar disorder—and suicidal behaviors has not been thoroughly investigated. Therefore, the present study aims to assess the effectiveness of IPSRT on social adjustment, suicidal behaviors, and manic symptoms in patients with bipolar disorder.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This quasi-experimental clinical trial was conducted on two groups of patients with bipolar disorder and a history of suicide attempts in 2023–2024. Twenty patients with bipolar disorder were selected using convenience sampling from Iran Psychiatric Hospital and Rasoul Akram Hospital.

Study participants and sampling

The study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the Iran University of Medical Sciences (Ethic code: IR.IUMS.REC.1402.226). Participants were selected from patients admitted to Iran Psychiatric Hospital and Rasoul Akram Hospital. Using numbered envelopes, participants were randomly assigned to two groups, experimental and control. Envelope number 1 was designated for the experimental group, and envelope number 2 for the control group. The sample size for this study was determined by G*Power software considering previous studies,[16] considering an effect size of 0.50, an alpha level of 0.05, and a power of 0.80. The sample included 20 individuals who were randomly assigned to two groups [Diagram 1]. This is the first study conducted in Iran involving these patients. Since the participants had a history of suicide attempts, this sample size was chosen to ensure caution and research accuracy. Inclusion criteria were: having a history of suicide attempts, age between 18 and 65 years, a diagnosis of bipolar disorder based on a clinical interview, and informed consent to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria included a diagnosis of schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, severe alcohol use disorder, or substance dependence. Interviews were conducted by a psychiatrist at the hospital, and to increase accuracy, the patients’ medical records were also reviewed.

Data collection tool and technique

After obtaining the ethics code from the Iran University of Medical Sciences and receiving a letter from the research committee, the researchers visited the relevant hospitals. After submitting the research proposal and explaining its process, the hospital staff agreed to cooperate with the researchers. Subjects expressed their willingness to participate in the treatment sessions after being informed in the hospital and discussing the research and treatment method. After completing the informed consent form, a structured clinical interview for disorders was conducted to confirm the diagnosis of bipolar disorder and comorbid conditions. Patients’ medication information was recorded by reviewing their medical files, and information regarding any readmissions, including outpatient or inpatient admissions, was also recorded. Individual sessions were held at Iran Psychiatric Hospital. In the first individual session, patients were informed about how disruptions in their social rhythm and daily life are associated with the recurrence of their symptoms, and that improving their daily routines is linked to better disease management. The timing of social activities was determined by the patients themselves. In the second session, the relationship between energy levels, mood, and daily routines was discussed, and problems related to SRM-II were addressed. The subjects received two 60-minute individual psychotherapy sessions. The duration of the therapy sessions was based on the protocol itself and similar. The topics covered in these individual sessions included the course of the illness, interpersonal domains, and an explanation of social rhythm assessment. Typically, five domains are assessed in the social rhythm assessment. These five domains included the time of getting out of bed, the first interpersonal interaction, starting work, eating dinner, and going to bed, based on the IPSRT protocol. Each domain included a target time for the individual to engage in the activity, the actual time they could perform it, and the number of people they interacted with during that specific time. The individual’s mood was then assessed; elevated mood indicated mania, while very low mood indicated depression. Mood was rated on a scale from -5 to + 5.[17] Identifying interpersonal relationships and setting target times for daily activities are key features of IPSRT. By considering their social rhythm assessment, individuals could understand how stressors disrupted their regular routines and biological rhythms, leading to new episodes of the illness. The target time or the best time for activities and the social rhythm were determined by the individuals themselves, with attention to the relationship between social rhythm and mood. This process helped individuals become aware of how a routine was linked to their energy levels and mood. After the first session, subjects were asked to monitor their daily social rhythm. In the second session, using an interpersonal interview, the connection between daily routines, interpersonal relationships, and mood was discussed, and any issues with the application and use of the social rhythm assessment were resolved before starting group sessions. After two individual sessions, the patients gained a comprehensive understanding of the connection between their illness, social rhythm disruptions, and interpersonal issues. During the individual sessions, patients were informed about group therapy, which would begin in the third session. Individuals learned that within the framework of regular routines—such as eating, interacting with others, maintaining appropriate sleep times, and regular medication use—their energy levels improved. The areas targeted during treatment included unresolved grief, mourning the loss of their health, interpersonal conflicts, and role transitions. To maintain the structure of IPSRT and quickly identify target areas for each participant, both individual and group sessions were conducted. Through individual sessions, a precise understanding of the illness’s problems and the relationship between personal issues and social rhythm was achieved. All individual and group therapy sessions were conducted by a clinical psychology PhD student under the supervision of a faculty member. Following the individual sessions, group sessions began. Group therapy sessions started by discussing SRM-II and the timing of daily social activities, followed by a review of homework and resolution of any issues. In the third session, group goals and the definition of daily activity schedules were reviewed. In the fourth session, the topic of grief and sadness related to being diagnosed with bipolar disorder and adherence to treatment principles was discussed. The fifth session focused on interpersonal relationships and their impact on daily activities. In the sixth session, patients discussed the roles they play in their daily lives and how these roles affect their daily activities. The seventh session addressed IPSRT strategies and their application in daily life. The eighth session covered disease management, relapse prevention, and the conclusion of group therapy. Before the group sessions began, the patients introduced themselves and got to know each other. Before the individual sessions, participants were informed about the rules of group therapy, including confidentiality, not interacting outside of group therapy sessions, and not sharing the content of the sessions with others. Patients were reminded about each group session through a phone call one day before the session. The training was based on the Social Rhythm and Interpersonal Therapy protocol specifically designed for patients with bipolar disorder.[17] The most important tool in this therapy was the completion of SRM-II, in which patients recorded their daily activities, work, and interpersonal interactions, while also evaluating their mood during each activity. This helped them identify when they were in a good mood and when they were not, allowing them to adjust their activities accordingly. After the conclusion of the face-to-face sessions, twelve additional sessions were conducted where patients discussed their social rhythm assessment tasks and other assignments. A summary of the therapy sessions is presented in Table 1. After the treatment was completed and the follow-up period was finished, the same intervention provided to the experimental group was also administered to the control group. During individual and group sessions, patients were instructed not to discuss the therapy content outside the sessions with others, and if they failed to comply with this requirement, they were asked to refrain from participating in the study. Additionally, the experimental and control groups had no knowledge of each other, as they confirmed they had not interacted with anyone from this hospital. Before the start of the treatment and immediately after the final session, the participants completed the questionnaires. Additionally, three months after the last session, they completed the follow-up phase. The post-test was conducted once, and the follow-up phase was conducted once as well. Patients were free to participate in the study, and they were informed that participation was completely voluntary and that they could withdraw from the study at any time. As a result, only patients who were interested in participating in the study and treatment naturally took part.

Structured clinical interview for DSM-5 disorders (SCID-5-CV)

The SCID-5-CV is a semi-structured interview designed to diagnose psychiatric disorders according to the DSM-5, administered by a psychiatrist.[18] The reliability and validity of this tool were studied by Shankman et al.[19] (2019), with findings indicating that the percentage of positive agreement between the interview and clinical diagnoses ranged from 73% to 97%, and the sensitivity and specificity of the interview were reported to be above 0.70. And the kappa coefficient for most diagnoses exceeded 0.70. The psychometric properties of the Persian version were examined by Shabani et al.[20] (2021), with results showing high kappa coefficients (above 0.80) for all disorders except for anxiety disorders. Moreover, the sensitivity of this tool for all diagnoses was reported to be above 0.80.

Suicidal behaviors questionnaire-revised (SBQ-R)

The SBQ-R is a 4-item (e.g., “How often have you thought about killing yourself in the past year”) questionnaire that assesses suicidal behavior in the past, present, and future. A higher total score indicates a greater likelihood of suicide attempts. In the original version of this questionnaire, a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.80 was reported for adult psychiatric patients.[21] In Iran, the concurrent validity of this questionnaire was determined to be 0.34, and its reliability, also measured by Cronbach’s alpha, was found to be 0.80.[22]

Social adjustment scale (SAS)

The Social Adjustment Scale was first developed by Weissman and Paykel and consists of 54 items (e.g., “During the last two weeks, have you felt anxious, worried, or upset when you were doing your?”). Respondents answer the questions based on their conditions. Each question is rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = not at all to 4 = severe). In one study, the reliability coefficient of this questionnaire was determined to be 0.93 using Cronbach’s alpha method. The social adjustment scale self-report contains skip-outs so that non-applicable items are omitted. To establish validity, the correlation coefficient was used, with reported correlations ranging from 0.32 to 0.98.[23] In an Iranian study, the reliability coefficient of the Social Adjustment Scale was reported to be 0.81 using Cronbach’s alpha method and 0.79 using the split-half method.[24]

The Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS)

The Young Mania Rating scale (YMRS) was designed by Young in 1978 and is an 11-item questionnaire (e.g., “Elevated mood: 0 - None, 1 - Slight or possibly increased, 2 - Noticeably elevated mood: optimistic, confident, cheerful; appropriate to the context, 3 - Elevated mood, inappropriate to the context; humorous, 4 - Euphoria, inappropriate laughter, singing”). Each item is scored from 0 to 4 or 0 to 8. The total score of the questionnaire ranges from 0 to 60. The reliability of this questionnaire, measured by Cronbach’s alpha, was found to be 0.78.[25] In Iran, the concurrent validity of this questionnaire was determined to be 0.87, and its reliability, also measured by Cronbach’s alpha, was found to be 0.72.[26]

Ethical consideration

This study was approved by the ethics committee of Iran University of Medical Sciences with the code IR.IUMS.REC.1402.226. In order to comply with ethical standards, all subjects filled out a consent form before starting the study. They could withdraw from the study at any time. All their personal information was kept confidential.

Statistical analysis method

SPSS-25 software was used for data analysis. Frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations were reported in the descriptive statistics section, while repeated measures analysis of variance was used for the inferential statistics.

Discussion

The results of the present study demonstrated that Social Rhythm and Interpersonal Therapy led to a significant improvement in social adjustment in patients with bipolar disorder. In a study conducted by Hoberg et al.[13] aimed at examining the effectiveness of group therapy focused on social and interpersonal rhythm on the social adjustment of bipolar depressed patients, the results showed that this therapy led to significant improvements in social adjustment among these patients. Specifically, they showed marked improvement in the post-test compared to the pre-test. Similarly, another study by Crowe et al.[27] investigating the effectiveness of social and interpersonal rhythm therapy on the social adjustment of patients with major depressive disorder, the results indicated that this treatment was associated with improved social adjustment. The findings of the present study align with the results of these two studies.[1327] Social Rhythm and Interpersonal Therapy focuses on managing interpersonal stressors and establishing daily routines, which directly improve home, work, and social functioning despite resistant symptoms. Treating the symptoms of bipolar disorders often does not lead to improved work and psychological functioning. Therefore, researchers assert that addressing social functioning is as important as addressing the symptoms of the disorder. Functional impairment is often an issue separate from symptoms such as depression. Even with simultaneous control of these symptoms, functional deficits remain in bipolar patients. Social Rhythm and Interpersonal Therapy focuses on social domains and daily routines. This therapy is associated with reducing interpersonal conflict, increasing social interaction with family and friends, and striving for secure social support, all of which lead to greater social adjustment for the individual. By emphasizing the stability of social rhythms, this therapy improves the biological rhythm, recognized as a significant predictor of functional impairment and daily activities. Life events and interpersonal conflicts lead to changes in an individual’s biological clock, which gradually causes the deterioration of their social functioning in various dimensions. In this therapy, by targeting interpersonal problems and social roles, the number and intensity of stressors based on interpersonal relationships and their impact on the regularity of daily routines are reduced. Ultimately, this therapy directly aims to increase and maintain the regularity of patients’ daily routines and their awareness of preserving this regularity. The goal of this therapy is not to eliminate the disease but to live with it and accept its chronic nature, allowing individuals to engage in their daily personal and social activities despite the symptoms. This therapeutic focus has a significant impact on reducing rehospitalization and improving functioning.

Alongside improving social adjustment, Social Rhythm and Interpersonal Therapy significantly reduce suicidal ideation in patients in the experimental group. Without considering mood episodes, interpersonal problems play a crucial role in suicide among bipolar patients.[6] Individuals experiencing family and interpersonal conflicts may feel less connected with their family and peers, which can lead to a sense of burden on others, causing them to view suicide as a means of escape from this feeling Additionally, interpersonal problems and social communication deficiencies may exacerbate challenges and conflicts for individuals. In Social Rhythm and Interpersonal Therapy, stabilizing social and interpersonal relationships across various dimensions—such as work, family, and friendships—helps address these challenges, resulting in reduced suicidal ideation. Moreover, by enhancing interpersonal relationships, this therapy facilitates access to social support for individuals and strengthens coping skills. Such support can serve as a protective barrier against hopelessness and suicidal tendencies, potentially preventing suicide. Therefore, addressing interpersonal problems and utilizing the Social Rhythm and Interpersonal Therapy approach appears to be a beneficial treatment for these patients.

Furthermore, it was found that social and interpersonal rhythm therapy did not have a significant effect on mania in bipolar patients, as no statistically significant difference was observed between the experimental and control groups in terms of mania scores in the post-test and follow-up phases. Similarly, the findings of Crowe et al.[16] who evaluated the effect of social and interpersonal rhythm therapy on mania in bipolar patients, showed that this treatment did not have a significant impact on mania, which is consistent with the findings of the present study. One reason for this lack of significant effect may be that this approach primarily emphasizes stabilizing social rhythms and interpersonal relationships. Therefore, it seems logical that this therapy may result in more substantial improvements in the social challenges of individuals rather than in manic symptoms. Additionally, the primary treatment for improving mania in these patients involves medication, which both groups received.

However, the present study has limitations that should be considered when generalizing the findings, and future research should address these issues. Although the study included a control group, allowing for comparisons, the sample size was limited because of various factors such as medical conditions and the need to maintain appropriate group sizes in the therapeutic setting. Additionally, the study relied on questionnaires, which have inherent weaknesses such as assessment errors and the potential for dishonest responses. In addition, weakness in completing the questionnaires due to the tiredness of participating in the sessions. Therefore, it is recommended that future research use larger sample sizes and more precise assessment methods.

Conclusion

Given the effectiveness of social and interpersonal rhythm therapy in improving social adjustment and reducing suicide attempts in bipolar patients, this therapeutic method can be considered a useful and efficient approach for mental health practitioners to enhance social adjustment and reduce suicide attempts in patients with bipolar disorder, especially those with a history of suicide.
