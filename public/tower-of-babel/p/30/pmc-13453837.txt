Multimodal neuromonitoring during aortic arch surgeries: A prospective study of neurologic outcomes and postoperative recovery
Authors: Josue Chery, Ye In Christopher Kwon, Daniel Adams, Zachary Rollins, Matthew Ambrosio, Zubair A Hashmi, Patricia Nicolato, Daniel Newton, Vigneshwar Kasirajan, Aida Sehic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveNeurologic complications remain a major cause of morbidity after aortic arch repair despite advances in cerebral protection. Multimodal intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (IONM) offers real-time assessment and opportunities for intervention but is underused. We evaluated its impact on neurologic outcomes in patients undergoing major aortic surgeries.MethodsWe retrospectively enrolled 134 adults undergoing consecutive aortic arch surgery with circulatory arrest (February 2018 to July 2024). Patients were stratified by IONM use and surgical urgency. The multimodal IONM protocol included electroencephalography, somatosensory-evoked potentials, brainstem auditory-evoked potentials, transcranial motor-evoked potentials, transcranial Doppler, and near-infrared spectroscopy. Primary outcome was a major intraoperative neurologic complication. Logistic and log-linear regression models adjusted for age, sex, and urgency assessed associations between IONM use and postoperative outcomes.ResultsOf 134 patients, 78 (58%) received IONM. Monitored patients had significantly lower neurologic complication rates (13% vs 39%; P < .001), shorter hospital stays (9 vs 13 days; P = .017), and reduced intubation duration (20 vs 46 hours; P = .007). Rates of major intraoperative neurologic complications were significantly lower in the monitored group (3.8% vs 29%; P < .001) but no differences were observed in the rates of postoperative neurologic complications (2.6% vs 7.1%; P = .2). IONM was associated with reduced risk of major neurologic complications (odds ratio [OR], 0.31; P = .02) and prolonged ventilation >48 hours (OR, 0.38; P = .03). IONM was associated with lower odds of neurologic complications in both emergent (OR, 0.17; P < .001) and elective (OR, 0.30; P = .01) surgeries.ConclusionsIONM during major aortic surgeries was associated with fewer adverse neurologic events and faster recovery, supporting its integration for improved cerebral protection across elective and emergent operations.Key Words: aortic repair, hypothermic circulatory arrest, neurophysiologic monitoring, cerebral perfusion

Multimodal IONM is associated with reduced neurologic complications during aortic repairs.

Central Message

Multimodal IONM during aortic repairs is associated with fewer neurologic complications and reduced ventilation time, supporting its routine integration as a core cerebral protection strategy.

Perspective

Neurologic injury remains a major source of morbidity after aortic arch surgery despite modern cerebral protection strategies. These findings suggest that multimodal IONM provides actionable real-time physiologic data that may reduce neurologic complications and accelerate recovery. Broader adoption of IONM may improve perioperative outcomes and resource use in high-risk aortic operations.

Hypothermic circulatory arrest (HCA) has been the foundation of cerebral protection by reducing the metabolic demand during complex aortic reconstruction. With increasing duration of HCA, neurologic complications and operative mortality increase.1,2 Although advances such as antegrade (ACP) and retrograde cerebral perfusion have improved cerebral protection, neurologic injury and stroke remain devastating complications.1,3,4 Therefore, opportunities remain to further enhance intraoperative cerebral monitoring and protection. Recognizing this ongoing challenge, the American Heart Association has recommended the use of intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (IONM), when available, to detect neurologic injury during cardiac surgery.5

Multimodal IONM has been shown to reduce the incidence of severe neurologic injury during both pediatric and adult cardiac surgery.6, 7, 8, 9 Modalities such as electroencephalography (EEG) and somatosensory-evoked potentials (SSEPs) offer real-time feedback on cortical and subcortical functions and have been used to guide perfusion management during aortic operations. Although SSEP and transcranial motor-evoked potentials (TcMEPs) are widely accepted for monitoring during open and endovascular repair of the descending thoracic aorta,10,11 there is limited evidence regarding their role in aortic arch repairs. Critically, the addition of brainstem auditory-evoked potentials (BAEPs), transcranial Doppler (TCD), and near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) for improved noninvasive cerebral oxygenation monitoring has yet to be rigorously evaluated. Thus, we evaluated the impact of expanded multimodal IONM on perioperative neurologic complications, intubation duration, and hospital length of stay in patients undergoing major aortic surgery with HCA.

Methods

Study Population and Design

This was an observational study in which we used a prospectively maintained institutional database of all consecutive aortic surgeries performed at a large tertiary referral center between February 1, 2018, and July 30, 2024. We included all adult patients (≥18 years old) who underwent aortic arch (hemi- or total) repairs requiring deliberate HCA with selective cerebral perfusion using IONM. Exclusion criteria included patients <18 years old, those requiring thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm repairs, those with isolated aortic valve operations, and those who experienced intraoperative death. Patients were stratified by use of IONM: monitored versus nonmonitored (Figure 1). NIRS was used in patients in both groups as part of the standard intraoperative monitoring protocol for all cardiac surgeries at our institution. Subgroup analyses were conducted by case urgency and use of IONM: monitored, elective versus nonmonitored, elective versus nonmonitored, and emergent versus monitored.

Flowchart of inclusion and exclusion criteria for patients.

There were no emergent cases that used IONM, because the logistical requirements of assembling the neuromonitoring team on an emergent basis precluded IONM use in these cases. Among patients who were not monitored during elective surgery (N = 33), the absence of IONM was attributed to the phased implementation of our neuromonitoring program. The majority of these cases were performed between 2018 and 2020, before the institutional protocol mandating IONM for all arch cases was established and the dedicated neurophysiology team was consistently available. From late 2020 and onward, IONM became routine for all elective arch operations. This study was approved by the Virginia Commonwealth University Institutional Review Board on September 24, 2018 (HM20020137). Consent was not required.

Aortic Pathology and Preoperative Assessment

Aortic pathology was classified as acute type A aortic dissection, degenerative aortic aneurysm (ascending and/or arch), or other (chronic dissection, intramural hematoma, penetrating aortic ulcer). Cerebral malperfusion was defined as clinical or imaging evidence of reduced cerebral blood flow secondary to aortic dissection flap extension into the arch vessels. The presence and severity of aortic atheroma was graded by epiaortic ultrasound and/or direct intraoperative inspection (grade I: normal or minimal intimal thickening; grade II: intimal thickening <3 mm; grade III: atheroma 3-5 mm; grade IV: atheroma >5 mm; grade V: mobile or protruding atheroma). All elective patients underwent preoperative computed tomography (CT) angiogram of the chest, head, and neck to assess for carotid stenosis, circle of Willis anatomy, vertebrobasilar disease, and characterization of aortic pathology. Patients with hemodynamically significant carotid stenosis (>70%) underwent staged or concomitant carotid revascularization. In emergent cases, CT angiogram of the aorta with head and neck runoff was obtained when clinically feasible.

Definitions and Outcomes

An intraoperative neurologic complication was defined as a new neurologic deficit, including hemiplegia, hemiparesis, aphasia, dysarthria, visual field deficit, or acute neurocognitive dysfunction, identified during or immediately after the operation. In the monitored group, these were identified by persistent IONM signal changes (>50% amplitude reduction and/or >10% latency prolongation in SSEP or BAEP, unilateral EEG suppression, or loss of TcMEP responses) confirmed by immediate postoperative neuroimaging (CT or magnetic resonance imaging) demonstrating a new ischemic lesion in the corresponding vascular territory. In the nonmonitored group, events were identified by new focal deficits evident upon emergence from anesthesia and confirmed on imaging within 24 hours. This terminology was adopted in lieu of “intraoperative stroke” to reflect the inherent difficulty of adjudicating stroke in a patient who cannot be examined during surgery. Complications were further classified as major (deficit persisting at hospital discharge) or minor (near-complete resolution before discharge).7 A postoperative neurologic complication was defined as a new neurologic deficit developing after documented intact neurologic examination upon initial emergence from anesthesia, confirmed on neuroimaging. A postoperative neurologic complication was defined as a new deficit developing after a documented intact neurologic examination upon emergence, confirmed on neuroimaging. The primary outcome was major intraoperative neurologic complications. Secondary outcomes included postoperative neurologic complications, all-cause in-hospital mortality, duration of mechanical ventilation, total intubation time, and total hospital length of stay (LOS).

Multimodal Intraoperative Neuromonitoring

The multimodal setup included EEG, SSEP, BAEP, TcMEP, TCD, and NIRS (Figure 2).12 Each modality was chosen to provide complementary information on cortical, subcortical, and spinal cord function and cerebral hemodynamics. EEG (8-channel configuration using the International 10-20 system; Fp1, Fp2, C3, C4, T3, T4, O1, O2 referenced to Cz) was used to monitor cortical activity and detect ischemic or anesthetic-related changes in real time. SSEPs were recorded bilaterally from upper and lower extremities using ulnar and posterior tibial nerve stimulation to assess the functional continuity of cortical, subcortical, spinal, and peripheral sensory pathways. BAEPs were obtained using bilateral auditory click stimuli to evaluate integrity of the auditory brainstem and deep brainstem structures. BAEP implementation requires bilateral ear insert transducers (standard audiology equipment) connected to the existing neuromonitoring platform. Interpretation follows established normative criteria for wave I, III, and V absolute latencies and I-V interpeak intervals. The primary limitation is that BAEPs are significantly attenuated by hypothermia below approximately 25 °C, necessitating baseline acquisition before cooling. TcMEPs were selectively used during procedures when spinal cord ischemia was a potential risk, allowing direct assessment of motor pathway function. TCD was applied bilaterally using 2-MHz transtemporal probes insolating at a depth of 50 mm to continuously measure middle cerebral artery flow velocities, detect embolic signals, and evaluate asymmetric perfusion patterns during HCA or rewarming. Bifrontal cerebral NIRS sensors were placed to monitor hemispheric oxygen saturation, whereas additional cervical and thoracolumbar sensors were deployed during descending or thoracoabdominal procedures to evaluate spinal cord perfusion.

Intraoperative Response Protocol

A standardized response protocol was used when IONM abnormalities were identified.

•Anesthesia optimization: The anesthesiologist verifies and corrects the anesthetic depth (targeting bispectral index 40-60 when available), confirms adequate mean arterial pressure (≥65 mm Hg on cardiopulmonary bypass [CPB], ≥70 mm Hg off CPB), ensures hematocrit ≥25%, and adjusts neuromuscular blockade when TcMEP signals are attenuated.

•Perfusion optimization: The perfusionist adjusts CPB flow rates (targeting cardiac index ≥2.4 L/min/m2), modifies ACP flow (typically 10-15 mL/kg/min, titrated by TCD velocities to maintain mean flow velocity >25 cm/s bilaterally), optimizes venous drainage, and adjusts warming/cooling temperature gradients (≤2 °C per 5 minutes to avoid rewarming overshoot and cerebral hyperthermia).

•Surgical intervention: The surgeon evaluates for cannula malposition, air in the arterial circuit, dissection flap compromise of arch vessels, or kinking of cerebral perfusion cannulae. Retrograde cerebral perfusion may be initiated if antegrade flow is compromised. TCD is used specifically to monitor bilateral MCA flow velocities during ACP; asymmetry >50% or mean velocity <25 cm/s triggers repositioning of cerebral perfusion cannulae or adjustment of ACP pump pressure. TCD also detects high-intensity transient signals indicative of microemboli, prompting surgical inspection of cannulation sites, deairing maneuvers, and filtration circuit verification. Persistent IONM abnormalities despite all 3 tiers of intervention prompted immediate postoperative cross-sectional neuroimaging before or upon arrival to the intensive care unit when clinically safe.

Operative Details

During the study period, hemiarch replacement with HCA was the preferred approach for patients with type A dissection or ascending aortic aneurysm. Total arch replacement with ACP was reserved for cases involving an intimal tear within the arch, arch aneurysm, circumferential arch dissection, or carotid dissection causing cerebral malperfusion with or without thrombosis. Accordingly, in all our arch repairs we used ACP. Moderate hypothermia at 24 to 28 °C was used in majority of cases, although deep hypothermia to 18 °C was used in complex arch cases (n = 15). Among the 15 deep hypothermia cases, 11 were monitored with IONM. During deep hypothermia, EEG, SSEP, BAEP, and TcMEP signals are expected to become isoelectric or unrecordable below approximately 18 to 20 °C. Central aortic cannulation was typically performed using the modified Seldinger technique under transesophageal echocardiographic guidance. To minimize anesthetic interference with IONM signals, total intravenous anesthesia was used whenever possible. When inhalational agents were necessary, end-tidal isoflurane was limited to ≤0.5%. All neurophysiologic data were recorded and interpreted in real time by a certified neurophysiologist present in the operating room (Figure 3). The attending surgeon reviewed any abnormal IONM findings to determine appropriate interventions. Persistent abnormalities prompted immediate postoperative imaging before transfer to the intensive care unit when safe.

Postoperative Neurologic Assessment

All patients underwent a standardized bedside neurologic examination by the intensivist team upon emergence from anesthesia and daily during their stay in the cardiac surgery intensive care unit. The assessment included level of consciousness, cranial nerve function, motor strength, speech and language, and visual field testing. Patients with any new deficits were evaluated by a consulting neurologist and neuroimaging (CT and/or magnetic resonance imaging) was obtained within 24 hours.

Statistical Analysis

Differences in baseline demographic and operative characteristics were compared between IONM-monitored and nonmonitored groups. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data or median with interquartile ranges for skewed data. Categorical variables are reported as frequency and percentage. Between-group comparisons were performed using the t test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and χ2 or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. To identify independent predictors of postoperative complications and prolonged ventilation (>48 hours), multivariable logistic regression was performed, adjusting for age, sex, urgency of surgery, and IONM status. Results were reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% CIs. For continuous outcomes such as LOS and intubation duration, multivariable linear regression was applied using log-transformation to correct positive skew. Exponentiation of beta coefficients (eβ) was used to interpret effect size as the percent change in geometric mean tube time. Backward stepwise selection (P < .02 for inclusion) was used for variable retention, and IONM-related terms were locked in all models. A sensitivity analysis was performed to examine the effect of surgical urgency, stratifying nonmonitored cases into emergent versus nonemergent subgroups, with repeat univariate comparisons of outcomes and baseline variables. All analyses were performed in R, version 3.6.2 (R Core Team), with significance set at P < .05.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

In total, 156 adult patients underwent major aortic repair during the study period. Of these, 22 monitored patients (14.1%) were excluded because their procedures did not require HCA or selective cerebral perfusion. A total of 134 patients met the inclusion criteria, among whom 78 (58.2%) received IONM (Table 1). Among 134 patients, aortic pathology included acute type A dissection in 51 (38.1%), degenerative aneurysm in 62 (46.3%), and other pathology in 21 (15.7%), with similar distribution between monitored and nonmonitored groups. All 23 emergent nonmonitored patients had acute type A dissection; 5 (21.7%) presented with preoperative cerebral malperfusion. Significant aortic atheroma (grade III or greater) was more prevalent in patients who were not monitored (35.7% vs 17.9%, P = .027), driven primarily by the emergent subgroup (43.5% vs 17.9%, P = .023). Median age and sex distribution were comparable between groups (64 vs 56 years; P = .3; 60% vs 59% male; P = .6). Surgical urgency differed significantly between groups, with 41% of nonmonitored cases performed emergently compared with none in the monitored cohort (P < .001). Median CPB time was 201 minutes in patients who were monitored versus 193 minutes in patients who were not monitored (P > .9). However, monitored cases were performed at significantly greater nadir temperatures (28.3 °C vs 24.0 °C; P < .001). Among elective cases, monitored procedures demonstrated slightly longer cerebral perfusion times (29 minutes vs 20 minutes; P = .029), whereas emergent nonmonitored cases had longer perfusion times and lower bypass temperatures compared with monitored elective operations (P < .001 for both).

Perioperative Outcomes

Major intraoperative neurologic complications occurred significantly less often in monitored cases compared with nonmonitored cases (3.8% vs 29%; P < .001) (Table 2). This difference remained robust when we compared elective monitored with elective nonmonitored cases (3.8% vs 21%; P = .007) and when compared with emergent nonmonitored cases (3.8% vs 39%; P < .001). Postoperative major neurologic complications occurred in 2.6% of patients who were monitored versus 7.1% of patients who were not monitored (P = .2), whereas minor postoperative neurologic complications were similar between groups (2.6% vs 0%; P = .5). In-hospital mortality was greater in patients who were not monitored compared with patients who were monitored (19.6% vs 5.1%, P = .012). Nonmonitored emergent cases also had greater mortality rates than monitored elective cases (21.7% vs 5.1%, P = .027), whereas nonmonitored elective cases showed a nonsignificant trend (18.2% vs 5.1%, P = .062).

Median LOS was shorter in the monitored cohort compared with nonmonitored (9 vs 13 days; P = .017). Patients who were monitored during elective surgery also had shorter stays than patients who were not monitored during emergent surgery (9 vs 14 days; P = .003). Intubation duration differed markedly by monitoring status. Median intubation time was 20 hours in monitored cases versus 46 hours in nonmonitored (P = .007). When stratified by urgency, patients who were monitored during elective surgery had shorter intubation times compared with emergent nonmonitored cases (20 vs 95 hours; P < .001). Rates of prolonged mechanical ventilation >48 hours were also significantly lower in the monitored group (22% vs 48%; P = .003). This difference persisted when we compared elective monitored versus emergent nonmonitored (22% vs 57%; P = .003) and trended toward significance when compared with elective nonmonitored (22% vs 42%; P = .05).

BAEP-Specific Findings

Among the 78 patients who were monitored, clinically significant BAEP changes (defined as >50% reduction in wave V amplitude or >1.0 millisecond prolongation of I-V interpeak latency) were observed in 6 patients (7.7%). Of these, 4 resolved with corrective interventions (blood pressure augmentation, ACP flow adjustment, or slowing of the rewarming gradient), and 2 patients with persistent BAEP changes had postoperative imaging confirming posterior fossa or brainstem infarcts. In both cases of confirmed posterior circulation injury, concurrent EEG and SSEP remained unchanged, indicating that BAEPs were the sole modality to detect these events. Among the 16 patients with major intraoperative neurologic complications who were not monitored, postoperative imaging revealed cerebellar or brainstem infarction in 3 patients (18.8%), events that would have been within the detection range of BAEP monitoring.

Multivariate Analyses

IONM was independently associated with reduced odds of major neurologic complications (OR, 0.31; P = .020) and decreased likelihood of prolonged mechanical ventilation (OR, 0.38; P = .032) (Figure 4; Table 3). IONM did not significantly affect overall LOS or intubation time after adjustment. In the linear regression model using log-transformed LOS, no independent association was observed between IONM use and hospital stay (eβ = −0.17; P = .186). For the log-transformed intubation time model, emergent surgery (eβ = −0.93; P = .016) and male sex (eβ = −0.53; P = .040) were associated with shorter intubation time.

Discussion

IONM is well established in both open and endovascular repair of the descending thoracic aorta7,8,10,11,13,14 and has also gained increasing acceptance during carotid endarterectomy.15 In a previous study, the authors reported that multimodal neuromonitoring was sensitive for early stroke detection but was limited by a low positive predictive value.6 They found no statistically significant difference in postoperative stroke rates between patients with and without IONM.6 However, their study was a proof of concept for identifying neurologic complications on the basis of multimodal monitoring without interoperative interventions and as such would not be expected to change outcomes. Their discussion describes multiple interventions they now use on the basis of monitoring findings such as retrograde cerebral perfusion and blood pressure augmentation. Our study attempts to limit patient bias through stratification by urgency, offering a more balanced assessment of IONM's independent association with neurologic and recovery outcomes. We demonstrate that multimodal IONM during major aortic surgeries with HCA is associated with significantly lower rates of intraoperative neurologic complications. In addition, patients are more likely to be extubated within 48 hours. These findings reinforce the utility of IONM as a real-time adjunct for detecting and mitigating cerebral ischemia, supporting its integration into routine practice for both elective and emergent aortic arch surgeries.

Importantly, all patients in this study with IONM signal loss and available postoperative imaging showed radiologic evidence of corresponding cerebral injury, reinforcing the clinical reliability of IONM. The absence of IONM changes during aortic arch surgery has a strikingly high negative predictive value of 97.1%, suggesting its excellent utility in informing the surgeon of the exceedingly low risk of early neurologic injury.6,7 At our institution, IONM is routinely applied in aortic arch operations. In the past 6 years, analysis of more than 365 multimodal IONM changes over 134 cases have revealed that 96% of alarms could be reversed within 2 attempts of corrective action by either the surgeon, perfusionist, or anesthesiologist. Together, these findings suggest that IONM plays a critical role in enabling timely intraoperative interventions that improve neurologic and perioperative outcomes, ultimately supporting better long-term recovery after high-risk aortic arch repair.

We have learned that IONM can alert the surgical team to impending adverse neurologic events well before a postoperative neurologic examination is possible, thereby enabling earlier diagnosis and timely endovascular intervention when indicated.16 Common alarm types and corresponding interventions included (1) unilateral EEG amplitude reduction >50% with concurrent ipsilateral SSEP latency prolongation, typically resolved by increasing mean arterial pressure or adjusting ACP flow; (2) bilateral SSEP amplitude loss during cooling, managed by deepening hypothermia or initiating ACP earlier; (3) TCD asymmetry >50% during ACP, prompting surgical inspection and repositioning of cerebral perfusion cannulae; (4) NIRS desaturation >20% from baseline, addressed by CPB flow augmentation and head repositioning; and (5) BAEP wave V prolongation during rewarming, managed by slowing the rewarming gradient and blood pressure augmentation.16 The presence of IONM alarms should prompt immediate cross-sectional cerebrovascular imaging as able to assess potentially treatable vascular occlusions. Nonetheless, IONM is not infallible. In one study, 2.3% of patients (n = 13) went on to develop an early adverse neurologic event despite no intraoperative IONM alarms, 5 of whom had exhibited large-vessel distribution infarcts that were not detected during monitoring.7 These limitations highlight opportunities for further refinement of IONM technology and multimodal integration to improve detection of subtle or deep ischemic injuries.

EEG and SSEP assess cortical and subcortical activity but are less effective for detecting ischemia in deep or posterior circulation, especially the brainstem and cerebellum. BAEPs bridge this gap by monitoring auditory pathway transmission through the pons and midbrain, offering continuous surveillance of regions otherwise inaccessible to cortical-based modalities. In our cohort, BAEP changes were identified in 6 patients, and in 2 cases of confirmed posterior fossa infarction, BAEPs were the sole modality to detect the event whereas EEG and SSEP remained unchanged. This finding underscores the unique contribution of BAEPs to comprehensive neuromonitoring, particularly in complex arch anatomy or cases with vertebrobasilar compromise. This is particularly useful in complex arch anatomy or cases with vertebrobasilar compromise or retrograde cerebral perfusion, where posterior circulation is vulnerable.17 TCD adds real-time hemodynamic context by tracking cerebral flow velocity and hemispheric perfusion balance, allowing rapid detection of malperfusion or cardioembolic events during perfusion transitions and rewarming.18 NIRS, although less specific, provides continuous regional oxygenation data that complements electrophysiologic monitoring. When integrated, NIRS may help distinguish true ischemia from artifact by correlating desaturation with concurrent EEG or SSEP changes. Thus, the integration of NIRS and TCD with EEG and evoked potential monitoring may provide complementary hemodynamic and oxygenation data that, on the basis of previous literature, has been suggested to improve the overall detection of cerebral ischemia compared with electrophysiologic monitoring alone.17, 18, 19, 20

The utility of IONM during deep HCA warrants further comment. At these temperatures, electrophysiologic signals (EEG, SSEP, BAEP, TcMEP) become isoelectric, which is expected and not indicative of pathology. However, IONM retains value in 3 key phases: (1) during cooling, signal attenuation that deviates from expected patterns or occurs asymmetrically may indicate focal ischemia before electrocerebral silence is achieved; (2) during rewarming, the speed, symmetry, and completeness of signal recovery is a sensitive indicator of brain viability and reperfusion quality while delayed or asymmetric recovery prompts immediate investigation; and (3) after full rewarming, failure to return to baseline signals constitutes an alarm for new ischemic injury. TCD and NIRS remain operational at all temperatures, providing continuous hemodynamic monitoring even during periods of electrophysiologic silence. Among the 11 deep hypothermia cases with IONM in our series, all demonstrated expected signal loss during circulatory arrest with subsequent recovery during rewarming, and none experienced perioperative neurologic complications.

In adult cardiac surgery, prolonged mechanical ventilation from postoperative respiratory failure is associated with significant mortality and morbidity.19 The increased likelihood of extubation before 48 hours in our monitored cohort is consistent with prior data linking better intraoperative cerebral physiology to fewer major organ complications and faster recovery.20 Desaturation and embolic load detected intraoperatively via NIRS and TCD, respectively correlate with postoperative neurocognitive dysfunction and other complications that can delay extubation. Limiting these insults facilitates earlier awakening and neurologic assessment, fewer reintubations, and less ventilator time.21 The consistent favorable trend direction for IONM and the approaching significance for prolonged ventilation suggest that IONM's most pronounced benefits in recovery metrics may be in preventing catastrophic neurologic events rather than incrementally shortening all recoveries. It is also important to recognize that preventing major neurologic events may offset the incremental cost of IONM.8 Our findings expand the clinical relevance of IONM, underscoring its potential impact on perioperative resource use and overall patient recovery trajectory.

Limitations

This observational, single-center study carries inherent selection and institutional bias. The absence of IONM in emergent cases represents the most significant confound, as these patients carry greater operative risk from hemodynamic instability, malperfusion syndromes, and acute dissection pathology. Although our elective-only subgroup analysis demonstrated significantly lower intraoperative neurologic complication rates with IONM, the small number of nonmonitored elective patients limits statistical power, and their concentration in the early program phase (2018-2020) introduces potential temporal confounding from evolving surgical technique. The greater prevalence of significant aortic atheroma in the nonmonitored group may further confound results. In addition, intraoperative corrective actions prompted by IONM changes were not systematically captured, preventing assessment of the specific mechanisms by which IONM may improve outcomes.

Interpretation of multimodal IONM requires specialized expertise and dedicated infrastructure, limiting generalizability. Signal changes can reflect anesthetic depth, temperature fluctuation, perfusion pressure, or technical artifact, necessitating real-time differentiation from true ischemic insults by an experienced neurophysiology team. Formal neurologic assessments were not routinely performed on all patients, particularly those without clinically apparent deficits, which is relevant given the low complication rates in the IONM group. Furthermore, the study was not designed to assess the incremental diagnostic accuracy of individual monitoring modalities, and granular data on cause-specific mortality and perioperative atrial fibrillation were not systematically captured. Future prospective studies should incorporate protocol-mandated neurologic evaluation, routine postoperative imaging, and determination of optimal modality combinations with standardized response pathways.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that multimodal IONM during major aortic surgery is both feasible and clinically impactful. At our institution, a dedicated neuromonitoring team present throughout each procedure enables continuous data interpretation and immediate feedback to the surgical and anesthesia teams. IONM facilitates early detection and reversal of cerebral ischemia, translating into lower neurologic complication rates and improved perioperative recovery. Although larger multicenter studies are warranted to validate these findings, this study reinforces existing evidence supporting the American Heart Association's recommendation and advocates that comprehensive multimodal IONM should be regarded as a standard component of neuroprotection in contemporary aortic arch reconstruction.
