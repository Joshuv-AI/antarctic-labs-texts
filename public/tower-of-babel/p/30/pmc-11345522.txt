Acute upper airway obstruction caused by cervical osteophytes in diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis: A case report
Authors: Ineke M.J. Pruijn, Henri A.M. Marres, Guido B. van den Broek
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importanceThe manifestation of acute upper airway obstruction, coupled with dysphagia and potential vocal cord paresis, typically raises suspicion of advanced laryngeal or pharyngeal malignancy. However, this case report illuminates an unusual systemic condition— diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH) with cervical osteophytes—that can resemble the clinical presentation of these malignancies.Case presentationTwo cases involving 78-year and 82-year-old males exhibiting progressive dysphagia, dysphonia, and acute upper airway obstruction due to substantial osteophytes at the C2-C4 levels are presented.Clinical discussion and conclusionClinicians should consider DISH in the differential diagnosis of dysphagia and upper airway obstruction after ruling out laryngeal and pharyngeal malignancies. Timely recognition and appropriate management of DISH has proven effective in relieving symptoms and restoring normal swallowing and breathing, preventing acute life-saving interventions, contributing to improved patient outcomes.

Introduction

Acute upper airway obstruction, dysphagia and vocal cord paralysis at elderly age can be highly suggestive symptoms of progressed pharyngeal or laryngeal malignancies or severe infections. Diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH), characterized by calcification and ossification of spinal ligaments and the regions where tendons and ligaments attach to bone, is a noninflammatory systemic condition able to mimic these malignancies particularly through the development of large cervical osteophytes.

We present two cases, in line with the SCARE criteria [1], of patients with cervical osteophytes resulting from DISH, manifesting in the distressing symptoms of dysphagia and upper airway obstruction.

Case presentation

A 78-year-old male presented to the emergency unit with progressive dyspnea, accompanied by inspiratory stridor due to acute upper airway obstruction. His relevant medical history revealed progressive dysphagia, weight loss, hoarseness and 15 packyears of smoking cigarettes. Immediate intubation secured his airway. Flexible laryngoscopy revealed laryngeal edema at the arytenoids and postcricoid tissue surplus. Suspecting laryngeal cancer, the patient was referred to our head and neck oncology center for further diagnosis and treatment.

In the intensive care unit, blood tests showed no signs of infection. A CT scan revealed no masses or suspicious lymph nodes but displayed extended anterior cervical osteophytes (Fig. 1). Direct laryngoscopy in the operating room confirmed prominent anterior osteophytes causing upper airway obstruction from the level of epiglottis to the subglottic region. The mucosa was normal, showing no signs of laryngeal or pharyngeal cancer and no biopsies were taken. Tracheotomy was performed. The otorhinolaryngologist and orthopedic surgeon jointly performed surgery through an anterior cervical approach via the left side and removed osteophytes from C2-C4 piecemeal (Fig. 2). Postoperative X-rays confirmed osteophyte removal and swallowing noticeably improved. Tracheotomy tube removal occurred a few days later, and the patient was discharged in good condition. A second patient, an 82-year-old male, presented with progressing symptoms of dysphagia and acute dyspnea during eating since several years. Discussion

Diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH), also known as Forestier disease or ankylosing hyperostosis, is commonly diagnosed using the Resnick and Niwayama criteria, which require the presence of flowing ossification along the anterolateral spine, over at least four contiguous vertebral bodies, with preservation of intervertebral disc height and without apophyseal joint ankylosis or sacroiliac joint changes. Incidence of DISH increases with age, with a mean age at diagnosis of 67 (SD 8.6; range 35–91) in affected patients, and is more prevalent in males (6:1) [2]. Differential diagnoses include spondylosis deformans, ankylosing spondylitis and ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament, although ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament may also occur in up to 50 % of patients with DISH.

The exact pathogenesis of DISH remains unclear, however various genetic, dietary and metabolic conditions are hypothesized to contribute to the abnormal osteoblastic differentiation and activity resulting in bone formation distinctive of DISH. Especially growth factors such as insulin, insulin-like growth factor and growth hormone are hypothesized to be involved in the pathogenesis of DISH as the prevalence of DISH is higher in people with obesity, type 2 diabetes, the metabolic syndrome and acromegaly.

The diversity in symptoms in patients with DISH is closely linked to the location of the osteophytes. Patients with thoracic or lumbar DISH typically present with musculoskeletal symptoms such as neck and/or back pain, morning stiffness, or a reduced range of motion, and they have an increased risk of fractures [3]. However, it is noteworthy that there are individuals with radiologically confirmed DISH who remain asymptomatic.

The incidence of cervical dysphagia contributary to DISH is estimated at 7:100.000 [4], yet dysphagia is a common symptom in patients with cervical involvement DISH (99 %) [2]. Osteophytes may manifest from C1 to C7, but the most affected vertebrae are typically C3-C5 [2]. Anterior, predominantly midline, osteophytes at C3-C7 can compress the esophagus, laryngeal recurrent nerves, or airway. If these osteophytes are larger, they may displace or compromise these structures, resulting in dysphagia, vocal cord paresis, challenges during intubation or eventually airway obstruction [5]. During laryngopharyngoscopy this may be seen as a protruding posterior pharyngeal wall, impairing visibility of the postcricoid or laryngeal region. Especially since these osteophytes progress gradually and mucosa remains normal, they may be hard to recognize as a cause of dysphagia. Additionally, chronic mechanical compression may result in local inflammatory reactions, causing edema, fibrous tissue formation and adhesions around the esophagus and soft tissues [6]. Nevertheless, acute upper airway obstruction requiring life-saving intervention, either by intubation or tracheostomy, is exceedingly rare and has been described in only 11 out of 419 patients (2.6 %) with cervical DISH [2].

The typical approach for treating symptomatic DISH is conservative, targeting perceived symptoms through physical and swallowing therapy, exercise, and symptomatic pain management. Nevertheless, surgical management becomes necessary in cases where patients exhibit progressive myelopathy, nerve root compression, thoracic outlet syndrome, dysphagia unresponsive to conservative measures, or airway obstruction due to cervical osteophytes.

Surgical treatment usually involves osteophytectomy through an anterolateral cervical approach, resulting in improved dysphagia in 95.5 % of the patients [2]. In both the presented patients the surgery was performed by an otolaryngologist and an orthopedic surgeon. Both patients underwent general anesthesia. The incision was made in one of the skin creases on the left side, along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle (SCM), using the so-called anterolateral cervical route. Although both left- and right-sided access can offer good exposure, we typically prefer the left side due to the recurrent laryngeal nerve's more medial and consistent path. This approach, based on our experience, provides excellent and rapid exposure with familiar anatomy and relatively low morbidity. After the incision through the platysma, exposure of the retropharyngeal and prevertebral space was visualized when the laryngotracheal–pharyngoesophageal complex was retracted medially and the SCM and carotid sheath were retracted laterally. Incision of the prevertebral fascia and retracting it laterally along with the longus colli muscles provided access to the osteophytes. Carefully the osteophytes were removed piece by piece using an osteotome. Palpatory control was employed to avoid sharp edges and prevent damage to the esophagus, along with intraoperative X-ray imaging of the cervical vertebrae. Afterward, the wound was closed in layers, and a drain was left in place. In literature, osteophyte regrowth was observed in 2 % of patients during a mean follow-up of 3.7 years, necessitating secondary resection in three out of five patients [2].

Conclusion

DISH is a rare and underreported cause of dysphagia and upper airway obstruction in the elderly patient. Timely diagnosis and treatment can avert complications such as vocal cord paresis and acute upper airway obstruction, resulting in a potential emergency tracheostomy. Laryngeal or pharyngeal malignancies presenting with similar symptoms should always be ruled out. Surgical treatment through an anterior cervical approach shows excellent results with restored swallowing and breathing after osteophyte removal.
