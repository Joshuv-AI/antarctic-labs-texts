ABSTRACT
Background:In Australia, chronic liver disease is now the third leading cause of premature death in people aged 50–59 years, and the fourth leading cause in all other 5-year age groups from 35 to 70 years in Australia. A consensus statement on the diagnosis and management of portal hypertension in patients with cirrhosis was developed by the Gastroenterological Society of Australia (GESA), the peak national organization for health care professionals and researchers working in gastroenterology and hepatology, to provide contemporary, evidence-based clinical guidelines. A summary of its recommendations is presented here.Methods:The consensus statement was developed in accordance with the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II instrument and the 2016 National Health and Medical Research Council Standards for Guidelines. A panel of 46 health care professionals with expertise in managing portal hypertension participated in the development of the consensus statement. The consensus development conference method was used to determine consensus for each recommendation, with a hybrid face-to-face and online meeting held in Noosa, Australia, on 3 August 2024, followed by 2 online meetings.Results:A total of 52 recommendations were approved by the consensus development group without dissent across the following domains: (1) diagnosis, risk stratification, and prevention of decompensation; (2) portal hypertension–related bleeding; (3) ascites and renal impairment; (4) hepatic encephalopathy; (5) sarcopenia, nutrition, and frailty; (6) thrombosis in cirrhosis; (7) cardiopulmonary complications; (8) surgery and portal hypertension; and (9) pregnancy and portal hypertension.Discussion:This is the first Australian consensus statement on the diagnosis and management of portal hypertension in patients with cirrhosis. The recommendations in this summary document provide standardized clinical guidance with the goal of improving outcomes for patients with cirrhosis and portal hypertension in Australia.

INTRODUCTION

The complications of portal hypertension are responsible for most of the morbidity and mortality in patients with cirrhosis. The Australian Burden of Disease Study (ABDS) 2024 found that the health burden from chronic liver disease (CLD) in disability-adjusted life years had increased 16.6% since 2003 and was higher in 2024 than in all 4 previous ABDS reports.1 CLD is now the third leading cause of premature death (measured by years of life lost) in people aged 50–59 years, and the fourth leading cause in all other 5-year age groups from 35 to 70 years.1 For Indigenous Australians, the standardized death rate from CLD is almost 3-fold higher than in non-Indigenous Australians.2 Of further concern is the predicted rising burden of CLD, predominantly due to increasing rates of metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD).3 In Australia, specialist hepatology services are concentrated in metropolitan centers, with CLD care in regional or remote areas often being provided by general gastroenterologists, general physicians, general practitioners, specialist nurses, or other health care professionals.

A consensus statement on the diagnosis and management of portal hypertension in patients with cirrhosis was developed by the Gastroenterological Society of Australia (GESA), the peak national organization for health care professionals and researchers working in gastroenterology and hepatology, to provide contemporary, evidence-based clinical guidelines specific to the Australian context. A summary of its recommendations is presented here, with the complete consensus statement available on the GESA website.4

METHODS

The consensus statement was developed in accordance with the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation (AGREE) II instrument5 and the 2016 National Health and Medical Research Council Standards for Guidelines.6 A panel of 46 health care professionals with expertise in managing portal hypertension participated in the development of the consensus statement (Supplemental Table S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C284). Key clinical questions in PICO (patient, intervention, control, outcome) format were developed to inform the literature review and subsequent recommendations (Supplemental Table S2, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C284).

The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach was used to appraise the certainty of evidence.7 Accordingly, the quality of evidence was assessed for each recommendation as high, moderate, low, or very low, with the strength of recommendation being either strong or weak. The consensus development conference method68 was used to determine consensus for each recommendation, with a hybrid face-to-face and online meeting held in Noosa, Australia, on 3 August 2024, followed by 2 online meetings. The project co-chairs and steering committee members were responsible for ensuring consensus was reached among consensus development conference participants and that all feedback was acknowledged and documented for the consensus meetings. It was established a priori that if the consensus development conference participants did not reach agreement on a recommendation, then a majority vote (>50%) of the 9 steering committee members would be required. If disagreement remained, the matter would be resolved by the 3 project co-chairs (Avik Majumdar, Golo Ahlenstiel, Simone I. Strasser). All 52 final recommendations were approved by the consensus development group without dissent.

The term compensated advanced chronic liver disease has been adopted by the Baveno consensus committee to better describe the continuum of severe liver fibrosis, where patients with CLD who are at risk of decompensation events can be identified using non-invasive tests (NITs).9 However, cirrhosis is still regarded as an acceptable term in Baveno VII consensus guidelines and remains in common use in Australia in both specialist and non-specialist settings. Therefore, cirrhosis has been used throughout this consensus statement to differentiate from non-cirrhotic portal hypertension, which is outside the scope of this consensus statement, and to avoid ambiguity for the target audience.

RECOMMENDATIONS

Diagnosis, risk stratification, and prevention of decompensation

Cirrhosis is characterized by extensive liver fibrosis leading to architectural distortion with nodule formation and affects about 1 in 200 Australians.1011 With the widespread use of NITs, liver biopsy performed solely for histological confirmation of cirrhosis is now uncommon.12 Compensated cirrhosis is a stable phase of disease that is associated with a good long-term prognosis if the cause of cirrhosis is treated. Portal hypertension may exist in patients with compensated cirrhosis, but without clinical signs of decompensation. Decompensated cirrhosis is defined by the presence of ascites, variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy (HE), or non-obstructive jaundice. Decompensation is associated with significantly reduced short-term survival in the absence of liver transplantation.9131415 The prognostic role of non-obstructive jaundice alone in defining clinical decompensation is currently being challenged.9161718

Clinically significant portal hypertension (CSPH) is defined by a hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) of 10 mm Hg or greater and is the threshold for the formation of varices. The presence of CSPH accurately identifies patients who are at risk of decompensation.91920 Although measurement of HVPG is only available in specialized centers, CSPH can be detected non-invasively. In patients with compensated cirrhosis, CSPH can be diagnosed by the presence of endoscopic varices or of portosystemic collaterals on imaging, or using NITs.

Cirrhosis can be diagnosed with a combination of clinical assessment, NITs, and conventional imaging, with liver biopsy only required where non-invasive assessment is inconclusive. Commonly used NITs include serum tests, such as FIB-4 (fibrosis-4), APRI (AST to platelet ratio index), Hepascore, and ELF (enhanced liver fibrosis test), or liver elastography using vibration-controlled transient elastography (FibroScan), ultrasound shear wave elastography [such as two-dimensional shearwave elastography (2D-SWE) or acoustic radiation force impulse imaging (ARFI)], or magnetic resonance elastography.12212223 The “rule of 5” proposed in the Baveno consensus statement provides a pragmatic approach to using liver stiffness measurement (LSM) with FibroScan for the assessment of cirrhosis and CSPH.9 The following LSM cut-offs are recommended:<5 kPa: normal5 to <10 kPa: cirrhosis unlikely10 to <15 kPa: possible cirrhosis15 to <20 kPa: cirrhosis confirmed20 to <25 kPa: cirrhosis and possible CSPH (confirmed CSPH if platelets <150×109/L)≥25 kPa: cirrhosis with CSPH confirmed.

Preventing decompensation in patients with compensated cirrhosis improves long-term survival. Treatment of the underlying cause of CLD is essential and is the optimal treatment strategy to prevent decompensation. In addition, all patients with CSPH should be considered for non-selective beta-blocker (NSBB) therapy to prevent decompensation and death.262728 Carvedilol is preferred because of its better tolerability and efficacy at the target dosage of 12.5 mg daily (as either 6.25 mg twice daily or 12.5 mg once daily), presumably due to the additional effect of alpha-1 adrenergic blockade on intrahepatic resistance.29 The dosage can be increased up to 25 mg in patients with hypertension. If propranolol is used, the minimal target dosage is 40 mg twice daily (up to 160 mg twice daily), titrating to a heart rate of 60 beats/min.17 Systolic blood pressure should be maintained above the renal perfusion pressure threshold of 90 mm Hg (equivalent mean arterial pressure, 65 mm Hg) for patients taking an NSBB. Furthermore, NSBBs should be avoided in patients with acute kidney injury (AKI), pre-existing bradycardia, or hypotension (see Recommendation 24).9151730

The number needed to treat in the PREDESCI study to prevent 1 episode of death or decompensation with NSBB therapy was 9, underscoring the importance of being confident in establishing a diagnosis of CSPH or risking overtreatment.26 Endoscopic screening for varices is no longer required for patients receiving target doses of NSBBs.

In patients in whom invasive or non-invasive assessment of CSPH is inconclusive, or NSBBs cannot be given, assessment for the presence or absence of high-risk varices should be performed. High-risk varices are confluent esophageal varices, with a diameter of 5 mm or greater, and are synonymous with “grade II or III varices,” “varices needing treatment,” and “large varices.”9 An LSM <20 kPa, together with a platelet count of ≥150×109/L, safely excludes patients with high-risk varices, with a negative predictive value over 95%.3132 Endoscopy is not required in these patients, and repeat LSM and platelet count should be performed annually.9 In patients where either LSM is ≥20 kPa or platelet count is <150×109/L, screening endoscopy or, where available, spleen stiffness measurement (SSM), using FibroScan should be performed. An SSM cut-off of 40 kPa using a dedicated 100 Hz probe can further reduce the need for screening endoscopy to rule out high-risk varices.933 For primary prophylaxis of variceal bleeding, NSBBs or endoscopic variceal band ligation (EVBL) can be performed. In patients with high-risk varices, EVBL should be repeated 1–3 months until eradicated, then endoscopic surveillance continued.

New decompensation events represent an increase in portal pressure; hence, endoscopy is recommended as part of the assessment of decompensation, regardless of whether NSBBs are being taken.15 The GESA Cirrhosis Care Bundle is available to guide management of patients with decompensated cirrhosis requiring hospitalization during the first 24 hours at the point of care (https://www.gesa.org.au/resources/clinical-practice-resources/).34

Portal hypertension-related bleeding

Acute variceal bleeding is a medical emergency. After stabilization and resuscitation of the patient, urgent upper gastrointestinal endoscopy should be performed within 12 hours.935 Restrictive use of blood products is recommended due to the effect of increasing portal pressure and other inherent transfusion-related adverse events. In the event of massive blood loss, massive transfusion protocols should be activated according to local practice (generally transfusion of between 6 and 10 units of packed red blood cells).3637 Vasoactive therapy and antibiotics should be initiated as soon as possible.1535 Routine use of proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) should be avoided because of the risk of infection.938 If PPIs are commenced in the course of early management of undifferentiated gastrointestinal bleeding, these should be discontinued once variceal bleeding is confirmed. Similarly, in the setting of undifferentiated bleeding, the use of octreotide will raise the gastric pH to the same extent as PPIs and therefore, PPIs are not required. PPIs do not appear to reduce the risk of band-induced ulcer bleeding or mortality if EVBL is performed.39

If initial hemostasis cannot be achieved at endoscopy, variceal tamponade is recommended, using balloon tamponade or the Danis self-expanding metal stent for esophageal varices. Danis stents have the advantages of fewer complications and earlier extubation and feeding compared with balloon tamponade.4041 Patients should be considered on a case-by-case basis for salvage transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) implantation for definitive management. A GESA consensus statement contains recommendations on the use of TIPS, including in the setting of uncontrolled hemorrhage.42 If TIPS is unavailable, patients should be urgently transferred to a center that offers TIPS implantation once variceal tamponade is established, and the patient is stabilized.

The endoscopic therapy for patients with actively bleeding esophageal varices or with recent stigmata of bleeding (fibrin plug or red signs) is EVBL, whereas the initial endoscopic management of gastric varices is often dependent on local expertise. Gastric varices are most often subtyped according to Sarin’s classification based on their location in the stomach and their relationship to esophageal varices, which then determines management (Supplemental Figure S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C284).4344 Specifically, gastroesophageal varices type 1 (GOV1), which are an extension of esophageal varices along the lesser curve of the stomach, can be managed by decompressing the column of esophageal varices with EVBL, with or without gastric variceal injection. Cardiofundal varices [gastroesophageal varices type 2 (GOV2) and isolated gastric varices type 1 (IGV1)] are managed endoscopically with either cyanoacrylate injection, thrombin injection, or endoscopic ultrasound-guided coiling.454647 Isolated gastric varices type 2 (IGV2) are uncommon; hence, data are lacking to suggest standardized treatment for these. Once initial hemostasis is achieved, cross-sectional portal venous phase imaging should be performed to understand the vascular anatomy of GOV2, IGV1, or IGV2 varices and ascertain whether further therapy is required. This may be further endoscopic therapy, transvenous obliteration (TVO), or TIPS. TVO is usually performed in the setting of a gastrorenal or splenorenal portosystemic shunt associated with cardiofundal varices. TVO encompasses several interventional radiology techniques, including balloon-assisted, coil-assisted, or plug-assisted retrograde transvenous obliteration (BRTO, CARTO, or PARTO) or antegrade venous obliteration (ATO).1748 Multidisciplinary decision making between interventional radiology, endoscopy, and hepatology specialists is essential.

After achieving endoscopic hemostasis, all patients who meet the recommended criteria should be considered for pre-emptive TIPS implantation to prevent early rebleeding and improve long-term survival.4950 For those in whom TIPS is not indicated or cannot be performed, an NSBB should be commenced before discharge from the hospital for secondary prophylaxis of variceal hemorrhage. Carvedilol is again the preferred NSBB, but propranolol can be used if target doses can be achieved. Precipitants for variceal bleeding should be actively investigated. Intravenous antibiotics and vasoactive therapy can be ceased between 2 and 5 days after successful hemostasis. Repeat endoscopic therapy should be performed every 1–3 months until variceal eradication is achieved. Once varices are eradicated, a surveillance endoscopy can be performed at 6 months, followed by indefinite annual endoscopic surveillance.35

An NSBB or routine endoscopy is not required for patients who have received a TIPS. However, regular Doppler ultrasound to assess stent patency should be performed at 1 week and then 3 months after TIPS implantation, followed by 6-monthly, with specific attention to portal vein velocity inferior to the TIPS.42

Severe portal hypertensive gastropathy can be challenging to manage. NSBBs and iron replacement remain the primary medical therapies.51 TIPS can be considered in patients with severe portal hypertensive gastropathy requiring frequent blood transfusions and without response to medical treatment.4252

Ascites and renal impairment

Ascites is present in up to 50% of patients with cirrhosis. Its severity is graded according to abdominal distension as mild (detectable by ultrasound only), moderate (clinically detectable), or severe (marked or tense abdominal distension). Uncomplicated ascites is when there is no evidence of infection or renal impairment.

A “no-added-salt” diet, with the aim of consuming no more than 2300 mg of sodium (equivalent to 6 g or 1 teaspoon of table salt) per day, is recommended for all patients with ascites. More aggressive sodium restriction may make food unpalatable and contribute to sarcopenia. A GESA patient resource on reducing salt intake is available (https://www.gesa.org.au/resources/patient-resources/).53

There is no role for water restriction in reducing ascites. Mobilization of moderate to severe ascites requires the addition of diuretic therapy with spironolactone (up to 400 mg/d) and furosemide (up to 160 mg/d). Patients with ascites that is refractory to diuretic therapy require large-volume paracentesis (LVP). During LVP, albumin replacement (20 g for every 2 L of ascites drained over 5 L) is recommended to prevent post-paracentesis circulatory dysfunction.155455 Regular intravenous albumin infusion using the ANSWER trial protocol of 40 mg of albumin twice weekly for 2 weeks, then weekly albumin, may be used as adjunctive therapy to mobilize ascites and improve outcomes.56 The use of weekly albumin in patients with refractory ascites is not as well established but appears to have some benefit.575859 NSBBs can be continued in patients with refractory ascites, provided their systolic blood pressure is maintained above 90 mm Hg, and there is no evidence of AKI.6061 If hyponatremia (sodium <130 mmol/L) is present, NSBBs should be withheld because of their potential effects on renal hypoperfusion.1562

Patients with refractory ascites should be assessed for TIPS implantation. If TIPS is contraindicated, liver transplant referral should be considered for appropriate candidates.42 Long-term ascites catheters in patients with refractory ascites should only be considered in the palliative care setting, given the risks of infection and other complications.63

Hepatorenal syndrome (HRS) is a complication of refractory ascites that occurs due to renal hypoperfusion and is associated with high rates of mortality. HRS is further subclassified by the International Club of Ascites into HRS-AKI and HRS–non-acute kidney injury (HRS-NAKI). HRS-AKI is defined as an increase in serum creatinine by 26.5 μmol/L or more within 48 hours, or ≥50% from the baseline value, without another cause of AKI and lack of response to adequate correction of hypovolemia within 24 hours. HRS-NAKI (previously type 2 HRS) is the persistence of HRS beyond 7 days and is further divided into HRS–acute kidney disease (HRS for <90 days) or HRS–chronic kidney disease (HRS >90 days).6264

Terlipressin given concurrently with albumin should be commenced as soon as the diagnosis of HRS-AKI is made, as delayed introduction is associated with poorer outcomes. Terlipressin can be given as a bolus injection (starting dose, 0.85 mg 6-hourly) or as a continuous infusion, with doses ranging between 1.7 and 3.4 mg/day. Continuous infusions are at least equally efficacious but have been shown to be associated with fewer adverse events than bolus injections. Dosing of both bolus and infusion terlipressin can be escalated every 48–72 hours based on response. Treatment can be given for up to 14 days.646566

Recurrent hepatic hydrothorax should be managed similarly to ascites. Patients should be considered for regular LVP and/or thoracentesis, TIPS, or liver transplantation. Due to the risk of infection, indwelling intercostal catheters are not recommended.67

Following discussion with a liver transplant center, transplant-eligible patients with refractory ascites or hydrothorax and/or HRS-NAKI may be considered for an ambulatory continuous terlipressin infusion as a bridge to transplantation. This treatment has been shown to reduce ascites accumulation and improve renal function, nutritional status, and quality of life.686970 An established hospital-in-the-home service and appropriate line-care are mandatory.

Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) is another complication of refractory ascites. Due to a lack of efficacy and rising concerns about multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs) in patients with decompensated cirrhosis, primary SBP prophylaxis is no longer recommended.7172 Secondary SBP prophylaxis with norfloxacin 400 mg daily or trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole 800 mg/160 mg daily remains recommended, although there are also concerns about the emergence of MDROs.

Hepatic encephalopathy

HE in patients with cirrhosis and portal hypertension is a clinical diagnosis with neuropsychiatric features ranging from mild cognitive impairment to coma.73 The disease spectrum can be classified into 2 broad categories—covert and overt HE74 —and graded further by the West Haven criteria (Table 1). The modalities used to diagnose covert HE include the Psychometric Hepatic Encephalopathy Score (PHES), Stroop test, electroencephalography, critical flicker frequency, inhibitory control test, and continuous reaction time.767778798081828384 The PHES and Stroop test have the advantage of being implementable without major equipment, although PHES has not been normalized to the Australian population. Lactulose is the initial treatment of choice and should be commenced on clinical suspicion of overt HE.87 Lactulose is an inexpensive, non-absorbable disaccharide that acts in the intestine to reduce ammonia load. For patients who do not tolerate lactulose or who experience a further episode of overt HE despite lactulose treatment, the non-absorbable antibiotic rifaximin is used.8889 Rifaximin (550 mg twice daily) acts on the intestinal microbiota to reduce ammonia production.88 Unlike other antibiotic classes previously studied in overt HE and due to its lack of systemic absorption, rifaximin is well tolerated with minimal side effects, providing an option for long-term use. Branched-chain amino acids can also be considered as adjunctive therapy as part of nutritional supplementation (see Recommendation 41).9091 The importance of addressing sarcopenia in patients with HE is discussed below. Large spontaneous portosystemic shunts are present in up to 28% of patients with cirrhosis and are implicated in HE.9293 Radiologic embolization of spontaneous portosystemic shunts has been shown to reduce hospitalization for HE in well-selected patients.94 Patients who have overt HE and associated poor quality of life despite optimal management should be considered for liver transplantation.

Sarcopenia, nutrition, and frailty

Malnutrition, sarcopenia, and frailty are interrelated complications of cirrhosis that are highly prevalent and independent predictors of mortality. Simple criteria for stratifying patients at high risk of malnutrition include a BMI <18.5 kg/m2 and decompensated cirrhosis.95 For patients who do not fit these criteria, rapid screening tests should be considered, such as the Royal Free Hospital Nutritional Prioritizing Tool that assesses fluid overload, dietary intake, and weight loss.96 The Liver Frailty Index includes a combined assessment of handgrip strength, time to do chair stands, and balance (calculator available at https://liverfrailtyindex.ucsf.edu).97 Other methods of frailty and sarcopenia measurement are summarized in Supplemental Table S5, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C284.

Dietary assessment and counseling by an accredited practicing dietitian regarding a high-energy, high-protein diet should be provided for all patients with decompensated cirrhosis and portal hypertension with sarcopenia. Oral nutrition supplements should be commenced promptly for patients unable to achieve adequate intake through diet alone.

Frequent eating throughout the day should be encouraged, with particular emphasis on including a late-evening snack containing carbohydrate and protein, as this shortens the long duration between dinner and breakfast.9899 Patients should avoid periods of fasting as much as possible. The recommended protein intake for patients with cirrhosis using ideal body weight is 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day;95100101 of which 0.2–0.25 g/kg/day may encompass branched-chain amino acids in patients with HE.90 A GESA patient resource for a high-protein and high-energy diet is available (https://www.gesa.org.au/resources/patient-resources/).102

For malnourished patients with cirrhosis who are unable to meet their nutritional requirements through an oral diet alone, consider a period of enteral feeding of at least 4 weeks to improve nutritional status and muscle strength.103

The benefits of low-intensity to moderate-intensity aerobic and resistance exercise training (usually in combination with dietary intervention) in patients with cirrhosis and portal hypertension include improvements in functional capacity, body composition, cerebral hemodynamics, cognitive function, and health-related quality of life.104105106 Where possible, exercise prescription and implementation should be delivered by a specialist exercise physiologist, as part of a multidisciplinary team, and should include a combination of aerobic and resistance training alongside behavior-change strategies to support adherence. An exercise prescription summary is provided in Supplemental Table S6, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C284.

Testosterone levels are often reduced in men with cirrhosis, primarily due to downregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis. Testosterone replacement in men who are deficient and are without contraindications (such as uncontrolled heart failure, polycythemia, or a history of a hormone-sensitive cancer) can increase muscle mass and hemoglobin level, reduce glycated hemoglobin level, and improve quality of life.107108

Thrombosis in cirrhosis

Thrombosis is common in patients with cirrhosis and portal hypertension, with the prevalence of portal vein thrombosis (PVT) estimated at 14%, which increases with liver disease severity.109 About 30%–50% of non-occlusive PVT may spontaneously recanalize. However, spontaneous recanalization is uncommon if more than 50% of the lumen is thrombosed, if the main portal vein is involved, or if there is extension into the superior mesenteric or splenic vein.110111 The recent IMPORTAL meta-analysis showed that anticoagulation of PVT was associated with a significant reduction in all-cause mortality, which was independent of PVT recanalization.112 Hence, the decision to anticoagulate patients with newly diagnosed PVT should be individualized based on patient factors, thrombus characteristics (such as degree and extent of occlusion, vessel involvement, signs of intestinal ischemia), and liver transplant candidacy, where anatomical preservation of the portal vein is preferred regardless of the extent of thrombus. In patients with <50% occlusion of a portal vein branch, observation with repeat imaging in 6–12 weeks is an appropriate strategy in non-transplant candidates. In those with 50% or greater occlusion, extension into the main portal or other large splanchnic veins, or signs of intestinal ischemia are indications for anticoagulation.113114

Patients with Child–Pugh A or B cirrhosis and thromboses can be safely treated with direct-acting anticoagulants (DOACs). DOACs are at least as safe and efficacious as traditional anticoagulants.115116117118 Commonly available DOACs are summarized in Table 2. Patients with Child–Pugh C cirrhosis who require anticoagulation should be managed with specialist hematology input.

Cardiopulmonary complications

The predominant cardiopulmonary complications associated with portal hypertension are cirrhotic cardiomyopathy (CCM), portopulmonary hypertension (PoPH), and hepatopulmonary syndrome (HPS).

CCM is chronic cardiac dysfunction seen in patients with cirrhosis of any etiology in the absence of a primary cardiac cause. CCM remains subclinical in most patients but can become overt during stress, such as liver transplantation or TIPS implantation and sepsis.119 Routine echocardiographic evaluation for CCM is recommended for patients being evaluated for liver transplantation or TIPS.

PoPH is defined as pulmonary hypertension occurring exclusively in the setting of portal hypertension. PoPH is uncommon, affecting only 2%–6% of patients with portal hypertension. Outcomes for patients with PoPH are worse than for those with other causes of pulmonary arterial hypertension and are driven by the severity of the underlying liver disease.120121 The diagnosis of PoPH is confirmed on right heart catheterization (Supplemental Table S7, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C284). Pulmonary vasodilator treatment is the mainstay of therapy, particularly as PoPH generally does not improve with liver transplantation.122 Patients with PoPH should be managed in conjunction with a pulmonary hypertension specialist. The presence of moderate or severe PoPH is not regarded as an indication for liver transplantation and is usually a contraindication, unless vasodilator treatment satisfactorily reduces pulmonary pressures.

HPS is caused by impaired pulmonary gas exchange due to pulmonary vascular dilatation in the setting of portal hypertension. HPS clinically manifests as platypnea (dyspnea on sitting or standing that is ameliorated when lying flat), with clubbing and often telangiectasias. Demonstrable orthodeoxia (reduction in oxygen saturation on standing) and the presence of pulmonary shunting on bubble contrast echocardiography or Technetium-99m macroaggregated albumin scan can confirm the diagnosis.122123 Liver transplantation is the only effective treatment for HPS.124

Surgery and portal hypertension

Perioperative risk stratification for patients with cirrhosis and portal hypertension is complex.125 Postoperative outcomes are worse than those in the general population due to greater risks of bleeding, circulatory disturbance, infection, malnutrition, renal dysfunction, drug clearance, and encephalopathy.125126 Considerations that influence surgical outcomes include the site and type of surgery, anesthesia, severity of liver disease, frailty, malnutrition, and presence of other extrahepatic comorbidities. Tools that may assist in the assessment include the Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD), Child–Pugh score, VOCAL-Penn score (calculator available at https://www.vocalpennscore.com), and HVPG measurement.127128129 Ultimately, scores should be used in conjunction with individualized multidisciplinary assessment (including surgery and anesthesia), considering alternatives and perioperative planning. When the surgical risk is not considered to be low, or the risk assessment itself is challenging, early discussion with a center with expertise in hepatology and surgery, and an intensive care unit and interventional radiology support is advised. A GESA guidance document on surgery in cirrhosis is available (https://www.gesa.org.au/resources/clinical-practice-resources/).130

There is no robust evidence that performing routine upper endoscopy in patients with cirrhosis and varices before transesophageal echocardiography significantly affects patient outcomes, or that transesophageal echocardiography poses a significant risk of bleeding, particularly variceal hemorrhage.131132133 The risk of gastrointestinal bleeding after transesophageal echocardiography is low and comparable to that in patients without varices.131132133

Pregnancy and portal hypertension

Pregnancy in women with cirrhosis mostly occurs in those with known cirrhosis, which allows time for prenatal planning. The physiological, hemodynamic, and neurohumoral changes of pregnancy can result in increased portal pressure (Table 3).134 In women with cirrhosis, these changes can aggravate portal hypertension and lead to decompensation.135

Cirrhosis in pregnant women is associated with adverse maternal and fetal outcomes. The incidence of variceal bleeding ranges from 0% to 16%, and decompensation is seen in up to 21% of women.135 Registry studies note that women with cirrhosis have an increased risk of miscarriage, cesarean section (36%), and pre-eclampsia (16%).136137 Adverse fetal outcomes, including preterm delivery and low birthweight, also appear to be more common among women with cirrhosis.

Management of planned conception in women with cirrhosis should involve a multidisciplinary team and must include a comprehensive assessment of liver disease severity, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy to assess for varices, and pre-pregnancy counseling regarding maternal and fetal risks.138 MELD score appears to predict maternal outcomes during pregnancy: a preconception MELD score of 10 or higher was associated with an increased risk of hepatic decompensation (ascites, encephalopathy, variceal bleeding) during pregnancy, whereas a MELD score of 6 or lower predicted freedom from adverse outcomes.139

Ideally, endoscopic surveillance for gastroesophageal varices should be performed in the 12 months before conception. If no varices are observed, endoscopy need not be repeated during the pregnancy. If small varices are observed, treatment with an NSBB (preferably carvedilol) should be considered, as varices can enlarge in pregnancy. Larger varices can be treated with banding or an NSBB as per recommendations (see Figure 1).

For women with newly diagnosed cirrhosis or for whom upper endoscopy has not been performed within the 12 months before conception, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy is recommended to be performed during the second trimester of pregnancy.

The most common decompensating event of cirrhosis during pregnancy is variceal bleeding.135 In suspected variceal bleeding, endoscopy can be safely performed at any stage of pregnancy, although a modified patient position to provide pelvic tilt may be required to avoid hypotension from pressure on the inferior vena cava.140141 Standard sedative agents (eg, midazolam, propofol) are safe to use during pregnancy.142 There are insufficient data to make a recommendation on the choice between EVBL or NSBBs for treating medium to large varices in pregnant women, and factors such as hypotension and the need for repeated endoscopy and sedation should be considered. Banding of varices in pregnancy is safe, and TIPS implantation has been successfully performed in refractory cases.143144 Prescribing guidelines should be consulted before use in pregnancy.
