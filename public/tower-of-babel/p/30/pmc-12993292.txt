Hyposplenism in systemic lupus erythematosus: biological and radiological characteristics and its association with increased risk of infection
Authors: Ludovic Trefond, Alexis Mathian, Samia Boussouar, Karim Maloum, Catherine Settegrana, Raphael Lhote, Micheline Pha, Matthias Papo, François Chasset, Fleur Cohen Aubart, Julien Haroche, Bruno Pereira, Miguel Hie, Zahir Amoura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of hyposplenism in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and its association with an increased risk of infection.MethodsThis single-centre study included patients with SLE who underwent a systematic evaluation for Howell-Jolly bodies (HJB) on peripheral blood smear, a marker of hyposplenism. Patients exhibiting HJB were classified as having hyposplenism (cases), whereas those without such inclusions were considered to have normal splenic function (controls). We collected radiological data to assess spleen morphology. The prevalence of infections and thrombosis was recorded.ResultsIn a tertiary care cohort of 245 patients, 23 (9.4%) demonstrated HJB on peripheral smear, after exclusion of those with a history of splenectomy or intrinsic haemolytic anaemia. In multivariable analysis, three factors were independently associated with hyposplenism: paediatric-onset SLE (OR 5.2, 95% CI 1.3 to 21.2, p=0.02), antiphospholipid syndrome (OR 6.8, 95% CI 2.2 to 20.8, p=0.001) and history of constitutional symptoms of SLE (OR 3.9, 95% CI 1.2 to 12.4, p=0.02). Patients with HJB had a higher risk of infection-related hospitalisation (OR 7.3, 95% CI 2.4 to 21.8, p<0.001), particularly pneumococcal infections (5/23 cases (21.7%) vs 1/222 controls (0.5%), p<0.001), but no increased risk of thrombosis (OR 1.6, 95% CI 0.3 to 7.8, p=0.57). Hyposplenism was associated with splenic hypoplasia identified on abdominal CT scan (6/23 assessed cases (26%) vs 0/166 assessed controls (0%), p<0.001), but not with splenic calcifications. Hyposplenism was also associated with eosinophilia, neutrophilia, monocytosis and thrombocytosis.ConclusionHyposplenism was observed in 9.4% of patients with SLE in a tertiary care cohort and was significantly associated with an increased risk of infection and radiological splenic hypoplasia. These findings highlight the importance of preventive measures for impaired splenic function, including patient and physician education, vaccination and infection surveillance in this population.

Introduction

The spleen plays a crucial role in immune defence. Loss of splenic function is most commonly due to surgical removal of the spleen but can also result from hyposplenism, also known as spleen hypofunction, that can be caused by several conditions, including haematological, infectious, autoimmune and gastrointestinal disorders.1 Hyposplenism can be detected in routine clinical practice by the presence of Howell-Jolly bodies (HJB; red blood cells containing nuclear remnants) on a blood smear2 and predisposes individuals to infections, particularly those caused by encapsulated bacteria such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis and Haemophilus influenzae type b.

Over the past few decades, improvements in the prognosis of patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) have been largely attributed to a reduction in infection-related mortality.3 4 In the 1970s, the standardised mortality rate due to infections in SLE was 31, decreasing to 6 in the 1990s and around 3 in the 2010s.35 Advances in antibiotic therapy and better management of immunosuppressive treatments have played a crucial role in this progress. However, despite these improvements, infections remain a major cause of morbidity and mortality in SLE. A meta-analysis estimated that patients with SLE have a 2.9-fold higher risk of severe infections compared with the general population,6 with infections accounting for about 25–30% of deaths in SLE.5 7 8 Pneumococcal infections represent approximately 2.4–8.3% of infections in patients with SLE.911 In the Netherlands, the incidence of pneumococcal infections in patients with SLE was found to be 13 times higher than in the general population (201/100 000 patients vs 15.6/100 000 patients).9 The underlying reasons for this increased susceptibility to pneumococcal infections remain poorly understood. SLE has been suggested as a potential cause of hyposplenism; however, data on its prevalence among patients with SLE remain limited. To date, three cohorts employing different methods to assess hyposplenism have been reported, with hyposplenism detected in 3 out of 70, 2 out of 44 and 3 out of 69 patients with SLE.1214 The mechanism linking SLE to hyposplenism is not well understood, though one hypothesis suggests that circulating immune complexes may interfere with reticuloendothelial function of the spleen.15 Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) and high-dose corticosteroid therapy—both commonly encountered in SLE—have also been independently reported as causes of hyposplenism.1 Additionally, splenic calcifications have been observed in SLE,16 though their impact on splenic function remains unclear.

This study was undertaken to (1) determine the prevalence of hyposplenism, (2) identify risk factors contributing to its development, (3) describe the biological and morphological characteristics and (4) describe the infection and thrombotic risks related to hyposplenism in patients with SLE.

Methods

Study design and population

Given that SLE has been reported as a cause of hyposplenism, HJB screening on peripheral blood smear after May-Grunwald Giemsa staining was performed randomly on blood samples collected during routine clinical care between June 2016 and June 2020 throughout this period on outpatients with SLE in a single tertiary care centre. Inclusion criteria in the study were: (1) SLE defined according to the 1997 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria or the 2019 European League Against Rheumatism/ACR classification criteria for SLE17 18 and (2) blood smear examination for HJB. Exclusion criteria were: a history of splenectomy or intrinsic haemolytic anaemias.

Screening and diagnostic assessment of hyposplenism

HJB counts were used to screen for and diagnose hyposplenism.1 Patients exhibiting HJB on peripheral blood smears were classified as having hyposplenism (cases), whereas those without such inclusions were considered to have normal splenic function (controls).

Outcome measurements

The following information was obtained from patients’ medical records throughout follow-up: age, sex, dates of SLE diagnosis, SLE manifestations, serological characteristics, drug regimen, associated APS (defined according to Miyakis et al19) and Sjögren’s disease (defined according to Shiboski et al20), infection-related hospitalisation and thrombosis. Patients were classified as antiphospholipid (aPL) positive if they had the persistent presence, during follow-up, of IgG or IgM anticardiolipin antibodies, anti-β2 glycoprotein I antibodies or lupus anticoagulant, detected on blood samples collected during routine care. Patients were classified as ‘elevated interferon-alpha (IFNα)’ if their serum IFNα levels had been assessed ≥2 IU/mL at least once before HJB testing.21 Monocytosis (>1000/μL), eosinophilia (>500/μL), neutrophilia (>7700/μL) and thrombocytosis (>450 000/μL) were considered present if they persisted for more than 6 months. Adequate pneumococcal vaccination was defined as receiving at least one dose of PPSV23 administered no sooner than 1 year after a PCV13 dose and at least 5 years after any previous PPSV23 dose.

Abdominal CT scans obtained for clinical indications unrelated to the presence of HJB during the observation period, with or without contrast administration, were reviewed by a single blinded expert radiologist trained in abdominal imaging and unaware of the clinical context. Splenic and hepatic attenuation, organ size, morphological features and the presence of calcifications or infarction were systematically assessed. Splenic hypoplasia was defined as a splenic length <7 cm on the craniocaudal axis.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are expressed according to statistical distribution as mean and SD. Between-group comparisons used Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, whereas those for continuous variables used Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U-test when the t-test assumptions were not met. To identify characteristics independently associated with the positivity of HJB, variables selected according to univariate results and to clinical relevance were entered into a multivariable logistic-regression model. Special attention was given to multicollinearity, assessed using the Farrar and Glauber test and estimating the variance inflation factor. Results were expressed as ORs and 95% CIs. We conducted additional sensitivity analyses using different model specifications, including the model reported in the manuscript as well as models incorporating all covariate combinations. In addition, to further ensure the robustness of our findings, we applied a bootstrap procedure to our multivariable analysis, allowing us to verify that the conclusions are adequately supported by the data. To assess the risk of infection associated with the positivity of HJB, after bivariable analyses, adjusted OR on age, immunosuppressant drug and pneumococcal vaccine were calculated by logistic regression. To assess the risk of thrombosis associated with the positivity of HJB, adjusted OR on age and APS was also calculated by logistic regression. Statistical analyses were computed with Stata software (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). The tests were two-sided, with type I error set at 5%.

Results

Prevalence of hyposplenism

Between June 2016 and June 2020, HJB screening was performed on 262 patients with SLE attending outpatient clinics in our tertiary care centre. The test was positive in 40 patients. After excluding patients with alternative causes of HJB positivity—specifically, intrinsic haemolytic anaemias (n=3) and prior splenectomy (n=14)—the prevalence of HJB positivity, indicative of hyposplenism, was 23 out of 245 patients (9.4%).

Splenic calcifications were identified in 9 of 245 patients (3.7%). These calcifications were diffusely distributed throughout both the deep and cortical regions of the spleen, each measuring less than 5 mm in diameter (figure 1). The number of calcifications per patient varied, ranging from a single lesion to more than 20 and was not significantly different between the groups. Splenic hypoplasia was identified in 6 patients (26.1%) within the hyposplenic group, whereas none of the 166 patients with normal splenic function who underwent abdominal CT imaging exhibited this feature (p<0.001) (figure 2). Notably, one patient exhibited progressive splenic involution, with spleen size decreasing from 110 mm in 2005 to 75 mm in 2014, and further to 45 mm in 2020 (figure 3).

Patients with hyposplenism exhibited a significantly higher prevalence of neutrophilia and monocytosis compared with those with normal splenic function. Additionally, thrombocytosis and eosinophilia were more frequently observed in the hyposplenic group, although these differences did not reach statistical significance.

In terms of infection severity, 8 patients (34.8%) in the hyposplenic group required hospitalisation, with two necessitating intensive care unit (ICU) admission. One patient in the hyposplenic group succumbed to pneumococcal pneumonia. Among patients with normal splenic function, 13 (5.9%) were hospitalised for infection, including 1 ICU admission. Among the 23 patients with hyposplenism, 5 (21.7%) experienced pneumococcal infections, compared with only 1 (0.5%) among the 222 patients with normal splenic function. Other infections were also reported. Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections were identified in one case and one control, while bacterial infections without an identified pathogen were observed in two cases and nine controls. Additionally, one control patient had a Candida infection, another had a Salmonella infection and another one an oral Streptococcus infection.

In bivariable analysis, thrombotic events were more frequent among patients with hyposplenism compared with those with normal splenic function. However, this association was no longer observed after adjusting for age and the presence of APS.

Discussion

Asplenia and hyposplenism impose a substantial clinical burden due to their association with an increased lifelong risk of severe, systemic infections—commonly referred to as overwhelming post-splenectomy infection—particularly those caused by encapsulated bacteria. SLE has historically been associated with hyposplenism,1 with a prevalence of approximately 4% reported in earlier studies.1214 However, these estimates are primarily based on small cohorts and dated methodologies, necessitating further research to ascertain the current prevalence. Moreover, the infectious and thrombotic risks associated with hyposplenism in patients with SLE have yet to be thoroughly reassessed. Our findings indicate, in a tertiary care centre, a 9% prevalence of hyposplenism among patients with SLE. Hyposplenism was associated with an elevated risk of infection requiring hospitalisation. Paediatric-onset SLE, APS and a history of constitutional symptoms of SLE were identified as risk factors associated with hyposplenism. Additionally, hyposplenism was associated with an increased risk of thrombosis, possibly confounded by its statistical association with APS.

In a review of 18 published cases of SLE-associated hyposplenism, sepsis occurred in 8 patients (44%), with S. pneumoniae identified as the causative agent in 7 cases and Salmonella in 1 case. Infection was fatal in six of these patients.22

Hyposplenism is more commonly known in other inflammatory diseases such as coeliac disease, where its prevalence ranges from 33% to 76%2 23 24 and inflammatory bowel diseases (IBDs) with rates of 35–45% in ulcerative colitis and 9–37% in Crohn’s disease. Notably, these diseases are also associated with an increased risk of infection. A Danish study of 74 156 patients with IBD reported a significantly increased risk of severe infections in Crohn’s disease (HR=1.99, 95% CI 1.59 to 2.49).25 Similarly, in coeliac disease, the risk of sepsis was significantly elevated (HR=2.6, 95% CI 2.1 to 3.0), with a particularly high risk of S. pneumoniae infection (HR=3.9, 95% CI 2.2 to 7.0) compared with the general population.26 A comparable increase in infection risk (2.0–3.5 times higher) has also been reported in splenectomised patients.27 The prevalence of hyposplenism in SLE appears lower than in gastrointestinal disorders; however, it remains significantly elevated and is associated with clinically relevant consequences. These findings support the systematic screening for hyposplenism in patients with SLE.

In this study, we assessed splenic function by the detection of HJBs on peripheral blood smears. While this method is practical and widely accessible in routine laboratory settings, it lacks the sensitivity to detect low-level splenic hypofunction, particularly in cases of mild hyposplenism. The gold standard for evaluating splenic function is the pitted red cell (PRC) count, which offers greater sensitivity but requires phase-interference microscopy—a technique not widely available in routine clinical practice. Notably, although the results from the two methods are highly correlated, HJB counts may fail to identify patients with slight increases in PRC counts (4–8%), underscoring the limitations of relying solely on HJB detection to detect low-level splenic hypofunction.1 2 To enhance our understanding of hyposplenism in SLE, further studies employing PRC counts are warranted. While hyposplenism in SLE is likely persistent and possibly irreversible, reversibility or progression over time cannot be excluded, as illustrated by the case shown in figure 3. As our study was limited to a single timepoint assessment per patient, we cannot draw conclusions regarding the temporal dynamics of splenic hypofunction.

Three principal mechanisms underlying hyposplenism have been identified in gastrointestinal disorders, including IBD and coeliac disease: reticuloendothelial atrophy, splenic atrophy and reticuloendothelial blockade.15 In SLE, impairment of the spleen’s reticuloendothelial function may result from circulating immune complexes, a mechanism that has also been observed in rheumatoid arthritis.28 Chronic complement activation, corticosteroid-induced reticuloendothelial atrophy or endothelial microangiopathy may also represent potential mechanisms contributing to hyposplenism. Another plausible explanation of hyposplenism in SLE is progressive splenic atrophy of ischaemic origin, as suggested by the statistical association we observed between hyposplenism and APS.

Splenic size on imaging was significantly reduced in SLE patients with HJBs compared with the control group. Splenic calcifications have been reported in several cases of SLE16 29 with the hypothesis suggesting that these calcifications could be linked to lupus vasculitis. A distinct histopathological feature known as ‘onion-skin lesions’ has been described in this context.30 Our study identified an association between APS and the risk of hyposplenism, although the presence of HJB was not associated with a higher prevalence of calcifications. Nevertheless, microcirculatory involvement has been implicated in other diseases as well. For example, in sickle cell disease, repeated thrombotic events within the splenic microvasculature lead to progressive autosplenectomy.31 While the hypothesis of an ischaemic origin as the driving factor behind hyposplenism in SLE is plausible, hyposplenism itself predisposes individuals to an increased risk of thrombosis.32 33 Therefore, rather than being a consequence of APS, hyposplenism might precede and contribute to the development of thrombotic events in patients with aPL antibodies. In the absence of longitudinal studies detailing the temporal relationship between the onset of hyposplenism and thrombotic events, we cannot definitively characterise the nature of this association.

Patients with lupus and hyposplenism, particularly those with HJB, are at an increased risk of severe infection, which highlights the importance of adhering to preventive recommendations. Although antibiotic prophylaxis is not recommended for hyposplenism, its use might be considered in selected patients with lupus, at high risk of infection, particularly those undergoing certain immunosuppressive therapies. International guidelines emphasise the importance of vaccination against S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, H. influenzae type b, influenza and SARS-CoV-2.1 Irrespective of potential hyposplenism, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention guidelines recommend pneumococcal vaccination for individuals under 50 years of age with immunocompromising conditions, including iatrogenic immunosuppression. The 2019 European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology recommendations define the target population for pneumococcal vaccination more broadly, referring to the ‘majority’ of patients with autoimmune inflammatory rheumatic diseases, without further specification.34 However, our observational data show that pneumococcal vaccination coverage was suboptimal and that hyposplenic patients were no more likely to be vaccinated than other patients in the cohort. These findings highlight the need for clinicians to promote vaccination among lupus patients living with SLE.

Our study has some limitations, primarily related to its retrospective design and the small number of certain events, which may have affected statistical power. As a tertiary care centre, particularly for lupus, our department is likely to manage patients with more severe and longstanding disease. Therefore, the sample of patients with SLE tested in our study, and the observed 9.4% prevalence of hyposplenism, likely do not reflect the prevalence of this complication in the broader lupus population, which is therefore probably lower. However, we may have underestimated the true prevalence of hyposplenism, as HJB detection has low sensitivity to detect spleen hypofunction, particularly in cases of mild hyposplenism.2 Another limitation is that our analysis only included hospitalised infections, whereas a more comprehensive assessment of all infections could have provided more precise insights. However, the strength of our study resides in the inclusion of the largest cohort reported in the literature to date.

Conclusion

Our result suggests that hyposplenism is not uncommon in SLE and is associated with an increased risk of infection, particularly pneumococcal infection. Given its potential impact on morbidity, we would recommend the screening for hyposplenism in patients with SLE, especially those with APS, paediatric onset of SLE, persistent neutrophilia, monocytosis, eosinophilia or thrombocytosis or reduced splenic size. Identifying at-risk patients could help guide preventive measures for impaired splenic function, such as vaccination strategies and closer infection monitoring, to improve outcomes in SLE.
