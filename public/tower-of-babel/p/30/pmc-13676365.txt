Reply to: “Evaluating the Clinical Readiness of General-Purpose Large Language Models in Evidence Synthesis”
Authors: Masaki Sakamoto, Shintaro Honda, Bungo Otsuki, Koichi Murata, Takayoshi Shimizu, Takashi Sono, Koichiro Shima, Takaki Yoshiyama, Ryohei Saito, Kosei Ono, Atsushi Tanaka, Shuichi Matsuda
Published: 2026

Reply

We thank the authors for their thoughtful commentary on our recent study1). Their Letter raises several important methodological considerations regarding the evaluation of large language models (LLMs) in evidence synthesis, and we welcome the opportunity to address these points.

Regarding Default Parameters and Prompt Optimization

The commentators note that the absence of prompt optimization may underestimate or inconsistently represent real-world deployment scenarios, when tailored prompting can substantially influence performance. We appreciate this observation; however, our decision to use default model parameters was intentional and methodologically grounded. The primary aim of our study was comparative: to characterize and contrast the inherent capabilities of different LLM architectures under identical, standardized conditions. Introducing task-specific prompt optimization would risk confounding model-level comparisons with prompt engineering skill, potentially masking meaningful differences between architectures. We fully acknowledge that optimized prompting strategies may yield superior performance in practice, and future studies exploring prompt engineering within evidence synthesis pipelines are an important and necessary next step.

Regarding Benchmark Contamination

The concern about potential data contamination in MedQA-based evaluations is well-recognized and merits serious consideration. As we noted in our limitations section, the possibility that some training datasets overlap with test benchmarks cannot be excluded for any of the evaluated models. We agree that this complicates the distinction between true clinical reasoning and memorized recall. However, contamination risk is not unique to MedQA; it represents a broad methodological challenge for any static benchmark used to evaluate contemporary LLMs. Our results should therefore be interpreted as performance estimates under current conditions rather than definitive measures of reasoning ability. Developing newly compiled, temporally held-out test sets represents an important methodological priority for the field.

Regarding Dataset Limitations and Class Imbalance

We agree that the use of a single meta-analysis dataset with a low inclusion rate (~6% to 7%) limits the generalizability of our screening performance findings. As we acknowledged in our limitations section, the class imbalance inherent to systematic review screening—when most records are excluded—may contribute to elevated kappa values by amplifying agreement on the majority (excluded) class. This observation is consistent with known properties of Cohen's kappa under imbalanced conditions. We agree that validation across multiple systematic reviews spanning diverse clinical domains, varying inclusion rates, and different evidence types is essential before drawing broader conclusions.

Regarding Calibration Variability

The commentators correctly identify that variability in confidence score calibration across model families poses a critical barrier to standardization. Our data support this concern: structured output with confidence scoring is a promising architectural feature, but consistency of calibration cannot be assumed across different model families. Rather than relying on model-native confidence scores, which inherently reflect architectural differences and calibration biases, future work should prioritize the development of model-agnostic evaluation frameworks that impose consistent, externally defined criteria for uncertainty quantification across LLM families. Development of such standardized benchmarks will be necessary to apply confidence-informed screening in clinical practice.

Regarding the Role of LLMs in Evidence Synthesis

We agree that LLMs should currently serve in a supportive rather than a substitutive role in evidence synthesis workflows. Near-expert agreement in abstract screening is encouraging, but agreement with human evaluators does not imply correctness, particularly given inherent inter-rater variability among expert reviewers themselves. Our study was designed to characterize current performance as a baseline, not to advocate for full automation. We believe that LLMs offer substantial potential for reducing reviewer burden and providing supplementary quality assurance in human-led workflows. Broader autonomous deployment requires future validation across diverse clinical domains, multilingual datasets, and complete systematic review pipelines.

Conclusion

The methodological considerations raised in this Letter are consistent with the limitations we identified in our original publication and reflect important boundaries in the state of the field. This exchange has clarified the current promise and limits of LLMs in clinical evidence synthesis. Future research should prioritize prompt optimization studies, development of contamination-resistant benchmarks, evaluation across diverse systematic review contexts, and thorough assessment of calibration and safety before broader clinical adoption.

The rapid pace of LLM advancement presents a particular challenge: by the time thorough, comprehensive evaluation frameworks are fully developed, the models under study may already be replaced by newer versions. In this context, targeted yet thorough evaluations—such as ours—serve a critical navigational function, providing empirical reference points that practitioners can consult in the absence of specialty-specific benchmarks. We hope this work contributes to the incremental development of such an evidence base.
