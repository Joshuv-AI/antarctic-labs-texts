Cost-Utility Analysis of Faricimab Versus Aflibercept in Treating Neovascular Age-Related Macular Degeneration (nAMD) in the United States
Authors: Ahmead Baljoon, Vakaramoko Diaby, Sandra Suther, Matthew Dutton, Askal Ali, Islam Eljilany
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/ObjectiveNeovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) imposes a substantial clinical and economic burden in the United States (US). Faricimab offers the potential for longer dosing intervals than aflibercept, but its economic value in the US setting has not been established. This study evaluated the cost utility of faricimab versus aflibercept for treating nAMD from the US payer perspective.MethodsA Markov model of five health states defined by best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) simulated disease progression over 5 years. Clinical inputs were drawn from pooled 1-year data of the TENAYA and LUCERNE phase III trials (n = 1329), which compared faricimab with aflibercept for treating nAMD patients. Direct medical costs encompassed drug acquisition, intravitreal administration, monitoring, office visits, and management of endophthalmitis. Outcomes included total costs, quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), and net monetary benefit (NMB) at a willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold of US$100,000/QALY. Scenario, deterministic, and probabilistic sensitivity analyses (10,000 iterations) were conducted.ResultsIn the base case, faricimab yielded 2.80 QALYs for US$52,797, whereas aflibercept produced 2.72 QALYs at US$62,367. Faricimab was therefore dominant (− US$9570; + 0.08 QALY), with an incremental NMB of US$17,981. Faricimab required markedly fewer injections over 5 years (22.6 vs 34). Scenario analyses did not materially change the directionality of the base-case results, with faricimab being dominant or cost effective. The model was most sensitive to drug acquisition prices and BCVA-state utilities, yet faricimab remained below the WTP threshold across all plausible ranges. Probabilistic analysis showed a 97% probability of faricimab being cost effective at US$100,000/QALY.ConclusionsFaricimab is estimated to be a cost-effective treatment option compared with aflibercept for nAMD from a US payer perspective. This finding may support its inclusion in treatment guidelines and formulary decisions, pending confirmation in real-world practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s41669-026-00643-0.

Key Points for Decision Makers

In our cost-utility analysis using pooled phase III data, faricimab was cost effective compared with aflibercept in patients with neovascular age-related macular degeneration, from a US payer perspective.The cost advantage of faricimab was primarily driven by a lower injection burden over the model horizon, with patients receiving an average of 22.6 injections compared with 34 injections in the aflibercept arm, which generated an obvious cost difference.These findings were in the same direction in scenario and sensitivity analyses.

Introduction

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a condition that affects a part of the retina called the macula. Consequently, patients may lose their central vision, which impairs their ability to see fine details, although their peripheral vision typically remains unaffected [1]. Furthermore, AMD is a significant contributor to global blindness and is the prevailing cause of blindness in industrialized nations [2, 3]. It has been estimated that approximately 19.8 million (12.6%) Americans aged 40 years and older were living with AMD in 2019, among whom 1.49 million had vision-threatening conditions [4].

The development of anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) treatments marked a revolution in neovascular AMD (nAMD) therapy, as they have significantly improved visual acuity (VA) and quality of life and have recently become the gold standard for treating nAMD [5]. Notably, these treatments include aflibercept (Eylea®), which inhibits VEGF-A, VEGF-B, and placental growth factors (PlGFs), and received approval for treating nAMD in 2011 [6].

Recently, interfering with the angiopoietin/tyrosine kinase pathway has revealed promising results for retinal vascular diseases [7], particularly by blocking the activity of angiopoietin-2 (Ang-2) to address the pathological deterioration in the retinal vasculature [8]. In this context, faricimab (Vabysmo®) is the latest anti-VEGF treatment that has been introduced as a monoclonal antibody exerting a bispecific action by neutralizing both VEGF and Ang-2. Faricimab was approved for nAMD by the United States Food and Drug Administration (US FDA) in January 2022 [9, 10]. It marks a pivotal development in the treatment of nAMD, particularly due to its novel dual-pathway approach. In light of the increasing demands on healthcare systems to allocate resources efficiently, the approval of faricimab offers healthcare policymakers and payers an opportunity to enhance both patient outcomes and economic sustainability [9, 10].

Notably, based on meta-analysis, faricimab offers a significant advantage in reducing central subfield thickness (CST), exhibiting a lower mean change than aflibercept (standardized mean difference [SMD] − 0.24; 95% CI − 0.39 to − 0.10; p = 0.001) [11]. This superior control over CST indicates that faricimab may more effectively reduce retinal edema, an outcome essential for maintaining long-term VA and structural stability in retinal diseases. Moreover, faricimab incurs a slightly higher per-dose cost compared with aflibercept.

TENAYA and LUCERNE are two identical phase III clinical trials [12] that compare the efficacy of faricimab and aflibercept in treating nAMD. These trials are registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (TENAYA, NCT03823287 and LUCERNE, NCT03823300). Faricimab, administered at flexible dosing intervals, adapts to individual response, which contrasts with the more frequent administration of aflibercept. Notably, the results highlighted faricimab’s potential for extended dosing intervals, reducing treatment frequency while maintaining effectiveness [12]. However, no study has specifically evaluated faricimab’s economic viability within the US healthcare system. Moreover, ophthalmologists are increasingly sensitive to pharmacoeconomic data due to the expensive nature of new drugs for nAMD [13].

This study evaluates the cost utility of faricimab compared with aflibercept in treating nAMD in the US using a Markov model to simulate disease progression. The study aims to determine which treatment provides better value from the payer's perspective, ultimately guiding healthcare resource allocation while optimizing patient outcomes within budget constraints.

Methods

Model Structure

A decision-analytic Markov model was developed to evaluate the long-term cost utility of faricimab compared with aflibercept for the treatment of nAMD. This modeling approach was selected to reflect the chronic and progressive nature of nAMD and to capture longitudinal changes in visual function, healthcare costs, and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) over time. The analysis was implemented using TreeAge Pro Healthcare 2024 (TreeAge Software, Williamstown, MA, USA).

The model was conducted from a healthcare payer perspective in the US and developed over a 5-year time horizon in the base-case analysis. This horizon was considered appropriate given the advanced age of the modeled population, with a pooled mean age of 75.9 years in the TENAYA and LUCERNE clinical trials [12], and the reported life expectancy of 77.5 years for the general US population [14]. Costs and health outcomes accrued beyond the first year were discounted at an annual rate of 3%, consistent with established economic evaluation guidelines [15]. All costs were inflated to 2024 US dollars (USD) using the Consumer Price Index (CPI) Calculator available at the US Bureau of Labor Statistics (https://www.bls.gov/data/inflation_calculator.htm) [16].

The model simulated a hypothetical cohort of patients diagnosed with nAMD, consistent with the inclusion criteria of the TENAYA and LUCERNE phase III clinical trials [12]. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were derived from pooled trial data and are summarized in Supplementary Table S1 in Electronic Supplementary Material 1 (ESM1). At model entry, patients were distributed across health states according to their baseline best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) in the worse-seeing eye (Supplementary Table S2, ESM1).

Disease progression was represented using five mutually exclusive health states defined by BCVA levels measured with the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) chart. The ETDRS chart is widely used in clinical practice due to its superior sensitivity and reliability in detecting clinically meaningful changes in VA, particularly in retinal diseases [17, 18]. Health states were defined in 15-letter increments as follows: VA ≥ 84 letters, 69–83 letters, 54–68 letters, 39–53 letters, and VA ≤ 38 letters, with an additional absorbing death state. Visual acuity states were based on the worse-seeing eye, consistent with prior economic evaluations and with established evidence linking worse-eye vision to HRQoL [19]. The overall model structure and permitted transitions are illustrated in Figure 1. For more details about cohort regrouping, dosing, and the number of injections, refer to the supplementary methods in ESM1.

A cycle length of 3-months was selected to align with the dosing schedules of faricimab and aflibercept and to adequately capture treatment-related changes in visual outcomes over time [20]. This cycle length is also consistent with methodologies used in previous cost-effectiveness analyses of anti-VEGF therapies for nAMD [21, 22]. During each cycle, patients could remain in their current BCVA state, transition to an adjacent better or worse BCVA state, or transition to death, as shown in Fig. 1. Death could occur from any health state.

Transition probabilities between BCVA health states were derived from pooled raw efficacy data from the TENAYA and LUCERNE trials [12]. The clinical plausibility of disease progression, the appropriateness of transition pathways, and the overall model structure were validated through consultation with an expert panel consisting of two ophthalmologists. Additional validation was informed by published clinical guidelines and recommendations for the diagnosis and management of nAMD [23]. Reporting of the economic evaluation adhered to the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) (available at https://www.equator-network.org/reporting-guidelines/cheers/) [24], with a completed checklist provided in Supplementary Table S3 (ESM1).

Within each cycle, patients accrued health-state–specific costs and utilities corresponding to their BCVA level. Health outcomes were expressed in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), calculated by applying utility weights specific to each BCVA health state. Total costs and total QALYs were accumulated over the full model horizon for each treatment arm. Model outputs included total and incremental costs, total and incremental QALYs, and the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), expressed as cost per QALY gained for faricimab compared with aflibercept. In addition, results were summarized using the net monetary benefit (NMB) framework, which integrates costs and health outcomes into a single metric using a willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold [25]. A WTP threshold of US$100,000 per QALY gained was applied, consistent with value-based benchmarks used by the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review [26].

Mortality assumptions were based on the mean age of patients in the pooled trial population (75.9 years) [12]. Age- and sex-specific mortality rates were obtained from the US Life Tables and weighted by gender [28]. To account for disease-related excess mortality, a relative risk of death associated with AMD of 1.09 was applied, resulting in adjusted mortality rates that more accurately reflect AMD-specific survival compared with unadjusted population estimates [29]. The model assumed identical mortality probabilities across BCVA states and treatments, as both faricimab and aflibercept share similar mechanisms of action and are not expected to differentially affect survival [30].

Drug acquisition costs were based on Medicare reimbursement rates for each anti-VEGF agent, using average sales price (ASP) data from Medicare Part B [31]. Administration and monitoring costs were derived from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) physician fee schedules (PFS) [32], and included the cost of intravitreal injections (Current Procedural Terminology [CPT] code 67028) [34]. The model incorporated one initial office visit (CPT code 99204) and subsequent follow-up visits (CPT code 92014). The initial office visit was assumed to occur once, while follow-up visits were incurred regularly over the 1-year treatment period. Office visit costs were obtained from Medicare reimbursement data reported in prior studies [35, 36].

Monitoring costs included optical coherence tomography (OCT) and fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA). Optical coherence tomography was assumed to be performed at every office visit, whereas FFA was assumed to occur only once at the initial visit. These assumptions were informed by the preferred practice pattern guidelines for AMD [23] and the TENAYA and LUCERNE trial protocols [37].

Endophthalmitis was the only adverse event explicitly included in the model, based on expert panel consensus regarding its clinical and economic relevance. Costs associated with managing endophthalmitis were obtained from Schmier et al. (2016) [33]. Event rates differed slightly between treatments, with higher rates observed for faricimab, as reported in Supplementary Table S4 (ESM1).

Utility Values

Utility inputs for each BCVA-linked Markov health state were derived from Brown et al. [38] and validated against age-specific population norms, consistent with prior ophthalmology cost-effectiveness frameworks [39] (Table 3). Although VA intervals differed across studies, the same utility values were applied. Within each VA interval, multiple VA score ranges were aggregated. For example, the 54–68 letter state included VA ranges of 68–73, 62–67, 55–61, and 43–54, as described by Hutton et al. [34]. The mean utility for this state was calculated as 0.75 based on the average of the corresponding utility values. To avoid exceeding age-appropriate population norms, vision-specific utilities were re-anchored using a multiplicative age adjustment. Given that the mean EQ-5D-5L utility for US adults aged ≥75 years is 0.811 [40]; the original vision utilities were multiplied by this factor while preserving relative decrements across BCVA states. Disutility associated with endophthalmitis was derived from Clark et al. [39], who reported utility values of 0.90 for patients experiencing endophthalmitis and 0.96 for control patients undergoing uncomplicated surgery. The resulting disutility of 0.06 was applied to quantify the quality of life decrement associated with endophthalmitis, consistent with time trade-off (TTO)-based utility estimation methods [39].

Cost-Effectiveness Analysis

Base-Case Analysis

The economic evaluation compared faricimab with aflibercept by estimating total and incremental costs and health outcomes over the 5-year model horizon. Results of the base-case analysis were summarized using both the ICER and the NMB framework.

The ICER was calculated as the ratio of the difference in total costs between faricimab and aflibercept to the difference in total QALYs gained and was expressed as cost per QALY gained. In parallel, the NMB was calculated to provide a complementary and decision-relevant metric that integrates costs and outcomes into a single monetary value. NMB was defined as:

Incremental NMB was computed as the difference in NMB between faricimab and aflibercept, with positive values indicating cost effectiveness at the specified WTP threshold.

Scenario Analyses

Scenario analyses were performed to examine the impact of alternative assumptions and model structures while maintaining consistency with the base-case framework. These analyses were designed to evaluate the robustness of the findings under clinically and methodologically plausible conditions and to aid interpretation for healthcare decision makers. In this context, separate scenarios were constructed in which each faricimab dosing regimen (Q8W, Q12W, and Q16W) was evaluated individually against the standard aflibercept regimen.

To further explore uncertainty in clinical inputs, additional scenario analyses were conducted by re-estimating the model using transition probabilities derived exclusively from the TENAYA phase III trial and exclusively from the LUCERNE phase III trial [12]. To reflect non-significant differences in the clinical trials, a neutral scenario was examined in which identical transition matrices were applied to both treatment arms, thereby removing differential efficacy assumptions (Supplementary Table S5; ESM1). Results across all scenarios were systematically reviewed to assess the influence of these alternative assumptions on model outcomes and to confirm the overall stability of the conclusions.

Deterministic Sensitivity Analysis

Deterministic sensitivity analyses (DSA) were conducted to assess the robustness of the base-case results to uncertainty in individual model parameters and to identify key drivers of cost-effectiveness outcomes. One-way sensitivity analyses were performed by varying model inputs across predefined ranges while holding all other parameters constant. Parameter ranges were based on the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) surrounding point estimates wherever such data were available from clinical trials, published literature, or reimbursement sources. In the absence of reported uncertainty, a ±25% range around the base-case value was applied, consistent with common practice in economic evaluations to reflect plausible parameter variability. For health-state utility values, a narrower ±10% range was used to avoid implausible deviations in preference-based measures.

Results of the DSA were presented using tornado diagrams. Depending on whether the base-case results were more naturally interpreted through the ICER or the NMB framework, either ICER-based or NMB-based tornado diagrams were reported to ensure a clear and policy-relevant interpretation of parameter influence on cost-effectiveness outcomes.

Probabilistic Sensitivity Analysis

To characterize joint parameter uncertainty and assess the probability that faricimab would be cost effective across a range of decision thresholds, a probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) was conducted using Monte Carlo simulation with 10,000 iterations. A fixed random seed was applied to ensure reproducibility of results.

Appropriate probability distributions were assigned to model parameters based on their statistical properties and established conventions in health economic modeling. Beta distributions were used for transition probabilities and utility values, reflecting their bounded nature between 0 and 1. Gamma distributions were applied to cost parameters to accommodate right-skewed distributions typical of healthcare costs. Joint uncertainty across parameters was propagated simultaneously in each simulation draw.

While Dirichlet distributions are theoretically appropriate when modeling uncertainty in multiple competing transition probabilities that must jointly sum to one, their use is most relevant when several transitions carry non-negligible probability mass. In the present model, transition structures were characterized by one dominant transition probability (typically remaining in the same BCVA state) and one or more residual transitions with negligible probability mass. Under these conditions, the use of independent beta distributions for individual transition probabilities provides a numerically stable and widely accepted approximation without materially affecting model outcomes, as supported by prior economic modeling practice [41].

Results of the PSA were summarized using incremental cost-effectiveness (ICE) scatterplots and cost-effectiveness acceptability curves (CEAC), illustrating the probability that faricimab is cost effective compared with aflibercept across a range of WTP thresholds.

Results

Base-Case Results

The base-case analysis over a 5-year time horizon demonstrated that faricimab was the economically dominant strategy compared with aflibercept (Table 4). Faricimab was associated with lower total costs (US$52,797 vs US$62,367) and greater health benefits (2.80 vs 2.72 QALYs), resulting in an incremental cost saving of US$9570 and a gain of 0.08 QALYs. Under these conditions, calculation of an ICER was unnecessary, as faricimab dominated aflibercept.

The cost advantage of faricimab was primarily driven by a lower injection burden over the model horizon, with patients receiving an average of 22.6 injections compared with 34 injections in the aflibercept arm. This reduction translated into lower drug acquisition, administration, and monitoring costs, which more than offset the marginally higher modeled rate of endophthalmitis observed with faricimab (0.0350 vs 0.0279).

When evaluated using the NMB framework at a WTP threshold of $100,000 per QALY, faricimab generated a higher NMB than aflibercept (US$227,261 vs US$209,280), corresponding to an incremental NMB of US$17,981. This positive incremental NMB indicates that faricimab delivers greater economic value relative to aflibercept at the specified threshold and reinforces the dominance finding from the ICER-based analysis.

Figure 2 presents the distribution of patients across VA health states over 20 Markov cycles. Compared with aflibercept, faricimab was associated with more favorable long-term visual outcomes, including a higher proportion of patients remaining in the highest VA state (VA ≥ 84; approximately 15% vs 10%) and sustained occupancy in the VA 69–83 state (approximately 35% vs 26%). In addition, faricimab reduced progression to the lowest VA state (VA ≤ 38), with the proportion of patients falling below 5% over time, whereas a slight increase was observed in the aflibercept arm. Together, these findings suggest that faricimab more effectively preserves visual function over time, translating into higher accrued QALYs and improved economic outcomes.

Scenario Analyses

Scenario analyses in Table 5 showed that economic outcomes were strongly influenced by the faricimab dosing interval. Under the Q8W regimen, faricimab was more costly than aflibercept (US$76,008 vs US$62,368) despite higher effectiveness (2.801 vs 2.716 QALYs; incremental QALYs 0.084). The resulting ICER of US$162,175 per QALY exceeded commonly cited WTP thresholds, indicating that faricimab was not cost effective at this dosing interval. Consistent with this finding, the incremental cost of US$13,640 translated into a lower NMB for faricimab (US$204,051) compared with aflibercept (US$209,280), suggesting limited economic value under the Q8W regimen.

The utility of the VA 69–83 state also significantly influenced the incremental NMB (US$16,162 to US$19,803), emphasizing the role of quality of life in the NMB.

The ICE scatterplot from the probabilistic sensitivity analysis showed that most simulations clustered in the south-east quadrant of the cost-effectiveness plane, indicating that faricimab was more effective and less costly than aflibercept in the majority of iterations. A smaller proportion of simulations appeared in the north-east quadrant; however, most of these points remained below the US$100,000 per QALY WTP threshold line, suggesting that faricimab remained cost effective even when associated with higher costs. Consistent with this pattern, the CEAC demonstrated a high probability of cost effectiveness for faricimab across the full range of WTP values examined, exceeding 95% at a US$100,000/QALY threshold and increasing further at higher thresholds, while aflibercept showed a persistently low probability of being cost effective. Together, these findings indicate that the base-case results are robust to joint parameter uncertainty and support faricimab as the economically preferred strategy.

Discussion

Faricimab has recently received FDA approval for the treatment of nAMD and diabetic macular edema (DME) [9]. This approval represents an important development in ophthalmic care, as faricimab has demonstrated the ability to maintain clinical efficacy with less frequent dosing compared with established anti-VEGF therapies such as aflibercept [12]. Reduced dosing frequency is clinically relevant in chronic retinal diseases, where long-term adherence and cumulative treatment burden remain key challenges in routine practice.

In the context of growing emphasis on value-based decision making in the US, this study provides the first cost-utility analysis evaluating faricimab relative to other FDA-approved anti-VEGF agents for the treatment of nAMD from a US payer perspective. Using clinical efficacy and dosing data from the TENAYA and LUCERNE phase III trials [12], the analysis assessed the cost effectiveness of faricimab compared with aflibercept, explicitly accounting for differences in injection frequency and per-injection costs. Within a 5-year time horizon, faricimab was estimated to be more cost effective than aflibercept, with results primarily driven by reductions in treatment burden and associated medical resource use. Although aflibercept was selected as the primary comparator due to its widespread use and robust clinical evidence base, the underlying mechanisms driving cost effectiveness in this analysis, namely treatment durability, injection frequency, and cumulative resource utilization, are relevant across other anti-VEGF agents commonly used in US clinical practice. Consequently, these findings may inform broader economic comparisons within the anti-VEGF class, particularly where dosing intensity differs meaningfully between treatments.

An important methodological consideration highlighted by this analysis is that cost-utility outcomes are not determined solely by point estimates of VA, such as mean BCVA measured at fixed time points. Although individual trial or pooled analyses may show small or statistically non-significant mean differences in BCVA at fixed timepoints, cost-utility outcomes depend on time spent across VA-defined health states. Even modest, consistent shifts toward better VA states can accumulate over repeated 3-month cycles, producing cumulative QALY gains. In our base case, this mechanism accounts for the ~0.08 QALY advantage observed for faricimab over 5 years, alongside cost differences driven by treatment burden and monitoring. While this is still a small gain and uncertain, even in a neutral efficacy scenario, there remain resource savings driven by cost effectiveness (that also will have expected patient benefits via reduced frequency of administration). This perspective is particularly relevant for chronic ophthalmic conditions, where long-term disease control rather than short-term visual gains often drives patient-centered outcomes.

The robustness of these findings was supported by PSA, which demonstrated consistently high probability of cost effectiveness for faricimab across a wide range of WTP thresholds. Deterministic sensitivity analyses further indicated that drug acquisition costs for both faricimab and aflibercept were among the most influential parameters in the model. These results underscore the importance of pricing assumptions in economic evaluations of anti-VEGF therapies and highlight the potential impact of price concessions, rebates, and contracting strategies on payer decisions. Importantly, the model structure allows for straightforward breakeven analyses; given the magnitude of differences in treatment frequency observed between therapies, faricimab would be expected to remain cost saving unless aflibercept were discounted substantially below net prices typically observed in routine US practice. This finding emphasizes that treatment durability may outweigh unit price differences when cumulative costs are considered over time.

The present findings are broadly consistent with economic evaluations conducted in other healthcare systems. A UK-based cost-effectiveness study reported that faricimab administered under a treat-and-extend (T&E) regimen was more effective and cost saving compared with ranibizumab and aflibercept administered pro re nata (PRN) for the treatment of DME [42]. In that analysis, faricimab dominated both comparators, generating cost savings between £5483 and £9655 and QALY gains ranging from 0.16 to 0.36. Similarly, the current analysis suggests that extended durability and reduced injection frequency can translate into economic benefits for patients with nAMD in the US setting. Additional evidence from Canada further contextualizes these findings. A Canadian cost-effectiveness study [43] suggested that faricimab was superior to ranibizumab, aflibercept, and brolucizumab when administered under typical T&E regimens in clinical practice for nAMD. From a societal perspective, faricimab was projected to be cost saving, largely due to reductions in injection visits and associated caregiver burden. These results highlight the importance of considering informal care costs when assessing the economic impact of retinal therapies, as such costs may represent a substantial proportion of the total burden of disease. Comparable insights have been reported in the US literature. A prior cost-effectiveness analysis comparing ranibizumab and aflibercept for the treatment of DME found that although aflibercept offered marginally greater effectiveness in patients with worse baseline VA, it was not cost effective due to higher overall costs and minimal QALY gains (US$44,423 vs US$34,529; 1.476 vs 1.466 QALYs, respectively) [44]. This observation highlights the importance of evaluating whether incremental clinical benefits justify additional costs, a consideration that is directly relevant to comparisons between faricimab and aflibercept. Moreover, long-term extrapolations in the Holekamp et al. study [44] suggest that cost-effectiveness conclusions may persist or even strengthen over extended time horizons, reinforcing the value of durability-focused therapies.

Some limitations of this analysis warrant consideration. Injection frequencies were derived from pooled estimates of two global phase III trials, which may not fully reflect real-world treatment patterns. Variability in adherence, clinician preferences, and healthcare delivery may influence outcomes in routine practice. In addition, the analysis included costs associated only with endophthalmitis, despite the potential for other ocular or systemic AEs with prolonged anti-VEGF therapy [45]. Mortality inputs were based on US population data, whereas the clinical trials enrolled international populations, and the 5-year time horizon may not capture lifetime costs and benefits. The use of VA-defined health states introduces further uncertainty, as each state encompasses a range of VA scores with heterogeneous utility values. The model also did not capture potential anatomical benefits, such as reductions in retinal thickness, due to the absence of validated mappings between these outcomes and HRQoL. As this study was conducted from a payer perspective, only direct medical costs were included. To address structural uncertainty, alternative time horizons and small injection-related disutilities were considered conceptually. Given that both interventions involve intravitreal injections and that any procedure-related disutility would be applied symmetrically across arms, the incremental effect is expected to remain unchanged. Similarly, shorter or longer time horizons would proportionally scale QALY accrual and costs but would not alter comparative conclusions because treatment effects evolve consistently across time. Importantly, uncertainty around the key model drivers, including transition probabilities, costs, and utilities, was already incorporated through probabilistic sensitivity analysis in accordance with ISPOR and NICE recommendations. Therefore, the base-case and PSA results are expected to remain robust under alternative scenario assumptions.

From a US payer perspective, these findings suggest that therapies offering durable disease control with reduced treatment frequency may deliver greater long-term value than agents associated with higher cumulative treatment burden. Incorporating treatment durability and breakeven considerations alongside unit pricing may support more efficient formulary placement and contracting strategies in value-based ophthalmic care.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that faricimab appears to offer superior economic benefits compared with aflibercept, positioning it as a more efficient use of healthcare resources. Our results confirm that faricimab might be a preferred option from a payer’s perspective due to its ability to achieve similar or tangible improved clinical outcomes with fewer injections, reducing both direct medical costs and the burden on patients and healthcare providers. While VA remains the primary outcome in most cost-effective studies, future research should also explore additional outcomes, such as retinal thickness reduction, to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the therapeutic benefits of faricimab in managing nAMD.
