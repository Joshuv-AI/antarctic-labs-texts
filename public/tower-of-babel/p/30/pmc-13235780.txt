Early Outcomes of Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation in Congenital Heart Surgery
Authors: Thibault Schaeffer, Sohal Hayat, Muneaki Matsubara, Jonas Palm, Teresa Lemmen, Paul Philipp Heinisch, Nicole Piber, Andrea Amici, Alfred Hager, Peter Ewert, Jürgen Hörer, Masamichi Ono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesExtracorporeal membrane oxygenation is widely used to treat cardiopulmonary failure after congenital heart surgery. This study evaluated patient characteristics, outcomes, and risk factors associated with perioperative veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation use.MethodsAll patients undergoing congenital heart surgery between 2001 and 2024 at our centre were included. Prevalence, outcomes, risk factors for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support, and predictors of in-hospital mortality following extracorporeal membrane oxygenation were analysed.ResultsAmong 9892 congenital heart operations, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation was required in 178 (1.8%) patients for a median of 6 days (interquartile range 4-9). Median age and weight at surgery were 2.8 months (interquartile range 0.4-25.2) and 4.1 kg (interquartile range 3.2-8.9), respectively. In patients ≤2 years, the Norwood procedure and surgery for coronary artery malformations were independent risk factors for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support. Successful weaning was achieved in 62% (n = 111), while 17% (n = 30) required a second extracorporeal membrane oxygenation run. In-hospital mortality after extracorporeal membrane oxygenation was 55% (n = 98), occurring a median of 16 days (interquartile range 5-34) after surgery; 27% of the patients died after a successful first weaning. Model-based predictions showed an 8-fold increase between days 6 and 12 of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support, with predicted mortality rising from 26% to 74%.ConclusionsApproximately 2% of patients undergoing congenital heart surgery require extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support with an overall survival rate of around 50%. Prolonged extracorporeal membrane oxygenation duration and acute renal failure requiring renal replacement therapy were independently associated with mortality. The sharp increase in mortality beyond 6 days of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation highlights the need for early reassessment of reversibility and timely consideration of alternative strategies.Keywords: extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, congenital heart surgery, mortality

Introduction

Despite substantial advances over recent decades, cardiac surgery for congenital heart disease (CHD) may still result in refractory cardiac or respiratory failure despite technically successful surgery. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), as a continuation to conventional cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), has increasingly been used as a rescue therapy in this setting.1 By maintaining adequate systemic perfusion and preventing ischaemic injury to vital organs, ECMO has been shown to improve postoperative survival in both paediatric and adult populations.2,3 Contemporary data indicate that approximately 2.5% of paediatric patients and 1% of adult patients with CHD require ECMO support following cardiac surgery, with reported in-hospital mortality rates ranging from 45% to 65%, depending on the underlying condition and indication for ECMO.4,5 Complications such as bleeding, end-organ dysfunction, infection, and neurological injury are frequent during ECMO support and contribute substantially to the associated poor prognosis.6 However, there is still limited understanding of which patients are most likely to require ECMO, which factors predict survival after ECMO initiation, and how long support should be continued before it is unlikely to affect outcome.

To better characterize the role, benefits, and limitations of ECMO in congenital heart surgery, we reviewed our institutional experience with the aim of identifying risk profiles associated with the need for ECMO support and factors linked to mortality despite following ECMO therapy.

Methods

Ethical statement

The institutional review board of the Technical University of Munich (approval number 2025-232-S-NP on 5 May 2025) approved the study. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for individual patient consent was waived.

Patients and data collection

This single-centre retrospective study reviewed all patients who underwent congenital heart surgery at the German Heart Centre Munich from January 2001 to January 2024. It specifically evaluated those who needed ECMO support during their hospital stay. The analysis was limited to patients receiving veno-arterial (VA) ECMO as primary support; cases with primary veno-venous (VV) ECMO were not included. Data were collected from our internal database.

End points and measured outcomes

The primary end point of the study was to identify factors associated with the use of pre-, intra-, and postoperative ECMO support in congenital heart operations. Secondary end points were to estimate the incidence of successful weaning from ECMO support and to identify risk factors for mortality among patients supported with ECMO as well as to quantify the association between ECMO duration and the risk of death.

Mortality was defined as in-hospital death following cardiac surgery. Infections included all documented cases of bacteraemia or clinically relevant infections requiring systemic antibiotic therapy. Bleeding was defined as thoracic or ECMO cannulation-site haemorrhage requiring surgical revision. Thrombus was defined as any echogenic mass detected within the heart or great vessels by transthoracic echocardiography or angiography. Thrombi limited to central vascular lines or the ECMO oxygenation circuit, as well as extravascular thrombi, were not reported. Acute kidney injury (AKI) was defined as the requirement for renal replacement therapy (RRT) due to hypervolaemia or electrolyte disturbances.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as numbers with percentages, and continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges. To estimate the cumulative incidence of successful ECMO weaning after the initial ECMO run, we used a competing risk model, as described by Fine and Gray, with mortality as a competing event. Because the age distribution of patients supported with ECMO was highly skewed, with 75% of patients receiving ECMO before the age of 1.8 years, age groups were defined as patients aged ≤2 years (132 of 5548 assisted with ECMO) and patients aged >12 years (30 of 2213 assisted with ECMO) to allow meaningful statistical inference. Patients aged between 2 and 12 years were excluded from age-stratified analyses because the limited numbers precluded reliable estimations.

A first logistic regression model was designed for the analysis of factors associated with the use of ECMO for the 2 subgroups defined by age (aged ≤2 years and aged >12 years). A second logistic regression model was used to analyse the factors associated with death in patients with perioperative ECMO support. Due to the small numbers in the older group, the second model was limited to patients aged 2 years or younger (n = 132). We defined the end point as death at any time before hospital discharge, irrespective of whether it occurred during ECMO support or after successful weaning (a binary outcome of death versus survival), and all patients were followed until death or discharge. Given the long study period, the surgical era was included as a covariate in the regression models, dichotomized at the median date of operation in the overall cohort (n = 9892). Corrective cardiac interventions (excluding ECMO-related procedures) were included as covariates in multivariable logistic regression analyses of mortality. Procedure-specific multivariable risk factor analyses for ECMO implants were performed for surgical procedures identified as high risk for ECMO use. To identify risk factors for mortality among survivors of a first ECMO weaning, an additional multivariable logistic regression model was used. Parameters associated with significant risk in the univariable models were included in the multivariable models. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. To avoid multicollinearity, redundant predictors were not simultaneously included in multivariable models, and binary were favoured over continuous variables. In the younger age group, weight and age at the operation were dichotomized using clinically predefined cut-offs in the multivariable model to facilitate interpretability. Collinearity among candidate predictors was assessed using variance inflation factors. Missing data were handled using complete-case (listwise) deletion in the regression models and cumulative incidence function analyses.

Finally, to assess the association between ECMO duration and the risk of death, we plotted the odds of mortality as a function of ECMO duration using natural cubic splines. To reduce skewness, ECMO duration was log-transformed and entered into the model as a natural cubic spline with 3 degrees of freedom. The model was adjusted for factors significantly associated with death in the multivariable regression model. To visualize the effect of duration, we derived adjusted odds ratios (ORs) for mortality across the range of ECMO duration for a baseline patient without any risk factors using 6 days of ECMO (median duration of support) as the reference. Odds ratios with 95% CIs were obtained from model-based predictions from the spline function, and daily increases in mortality risk were quantified.

Statistical analyses were performed using R-statistical software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Patient characteristics

During the study period, 9892 patients underwent congenital heart surgery. Among them, 178 patients (1.8%) were supported with ECMO. With a median age of 2.8 (0.4-25.2) months at surgery, the majority of patients supported with ECMO were infants. Patient characteristics with and without ECMO support are shown in Table 1. In patients aged ≤2 years, the highest frequency of ECMO was observed in those having the Norwood procedure (11.1%, n = 45), followed by those undergoing an aortopulmonary shunt (3.6%, n = 14). In the older group, the double-switch procedure (100%, n = 2), aortic arch repair (5.7%, n = 2), repair of a coronary anomaly (5.0%, n = 2), repair of Ebstein’s anomaly (3.8%, n = 3), and mitral valve replacement (2.9%, n = 3) were the procedures most frequently associated with ECMO. In patients who received ECMO in the intensive care unit, the median time from the surgical procedure to ECMO implant was 1 (0-4) day. The most frequent indication for postoperative ECMO support was low cardiac output (n = 12, 29%). In 4 patients, postoperative ECMO was implanted in patients on CPR with a low-flow duration ranging from 30 to 55 min. In-hospital outcomes after the ECMO implant

Median duration of ECMO support was 6 days (4-8.8). The cumulative incidence of the first ECMO weaning was 48% after 7 days and 63% after 20 days (Figure 2). A second ECMO support was needed in 30 patients (16.9%) with a median duration of 7 (4-10) days. In 9 patients (5.1%), initial VA-ECMO was converted into VV-ECMO.

Complications with ECMO included AKI requiring RRT (n = 81, 45.5%), thrombus (n = 74, 41.6%), infection (n = 51, 28.7%), bleeding (n = 71, 39.9%), and neurological complications (n = 60, 33.7%).

Of the patients initially weaned from ECMO (n = 111, 62.4%), 14 (12.6%) died within 14 (3.5-33) days without a second ECMO run and 16 (53.3%) died with a second ECMO, representing a short-term mortality rate of 27% despite a first successful ECMO weaning. Overall mortality after ECMO support was 55.1% (n = 98) within 16 (5-34) days. Mortality rates after ECMO support were 53.4% (n = 71) and 66.7% (n = 20) in patients aged ≤2 and >12 years, respectively.

In patients younger than ≤2 years, risk factors for ECMO support were preoperative CPR (odds ratio [OR] 6.29, P < .001), the Norwood procedure (OR 2.6, P = .005), and correction of coronary artery anomalies (OR 6.55, P = .013). Other independent risk factors were CPB time (OR 1.02 [min], P < .001) and the Society of Thoracic Surgeons–European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (STAT) category ≥4 (OR = 1.96, P = .045).

In patients >12 years old, preoperative CPR (OR 17.3, P < .001), CPB time (OR 1.02 [min], P < .001), and preoperative vasoactive support (OR 5.5, P = .049) could be identified as independent risk factors for ECMO support.

Procedure-specific risk of an ECMO implant

Within the Norwood procedure subgroup, preoperative cardiopulmonary resuscitation and longer cardiopulmonary bypass time were independently associated with ECMO use, whereas no significant risk factors were identified among patients undergoing correction of coronary artery anomalies (Tables S3 and S4).

Prediction of mortality with ECMO support

In the adjusted analysis, ECMO duration was nonlinearly associated with mortality (Figure 3). Model-based predictions showed that after an initial phase of very high mortality, mortality risk reached a nadir around days 4 to 5 and subsequently increased steadily beyond day 6 of support, with the odds of death increasing more than 8-fold between days 6 and 12. On the probability scale, this corresponded to an increase in predicted mortality from 26% at day 6 to 74% at day 12.

Discussion

Summary of the results

In this study of nearly 10 000 patients admitted for congenital heart surgery, the ECMO support rate was 1.8%. The most common indication for ECMO was the inability to wean patients from CPB in the operating room. In very young patients (≤2 years), the Norwood procedure and an operation for coronary malformation were independently associated with an increased risk of ECMO, whereas preoperative CPR and longer CPB times were associated with ECMO use irrespective of age. Although 62% of patients were initially successfully weaned from ECMO, 27% of those patients ultimately subsequently died, resulting in an overall short-term mortality of 55%. Among patients who survived an initial ECMO weaning, acute renal failure requiring renal replacement therapy remained the only factor independently associated with mortality, underscoring the dominant prognostic impact of persistent end-organ dysfunction even after apparent recovery from ECMO.

Occurrence of and risk factors for ECMO in congenital heart surgery

The ECMO rate observed was consistent with that reported internationally.4 Several risk factors for ECMO support were identified for the paediatric subgroup, including the need for CPR prior to the operation, the Norwood procedure, and prolonged CPB, in line with previous reports.7–11 Also, in the subgroup of patients undergoing the Norwood procedure, preoperative cardiopulmonary resuscitation and longer cardiopulmonary bypass time were associated with subsequent ECMO support. These associations, consistent with established clinical experience, support early risk anticipation and preparedness for ECMO in high-risk patients having the Norwood procedure. Low weight (<3 kg) and young age (≤6 weeks), which have been reported elsewhere as independent risk factors, were not significant in our cohort. In our practice, the patient’s preoperative condition and the underlying diagnosis are key determinants of prognosis and generally outweigh the patient’s age and weight alone. In this regard, the extent of preoperative vasoactive substances and the serum lactate level are reliable indicators of haemodynamic status and have been shown to correlate with the outcome, as reported by Kuraim et al.8 These authors also highlighted persistent anatomical malformations as a cause of failure to wean from CPB during cardiac surgery, underscoring the importance of systematically assessing for residual lesions after surgical correction or palliation. When feasible, such lesions should be addressed promptly to prevent progression to multiorgan failure, which may ultimately necessitate ECMO support. Consistent with previous reports, an operation for coronary anomalies has also been described as high risk in very young patients.8,12 Taken together, these observations emphasize the need for heightened vigilance and meticulous operative planning in children undergoing high-risk procedures, as well as optimization of preoperative haemodynamic status.

Data on the use of perioperative ECMO in adults with CHD remain limited. In a single-centre study, Acheampong et al.13 reported a 1.1% rate of mechanical circulatory support (including intra-aortic balloon pump and ECMO), most commonly in patients with pulmonary atresia with an intact ventricular septum, tetralogy of Fallot, and the Ebstein anomaly. Using a national database and consistent with our findings, Dolgner et al.5 reported an ECMO utilization rate of 1% in this population, identifying univentricular physiology, surgical complexity, age, and renal failure as risk factors. In the present study, the highest rates of ECMO support in patients older than 12 years were observed with double-switch procedures, aortic arch reconstruction, and mitral valve replacement. As in the paediatric population, preoperative CPR, preoperative vasoactive support, and CPB time were independently associated with ECMO support. Here, too, careful patient selection and appropriate surgical planning may help reduce the need for postoperative circulatory support.

Outcomes and prediction of mortality

The types and rates of complications associated with ECMO in this study were comparable to those previously reported.11 Other adverse events, such as vascular access occlusion and ventricular dilatation, are recognized as potentially fatal complications with ECMO but were not specifically addressed in the present analysis.6

Overall mortality was comparable to that reported in recent meta-analyses and international registries.1,7,11,14 As with predictors of ECMO use, predictors of mortality during ECMO are reported inconsistently in the literature. Univentricular physiology and the presence of genetic anomalies are well-established risk factors for poor outcomes during ECMO support.4,7 Management of ECMO in the presence of a systemic-pulmonary shunt is particularly challenging in neonates and low-birth-weight infants, which likely contributes to the mortality associated after the Norwood procedure and other palliative operations in premature infants despite ECMO support.9,15 Consistent with our findings and those of previous studies, the development of severe renal failure appears to be an early marker of multi-organ dysfunction and is associated with a poor prognosis during ECMO.11 Finally, as with failure to wean from CPB, unsuccessful ECMO weaning may result from persistent anatomical defects.8 Such defects must be actively sought out and, when feasible, addressed promptly, because early correction has been shown to improve survival in patients supported with ECMO.8,12

The continued accumulation of mortality risk during prolonged support, as illustrated in this study, underscores the need for ongoing daily reassessment. As such, diagnostic and therapeutic strategies aimed at facilitating myocardial recovery, identifying reversible complications or anatomical defects, or enabling ECMO weaning should be actively pursued, because continued support without a clear trajectory towards recovery may be associated with increasingly unfavourable outcomes. By providing time-updated estimates of prognosis, the model may support clinical decision-making, inform discussions with families, and help contextualize expectations when recovery does not occur and additional therapeutic options are no longer available.

Limitations

The retrospective design is subject to selection bias, particularly regarding the timing and indications for an ECMO implant. Although we adjusted for measured clinical and procedural variables, residual confounding due to unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. The heterogeneity of congenital heart diagnoses and procedures, together with the limited representation of rare high-risk operations and adult patients, constrained procedure-specific risk assessment. Although surgical era was included as a covariate, this approach may not fully capture gradual or procedure-specific improvements in surgical and perioperative management over time. Assessment of corrective cardiac operations during ECMO was limited by the time-dependent nature of surgical interventions, potential immortal time bias, and substantial heterogeneity of procedures and clinical contexts. The subgroup analysis of patients surviving a first ECMO weaning was limited by sample size and survivor selection, which may have reduced the power to identify additional risk factors beyond acute renal failure. Outcomes of patients supported with ECMO after CPR may differ from those of patients receiving ECMO for other indications, and the lack of detailed resuscitation-related variables limited adjustment for this heterogeneity. In addition, retrospective attribution of the cause of death during ECMO was limited by disease severity, frequent multi-organ failure, treatment-limiting decisions, and the lack of detailed causal information, precluding reliable determination of the specific mechanisms of death.

Our results are limited to short-term mortality and do not provide any information on the long-term mortality or morbidity among children weaned from ECMO. In particular, neurological complications observed during ECMO support may lead to severe cognitive and motor developmental impairment in ECMO survivors, which was not assessed in this study.16

Finally, as suggested by results from recent studies, incorporation of biomarkers—which were not available in our data set—into survival prediction models may improve their accuracy while enhancing objectivity and reproducibility. Future research in this direction is warranted.

Conclusions

In this study, ECMO was required in approximately 2% of patients undergoing congenital heart surgery, and survival in this high-risk population was approximately 50% despite ECMO. In young children, operations such as the Norwood procedure and correction of coronary malformations were more frequently associated with ECMO support, whereas prolonged ECMO duration and acute renal failure requiring renal replacement therapy were independently associated with death. The marked increase in the number of deaths beyond 6 days of ECMO underscores the importance of early reassessment of reversibility and timely consideration of recovery-oriented or alternative care pathways during prolonged support.
