A multicentre observational study on landiolol use, efficacy, and safety in European patients with supraventricular arrhythmia (Landi-UP)
Authors: Al-Medina Dizdarevic, Marek Šramko, Norman Mangner, Béla Merkely, Endre Zima, Nikolaou Nikolaos, Daniel Scherr, Johann Knotzer, Paul Köglberger, Zoi Nouvaki, Martin Martinek, Tomas Hnat, Carina Bethlehem, Zlatko Fras, Waldemar Goździk, Anastasia Kotanidou, Wojciech Dąbrowski, Maciej Lesiak, Karina Stefanska-Wronka, Martin Unger, Jan Sus, Christoph Klade, Kurt Krejcy, Olivier Bouvet, Eva Oravec, Günther Krumpl, Jolanta Siller-Matula
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsLandiolol is an ultrashort-acting, highly cardio-selective beta1-adrenergic receptor blocker used for short-term control of tachyarrhythmias. While its real-world use has been described in Japan, data from European clinical practice are lacking. This study aimed to collect real-world data on landiolol use, effectiveness, and safety across diverse clinical settings in Europe.Methods and resultsThis multicentre, observational study included adult patients with supraventricular arrhythmias treated with landiolol. Patient characteristics, drug utilization patterns, efficacy, and safety were assessed. In total, 450 patients were enrolled and 449 were analysed (median age: 72 years, IQR 63–78) from 17 sites across 8 European countries. Most patients (73.1%) received landiolol for atrial fibrillation. The median (IQR) duration of infusion was 8.9 (2.3–34.0) h, with starting, minimum, and maximum doses of 5.3 (2.5–10.0), 4.0 (1.7–10), and 10.0 (6.0–30.0) µg/kg/min, respectively. Heart rate (HR) control, defined as HR ≤110 b.p.m. or a reduction of >20% from baseline, was achieved in 74.2% of patients within 4 h after treatment discontinuation, with 36.9% of arrhythmia patients restoring sinus rhythm. Blood pressure (BP) remained stable throughout treatment. A total of 123 adverse events and 113 major adverse cardiac events were reported, none of which were related to landiolol.ConclusionIn European clinical practice, landiolol dosing adhered to product information recommendations. Landiolol was effective in controlling HR, with minimal impact on BP. The treatment was well tolerated, and no new safety signals were identified. These findings support the efficacy and safety of landiolol across diverse clinical settings in Europe.Keywords: Landiolol, Ultra-short acting beta-blocker, Observational study, Real-world data, Supraventricular arrhythmia, Heart rate control

Key Question: How is landiolol used in routine clinical practice across Europe, and what are its efficacy and safety profiles in patients with different types of arrhythmias, hospital settings, comorbidities, and concomitant treatments or interventions?

Key Finding: Landiolol was administered in accordance with the product information recommendations. It effectively controlled heart rate with minimal impact on blood pressure across various clinical settings, was well tolerated, and no new safety concerns were observed.

Take-home Message: The findings of this study support the efficacy and safety of landiolol across diverse clinical settings in Europe.

Introduction

Landiolol hydrochloride is an ultrashort-acting, highly cardio-selective beta1-adrenergic receptor blocker with a rapid onset of action, used for the short-term control of tachyarrhythmias.1–3 Initially developed in Japan, where landiolol has been used for more than 20 years. It was introduced to the European market in 2017 and is currently indicated for the treatment of supraventricular tachycardia and for the rapid control of ventricular rate in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) or atrial flutter (AFL) in perioperative, postoperative, or other circumstances where short-term control of the ventricular rate with a short-acting agent is desirable, as well as for non-compensatory sinus tachyarrhythmia where, in the physician’s judgment, the rapid heart rate (HR) requires specific intervention.1 Observational studies have the potential to provide new insights into medicine utilization and its effects in different patient groups. Furthermore, a Phase IV trial gives additional information on clinical drug use according to the product information and possible new safety signals. While an observational study describing the real-world use of landiolol has been conducted in the Japanese population,4 no real-world data have been collected in European countries. This observational study aimed to report landiolol utilization patterns in routine medical practice across eight European countries. We report its efficacy and safety in various types of arrhythmias, different hospital settings, and comorbidities, as well as concomitant treatments and interventions.

Methods

Ethics

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles originating from the Declaration of Helsinki and its full amendments, and in the general spirit of the ICH-GCP Good Clinical Practice Guidelines. The Central Ethics Committee (EC) overseeing this study was the EC of the Medical University of Vienna (EC No: 1491/2020), while each participating country had its own national or site-specific EC. The ECs approved the study protocol, protocol amendments, informed consent forms, and other relevant documents prior to site initiation.

Trial design and objectives

The Landi-UP trial was an observational, multicentre, prospective study designed to monitor drug utilization patterns, patient characteristics, effectiveness, and safety using real-world data from patients treated with landiolol. Data on patients were collected either retrospectively, dated from the time of study initiation at the respective site, or prospectively throughout the entire study. The primary objective of this study was to characterize drug utilization patterns in patients treated with landiolol. The secondary objectives included evaluating the effectiveness and safety of landiolol treatment in a real-world setting, describing the characteristics of patients treated with landiolol, assessing the length of stay in the intensive care unit (ICU), emergency department (ED), and hospital, and surveying the major cardiac outcomes of patients treated with landiolol up to 180 days.

Patients

Adult patients aged ≥18 years who received landiolol for the treatment of supraventricular arrhythmias (SVA) were included in the study. Patient selection, diagnostic, and monitoring procedures depended on the treating physician or local guidelines and were not dictated by the protocol. Patients were informed about the study as soon as they were able to understand its scope. Every effort was made to obtain informed consent before entering data into the study database. To prevent selection bias, a waiver for retrospective routine data collection without prior consent was granted for patients who died or, in all countries except Austria, were otherwise incapable of consenting during hospitalization. In Austria, for patients unable to provide consent during hospitalization, all efforts were made to obtain informed consent after discharge but prior to data collection.

Treatment

Landiolol hydrochloride was administered intravenously via infusion. The decision to initiate treatment with landiolol was made by the treating physician and was expected to be in accordance with the prescribing recommendations in the particular country. The recommended dosing ranged from 1 µg/kg/min for patients with cardiac dysfunction to a maximum short-term maintenance dose of 80 µg/kg/min, with an optional bolus dose of 100 µg/kg.

Procedures

Patients’ visit schedules followed the local standard of care, with additional visits arranged at the discretion of the treating physician. The visit schedule is provided in Supplementary material online, Table S2. The study did not interfere with the usual care of patients. For each patient, landiolol exposure and its effect on cardiac rhythm and rate control were assessed. Major adverse cardiac events (MACEs) were recorded up to 6 months after the first administration of landiolol during re-visits scheduled according to the standard of care. A patient completed the study either upon hospital discharge or after reaching the optional 6-month follow-up visit. No additional information was collected solely for the purpose of this study.

Study endpoints

Primary endpoints

The primary endpoints were utilization patterns related to landiolol exposure and included the duration of infusion, total dose, starting dose, minimum and maximum doses, and the preferred route of application (central or peripheral venous access).

Secondary endpoints

Effectiveness of landiolol treatment was based on the following criteria: the proportion of patients achieving HR control (defined as HR ≤ 110 b.p.m., which is the HR often sufficient to improve AF-related symptoms,5,6 or 20% less than baseline, which is often considered a clinically meaningful HR reduction) within 4 h after landiolol discontinuation, the proportion of patients achieving more aggressive HR control (defined as HR ≤ 90 b.p.m.) within 4 h after landiolol discontinuation, the proportion of patients recovering a sinus rhythm (spontaneous recovery or cardioversion) within 4 h after landiolol discontinuation, as assessed by the investigator, and the proportion of patients requiring additional pharmacological or electrical cardioversion for rhythm control during their hospital stay. Time to achieve HR ≤ 110 b.p.m. was calculated as the difference between the start time of HR control (defined as HR ≤ 110 b.p.m. or 20% less than baseline) and the end time of landiolol treatment. If HR control was not achieved within the pre-specified time, the event was censored at 180 days. Secondary safety endpoints included adverse events (AEs) recorded up to 4 h after landiolol discontinuation, AEs requiring treatment discontinuation or requiring specific therapy, serious AEs (SAEs), length of ICU/ED and hospital stays, and MACEs up to 180 days post-landiolol initiation. MACEs comprised transient ischemic attack or ischemic stroke, MI (ST-elevation myocardial infarction [STEMI]/ non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction [NSTEMI]), cardiovascular death, all-cause mortality, unstable angina, heart failure (HF)-related hospitalization, and de-novo AF requiring specific treatment. An AE was defined as any unexpected medical occurrence in a patient receiving landiolol, including clinically significant symptoms, abnormal test findings, hypersensitivity, or changes in physical examination. An SAE was defined as an AE resulting in death, life-threatening conditions, hospitalization or its prolongation, significant disability, or congenital anomalies. Medical history, the reason for landiolol use, left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction, and the use of concomitant medications were used for the characterization of patients receiving landiolol treatment.

Statistical analyses

No formal sample size calculation has been performed. The sample size of 450 patients was considered adequate to investigate the objectives of the study. Individual subject data were collected from electronic case report forms, central laboratories, external sources, and any derived data. The primary data source was used for all analyses. Statistical analyses for the primary and secondary endpoints were performed according to the Statistical Analysis Plan (SAP) using SPSS® software (IBM SPSS, Version 29.0.1.0 (171), IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The SAP is available in the Supplement.

Subgroup analyses for the primary and secondary endpoints were pre-specified in the SAP and were conducted for subgroups based on the type of arrhythmia, hospital setting, presence of comorbidities (sepsis, HF, and AMI), use of concomitant treatments (antiarrhythmics and inotropes), and concomitant interventions (electrical cardioversion and cardiac ablation).

Results

Study participants

A total of 450 patients treated with landiolol were enrolled in the study between 9 July 2020 and 31 December 2023. In total, 449 patients provided evaluable data and were included in the statistical analyses. The study was conducted across eight European countries: Austria, the Czech Republic, Greece, Germany, Hungary, Poland, the Netherlands, and Slovenia. There were 17 sites that enrolled at least one patient. The disposition of patients is shown in Figure 1.

Study flowchart. 1Other settings include normal hospital ward (n = 38), postoperative monitoring care (n = 1), outpatient clinic (n = 1) and other (n = 1).

The median (IQR) age of patients was 72 (63–78) years, with a majority being male (59.5%, 267/449). The study predominantly enrolled Caucasian patients (98.2%, 441/449), with the remaining participants being Asian (1.8%, 8/449). The median (IQR) HR at baseline was 133 (114–149) b.p.m. Most patients (77.1%, 346/449) suffered from comorbidities, with arterial hypertension being the most common, affecting 50.8% (228/449) of patients. The primary indication for landiolol treatment was AF (73.1%, 328/449), followed by ST (10.5%, 47/449) and AFL (9.4%, 42/449). At the time of arrhythmia onset, 51% (229/449) of patients were being treated in the ICU, 39.9% (179/449) in the ED, and 8.5% (38/449) in a normal hospital ward (NHW). During landiolol treatment, 78% (350/449) of patients received concomitant antiarrhythmic agents, including 25.4% (114/449) who received amiodarone and 16.5% (74/449) who received other beta-blockers. Additionally, among the 449 patients, 12% (54/449) received inotropic agents, 6% (27/449) underwent electrical cardioversion, and 0.9% (4/449) underwent cardiac ablation. Primary endpoint

Among the 449 patients treated with landiolol and analysed in the study, 425 (94.7%) received a single treatment, while 24 (5.3%) received repeated treatments. Of these, 20 patients (4.5%) received 2 treatments, and 4 patients (0.9%) received 3 treatments, resulting in a total of 477 treatments. The median (IQR) duration of landiolol infusion was 8.9 (2.3–34.0) h. The median (IQR) starting, lowest, and highest landiolol infusion rates were 5.3 (2.5–10.0), 4.0 (1.7–10.0), and 10.0 (6.0–30.0) µg/kg/min, respectively. The highest total median (IQR) doses were 981 (276–1932), 540 (294–1605), and 498 (214–1200) mg and were administered to patients with ST, patients treated in the ICU, and patients with sepsis, respectively. The lowest total median dose of 300 mg was administered to patients treated in the ED, as well as those with AF and AMI. In 53.0% (253/477) of treatment cases, landiolol infusion was administered via a central line, while a peripheral venous access was used in 45.1% (215/477) of cases.

Secondary endpoints

Efficacy endpoints

In total, 74.2% (333/449) of patients achieved HR control within 4 h after landiolol treatment discontinuation. The median (IQR) time to HR control ≤110 b.p.m. was 181 (120–2714) min. In total, 49.9% (224/449) of patients achieved an HR ≤90 b.p.m. within 4 h after landiolol treatment discontinuation. In total, 36.9% (148/401) of arrhythmia patients (excluding patients present with ST) recovered a sinus rhythm within 4 h after landiolol treatment discontinuation. A total of 42.3% (190/449) patients required additional pharmacological or electrical cardioversion for rhythm control. Electrical cardioversions (170/449) were more frequent than pharmacological cardioversions (59/449). For surviving patients, the median (IQR) length of stay in the ICU, ED, and hospital was 11.0 (5.0–19.5), 1.0 (1.0–7.0), and 9.0 (3.0–17.0) days, respectively. Safety endpoints

A total of 123 AEs occurred in 17.6% (79/449) of patients. Of these, 56 AEs in 8% (36/449) of patients were classified as SAEs. The most frequently reported AEs were AF (5.3%, 24/449) and hypotension (2.0%, 9/449). One case of bradycardia was reported in a single patient. None of the AEs were related to the landiolol treatment. Six AEs led to treatment discontinuation in six patients (1.3%). Among the 449 patients, 52 AEs required specific therapy in 33 (7.4%) patients. A total of 113 MACEs were reported in 24.9% (112/449) of patients. Within 6 months of landiolol treatment, 24.5% (110/449) of patients died. Discussion

This study primarily aimed to examine drug utilization patterns of landiolol in everyday medical practice, to understand the characteristics of the user population, and to evaluate the relationship between recommended use and actual clinical practice. Most patients (90%) received intravenous landiolol in the intensive or emergency care setting, predominantly for ventricular rate control in AF (73%). Antiarrhythmic agents were co-administered in most patients (78%).

Doses of landiolol used in clinical routine practice were aligned with the instructions stated in the summary of product characteristics (SmPC), and no patient received a dose outside the recommended dosing range.1 Only one patient received a bolus dose of 100 µg/kg, suggesting that a loading dose is not typically considered necessary in clinical routine. The doses of landiolol administered in this study were relatively low compared with the dosing range specified for the product,1 although they were higher than the doses reported in an observational study of landiolol use in AF/AFL patients with chronic HF in Japan.4 This finding applies to both the general population included in our study and the subgroup of patients with HF. On the other hand, landiolol was administered for a shorter duration in our study compared with Yamashita’s study (median duration of 8.9 h vs. 49.0 h), which could be explained by the inclusion of solely chronic HF patients requiring prolonged treatment in the Japanese study.4 The rather conservative dosing in our study may reflect the high number of patients with cardiac dysfunction in this study and aligns with the recommendations in the SmPC, which recommends a dose range of 1–10 µg/kg/min.1 It may also have been influenced by the inclusion of a large number of multimorbid patients requiring concomitant medication, such as antiarrhythmics, antihypertensives, and inotropes, which necessitate careful titration of landiolol.1 The multimorbidity and polypharmacy observed in this observational study reflect clinical practice. Additionally, the study protocol did not provide specific guidance on landiolol dosing beyond the requirement to use the drug in accordance with the SmPC. This absence of protocol-defined dosing instructions, combined with limited familiarity with landiolol at some participating sites at the time of study conduct, may have resulted in more cautious titration and, in some cases, lower initial doses than those commonly used in current clinical practice.

In 32.7% (156/477) of treatment cases, landiolol was administered over the 24 h duration recommended by the SmPC.1 The duration of landiolol administration varied depending on the type of facility, with the shortest duration observed at the ED (median of 2.0 h), and longer durations at the ICU and NHW (median duration of 21.6 and 22.0 h, respectively). The longest administration duration was required for the treatment of ST, which also resulted in the highest total doses in these patients. Severe concomitant illnesses, such as sepsis, HF, and AMI, prolonged the duration of landiolol administration. The patients with concomitant administration of antiarrhythmic agents were administered landiolol for a shorter duration vs. those without (7.3 vs. 17.0 h), which may reflect a need for faster sinus rhythm conversion in patients requiring combination treatment. Conversely, the use of inotropic agents was associated with a longer duration of landiolol administration (27.0 vs. 7.0 h), likely reflecting the treatment of critically ill patients with refractory arrhythmias who required combination therapy.

Patients suffering from sepsis and AMI received approximately half the landiolol dose compared with patients without these conditions, while the doses in patients with and without HF were similar. The median starting dose of 3.2 μg/kg/min administered to sepsis patients in our trial was higher than the starting doses used in the randomized clinical trials (RCTs), where titration began at a 1 μg/kg/min dose.7–9 The mean duration of administration in clinical practice (36.9 h) was shorter than that reported in the RCTs (67.3–94.5 h),7–9 while the mean (SD) maximum dose of 9.7 (14.1) μg/kg/min in our study was comparable to the value of 7.0 (6.3) μg/kg/min reported in the Japanese study.7 The starting dose for AMI patients in our study was nearly identical to that observed in two Japanese clinical studies, where the infusion began at 3 μg/min/kg.10,11 The doses administered to patients with HF were consistent with those observed in clinical studies involving Japanese patients.12

HR control was achieved in most patients across various arrhythmia origins, as well as in patients with different hospital settings, those with concomitant illnesses, and those on other medications and undergoing interventions. The highest proportion of responders was observed in ST patients and those treated in the ICU. HR control was consistently high regardless of comorbidities, such as AMI or HF (see Supplementary material online, Table S12). These efficacy results align with findings from RCTs in various populations, including patients after cardiac surgery,13,14 with AMI,10 sepsis,7 or LV dysfunction.15 Similar response rates (77.5% of patients with a 20% HR reduction) were reported in an observational study of Japanese patients with chronic HF.4 The doses of landiolol used in this study are also in line with those used in the Japanese RCTs and cohorts, confirming a similar dose-response profile between Japanese and Caucasian patients, as previously discussed by Shiga et al.12 The concomitant use of antiarrhythmics was associated with slightly lower HR control compared with patients not using these agents. This difference may be explained by the more compromised patients requiring additional antiarrhythmic therapy and the expectedly lower response rates in this population. Landiolol demonstrated a consistent HR control across different subgroups of patients (see Supplementary material online, Table S9), which aligns with the HR reduction observed in existing Japanese clinical routine use cohorts.4,16–18 Our findings are comparable to a recent Caucasian cardiac surgery cohort, which reported a 64% HR response and a 26% sinus rhythm conversion within 4 h.19 A small Japanese cohort already showed that AF surgical patients with cardiac disease tended to respond better than AF non-surgical patients without cardiac disease.16

The percentage of patients who recovered a sinus rhythm within 4 h after landiolol discontinuation was lower than the rates of conversion to sinus rhythm reported in the systematic review assessing landiolol for the treatment of new-onset AF in critical care,20 however, it may be influenced by different evaluation periods within the individual studies. The conversion rate reported at 4 h is comparable with the rate observed at the same time point in the study by Chapalain et al.19 The proportion of patients with AF and AFL who recovered sinus rhythm was comparable to the findings in Yamashita’s study.4 Most patients in the study received antiarrhythmic agents other than landiolol. However, the proportion of patients who achieved sinus rhythm restoration was higher among those receiving landiolol without any additional antiarrhythmic agents compared with those receiving landiolol in combination with other antiarrhythmics [43.7% (31/99) vs. 35.5% (117/350), Supplementary material online, Table S13]. Additionally, patients who underwent electrical cardioversion during the study had a slightly lower rate of sinus rhythm restoration compared with those who did not undergo cardioversion [33.3% (9/27) vs. 37.2% (139/422), Supplementary material online, Table S14]. These findings suggest that the restoration of sinus rhythm was not substantially influenced by the concomitant use of other antiarrhythmic agents or electrical cardioversion.

Landiolol was generally well tolerated, with a very low incidence of hypotension (2%) and bradycardia (0.2%), reaffirming its favourable safety profile. The percentage of patients experiencing AE leading to landiolol discontinuation was as low as 1.3% (6/449), which is similar to the percentage (3.7%) observed in a recent Caucasian cohort in cardiac surgery.19 These findings are consistent with recent evidence that landiolol shows a relatively low risk of hypotension and bradycardia.21 Importantly, no AEs and MACEs were related to landiolol treatment. This real-world data reveal no new safety signals resulting from intravenous landiolol use, either in the overall population or in the different subgroups analysed.

Generalizability and limitations

The study included adult patients, predominantly Caucasians, who received landiolol for any of the indications listed in the SmPC across a broad range of hospital settings, with varying concurrent illnesses and concomitant treatments. The medical heterogeneity of the population enhances the applicability of study findings to clinical practice. Additionally, the study’s broad coverage of countries and participating sites ensures a good representation of treatment strategies, reflecting different medical and institutional guidelines. The study population and design are considered well-suited to address the study objectives. The main limitations of this study are associated with its observational nature. Firstly, there was no control group. Since the study was conducted in a routine clinical setting under diverse circumstances, the data recording did not meet the usual standards of clinical trials, resulting in a considerable amount of missing data. Although the aim was to capture the HR value closest to landiolol discontinuation, the efficacy of the treatment was assessed within the window of 4 h after landiolol discontinuation. This timeframe was established in consideration of the limitations associated with frequent documentation in routine clinical practice and is acknowledged to be less stringent than the recording requirements typically employed in interventional trials. Patients who achieved HR control during treatment but did not maintain it after discontinuation were not classified as responders, which may have led to an underestimation of response rates. Assessment of the time to HR control was dependent on the frequency of HR measurements during and after treatment, which was often sparse and differed between sites. Therefore, the values reported for this endpoint do not correlate with the prompt onset of action of landiolol, which typically occurs within a few minutes.22 The available data lack sufficient granularity to distinguish between patients with typical and atypical AFL. This study included only the patients suffering from SVA and does not provide the information about utilization patterns, efficacy, and safety for patients with ventricular arrhythmias, for whom landiolol is recommended in the treatment guidelines.23

Conclusion

Dosing of landiolol in clinical routine practice aligned with the recommendations outlined in the SmPC. Its usage may reflect the high number of patients with cardiac dysfunction in this study and was consistent with the SmPC recommendation of 1–10 µg/kg/min. Landiolol demonstrated effective control of SVA across its various subtypes while having minimal impact on BP. It was generally well tolerated, with a low incidence of hypotension and bradycardia, underscoring its favourable safety profile and the effectiveness of risk management measures. None of the AEs or MACEs were related to landiolol, and no new safety concerns were identified. This real-world data strengthen the evidence base for landiolol and supports its use in patients with SVA of various origins in a wide range of hospital settings, including those with diverse concurrent illnesses, concomitant medications, and interventions.
