Diversity and Feature Selectivity of Primate Retinal Ganglion Cells
Authors: Natalie G. Bernstein, Teresa Puthussery
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The primate retina transmits visual information to the brain through parallel pathways carried by diverse retinal ganglion cell (RGC) types, each of which encodes specific features in the visual environment. These parallel channels provide input to a variety of brain regions to support both conscious and reflexive visual functions. Although the midget, parasol, and small bistratified pathways have dominated classical models of primate vision, accumulating evidence points to a broader diversity of wide-field RGC types whose functions are less well understood. Recent breakthroughs in single-cell sequencing have provided new insights into the transcriptomic signatures of these wide-field cells, uncovering molecular markers that can be used to link sparse RGC types to their distinct morphologies and physiological functions. This review synthesizes current knowledge about the diversity of wide-field RGCs in the primate retina and highlights recent insights into cells that encode complex visual features, such as direction-selective ganglion cells. We emphasize how integrative methodological approaches are refining our understanding of the roles of different wide-field RGC types in primate vision.

INTRODUCTION

Background

The vertebrate retina is one of the most comprehensively characterized and experimentally accessible circuits in the central nervous system. Across only two synapses, the retina performs complex computations to extract salient features from visual input. This information is encoded by retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) as trains of action potentials, which are sent to the brain to generate visual perception and visually guided behavior. In humans and other foveated primates, retinal output is, by necessity, dominated by midget RGCs, which have the smallest receptive fields and are thus crucial for our exquisite high-acuity spatial vision. Due to their abundance, midget RGCs are very well-characterized, but the primate retina contains upward of 15 other RGC types that are present at much lower densities and thus have received much less attention. In this review, we focus on the properties of this highly diverse group of primate RGC types. We consider the idea that these RGC types are conserved across species because they serve indispensable functions for visual processing. Ultimately, a complete understanding of the neural output of the primate retina will be essential for developing a complete computational model of the visual system. Moreover, a deeper understanding of primate retinal circuits will be important for developing improved diagnostics and next-generation therapeutics for the treatment of blinding neurodegenerative diseases.

Scope of This Review

This review focuses on recent developments in understanding the transcriptomic, morphological, and functional diversity of RGCs in the primate retina. Since the properties of midget, parasol, and small bistratified RGCs have been reviewed in detail elsewhere (Field & Chichilnisky 2007; Grünert & Martin 2020, 2021; Lee et al. 2010), we focus on the less well-characterized low-density RGC types. We discuss recent advances in single-cell transcriptomic analysis that allow for an empirically derived comparison of RGC types across species. This development is paradigm shifting, as the vast amount of knowledge around RGCs in nonprimate species can now be leveraged to advance understanding of sparse RGC types in primates, including humans. Our focus is primarily on the macaque retina, given its evolutionary proximity and high level of molecular, morphological, and functional homology to the human retina. We highlight important differences between the macaque, marmoset, and human retina where relevant.

OVERVIEW OF RETINAL PROCESSING AND ORGANIZATION

The basic structure of the primate retina is essentially identical to that of other vertebrates, with three neuronal layers alternating with two synaptic (plexiform) layers. Retinal processing is distributed across three major classes of glutamatergic neurons—photoreceptors, bipolar cells, and ganglion cells (GCs)—which form the excitatory vertical pathway, while lateral inhibitory interactions with horizontal cells and amacrine cells serve to modify and refine these signals.

Rod and cone photoreceptors transduce the image on the retina into a continuously variable rate of glutamate release from their synaptic terminals. In the outer plexiform layer, this neural representation is refined by inhibitory feedback from two types of horizontal cells, which serve to regulate signal gain, facilitate light adaptation, and establish center-surround and color-opponent receptive fields (for a review see, Thoreson & Mangel 2012). This processed photoreceptor output signal is then transmitted to ~12 bipolar cell types (reviewed in Grünert & Martin 2020, 2021), which depolarize in response to light increments (ON bipolar cells) or decrements (OFF bipolar cells). Each bipolar cell type represents a parallel output channel that carries distinct spatial, temporal, and chromatic information.

In the inner plexiform layer (IPL), the bipolar cell signal is further modified through inhibitory interactions with amacrine cells. This diverse class of GABAergic and glycinergic interneurons underlies additional spatiotemporal signal refinement and is crucial for generating the feature selectivity of different RGC types. RGCs are the sole output neurons of the retina, and they relay visual signals as trains of action potentials to retinorecipient areas in the brain that are involved in conscious and unconscious visual functions. As with most neurons in the retina, the receptive field of each RGC type is organized into a nonrandom mosaic, such that every point of the visual field is sampled by each functional cell type with minimal redundancy.

RETINAL GANGLION CELL DIFFERENCES BETWEEN PRIMATES AND OTHER MAMMALS

There are several anatomical and functional features that distinguish the primate retina from those of other mammals. Perhaps most notable is the presence of a fovea, an anatomical specialization present in the retinas of humans and other haplorrhine primates but absent from the retinas of nonprimate mammals. The fovea, which spans the central 1 mm of the macaque retina (Provis et al. 2013), is characterized by its high packing density of cones and midget RGCs, which support high spatial acuity vision. Within the fovea, each cone transmits signals to a single ON midget bipolar cell and a single OFF midget bipolar cell, which, in turn, form one-to-one connections with the corresponding ON and OFF midget RGCs (Kolb & Dekorver 1991, Kolb & Marshak 2003). This “private line” connectivity preserves the fine-grained spatial information captured by the cone array. Notably, foveal cones also contact a variety of other bipolar cell and RGC types, indicating that the fovea can support a diverse range of visual functions (Kim et al. 2025, Patterson et al. 2022, Percival et al. 2013).

The requirement for high spatial acuity means that the overwhelming majority (~60%) of primate RGCs are ON and OFF midget RGCs (Dacey 1993, Kim et al. 2022, Peng et al. 2019). The primate retina also contains ON and OFF parasol RGCs, which are functionally similar to the motion-sensitive Y-like RGCs of the cat and the transient alpha-type cells in rodents and account for a further ~15% of RGCs. Together with the blue-yellow color-opponent small bistratified RGCs, these five major cell types comprise ~80% of the ~1–1.5 million RGCs in the primate retina (Kim et al. 2022). This accounting leaves 15 or more RGC types that have been less well-studied, with each comprising only ~0.5–1.5% of the total population (Kim et al. 2022). These low-density RGCs have larger (wide-field) receptive fields, such that fewer cells are needed for uniform feature extraction across the visual field.

The relative abundance of midget, parasol, and small bistratified RGCs has made resolving the physiological functions of the remaining low-density RGCs a formidable task. Emerging evidence suggests that at least some of these sparse types exhibit complex receptive field properties and contribute to visual reflexes that function in parallel with conscious, image-forming vision (Kim et al. 2022, Wang et al. 2023). Indeed, intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs), the most wide-field and thus lowest-density RGC type, are crucial for essential physiological functions such as circadian photoentrainment and pupillary light reflexes (Dacey et al. 2005, Gamlin et al. 2007). Thus, there is already strong evidence that numerical abundance is not a proxy for functional significance and that even sparse neuronal populations can be essential for fundamental physiological processes that are vital for survival.

Each RGC type conveys specialized visual signals to a diverse set of brain regions with distinct roles in modulating visual behavior and perception. Thus, understanding the retinorecipient targets and downstream circuitry of wide-field RGC types will be critical for determining their roles in visual processing. In the next section, we provide a brief overview of the central projection targets of primate RGCs and consider the functional significance of these pathways.

PRIMATE RETINORECIPIENT AREAS

Primate RGCs send axonal projections to a diverse array of retinorecipient regions in the brain (Dacey 2004, Dacey et al. 2003, Hafed et al. 2023, Hannibal et al. 2014, Kim et al. 2022, Kwan et al. 2019, Liao et al. 2016, Perry et al. 1984, Santana et al. 2023). The dorsal lateral geniculate nucleus (dLGN) of the thalamus acts as the primary relay between the retina and the primary visual cortex, modulating RGC input to facilitate conscious image-forming vision (Briggs & Usrey 2011, Merigan & Maunsell 1993). The dLGN is a laminated, retinotopically organized structure composed of functionally and neuroanatomically distinct magnocellular, parvocellular, and koniocellular layers (Hendry & Yoshioka 1994, Usrey & Alitto 2015). The magnocellular and parvocellular layers predominantly receive input from parasol and midget RGCs, respectively (Leventhal et al. 1981, Perry et al. 1984). In contrast, the koniocellular layer receives input from a more heterogeneous group of RGCs (Percival et al. 2011, 2013; Szmajda et al. 2008), including small bistratified RGCs and other wide-field RGC types. Some koniocellular retinogeniculate pathways bypass V1 and are thought to contribute to “blindsight” capabilities, enabling residual visual behaviors after V1 damage (Hendry & Reid 2000).

The primate superior colliculus (SC) is a conserved midbrain structure that plays a critical role in sensorimotor transformations. An estimated ~5–10% of primate RGCs innervate the superficial layers of the SC (Perry et al. 1984, Zheng et al. 2024). Retinal input to the SC integrates into circuitry that mediates rapid stimulus detection, orienting behaviors (e.g., saccade and pursuit eye movements), spatial attention, and other visually guided cognitive functions (reviewed in Hafed et al. 2023, Krauzlis et al. 2013). A recent retrograde tracing study suggests that retinal input to the macaque SC arises predominantly from axon collaterals of cells that also project to the dLGN (Zheng et al. 2024). However, the specific RGC classes that give rise to these bifurcating projections and the functional implications of this shared wiring remain unresolved.

In addition to these major targets, tracing studies have determined that RGCs project to several other subcortical areas, including nuclei involved in gaze stabilization (accessory optic system), visual attention (pulvinar nucleus), pupillary control (pretectal olivary nucleus), and circadian photoentrainment (suprachiasmatic nucleus) (Grünert et al. 2021, Kim et al. 2022, Santana et al. 2023). Notably, some wide-field RGC types send axon collaterals to multiple retinorecipient areas, while others, including those that project to the medial subdivision of the inferior pulvinar nucleus, may innervate a single retinorecipient area (Grünert et al. 2021). Further research is needed to achieve a comprehensive understanding of how RGC classification relates to projection patterns and how retinal inputs to these brain regions drive behavior.

APPROACHES FOR CLASSIFICATION OF PRIMATE RETINAL GANGLION CELL TYPES

The comprehensive classification and characterization of an RGC type require integration of complementary methods for resolving molecular signatures, morphological architecture, mosaic organization, functional properties, and local and central circuit connectivity. Although comprehensive surveys exist for some of these modalities (e.g., morphology, transcriptomics), bridging independent classification schemes remains an outstanding challenge. Figure 1 summarizes what is known regarding the morphologies, functions, central projections, and transcriptomic identities of primate RGC types. In the following section, we discuss insights gained from independent classification efforts and potential strategies to unify datasets to achieve a more robust, multimodal classification.

Molecular Diversity of Retinal Ganglion Cells

RGCs have historically been classified primarily on the basis of their morphology and function. While effective, these modalities are relatively low-throughput and can lead to ambiguities in cell type classification. The advent of single-cell RNA sequencing (i.e., single-cell transcriptomics) has revolutionized our understanding of the diversity of vertebrate RGC types. This approach enables parallel sequencing of thousands of cells to distinguish cell types based on shared transcriptional profiles (Figure 2a). This method has revealed that there are at least 18 RGC types in the macaque retina (Peng et al. 2019), 16 in the marmoset retina (Hahn et al. 2023, Zhang et al. 2024), and 21 in the human retina (Hahn et al. 2023, Yan et al. 2020). Beyond mere cell type enumeration, these rich datasets facilitate the identification of molecular markers to label RGC subtypes and provide crucial insights into cell function, connectivity, and transcriptional fate specification.

Recent transcriptomic studies have also revealed new insights into the evolutionary relationships between primate RGCs and those from other well-studied species such as mice. In a landmark study, Hahn et al. (2023) integrated the single-cell transcriptomes of RGCs from 12 different mammals to enable a broad comparative analysis. By clustering cells across all species based on shared transcriptional signatures, they revealed orthologous cell types, or orthotypes, cell populations in different species that share a conserved transcriptomic identity derived from a common ancestral cell type. This analysis showed that ON and OFF midget RGCs, which were assumed to be primate specializations, are orthologs of the sustained ON and OFF α-RGCs in rodents. Similarly, ON and OFF parasol RGCs were identified as the orthologs of transient ON and OFF α-RGCs, respectively. Despite their common evolutionary origins, the proportions of midget and parasol RGCs in the primate retina are markedly higher than those of their rodent α-RGC counterparts, suggesting that the density and functions of different RGC types have evolved to meet species-specific visual needs.

Comparative approaches also provide a framework for predicting the functions of low-density primate RGC types by linking them to their mouse counterparts, where transcriptomic identity and functional properties are more clearly established (Goetz et al. 2022, Huang et al. 2022). Cross-species orthotype mapping suggests that some mouse RGC types, such as ipRGCs and ON-type direction-selective ganglion cells (DSGCs) (Hahn et al. 2023, Peng et al. 2019, Wang et al. 2023), are highly conserved from lower vertebrates to humans, while others, including the two most abundant mouse transcriptomic clusters (clusters C1 and C2; Tran et al. 2019), have no clear primate ortholog (Hahn et al. 2023). Collectively, these findings suggest that evolutionary pressures have conserved certain core RGC functions while modifying or even eliminating others to suit distinct ecological niches.

Single-cell transcriptomic methods have yielded a foundational molecular reference that can now be leveraged to classify RGCs using spatially resolved methods that preserve tissue structure. Such spatial methods are important, as dissociation-based methods have some intrinsic limitations. For instance, the retina contains ~120 million photoreceptors, the vast majority of which are rods, and only 1–1.5 million RGCs. This numerical disparity means that the necessary methods used to enrich RGCs for single-cell sequencing could introduce sampling bias toward or against certain cell types. Moreover, the numerical dominance of midget RGCs could result in the undersampling of low-density RGC types, particularly those whose transcriptomic signatures do not deviate markedly from those of other clusters. Finally, while foveal and peripheral samples were processed separately in prior studies (e.g., Peng et al. 2019), the aggregation of samples from different peripheral regions precludes the detection of topographic anisotropies in cell type distribution. Molecular classification methods that preserve tissue context, such as spatial transcriptomics or highly multiplexed immunohistochemistry, would allow for mosaic analysis to confirm the correspondence between molecular clusters and RGC types, thereby reducing classification errors such as over- or underclustering (Nimkar et al. 2025) (Figure 2b). Spatial methods would also reveal whether the cell bodies of different RGC types are restricted to the ganglion cell layer (GCL) or are partially or completely displaced to the inner nuclear layer. Perhaps most importantly, nondestructive spatial methods provide a means for direct spatial registration of molecular and functional data (e.g., from calcium imaging) for high-throughput cell classification.

Morphological Diversity of Retinal Ganglion Cells

The diverse morphologies of primate RGCs were first revealed in the early Golgi studies of Cajal, which detailed cells differing in soma size, axon diameter, and, most strikingly, dendritic morphology. Since then, our appreciation of morphological diversity has expanded substantially through the use of retrograde labeling with fluorescent tracers (Dacey et al. 2003, Rodieck & Watanabe 1993), particle-mediated dye or gene transfer (i.e., biolistics; Masri et al. 2019), and intracellular tracer injections (Chandra et al. 2017; Lee et al. 2016, 2024; Wang et al. 2023; Yamada et al. 2005) (Figure 2c).

Macaque RGCs have been classified primarily on the basis of their distinct dendritic morphologies. The current nomenclature describes cells based on four main characteristics: the depth of dendritic stratification in the IPL (inner or outer, mono- or bistratified), the dendritic arbor diameter (small, large, or “giant”), the dendritic structure (smooth, thorny, recursive, or sparse), and the axial extent of dendritic stratification within the IPL (narrow or broad) (Figure 2c). The depth of dendritic stratification in the IPL is particularly informative for inferring basic physiological properties (ON, OFF, or ON-OFF) and identifying potential presynaptic circuit partners.

The most comprehensive morphological classification of macaque RGCs (Figure 1b) was generated by retrograde labeling with fluorescent dextran conjugates from three major recipient regions: the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN), SC, and pretectal nuclei (which likely included the pregeniculate nucleus, nucleus of the optic tract–dorsal terminal nucleus, and pretectal olivary nucleus) (Kim et al. 2022). This survey described 17 morphologically distinct RGC types based on the classification parameters outlined above and showed preliminary evidence that each forms an orderly mosaic, reinforcing their identity as discrete types (Kim et al. 2022). One of these types, the recursive monostratified RGC, consists of three overlapping mosaics that are assumed to be discrete types, bringing the total number of morphological types in this survey to 19 (Kim et al. 2022; see Section 7.1.1). A similar number of morphological types was identified in a survey of the marmoset retina (Masri et al. 2019).

Since primate RGCs target an estimated 21 brain regions (Santana et al. 2023), backlabeling from the LGN, SC, and pretectum alone may not be sufficient to capture all types. Indeed, additional morphologically distinct RGCs that could account for at least some of the remaining ~3% of RGCs have been described. For example, the so-called tufted RGCs (Lee et al. 2024, Yamada et al. 2005), the stratum (S) 3-stratifying narrow thorny RGCs (Yamada et al. 2005), and the outer recursive monostratified RGCs (Percival et al. 2013) are examples of cell types that were not described in the most recent morphological survey (Kim et al. 2022).

Although morphological analyses have greatly advanced understanding of RGC diversity, these metrics alone may not be sufficient to unequivocally classify all cell types. Combining molecular markers with single-cell fills would enable more robust classification (Figure 2c). This has been achieved, for example, by immunostaining for specific transcription factors or other protein markers that identify specific RGC types, then filling those immunolabeled cells with fluorescent tracers to reveal their complete morphology (Chandra et al. 2017, Lee et al. 2016, Nasir-Ahmad et al. 2021). However, with a few exceptions, such studies have used markers that label multiple RGC types, making it difficult to determine the precise correspondence between a given morphological type and its transcriptomic cluster identity. Future work using RGC subtype selective markers (Liu et al. 2023, Wang et al. 2023), or cell type–targeted adeno-associated viruses (AAVs), will be critical to address this knowledge gap.

Functional Diversity of Retinal Ganglion Cells

The functional diversity of the primate RGC types has been studied using a variety of in vivo and ex vivo experimental approaches spanning single-cell to multi-unit recordings (Figure 2d). Large-scale multielectrode array (MEA) recordings have proven particularly valuable for classifying wide-field primate RGC types since they enable simultaneous recording of visually evoked activity from hundreds of RGCs (Kling et al. 2024, Litke et al. 2004, Rhoades et al. 2019, Shah et al. 2022). A recent study using white noise stimuli revealed at least 32 potential macaque RGC types, each with distinct spatial, temporal, and chromatic response characteristics (Kling et al. 2024). Types were distinguished by their characteristic spike-triggered average stimulus ensemble (the average visual stimulus in the frames immediately preceding each recorded spike), electrical images (spatiotemporal maps of the extracellular voltage signal from a given cell), and intrinsic properties (interspike intervals). Interestingly, some cell types displayed unexpected nonclassical receptive field properties, including patchy substructure. Moreover, more types than expected showed prominent S-cone inputs, suggesting the presence of novel chromatic channels. Future studies examining the molecular and morphological properties of these cell types will be important to corroborate the classification.

Although approaches such as MEA are high throughput, they are technically challenging in the macula, where the GC layer and overlying axon bundles are thick and RGCs are laterally offset from the photoreceptors that drive them. MEA recordings may also bias against the detection of cell types with small somas and low current densities. The use of alternative functional methods, such as calcium or voltage imaging in the ex vivo retina or the living eye (Godat et al. 2024, McGregor et al. 2018, Wang et al. 2023), could overcome some of these biases, but such methods are not without their own unique challenges and limitations (Euler et al. 2019, Patterson et al. 2025).

An important consideration for ongoing studies will be ensuring that functional data acquired with different recording modalities and in different laboratories can be readily compared. Work in primates and other species suggests that even fundamental properties such as ON versus OFF response polarity may show strong dependency on stimulus conditions such as background light levels (Geffen et al. 2007, Tikidji-Hamburyan et al. 2015). Other biological and experimental factors can also influence RGC response properties [e.g., sex, eccentricity, mode of euthanasia (Shah et al. 2022)]. Thus, it will be crucial for future studies to align functional data with orthogonal methods for cell classification, such as molecular and/or morphological profiling. In the next section, we describe some approaches that could serve to achieve this goal.

Integrative Approaches for Retinal Ganglion Cell Classification

As discussed above, obtaining a robust census of primate RGC types requires integrative approaches that can reliably link transcriptomic identity, morphology, and physiology. One promising strategy to achieve this is using single-cell transcriptomic atlases to identify type-specific molecular markers. These markers can serve as an anchor to selectively target transcriptomically defined clusters for morphological and functional characterization. Wang et al. (2023) recently validated such a workflow for the identification of ON-DSGCs in primates (see Section 7.1.1 and Figure 3). A critical aspect of the approach was the use of population-level calcium imaging in conjunction with post hoc immunostaining to identify specific RGC types. This strategy allowed a low-density RGC type to be recovered with sufficient sample size while enabling direct spatial alignment between functional and molecular data. A similar approach has been applied to register functional data from MEA recordings with specific RGC types (Li et al. 2015). Cell type–specific molecular markers could also be applied to retinas after retrograde labeling from different retinorecipient regions to map the projection targets of different RGC types.

An alternative approach is the use of multimodal single-cell methods such as Patch-seq (Cadwell et al. 2016, Fuzik et al. 2016), which integrate electrophysiological recordings, morphology recovery, and transcriptomics within individual cells. While a variant of this approach has been used successfully in the mouse retina (Goetz et al. 2022, Huang et al. 2022), its inherently low throughput, combined with the sparsity of many primate RGC types, makes it a challenging method for cataloging rare cell populations. A recent study used antibodies raised against the extracellular epitope of melanopsin to label ipRGCs for targeted functional recordings in the ex vivo macaque retina (Liu et al. 2023). A similar approach, using antibody markers informed by the single-cell transcriptome, could similarly be used to target other wide-field RGC types for electrophysiological recordings. Moreover, transcriptomic datasets could be utilized to design AAV enhancers to target and manipulate specific RGC types, an approach that has been successfully applied to other parts of the primate brain (Li et al. 2025, Mich et al. 2021).

Another crucial element to advance understanding of the functions of low-density RGC types is the use of connectomic circuit mapping (for a review, see Kim & Dacey 2026) (Figure 2). Recent methodological advances in electron microscopy have made it possible to generate high-resolution volumetric reconstructions of human and nonhuman primate retinas, yielding unprecedented insights into RGC circuit connectivity (Kim et al. 2022, 2024; Patterson et al. 2020, 2022, 2024; Wool et al. 2018) (Figure 2e). Application of these methods after large-scale functional recordings offers a powerful strategy to link physiological response properties to underlying synaptic connectivity (Aji et al. 2023).

DIFFERENCES IN RETINAL GANGLION CELL TYPES IN THE FOVEA AND PERIPHERAL RETINA

Although the transcriptomic catalog of cell types in the fovea matches that of cell types in the peripheral retina for photoreceptors, horizontal cells, and bipolar cells, some amacrine cell and low-density RGC types appear to be absent or underrepresented in the fovea (Peng et al. 2019). This finding seemingly aligns with a recent connectomic study, which shows that the output of human foveal cones (~1 degree eccentricity) is routed primarily, although not exclusively, into midget RGCs, parasol RGCs, and small bistratified RGCs (~92% of all output synapses from a single cone; Kim et al. 2025). These findings challenge the long-held assumption that every foveal cone provides input to all RGC pathways and suggest that some low-density RGCs are involved primarily in peripheral visual functions that do not rely on the high foveal cone density. In line with this idea, the proportion of wide-field RGCs in the marmoset retina increases with eccentricity, while the proportion of midget RGCs declines (Hahn et al. 2023, Masri et al. 2019). Similarly, the proportion of human midget RGCs declines from ~95% in the fovea to ~50% in the periphery (Dacey 1993), although estimates from transcriptomic studies suggest a higher proportion of midget RGCs in the periphery of the macaque and human retinas compared to the marmoset retina (Hahn et al. 2023). Future studies utilizing cell type–specific markers are required to more precisely estimate the densities of distinct wide-field RGC populations in diverse species and retinal regions.

Beyond these differences in cell type inventory, the functional and molecular properties of a given RGC type can also vary between the foveal and peripheral retina. For example, electrophysiological studies have demonstrated that foveal ON midget RGCs receive markedly less inhibitory input than their peripheral counterparts (Crook et al. 2011, Sinha et al. 2017). This functional finding was further corroborated by reduced expression of inhibitory neurotransmitter receptor subunits in foveal RGCs relative to RGCs in the periphery (Crook et al. 2011, Peng et al. 2019, Sinha et al. 2017).

Understanding regional variations in RGC distribution and function will be critical for interpreting psychophysical experiments and building improved models of foveal and peripheral visual processing. Bridging the gap between molecular findings and visual function will require a multifaceted approach, combining dense connectomic reconstruction, identification of robust cell type–specific markers, and improved tools for comparing the functional properties of foveal and peripheral RGCs.

DIVERSITY OF WIDE-FIELD PRIMATE RETINAL GANGLION CELL TYPES

In this section, we describe in more detail the known morphologies, connectivity, central projections, transcriptomic clusters, and functions of the various low-density primate RGC types (Figure 1). We consider these populations in light of a widely held view that complex feature selectivity in RGCs has been evolutionarily reduced in primates due to the expansion of cortical processing (discussed in Gollisch & Meister 2010). Counter to this view are recent findings detailing the conservation of primate RGCs that encode complex features such as direction-selective and object motion–sensitive GCs.

We begin by discussing the cell types that are most well-characterized, and then we consider those whose functions are less clear. We conclude with other feature encoders that are presumed to be present based on recordings from LGN neurons but are not yet firmly established at the retinal level.

Recursive Retinal Ganglion Cells: Direction-Selective Ganglion Cells

DSGCs are arguably the archetypal example of an RGC that exhibits complex receptive field properties. These cells characteristically respond strongly to edges moving across their receptive field in the cell’s preferred direction but weakly to the same edges moving in the opposite or null direction (for reviews, see Mauss et al. 2017, Vaney et al. 2012). Both ON and ON-OFF DSGCs have been described in lower vertebrates, with the directional tuning in both types arising primarily from directionally asymmetric inhibition provided by starburst amacrine cells (SACs). Although DSGCs were first discovered in rabbits in the 1960s (Barlow & Levick 1965, Barlow et al. 1964), definitive proof of their presence in the primate retina took almost another 60 years. Until then, there had been debate as to whether primate DSGCs were present at such low density that they had simply evaded detection, or whether their function had been superseded by cortical processing. The conservation of SACs in primates, including humans, made the former hypothesis seem more likely (Rodieck 1989, Rodieck & Marshak 1992). As detailed below, the discovery of a common gene defect that disrupts SAC function in mice and causes nystagmus in both mice and humans made it highly likely that DSGCs were present in the human retina.

Based on their costratification with SACs, recursive RGCs, which are characterized by dendritic segments that curve back toward the soma, had been proposed as likely candidate DSGCs in the macaque retina (Dacey 2004). In Sections 7.1.1 and 7.1.2, we discuss a series of breakthrough studies that identify the recursive RGC types as DSGCs in primates.

Recursive monostratified retinal ganglion cells: ON-type direction-selective ganglion cells.

In lower vertebrates, ON-DSGCs provide the afferent directional signals for the optokinetic reflex, which works in concert with the vestibular and oculomotor systems to stabilize the retinal image during head or body movements (Oyster et al. 1980, Simpson et al. 1979). In nonprimate species, there are three subtypes of ON-DSGCs that each “tile” the retina in a regular mosaic and respond to motion along preferred axes that align with the planes of the semicircular canals (Simpson et al. 1979, but see Sabbah et al. 2017, Tiriac et al. 2022). ON-DSGCs project to brain regions involved in generating compensatory eye and head movements, specifically the dorsal, lateral, and medial terminal nuclei and the nucleus of the optic tract.

An early clue that ON-DSGCs might be conserved in primates came from evidence of a sparse population of RGCs that were retrolabeled from the macaque nucleus of the optic tract–dorsal terminal nucleus (Telkes et al. 2000). A later, groundbreaking study by Yonehara et al. (2016) showed that mutations in FRMD7—the gene most commonly associated with congenital horizontal nystagmus in humans—disrupted both the horizontal optokinetic reflex and horizontal directional tuning in DSGCs in Frmd7 mutant mice. Importantly, FRMD7 was localized to SACs in both the mouse and primate retinas, suggesting conserved retinal circuitry underlying the phenotype. The link between human gaze stabilization disorders and the ON-DSGC circuit was further bolstered by evidence that the nystagmus that accompanies some forms of congenital stationary night blindness arises from aberrant oscillatory activity in ON-DSGCs (Kamermans et al. 2023, Winkelman et al. 2019). Collectively, these findings suggest a retinal locus for some forms of nystagmus (Kamermans et al. 2023), challenging the prevailing view that this condition arises only from altered signaling in higher brain regions (e.g., brainstem, cerebellum, vestibular system). These clinical findings reinvigorated the search for primate ON-DSGCs.

The first breakthrough came from an elegant serial block-face electron microscopy study, which identified the recursive monostratified RGC as a candidate ON-DSGC in the macaque retina (Patterson et al. 2022). Reconstructed from the fovea, these cells exhibited the structural and synaptic hallmarks of ON-DSGCs, including the expected asymmetric wiring with SACs (Figure 3e). Shortly thereafter, Wang et al. (2023) used a multimodal approach to link recursive monostratified RGCs to a specific transcriptomic cluster and to establish that these cells were functionally direction selective (Figure 3a–d). The approach leveraged molecular clues from an existing single-cell transcriptomic dataset (Peng et al. 2019)—specifically the expression of inhibitory neurotransmitter receptor subunits found in ON-DSGCs in other species—to narrow down a candidate ON-DSGC to a specific transcriptomic cluster, peripheral RGC 10 (pRGC10), and to identify the transcription factor basonuclin 2 (BNC2) as a marker of that cluster. Filling BNC2-positive RGCs with fluorescent tracers revealed a dendritic morphology consistent with that of recursive monostratified RGCs, as well as cofasciculation with ON-SAC dendrites. Using two-photon calcium imaging, the authors confirmed that the BNC2-positive RGCs had ON-type light responses and exhibited direction selectivity that was abolished by the application of gabazine, a γ-aminobutyric acid type A (GABAA) receptor antagonist that blocks inhibitory input from amacrine cells, including SACs (Wang et al. 2023) (Figure 3d). The effect of the GABAA receptor antagonist on primate ON-DSGCs is consistent with results from every other species that has been tested, supporting the notion that the detailed retinal circuitry for direction selectivity is conserved in primates.

Findings from comparative transcriptomics further support the conservation of ON-DSGCs in primates, showing that macaque and mouse ON-DSGCs map to the same orthotype and that an orthologous cell type is also present in humans (Hahn et al. 2023, Wang et al. 2023) (Figure 3a). Moreover, BNC2 appears to be a conserved marker of ON-DSGCs across different mammalian species (Hahn et al. 2023), suggesting a possible role for this transcription factor in cell fate specification (Shekhar et al. 2022). While functional confirmation is pending, transcriptomic evidence also suggests that primates, like other mammals, possess three ON-DSGC subtypes. Specifically, the macaque single-cell transcriptome points to three putative ON-DSGC subclusters (Figure 3a), only one of which expresses FSTL4 (Wang et al. 2023), a marker of upward motion–preferring cells in other species (Yonehara et al. 2008, 2009). Furthermore, the estimated coverage factor of recursive monostratified RGCs is ~3.5 (Kim et al. 2022, Wang et al. 2023), suggesting the presence of three subtypes that form distinct mosaics. Further studies are needed to determine whether these transcriptomic subclusters separately tile the retina, as expected for distinct RGC types, and whether they encode different preferred directions.

Together, these studies establish the presence of RGC types in primates that encode complex visual features as they do in other vertebrates. While ON-DSGCs comprise ~2.5% of macaque RGCs, they make up ~6% of all mouse RGCs (Sabbah et al. 2017). Such differences illustrate how the proportions of orthologous cell types can differ across species, presumably to adapt to specific behavioral needs, ecological niches, and higher-level visual processing.

Recursive bistratified retinal ganglion cells: ON-OFF-type direction-selective ganglion cells.

In lower mammals, ON-OFF DSGCs can be divided into four subtypes that respond preferentially to motion along the four cardinal axes of the visual field (i.e., up, down, anterior, posterior) (Vaney et al. 2012). These cells have been proposed to encode either local object motion (Cafaro et al. 2020, Chiao & Masland 2003, Ölveczky et al. 2003) or optic flow (Sabbah et al. 2017).

Recursive bistratified RGCs were recently identified as ON-OFF DSGCs in the macaque retina (Kim et al. 2022). These cells, which project to the LGN and SC, have recursively branching, bistratified dendrites that costratify near the dendrites of ON- and OFF-SACs (Kim et al. 2022, Moritoh et al. 2013). They exhibit ON-OFF physiology and directional tuning and are gap junction coupled to a polyaxonal wide-field amacrine cell type (i.e., A1 amacrine cell) that is itself direction selective. Whether directional tuning in macaque ON-OFF DSGCs arises from these coupled amacrine cells or from asymmetric SAC-mediated inhibition remains unknown. However, primate ON-SACs respond more strongly during centrifugal versus centripetal motion relative to the soma and thus exhibit the directional tuning that confers direction selectivity to RGCs in other species (Euler et al. 2002, Kim et al. 2022). While connectomic tracing of the synaptic outputs of foveal SACs in the macaque retina showed connections with ON-DSGCs, no connections to a candidate ON-OFF DSGC were seen (Patterson et al. 2022); however, a similar analysis is yet to be performed in the peripheral retina. Indeed, the ratio of OFF-SACs to ON-SACs is markedly lower in the macaque fovea compared to the peripheral retina, suggesting eccentricity-dependent differences in the ON-OFF direction-selective circuit (Mariani & Hersh 1988, Rodieck & Marshak 1992).

In contrast to other species, macaque ON-OFF DSGCs appear to form a single mosaic, and functional evidence suggests that they encode only one preferred direction. Unlike for the ON-DSGCs, there is no clear transcriptomic cluster in the marmoset, monkey, or human retina that maps to the same orthotype as ON-OFF DSGCs in other species. Further work is therefore needed to understand whether primate ON-OFF DSGCs are orthologs of the ON-OFF DSGCs that have been extensively studied in other vertebrates.

Giant Sparse Retinal Ganglion Cells: Intrinsically Photosensitive Retinal Ganglion Cells

As in other vertebrates, ipRGCs expressing the photopigment melanopsin (encoded by the gene Opn4) have been described in several primate species, including humans (Dacey et al. 2005, Hannibal et al. 2004, Jusuf et al. 2007, Liao et al. 2016, Nasir-Ahmad et al. 2019, Provencio et al. 2000). Morphologically, ipRGCs have large somas and very-wide-field and sparsely branched dendrites that are typically monostratified in the IPL. There are at least two distinct subtypes of melanopsin-expressing ipRGCs in primates (M1 and M2), which together make up 0.2–0.5% of all RGCs (Dacey et al. 2005, Liao et al. 2016). M1 cells have somata in the GCL and the inner nuclear layer and dendrites that stratify in S1 of the IPL, whereas M2 cell somata are located in the GCL with dendrites that stratify in S5. Both cell types have intraretinal axon collaterals that extend into the IPL and are the likely conduit for intraretinal signaling to dopaminergic amacrine cells (Joo et al. 2013). ipRGCs project to brain areas involved in circadian entrainment (suprachiasmatic nucleus), pupillary constriction (pretectal olivary nucleus), and image formation (LGN), as well as other regions (Do 2019, Gamlin et al. 2007, Liao et al. 2016).

The first functional recordings of primate ipRGCs came from the macaque retina after backlabeling from the pretectal olivary nucleus and LGN (Dacey et al. 2005). The melanopsin-mediated intrinsic photosensitivity of these cells was revealed by pharmacological blockade of rod and cone input. Under these conditions, spike rates increased linearly over a 3–4 log unit intensity range, consistent with a role for these cells in irradiance encoding. A later study found that each individual M1 cell signals across different light intensity ranges such that the population collectively encodes a dynamic range of ~5 log units (Liu et al. 2023). In addition to their intrinsic photosensitivity, M1 and M2 cells receive ON pathway input from rods and cones. Under photopic conditions, ipRGCs exhibit a spatially overlapping, spectrally opponent receptive field with an antagonistic S-OFF/LM-ON response profile (Dacey et al. 2005). Unlike many RGC types, ipRGCs lack strong inhibitory receptive field surrounds.

The functional properties of primate ipRGCs arise from their unique circuit connectivity. Both M1 and M2 cells exhibit ON-type physiology, which was initially surprising for M1 cells given their dendritic stratification in the OFF sublamina of the IPL. This unexpected response polarity was later explained by the presence of en passant inputs in the outer plexiform layer from the axons of an ON-type cone bipolar cell (Grünert et al. 2011). The S-OFF responses in M1 cells likely arise from an amacrine cell that itself receives near-exclusive S-cone input from S-ON bipolar cells (Patterson et al. 2020).

Unlike most other low-density RGC types, clusters corresponding to ipRGC types can be readily identified in primate single-cell transcriptomic datasets based on selective OPN4 expression (Hahn et al. 2023, Peng et al. 2019). The macaque peripheral RGC cluster, pRGC18, and human cluster RGC12 have the highest OPN4 levels and likely correspond to the M1 type. Two other OPN4-expressing RGC clusters are evident in the macaque, but their morphological correlates have not been established. It is possible that additional ipRGCs that express lower levels of OPN4 are present in primates as in the mouse (Sonoda et al. 2018).

Broad Thorny Retinal Ganglion Cells: Object Motion–Sensitive Ganglion Cells

Broad thorny RGCs comprise ~1.3% of the total RGC population (Dacey 2004, Kim et al. 2022). They have distinctive morphology, with two or three primary dendrites that give rise to a dense meshwork of secondary dendrites and branchlets from which many spine-like structures (thorns) emanate (Kim et al. 2022, Masri et al. 2019, Puller et al. 2015). While the dendrites of most primate RGC types are axially restricted to narrow bands within the IPL, broad thorny RGCs are so named because their dendrites stratify within a broad region of the IPL, spanning between the ON- and OFF-SAC dendrites (Dacey 2004, Yamada et al. 2005). Retrograde labeling studies indicate that broad thorny RGCs project to the LGN, SC, and pretectum (Dacey et al. 2003, Kim et al. 2022, Rodieck & Watanabe 1993).

Consistent with their dendritic stratification in the middle of the IPL, broad thorny RGCs exhibit ON-OFF responses to light stimuli (Bordt et al. 2021, Puller et al. 2015). Pharmacological experiments confirm that the excitatory and inhibitory inputs to these cells arise from both the ON and OFF pathways (Bordt et al. 2021). Broad thorny RGCs show strong receptive field surround suppression that is more pronounced for ON than for OFF stimuli, which is mediated by presynaptic inhibition of excitatory inputs as well as feedforward postsynaptic inhibition (Puller et al. 2015). Serial block-face electron microscopy revealed that broad thorny RGCs receive most of their synaptic inputs from amacrine cells with only ~2% of inputs from ON and OFF bipolar cells (Bordt et al. 2021), a result that is perhaps unexpected given the sizeable light-evoked excitatory input currents to these cells (Bordt et al. 2021).

Interestingly, broad thorny RGCs show a strong preference for local motion versus global motion stimuli. These cells are tuned to motion contrast, firing robustly when motion trajectories are noncoherent between the receptive field center and surround (Appleby et al. 2026, Puller et al. 2015). These properties are similar to those of object motion–sensitive GCs in other species (Jacoby & Schwartz 2017, Ölveczky et al. 2003), as well as neurons in the primate SC (Davidson & Bender 1991). Object motion–sensitive neurons may be important for distinguishing objects moving in the environment from global motion on the retina that results from body, head, or eye movements. Given the known projection to the SC, these cells may be important for selecting visual targets for orienting eye movements such as saccades or visual pursuit (Puller et al. 2015).

Narrow Thorny Retinal Ganglion Cells

In addition to the broad thorny RGC, three different types of narrow thorny RGCs have been identified in primates (Dacey et al. 2003; Yamada et al. 2005). In contrast to broad thorny RGCs, the dendrites of narrow thorny RGCs occupy a narrower depth within the IPL and their branches are less dense (Kim et al. 2022, Masri et al. 2019, Moritoh et al. 2013). Inner narrow thorny RGCs stratify just proximal to ON-SACs, whereas outer narrow thorny RGCs costratify with OFF-SAC dendrites (Ivanova et al. 2010, Kim et al. 2022, Masri et al. 2019, Moritoh et al. 2013, Percival et al. 2014). The inner type receives excitatory input from DB6 bipolar cells, whereas the circuit connectivity of the outer type remains unclear. Together, the inner and outer narrow thorny RGCs comprise ~3% of the total RGC population (Kim et al. 2022). A third narrow thorny RGC, which stratifies in the middle (S3) of the IPL, has been described in the macaque (Percival et al. 2013, Yamada et al. 2005); the narrow ON-OFF cells of the marmoset share some morphological features (Percival et al. 2013). The stratification profile and tortuous, thorny dendritic architecture of the S3 narrow thorny RGCs are reminiscent of the well-characterized local edge detector RGCs in the rabbit (Amthor et al. 1989) and the “high-definition” family of RGCs described in the mouse, but further work is needed to investigate cross-species correspondence.

Cells with narrow thorny morphology have been retrogradely labeled from the LGN, SC, and pretectum in the macaque (Dacey et al. 2003, Kim et al. 2022, Rodieck & Watanabe 1993). In the marmoset, retrograde tracing revealed that ON narrow thorny RGCs project to koniocellular layer K1 of the LGN, a region that contains cells that exhibit high contrast and temporal sensitivity (Percival et al. 2014). Further electrophysiological studies are needed to determine the precise role of narrow thorny RGC types in visual function.

Smooth Monostratified Retinal Ganglion Cells

Smooth monostratified RGCs (Dacey 2004) comprise a paramorphic pair of ON and OFF types that each tile the retina with minimal overlap (Rhoades et al. 2019) and together constitute ~2.6% of all macaque RGCs (Kim et al. 2022). These cells have been backlabeled from both the LGN and SC and likely send axon collaterals to both regions (Crook et al. 2008). The stratification of smooth monostratified RGCs matches that of the corresponding ON and OFF parasol RGCs, but their receptive fields and dendritic arbors are ~2–3 times larger (Crook et al. 2008, Rhoades et al. 2019). Smooth monostratified RGCs are characterized anatomically by their smooth (spineless) and sparse radiate dendrites that show few dendritic crossings and have prominent swellings at dendritic branch points. The relatively large somata of these cells have enabled targeted single-cell electrophysiological recordings of this low-density RGC type (Crook et al. 2008, Rhoades et al. 2019).

Initial recordings of smooth monostratified RGCs revealed achromatic receptive fields with high temporal and contrast sensitivity and nonlinear frequency-doubled responses (Crook et al. 2008, Petrusca et al. 2007). Subsequently, studies utilizing high-density multielectrode recordings identified an unusual substructure of the receptive field, characterized by localized high-sensitivity hotspots within regions of lower sensitivity (Rhoades et al. 2019). These hotspots were large enough to encompass inputs from several primate bipolar cells, indicating a nonuniform integration of the upstream bipolar cell arrays. Consistent with these physiological findings, connectomic reconstructions of ON-type smooth monostratified RGCs revealed anatomical hotspots comprised of regions of increased excitatory bipolar cell input surrounded by dendritic segments with denser inhibitory amacrine cell input (Patterson et al. 2024). Focal light stimulation in functional hotspots produced distinct spike signatures, suggesting that postsynaptic dendritic spikes might serve to correlate the already nonlinear inputs from groups of overlying bipolar cells (Rhoades et al. 2019). Rhoades et al. (2019) suggest, similar to a previous notion regarding dendritic spikes in ON-OFF DSGCs (Oesch et al. 2005), that this receptive field structure might enable motion sensitivity over a spatial scale smaller than the whole receptive field since the smaller subunits are sufficient to produce spiking. Further work will be required to determine whether this motion hyperacuity within the smooth monostratified RGC receptive field is essential for a specific aspect of visual function.

Large Bistratified Retinal Ganglion Cells

Large bistratified RGCs account for ~1.5% of the total macaque RGC population. While their stratification patterns in S2 and S4 mirror those of small bistratified RGCs, they are distinguished by significantly larger and more sparsely branched dendritic arbors (Dacey et al. 2003, Kim et al. 2022). Large bistratified RGCs exhibit S-ON center responses and LM-ON-OFF surround responses to cone isolating stimuli (Dacey et al. 2003, Kim et al. 2024). In contrast to small bistratified RGCs, whose dominant bipolar input is from blue cone bipolar cells, large bistratified RGCs in the human fovea receive input from a variety of bipolar types including the blue cone bipolar, ON and OFF midget bipolar, and other diffuse bipolar cell types (Kim et al. 2024). Given the concomitant input from blue cone bipolar cells and L and M cone-selective foveal midget bipolar cells, large bistratified RGCs may play a role in noncardinal chromatic signaling (Godat et al. 2024), although further functional work is needed to assess this hypothesis.

Sparse Monostratified Retinal Ganglion Cells

Inner and outer sparse monostratified RGCs (referred to in some studies as large sparse GCs) are a paramorphic pair of cells that together constitute ~1.8% of the total RGC population. These wide-field cells have been identified in the macaque retina (Kim et al. 2022; Masri et al. 2019; Percival et al. 2011, 2013; Yamada et al. 2005), marmoset retina (Kim et al. 2022; Masri et al. 2019; Percival et al. 2011, 2013; Szmajda et al. 2008; Yamada et al. 2005), and human retina (Nasir-Ahmad et al. 2021, Peterson & Dacey 1999) and have sparsely branched dendrites that lack spiny protrusions (Dacey 2004). Sparse monostratified RGCs are among the types that express the transcription factor SATB2 in the macaque and human retinas (Dhande et al. 2019, Nasir-Ahmad et al. 2021), but this expression pattern is not conserved in the marmoset retina. An RGC with similar dendritic morphology, but with bistratified dendrites, has also been described in macaques and humans (Nasir-Ahmad et al. 2021, Peterson & Dacey 2000) but has not been classified separately. Preliminary functional recordings of the ON type suggest an LM-ON/S-OFF spectrally opponent response profile (Dacey 2004).

OTHER PREDICTED PRIMATE FEATURE DETECTOR RETINAL GANGLION CELLS

We conclude by discussing feature detector RGCs found in other mammals that have not yet been directly identified in the primate retina, or for which evidence is limited, but that are likely to exist given evidence from LGN recordings.

Orientation-Selective Retinal Ganglion Cells

Orientation-selective (OS) neurons respond maximally to an elongated visual stimulus, such as a bar or an edge, oriented along their preferred axis. Certain RGC types in nonprimates exhibit strong orientation tuning aligned to the cardinal axes (Baden et al. 2016; Levick 1967; Nath & Schwartz 2016, 2017; Venkataramani & Taylor 2010, 2016), but such cells have not been identified in primates. However, a sparse subset of koniocellular neurons in the marmoset LGN show strong orientation tuning that is best explained by direct RGC input (Cheong et al. 2013). The strength of the orientation tuning of these LGN cells is markedly stronger than the weak orientation biases in macaque magno- and parvocellular neurons in the LGN (Smith et al. 1990) and retina (Passaglia et al. 2002). Notably, some patients with V1 lesions retain the ability to discriminate orientation in the absence of conscious awareness (Weiskrantz et al. 1995), a form of blindsight that may be explained by direct projections from koniocellular neurons to the extrastriate visual areas (Schmid et al. 2010).

What might be the functional role of subcortical OS signals in conscious or unconscious visual perception? The sparsity of OS RGCs in the primate LGN suggests that they do not generate the strong orientation tuning seen in V1 (Cheong et al. 2013). Rather, OS RGCs may play a role in establishing cortical orientation columns early in development (Vidyasagar et al. 1996) or be important for providing visual feedback for postural, eye, or head position. Future work is necessary to determine whether OS RGCs are a conserved orthotype in primates and, if so, to determine the roles of these cells in primate visual perception and behavior.

Suppressed-by-Contrast Retinal Ganglion Cells

Originally described in the cat and rabbit retinas, suppressed-by-contrast RGCs are characterized by a high tonic firing rate that is suppressed by spatiotemporal modulation within their receptive field, regardless of contrast polarity (Levick 1967, Rodieck 1967). Similar cells have since been characterized in the mouse retina (Jacoby & Schwartz 2018, Tien et al. 2015) and rabbit retina (uniformity detector RGCs; Sivyer et al. 2010).

Cells with a suppressed-by-contrast response profile have been documented in the macaque retina and marmoset LGN. In the macaque retina, wide-field RGCs with high baseline activity that is suppressed by moving stimuli but not static flicker have been described (de Monasterio 1978, de Monasterio & Gouras 1975, Scobey et al. 1979). Similarly, in the marmoset LGN, suppressed-by-contrast RGCs exhibited band-pass spatial and temporal tuning with peak response suppression at ~1.9 cycles/degree and ~15 Hz, respectively (Tailby et al. 2007). The physiological role of suppressed-by-contrast RGCs remains unknown, but they have been proposed to play a role in contrast gain modulation, accommodation, and saccadic suppression (Jacoby & Schwartz 2018, Troy et al. 1989).

SUMMARY AND FUTURE ISSUES

The field of primate retinal neuroscience has entered an exciting era where modern tools for molecular profiling, circuit reconstruction, and high-throughput physiology promise to accelerate progress toward resolving the specific roles of wide-field RGC types. To leverage these technological advances, it will be crucial to develop a standardized classification scheme that incorporates robust molecular markers to enable integration of data across different modalities. Such frameworks are also essential for harmonizing findings from different laboratories and for aligning primate cell types with those from other species.
