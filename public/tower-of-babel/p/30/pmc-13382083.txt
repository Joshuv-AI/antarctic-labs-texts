Moving Upstream
Authors: Marco Giuseppe Del Buono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Heart failure (HF) represents one of the leading causes of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality worldwide, with acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF) accounting for millions of hospitalizations annually.1 Despite significant advances in guideline-directed medical therapy targeting neurohormonal pathways, rehospitalization rates remain unacceptably high and in-hospital mortality has changed little over the past 2 decades.1 A growing body of evidence implicates systemic and myocardial inflammation as central contributors to HF pathophysiology and progression. Elevated levels of circulating cytokines including interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α have been consistently associated with HF severity and adverse outcomes.2,3 Yet, despite this biological rationale, no anti-inflammatory therapy has achieved regulatory approval for HF. Trials targeting individual downstream mediators such as IL-1 or IL-6 have yielded mixed results, partly reflecting the redundancy and complexity of inflammatory signaling cascades.2 This has prompted renewed interest in upstream, more proximal targets capable of broadly attenuating innate immune activation without compromising host defense.

Cluster of differentiation 14 (CD14) occupies a critical proximal node within the innate immune signaling network. As a coreceptor for Toll-like receptor 4 on monocytes and macrophages, CD14 amplifies pattern recognition responses to damage-associated molecular patterns including those released by injured or stressed cardiomyocytes, thereby activating downstream cascades culminating in inflammasome priming and pro-inflammatory cytokine release.4 The appeal of CD14 as a therapeutic target lies in its strategic position upstream of multiple inflammatory pathways: blockade at this level may simultaneously suppress IL-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α without the redundancy limitations inherent to single-cytokine strategies. Moreover, CD14 blockade does not deplete monocytes or macrophages, thereby preserving broader immune surveillance. Preclinical data in murine ST-elevation myocardial infarction models demonstrated that anti-CD14 therapy reduced infarct size, fibrosis, and macrophage infiltration while preserving contractility.4 Epidemiological data reinforce this rationale: elevated circulating soluble CD14 is independently associated with incident HF and increased cardiovascular mortality in older adults.5 Once CD14 is activated, it undergoes proteolytic cleavage to release presepsin, a circulating fragment that serves as a quantifiable biomarker of CD14-pathway activation and may identify patients with the greatest degree of innate immune engagement.

In this context, Abbate et al.6 in this issue of the journal report the first-in-class application of atibuclimab (IC14), a function-blocking monoclonal antibody against CD14, in patients hospitalized with ADHF and reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). This 2-center, open-label, single-arm phase 1b pilot study enrolled 10 adults with LVEF <40% and systemic inflammation (C-reactive protein [CRP] >3 mg/L), who received a single intravenous infusion of IC14 at 20 mg/kg.

The primary outcome of safety was met. No treatment-related serious adverse events or deaths were recorded. Eleven adverse events occurred in 7 patients, all deemed expected given the severity of their underlying HF and hospitalization, and all assessed as unlikely or not related to the study drug. Vital signs remained stable during infusion.6 This safety profile is consistent with earlier IC14 experience in COVID-19 pneumonia and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.7,8 However, median alanine aminotransferase nearly doubled from 26 to 46 U/L, and aspartate aminotransferase rose from 28 to 45 U/L by day 10. Although these changes are clinically insignificant, in the context of a CD14-blocking antibody that modulates innate immune activity in hepatic Kupffer cells, prospective and granular hepatic monitoring should be considered for future studies.

Beyond safety, the study provides compelling pharmacodynamic and exploratory clinical data. CD14 receptor occupancy (RO) reached a median of 97% at day 10, confirming near-complete target engagement after a single infusion. This level of RO was maintained above 50% at day 29, demonstrating a pharmacodynamic effect that spans the critical early post-discharge window, a period of heightened vulnerability in patients with ADHF.

Biomarker data support mechanistic plausibility. CRP declined by a median of 46% at day 10 and 67% at day 29. B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) decreased by 48% at day 10. Presepsin, a direct marker of CD14-pathway activation, fell dramatically by 86% within 24 hours, providing mechanistic confirmation of target engagement at the pathway level. Ex vivo whole-blood lipopolysaccharide challenge assays corroborated these findings, demonstrating a sustained 94% to 97% reduction in IL-6 production at 48 hours and day 10, with additional suppression when exogenous IC14 was added in vitro at baseline, confirming a direct pharmacologic effect. LVEF improved significantly from 26% to 32% at day 10 (P = 0.008), and patient-reported outcomes improved markedly, with Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire overall score rising from 21 to 64 at day 29.

Important limitations deserve emphasis. The absence of a randomized control arm fundamentally limits causal inference. In ADHF, substantial improvements in symptoms, BNP, congestion scores, and even LVEF are routinely observed with standard-of-care diuresis and neurohormonal therapy in the days following admission. All enrolled patients received guideline-directed medical therapy, making it impossible to disentangle the contribution of IC14 from the expected natural trajectory of treated ADHF. The BNP reduction was statistically significant at day 10 (P = 0.038) but lost significance by day 29 (P = 0.13), and in the context of an uncontrolled trial, the BNP is expected to fall substantially with diuresis alone. Similarly, patient-reported outcomes are particularly susceptible to expectation and Hawthorne effects. By contrast, the objective biomarker changes (CRP, BNP, presepsin) are less susceptible to this bias and arguably provide the more reliable signal of biological activity. The sample size of 10 patients, although appropriate for a safety and pharmacodynamic pilot study, precludes any estimation of clinical efficacy. The narrow inclusion criteria (LVEF <40%, CRP >3 mg/L, required diuretic use) substantially limited eligibility, with only 24 of 306 screened patients ultimately qualifying. This enriched population may not reflect the broader, more heterogeneous ADHF population encountered in clinical practice. A meaningful real-world trial would need to define appropriate enrichment strategies balancing tractability with generalizability.

An important pharmacodynamic inconsistency warrants discussion. Presepsin, the direct downstream product of CD14 cleavage, fell dramatically at 24 hours and day 10, but returned close to baseline by day 29, even as CRP continued to decline. These biomarkers track distinct processes: presepsin reflects acute CD14 pathway activation, whereas CRP mirrors broader hepatic IL-6 signaling. Their divergence at day 29, coinciding with partial RO recovery, raises the question of whether the anti-inflammatory effects observed at day 29 are still attributable to residual CD14 blockade or reflect the natural resolution of the index ADHF episode. This dissociation is biologically informative and warrants prospective mechanistic investigation.

Several design considerations must be addressed. First, biomarker-guided patient selection will be critical. Prior COVID-19 experience with IC14 suggested that patients with elevated baseline presepsin derived disproportionate benefit, and the current findings confirm high presepsin levels in acute HF with reduced ejection fraction.9 Incorporating presepsin or soluble CD14 as enrichment criteria could enhance signal detection in phase 2. Second, repeat dosing strategies merit exploration. Given the pharmacodynamic waning observed by day 29, a maintenance infusion at 2 to 4 weeks post discharge may be necessary to cover the period of maximal residual risk. Third, the choice of primary endpoint will be pivotal. Trials of anti-inflammatory strategies in HF have historically struggled with endpoint selection; Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire–based patient-reported outcomes, as recently validated in HF trials, may offer an attractive and clinically meaningful primary endpoint.10

Finally, this phase 1b pilot trial contributes to an emerging body of evidence that supports exploring the integration of inflammation-targeting strategies alongside neurohormonal blockade, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibition, and diuretic optimization in the management of ADHF. Furthermore, the translational interest in CD14 blockade extends beyond acute decompensation. With ongoing early-phase trials investigating atibuclimab in other inflammation-driven conditions such as ST-elevation myocardial infarction (Pilot Study of IC14 [Atibuclimab], an Anti-CD14 Monoclonal Antibody, to Treat STEMI; NCT06678074) and arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (IC14 [Atibuclimab] in Arrhythmogenic Cardiomyopathy; NCT06275893), proximal CD14 inhibition represents a promising, albeit early-stage, therapeutic target for mitigating myocardial injury and pathological remodeling. The next critical step is to determine, through adequately powered randomized trials, whether these encouraging pharmacodynamic signals can translate into meaningful improvements in the outcomes that matter most to patients.
