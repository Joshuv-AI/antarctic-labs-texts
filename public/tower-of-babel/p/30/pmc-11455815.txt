Targeting Non-V600 Mutations in BRAF: A Single Institution Retrospective Analysis and Review of the Literature
Authors: Hirra A Chaudhary, Timothy L Cannon, Arthur Winer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveWhile successful treatment paradigms for BRAF V600 mutations have been developed, 10% of BRAF mutations are not at V600 and lack a standard treatment regimen. This study summarizes the current body of knowledge on the treatment of non-V600 mutations and reports a single institution experience.MethodsWe conducted a literature review to summarize relevant preclinical and clinical published data on the response of non-V600 mutations to targeted therapies. We performed a retrospective analysis of INOVA Schar Cancer patients registered in our Molecular Tumor Board database with non-V600 BRAF mutations who were recipients of targeted therapy and assessed their time to next treatment and best response.ResultsPublished preclinical and clinical data have demonstrated limiting results in the response of non-V600 mutated cancers to targeted therapies. Response rates were variable for the major classes of BRAF mutations including class II and class III mutations as well as, BRAF fusions. Data collected from our INOVA cohort offered promising results with one patient achieving partial remission and two patients achieving stable disease.ConclusionsThis article reflects the current understanding of targeted therapies in non-V600 mutations. Further large-scale studies separating BRAF mutations based on their mechanism of activation will expand our understanding.

Key Points

There are multiple clinical and preclinical studies depicting meaningful responses of nonV600 BRAF mutated cancers to targeted therapies, but overall results are limited.Reflection of our patient cohort at INOVA Schar noted three cases of promising results to targeted therapy with non-V600 BRAF mutations.Further large-scale reviews of studies separating BRAF aberrations based on their mechanism of activation will further our understanding of the effect of targeted therapies on these cancer types.

Introduction

The serine-threonine kinase BRAF plays a critical role in the MAPK pathway promoting cell growth and survival. BRAF oncogenic mutations result in constitutive activation of this pathway and have been implicated in multiple cancer types, including melanoma, colorectal cancer, and lung cancer. Of all solid tumors, 7–15% are found to have these mutations with a greater frequency in certain disease types such as melanoma. The most common BRAF variant is the V600E point mutation, which comprises 90% of all pathogenic BRAF mutations in cancer [1, 2]. While BRAF V600E-specific targeted monotherapies, such as vemurafenib, resulted in improved progression-free survival and overall survival in treating BRAF V600 mutated cancers, subsequent studies showed further improvement using combination therapy with the addition of a MEK inhibitor [3, 4]. Targeted therapies using combinations of BRAF V600 and MEK inhibitors have improved outcomes across tumor types, which has led to the US Food and Drug Administration pan-tumor approval for dabrafenib and trametinib in all BRAF V600E mutated solid tumors [5].

While non-V600 mutations make up 10% of all BRAF mutated cancers, they lack a standard targeted therapy paradigm. Current class I monomer selective BRAF inhibitors inhibit mutant BRAF V600E proteins, which signal as monomers instead of wild-type BRAF that signals as a dimer [6]. Non-V600 mutations are often subdivided into the remaining classes based on their mechanism of activation and are generally categorized as class II, class III, and fusions (Fig. 1). Class II mutations are constitutively active dimers that are RAS independent with intermediate to high kinase activity. These mutations are primarily found in the activating site or P-loop segment of BRAF, such as K601 and L597 or G464 and G469, respectively. Class III mutations have low-level kinase activity and require upstream RAS signaling for dimerization. These aberrations often coexist with other MAPK pathway or other pathway activating mutations and lead to feedback upregulation of additional cellular growth and survival pathways. Mutations are found in the P-loop, catalytic loop, or DFG motif, such as G466, N581, and D594 [7, 8].

Classes of BRAF mutations that can result in activation of the MAPK pathway and examples of common mutations in each class. Class I mutations are the most common mutation that result in highly activating monomers. Class II mutations dimerize and moderately increase kinase activity. Class III mutation are kinase dead, but often result in paradoxical activation of CRAF via a feedback loop. BRAF fusions are activating fusions that retain the kinase domain. BRAF inhibitors, MEK inhibitors, and ERK inhibitors are drugs that target within the MAPK pathway [12].

The third category of non-V600 BRAF alterations are fusions. Activating BRAF fusions are those that result in retention of the kinase domain and loss of the N-terminal CR1 auto-inhibitory domain. These changes lead to constitutively active dimers. Examples of this include a fusion between KIAA1549 and the BRAF gene, which results in duplication of a 2-MBP region that fuses the 5′ end of the KIAA1549 gene and the 3′ end of BRAF [7, 9, 10]. BRAF V600E mutations have shown to have positive effects on progression-free survival, especially in melanoma, but the prognosis of non-V600 mutated cancers is less clear [11]. Currently, there is a gap in clinical knowledge about the response of non-V600 mutations to targeted therapies. With an increase in genomic profiling of tumors with non-V600 BRAF mutations, it is important to integrate and expand on data that can substantiate the use of targeted therapies for these patient cases. In this paper, we endeavor to summarize current data on the effect of targeted therapies on non-V600 mutations and report results within our institutional database to help further understanding of what best practices might be for this patient population.

Review of Literature

Clinical trials assessing the response of BRAF non-V600 mutated tumors to targeted therapies remain in progress [13, 14]. However, there are published preclinical and clinical reports of tumor response to these treatments. A bibliographic search was conducted in PubMed to compile current relevant publications. Response data to targeted therapy regimens from multiple different cancer types were collected (Table 1). Studies assessing targeted therapies in non-V600 mutated patients were most prevalent in the metastatic melanoma population. These studies disproportionately involved V600 monotherapies, which may limit their utility. However, we also included studies that involved MEK inhibition alone or combination therapies of MEK and RAF inhibitors. The clinical results included objective response rate and clinical benefit rate.

Preclinical Data

Inhibition of tumor growth and apoptosis induction has been demonstrated in preclinical studies of BRAF non-V600 mutated cell lines with the use of RAF-specific and MEK-specific inhibitors. This has been demonstrated in cell lines with several different tumor types in both class II and class III mutations [15–17]. The susceptibility to MAPK pathway inhibition is attributed to the increased BRAF kinase activity in class II mutations and paradoxical CRAF activation in the “kinase dead” class III alterations.

When discussing RAF inhibition, it is important to understand the types of inhibitors. Many of the studies referred to in this paper utilize selective BRAF inhibitors, which are known to inhibit the mutant monomeric BRAF-V600 protein, with little to no activity against RAF dimers. Their specificity for the activated mutant BRAF-V600 monomer may lead to acquired resistance, and may explain in part the unpredictability in their ability to inhibit non-V600 BRAF mutants. More recently, pan-RAF inhibitors able to target wild-type RAF dimers have been developed and are starting to be used to target BRAF-mutant cancers and early data demonstrate activity against non-V600 mutations [18–20].

While tumor suppression has been described both with combinations of MEK and BRAF inhibition and with MEK inhibition alone, some studies noted more robust inhibition with the combination, which became the basis for future clinical trials [21, 22].

Clinical Data on Targeted Therapy

Much of the clinical data accumulated for treating the uncommon BRAF variants come from case series. As described in Table 1, several publications describe responses to combination therapy or MEK inhibition alone in Class II mutations [23–30]. There are very few reports that support the efficacy of currently available targeted treatment in class III mutations [25–30]. NCI Match is the largest relevant prospective study and noted only 1/32 responses in patients with BRAF non-V600 alterations (almost all were class II and III) [25]. Menzer et al. noted five responses to MEK inhibition in class 2 patients [27]. While activating BRAF fusions are less common, there is a growing body of case series suggesting high response rates. Notably, Hendifar et al. noted four out of five patients with pancreatic cancer with BRAF fusions had responses [24].

INOVA Patient Data on Targeted Therapy Use in Non-V600 Mutated Cancer

Data provided by the Molecular Tumor Board Registry in INOVA Schar Cancer Institute were collected and organized to understand the results of targeted therapy regimens on non-V600 BRAF cancers within this institution’s patient cohort. Patient data from the start of the Molecular Tumor Board Registry in 2016 to the data cut-off date of 17 May, 2022 found 47 patients with non-V600 BRAF mutations including class II, class III, fusions, and amplifications. The non-V600 BRAF mutations within this cohort were seen across cancer types. The three most common cancer types amongst these patients were colon adenocarcinoma, prostate acinar adenocarcinoma, and lung adenocarcinoma. Rare cancers were also represented including pediatric soft-tissue leiomyosarcoma, ovary endometrioid adenocarcinoma, and fallopian tube serous carcinoma. Class III mutations were most prevalent with 23 patients, ten patients with class II mutations, seven with fusions, and seven with amplifications.

After reviewing the clinical information of this cohort, it was determined that six of the 47 patients with non-V600 BRAF mutated cancers received a specific targeted therapy regimen. Two patients received second-line targeted therapy and four patients received third-line targeted therapy. For each patient, the type of targeted therapy and duration of treatment were further identified. The time to next treatment measurement was calculated in days from the start of the targeted therapy regimen to the end of therapy or the decision to withdraw from care (Table 2). Ultimately, the time to next treatment was shown to be variable per patient with one patient achieving a partial response, two achieving stable disease, and the rest ultimately progressing either because of a lack of response or having to stop therapy because of drug toxicity before an adequate trial could be given.

Notable Patient Cases

Case 1

The patient was diagnosed with germline BRCA2 mutation-associated metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma to the liver at age 51 years and was treated with FOLFIRINOX for nine cycles achieving a PR before transitioning to 5-FU and nanoliposomal irinotecan due to accumulating neuropathy. After an additional 6 months on chemotherapy, she elected to rotate to targeted therapy owing to significant chemotherapy-related fatigue. Because of the presence of a BRAF fusion in her primary tumor, she was placed on trametinib at the recommendation of our Molecular Tumor Board. Her course was complicated by a grade 2 facial rash attributed to trametinib 2 weeks into treatment. This necessitated an intermittent pause of therapy and a dose reduction. Otherwise, she tolerated therapy well, achieving a partial response with a reduction in the volume of multiple liver tumors (Fig. 2). She had progressive shrinkage of her cancer throughout her treatment with trametinib and it became difficult to appreciate any residual malignancy.

Computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis of Case 1 with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma before third-line therapy with trametinib and a follow-up scan 224 days into treatment. Infiltrative changes were less apparent surrounding the pancreatic head in the mesentery while on targeted therapy

She remained on therapy for over 16 months before progressing and moving to fourth-line gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel because of a recurrence in the right lobe of the liver. Ultimately, she was treated for 3.5 years from diagnosis to the time of her death, illustrating the benefit of systemic therapy in a patient harboring both BRCA2 germline and somatic BRAF fusion alterations.

Case 2

The patient was diagnosed with localized high-risk Gleason 4+4=8 prostate adenocarcinoma that was initially treated with radiation plus long-term androgen deprivation therapy with a nadir of prostate-specific antigen to <0.1 ng/mL after initial treatment. However, 3 years later, his prostate-specific antigen began to rise despite the initiation of bicalutamide, eventually leading to the development of bony metastatic disease 6 years after definitive therapy.

He was treated with several standard medications for metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer including docetaxel for six cycles followed by enzalutamide before progressing in the lungs with rising prostate-specific antigen. At that time, next-generation sequencing revealed a class II BRAF mutation (K601E). He began treatment on a clinical trial of dual BRAF/MEK inhibition with vemurafenib and cobimetinib that stabilized his disease for 3 months before he ultimately had to discontinue therapy because of grade 3 transaminitis.

Case 3

This patient is a 60-year-old man who presented with appendicitis and had emergency surgery that revealed a pT4 adenocarcinoma of the cecum/appendix with two out of three lymph nodes positive for malignancy pN1b. Eight months after completing 12 cycles of adjuvant FOLFOX, a 4.1 cm oligometastasis was found in his liver on surveillance scans, which was resected and next-generation sequencing revealed a class 3 BRAF (G466,V) mutation. He subsequently progressed and was treated with 3 months of FOLFIRI followed by an additional liver resection with a formal right hemicolectomy followed by an additional 3 months of FOLFIRI before receiving capecitabine and bevacizumab maintenance therapy.

At the time of progression, he consented to epidermal growth factor receptor-pathway directed therapy with the ERK inhibitor ulixertinib but did not tolerate the therapy well as it quickly caused altered his mental status after 5 days on treatment. Despite cessation of therapy, his altered state continued with waxing and waning orientation, ultimately leading to his enrollment in hospice. The patient passed away approximately a month after this trial of therapy, illustrating the potential serious toxicities of targeting this pathway.

Case 5

The patient was a 49-year-old woman who was diagnosed with poorly differentiated metastatic pancreatic neuroendocrine carcinoma with a Ki-67 of 57%. She was treated with eight cycles of carboplatin and etoposide before progressing to a left adrenal metastasis. Because of an MCC-BRAF fusion identified on her next-generation sequencing, she was started on the MEK inhibitor trametinib. Her course was complicated by a Grade 2 facial rash and intermittent cytopenia requiring dose reductions and occasional treatment interruptions. While on therapy, her disease was under control with an interval decrease in the size of her left adrenal metastasis and stable retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy. She was able to remain on therapy for just over 12 months before ultimately progressing.

Discussion

This article demonstrates the current body of research in the treatment of non-V600 mutations. While our patient cohort included patients with a prolonged time to next treatment and clinical responses, our review of available clinical trials demonstrates that responses in class III mutations are very rare, while responses in patients with BRAF fusions are common. While responses have been reported in class II mutations, the majority of patients do not respond, and it is unclear whether the addition of BRAF-specific inhibitors improve the response rate.

With the current limited body of knowledge, it is hard to draw definitive conclusions about the meaningful activity of the current generation of anti-BRAF inhibitors on non-V600E BRAF alterations. The wide array of responses seen in our small cohort, from rapid progression to prolonged disease stability, supports the argument that BRAF mutations should be studied based on specific mechanisms of action rather than as one homogenous group. Diversifying clinical studies based on mutation type using the BRAF class system could organize our understanding of this topic. Data science and large-scale research reviews will help move the field forward.

There are several limitations in our current ability to treat these variants. BRAF V600-specific inhibitors such as vemurafenib, dabrafenib, and encorafenib are small molecules that have high specificity for V600E mutant cells, preferentially inhibiting the active confirmation of BRAF that results from variants at codon 600 but are generally inactive against variants at other locations [31]. Preclinically, they have activity against other variants that result in different confirmations, with V599 being an exception. Novel non-V600 agents that target these alterations specifically, similar to what has been achieved for G12C-specific alterations in KRAS for colorectal and lung cancer, may lend to more potent inhibition of these relatively rare BRAF alterations [32, 33].

The strategies tested in our study were primarily inhibition of downstream targets, as there are not currently agents that address the less common BRAF variants.

Proposed mechanisms of resistance to the MAPK pathway include activation of parallel pathways such as the PI3K/AKT pathway, upregulation of downstream effectors such as ERK, paradoxical activation of other RAF isotypes such as CRAF, and upregulation of epidermal growth factor receptor [34–43]. In order to combat these avenues of tumor resistance, combination therapies and use of downstream inhibitors have worked in the V600E setting and should be explored for other BRAF alterations [44–52]. The use of pan-RAF inhibitors should also be further studied in non-V600 mutated cancers as preclinical studies have shown promising results [53, 54]. Strategies that use immunotherapy combinations or engineered T cells against the BRAF variant may also demonstrate more activity against these relatively resistant oncogenic BRAF variants [55–58].

Conclusions

Meaningful responses to targeted therapy are described in the existing literature and were seen in our cohort of patients, but the responses are relatively rare and not durable. Given the frequency of these mutations, further research and drug development is needed to improve outcomes in this population.
