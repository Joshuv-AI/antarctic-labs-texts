Manganese-loaded carbon nanoparticles ameliorate ANCA-associated vasculitis by inhibiting ferroptosis
Authors: Yiru Wang, Yongman Lv, Chengcheng Fu, Xiyue Zhao, Hao Wu, Jiangjiang Gu, Qingquan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody-associated glomerulonephritis (ANCA-GN), a life-threatening vasculitis manifestation, involves systemic endothelial injury and rapid renal decline. Despite limited therapies, the role of ferroptosis, a newly characterized form of regulated cell death, in ANCA-GN remains unexplored. Here, elevated neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) were identified in ANCA-GN patients and experimental vasculitis models, correlating with endothelial injury markers. Mechanistically, NETs triggered ferroptosis in glomerular endothelial cells (GEnCs) by suppressing manganese superoxide dismutase (MnSOD) activity, exacerbating oxidative stress. To address this, manganese-loaded glucose-based carbon nanoparticles (GCNPs/Mn) were synthesized, demonstrating biocompatibility and antioxidant properties. In vitro, GCNPs/Mn restored MnSOD activity, mitigated mitochondrial dysfunction, and suppressed NF-κB signaling, reversing NET-induced ferroptosis. In ANCA-GN rat models, GCNPs/Mn administration reduced renal injury, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis-related protein dysregulation. Mechanistic studies revealed lysosomal Mn release from GCNPs/Mn enhanced MnSOD activity, linking its therapeutic efficacy to antioxidant defense. This study identifies ferroptosis as a novel driver of GEnC injury in ANCA-GN and pioneers GCNPs/Mn as a MnSOD-targeted nanotherapy, bridging critical gaps in understanding and treating this rare disease.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03725-z.Keywords: ANCA-associated vasculitis, Neutrophil extracellular traps, Manganese, Manganese superoxide dismutase, Ferroptosis, Nanotherapy

Introduction

Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis (AAV) is a systemic autoimmune disease predominantly affecting both small and medium blood vessels and often involves multiple organs, such as the kidneys, lungs, and heart [1]. In Asian populations, microscopic polyangiitis predominates, and in China specifically, approximately 85% of AAV patients experience renal involvement [2, 3]. Renal failure remains a leading cause of death in patients with AAV. The prognosis of ANCA-associated glomerulonephritis (ANCA-GN) is poor with a high recurrence rate. Current treatment regimens involve glucocorticoids combined with cytotoxic drugs. However, this therapeutic approach is connected with severe complications, resulting in persistently high mortality and recurrence rates. Notably, a clinical analysis of Chinese patients revealed that 20.9% of patients with AAV die within 12 months of diagnosis, highlighting the serious threat AAV poses to human life [4]. Improving long-term patient survival remains a challenging task, necessitating the exploration of novel and effective treatment strategies.

AAV is characterized by substantial damage to endothelial cells, with the injury being specifically directed at glomerular endothelial cells (GEnCs) [5]. The etiology and pathogenesis of AAV are not completely understood, but current research suggests that ANCA induces endothelial cell damage chiefly through the activation of neutrophils, including neutrophil degranulation and the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) [6]. NETs, composed primarily of histones, elastase, and myeloperoxidase, have been found to positively correlate with ANCA titers and C-reactive protein levels in patients with AAV, indicating a close association between NETs and the inflammatory response in AAV [7]. Moreover, previous studies have demonstrated that NETs are present in the glomerular capsules of patients with AAV, and their deposition is a key factor in renal damage [8]. In MPO-AAV mouse models, inhibiting NET formation with alvelestat and BAY 85-8501 reduces glomerular NET accumulation and MPO deposition, thereby alleviating glomerular injury and suppressing the progression of AAV and renal damage [9]. These findings further underscore the important role of NETs in the pathogenesis of AAV.

Despite extensive research focusing on the correlation between NET levels and disease severity, as well as methods aimed at inhibiting NET formation, the precise mechanism by which NETs cause vascular endothelial cell damage remains elusive. Recent studies have revealed that NETs play a critical role in promoting ferroptosis in various diseases, including acute lung injury and intestinal ischemia-reperfusion [10, 11]. Ferroptosis, closely related to iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation [12], has been implicated in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases [13]. Significant iron deposition has been reported in both mouse models of lupus nephritis and other inflammatory autoimmune diseases [14, 15]. The successful application of ferroptosis inhibitors in lupus-prone mouse models provides evidentiary support for the role of ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of lupus nephritis. For instance, the ferroptosis inhibitor liproxstatin-1 effectively suppresses lipid reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in neutrophils, significantly relieving symptoms of lupus nephritis in mice [16]. Additionally, several proteins involved in iron homeostasis, such as hepcidin, ferritin, transferrin, and neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin, have been identified as biomarkers of AAV disease activity, with their levels correlating intimately with disease severity and activity [17].

Nonetheless, the exact role of ferroptosis in AAV-induced renal injury remains unknown. Exploring the potential role of ferroptosis in AAV may uncover new aspects regarding its pathogenesis, thereby offering novel treatment possibilities. Although various ferroptosis inhibitors have been developed, many exhibit poor biosafety, stability, or pharmacokinetic properties, limiting their clinical application. Consequently, there is a pressing need for the development of new therapeutic agents. In recent years, there has been a growing focus on the application of nanotechnology in the treatment of kidney diseases, such as nano-targeted delivery systems [18]. Mn-based nanomaterials, with their unique antioxidant properties and high biosafety, have shown therapeutic potential in numerous inflammatory diseases [19]. Our preliminary screening experiments have discovered that manganese-loaded, glucose-based carbon nanoparticles (GCNPs/Mn), in addition to their antioxidant properties, display substantial ferroptosis-inhibiting effects. Therefore, we explored the potential application of GCNPs/Mn in regulating ferroptosis to ameliorate endothelial cell damage in AAV. Furthermore, we investigated the role and mechanism of GCNPs/Mn in alleviating NET-induced GEnC injury.

Materials and methods

Study population and samples

This study was conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Clinical Research of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Approval No. TJ-IRB202403040). Participants were enrolled based on the following criteria: (1) patients with AAV tested positive for ANCA antibodies using immunofluorescence and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay methods and satisfied the vasculitis classification criteria established at the 2012 Chapel Hill Conference, the 1990 ACR diagnostic criteria, or the 2022 ACR/EULAR classification criteria; (2) healthy individuals with normal examination and test results were also included. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with secondary vasculitis, diabetes, cancer, metabolic syndrome, hyperthyroidism, other kidney diseases, or other autoimmune disorders; (2) patients taking immunosuppressive drugs, such as propylthiouracil; and (3) healthy individuals with abnormal examination or test results.

Human peripheral blood neutrophil and mouse neutrophil isolation

Human peripheral blood neutrophils were isolated using a TBD kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (TBD Science Biotech Company, China). Briefly, the blood was layered on a separating fluid and centrifuged. The neutrophil layer was aspirated, cleaned, and treated with a red cell lysis buffer. After the washing procedure, neutrophils were resuspended in the RPMI1640 phenol red-free complete medium containing 3% FBS and counted for experimental purposes. Neutrophils were isolated from murine bone marrow as previously described [20].

Determination of cf-DNA

For cell free-DNA (cf-DNA) detection (Vazyme, China), Equalbit dsDNA HS Standard #2 was serially diluted with Standard #1 to prepare dsDNA standards at concentrations of 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10 ng/µL, with gentle vortexing to ensure mixing. Then, 10 µL of each standard and sample were added to a 96-well plate, followed by 190 µL of working solution. The plate was incubated at room temperature (25℃) for 2 min, and the signal was detected using a fluorescence reader at 485 nm excitation and 528 nm emission. A standard curve was plotted to calculate sample concentrations.

Preparation and characterization of GCNPs and GCNPs/Mn

100 mg of glucose was added into 10 mL of ultra-pure water, which is magnetically stirred to dissolve completely. Then the obtained solution was transferred to a 15 mL of Teflon-equipped stainless-steel autoclave and kept in an oven at 180 °C for 6 h. After the reaction is completed, the mixture was cooled down naturally and centrifuged with 8,000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was taken and stored as the solution of GCNPs. Then, take 10 mL of GCNPs solution, add 500 µL KMnO4 solution (0.1 M) under magnetic agitation, and heat to 80 °C for 1 h. After the reaction was cooled, the dialysis bag was used for dialysis for 24 h (MWCO = 500, renew water every 12 h). The final solution in the dialysis bag is GGCNPs/Mn.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of GCNPs and GCNPs/Mn were observed by an H-7650 electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and Zeta potential results of GCNPs and GCNPs/Mn were conducted using a Nano-ZS90 Zetasizer laser particle sizer (Malvern, UK). Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy of GCNPs and GCNPs/Mn were performed on a NEXUS 670 FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) results of GCNPs and GCNPs/Mn were obtained using a K-Alpha XPS instrument (Thermo Scientific, USA). Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mappings of GCNPs and GCNPs/Mn were collected on a JEM-F200 field emission high-resolution transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Japan).

Cell culture and treatment

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and mouse glomerular endothelial cells (MRGECs) cells were grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin − streptomycin at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The stimulant concentrations were GGCNPs/Mn 15 µg/mL, GGCNPs 15 µg/mL, human NETs 1000 ng/mL (HUVEC), mouse NETs 500 ng/mL (MRGEC), Fer-1 0.5 µM, bay11-7082 1 µM, respectively. Intervention cells for 24 h.

Live/dead cell double staining

Calcein-AM and PI were stored on ice and protected from light (Abbkine, China). The 10× Assay Buffer was diluted to 1× with deionized water and preheated. The staining solution was prepared by adding Calcein-AM and PI to the diluted buffer. Cells were cultured in a 24-well plate at 37 °C for 24 h and then stimulated with the required stimulants. After stimulation, the cells were washed, stained, incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 15–30 min, and washed again. The samples were then observed under a fluorescence microscope using appropriate filters.

Western blotting

Western blotting (WB) was performed using specific primary antibodies against TFR1, manganese superoxide dismutase (MnSOD) (ABclonal, China), COX2 (Wanlei, China), CPX4 (Abcam), ICAM-1 (Proteintech, China), VCAM-1 (Boster, China) and GAPDH (Abways, China. Glass plates were cleaned, dried, and arranged for gel casting. Gels were prepared and solidified, and electrophoresis was run with a protein marker and samples. The voltage was adjusted during the electrophoresis process. Proteins were then transferred to a PVDF membrane in pre-cooled transfer buffer. The membrane was blocked with non-fat milk, incubated with primary and secondary antibodies, and washed with TBST. Finally, ECL substrate was applied, and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ (https://imagej.en.softonic.com/) for protein expression analysis.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using a standard TRIZOL RNA extraction protocol (Takara). RNA reverse transcription was conducted using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, China), and we then performed real-time PCR using SYBR Green Supermix (Vazyme, China). The primers used in RT-PCR are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Detected genes were normalized to the internal control, GAPD.

Determination of iron content

The iron content was determined by a colorimetric method (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China). Briefly, 10 mg of rat kidney tissue or 2 × 106 cells were collected, homogenized with physiological saline, and centrifuged to obtain the supernatant. The protein content was measured using a BCA kit. The reagents were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The reagents were added to the test tubes following a specific order, mixed, incubated in boiling water, cooled, and centrifuged. The supernatant was transferred to a microplate. The absorbance was measured at 520 nm, and the iron content was calculated based on standard and blank wells.

Determination of malondialdehyde

Samples were prepared from plasma, rat kidney tissue (10 mg), or approximately 2 × 106 cells. Kidney tissue or cells were homogenized, and protein content was measured using a BCA kit. The reagents are prepared sequentially following the manufacturer’s instructions (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China). The MDA detection working solution was added to samples, mixed, and boiled. The supernatant was collected by centrifuging. The absorbance was measured at 532 nm, and a standard curve was used to calculate the MDA content.

Mitochondrial membrane potential

For the detection of mitochondrial membrane potential using TMRE staining (Beyotime, China), cells were cultured in a 24-well plate and subjected to specific stimulation. After stimulation, cells were washed with PBS, and then incubated with TMRE staining solution for 30 min at 37 °C. Following incubation, cells were washed with pre-heated medium, stained with Hoechst 33,342 for 20 min, washed again, and then observed under a fluorescence microscope for imaging.

FITC-BSA cell permeability measurement

Cells were inoculated into the upper chamber of a Transwell plate (Corning) at a density of 105 cells/mL, with medium in the lower chamber. After reaching an appropriate density, the cells were starved for 8 h and then treated with interventions for 24 h. FITC-BSA (Solarbio, China) was added, and the plate was incubated for 2 h. Fluorescence intensity was measured in both chambers, and the permeation coefficient was calculated using the formula: Permeation coefficient = (Lower chamber fluorescence) ÷ (Upper + Lower chamber fluorescence) × 100%.

Intracellular ROS and lipid peroxide assay

For the detection of ROS using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe (Beyotime, China) and lipid peroxidation using the BODIPY 581/591 C11 fluorescent probe (Invitrogen), cells were cultured in a 24-well plate and subjected to specific stimulation treatments. After stimulation, the DCFH-DA probe was diluted to a final concentration of 10 µM using serum-free medium at a ratio of 1:1000 and added to the cells for ROS detection. For lipid peroxidation detection, cells were washed with PBS, and then incubated with BODIPY 581/591 C11 staining solution at a final concentration of 2.5 µM for 30 min at 37 °C. Following incubation, cells were washed with PBS to remove any unbound probe or dye. For both detections, cells were stained with Hoechst 33,342 (Solarbio, China) for 20 min, washed again with PBS, and observed under a fluorescence microscope in a light-protected environment for imaging.

PAS staining

After dewaxing and hydrating, paraffin sections were immersed in 1% periodic acid solution for 10 min and rinsed. Schiff’s reagent (Biossci, China) was applied for 10 min, followed by hematoxylin nuclear staining for 2–5 min. The sections were rinsed, differentiated in 1% hydrochloric acid alcohol, dehydrated, air-dried, mounted, and then observed under a microscope.

DHE staining

Frozen tissue sections were warmed to room temperature and then stained with DAPI working solution for 3 min under light-protected conditions. The sections were washed with TBST to remove unbound dye, followed by incubation with DHE working solution (Sigma) for 1 h at room temperature. After washing with TBST to remove excess dye, the sections were mounted, stored at 4 °C under light-protected conditions, and observed under a microscope for imaging and analysis.

Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS)

Mn levels were determined using ICP-MS. Briefly, 0.2 g of tissue was stored at −80 °C. The tissue was digested with concentrated nitric acid and perchloric acid, heated at 180 °C for 1 h, and then open-heated until dried. The precipitate was dissolved in 1% nitric acid, and the solution was diluted to 10 mL. Briefly, for cell sample processing, nanomaterials were added when the cells reached about 80% confluency. The cells were incubated for 24 h and then washed with PBS. After digestion, the cells were centrifuged, and the cell pellets were dispersed in 2 ml pure water and then dialyzed in a beaker for about 6 h. Then 10 ml of dialysate was taken and tested. A standard curve for Mn was prepared, and the samples were filtered and analyzed by iCAP Q (Thermo Fisher). The Mn content was calculated based on the standard curve.

Experimental autoimmune vasculitis model

The experimental autoimmune vasculitis (EAV) rat model induced by MPO was performed as previously described [21]. A preliminary experiment investigated nanomaterial duration in female WKY rats injected with GCNPs/Mn via the tail vein. Tissue and serum samples were collected on days 1, 7, 14, and 28 post-injections. For the human serum albumin (HSA) group, rats were injected with 1600 µg/kg HSA (MCE) mixed with Freund’s adjuvant (Sigma) on Day 0 and received 1 µg of pertussis toxin (List Labs) on Days 0 and 2. PBS was injected via the tail vein every other day starting from Day 28. Rats were sacrificed after 56 days. The MPO-AAV group received the same treatment but with MPO instead of HSA. Rats were sacrificed after 56 days. The MPO-AAV + GCNPs/Mn group received MPO and pertussis toxin treatments and was injected with GCNPs/Mn (10 mg/kg) via the tail vein every other day starting from Day 28. Rats were sacrificed after 56 days. The animal experiments were performed according to the procedures approved by the Scientific Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Approval No. TJH-202305024).

Establishment of rat vasculitis models induced by propylthiouracil (PTU-AAV)

Female WKY rats aged (6 weeks old) were utilized in this experiment. The control (CTRL) group received 10 mL of 5% glucose water daily for 30 days, with intraperitoneal injections of 100 µL PBS on Days 0 and 7 and tail vein injections of PBS every other day starting from Day 14, without any other special treatments. For the PTU-AAV group, rats were administered 10 mL of water containing PTU (MCE) (10 mg) and glucose (5%) daily for 30 days, with intraperitoneal injections of 100 µL PMA (10 µg/mL) on Days 0 and 7, and tail vein injections of PBS every other day starting from day 14. For the PTU-AAV + GCNPs/Mn group, rats received the same PTU and PMA treatments as the PTU-AAV group. Starting from Day 14, they were injected with GCNPs/Mn (10 mg/kg) via the tail vein every other day, with no other special treatments. Rats were euthanized 30 days after the start of the model, and tissue specimens were collected.

SiRNA transfection

The siRNA sequences used are detailed in supplementary Table 2. Prior to transfection, cells were plated in a 12-well plate and cultured under suitable conditions. Transfections were performed when cell confluence reached approximately 30%, with cells given fresh medium without antibiotics 30 min prior to transfections. In EP tubes, mixtures of Opti-MEM and siRNA solution, as well as Opti-MEM and Lipometer 3000 transfection reagent (Vazyme, China), were prepared and incubated for 10 min. The mixtures were then combined, and 100 µL was added to each cell well. After 72 h, the cells were collected for RNA extraction to assess transfection efficiency.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as the mean ± the standard error mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using an ANOVA analysis for multiple comparisons, while a t test was used for two groups. GraphPad Prism 9.0 software was used for statistical analyses. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant difference.

Results

NETs are increased in humans and rats with AAV and related to endothelial cell injury

To clarify the role of NETs in ANCA-GN, we evaluated the ability of neutrophils isolated from healthy individuals and patients with ANCA-GN to release NETs. Both the NET-release capacity and cf-DNA levels were significantly higher in neutrophils from ANCA-GN patients as compared to the healthy control group (Fig. 1A–B), indicating that neutrophils from patients with ANCA-GN possess an enhanced ability to release NETs. Furthermore, the serum NET levels were considerably elevated in patients with ANCA-GN as compared to healthy individuals (Fig. 1C).

Immunofluorescence staining of renal tissue sections from ANCA-GN patients showed significantly increased expression levels of glomerular NET markers, MPO and NE, as compared to the control group (Fig. 1D), suggesting increased glomerular NET deposition in ANCA-GN patients. This finding suggested the involvement of NET formation in the pathological process of glomerular alterations in ANCA-GN. Additionally, we established two rat models of vasculitis to validate the role of NETs in vivo. The results showed that serum NETs levels were also significantly increased in vasculitis rats (Fig. 1E–F).

To explore the relationship between serum NET levels and endothelial cell damage, we measured serum NET levels (cf-DNA levels) and endothelial cell damage markers, ICAM-1 and VCAM-1, in patients with ANCA-GN and performed correlation analysis. We found a positive correlation between serum NET levels and ICAM-1 or VCAM-1 levels (Fig. 1G). These findings suggest that endothelial cell damage may be associated with NET formation.

Ferroptosis is present during ANCA-GN progression

Ferroptosis is a complex pathological process involving numerous indicators. Serum ferritin, a protein that stores iron, is intimately associated with ferroptosis. Additionally, iron overload can result in oxidative stress, leading to the production of harmful lipid peroxides, such as MDA, which further exacerbates cellular damage. A decrease in endogenous antioxidant enzyme activity, such as MnSOD, also contributes to ferroptosis [22]. Thus, we assessed serum ferritin and MDA levels, as well as MnSOD activity in patients with ANCA-GN. We found that serum ferritin and MDA levels were significantly increased in patients with ANCA-GN as compared to healthy individuals (Fig. 2A–B), while MnSOD activity was significantly decreased (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, we found a positive correlation between serum cf-DNA levels and MDA levels (r = 0.46, P = 0.015) (Fig. 2D), and a negative correlation between MnSOD activity and MDA levels (r = −0.52, P = 0.005) (Fig. 2E). To further examine the occurrence of ferroptosis in glomeruli, we performed immunofluorescence staining for TFR1 in renal tissues (Fig. 2F). Notably, TFR1 expression was significantly increased in the glomeruli of both patients with ANCA-GN and rats undergoing experimental vasculitis. WB analysis revealed a significant upregulation of the ferroptosis-promoting protein TFR1 and a downregulation of the ferroptosis-suppressing protein GPX4 in the renal tissues of vasculitis rats (Fig. 2G). These results demonstrated the presence of ferroptosis in renal tissues during the pathological progression of ANCA-GN, which may be related to NET formation.

NETs induce endothelial impairment via ferroptosis and decrease MnSOD activity

Based on these findings, we hypothesized that NETs play a critical role in endothelial cell injury and kidney damage in ANCA-GN by activating ferroptosis. Decreased mitochondrial membrane potential, oxidative stress, and lipid peroxidation are hallmarks of ferroptosis. Therefore, we used TMRE, DCFH-DA, and C11-BODIPY fluorescent probes to detect mitochondrial membrane potential, oxidative stress, and lipid peroxidation, respectively (Fig. 3A). The results demonstrated that NETs significantly decreased mitochondrial membrane potential in endothelial cells and exacerbated cellular oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation. Intervention with a ferroptosis inhibitor markedly reversed the decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential and attenuated oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation. WB analysis showed that NET treatment led to increased expression of ferroptosis-promoting proteins, such as TFR1 and COX2, and decreased expression of the ferroptosis-suppressing protein GPX4, while the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 reversed these NET-induced alterations in ferroptosis-related protein expression (Fig. 3B). Previous results have confirmed decreased MnSOD activity in the serum of AAV patients. As such, we next explored the changes in MnSOD expression and activity during NET-induced ferroptosis in endothelial cells. We found that neither the transcriptional nor protein levels of MnSOD were significantly altered in the two types of endothelial cells after NET intervention (Supplementary Fig. 1 A, C), but MnSOD activity was significantly decreased in both cell types (Supplementary Fig. 1B). We then assessed the impact of NETs on endothelial cell activation injury. NETs increased the release of LDH from endothelial and upregulated expression of ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 (Fig. 3C). Intervention with a ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 attenuated NET-mediated endothelial cell impairment, suggesting that ferroptosis is involved in NET-induced endothelial cell injury.

Synthesis and characterization of GCNPs/Mn

A schematic illustration of the synthesis of nanomaterials is shown in Fig. 4A. Initially, a glucose aqueous solution was hydrothermally treated at 180 °C for 6 h, resulting in a brown solution of GCNPs that appeared yellow and transparent upon dilution. Subsequently, purple potassium permanganate (KMnO4) was added for an oxidation-reduction reaction, yielding a colorless and transparent solution of GCNPs/Mn (Fig. 4A). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to observe the morphology and size of the nanomaterials, revealing that both GCNPs and GCNPs/Mn exhibited discrete spherical shapes, with average sizes of 37.4 and 37.5 nm, respectively (Fig. 4B–C), indicating that the size of the nanomaterials did not significantly change after adding KMnO4. Dynamic light scattering measurements were conducted to determine the hydrodynamic diameters of the nanomaterials, which revealed a uniform distribution with diameters of 40.6 ± 2.7 nm for GCNPs and 41.3 ± 2.8 nm for GCNPs/Mn (Fig. 4D), consistent with the TEM results. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy was further utilized to characterize the functional groups present in the nanomaterials. As shown in Fig. 4E, both GCNPs and GCNPs/Mn exhibited similar absorption peaks, including an O-H stretching vibration peak at around 3300 cm−1, a C-H stretching vibration peak at around 2923 cm−1, a C = O stretching vibration peak at around 1660 cm−1, and a C-O stretching vibration peak at around 1018 cm−1. Additionally, GCNPs/Mn showed a new absorption peak at 1580 cm−1, possibly due to a shift in the C = O absorption peak towards a lower wave number after Mn loading. Zeta potential measurements (Fig. 4F) revealed that the potential of the nanomaterials changed from −13.43 ± 0.29 mV for GCNPs to −20.07 ± 0.25 mV for GCNPs/Mn after Mn loading. This could be attributed to the increased number of carboxyl groups and negative charges on the nanomaterials after KMnO4 oxidation, enhancing the physical stability of GCNPs/Mn in aqueous solutions. The DLS size and Zeta potential of GCNPs/Mn in PBS buffer (pH = 7.4) were measured to show their dispersibility and stability. As demonstrated in Supplementary Fig. 2A-B, GCNPs/Mn had size around 40 nm and Zeta potential about − 20 mV for keeping 1, 3, 5, and 7 days, which indicated that they were well dispersed and highly stable in physiological environment. Subsequently, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy was employed to evaluate the surface elements and bonding of the nanomaterials. The results, shown in Fig. 4G, indicated that the proportions of Mn, C, and O elements in GCNPs and GCNPs/Mn were 0.14%: 69.57%: 30.30% and 1.58%: 68.17%: 30.25%, respectively. The significant increase in Mn content confirmed the successful loading of Mn onto the GCNP carrier. To confirm this result, energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mappings of TEM imaging were conducted for GNCNPs and GNCNPs/Mn. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2 C, GCNPs only consisted of C and O elements in the nanoparticles, and GCNPs/Mn particles were composed of evenly distributed Mn, C and O elements, indicating Mn was loaded successfully on GCNPs/Mn. For C 1 s spectra, GCNPs/Mn have more C = O than GCNPs, and the peak intensity of C-O deceased in GCNPs/Mn as compared to GCNPs, which was contributed to the oxidation of KMnO4. In the Mn 2p spectrum of GCNPs/Mn, it was clear that Mn (II) and Mn (IV) co-existed, which were formed by the reduction of KMnO4.

To assess the ROS-scavenging ability of GCNPs/Mn, in vitro ROS scavenging assays were performed. As shown in Fig. 4H, GCNPs/Mn have the ability to scavenge SOD-like O2•− and scavenge OH•. These results showed that GCNPs/Mn performed antioxidant enzyme-mimicking activities to scavenge broad-spectrum ROS, especially O2•−. Furthermore, it was further interesting to find that Mn is easily precipitated in the nanomaterials under acidic conditions (Fig. 4I).

GCNPs/Mn inhibit NET-induced ferroptosis in GEnCs

Given that GCNPs/Mn have the ability to mimic antioxidant enzyme activity, we next investigated whether GCNPs/Mn have the potential to inhibit ferroptosis. PI staining showed that supplementation with GCNPs/Mn had no obvious cytotoxicity, even at concentrations up to 100 µg/mL for 24 h (Supplementary Fig. 3 A). The results indicated that GCNPs/Mn had suitable cytocompatibility. Importantly, after co-culturing GCNPs/Mn with HUVECs or MRGECs for 30 min, we found that GCNPs/Mn visibly entered the cells, and these nanomaterials maintained a high concentration within the cells even after 24 h (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Based on these observations, we selected to pre-incubate the cells with GCNPs/Mn for 30 min prior to NET treatment for the subsequent cell experiments. Our preliminary cell experiments revealed that PI-positive staining increased significantly in ferroptosis death inducer RSL3 and Erastin intervention groups. However, cell death was almost completely suppressed by supplementation with GCNPs/Mn (Supplementary Fig. 4 A–B). Furthermore, we employed CPT (camptothecin) and DOX (doxorubicin) to inducer apoptosis, and LPS (lipopolysaccharide, 2ug/ml) to inducer pyroptosis. Supplementation with GCNPs/Mn did not suppress apoptosis and pyroptosis (Supplementary Fig. 4C-D). These results indicated that GCNPs/Mn exhibited favorable cytocompatibility and a significant inhibitory effect on ferroptosis under the tested conditions.

To further investigate whether GCNPs/Mn can alleviate NET-induced HUVEC and MRGEC damage, CCK-8 assays revealed that supplementation with GCNPs/Mn prominently restored the relative decline in cell viability caused by NETs in both HUVECs and MRGECs within 24 h (Fig. 5A). We further found that 15 µg/mL GCNPs/Mn exhibited a suitable protective effect, and we selected 15 µg/mL GCNPs/Mn as the optimal intervention concentration for subsequent cellular experiments. GCNPs/Mn treatment significantly improved NETs-induced cell injury(Fig. 5B). Cell death analysis, as evidenced by PI staining, showed that GCNPs/Mn inhibited NET-induced death of HUVECs and MRGECs. The inhibitory effect was almost identical to that of Fer-1 (Supplementary Fig. 5). These results indicated that the protective effect of GCNPs/Mn on endothelial cells may be related to the functionality of the Mn element supplementation.

Lipid peroxidation and decreased mitochondrial membrane potential are common biochemical characteristics of ferroptosis. We found that NETs reduced the mitochondrial membrane potential of HUVECs and MRGECs, while supplementation with GCNPs/Mn prominently restored the change in mitochondrial membrane potential, as shown by TMRE fluorescence microscopy staining (Fig. 5C). NETs also increased total intracellular ROS and lipid peroxidation, as shown by DCFH-DA and BODIPY 581/591-C11staining via fluorescence microscopy analysis, respectively. We found that supplementation with GCNPs/Mn improved this increase in total intracellular ROS and lipid peroxidation, which were aligned with the effects of Fer-1 intervention (Fig. 5C). Both HUVECs and MRGECs displayed disappearance of mitochondrial cristae and mitochondrial vacuolation after exposure to NETs, indicative of ferroptosis alterations, while GCNPs/Mn significantly mitigated this mitochondrial morphological change (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Furthermore, HUVECs and MRGECs treated with NETs exhibited significantly elevated MDA and iron levels. However, following intervention with GCNPs/Mn, both MDA and iron levels decreased markedly, consistent with the outcomes of Fer-1 intervention (Fig. 5D–E). WB analysis showed that the expression levels of TFR1 and COX2 proteins in NETs + GCNPs/Mn group were significantly lower than those in NETs group, while the expression level of GPX4 protein showed the opposite trend. When comparing the cells treated with GCNPs/Mn and Fe-1, there was no significant difference in the expression levels of these proteins (Fig. 5F). Taken together, these results indicated that GCNPs/Mn effectively counteract NET-induced ferroptosis, indicating its potential as a cellular protective agent.

GCNPs/Mn attenuate NET-mediated GEnC injury by inhibiting ferroptosis

We next investigated the effect of GCNPs/Mn on NET-induced injury in GEnCs. First, we assessed cell activation by measuring the levels of LDH and found that as compared with untreated cells, the level of LDH release was increased in MRGECs or HUVECs stimulated by NETs (P < 0.01), while GCNPs/Mn supplementation effectively decreased the level of LDH release (P < 0.05) (Fig. 6A). Endothelial cell permeability was performed to study the vascular barrier function. NETs treatment increased the permeability percentage of both HUVEC and MRGEC monolayers. However, intervention with GCNPs/Mn markedly lowered this permeability (Fig. 6B). Immunofluorescence staining of ZO-1 was used to evaluate the structure of the tight junction in HUVEC and MRGEC monolayers, and NETs resulted in disruption of intercellular boundaries and reduced ZO-1 fluorescence intensity. However, combined application of NETs and GCNPs/Mn restored the sharper boundaries and increased ZO-1 fluorescence (Fig. 6C). Previous studies have reported that ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 are considered biomarkers of endothelial cell activation injury [23, 24]. The protein levels of ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 increased significantly after treatment with NETs. Notably, GCNPs/Mn supplementation reduced the protein levels of ICAM-1 and VCAM-1in HUVECs and MRGECs treated with NETs. In addition, the expression of ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 was not significantly different between the NETs + GCNPs/Mn and NETs + Fer-1 groups (Fig. 6D). These results suggested that GCNPs/Mn can alleviate NET-induced vascular endothelial damage.

GCNPs/Mn alleviate NET-induced GEnC ferroptosis via the MnSOD/NF-κB signaling pathway

We then explored the underlying mechanism of GCNPs/Mn inhibiting NET- induced ferroptosis in GEnCs. Mn is a crucial cofactor/activator for various enzymes in biological systems, participating in enzymatic catalysis and maintaining normal physiological functions [25]. Notably, Mn acts as a cofactor for MnSOD, and the presence of Mn is vital for MnSOD activity. We first assess the regulatory effect of GCNPs/Mn on MnSOD activity mediated by NETs in endothelial cells. NET intervention reduced MnSOD activity, while GCNPs/Mn substantially restored it (Fig. 7A). These results indicated that GCNPs/Mn regulates MnSOD activity in endothelial cells. To determine the precise subcellular distribution of GCNPs/Mn and its potential mechanism, we labeled major organelles and incubated the cells with GCNPs/Mn. Confocal microscopy revealed that a significant portion of PMO-DiI co-localized with LysoTracker, indicating its primary presence in lysosomes (Fig. 7B). In contrast, PMO-DiI did not co-localize with mitochondria (Supplementary Fig. 7 A). To ascertain whether Mn ions were released from GCNPs/Mn within lysosomes (pH 4.5-5.0) [26], we have previously confirmed that manganese in GCNPs/Mn is released under a simulated lysosomal acid environment (pH = 5) by ICP-MS detection (Fig. 4I). To further validate the acid responsiveness indicated by the in vivo manganese accumulation data, we conducted in vitro cellular experiments. Specifically, after incubating GCNPs/Mn with cells for 24 h, the cell pellets were collected, dissolved in pure water, and subjected to dialysis for 6 h. We observed a significant increase in manganese concentration in the dialysate of the GCNPs/Mn-treated group, confirming that the intracellular manganese level rises after GCNPs/Mn enter the cells (Fig. 7C). This result suggested that Mn dissociated from GCNPs/Mn in lysosomes, thereby increasing the intracellular Mn levels and enhancing MnSOD activity.

Next, we assessed the relationship between MnSOD activity and NET-induced ferroptosis in GEnCs. MnSOD activity is connected to MnSOD protein levels, therefore, we used siRNA technology to decrease the MnSOD protein levels in cells. We screened out ideal siRNA sequences for subsequent cellular experiments through qRT-PCR (Supplementary Fig. 7B). After the transcriptional level of endothelial cell MnSOD decreased, the activity of MnSOD significantly reduced (Supplementary Fig. 7 C).

In both HUVECs and MRGECs, MnSOD knockdown (MnSOD siRNA + NETs) significantly upregulated TFR1 and COX2 expression and downregulated GPX4 expression. However, GCNPs/Mn administration (MnSOD siRNA + NETs + GCNPs/Mn) did not alter these protein expression changes caused by MnSOD knockdown (Fig. 7D). We also observed a substantial increase in DCFH-DA fluorescence intensity in MnSOD siRNA + NETs group compared with si-NC + NETs group (Supplementary Fig. 8 A). The fluorescence intensity of TMRE showed the opposite trend (Supplementary Fig. 8B). Similarly, compared with si-NC group, knockdown of MnSOD (MnSOD siRNA + NETs) significantly increased the protein levels of ICAM-1 and VCAM-1. However, after MnSOD knockdown, GCNPs/Mn supplementation failed to significantly reduce the protein levels of ICAM-1 and VCAM-1(Fig. 7E, Supplementary Fig. 9 A). These results demonstrated that MnSOD activity affects ferroptosis in GEnCs, and the ability of GCNPs/Mn to alleviate NET-induced ferroptosis and injury in endothelial cells may depend on the activity of MnSOD.

To further investigate the mechanism underlying the inhibition of ferroptosis by GCNPs/Mn, we performed RNA-seq on HUVECs treated with either NETs alone or in combination with GCNPs/Mn. A total of 501 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, with 189 upregulated genes and 312 downregulated genes identified in the NETs + GCNPs/Mn group as compared to NETs-only group. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs were primarily enriched in the MAPK signaling pathway, NF-κB signaling pathway, and ferroptosis-related pathways (Fig. 7F). Previous studies have demonstrated that promoting MnSOD expression inhibits activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway [27]. We found NETs treatment significantly increased p-P65/P65 and p-IκBα/IκBα protein levels in HUVECs and MRGECs, and this trend was counteracted by GCNPs/Mn (Fig. 7G, Supplementary Fig. 9B). This inhibitory effect was similar to that of the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1. To further clarify that the NF-κB signaling pathway mediates GCNPs/Mn to inhibit ferroptosis of glomerular endothelial cells induced by NETs by regulating MnSOD activity, we used the NF-κB inhibitor BAY 11–7082 in combination with MnSOD siRNA intervention. As shown in Fig. 7H, compared with si-NC + NETs + GCNPs/Mn group, the cell viability decreased significantly after reducing MnSOD activity in MnSOD siRNA + NETs + GCNPs/Mn group. However, this downward trend can be improved by BAY 11-7082 inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway. Reducing the MnSOD activity can increase the MDA level caused by NETs intervention. Supplementation with GCNPs/Mn reduced the increase in MDA levels, but this effect was attenuated by BAY 11–7082 (NF-κB inhibition) (Fig. 7I). The changing trend of mitochondrial membrane potential in each group was consistent with the cell viability (Supplementary Fig. 9D). To further explore whether the alteration of MnSOD activity would affect the regulation of the NF-κB signaling pathway mediated by GCNPs/Mn, we reduced the expression of MnSOD by siRNA to decrease its activity. We found that decreasing MnSOD activity enhanced the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, as evidenced by an increase in P65 phosphorylation in the MnSOD siRNA + NETs group (Fig. 7J, Supplementary Fig. 9 C). Additionally, the protein levels of p-P65/P65 in the MnSOD siRNA + NETs + GCNPs/Mn group were higher than in the si-NC + NETs + GCNPs/Mn group and reduced than in the MnSOD siRNA + NETs group.

These results indicated that NF-κB signaling pathway is involved in NET-induced endothelial cell damage, and MnSOD activity is related to GCNPs/Mn. Altering MnSOD activity affects the inhibitory effect of GCNPs/Mn on ferroptosis. Collectively, GCNPs/Mn inhibit ferroptosis and alleviate NET-induced GEnCs damage through the MnSOD/NF-κB signaling pathway.

GCNPs/Mn attenuate renal injury in experimental vasculitis rats by inhibiting ferroptosis

To investigate the safety of intravenous injection of GCNPs/Mn, a hemolysis assay was performed. The results demonstrated that the hemolysis rates of different concentrations of GCNPS/Mn were all below 5% (Supplementary Fig. 10 A). Furthermore, serum myocardial, liver, and kidney injury markers were measured at various time points, and HE pathological staining of these organs showed no significant changes after injection of GCNPs/Mn (Supplementary Figs. 10B–C), indicating the suitable biocompatibility of GCNPs/Mn. Additionally, ICP-MS analysis was used to evaluate the biodistribution of GCNPs/Mn by measuring Mn levels in major tissues at various times post-injection. We found that Mn levels significantly increased only in the kidney at day 1 post-injection, suggesting that GCNPs/Mn preferentially accumulated in the kidney. At day 3 post-injection, Mn concentrations in the kidney were reduced to normal levels, and the Mn concentrations did not change significantly in other major organs. Thus, we established a treatment frequency of once every other day (Supplementary Fig. 10D).

To further explore the effects of GCNPs/Mn on animal models of AAV, MPO-AAV and PTU-AAV rat models were established. The schematic illustrations of the establishment of the animal models and treatment schedule are shown in Fig. 8A. The levels of serum BUN and serum creatinine (Scr), urinary protein, and hematuria were significantly increased in the vasculitis model group, but the levels of these biochemical markers were significantly restored after injection with GCNPs/Mn in both the MPO-AAV and PTU-AAV rat models (Fig. 8B). PAS staining showed that degrees of glomerular necrosis and proportions of crescent formation were also significantly reduced after injection with GCNPs/Mn (Fig. 8C). These results indicated that injection of GCNPs/Mn effectively alleviated renal tissue damage and functional injury in murine vasculitis models.

Next, we investigated the characteristics of ferroptosis in animal models of vasculitis. We assessed oxidative stress levels, lipid peroxidation levels, and iron overload using a DHE fluorescence probe, MDA levels, and tissue iron content assay, respectively. We found that the vasculitis rats exhibited drastically enhanced DHE fluorescence intensity (Fig. 8D), elevated MDA levels (Fig. 8F), and increased iron content in renal tissues (Fig. 8G) as compared with the control group. Moreover, treatment with GCNPs/Mn largely alleviated these changes in the vasculitis rats. This finding was also confirmed by evaluating the ultrastructural changes of GEnCs via electron microscopy. We observed that mitochondrial shrinkage, cristae disappearance, and mitochondrial vacuolation were largely attenuated after treatment with GCNPs/Mn (Fig. 8E). Additionally, we observed that treatment with GCNPs/Mn significantly elevated MnSOD activity in the renal tissues of MPO-AAV rats (Fig. 8H).

In addition, we explored the effects of GCNPs/Mn on the expression of ferroptosis-related proteins in vasculitis rats. The levels of pro-ferroptosis proteins TFR1 and COX2 were significantly increased, while level of GPX4 was significantly decreased in the renal tissue of vasculitis rats. After treatment with GCNPs/Mn, the expression of TFR1 and COX2 were significantly suppressed (Fig. 8I). In addition, the levels of oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and iron death-related proteins were also assessed in PTU-AAV vasculitis rats (Supplementary Fig. 11A-E). These findings suggested that the GEnCs in the MPO-AAV group exhibited an increase in ferroptosis, whereas treatment with GCNPs/Mn ameliorated ferroptosis in GEnCs. We also evaluated the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway in vasculitis rat model by WB analysis. The levels of p-P65 increased in MPO-AAV rats, while treatment with GCNPs/Mn significantly suppressed these increases (Fig. 8J), indicating that GCNPs/Mn can inhibit the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway.

Discussion

AAV, a complex autoimmune inflammatory disease with an incompletely deciphered etiology, poses a significant threat due to its high destructiveness and potential lethality. Notably, recent research has highlighted NETs as a central factor in the pathogenesis of ANCA-GN [28]. In this study, our initial investigations revealed that ANCA-GN patients exhibited substantially elevated serum NET levels, concomitant with NET deposition in the glomeruli. Mechanistically, NETs suppressed MnSOD activity, exacerbating mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and lipid peroxidation, thereby triggering GEnC ferroptosis. The developed GCNPs/Mn demonstrated robust biocompatibility and antioxidant properties in vitro, effectively rescuing NET-induced ferroptosis via lysosomal Mn release, enhancing MnSOD activity to suppress ferroptosis-related protein dysregulation. Furthermore, intravenously injected GCNPs/Mn in MPO-AAV and PTU-AAV rat models show good biocompatibility and could significantly alleviate renal function impairment and the damage of glomerulus ultrastructural.

Recent studies have uncovered that NETs can inhibit autophagy in intestinal mucosal microvascular endothelial cells, leading to excessive ROS generation and lipid peroxidation—both of which drive endothelial cell ferroptosis and subsequently affect intestinal microvascular function [11]. As research on autoimmune diseases continues to deepen, the importance of ferroptosis in autoimmune diseases, such as systemic lupus erythematosus, has become increasingly prominent [29, 30]. Additionally, ferroptosis is closely associated to various kidney diseases, including acute kidney injury (AKI), diabetic nephropathy, polycystic kidney disease, and renal fibrosis [31, 32]. Notably, we observed markedly elevated levels of serum ferritin and MDA, along with a significant decrease in MnSOD activity in ANCA-GN patients. Serum MDA levels were positively correlated with NET levels and negatively correlated with MnSOD activity. This discovery not only corroborated previous reports but also provided tangible evidence of NET formation during the progression of ANCA-GN. Moreover, a positive correlation was discerned between NETs and the levels of serum endothelial cell injury markers, implying a potential link between NET formation and endothelial cell damage in ANCA-GN. In vitro experiments further confirmed that NETs induce both ferroptosis and activation-induced injury in endothelial cells, and these effects were reversed by the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1. These findings suggested that ferroptosis may occur during the progression of ANCA-GN and that NETs participate in endothelial cell activation and injury, particularly through NET-induced ferroptosis.

Emerging evidence has underscored the promising potential of ferroptosis as a therapeutic target in NET-induced endothelial cell activation and injury. Ferroptosis, a regulated form of cell death, is orchestrated by the loss of GPX4 activity and the subsequent accumulation of lipid-based ROS [33]. Notably, our findings revealed that NETs diminish MnSOD activity in endothelial cells, which may further exacerbate ferroptosis, highlighting the intricate interplay between these processes. Although the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 was effective in preventing NET-mediated endothelial cell activation and injury, its clinical application is hindered by instability in vivo, underscoring the urgency for novel therapeutic agents. Over the past decade, Mn-based nanomaterials have emerged as promising candidates in biomedical research due to their exceptional biocompatibility and efficient ROS-scavenging capabilities [19, 34]. Their unique properties confer significant advantages in inhibiting ferroptosis and treating ferroptosis-associated diseases. Several reports have demonstrated that Mn-based nanomaterials can effectively suppress ferroptosis via modulating intracellular ROS levels in various disease models. For instance, polyacrylic acid-coated Mn3O4 nanoparticles exhibited robust ROS-scavenging efficacy, particularly against hydroxyl radicals, both in vitro and in vivo [35]. This ROS-scavenging action mitigated lipid peroxidation, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis. Further, Mn3O4 nanomaterials alleviated acute liver injury induced by acetaminophen and ischemia/reperfusion, providing new avenues for ferroptosis-related liver disease treatment. Similarly, MnO2 nanoparticles inhibited ferroptosis by suppressing ferritinophagy and activating the P62-NRF2-GPX4 antioxidant signaling pathway, attenuating acetaminophen-induced liver damage [36].

In recent years, the application of Mn-based nanomaterials in renal diseases has also gained traction. For example, Mn3O4 nanoflowers, leveraging their SOD- and catalase-like activities, eliminated ROS and sequestered released cf-DNA, thereby not only mitigating immune responses and inflammation but also protecting against cisplatin- and ischemia/reperfusion-induced AKI in mice [37]. Selenium-loaded carbon quantum dots and phenylenediamine-based carbon nanodots have shown efficacy in ameliorating AKI, with their preferential renal accumulation and broad-spectrum antioxidant properties [38, 39]. Given our previous observation that NETs reduced MnSOD activity in endothelial cells, we synthesized and screened GCNPs/Mn, which were found to inhibit RSL3-induced ferroptosis. Our in vitro experiments subsequently confirmed that GCNPs/Mn effectively reversed NET-induced alterations in endothelial cell mitochondrial membrane potential, oxidative stress, and lipid peroxidation, accompanied by changes in ferroptosis-related protein expression patterns. These results were similar to the effects observed with the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1. Additionally, GCNPs/Mn reduced NET-mediated endothelial cell permeability and activation injury, suggesting its protective role against NET-induced endothelial damage. To further validate our findings, we performed animal experiments using two rat models of vasculitis. Our results showed the presence of ferroptosis in the renal tissues of these models and established that GCNPs/Mn could inhibit ferroptosis, enhance MnSOD activity, and improve renal tissue injury in vasculitis rats. These findings not only support the potential of GCNPs/Mn as a novel therapeutic agent for AAV, but also underscore the critical role of ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of AAV-induced renal damage.

MnSOD, a member of the superoxide dismutase family, primarily resides within the mitochondrial matrix and plays a pivotal antioxidant role in ferroptosis. By enhancing MnSOD activity, ferroptosis can be effectively inhibited. The primary function of MnSOD is to scavenge mitochondrial matrix superoxide anion radicals, such as O2•−, thereby reducing ROS levels and safeguarding cells from oxidative stress damage. MnSOD dysfunction leads to excessive generation of highly reactive oxidants, which, through the Fenton reaction, elevates ROS and promotes lipid peroxidation, ultimately triggering ferroptosis [40]. Studies have confirmed that augmenting MnSOD activity during ischemia-reperfusion suppression mitigates mitochondrial ROS levels, alleviating renal tubular epithelial cell ferroptosis, and renal ischemia-reperfusion injury [41]. Additionally, the angiotensin receptor blocker, LCZ696, has been shown to promote MnSOD deacetylation, enhance MnSOD activity, and inhibit ferroptosis, thereby ameliorating doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity [42]. A previous study demonstrated that genetic ablation or pharmacological inhibition of MnSOD increases intracellular ROS levels and facilitates ferroptosis, underscoring the influence of MnSOD expression and activity on intracellular ROS homeostasis [22]. Moreover, MnSOD activity is modulated by post-translational modifications, with SIRT3 upregulation facilitating MnSOD deacetylation and subsequent activation [43]. In AAV patients, serum superoxide dismutase (SOD) levels were shown to be markedly lower than those in healthy controls, and SOD levels were correlated closely with ANCA clinical scores, inflammatory markers, and adverse prognoses [44], suggesting that MnSOD levels or activity may impact AAV disease progression. Our findings revealed decreased MnSOD activity in ANCA-GN patient sera, inversely correlating with MDA levels. Following NETs exposure in GEnCs, MnSOD transcription and protein levels remained unchanged, but MnSOD activity significantly declined. Notably, improvement in cellular ferroptosis was accompanied by an increase in MnSOD activity. In this study, NET-induced injury in both HUVECs and MRGECs was accompanied by reduced MnSOD activity. Further, siRNA-mediated inhibition of MnSOD activity exacerbated ferroptosis and endothelial cell activation injury. As Mn is an essential cofactor for MnSOD, it binds to the active site of the enzyme, enhancing its catalytic activity and antioxidant function. Mn deficiency leads to decreased MnSOD activity and compromised antioxidant capacity. Given the challenges in producing recombinant human MnSOD in prokaryotic or simple eukaryotic systems and the uncertainties associated with MnSOD gene therapy, novel strategies are needed to address these possible risks. Thus, utilizing biomaterials to appropriately elevate in vivo Mn levels, thereby maintaining MnSOD function and augmenting the antioxidant stress capacity, represents a promising therapeutic approach. Mn-based nanoparticles have emerged as a research focus in recent years for the diagnosis and treatment of various diseases [45]. Here, we demonstrated that the nanocarrier, GCNPs, alone was ineffective in inhibiting ferroptosis, whereas Mn-loaded GCNPs/Mn significantly inhibited ferroptosis and ameliorated endothelial cell damage. The mechanism underlying this protection involves the dissociation of Mn from GCNPs/Mn in the lysosome, thereby increasing the intracellular Mn levels and enhancing the MnSOD activity. Furthermore, by inhibiting both the expression and activity of MnSOD using MnSOD-specific siRNA, we observed impaired therapeutic efficacy of GCNPs/Mn, further corroborating the crucial role of MnSOD activity in GCNPs/Mn-mediated ferroptosis inhibition. Our results suggested that GCNPs/Mn inhibit the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway by upregulating the activity of MnSOD, thereby alleviating endothelial cell damage caused by NET-induced iron death. These findings highlight the potential of Mn-based nanomaterials in targeting ferroptosis and suggest avenues for the development of novel therapeutics for AAV and related diseases.

Building on the role of MnSOD, the NF-κB signaling pathway emerges as a critical downstream mediator in the regulatory network governing ferroptosis and endothelial injury. NF-κB, a key transcription factor family, orchestrates the expression of genes involved in inflammation, immune responses, and cell survival, and its dysregulation is closely linked to endothelial dysfunction in various diseases, including AAV [46]. In renal pathologies such as glomerulonephritis, NF-κB activation in endothelial cells promotes the expression of adhesion molecules and proinflammatory cytokines, exacerbating endothelial barrier disruption and leukocyte infiltration [47]. Notably, our study observed that NETs, known to drive endothelial injury in AAV, induce robust NF-κB activation in both HUVECs and MRGECs, which was associated with heightened ferroptosis and endothelial activation—consistent with reports that NF-κB signaling participates in ferroptosis regulation in renal diseases [48].

The crosstalk between MnSOD and NF-κB forms a pivotal regulatory axis in this process, with ROS acting as a central messenger. As established earlier, MnSOD deficiency elevates mitochondrial ROS by impairing superoxide anion scavenging; our data further confirmed that siRNA-mediated MnSOD knockdown increases mitochondrial ROS levels, providing direct evidence that MnSOD dysfunction amplifies oxidative stress. This is biologically significant because ROS are well-recognized activators of NF-κB: excessive ROS can trigger the phosphorylation and degradation of IκBα, releasing NF-κB dimers (e.g. p65/p50) to translocate into the nucleus and initiate target gene transcription [49]. Conversely, NF-κB can also modulate MnSOD expression: studies have shown that the p65 subunit of NF-κB binds directly to the intronic enhancer region of the MnSOD gene, enhancing its transcriptional activity and thus upregulating MnSOD expression [50]. This bidirectional regulation underscores a dynamic balance between MnSOD, ROS, and NF-κB.

In our study, this axis was functionally validated in the context of GCNPs/Mn-mediated protection. We found that GCNPs/Mn, by enhancing MnSOD activity, reduced mitochondrial ROS accumulation, thereby suppressing NF-κB activation—an effect supported by transcriptome sequencing and protein analyses. Critically, inhibiting MnSOD activity weakened the ability of GCNPs/Mn to suppress NF-κB, confirming that MnSOD is upstream of NF-κB in this pathway. Moreover, NF-κB inhibition reversed the exacerbation of ferroptosis caused by MnSOD knockdown, with concomitant changes in ferroptosis markers (e.g., increased GPX4, decreased TFR1 and COX2), highlighting the functional relevance of this axis in regulating ferroptosis.

Together, these findings delineate a coherent MnSOD/ROS/NF-κB pathway: GCNPs/Mn augments MnSOD activity to reduce mitochondrial ROS, which in turn inhibits NF-κB activation, ultimately alleviating NET-induced ferroptosis and endothelial injury. This axis not only clarifies the mechanistic basis of GCNPs/Mn efficacy but also underscores the potential of targeting this interplay for therapeutic intervention in AAV and related endothelial disorders. Nevertheless, our study, while offering valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying NET-mediated ferroptosis in GEnCs, is not without limitations. First, we were constrained by experimental conditions, and as such, were compelled to resort to MRGECs instead of human GEnCs to explore the effects of NETs. Future studies should endeavor to validate our findings using human GEnCs to ensure translational relevance. Second, we did not utilize specific inhibitors of NET formation or PAD4-knockout mice in vivo to definitively confirm the role of NETs in inducing ferroptosis in GEnCs. Additionally, data regarding expression levels of NETs are absent from our in vivo experiments, an aspect that merits further investigation in subsequent research. Although the deleterious effects of NETs on GEnC activation in AAV pathogenesis have been established, we aimed to substantiate the involvement of NET-induced ferroptosis in AAV-related renal injury through in vivo suppression of NET expression. Moreover, we only evaluated the effect of GCNPs/Mn on ferroptosis, pyroptosis and apoptosis. Their potential role in regulating other cell death pathways, such as necroptosis, has not been investigated, which warrants further exploration in future studies. Lastly, our study did not evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of GCNPs/Mn at various stages of AAV disease to ascertain the optimal timing for its administration. Further investigations are warranted to elucidate this aspect and optimize the clinical potential of GCNPs/Mn in AAV management.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrated an essential role of NETs in mediating endothelial cell injury and renal damage in AAV via the induction of ferroptosis. Notably, GCNPs/Mn effectively mitigated NET-induced ferroptosis and endothelial cell damage both in vitro and in vivo, which was most likely due to the ability of GCNPs/Mn to inhibit ferroptosis, in addition to its antioxidant properties. The therapeutic efficacy of GCNPs/Mn was found to be closely associated with its capacity to enhance MnSOD activity and inhibition of the NF-κB signaling pathway. These findings offer novel insights into the pathogenesis of AAV and present promising therapeutic strategies for this disease. Further research is warranted to elucidate the detailed mechanisms by which GCNPs/Mn modulate ferroptosis and to assess its clinical potential in AAV patients.
