Adipokine Nesfatin‐1 Mediates Endothelial Dysfunction by Suppressing MEF2B / BMPR1A During Pulmonary Vascular Remodeling
Authors: Jie Liu, Yu Guo, Jing Huang, Zhenqiang Gao, Muzhi Zhang, Yufeng Jia, Huanyu Long, Xin Xue, Hanxiao Zhang, Wenhua Shi, Cong Li, Yonghong Zhang, Shuanying Yang, Aijun Liu, Lei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHow perivascular adipose tissue (PVAT) controls vascular remodeling and perivascular inflammation during pulmonary hypertension progresses remains unknown.MethodsWestern blotting, ELISA, immunohistochemistry, and immunofluorescence were used to measure proteins expression. Sugen 5416 combined with hypoxia (SuHx) mouse models were established in C57BL/6 mice, and treated with CL‐316243. Adeno‐associated virus vector delivery was used to specifically delete nesfatin‐1 in adipocytes of lung in mice. Transthoracic echocardiography was performed to evaluate the function of right ventricle. Assays for cell angiogenesis and adhesion were used to analyze endothelial cell function. A noncontact transwell coculture model was used to evaluate the interaction between different types of cells.ResultsPVAT dysfunction participates in SuHx‐induced pulmonary vascular remodeling, PVAT exhibits features of white adipose tissue and the adipocytokine nesfatin‐1 is the key mediator. Browning of white adipose tissue via CL‐316243 treatment attenuates pulmonary hypertension phenotype in a SuHx–pulmonary hypertension mouse model. Specific deletion of nesfatin‐1 in adipocytes of lung attenuates SuHx‐induced pulmonary vascular remodeling. Nesfatin‐1 secretion is induced by IL‐17/Th17 via PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. In vitro, nesfatin‐1 promotes angiogenesis, adhesion, and apoptosis of endothelial cells. Coculture with 3T3‐L1 adipocytes promotes endothelial dysfunction mediated by nesfatin‐1. Mechanistic research shows that nesfatin‐1 mediates endothelial dysfunction by downregulating BMPR1A expression via MEF2B at the transcriptional and posttranslational levels.ConclusionsPVAT dysfunction participates in pulmonary vascular remodeling through nesfatin‐1–mediated endothelial dysfunction, suggesting that PVAT and nesfatin‐1 may constitute novel therapeutic targets for pulmonary hypertension.

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a major global health issue. Present estimates suggest a PH prevalence of ≈1% of the global population, 1 incurring a heavy economic burden. Pulmonary vascular remodeling is the key structural alteration in PH. It is not only characterized by an accumulation of different vascular cells in the pulmonary arterial wall but also by an exaggerated perivascular infiltration of immunity/inflammatory cells. 2 Current PH treatments do not specifically target pulmonary vascular remodeling and inflammation. There is an urgent need to clarify the pathobiological mechanisms underlying the vascular remodeling and the immunity/inflammation during PH. Therefore, the relentless progression of vascular remodeling continues to drive poor long‐term outcomes, underscoring the critical need to identify novel cellular targets and signaling pathways that specifically govern this process.

Both hypoxia and endothelial dysfunction are important contributing factors to the development of pulmonary vascular remodeling, 3 , 4 so combination of a vascular endothelial growth factor receptor antagonist (Sugen 5416) and chronic hypoxia (SuHx) could cause exaggerated PH with angioobliterative lesions, resembling human pulmonary arterial hypertension. 5 In this study, we use an SuHx mouse model to clarify the pathogenesis of pulmonary vascular remodeling during PH.

Recent research of PH focuses on changes in the outer layer of the vessels, 6 such as perivascular inflammation, especially T lymphocytes. Perivascular adipose tissue (PVAT) is a specialized type of adipose tissue that surrounds most mammalian blood vessels, including pulmonary vessels. It not only offers mechanical support for vessels but also regulates vascular physiology by secreting adipokines and cytokines. 7 PVAT has been confirmed to be a major contributing factor in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular diseases. 8 Perivascular inflammation could passively spread from the adventitia into PVAT, and PVAT dysfunction can promote adventitial inflammation and vascular remodeling. 9 However, how PVAT interacts with vascular remodeling and perivascular inflammation during PH progresses remains unknown.

PVAT plays a fundamental role in vascular physiology via production of adipokines. Both PVAT adipocytes and infiltrating immune cells represent important sources of the mediators. The novel adipocytokine nesfatin‐1 is identified by bioinformatic analysis in this study, which has been shown to increase arterial blood pressure in rats 10 and also cause vascular remodeling by promoting vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) proliferation in hypertensive rats. 11 However, whether nesfatin‐1 is a mediator of cross‐talk between PVAT and vascular cells/immune cells during the pulmonary vascular remodeling process remains to be investigated.

We have previously proved that endothelial dysfunction and T helper cell 17 (Th17) cell response play important role in the pulmonary vascular remodeling. 12 , 13 , 14 This study aimed to clarify the role of nesfatin‐1 in the cross‐talk between adipocyte and endothelial cell, adipocyte and Th17 cell during PH development, and the potential mechanisms. The present study may provide potential therapeutic targets for pulmonary vascular remodeling.

Bioinformatic Analysis

The gene expression data set GSE113439 was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus database and analyzed using R version 4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) in RStudio. Raw count data were imported into R and processed using the DESeq2 package version 1.50.2.

Genes with low counts across all samples were filtered out before analysis (eg, genes with total counts <10 across all samples were excluded). Library size normalization was performed using the median‐of‐ratios method implemented in DESeq2 to estimate size factors and account for differences in sequencing depth. Gene‐wise dispersion parameters were estimated, and differential expression analysis was conducted by fitting a negative binomial generalized linear model for each gene.

Wald tests were used to assess statistical significance of group differences. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate. Genes with an adjusted false discovery rate <0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change ≥1 were defined as differentially expressed genes.

Transcriptome Analysis

The clustering of the index‐coded samples, derived from human pulmonary artery endothelial cells (hPAECs), was performed on a cBot Cluster Generation System using TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3‐cBot‐HS (Illumina, San Diego, CA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After cluster generation, the library preparations were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq platform, and 150 bp paired‐end reads were generated. Differential expression analysis between the 2 groups (3 biological replicates per group) was performed using the DESeq2 R package version 1.50.2 in R version 4.4.3. Raw count data were used as input. Genes with low expression were filtered before analysis (genes with a total count <10 across all samples were excluded).

Normalization was carried out using the median‐of‐ratios method implemented in DESeq2 to estimate size factors and account for differences in sequencing depth. Dispersion parameters were estimated for each gene using the default DESeq2 pipeline, which fits gene‐wise dispersion estimates and shrinks them toward fitted values using empirical Bayes methods.

A negative binomial generalized linear model was fitted for each gene using the design formula ~group, where “group” represented the experimental condition. Wald tests were performed to evaluate statistical significance between groups. Log2 fold changes were estimated and shrunk using the default DESeq2 procedure.

Raw P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate. Genes with an adjusted false discovery rate <0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change ≥1.0 were considered differentially expressed genes.

Cell Culture and Preparation

Human pulmonary artery endothelial cells (PAECs) or mouse PAECs (ScienCell Research Laboratories, Carlsbad, CA) were cultured in culture medium (endothelial cell medium), supplemented with penicillin/streptomycin, growth supplements and 5% (v/v) FBS and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified normoxia condition (21% O2, 5% CO2, 74% N2) or hypoxia condition (3% O2, 5% CO2, 92% N2). Cells were passaged (passages 4–6) after reaching 80% to 90% confluence, detached with 0.05% trypsin, 0.04% EDTA (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) in PBS. In this study, the human PAECs were treated with rh nesfatin‐1 (10 ng/mL).

A 3T3‐L1 murine preadipocyte cell line (Procell, Wuhan, China) was cultured in DMEM (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C. 3T3‐L1 adipocyte differentiation was used to generate adipocyte‐like cells for experiments, to induce 3T3‐L1 cells to differentiate into adipocyte‐like cells. Cells were treated with isobutylmethylxanthine (0.5 mmol/L), insulin (1 mg/L), and dexamethasone (1.0 μmol/L) in DMEM complete medium for 48 hours, and then the cells were treated with insulin (1 mg/L) in DMEM complete medium for another 48 hours. 3T3‐L1 cells were characterized for adipocyte traits after differentiation, and oil Red O staining was used to visualize lipid in 3T3‐L1 cells. In this study, the 3T3‐L1 cells were treated with interleukin‐17 (10 ng/mL) or LY294002 (30 μM).

CD4+ T cells were purified from the spleen of WT C57BL/6 mice with the CD4 T cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Gaithersburg, MD) by using an AutoMACS magnetic cell sorter (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Purified cells were activated with soluble anti‐CD3ε:CD28 (1 μg/mL; BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA) on 24‐well plates (106/well). Th17‐skewing condition was as follows: 30 ng/mL interleukin‐6, 2.25 ng/mL transforming growth factor‐β, 20 ng/mL interleukin‐1β, 30 ng/mL interleukin‐23, 1 μg/mL anti–interferon‐γ, and 2.5μg/mL anti–interleukin‐4.

Human embryonic kidney cells (HEK293T; ATCC, Gaithersburg, MD) were cultured using DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. HEK293T cells were used for coimmunoprecipitation, ubiquitination, and fluorescent reporter gene assays. All cells used in experiments were between passages 4–7.

Tube Formation Assay

To detect the PAECs tube formation ability, a tube formation assay was performed. BD matrigel (BD Biosciences) was distributed in a 24‐well plate (200 μL/well) on ice and allowed to solidify at 37 °C for at least 30 minutes. After the matrigel mixture solidified, PAECs (1 × 105 per well) were gently added to each of the triplicate wells. Tube formation was quantitatively measured by calculating the total tube length and tube number of tube‐like structures using the NIS‐Elements Basic Research‐Microscope Imaging Software (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Tracks of endothelial cells that were organized into networks of cellular cords were counted in 6 random fields.

Adhesion Assay

Differently treated cells were trypsinized and seeded on top of fibronectin (30 μL 20 μg/mL; BD Biosciences)‐coated 96‐well plate (1 × 104 per well in 5% FBS and growth factor–free endothelial cell medium) and incubated for 30 minutes at 37 °C. After being washed with PBS, the adhesion cells were photographed (6 pictures per well, 6 wells for 1 group), and the numbers of adhesion cells were calculated by ImageJ software.

Cell Coculture Assay

A noncontact transwell coculture model was used to evaluate the interaction between mouse PAECs and 3T3‐L1 adipocytes or 3T3‐L1 adipocytes and splenic lymphocytes. For PAECs and 3T3‐L1 adipocyte coculture, PAECs were cultured in the lower compartment and 3T3‐L1 adipocytes were cultured in the upper compartment, tube formation ability, adhesion ability, and apoptosis of PAECs were evaluated after 24 hours of coculture with different treatments. For 3T3‐L1 adipocytes and splenic lymphocytes coculture, splenic lymphocytes were cultured in the lower compartment, and 3T3‐L1 adipocytes were cultured in the upper compartment, after 24 hours of coculture with different treatments; nesfatin‐1 in the upper compartment was measured.

Small Interfering RNA Preparation and Treatment

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) oligonucleotides with 2 thymidine residues at the 3′ end of the sequence were purchased from GenePharma (Suzhou, China). Cells were transiently transfected with siRNA in Opti‐MEM medium (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen), according to the manufacturer's recommendation. Briefly, for transfection of siRNA, cells at 50% to 70% confluency were transfected with small interfering nesfatin‐1 or negative control using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen; 13 778 150) according to manufacturer's protocol. Twenty microliters of mixture containing siRNA (20 pM for each) and transfection reagents (5 μL for each) were added to the cells for 6 hours at 37 °C and then replaced with fresh culture medium. The sequences are as follows:

Small interfering nesfatin‐1: sense: 5′‐GCAGACAUAGAGGAGAUAATT‐3′, antisense: 5′‐UUAUCUCCUCUAUGUCUGCTT‐3′;

siRNA negative control: sense: 5′‐UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT‐3′, antisense: 5′‐ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT‐3′.

Terminal Deoxynucleotidyl Transferase dUTP Nick End Labeling Assay

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling BrightRed Apoptosis Detection Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) was used to reflect the level of cell apoptosis. According to the manufacturer's protocols: (1) fixation: fix the slide with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes, and wash the slide with PBST 3 times, 3 minutes each time; (2) cell permeabilization: 0.2% Triton X‐100 (diluted with 1 × PBS) permeabilize at room temperature for 5 minutes, and wash the slide with PBS 3 times, 3 minutes each time; (3) equilibration: add 1 × equilibration buffer (dilute 5 × equilibration buffer with deionized water at 1:10) to the permeabilized slide, and balance at room temperature for 15 minutes. Wash the slices with PBS 3 times, 3 minutes each time; (4) add reaction working solution: remove 1 × equilibration buffer with absorbent paper, and then add reaction working solution to the slide, and incubate at 37 °C for 60 minutes; (5) restain the nucleus: add DAPI and incubate in the dark for 5 minutes, stain the slide, and wash off the excess DAPI with PBS for 5 minutes × 4 times; (6) mount: Drop the antifade mounting medium on the slide, and stick the side of the slide with cells on it. First lay one side flat, and then gently put down the other side to avoid bubbles; (7) microscope photography: the slide can be observed or imaged under a fluorescence microscope.

Flow Cytometric Analysis of Apoptosis

Fluorescein isothiocyanate Annexin V and Propidium Iodide Apoptosis Kit (Keycell, Wuhan, China) is used to detect the externalization of phosphatidylserine in apoptotic cells. Briefly, cells are digested and collected with EDTA‐free trypsin; wash the cells twice with PBS (800g centrifugal force, centrifuge for 5 minutes) to collect 1 to 5×105 cells; add 500 μL binding buffer and gently blow to form a single cell suspension; add 5 μL annexin V–fluorescein isothiocyanate and mix well; then, add 5 μL propidium iodide and mix well; react at room temperature, avoid light, for 5 to 10 minutes; observe and detect by flow cytometer within 1 hour.

Animal Models

The experimental animals were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Capital Medical University (Approval Nos. 2022–1026) and conducted in strict accordance with the Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals. All procedures also conformed to the guidelines of Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes. The study adhered to the 3R principles (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement) to ensure the ethical and scientific integrity of animal research.

Animals were housed in accordance with the regulations for the Management of Laboratory Animals published by the Ministry of Science and Technology of the People's Republic of China. Eight‐week‐old male C57BL/6 mice weighing 20 to 25 g were bought from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Company (Beijing, China). All the mice were housed in a 12‐hour light/12‐hour dark cycle, and food and water were available ad libitum. Mice were allowed to acclimate for 3 days and were then exposed to normobaric hypoxia (10% O2) in an airtight plexiglass chamber (Attendor110 Hariolab; Hua Yue Medical Technology Co., Guangzhou, China), combined with a subcutaneous injection of Sugen 5416 (20 mg/kg, once weekly) for 4 weeks. This treatment defined the SuHx group, representing a Sugen 5416 plus hypoxia–induced PH mouse model. Control mice, referred to as the control at 4 weeks (C4W) group, were maintained under room air conditions for the same duration. All animals were anesthetized with isoflurane (3% for induction, 1.5% for maintenance), administered via inhalation using a calibrated vaporizer. For terminal procedures, an overdose of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg) was administered by intraperitoneal injection. All efforts were made to minimize the suffering of the animals.

Adeno‐Associated Virus Vector Delivery

pHBAAV‐FABP4‐m‐Nucb2 (nesfatin‐1) short hairpin RNA‐ZsGreen and pHBAAV‐FABP4‐ZsGreen control vector were purchased from Hanbio Tech (Shanghai, China). The genomic titer was 1.7 to 1.8×1012 genomic copies/mL determined by quantitative polymerase chain reaction. For delivering adeno‐associated virus (AAV)‐Nucb2 (nesfatin‐1) short hairpin RNA, male C57BL6 mice (8 weeks old) were administered AAV‐delivering or AAV empty capsid as negative control as previously described. 15 AAV vectors were administered in a volume of 100 μL with a titer of 1×1011 viral genome copies of warm sterile saline via intratracheal injection followed by anesthesia. After the treatment, mice were placed on a warm pad until they woke up. Then, the model of hypoxia‐induced PH began to be generated.

Measurement of the Right Ventricular Systolic Pressure

Mice were anesthetized with 2% pentobarbital (50 mg/kg IP). Right ventricular (RV) systolic pressure was used as an indicator for the mean pulmonary arterial pressure. Our laboratory usually used the method of closed‐chest insertion into the RV to measure the RV systolic pressure. A 22‐gauge needle connected to a pressure transducer, which was interfaced with the PowerLab system (AD Instruments, Sydney, Australia), was inserted into the RV of anesthetized animals through a xiphocostal angle approach; the waveform was used to confirm the position of the needle. Data were recorded using the Chart program, which was included with the PowerLab system. The mice were then euthanized, and the hearts and lungs were collected.

Lung Tissue Preparation and Morphometric Analysis

The left lungs embedded in paraffin were serially sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm for standard hematoxylin and eosin staining, immunohistochemistry, and immunofluorescence. Pulmonary vessels with external diameters <100 μm were selected. Medial thickness was counted as follows: percent medial thickness=(circumferenceext/π−circumferenceint/π)/(circumferenceext/π)×100. The circumferenceext and circumferenceint were demarcated by the external and internal elastic lamina. Images of pulmonary vessels were captured with a Nikon microscope digital camera system (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), and circumferences were measured with its image analysis program.

Measurement of RV Hypertrophy

Heart tissue was removed. The RV free wall was dissected from the left ventricle and ventricular septum. The RV hypertrophy index was calculated by the wet weight ratio of the RV to the left ventricle plus septum as the formula: RV/(left ventricle +septum)×100%. The ratio of RV weight to body weight was also examined. For wheat germ agglutinin staining, slides were incubated for 30 minutes with 5 μM wheat germ agglutinin dying (σ) in the dark. The cell nuclei were stained with DAPI. The slides were sealed with antifade mounting medium and then observed using a fluorescence microscope.

Echocardiography

Echocardiography was performed using the Vevo 2100 high‐resolution imaging system (FUJIFILM VisualSonics, Toronto, Canada) equipped with an 18‐ to 38‐MHz (MS400, mouse cardiovascular) scan head. Transthoracic echocardiography was performed to measure pulmonary valve (PV) peak velocity, pulmonary acceleration time, RV free wall thickness (RVWT), and tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE). In brief, we used M‐mode from the right parasternal short‐axis view to measure the RV parameters at the end of a diastole. To determine TAPSE, the M‐mode cursor was oriented to the junction of the tricuspid valve plane and the RV free wall using the apical 4‐chamber view. The peak velocity of the blood flowing through the PV and pulmonary acceleration time is determined at the tips of the PV cusps.

Immunofluorescence Staining

For propidium iodide dye experiments, cells were stained with 5 μl Hoechst33342 and 5 μl propidium iodide and coincubated for 20 to 30 minutes at 4 °C. Images were taken using a biological microscope (BX53; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). For immunofluorescent staining of lung sections for perilipin 1, CD31, and α‐smooth muscle actin, 4‐μm thick serial sections of paraffin‐embedded lung tissue were overnight incubated with primary antibodies for perilipin 1 (rabbit, 1:200 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA), CD31 (rabbit, 1:200 dilution; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and α‐smooth muscle actin (rabbit, 1:200 dilution; Boster Biological Technology, Pleasanton, CA) at 4 °C, and then incubated with secondary antibodies, Dylight 594– and Dylight 488–conjugated secondary antibodies (1:200, EarthOx Life Sciences, Burlingame, CA) for 1 hour at room temperature. After washing in PBS, sections were counterstained with DAPI and mounted using fluorescent mounting medium. Images were taken using a confocal laser scanning microscope (TCS SP8 STED; Wetzlar, Germany).

Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay

ELISA was conducted to detect nesfatin‐1 protein levels according to the manufacturer's protocols. The collected samples and standards were prepared, and then they were added into the appropriate wells, respectively. Detection Reagent A, Detection Reagent B, and Substrate Solution were added for incubation for 30 minutes at 37 °C, respectively. The reactions were terminated with stop solution, and the optical density at 450 nm was measured with a microplate reader (STNERGY/H4, BioTek, Winooski, Vermont; E‐EL‐H2373, Elabscience, Wuhan, China).

Western Blot

Proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% nonfat dry milk for 1 hour, followed by incubation in nesfatin‐1 (rabbit, 1:2000 dilution; NOVUS, St. Charles, MO), UCP‐1 (mouse, 1:800 dilution; NOVUS), PR domain containing 16 (rabbit, 1:2000 dilution; Bioss, Beijing, China), cell death–inducing DNA fragmentation factor α–like effector A (CIDEA) (rabbit, 1:1000 dilution; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), leptin (rabbit, 1:1000 dilution; Bioworld, Bloomington, MN), p‐PI3K (rabbit, 1:1000 dilution; Affinity Biosciences, Cincinnati, OH), PI3K (rabbit, 1:1000 dilution; Goodhere Biological Technology, Hangzhou, China), phosphorylated AKT (rabbit, 1:3000 dilution; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), AKT (rabbit, 1:600 dilution; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), phosphorylated mTOR (mammalian target of rapamycin) (rabbit, 1:1000 dilution; Bioss, Beijing, China), mTOR (rabbit, 1:1000 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA), Bcl‐2 (rabbit, 1:1500 dilution; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), Bax (rabbit, 1:8000 dilution; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), MEF2B (rabbit, 1:500 dilution; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), BMPR1A (rabbit, 1:500 dilution; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), UBE2C (rabbit, 1:500 dilution; Abcam, Cambridge, MA), UBR7 (rabbit, 1:500 dilution; Abcam), GAPDH (rabbit, 1:1000 dilution; Goodhere Biological Technology, Hangzhou, China) and β‐actin (mouse, 1:2000 dilution; Zhongshan gold bridge, China) antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After the overnight incubation, for chemiluminescence detecting, membranes were washed with tris buffered saline with tween buffer and reincubated with a goat antirabbit or mouse immunoglobulin G–HRP secondary antibody. The membranes were washed with tris buffered saline with tween buffer and reacted with the electrochemiluminescence substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and exposed to x‐ray film. For fluorescent detecting, the membrane was then treated with IRDyeTM800 (green) or IRDyeTM680 (red) conjugated affinity purified anti‐rabbit/mouse immunoglobulin G (1:10000 dilution, LI‐COR, Lincoln, NE). The positive Western bands were visualized using a LI‐COR Odyssey infrared double‐fluorescence imaging system (LI‐COR). The value of the relative density of each target protein band was normalized to the density of the corresponding GAPDH/β‐actin band.

Coimmunoprecipitation

Interactions were validated in HEK293T cells transfected with exogenous plasmids expressing GFP (green fluorescent protein)‐tagged MEF2B, 2×Myc‐tagged UBE2C, V5‐tagged UBR7, 3×Flag‐tagged BMPR1A. After transfection for 48 hours, the cells were lysed using cell lysis with a protease inhibitor cocktail. After centrifugation for 15 minutes at 11800g and 4 °C, supernatant was collected, with 100 μL of supernatant usually being removed to be treated as the “input.” Then, the remaining supernatant was incubated with agarose immunoprecipitation beads for tag combination at 4 °C overnight on a rotary mixer. The beads were then washed 3 times with high‐salt wash buffer and 3 times with low‐salt wash buffer. Boil with 2×loading buffer for 10 minutes to elute the precipitated proteins from the beads. Then, immunoprecipitated proteins were subjected to western blotting.

Ubiquitination Analysis

Ubiquitination assays were performed using HEK293T cells transfected with exogenous plasmids expressing hemagglutinin–ubiquitin (K48), GFP‐tagged MEF2B, 2×Myc‐tagged UBE2C, V5‐tagged UBR7, or 3×Flag‐tagged BMPR1A. After 48 hours of transfection (with the cell‐permeable proteasome inhibitor MG132 added to the cell cultures during the last 6 hours), the cells were harvested and lysed using cell lysis buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail. The lysates were immediately boiled for 10 minutes in the presence of 1% SDS, followed by dilution with lysis buffer until the SDS concentration reached 0.1%. Cell extracts were immunoprecipitated with Flag‐specific beads to enrich the BMPR1A protein combined with ubiquitin chains. Precipitated proteins were analyzed by immunoblotting with an anti‐hemagglutinin antibody.

Fluorescent Reporter Gene Assay

To investigate the regulatory role of MEF2B gene to BMPR1A gene promoter, a fluorescent reporter gene assay was performed. Briefly, BMPR1A gene promoter region (500–2000 bp upstream of the transcription start site) was cloned into a fluorescent reporter plasmid (pGL3‐Basic). HEK293T cells were cotransfected with pGL3‐BMPR1A‐promoter and MEF2B overexpression plasmid (pcDNA3.1 [+]). After transfection for 48 hours, Firefly and Renilla luciferase were determined using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay System.

Statistical Analysis

Data were presented as mean± SD. Comparison between the groups of data was evaluated using the Student's unpaired t test. For multiple comparisons, 1‐way or 2‐way ANOVA was used with a Bonferroni post hoc test. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The experiments were performed at least in 3 independent assays under similar conditions.

Results

PVAT Dysfunction in SuHx‐Induced Pulmonary Vascular Remodeling

Adipose tissues classified into white (WAT) and brown adipose tissue (BAT) on the basis of function and morphology in mammals. 16 , 17 WAT has only a small amount of cytoplasm and monocular lipid droplets, and cell size is large, whereas BAT has multilocular lipid droplets and a high concentration of mitochondria expressing UCP1 (uncoupling protein 1), and the cell size is comparatively small. In vascular pathologies, PVAT increases in volume and becomes dysfunctional, and PVAT dysfunction in PH is unknown. In our study, we observed morphological changes in PVAT under hypoxic conditions. Perilipin 1, a lipid droplet‐associated protein highly expressed in adipocytes, was used in Figure 1A to specifically identify and localize adipose tissue in PVAT. In the SuHx‐induced PH mouse model, the size of adipocytes in PVAT was markedly increased compared with the C4W group (Figure 1A). BAT‐specific protein UCP1, CIDEA (Cell Death‐Inducing DFFA‐Like Effector A), and transcriptional regulator PRDM‐16 (PR domain containing 16) were also downregulated in SuHx–PH mouse model, while the WAT‐specific protein leptin was upregulated (Figure 1B). These results indicate that PVAT exhibits features of WAT during PH development.

Upregulation of Nesfatin‐1 in PH

The function of PVAT adipocytes depends on the expression and release of adipokines. 18 PVAT‐derived adipokines are crucial in maintaining the homeostasis of the cardiovascular system by endocrinal regulation of different cell types, so we explore the potential key adipokine that mediates pulmonary vascular remodeling. Previous studies have identified >20 known adipokines that participate in cardiovascular disease. 19 To assess their expression in PH, the data set GSE113439 from the Gene Expression Omnibus database was analyzed. Among these adipokines, only nesfatin‐1 was found to be upregulated in patients with pulmonary arterial hypertension (Figure 1C). We confirm that nesfatin‐1 was upregulated in the SuHx–PH mouse model, and 3T3‐L1 adipocytes secreted more nesfatin‐1 under hypoxia condition (Figure 1D). Additionally, ELISA analysis showed that serum levels of nesfatin‐1 were significantly elevated in patients with pulmonary arterial hypertension compared with those of controls (Figure 1E). These results indicate that nesfatin‐1 is a crucial adipokine in the pathogenesis of PH.

From the above results, we know that PVAT exhibits features of WAT, then we explore that role of browning of WAT in the pulmonary vascular remodeling of SuHx–PH mouse model. CL‐316243 is a selective adrenergic receptor‐β3 agonist could induce browning of adipocytes in vitro and WAT browning in rodents, 20 we also proved that activation of adrenergic receptor‐β3 agonist effectively induces browning in both normal and disease‐model conditions (Figure S1). CL‐316243 alleviated the PH phenotype of the SuHx mouse model, indicated by a decrease in RV systolic pressure relative to DMSO treatment. Vascular remodeling of PH is mainly seen in distal pulmonary arterioles, so the percent medial thickness of <100 μm intrapulmonary arterioles was calculated. Pulmonary morphometrics displayed extenuated pulmonary vascular remodeling in the SuHx–adrenergic receptor‐β3 agonist group relative to the SuHx group (Figure 2A). Less than100 μm intrapulmonary arteriole remodeling was obvious in the SuHx model, and CL‐316243 significantly reversed pulmonary vascular remodeling indicated by an obvious decrease in percent medial thickness in CL‐316243‐treated arterioles <100 μm.

Increased Nesfatin‐1 Secretory Mechanism

The nesfatin‐1 secretion level increases in the SuHx–PH model, but its secretory mechanism remains to be investigated. In cardiovascular disease, PVAT becomes a metabolically active synthetic tissue that produces proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines and immune/inflammatory cells infiltrate in PVAT, including T cells, B cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, natural killer cells, and the like. 21 , 22 Our previous study demonstrated the crucial role of Th17 cells in the pulmonary vascular remodeling 12 ; therefore, we speculate that Th17 cells infiltrating in PVAT may be involved in the secretion of nesfatin‐1 by adipocytes. Our results showed that coculture with splenic lymphocytes promoted nesfatin‐1 secretion by 3T3‐L1 adipocytes, and Th17 differentiation of splenic lymphocytes could promote nesfatin‐1 secretion further (Figure 4A). When treating 3T3‐L1 adipocytes with interleukin‐17 (10 ng/mL), the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway was activated and nesfatin‐1 was upregulated, while PI3K inhibitor LY294002 (30 μM), which can block PI3K/AKT signaling, reversed the interleukin‐17–induced nesfatin‐1 upregulation (Figure 4B and 4C). These results demonstrate that Th17/interleukin‐17 promotes nesfatin‐1 secretion of 3T3‐L1 adipocytes via PI3K/AKT/mTOR activation.

Nesfatin‐1 Mediates Endothelial Dysfunction by Downregulating BMPR1A via MEF2B at the Transcriptional and Posttranslational Levels

Finally, we investigated the molecule mechanism of nesfatin‐1–induced endothelial dysfunction. Illumina RNA sequencing was used to profile the transcriptome in PAECs treated with nesfatin‐1 to gain insight into nesfatin‐1's direct effects on the endothelial function. PAECs treated with or without nesfatin‐1 were sequenced, mapped, analyzed, and annotated. The DESeq R package (1.10.1) and DEGSeq R package (1.12.0) were used to identify the differential expressed genes, among the downregulated genes. We have identified a key gene, BMPR1A, in the pathogenesis of PH (Figure 7A). BMPR1A is key to maintain endothelial identity and to prevent excessive endothelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition, 23 indicating that nesfatin‐1 induced endothelial dysfunction by downregulating BMPR1A. Notably, the transcription factor MEF2B was concomitantly downregulated with BMPR1A in the nesfatin‐1–treated group (Figure 7A), suggesting that nesfatin‐1 may downregulate BMPR1A via MEF2B. We confirmed the expression patterns of BMPR1A and MEF2B by real‐time polymerase chain reaction and western blot assay both in vitro (Figure 7B) and in vivo (Figure S4), and nesfatin‐1 indeed induced downregulation of BMPR1A and MEF2B in endothelial cells, and BMPR1A and MEF2B were also downregulated in SuHx–PH mouse model. Taken together, our data indicated that nesfatin‐1 might transcriptionally control BMPR1A through MEF2B during endothelial dysfunction.

To test above hypothesis, we searched JASPAR (http://jaspar.genereg.net) to find putative binding sites in the BMPR1A promoter for MEF2B. JASPAR analysis reveals potential binding sites, which are quantitatively scored on the basis of the probability that each nucleotide is observed at each position in the binding motif versus the consensus sequence for known binding sites. Sequence analysis by JASPAR showed 5 putative binding sites totally. The top 2 binding regions of MEF2B in the promoter sequence of BMPR1A are shown in Figure 7C. Based on this potential binding site, BMPR1A promoter was constructed into the pGL3‐basic vector. The western blotting result showed that, compared with the pcDNA3.1–3xflag group, MEF2B‐3xflag markedly enhanced the expression of MEF2B in HEK‐293 T cell. Compared with the vector+pGL3‐basic group, the luciferase activity of the vector+pGL3‐BMPR1A‐promoter group and the MEF2B+pGL3‐BMPR1A‐promoter group were significantly elevated. Compared with the vector+pGL3‐BMPR1A‐promoter group, the luciferase activity of the MEF2B+pGL3‐BMPR1A‐promoter group was further increased. These data suggest that MEF2B can directly bind to the BMPR1A promoter and participate in BMPR1A transcription (Figure 7D). Therefore, these findings indicated that MEF2B controlled the expression of BMPR1A by regulating its transcription. In vitro, nesfatin‐1–induced endothelial dysfunction was reversed by MEF2B overexpression (Figure 7E and 7F).

The above results indicated that MEF2B regulates BMPR1A at the transcriptional level. Meanwhile, we have also observed that among the upregulated genes, the E2 ubiquitin‐conjugating enzyme UBE2C and E3 ubiquitin ligase UBR7 were upregulated, which was divergent from the MEF2B expression pattern (Figure 8A), the expression patterns of UBE2C and UBR7 were confirmed by western blot assay in vivo (Figure 8B). At the same time, we also discovered UBE2C and UBR7 were upregulated in SuHx–PH mouse model (Figure S4). Within the same experimental group, DMSO treatment did not affect the half‐life degradation rate of BMPR1A protein, and combined treatment with cycloheximide accelerated the degradation rate of BMPR1A protein, indicating a relatively shorter half‐life of BMPR1A. UBR7 promoted the degradation of BMPR1A by shortening its half‐life, and UBE2C further enhanced UBR7‐mediated degradation of BMPR1A. The MG132 chase assay showed that UBR7 decreased the half‐life of endogenous BMPR1A protein after transfecting plasimid of UBR7 into PAECs (Figure 8C). Cotransfection of UBE2C and UBR7 further weakened the half‐life process of BMPR1A. From this, we deduced that MEF2B is likely to modulate BMPR1A via the ubiquitin‐proteasome pathway at the posttranslational level. To demonstrate whether BMPR1A bound to UBR7, UBE2C, and MEF2B, we performed a coimmunoprecipitation assay. The input results showed that the plasmids MEF2B‐GFP, UBE2C‐2xMyc, UBR7‐V5, and BMPR1A‐3xFlag were all successfully expressed (Figure 8D). The anti‐Flag antibody was used to perform coimmunoprecipitation assay on HEK‐293 T cells transfected with MEF2B‐GFP, UBE2C‐2 × Myc, UBR7‐V5, and BMPR1A‐3 × Flag (Figure 8E). The BMPR1A‐3 × Flag protein was markedly precipitated under UBR7, UBE2C, and MEF2B protein. Next, coimmunoprecipitation assay using anti‐Myc antibody revealed that UBE2C‐2xMyc protein were found to be precipitated by the UBR7, BMPR1A, and MEF2B protein (Figure 8F). Similarly, results using anti‐GFP antibody revealed that MEF2B‐GFP proteins were precipitated by UBR7, UBE2C, and BMPR1A proteins (Figure 8G); coimmunoprecipitation assays using anti‐V5 antibody revealed that UBR7‐V5 proteins were precipitated by UBE2C, BMPR1A, and MEF2B proteins (Figure 8H). In conclusion, the results demonstrated that the BMPR1A protein interacted with UBR7, UBE2C, and MEF2B protein.

Discussion

In this study, to our knowledge, we are the first to investigate the role of PVAT in the SuHx‐induced pulmonary vascular remodeling development and its potential mechanism. We demonstrated PVAT dysfunction in SuHx‐induced pulmonary vascular remodeling, and the adipocytokine nesfatin‐1 is the key mediator. Browning of WAT via CL‐316243 treatment attenuates PH phenotype in the SuHx–PH mouse model. In addition, we showed that specific deletion of nesfatin‐1 in adipocytes of lung attenuates SuHx‐induced pulmonary vascular remodeling. Nesfatin‐1 mediated cross‐talk between 3T3‐L1 adipocytes and PAECs, participating in endothelial dysfunction. Th17/interleukin‐17 promote nesfatin‐1 secretion by 3T3‐L1 adipocytes via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. nesfatin‐1 downregulated BMPR1A via MEF2B at the transcriptional and posttranslational levels during endothelial dysfunction. Therefore, PVAT and nesfatin‐1 could be used as potential therapeutic targets in the treatment of PH.

PVAT and Vascular Disease

Adipose tissue is classified into WAT (energy storage) and BAT (thermogenic) types. PVAT surrounds blood vessels, directly communicating with the vascular wall to regulate tone and inflammation. PVAT dysfunction is linked to cardiovascular diseases, promoting vascular inflammation, remodeling, and atherosclerosis. 24 , 25 PVAT has also been confirmed to be related to neointimal hyperplasia, abdominal aortic aneurysm, and arterial stiffness and vasculitis, 26 though mechanisms require further investigation. While the role of PVAT in PH is less studied, this work demonstrates that PVAT dysfunction contributes to PH pathogenesis via interactions with vascular and immune cells.

PVAT and Vascular/Immune Cells Cross‐Talk

PVAT is essential for vascular homeostasis through bidirectional cross‐talk with blood vessels. While the traditional “inside‐out” hypothesis posits endothelial dysfunction as the initial event in vascular disease, 27 emerging evidence supports a complementary “outside‐in” paradigm where PVAT‐derived signals drive pathogenesis. PVAT‐mediated outside‐in signaling involves proinflammatory adipocytokines from adipocytes and immune cells, contributing significantly to vascular disease progression. Specifically, PVAT regulates adventitial inflammation and fibroblast dynamics and promotes VSMC proliferation through extracellular vesicle–mediated microRNA transfer. 28 Furthermore, dysfunctional PVAT facilitates intimal thickening, demonstrating intricate cross‐layer communication within the vascular wall. 29 Our research identifies PVAT‐derived nesfatin‐1 as a key mediator promoting endothelial dysfunction during pulmonary vascular remodeling in a SuHx–PH model, underscoring the importance of PVAT‐initiated outside‐in signaling in pulmonary hypertension pathogenesis.

PVAT comprises adipocytes, progenitor cells, and immune cells, forming an immunologically active niche. Adipocytes exhibit immunoregulatory capacity by stimulating immune cells to secrete inflammatory mediators, 30 while PVAT‐infiltrating T lymphocytes contribute to perivascular inflammation in hypertension and vascular injury. 31 Dysfunctional PVAT secretes adipocytokines and chemokines that could target T cells. 32 Among various immune infiltrates, our findings specifically implicate Th17 cells as predominant regulators of nesfatin‐1 secretion. 12 This selectivity also stems from the unique inflammatory milieu orchestrated by Th17 cells, characterized by sustained interleukin‐17A production, which has been shown to modulate adipokine expression and adipocyte metabolism. Moreover, interleukin‐17A can synergize with hypoxic stress to amplify local inflammatory cascades within PVAT, potentially driving nesfatin‐1 transcription. Thus, Th17‐mediated inflammation may serve as a dominant immunological cue that governs nesfatin‐1 secretion, distinguishing it from the effects of other infiltrating immune subsets. In this study, Th17/interleukin‐17 could promote adipocytes to secrete nesfatin‐1 by the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway.

Nesfatin‐1 and Vascular Cells

PVAT‐derived mediators including adipokines contribute to vascular remodeling 19 , 33 through regulating vascular cells; many adipokines are secreted from PVAT, while the role of most of them in vascular remodeling and the underlying mechanisms remain to be investigated. The novel adipokine nesfatin‐1 was reported to be involved in the process of vascular diseases with proinflammatory, proliferation, migration, and apoptotic properties. The nesfatin‐1 level was increased in coronary artery disease as an independent risk factor. 34 Current research mainly focuses on the effect of nesfatin‐1 on the VSMC. Nesfatin‐1 was upregulated in a hypertensive rat model and was expressed in VSMCs. It promoted VSMC phenotypic alteration from differentiated to dedifferentiated cells and proliferation via PI3K/Akt/mTOR and JAK2/STAT3 pathway activation 11 and also stimulated migration by activating matrix metalloproteinase‐2 and matrix metalloproteinase‐9 and downregulating peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor‐γ. 35 The association of nesfatin‐1 with vascular adventitial fibroblasts remains unclear; nesfatin‐1 could induce the production of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines in synovial fibroblasts via the PI3K/AKT pathway and the nuclear factor‐κB pathway, 36 , 37 and inhibition of nesfatin‐1 suppresses the proliferation, migration, and invasion of synovial fibroblasts in vitro. 38 It is reported that knockdown of nesfatin‐1 ameliorated neointima formation following rat carotid injury, 35 and nesfatin‐1 application could induce vasoconstriction via inhibition of nitric oxide production of endothelial cells. 39 The role of nesfatin‐1 in endothelial dysfunction remains to be investigated; our study showed that nesfatin‐1 promoted angiogenesis, adhesion, and pyroptosis of PAECs in vitro.

Nesfatin‐1 Related Intracellular Signaling

Nesfatin‐1 lacks specific receptors, and it could trigger multiple intracellular signaling cascades because of its multifaceted nature. The reported intracellular signaling cascades initiated by nesfatin‐1 include the AMP‐activated protein kinase signaling pathway, mitogen‐activated protein kinase signaling pathway, corticotropin‐releasing factor signaling pathways, melanocortin signaling pathway, ion currents, nitric oxide–cGMP system, AKT signaling pathway, and so on. 40 To the best of our knowledge, we have discovered for the first time that nesfatin‐1 impairs BMP (bone morphogenetic protein) signaling, potentially resulting in pulmonary vascular remodeling. As for the molecular mechanisms underlying MEF2B‐mediated regulation of BMPR1A, first, our findings demonstrate that MEF2B, as a transcription factor, directly governs BMPR1A expression; second, MEF2B modulates BMPR1A through the ubiquitination pathway. Our results show that MEF2B suppresses UBE2C/UBR7‐mediated ubiquitination degradation of BMPR1A. In summary, when MEF2B is downregulated by nesfatin‐1, reduced transcription coupled with enhanced ubiquitination‐mediated degradation collectively drive BMPR1A downregulation. In this study, we showed that nesfatin‐1 downregulated BMPR1A via MEF2B at the transcriptional and posttranslational levels. These findings significantly expand our understanding of nesfatin1's biological functions beyond its well‐characterized roles in appetite suppression and energy homeostasis, positioning it as a critical modulator of BMP signaling pathways with potential implications in PH pathogenesis, but nesfatin‐1 may be involved in the regulation of various signaling pathways. Further research is needed to fully understand its exact cascades.

PVAT as a Potential Target for PH

PVAT contains both WAT and BAT. PVAT can be WAT‐like, BAT‐like, or mixed adipose tissue. Promoting PVAT browning might have a protective effect on vascular health. 41 WAT can be converted into beige adipose tissue, which expresses UCP1 and resembles BAT morphologically; this phenomenon is referred to as “browning.” There are currently a few strategies that are able to induce browning in WAT, including cold challenge or the application of growth factors such as fibroblast growth factor 21, atrial natriuretic peptide, BMPs, and specific β3‐adrenergic receptor agonist (CL316243). 42 Activating BAT and inducing browning of WAT can be a novel therapeutic strategy for obesity and type 2 diabetes by improving glycolipid metabolism and insulin resistance. 43 Browning of PVAT prevented the development of atherosclerotic plaques and hypertension in mouse models. 7 , 44 In this study, we demonstrate PVAT of an SuHx–PH mouse model exhibits a white‐like phenotype, and the induction white‐to‐brown transition of white‐like PVAT attenuates PH phenotype in an SuHx–PH mouse model significantly, suggesting browning adipose tissue could be a novel therapeutic strategy for PH.

A limitation of the present study is the relatively small sample size (eg, n=3 per group), which may affect the robustness of statistical inference. In this context, the use of ANOVA relies on large‐sample approximations and assumptions such as normality and homogeneity of variance, which may not be fully met. Therefore, the resulting P values should be interpreted with caution, as they may not accurately reflect the true level of statistical significance. To address this concern, we additionally performed an exact permutation test, which does not depend on distributional assumptions and is more suitable for small‐sample analyses. Notably, the results obtained from the permutation test were consistent with those from the ANOVA, supporting the reliability of our findings. Nevertheless, future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to further validate these results and strengthen the statistical power of the analysis.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the present study indicates that PVAT dysfunction participates in pulmonary vascular remodeling in a mouse SuHx–PH model through nesfatin‐1–mediated endothelial dysfunction. Our study also establishes nesfatin‐1 as a novel functional regulator of BMPR1A via MEF2B, operating at both transcriptional and post‐translational levels. These findings open new avenues for targeting the nesfatin‐1–MEF2B–BMPR1A axis in PH associated with BMP signaling dysregulation. In addition, this study showed that BAT could be a potential therapeutic strategy for PH. Therefore, our study suggested that PVAT and associated adipokines may constitute a novel therapeutic target for PH.
