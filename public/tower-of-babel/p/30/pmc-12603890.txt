Pure Red Cell Aplasia Associated With Thymic Tumors, a Nationwide Retrospective Study
Authors: Mylène Hemmer, Sylvain Moinard, Olivier Lambotte, Marion Malphettes, Louis Terriou, Bertrand Lioger, Guillaume Le Guenno, Jean‐François Viallard, Marc Michel, Hervé Lobbes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPure red cell aplasia (PRCA) is the most frequent autoimmune cytopenia associated with thymic tumors (TTs). In a nationwide retrospective study, we included 41 patients (22 women, median age 62 years). At PRCA diagnosis, the mean hemoglobin level was 6.6 ± 2.1 g/dL, and the reticulocyte count was 6 ± 5 × 109/L. PRCA was diagnosed before TT (8%, median delay 52 months), simultaneously (46%) or after TT (46%, median delay 34 months). Fourteen patients (34%) had definite Good syndrome. Thymectomy without immunosuppressive treatment provided a single sustainable PRCA response. Twenty‐two patients (54%) experienced multiple PRCA relapses (Median 2). When PRCA was present at TT diagnosis, the risk of PRCA relapse was higher (p < 0.01), while TT staging, TT relapse, and Good syndrome were not associated with PRCA relapses. Corticosteroids led to a 77% initial response rate with frequent relapses during taper or at discontinuation. Cyclosporine A provided a 71% response rate. Overall response rates and time to response were similar with corticosteroids and cyclosporine A. Sirolimus led to a 50% response rate in refractory cases. Severe infectious events requiring hospitalizations were frequent (44%). After a mean follow‐up of 50 months, five patients (12%) died, three of whom died from TT relapse. Good syndrome was not significantly associated with an increased risk of infection, PRCA relapse, or death. Our findings highlight the severe phenotype of the association of TT and PRCA. While most patients achieve PRCA response under immunosuppressive therapy, high infection incidence and thymoma relapse are responsible for severe morbidity.

Introduction

Pure red cell aplasia (PRCA) is a rare bone marrow failure condition characterized by isolated normocytic normochromic anemia with a low reticulocyte count and, by definition, a profound reduction of erythroblast precursors (< 5%) [1]. Epidemiological data have mostly been derived from Asian cohorts with an estimated annual incidence of 1.06 per million [2]. PRCA can be congenital (Blackfan‐Diamond Anemia) or acquired.

Thymic tumors (TTs) are the most frequent solid cancers associated with PRCA [3]: TT are found in 10% of patients with PRCA, whereas approximately 2%–5% of patients with TT have PRCA [4]. In a recent large database from Korea, among second PRCA forms, thymoma was the second most frequent condition after rheumatologic diseases [5]. Thymoma can be associated with a wide variety of autoimmune manifestations, including myasthenia gravis (MG), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), encephalitis, and polymyositis [6]. The course and outcome of TT‐associated PRCA and the best therapeutic strategy remain largely unknown due to the rarity of this condition [7]. Our study aims to describe the features of TT‐PRCA patients, the disease course and prognosis, including the efficacy and safety of treatments.

Methods

This study is part of the French National PRCA cohort (“EPIC‐F: érythroblastopénies auto‐immunes, cohorte nationale Française”) set up in 2020 and now including 134 adult patients with acquired PRCA. French hematology and internal medicine departments from the research network on autoimmune cytopenia were invited to include their patients in the cohort. Medical staff identified eligible patients through electronic records using both the International Classification of Diseases and the Hematological laboratory database. Data were retrospectively collected from January 1990 to June 2020, and then cases were prospectively included in the cohort.

We included adult patients (≥ 18 years old) with a diagnosis of PRCA (hemoglobin < 12 g/dL with reticulocytopenia < 10 × 109/L) and a bone marrow aspirate showing a differential erythroblast count < 5%. Parvovirus B19 infection was ruled out with serology and polymerase chain reaction in the peripheral blood and/or bone marrow aspiration. Additionally for this study, suspicion of a TT based on a diffuse enlargement of the gland on a thoracic CT scan or positron‐emission tomography was required. Pathological examination after biopsy or surgical removal was mandatory to classify the TT as thymoma, thymic hyperplasia or thymic carcinoma. Exclusion criteria were other causes of PRCAs such as drug‐induced PRCA, pregnancy‐related PRCA, ABO‐mismatch stem cell transplantation related PRCA, autoimmune PRCA without TT, or myelodysplastic related PRCA. Good syndrome was defined as the coexistence of thymoma, antibody production defect (either hypogammaglobulinemia (< 6 g/L) or reduced class or subclass immunoglobulin levels) associated with a marked reduction of CD19+ B cells (< 1% of total lymphocytes) [8, 9]. SLE was defined with the ACR/EULAR 2019 classification [10]. Patients with compatible blood flow cytometry, bone marrow infiltrate > 10% and Stat3 mutation were diagnosed with large granular lymphocyte leukemia (LGL) [11].

For PRCA, response to treatment was defined as complete response (CR) when the hemoglobin level increased above 12 g/dL with transfusion independence over a period of 8 weeks, or partial remission (PR) in the case of transfusion independence without normalization of hemoglobin. Lastly, no‐response (NR) was defined by the need for repeated transfusions. The overall response rate was defined as the proportion of patients experiencing at least PR. PRCA relapse was defined as the occurrence of anemia in patients with a CR or transfusion requirements in patients with a partial response.

TT and PRCA diagnosis were considered simultaneous if the delay between the two diagnoses was < 4 months. Surgical resection efficacy on PRCA was assessed only in patients who had a concomitant PRCA and TT diagnosis and in patients who had a TT diagnosis after the PRCA diagnosis. The immunomodulatory effect of intravenous immunoglobulin on anemia was assessed only for patients without Good Syndrome as all of them received this treatment as well as anti‐infectious prophylaxis.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.0 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria) and Stata software (version 15, Statacorp, College Station, USA). Categorical data were described with numbers (percentages). Quantitative data were described with mean (s.d.) or median (range) with interquartile range [Quartile 1: Q1—Quartile 3: Q3]. Differences between groups were assessed with Fisher's exact test for categorical data and the Mann–Whitney test for continuous data, and the log‐rank test for survival curves. Statistical significance was set at two‐tailed p < 0.05.

Ethics

The study was conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki and approved by the local Ethics Committee (IRB00013412, “CHU de Clermont Ferrand IRB #1,” IRB number 2022‐CF040) in compliance with the French policy of individual data protection.

Results

Patients' Characteristics

Forty‐one patients including 22 women (54%) representing 31% of the nationwide cohort population with a median follow‐up of 50 months were included. The main characteristics are shown in Table 1. At PRCA diagnosis, the mean hemoglobin level was 6.6 (±2.1) g/dL, the mean reticulocyte count was 6.18 (±4.9) × 109/L, and the mean erythroblast differential count on bone marrow examination was 0.89 (±1.5)%. PRCA and TT were diagnosed concomitantly in 19/41 patients (46%) while 19 patients developed PRCA after a median delay of 34 months ([Q1–Q3: 11.5–63]) after the TT diagnosis. Three patients were diagnosed with TT 52 months after PRCA.

After pathological analysis, 36 patients had a thymoma, 3 patients had a thymic hyperplasia, one patient had a squamous cell carcinoma, and one patient had a cystic lesion of indeterminate origin. Fourteen patients had Good syndrome (mean gammaglobulin 4.7 ± 1.7 g/L, range: 1.8–7 g/L on serum protein electrophoresis). The direct antiglobulin test was positive in 10/18 patients (IgG 6/10, IgG + C3d 4/10). The antinuclear antibodies titer was ≥ 1:160 in 23/35 patients, while only three patients had SLE. The positivity of antinuclear antibodies was associated with the onset of immune thrombocytopenia or autoimmune hemolytic anemia during the follow‐up (p = 0.04), while only 3 patients fulfilled the ACR/EULAR 2019 diagnosis criteria for SLE. Two patients fulfilled the diagnosis criteria for LGL.

Effect of Surgical Resection of TT on PRCA

All the patients with a concomitant PRCA and TT diagnosis underwent a surgical resection (n = 19). Among the three patients who had a TT diagnosis after the PRCA diagnosis, only two underwent a surgical procedure. Finally, among the 21 patients who were assessed for surgical procedure efficacy on the course of PRCA, only two patients experienced a response to surgery. One patient with thymic hyperplasia achieved CR after thymectomy without any additional therapy, and one patient with a thymoma achieved a CR of 5 years after thymectomy and finally experienced multiple relapses requiring immunosuppressive therapy. The 19 remaining patients who underwent surgery for TT during PRCA had already been diagnosed and required immunosuppressive therapy after surgery to manage the PRCA.

PRCA Course and Outcome

The “single flare subgroup” included 19 patients: among them, 16 responded to the firstline treatment, two patients required two lines of treatment to achieve a response and a single patient experienced treatment failure despite multiple lines of treatment. The “multiple PRCA relapses” subgroup included 22 patients (54%) who eventually responded to the first line treatment but experienced at least one relapse requiring several treatment lines (median of PRCA relapses: 2). The main characteristics are summarized in Table 2. Patients with multiple PRCA relapses had a significantly lower reticulocyte count at PRCA diagnosis (4.2 ± 3.3 vs. 8.5 ± 5.7 × 109/L, p < 0.01) and experienced the onset of other autoimmune cytopenia more frequently (p < 0.001). Age at PRCA onset, the positivity of antinuclear antibodies, direct antiglobulin test positivity, thymoma grade or thymoma relapses were not significantly different between single or multiple PRCA relapses. Serious infectious events and death rate were similar between groups. The clinical presentation, biological characteristics, and PRCA course of the 14 patients with Good syndrome was similar to the rest of the cohort.

Immunosuppressive Therapy Efficacy on PRCA

Single PRCA Flare

The single PRCA flare subgroup encompassed 18 patients who received immunosuppressive therapy and one patient who experienced a CR after thymectomy without any additional treatment. Among the 18 patients treated with immunosuppressive therapy, 15 experienced a response with the first‐line treatment including corticosteroid monotherapy n = 12, corticosteroids with intravenous immunoglobulins (2 g/kg) n = 2, and rituximab n = 1. Two patients experienced a failure of corticosteroids as first‐line and required cyclosporine A to achieve CR. A single patient experienced treatment failure despite five lines of immunosuppressive therapy including corticosteroids, cyclosporine A, mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and danazol. Figure S1 shows the response to immunosuppressive treatment. Corticosteroids were used at 1 mg/kg (mostly 60–80 mg per day) and provided an overall response rate of 80% (CR rate 53%, partial response rate 29%). Cyclosporine A (3 mg/kg, 100–150 mg per day) provided a CR rate in two out of 3 patients, and rituximab provided a CR rate in 1 of 2 patients. Three patients treated with corticosteroids as monotherapy for a median delay of 6 months experienced no relapse during the follow‐up (range: 6–171 months), while 14 patients required a low maintenance dose of corticosteroids (5–10 mg per day, follow‐up 11 months).

Cyclosporine A was used 25 times, providing an overall response rate of 72% (CR rate 56%, partial response rate 16%). Corticosteroids were used 22 times, providing an overall response rate of 77% (CR rate 54%, partial response rate 23%). As first‐line (n = 13), corticosteroids provided an 85% response rate: all the patients relapsed during tapering (n = 7, dosage < 10 mg per day) or at corticosteroid discontinuation (n = 6). Rituximab was used 6 times, providing a 50% overall response rate (partial response rate 33%, CR rate 17%). Sirolimus was used 6 times (1–2 mg per day, with a monitoring to obtain a trough concentration from 4 to 10 ng/mL), providing a response rate of 50% (CR rate 33%, partial response rate 17%) in refractory patients who experienced failure with at least two lines of treatment.

Patients receiving corticosteroids and cyclosporine A experienced the first response within a similar delay (p = 0.07), as shown in Figure 2. Other Treatments

Cyclophosphamide was used for two patients who fulfilled the LGL criteria diagnosis (bone marrow infiltrate > 10% with compatible flow cytometry and TCR rearrangement), with no response: these patients finally responded to sirolimus treatment. Two patients received azathioprine (1 CR) and two patients received mycophenolate mofetil (2 NR). Four of the five patients who received anabolic androgenic steroid (danazol) had NR; a single patient experienced CR while danazol was associated with sirolimus. Ten patients received erythropoietin as monotherapy or as an adjunct to immunosuppressive therapy, with no efficacy in any of the patients.

Mortality and Outcome

Among 38 patients who underwent thymectomy, nine patients (24%) received additional radiotherapy. Nine patients received chemotherapy (mostly cisplatin, doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide) associated with radiotherapy in two cases. In total, 16 (42%) experienced thymoma relapse. Three patients died of thymoma complications (acute respiratory distress due to thymic compression n = 1, cardiorespiratory arrest secondary to acute polyradiculoneuritis n = 1, severe flare up of MG n = 1). WHO staging and Masaoka grading were not significantly associated with an increased risk of mortality or PRCA relapse.

Twenty‐seven patients (66%) required at least two red blood transfusions, with a median number of 10 units (Q1–Q3: 6–22, maximum 190). Iron overload assessment was not reported for most patients: at the end of the follow‐up, two patients received iron chelators at the end of the follow‐up, and one patient in sustained CR underwent therapeutic phlebotomies. Overall, 64% of patients (26/41) experienced at least one adverse event, with a similar rate in patients with single or multiple PRCA relapses. Four patients experienced a warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia during follow‐up (while in remission from PRCA) with evidence of hemolysis (elevated lactate dehydrogenase, high reticulocyte count, reduced haptoglobin level): all of them had a positive direct antiglobulin test at PRCA diagnosis.

18 patients (18/41) experienced at least one severe infection, mostly pulmonary infections (47%) requiring hospitalizations and intravenous treatment. The rate of severe infections leading to hospitalization or death was similar between patients with a single flare and multiple PRCA relapses, and between patients with Good syndrome and the rest of the cohort. Two patients died of infection (cytomegalovirus pneumonia n = 1, aspergillosis n = 1). Cyclosporine A was associated with acute kidney injury in five cases (23%), leading to a decrease in the dosage. One patient discontinued Cyclosporine A because of thrombocytopenia.

Discussion

We report here the second largest cohort [7] of TT associated PRCA encompassing 41 patients with rigorous inclusion criteria and a 50‐month median follow‐up duration. TT preceded the onset of anemia in half of the cases, with a prolonged delay (median 34 months): as such, careful monitoring of blood count should be prompted even several years after the diagnosis of TTs. At PRCA diagnosis, patients were profoundly anemic, with drastic erythroblast reduction on bone marrow examinations. Patients may have positive antinuclear antibody positivity (54%) and the direct antiglobulin test may be positive (24%) even without hemolysis; thus, these characteristics should not rule out PRCA diagnosis. The proportion of patients affected by Good Syndrome [12] is a key feature of our cohort: interestingly, this condition was not associated with a more severe PRCA course nor an increase in mortality or morbidity. PRCA course is characterized by a high risk of relapses in our cohort. A lower baseline reticulocyte count was the only factor associated with the risk of relapse.

Surgical procedures were largely ineffective on PRCA, as previously reported [4, 13]. Details on surgical procedures (thymectomy versus extended thymectomy, complete resection status) were not available in most cases. However, surgical procedures remain the cornerstone of thymoma treatment as it is the main prognostic factor for thymoma‐related death [14, 15]: complete resection is strongly associated with longer survival [16] even in advanced stage [17]. Virtually all the patients required immunosuppressive therapy: the therapeutic strategy was heterogeneous, but corticosteroids and cyclosporine A were widely used. The overall response rates of corticosteroids and cyclosporine A were in line with the literature, respectively 77% and 71%. Recent evidence of excessive immune dysregulation associated with T CD8 lymphocyte activation in the bone marrow of patients with thymoma‐associated PRCA [18] may explain the good efficacy of therapies targeting T lymphocytes such as cyclosporine A [19]. We found no statistical difference in achieving a sustainable response and no difference in delay in achieving the first response between cyclosporine A and corticosteroids. However, most patients received cyclosporine A as a second‐or‐later line therapy after relapse during corticosteroid taper or after corticosteroid failure. The use of a recent nomogram model to predict the efficacy of cyclosporine could be useful to identify early patients who may benefit from this treatment to avoid corticosteroid use [20], but few data on ferritin levels and no data on CD4/CD8 lymphocyte ratio were available in our study. Cyclosporine A concentration monitoring was used by most physicians to maintain a trough concentration from 150 to 200 ng/mL. Most practitioners in our study are internists, involved in the referral network for autoimmune cytopenia, using corticosteroids as first‐line treatment by analogy with immune thrombocytopenia and autoimmune hemolytic anemia. However, frequent relapses during corticosteroid taper are in agreement with the study by Hirokawa et al. [7], highlighting the need for maintenance therapy.

The efficacy of rituximab [21] and sirolimus was rarely reported in previous studies on TT associated PRCA. A recent study highlighted the efficacy of sirolimus in patients with primary or secondary PRCA refractory or intolerant to cyclosporine A [22]. Additionally, Yang et al. reported their own experience of sirolimus efficacy as first‐line therapy in a prospective cohort [23]. In our study, sirolimus was used for six patients, leading to a 50% overall response rate, a rather elevated rate in these refractory cases. Rituximab provided an overall response rate of 50%. These results are consistent with a recent meta‐analysis of immunosuppressive efficacy [24, 25] and a recent cohort of PRCA [26].

The high prevalence of infectious events in our cohort reflects the burden of immunosuppressors since all the patients with Good syndrome received immunoglobulin replacement therapy. The incidence of infectious effects was similar regardless of the number of PRCA relapses, emphasizing the effect of continuous immunosuppressive therapy to obtain a sustainable response. However, the retrospective design of our study was responsible for missing data on anti‐infectious prophylaxis and vaccine status.

Conclusion

The association of PRCA and thymoma has long been described. However, the best therapeutic strategy remains unclear. Surgical procedures are inefficient and a significant proportion of patients experience relapses during corticosteroid tapering or cyclosporine A discontinuation. Sirolimus seems a promising alternative as a rescue therapy in refractory or multiple relapsing forms. Therapeutic escalation led to an increased risk of infections requiring careful preventive measures such as vaccinations. Further studies are required to better assess the risk– benefit ratio of sirolimus used earlier in the treatment strategy.
