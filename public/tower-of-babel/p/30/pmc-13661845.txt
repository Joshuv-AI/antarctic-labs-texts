HBO1 suppresses anti-tumor immunity of CD8⁺ T Cells in thyroid cancer by recruiting BRD4 to regulate VTCN1 expression
Authors: Yan Chen, Feng Zeng, Shan Liao, Yan Lei, Qian He, Yanhong Zhou, Yanling Li, Chen Long
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThyroid cancer is the most common endocrine malignancy. Although surgery remains the primary treatment, advanced disease frequently recurs or metastasizes, and subsequent therapies yield suboptimal outcomes. Immunotherapy holds promise, yet the underlying immune-evasion mechanisms remain incompletely understood. HBO1, a multifunctional acyltransferase, plays a significant role in tumor immune regulation. Our previous research revealed that HBO1 is highly expressed in thyroid cancer tissues, promotes malignant progression, and inhibits CD8+ T cell function. To further elucidate the role and mechanism of HBO1 in thyroid cancer immune regulation, we conducted the following investigations. Firstly, using an in vitro co-culture model analyzed by flow cytometry and ELISA assays, we found that HBO1 overexpression induces CD8+ T cell apoptosis and significantly suppresses their cytotoxic function and secretion of effector molecules. Secondly, leveraging bioinformatic analysis of CUT&Tag datasets combined with in vitro cellular experiments, we found that HBO1 was associated with increased VTCN1 expression in an H3K14 acetylation-dependent manner, thereby contributing to CD8+ T cell dysfunction. Subsequently, employing RNA-seq analysis, ChIP-qPCR, dual-luciferase reporter assays, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), and in vivo experiments, we further explored the underlying mechanism and found that HBO1 may facilitate BRD4 enrichment at the VTCN1 promoter through H3K14 acetylation, thereby promoting VTCN1 transcription and contributing to anti-tumor immune suppression in thyroid cancer. Finally, through IP-MS and Co-IP experiments, we identified the E3 ligase TRIM21 as a potential upstream regulator of HBO1. TRIM21 modulates HBO1 acetyltransferase activity by promoting its ubiquitination-mediated degradation, consequently influencing HBO1-associated immune escape in thyroid cancer. In summary, this study suggests that HBO1 may regulate VTCN1 expression through an H3K14ac-BRD4-associated mechanism in thyroid cancer, thereby contributing to suppression of CD8+ T cell anti-tumor immunity. In addition, TRIM21 may participate in the regulation of HBO1 protein stability and its associated immune-related signaling. Our findings provide new experimental evidence for understanding immune escape mechanisms and exploring potential immunotherapeutic targets in thyroid cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00262-026-04491-z.Keywords: Thyroid cancer, HBO1, BRD4, VTCN1, Anti-tumor immunity

Introduction

Thyroid carcinoma (TC) is among the most common endocrine malignancies [9, 54]. Clinically, standard management comprises surgical resection, thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) suppression, and radioactive iodine (131I) therapy [3], [9, 21, 39]. Despite generally favorable outcomes, a subset of Chinese patients develop recurrence or distant metastasis within 10 years, progressing to radioactive iodine-refractory differentiated thyroid cancer (RAIR-DTC). For these patients, therapeutic options are limited, with median survival of 3–5 years and a 10-year survival rate of ~ 10%, reflecting a dismal prognosis [2, 31].

Immunotherapy has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for advanced malignancies, including thyroid cancer [53, 58]. Current approaches for thyroid cancer primarily involve immune checkpoint inhibitors (targeting PD-1, PD-L1, and CTLA-4) and adoptive cellular therapies [13, 16, 47, 61]. While checkpoint inhibitors have improved survival in multiple tumor types, their efficacy in advanced thyroid cancer remains inconclusive and requires further clinical validation [43, 73]. Thus, elucidating the mechanisms of immune evasion, identifying novel therapeutic targets, and developing effective immunotherapeutic strategies represent critical priorities to improve outcomes for patients with refractory advanced thyroid cancer.

HBO1 (KAT7/MYST2), a core member of the MYST family of histone acetyltransferases (HATs), regulates diverse physiological processes—including DNA replication/repair, gene transcription, protein ubiquitination, immune modulation, stem cell pluripotency, and embryogenesis[33, 42], and is implicated in multiple pathologies [5, 59, 71]. Mechanistically, HBO1 primarily catalyzes histone H3 lysine 14 (H3K14ac) and H4 acetylation, promoting chromatin relaxation to facilitate DNA accessibility for replication and transcription [20, 50]. This epigenetic modification critically governs cellular proliferation, gene expression, and signal transduction [1, 10, 11, 22, 28, 29, 34, 38, 66]. Beyond its epigenetic functions, HBO1 critically regulates immune cell development and antitumor immunity [23]. During T-cell development, HBO1 directly binds the CD8 promoter, catalyzing H3K14 acetylation to activate CD8 transcription [41]. Moreover, HBO1 modulates CD8⁺ tissue-resident memory T cells (Trm) and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) through its interaction with the SCML4-BRPF2-ING4 complex, which cooperatively enhances H3K14 acetylation at immune gene loci [15]. This epigenetic mechanism positions HBO1 as a promising immunotherapeutic target for overcoming tumor immune evasion.

VTCN1 (B7-H4), a V-set domain-containing T-cell activation inhibitor 1, is primarily implicated in immune suppression. It inhibits T-cell activation, proliferation, and cytokine production, while also inducing T-cell apoptosis [62]. As a transmembrane protein, VTCN1 shares sequence homology with other B7 family members and functions as a key immune checkpoint molecule [6]. Accumulating evidence suggests that VTCN1 is overexpressed in various cancers, where it drives tumorigenesis by promoting cell proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and anti-apoptotic processes [60]. For instance, in small cell lung cancer (SCLC), elevated VTCN1 expression is associated with shorter 5-year overall survival [7]. Similarly, in lung cancer, VTCN1 enhances tumor immune escape [72]. Moreover, VTCN1 modulates the immune microenvironment through regulating the frequency and function of regulatory T cells (Tregs) [44]. In pancreatic cancer, high VTCN1 expression correlates with reduced infiltration of CD8⁺ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) [67]. Further studies demonstrate that VTCN1 expression is significantly upregulated in human thyroid cancer tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues and negatively correlates with TIL density. Additionally, high VTCN1 expression predicts poorer prognosis in thyroid cancer patients [75]. In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), VTCN1 accelerates tumor progression via the AMPK/mTOR pathway, activating epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related proteins and cancer stem cell markers [30]. Collectively, these findings highlight a consistent association between VTCN1 overexpression and impaired effector T-cell recruitment and activity across multiple malignancies. However, the mechanistic link between VTCN1 and T-cell dysfunction, particularly in thyroid cancer, remains poorly understood. Our bioinformatic analysis revealed that VTCN1 expression in thyroid cancer may be regulated by H3K14 acetylation, although the exact mechanism warrants further investigation. Notably, a bispecific antibody (BsAb) targeting VTCN1 (B7-H4)/CD3 has exhibited anti-tumor efficacy in both in vitro and in vivo models of humanized breast cancer [26]. Similarly, the VTCN1 (B7-H4)-targeted antibody–drug conjugate AZD8205 shows promising therapeutic activity in human cancer cell lines and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models [26]. Another VTCN1 (B7-H4)-directed antibody–drug conjugate suppressed tumor growth in 28 breast cancer PDX models and has advanced to Phase I clinical trials [55]. These advances underscore VTCN1 as a potential immunotherapeutic target for combination strategies in thyroid cancer.

In this study, we demonstrate for the first time that HBO1 is overexpressed in thyroid cancer tissues. Mechanistically, HBO1 recruits the transcription factor BRD4 to the transcription start site (TSS) of the immune checkpoint gene VTCN1 via H3K14 acetylation, thereby potentiating its transcription. This upregulation of VTCN1 suppresses CD8⁺ T-cell activity, facilitating tumor immune escape. Additionally, mass spectrometry analysis revealed the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM21 as an upstream negative regulator of HBO1. Notably, TRIM21 expression is downregulated in thyroid cancer tissues, resulting in impaired HBO1 degradation and consequently increased HBO1 protein stability. This leads to HBO1 accumulation, which drives its pro-tumorigenic effects. Our study systematically elucidates HBO1’s role in thyroid cancer immune evasion and its molecular mechanisms. These findings provide critical insights for identifying novel immunotherapeutic targets and designing combination therapies for advanced, radioiodine-refractory thyroid cancer.

Results

HBO1 is highly expressed in thyroid cancer and suppresses CD8⁺ T Cell function to promote immune escape

HBO1 is highly expressed in multiple cancer types and has been implicated in malignant progression [5, 59, 71]. To evaluate HBO1 expression in thyroid cancer, we first analyzed transcriptomic data from the TCGA database using TIMER2.0 (http://timer.cistrome.org/). This analysis revealed significantly higher HBO1 expression in thyroid tumor tissues than in adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1A). To validate these findings, we performed RT-qPCR and Western blotting on clinically annotated thyroid cancer specimens. Consistent with the bioinformatic data, both HBO1 mRNA (Fig. 1B) and protein (Fig. 1C) levels were markedly upregulated in tumor tissues compared to matched normal controls, supporting the observation that HBO1 is highly expressed in thyroid cancer.

To further investigate HBO1’s role in thyroid cancer progression, we generated HBO1 overexpression and knockdown constructs. Successful modulation of HBO1 expression in TPC-1 and 8505C cell lines was confirmed by RT-qPCR (Supplementary Fig. 1A-D). Functional studies revealed that HBO1 overexpression significantly enhanced thyroid cancer cell proliferation, as demonstrated by EdU incorporation assays and flow cytometry analysis, while HBO1 knockdown markedly suppressed proliferation (Fig. 1D-G). Matrigel invasion assays showed that HBO1 overexpression promoted cellular invasion, whereas its knockdown substantially inhibited this process (Fig. 1H). Wound healing assays further supported these findings: HBO1-overexpressing cells exhibited accelerated wound closure compared to controls, while HBO1 knockdown significantly impaired migratory capacity, as evidenced by wider scratch widths (Fig. 1I-J). Taken together, these results suggest that HBO1 contributes to thyroid cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and migration and is associated with malignant progression.

HBO1 plays a multifaceted role in immune regulation, having been shown to modulate CD8 gene expression via H3K14 acetylation [42] and to collaborate with the transcription factor SCML4 in regulating T cell effector molecule secretion [15]. To investigate HBO1’s potential involvement in immune modulation within the thyroid cancer microenvironment, we employed the TIMER2.0 database (http://timer.cistrome.org/) to examine the relationship between HBO1 expression and immune cell infiltration. Our analysis revealed a significant inverse correlation between HBO1 expression levels and CD8⁺ T cell infiltration in thyroid cancer (Fig. 2A). These findings suggest a potential association between elevated HBO1 expression and reduced CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration in thyroid cancer, which may contribute to tumor immune evasion.

To experimentally validate this hypothesis, we established an in vitro co-culture system. Thyroid cancer cells with verified HBO1 overexpression or knockdown were co-cultured with CD8⁺ T cells isolated from healthy donors for 48 h. Flow cytometric analysis of the harvested CD8⁺ T cells demonstrated that HBO1-overexpressing thyroid cancer cells significantly increased CD8⁺ T cell apoptosis compared to control cells (Fig. 2B-C). Conversely, co-culture with HBO1-knockdown cells resulted in significantly reduced CD8⁺ T cell apoptosis. These findings suggest that elevated HBO1 expression in thyroid cancer cells may contribute to increased CD8⁺ T-cell apoptosis and impaired antitumor immune responses.

CD8⁺ T cells mediate tumor cell elimination primarily through the secretion of effector cytokines including IFN-γ, TNF-α, and granzyme B (GZMB). To comprehensively evaluate the functional impact of HBO1 modulation on CD8⁺ T cell activity, we employed multiparametric flow cytometry, ELISA, and RT-qPCR analyses. Flow cytometric quantification revealed that HBO1-overexpressing thyroid cancer cells significantly diminished the population of IFN-γ⁺, TNF-α⁺, and GZMB⁺ CD8⁺ T cells compared to control cells (Fig. 2D-I). Conversely, co-culture with HBO1-knockdown cells enhanced the proportion of cytokine-producing CD8⁺ T cells. These findings were further substantiated by ELISA measurements of culture supernatants, which demonstrated significantly reduced secretion of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and GZMB in HBO1-overexpression conditions, with elevated levels observed in HBO1-knockdown co-cultures (Fig. 2J-L). At the transcriptional level, RT-qPCR analysis confirmed parallel changes in cytokine gene expression, with marked downregulation of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and GZMB mRNA in CD8⁺ T cells exposed to HBO1-overexpressing tumor cells, and significant upregulation following co-culture with HBO1-knockdown cells (Fig. 2M-P).

Collectively, these results suggest that elevated HBO1 expression in thyroid cancer is associated with increased CD8⁺ T-cell apoptosis and reduced cytotoxic cytokine production (IFN-γ, TNF-α, and GZMB), which may contribute to tumor immune evasion and progression.

HBO1 activates the immune checkpoint VTCN1 via H3K14 acetylation to suppress CD8⁺ T Cell function

As a lysine acetyltransferase, HBO1 primarily regulates gene expression in cancer by catalyzing the acetylation of histone H3K14, H3K9, H4K5, and H4K12. To investigate the role of HBO1 in these histone modifications in thyroid cancer, we overexpressed and knocked down HBO1 in thyroid cancer cell lines TPC-1 and 8505C. Western blotting was used to assess the acetylation levels of H3K14, H3K9, H4K5, and H4K12. The results showed that HBO1 overexpression markedly enhanced H3K14 acetylation, whereas HBO1 knockdown markedly reduced it (Fig. 3A). These findings indicate that HBO1 is associated with the regulation of H3K14 acetylation in thyroid cancer cells.

Our previous results showed that HBO1 overexpression was associated with impaired cytotoxic effector function of CD8⁺ T cells and increased H3K14 acetylation in thyroid cancer cells. Based on these observations, we hypothesized that HBO1 may modulate the expression of an immune-related gene through H3K14 acetylation, thereby potentially influencing CD8⁺ T-cell function and tumor progression. To test this hypothesis, we performed an integrative analysis, intersecting the H3K14ac CUT&Tag dataset with a panel of known immune checkpoint molecules. This approach identified VTCN1 as a candidate immune checkpoint molecule potentially regulated by H3K14 acetylation (Fig. 3B). Next, we genetically modulated HBO1 expression (overexpression or knockdown) in TPC-1 and 8505C cells and quantified VTCN1 mRNA and protein levels using RT-qPCR and Western blotting, respectively. Consistent with our hypothesis, HBO1 overexpression led to a significant upregulation of VTCN1 at both transcriptional and translational levels, whereas HBO1 knockdown resulted in a pronounced downregulation (Fig. 3C-D). Collectively, these data suggest that HBO1 may positively regulate VTCN1 expression in thyroid cancer cells.

To further investigate whether HBO1 modulates VTCN1 expression through H3K14 acetylation, we overexpressed HBO1 in TPC-1 and 8505C cells and concurrently treated them with WM-3835, a selective inhibitor of HBO1 acetyltransferase activity that has been previously reported to specifically suppress HBO1-mediated histone acetylation [19]. Western blot analysis demonstrated that HBO1 overexpression robustly increased VTCN1 protein levels (Fig. 3E). Notably, this upregulation was substantially reversed upon WM-3835 treatment (Fig. 3E), indicating that HBO1 acetyltransferase activity contributes to VTCN1 induction. Collectively, these findings suggest that HBO1 may promote VTCN1 expression in thyroid cancer through an H3K14 acetylation-dependent mechanism, which is associated with impaired CD8⁺ T-cell effector function and tumor immune evasion.

To functionally validate the role of VTCN1 in HBO1-mediated immune suppression, we established a co-culture system using HBO1-overexpressing (HBO1-OE) thyroid cancer cells with concurrent VTCN1 knockdown (VTCN1-KD) and CD8⁺ T cells. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that CD8⁺ T cells co-cultured with HBO1-OE cells exhibited a marked increase in apoptosis compared to controls. Importantly, this pro-apoptotic effect was substantially abrogated when VTCN1 was knocked down in HBO1-OE cells (HBO1-OE+VTCN1-KD) (Fig. 3F). These results suggest that HBO1-induced CD8⁺ T-cell apoptosis is associated with VTCN1 upregulation in thyroid cancer cells.

Flow cytometry and ELISA analyses of CD8⁺ T cells co-cultured with HBO1-overexpressing (OE)+VTCN1-knockdown (KD) cells revealed significant reductions in the frequencies of IFN-γ⁺, TNF-α⁺, and GZMB⁺ CD8⁺ T cells compared to control co-cultures (Fig. 3G-H). Conversely, co-culture with HBO1-OE+VTCN1-KD cells markedly increased the proportion of cytokine-positive CD8⁺ T cells. ELISA quantification further demonstrated that IFN-γ, TNF-α, and GZMB secretion by CD8⁺ T cells was significantly suppressed following co-culture with HBO1-OE cells, whereas secretion levels were substantially elevated in the HBO1-OE+VTCN1-KD group (Fig. 3I).

Taken together, these findings suggest that HBO1 may contribute to CD8⁺ T-cell apoptosis and impaired cytotoxic effector function through regulation of VTCN1 expression in thyroid cancer cells, thereby potentially promoting tumor immune evasion.

HBO1 recruits BRD4 through the acetylation of H3K14 to transcriptionally activate the immune checkpoint VTCN1

To dissect the molecular mechanism by which HBO1 regulates VTCN1 expression, we overexpressed HBO1 in TPC-1 thyroid cancer cells and performed RNA-seq analysis. Among the top 300 upregulated protein-coding genes identified from RNA-seq, we intersected these with transcription factors predicted to regulate VTCN1 based on the GTRD database. This analysis yielded five candidate transcription factors potentially involved in VTCN1 regulation (Fig. 4A). Among these candidates, BRD4 was prioritized for further investigation because it contains a bromodomain capable of recognizing acetylated lysine residues on histone tails and has been widely implicated in chromatin remodeling and transcriptional activation. Considering that HBO1 primarily functions as a histone acetyltransferase regulating H3K14 acetylation, BRD4 was considered the most biologically relevant candidate linking histone acetylation to transcriptional activation of VTCN1. Based on this, we hypothesized that HBO1 promotes the acetylation of histone H3 at lysine 14 (H3K14ac), which in turn may facilitates the recruitment of BRD4 to the promoter region of VTCN1, thereby contributing to the regulation of its transcription. To validate this hypothesis, we first obtained the 2000 bp nucleotide sequence upstream of the VTCN1 gene (promoter region) from the NCBI database (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). Prediction using the JASPAR database revealed four potential binding sites for the transcription factor BRD4 within the VTCN1 promoter region. Subsequently, we used ChIP-qPCR technology to validate BRD4 binding to these four sites. The results showed significant enrichment at predicted site 1 of VTCN1 in the chromatin precipitated with the BRD4 antibody, suggesting that BRD4 is associated with binding to site 1 of the VTCN1 promoter region (Fig. 4B). Next, we constructed dual-luciferase reporter gene vectors for the wild-type and mutant versions of site 1 in the VTCN1 promoter region and employed a dual-luciferase reporter assay to further verify whether BRD4 binds to site 1 of the VTCN1 promoter region. The results showed that in thyroid cancer cells, dual-luciferase activity was significantly enhanced in the group overexpressing HBO1 and co-overexpressing the wild-type VTCN1 promoter site 1 (Fig. 4C). Conversely, dual-luciferase activity was significantly weakened in the group overexpressing HBO1 and co-overexpressing the mutant VTCN1 promoter site 1 (Fig. 4C). These results support the notion that BRD4 interacts with site 1 of the VTCN1 promoter region. In summary, the experimental results suggest that HBO1-mediated H3K14 acetylation is associated with BRD4 recruitment to the VTCN1 promoter and may contribute to the regulation of VTCN1 expression in thyroid cancer.

To further verify whether the role of the transcription factor BRD4 in the transcriptional activation of VTCN1 is regulated by HBO1’s acetylation function, we first used Co-IP (co-immunoprecipitation) technology to detect if H3K14ac recruits BRD4. The results showed that the transcription factor BRD4 was present in the protein precipitate bound to acetylated histone H3K14 (Fig. 4D). Subsequently, we overexpressed HBO1 in thyroid cancer cell lines TPC-1 and 8505C and used Co-IP to detect whether the recruitment of BRD4 by H3K14ac is affected by HBO1. The results showed that after HBO1 overexpression, compared to the control group, the BRD4 content in the H3K14ac-bound protein precipitate significantly increased (Fig. 4E). These findings suggest that HBO1 overexpression enhances the association between H3K14ac and BRD4. Subsequently, in thyroid cancer cell lines TPC-1 and 8505C, we performed experiments involving overexpression of HBO1 alone and overexpression of HBO1 combined with knockdown of BRD4. We then used RT-qPCR, Western blot, and immunofluorescence assays to detect the effect of HBO1 recruiting BRD4 on VTCN1 mRNA and protein levels. The results showed that when HBO1 was overexpressed, VTCN1 mRNA and protein levels significantly increased, and fluorescence intensity was significantly enhanced; however, when HBO1 was overexpressed while BRD4 was knocked down, VTCN1 mRNA and protein levels significantly decreased, and fluorescence intensity was significantly weakened (Fig. 4F-H). These results suggest that HBO1 may promote VTCN1 transcriptional expression through a BRD4-dependent mechanism.

Collectively, these findings support a model in which HBO1-mediated H3K14 acetylation is associated with enhanced BRD4 recruitment to the VTCN1 promoter, thereby contributing to VTCN1 transcriptional activation in thyroid cancer cells.

In vivo evidence confirms that the HBO1/VTCN1 Axis promotes thyroid cancer progression

To further evaluate the role of HBO1 and VTCN1 in thyroid cancer growth in vivo, we employed female BALB/c nude mice. Twenty 4-week-old mice were randomized into four groups (n = 5 per group): NC, HBO1-overexpressing, NC+shVTCN1, and HBO1-overexpressing+shVTCN1. TPC-1 cells (5 × 106 cells in 150 µL saline) were subcutaneously injected into the right axilla. Tumor dimensions were measured every 3 days (volume = length × width2 × 0.5). After 2 weeks, mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised and imaged. Results demonstrated that HBO1 overexpression significantly accelerated tumor growth (largest volume, fastest rate), while concurrent VTCN1 knockdown markedly suppressed tumor expansion (Fig. 5A-C). These findings suggest that VTCN1 contributes to HBO1-associated tumor growth in vivo.

Subsequently, we further performed immunohistochemical analysis on the extracted tumor tissues to detect the expression levels of HBO1 and VTCN1, as well as the expression of the proliferation index Ki67 in each group. The results showed that Ki67 was strongly positively expressed in the tumor tissues of the HBO1 overexpression group, while the positive rate of Ki67 was significantly reduced in the tumor tissues of the group overexpressing HBO1 while knocking down VTCN1 (Fig. 5D-F). These findings further support an association between HBO1/VTCN1 signaling and enhanced tumor cell proliferative activity in vivo.

Low expression of TRIM21 in thyroid cancer promotes hbo1 protein stability, enabling its acetyltransferase function and thereby mediating thyroid cancer immune escape

Based on the above results, our findings suggest that HBO1-mediated histone H3K14 acetylation facilitates the transcription factor BRD4 binding to chromatin and is associated with the transcriptional initiation of the immune checkpoint VTCN1. Human HBO1 consists of an N-terminal domain (NTD) and a C-terminal MYST domain, with the MYST domain being the core for HBO1’s acetyltransferase function [50, 52]. Therefore, HBO1 acetyltransferase activity may contribute to VTCN1 regulation and tumor progression in thyroid cancer. However, why HBO1 is stably highly expressed in thyroid cancer and effectively exerts its acetyltransferase function remains unclear. Thus, to further investigate the mechanism by which HBO1 is stably highly expressed and effectively functional in thyroid cancer, we conducted the following research.

First, we used IP-MS (Immunoprecipitation-Mass Spectrometry) technology to identify potential interacting proteins of HBO1. Screening based on protein quantitative fold change (Fold change) and P-value (P < 0.05) showed that ubiquitin-related proteins ranked highly, among which the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM21 had the largest fold change (Fig. 6A). Next, we used the MERAV database to analyze and compare the expression levels of TRIM21 in normal thyroid tissue versus thyroid cancer tissue. The results showed that TRIM21 expression levels were significantly lower in thyroid cancer tissues than in normal tissues (Fig. 6B). Subsequently, we further investigated the regulatory relationship between HBO1 and TRIM21 in vitro using RT-qPCR and Western blot techniques. RT-qPCR results showed no mutual regulation between TRIM21 and HBO1 at the transcriptional level (Fig. 6C; Supplementary Fig. 2A-F). However, Western blot results showed that when TRIM21 was overexpressed, HBO1 protein expression levels decreased; conversely, when TRIM21 was knocked down, HBO1 protein expression levels significantly increased (Fig. 6D). These results indicate that TRIM21 negatively regulates HBO1 protein abundance at the post-transcriptional level.

The above results suggest that TRIM21 might regulate HBO1 protein stability through ubiquitination. To further verify this, we first used Co-IP technology to detect whether TRIM21 interacts with HBO1. The results showed that HBO1 and TRIM21 do interact (Fig. 6E). Next, in thyroid cancer cell lines TPC-1 and 8505C, we overexpressed TRIM21 and added the protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide (CHX), setting different time gradients. The effect of TRIM21 on HBO1 protein stability was assessed by Western blotting, followed by quantitative densitometric analysis of HBO1 band intensities at each time point, using GAPDH as the loading control. The results showed that in control cells, HBO1 protein levels gradually declined with prolonged CHX treatment, whereas in TRIM21-overexpressing cells, HBO1 protein decreased more rapidly. At multiple time points, the relative HBO1/GAPDH expression levels were significantly lower in the TRIM21-overexpressing group than in control cells, indicating a markedly shorter protein half‑life (Fig. 6F). These findings suggest that TRIM21 overexpression is associated with reduced HBO1 protein stability in thyroid cancer cells. It is reported that the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway is the primary route by which most E3 ubiquitin ligases influence substrate protein ubiquitination and degradation. Therefore, to further verify whether TRIM21 affects HBO1 protein stability via the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway, we overexpressed TRIM21 in thyroid cancer cell lines TPC-1 and 8505C, treated with CHX, and either added or did not add the proteasome inhibitor MG132. We then detected HBO1 protein expression using Western blot. The results showed that when TRIM21 was overexpressed, HBO1 protein levels decreased; however, when TRIM21 was overexpressed concurrently with the addition of the proteasome inhibitor MG132, HBO1 protein levels recovered (Fig. 6G). These results support the possibility that TRIM21 promotes HBO1 degradation through a proteasome-dependent mechanism.

Protein degradation via the proteasome pathway usually depends on the binding of ubiquitin proteins. To confirm whether HBO1 binds to ubiquitin protein, we co-transfected TRIM21 plasmid and HA-tagged ubiquitin plasmid (HA-Ub) into thyroid cancer cell lines TPC-1 and 8505C, and treated them with MG132. Cells were collected 48 h later, total protein was extracted, precipitated using HBO1 antibody, and then detected by Western blot. The results showed that under MG132 treatment, HBO1 bound to ubiquitin protein in the NC group; however, after TRIM21 overexpression combined with MG132 treatment, the proportion of HBO1 bound to ubiquitin protein significantly increased (Fig. 6H). This indicates that TRIM21 promotes the binding of HBO1 to ubiquitin protein and its subsequent degradation via the proteasome pathway. It is reported that TRIM21 primarily depends on K48 or K63 ubiquitin chains to bind substrate proteins, promoting their ubiquitin-dependent degradation [40, 68]. Therefore, we further transfected TRIM21 plasmid and HA-tagged ubiquitin plasmids (Ub-K63 or Ub-K48) into thyroid cancer cell lines TPC-1 and 8505C, and treated them with MG132. Cells were collected 48 h later, total protein was extracted, precipitated using HBO1 antibody, and detected by Western blot. The results showed that under MG132 treatment, the ubiquitination level of HBO1 was significantly increased in the TRIM21 overexpression group, with more binding of K48 ubiquitin chains to HBO1 (Fig. 6I). Collectively, these results suggest that TRIM21 facilitates K48-linked ubiquitination of HBO1 and is associated with enhanced proteasome-mediated degradation of HBO1.

To further verify whether TRIM21-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of HBO1 affects HBO1’s acetyltransferase function, we performed the following experiments in thyroid cancer cell lines TPC-1 and 8505C: overexpression of HBO1 alone and overexpression of HBO1 combined with overexpression of TRIM21. Subsequently, we used Western blot to detect the acetylation level of H3K14. The results showed that in thyroid cancer cells, overexpression of HBO1 significantly increased H3K14 acetylation; however, after co-overexpression of HBO1 and TRIM21, H3K14 acetylation levels were significantly reduced (Fig. 7A-B). These findings suggest that TRIM21-mediated reduction of HBO1 protein abundance is accompanied by decreased H3K14 acetylation levels, indicating a potential suppression of HBO1 acetyltransferase activity. Finally, to further confirm that TRIM21 inhibits HBO1’s acetyltransferase function by promoting its ubiquitination and degradation, thereby impacting the malignant progression of thyroid cancer, we performed the following experiments in thyroid cancer cell lines TPC-1 and 8505C: overexpression of TRIM21 alone and overexpression of TRIM21 combined with overexpression of HBO1. Then, we used EdU cell proliferation assay combined with flow cytometry, Matrigel invasion assay, and scratch wound healing assay to detect the effects of TRIM21 promoting HBO1 ubiquitination and degradation on the proliferation, invasion, and migration abilities of thyroid cancer cells. The results showed that thyroid cancer cells overexpressing TRIM21 had reduced proliferation capacity, whereas after co-overexpression of TRIM21 and HBO1, the proliferation capacity of thyroid cancer cells significantly increased (Fig. 7C-D). Matrigel invasion assay results showed that thyroid cancer cells overexpressing TRIM21 had reduced invasion capacity, whereas after co-overexpression of TRIM21 and HBO1, the invasion capacity of thyroid cancer cells significantly increased (Fig. 7E). Scratch wound healing assay results showed that compared to the control group, the scratch width was wider in thyroid cancer cells overexpressing TRIM21, indicating inhibited healing ability; however, after co-overexpression of TRIM21 and HBO1, the scratch width in thyroid cancer cells significantly narrowed, promoting their healing ability (Fig. 7F-G). The above results indicate that overexpression of TRIM21 promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of HBO1, inhibits its acetyltransferase function, and ultimately suppresses the malignant progression of thyroid cancer.

In summary, our findings suggest that reduced TRIM21 expression contributes to HBO1 protein stabilization and enhanced HBO1-dependent histone acetylation in thyroid cancer cells. Through its upstream regulation of HBO1, TRIM21 may indirectly influence VTCN1-associated immunosuppressive signaling. However, the present study primarily demonstrates a tumor cell-intrinsic regulatory mechanism, and further studies are required to determine whether TRIM21 directly affects antitumor immune responses.

Discussion

Thyroid cancer (TC), as the most common endocrine malignancy, has shown a significant increase in incidence over the past 30 years, ranking ninth globally [9, 37, 56]. Clinically, the treatment of thyroid cancer primarily relies on surgical resection combined with thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) suppression and radioiodine (I-131) therapy [3, 9, 21, 39]. Although most patients achieve favorable outcomes, a subset experiences recurrence and distant metastasis, leading to a 10-year survival rate as low as 10% and poor prognosis [31]. Therefore, in-depth investigation into the pathogenesis of thyroid cancer and identification of novel therapeutic targets are critically important. During the development and progression of thyroid cancer, numerous tumor-associated genes exhibit aberrant expression, with HBO1 being one such example. As a member of the MYST acetyltransferase family, HBO1-mediated acetylation of histones is a key mechanism regulating cellular processes including gene transcription, protein ubiquitination, and signal transduction [52, 63]. Recent studies particularly highlight HBO1’s role in immune cell development and function [23, 42, 52, 65]. Immunotherapy, which enhances the immune system’s ability to recognize and eliminate tumor cells, has emerged as a novel strategy for treating advanced malignancies [58]. While immune checkpoint inhibitors demonstrate efficacy in various cancers, their effectiveness in advanced thyroid cancer requires further validation [13, 16, 47, 61]. Despite the significant role of HBO1 in thyroid cancer, research on its involvement in thyroid carcinogenesis remains limited. Furthermore, reports on immune checkpoint inhibition and improvement of immunotherapy efficacy in thyroid cancer are scarce [43, 46, 73], necessitating further investigation into HBO1’s role in immune escape to identify potential immunotherapeutic targets. Through bioinformatics analysis and clinical sample validation, we found that HBO1 is highly expressed in thyroid cancer tissues and may contribute to immune evasion through modulation of CD8+ T-cell function. To elucidate the potential mechanism by which HBO1 participates in immune evasion in thyroid cancer, we conducted in vitro co-culture experiments of tumor cells with CD8+ T cells to explore intercellular interactions within the tumor immune microenvironment. The results revealed that elevated HBO1 expression was associated with reduced CD8+ T-cell infiltration in thyroid cancer tissues, while in vitro co-culture experiments further suggested that HBO1 suppresses the cytotoxic activity of CD8+ T cells against thyroid cancer cells.

HBO1, as a multifunctional acyltransferase, influences transcriptional activation and promotes gene expression by modifying multiple histone acetylation sites [52]. Aberrant H3K14 modification is closely associated with tumors and immune-related diseases [15, 38], suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target. In endothelial cells, H3K14 acetylation affects endothelial activation, thereby suppressing regulatory T cells, effector T cells, and macrophages, ultimately modulating immune responses [28, 29, 66]. Current research indicates that HBO1 primarily regulates H3K14 acetylation levels. Given the reported role of H3K14 acetylation in immune regulation, HBO1 may participate in the modulation of T-cell activity and function through epigenetic mechanisms. Our study confirmed that high HBO1 expression in thyroid cancer cells activates H3K14 acetylation. Importantly, although our results demonstrated that HBO1 promotes H3K14 acetylation and impairs CD8+T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity, the present study did not directly establish a causal relationship between H3K14 acetylation and T-cell suppression. Further mechanistic studies are required to determine whether HBO1-mediated immune regulation is entirely dependent on H3K14 acetylation. Previous studies demonstrate that HBO1 deficiency reduces histone H3K14 acetylation by over 90%, with no significant changes detected at other histone modification sites [63]. This underscores the high specificity of HBO1 for H3K14 and its indispensable role as a transcriptional activator in maintaining H3K14 acetylation during development. HBO1 often functions within complexes; for instance, it forms complexes with bromodomain-containing protein 1 (BRD1) and PHD finger protein 3 (BRPF3) [5]. Specific H3K14 acetylation by this complex promotes DNA replication. BRD1 and HBO1 exhibit significant genomic co-localization, and BRD1 deficiency in erythroid cells markedly reduces global H3K14 acetylation levels [14]. In summary, H3K14 acetylation is predominantly regulated by the lysine acetyltransferase HBO1; however, its specific mechanistic role in thyroid cancer remains unclear.

Histone acetylation modifications are generally associated with transcriptional activation [70]. Aberrant H3K14 modification influences the pathogenesis and progression of various diseases, particularly tumors and immune-related disorders [15, 32, 38]. Consequently, the modification status of H3K14 represents a potential therapeutic target. Studies show that hyperacetylation of H3K14 can affect the expression of specific genes, promoting DNA repair and transcriptional activation. H3K14 acetylation upregulates CTNNB1 expression, activating the Wnt/β-Catenin signaling pathway, influencing cell differentiation, and ultimately contributing to tumorigenesis [59]. Application of histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACi) increases H3K14 acetylation, subsequently enhancing transcription from the BDNF promoter II [14]. Collectively, H3K14 acetylation generally promotes transcriptional activation and elevates downstream gene expression. T cells are intimately linked to the immune system. Pro-atherogenic lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) can activate trained immunity pathways (TIPs) in human aortic endothelial cells (HAECs) by upregulating H3K14 acetylation [35]. During T cell activation, loss of histone deacetylase HDAC9 increases H3K14 acetylation, promoting inflammation and the production of cytokines and chemokines, thereby activating T cells [74]. H3K14 acetylation also impacts endothelial cell activation, suppressing induced regulatory T cells, regulatory B cells, effector T cells, and macrophages, ultimately modulating immune responses [28, 29, 66]. These findings suggest that H3K14 acetylation may contribute to the regulation of interactions between target cells and immune cells, including T cells. Current evidence indicates that HBO1 is a major regulator of H3K14 acetylation. Given the reported immunomodulatory functions of H3K14 acetylation, alterations in HBO1 expression may influence T-cell responses through changes in chromatin accessibility and gene transcription.

VTCN1 (B7-H4), a transmembrane protein with potent immunosuppressive functions, is frequently associated with poor prognosis and is highly expressed in malignancies such as lung cancer, salivary gland cancer, ovarian cancer, and thyroid cancer. It plays critical roles in tumor cell proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and anti-apoptosis, serving as a key immune checkpoint molecule [6, 12, 36, 64, 69]. VTCN1 modulates the immune microenvironment by influencing the number and activity of regulatory T cells [6, 28, 29, 44, 51, 66].

Our study revealed that in thyroid cancer cells, HBO1-mediated H3K14 acetylation promotes chromatin accessibility, facilitating BRD4 recruitment to the VTCN1 promoter region and thereby enhancing VTCN1 transcriptional activation. In addition, BRD4 was selected for mechanistic validation based on RNA-seq analysis, GTRD database prediction, and its established role as a reader of acetylated histones that facilitates transcriptional activation at super-enhancers and promoter regions [17, 77]. Although our data support a critical role for BRD4 in HBO1-mediated VTCN1 transcriptional activation, the potential contribution of other acetylation-dependent transcriptional regulators cannot be excluded and warrants further investigation. It is well established that VTCN1 functions as an immunosuppressive checkpoint molecule capable of attenuating effector T-cell activity and facilitating tumor progression. Consistent with these observations, our findings suggest that the HBO1-VTCN1 axis contributes to immune evasion and tumor progression in thyroid cancer.

However, the immunological effects of the HBO1-VTCN1 axis were primarily evaluated using in vitro CD8⁺ T-cell co-culture systems and xenograft models in nude mice. Although our data support a role for HBO1 in regulating VTCN1 expression and influencing CD8⁺ T-cell effector function, these experimental systems cannot fully recapitulate the complexity of the tumor immune microenvironment. Previous studies have highlighted the critical contributions of diverse immune cell populations, stromal components, and cytokine networks in shaping antitumor immunity [4, 58]. Therefore, further validation in immunocompetent mouse models will be necessary to clarify the in vivo immunological significance of the HBO1-VTCN1 signaling axis. Moreover, although our results support a model in which HBO1-mediated H3K14 acetylation enhances BRD4 recruitment and promotes VTCN1 transcription, some conclusions remain based on correlation analyses and experimental model systems. Therefore, the causal relationships among HBO1, VTCN1, and CD8⁺ T-cell dysfunction require further validation in clinically relevant models and larger patient cohorts. Future studies should also explore whether HBO1 regulates antitumor immunity through additional downstream pathways beyond VTCN1. However, the upstream mechanisms regulating HBO1 stability and activation of the HBO1-VTCN1 signaling axis remain incompletely understood and warrant further investigation.

This study is the first to reveal TRIM21, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, as a regulator of HBO1 protein stability. TRIM21, a member of the TRIM (tripartite motif) family, typically mediates substrate ubiquitination via its RING domain and participates in tumorigenesis. For example, TRIM25 promotes hepatocellular carcinoma progression through K48-linked ubiquitination [57]. Our findings indicate that TRIM21 promotes K48-linked polyubiquitination of HBO1, which is associated with accelerated proteasome-dependent degradation of HBO1. This mechanism exhibits synergistic regulation with the previously reported SCF-FBXW15-mediated ubiquitination of HBO1 [76], yet TRIM21’s regulation displays notable tissue specificity. MERAV database analysis indicates significantly lower TRIM21 expression in thyroid cancer tissues compared to normal tissues, likely contributing to the abnormal accumulation of HBO1 in tumors. Interestingly, TRIM21 often exhibits dual roles in cancer: it promotes EMT by stabilizing Snail protein in breast cancer [24], while in this study, it acts as a tumor suppressor, highlighting the diversity of its biological effects across cancer types. We further found that TRIM21-mediated HBO1 degradation was accompanied by reduced H3K14 acetylation and attenuation of the BRD4-VTCN1 signaling axis. Notably, although the present study identified TRIM21 as an upstream regulator of HBO1 stability and demonstrated its inhibitory effects on the HBO1-BRD4-VTCN1 signaling axis, the current evidence primarily reflects a tumor cell-intrinsic regulatory mechanism. Previous studies have shown that TRIM21 participates in multiple immune-related processes, including innate immune signaling and interferon responses [8, 25]. However, whether TRIM21 directly regulates CD8⁺ T-cell activity or contributes to remodeling of the thyroid cancer immune microenvironment remains unclear. Therefore, further studies using immunocompetent models will be required to determine the immunological significance of TRIM21 in thyroid cancer. Given that K48 ubiquitination is the primary signal for 26S proteasome degradation [48], this provides a theoretical basis for developing small-molecule modulators targeting the TRIM21-HBO1 axis. Future studies should focus on resolving the structural basis of TRIM21 recognition of HBO1 (e.g., whether binding occurs via the NTD or MYST domain) and determining whether restoration of TRIM21 expression can modulate HBO1-dependent tumor progression and immune-associated signaling in thyroid cancer.

Despite these findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. In this study, HBO1 overexpression was used to mimic the aberrantly elevated HBO1 levels observed in thyroid cancer, consistent with public database analyses and clinical specimens. Moreover, the combination of gene overexpression and shRNA-mediated knockdown is a widely accepted strategy for functional validation and has been extensively applied in studies of HBO1 and other epigenetic regulators [27, 45]. To minimize potential bias, both gain- and loss-of-function experiments were performed. Nevertheless, ectopic overexpression may exceed physiological levels and influence chromatin accessibility and transcriptional regulation. Therefore, future studies using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing will be valuable for validating these findings under more physiologically relevant conditions.

In summary, our results support a model in which HBO1-mediated H3K14 acetylation enhances BRD4 recruitment to the VTCN1 promoter, promoting VTCN1 transcription and contributing to immunosuppressive signaling in thyroid cancer. Notably, we identified TRIM21 as an upstream negative regulator of HBO1 protein stability that promotes K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasome-dependent degradation of HBO1. This discovery unveils a novel mechanism where epigenetic regulation and protein stability dysregulation synergistically promote tumor progression. Considering the central role of HBO1 in regulating VTCN1 expression and tumor progression [10, 11, 18, 49], the TRIM21-HBO1 axis may represent a promising therapeutic target in thyroid cancer. However, whether TRIM21 directly influences antitumor immune responses remains to be determined.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

The human thyroid cancer cell lines TPC‑1 and 8505C were provided by the Tumor Research Institute of Xiangya School of Basic Medical Sciences, Central South University. Both lines were originally obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). TPC‑1 cells were cultured in RPMI‑1640 medium, and 8505C cells in DMEM. All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin‑streptomycin (Beyotime). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. When confluence reached 80–90%, cells were passaged. Mycoplasma contamination was tested monthly using the MycoAlert™ kit (Lonza).

Vectors and plasmids

HBO1 pcDNA3.1‑Flag‑C, TRIM21 pcDNA3.1‑HA‑C, BRD4 pcDNA3.1‑His‑C and the empty vector pcDNA3.1‑Flag‑C were purchased from UNIBIO (Changsha, China). shHBO1‑pLKO.1‑puro, shVTCN1‑pLKO.1‑puro, shTRIM21‑pLKO.1‑puro and control pLKO.1‑puro were obtained from Qingke Biotech (Beijing, China). Dual‑luciferase reporter plasmids pGL3‑Basic‑VTCN1‑promoter‑WT and ‑MT were purchased from UNIBIO. The Renilla luciferase control vector pRL‑TK was maintained in our laboratory. HA‑tagged ubiquitin constructs pCMV‑HA‑Ub, pCMV‑HA‑Ub(K48) and pCMV‑HA‑Ub(K63) were kindly provided by Prof. Zhou Ming (Xiangya School of Basic Medical Sciences, Central South University).

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis and RT‑qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (NCMBiotech, Beijing, China). First‑strand cDNA was synthesized according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative PCR was performed with a SYBR Green qPCR kit (Yugong Life Science, Jiangsu, China). Relative gene expression was calculated by the 2–ΔΔCt method, using GAPDH as the internal control. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted using RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors (Beyotime). Protein concentration was measured by the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% non‑fat milk for 2 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by secondary antibodies. Bands were visualized with ECL substrate (NCM). Antibody details are provided in Supplementary Table 2. The HBO1 inhibitor WM-3835 was purchased from Selleck Chemicals (Cat. No. S9805) and dissolved in DMSO to prepare a stock solution. Based on preliminary optimization experiments, cells were treated with 5 μM WM-3835 for 48 h before subsequent analyses, while control cells received an equivalent volume of DMSO.

Scratch wound‑healing assay

Cells were seeded in 6‑well plates and grown to 90% confluence. A linear scratch was made using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip. Detached cells were removed by washing with PBS, and serum‑free medium was added. Images were captured at 0, 24, and 48 h under an inverted microscope. Wound closure was quantified using ImageJ.

Transwell invasion and migration assays

Transwell chambers with 8 µm pores (BeyoGold™, Beyotime) were used. For invasion assays, inserts were pre-coated with Matrigel (Corning). A total of 3 × 105 cells suspended in 200 µL serum‑free medium were seeded into the upper chamber; while 600 µL medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. After 24 h, non‑migrated cells were removed from the upper surface, and inserts were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Migrated cells were counted in five random fields per well under a microscope.

EdU proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was measured using the BeyoClick™ EdU‑488 kit (Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were fixed, permeabilized, incubated with EdU working solution for 2 h, then with Click reaction solution for 30 min, and finally counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 for fluorescence imaging.

T cell–tumor cell co‑culture

PBMCs were isolated from healthy donors by Ficoll‑Hypaque density gradient centrifugation (GE Healthcare). Cells were cultured in RPMI‑1640 medium supplemented with CD3/CD28 beads (Miltenyi) and cytokines (15 ng/mL IL‑2, 5 ng/mL IL‑7, 10 ng/mL IL‑15) for 7 days, with the medium refreshed every 2 days. Transfected TPC‑1 or 8505C cells were plated in 6‑well plates, allowed to adhere, and co‑cultured with activated T cells at a 1:10 ratio.

ELISA

Standards were reconstituted to 100 ng/mL and serially diluted to generate a standard curve. Co‑culture supernatants were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 20 min, diluted as needed, and 100 µL of each standard or sample was added per well. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 80 min, washed three times, incubated with biotin‑conjugated antibody for 50 min, washed, incubated with enzyme conjugate for 50 min, washed, developed with TMB substrate for 20 min, stopped with acid, and read at 450 nm. Concentrations were calculated from the standard curve.

Flow cytometry

After 6–12 h co‑culture, T cells were stained with anti‑CD3 and anti‑CD8 antibodies. Apoptosis was assessed by Annexin V/PI staining (Lianke Biotech AP101). Intracellular IFN‑γ, TNF‑α and granzyme B were detected following fixation and permeabilization. Data were acquired on a BD LSRFortessa X‑20 and analyzed using FlowJo v10 software.

ChIP‑qPCR

ChIP was performed using the Beyotime kit (P2078) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cross-linked chromatin was sonicated, immunoprecipitated, de‑cross-linked, and DNA enrichment was quantified by qPCR.

Dual‑luciferase reporter assay

Reporter assays were carried out using the Uelandy dual‑luciferase kit (F6075S). VTCN1‑WT‑Luc or VTCN1‑MT(S1)‑Luc and BRD4 expression vectors were co‑transfected with pRL‑TK and incubated for 48 h. Cells were lysed, and firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured sequentially. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

Co‑immunoprecipitation

Cells were lysed on ice for 30 min in buffer containing protease inhibitors. Lysates were incubated with antibody‑conjugated magnetic beads for 2 h at room temperature. Beads were washed, bound proteins were eluted, and interactions were analyzed by SDS–PAGE and Western blot.

Immunofluorescence

Cells (5 × 104 per well) were seeded on coverslips and grown to ~ 60% confluence. After fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilization with 0.2% Triton X‑100 (omitted for membrane proteins), cells were blocked, incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C, then with fluorescent secondary antibody for 1 h at 37 °C. Nuclei were stained with DAPI mounting medium. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope.

Immunohistochemistry

FFPE tumor sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer at 98 °C for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase was quenched, and sections were blocked with 3% BSA for 30 min. Primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies and DAB detection. Staining intensity was scored using the H‑score (0–3). Reagent details are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Xenograft studies

Four‑week‑old female BALB/c nude mice were purchased from Hunan Slack Jingda Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. and maintained under pathogen‑free conditions. All procedures were approved by the Central South University Animal Ethics Committee (CSU‑2023‑0337). Mice (n = 20) were randomized into four groups (n = 5 each). Each mouse received a subcutaneous injection of 5 × 106 HBO1‑overexpressing 8505C cells. Tumor size was measured every three days using the formula (long diameter × short diameter2) × 0.5. At the endpoint, mice were euthanized and tumors were harvested.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least three times. Data are expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using unpaired two‑tailed t tests; multiple groups were analyzed by one‑way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (ns, not significant; *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001).
