Efficacy of 1 L PEG Ascorbate vs Sodium Picosulfate With Magnesium Citrate Bowel Preparations for Capsule Colonoscopy and Pan-Intestinal Capsule Endoscopy: A Prospective Study
Authors: João Carlos Gonçalves, Mariana Souto, Joana Magalhães, Bruno Rosa, José Cotter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
INTRODUCTION:Capsule colonoscopy (CC) and pan-intestinal capsule endoscopy (“panendoscopy”) require effective bowel cleansing; however, evidence on ultra-low-volume regimens is lacking. This study compared the efficacy of 1 L polyethylene glycol with ascorbic acid (PEG-Asc) vs sodium picosulfate with magnesium citrate (SPMC) in CC and panendoscopy procedures.METHODS:In this prospective, single-center, researcher-blinded, randomized trial, 220 consecutive adult outpatients referred for CC or panendoscopy were assigned 1:1 to receive split-dose PEG-Asc or SPMC. Primary outcomes were adequate bowel cleansing and procedure completion, while secondary outcomes included tolerability and clinically relevant endoscopic findings.RESULTS:Adequate bowel cleansing was achieved in 70.0% of the PEG-Asc group vs 47.1% of the SPMC group (P = 0.001). PEG-Asc achieved higher rates of excellent segmental cleansing in the right (32.6% vs 9.4%; P < 0.001) and transverse colon (38.8% vs 15.6%; P < 0.001). Overall completion rates were similar between regimens (PEG-Asc 68.9% vs SPMC 66.7%; P = 0.742), but with CC procedures achieving 62.7% and panendoscopy 87.2% (P = 0.004). Tolerability was higher with SPMC, reflected by fewer reports of nausea (27.6% vs 42.2%; P = 0.035) and vomiting (9.2% vs 24.4%; P = 0.005), and a greater proportion of patients rating the preparation as “easy” (34.7% vs 21.1%; P = 0.039). Clinically relevant findings, including polyps ≥6 mm, were similar across both groups.DISCUSSION:PEG-Asc provides superior bowel cleansing for CC and panendoscopy, whereas SPMC is better tolerated. These findings support PEG-Asc as the first-line ultra-low-volume bowel preparation for capsule-based procedures.

INTRODUCTION

Capsule colonoscopy (CC) is currently indicated in patients in whom a complete conventional colonoscopy is not possible to achieve, whether because of technical difficulties such as looping or sharp angulation, patient refusal, or contraindications to sedation (1,2). Several studies have assessed the diagnostic performance of CC in adenoma detection, supporting its role as a sensitive tool for detecting colorectal neoplasia (3–6). A meta-analysis including 13 studies and 2,485 patients reported polyp detection rates ranging from 24% to 74%, with sensitivity for polyps >6 and ≥10 mm of 79%–96% and 84%–97%, respectively, superior to CT colonography (7).

An alternative use of the “double-headed” capsule devices is a pan-intestinal capsule endoscopy (“panendoscopy”). This modality allows simultaneous evaluation of the small bowel and colon, which has been primarily used in the contexts of inflammatory bowel disease and obscure gastrointestinal bleeding (8,9). It contributes to the diagnosis and monitoring of Crohn's disease because panendoscopy provides a measurable assessment of luminal disease activity within a single procedure (10,11). In obscure gastrointestinal bleeding, panendoscopy has demonstrated superior sensitivity for lesion detection compared with colonoscopy, after a negative esophagogastroduodenoscopy, with the potential to obviate further invasive diagnostic interventions (12).

The diagnostic yield of CC and panendoscopy is strongly influenced by bowel cleansing quality and completion rates. The inability to wash and aspirate makes capsule procedures particularly vulnerable to the effects of residual stool and bubbles, which may impair mucosal visualization and reduce diagnostic accuracy. A recent meta-analysis from our group, including 31 studies and 4,072 patients submitted to bowel preparation for CC or panendoscopy, reported a pooled adequate cleansing rate of 72.5% and a wide variability of existing multistep protocols (13). Although formal quality standards for CC and panendoscopy have yet to be defined, further improvement of bowel preparation regimens is essential to enable their broader clinical adoption.

Ultra-low-volume bowel preparations (≤1 L), such as 1 L polyethylene glycol with ascorbic acid (PEG-Asc) or sodium picosulfate with magnesium citrate (SPMC), have demonstrated similar cleansing efficacy to higher-volume percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy regimens in conventional colonoscopy while improving tolerability and patient adherence (14). Nonetheless, evidence regarding their performance in capsule-based procedures is missing. To address this gap, this study aimed to compare the bowel cleansing efficacy of PEG-Asc and SPMC in patients undergoing CC or panendoscopy. Patient tolerability and clinically relevant findings were also analyzed between the 2 purgative solutions.

METHODS

Population selection and data collection

Between January 1, 2024, and June 30, 2025, consecutive outpatients referred for CC or panendoscopy were prospectively enrolled. Eligible individuals were adults who were able to provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria included contraindications to capsule endoscopy or to the use of PEG-Asc or SPMC. Patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive bowel preparation with either PEG-Asc or SPMC using a block randomization method. All participants were contacted by mail with the scheduled date and time of the procedure, along with standardized written instructions for bowel cleansing. Patients who did not attend the scheduled procedure were not included in the analysis (Figure 1).

Demographic and clinical variables were prospectively collected, including age, sex, whether there were impaired mobility, chronic laxative use, antidepressant use, previous diagnosis of diabetes mellitus, history of abdominal surgery, and prior inadequate bowel preparation.

Bowel preparation

Patients were instructed to discontinue oral iron supplementation 1 week before the capsule procedure. Two days before the procedure, patients took oral senna 12 mg tablets, 1 in the morning with breakfast and 1 in the evening before bedtime, and were advised to avoid high-fiber foods including vegetables, fruits, and cereals. They were instructed to drink at least 2 L of clear liquids throughout the day. On the day preceding the procedure, the same senna regimen was repeated. After 2:00 pm, patients were asked to follow a clear liquid diet consisting of water, tea, pulp-free juices, and optionally, light-colored gelatin.

For bowel cleansing, patients received either PEG-Asc or SPMC in a split-dose regimen. On the evening before the procedure at 8:00 pm, the first PEG-Asc dose was dissolved in 500 mL of water and consumed over 1 hour, followed by an additional 500 mL of clear fluids. For SPMC, the first sachet was dissolved in 150 mL of water and ingested, after which patients drank 1.5–2 L of clear fluids over 1–2 hours. On the morning of the procedure at 6:00 am, the second dose of each preparation was administered in the same manner as the evening dose.

Tolerance questionnaire

On arrival at the Endoscopy Unit, patients were invited to complete a tolerance questionnaire. The survey assessed whether the full preparation had been ingested (yes/no), included a 5-point scale to rate the ease of ingestion (“very easy,” “easy,” “tolerable,” “difficult,” or “very difficult”), and collected information on adverse events. To provide a visual impression of tolerability, the response scale was color-coded, ranging from light green (“very easy”) to dark red (“very difficult”). Patients were also specifically asked about headache, nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain, with the option to report any additional symptoms not listed. Serious adverse events were defined as those requiring emergency department evaluation and/or hospital admission.

Capsule procedure

Patients received a 10 mg domperidone tablet 30 minutes before capsule ingestion. Thereafter, a PillCam COLON 2 or PillCam CROHN’S capsule was swallowed with approximately 150 mg of simethicone (oral drops). One hour later, capsule location was assessed using the real-time viewer on the recorder device. If the capsule remained in the stomach, an additional 10 mg of domperidone was administered and the assessment repeated after 30 minutes. In cases where the capsule had still not passed into the duodenum, endoscopic delivery to the duodenum was performed.

Once small-bowel entry was confirmed, the first booster dose was administered, consisting of 30 mL of sodium phosphate followed by 1 L of water. Three hours later, a second booster was given consisting of 15 mL of sodium phosphate with 500 mL of water. One hour after the second booster, patients were allowed a light meal. If capsule expulsion had not occurred within 1 hour of the meal, a 10 mg bisacodyl suppository was administered. Video recording continued until capsule expulsion or until depletion of the capsule or recorder battery.

Capsule video analysis

Capsule procedure videos were reviewed by 2 independent, experienced readers (>500 procedures each) using the RAPID Reader software v9.0 in “twin head mode,” allowing simultaneous visualization of both cameras at 8–15 frames per second. The capsule readers were blinded to the type of bowel preparation administered.

Bowel cleansing quality was assessed using the Colon Capsule Cleansing Assessment and Report scale (15). The colon was divided into 3 segments, right-sided, transverse, and left-sided based on the anatomic landmarks. For each segment, the percentage of mucosa clearly visualized was scored as follows: 0 = <50%; 1 = 50–75%; 2 = >75%; 3 = >90%, accounting for solid debris, liquid opacity, and bubbles. Overall cleansing was graded as inadequate (0–5), good (6–7), or excellent (8–9). Importantly, if any segment scored ≤1, the overall preparation was classified as inadequate, irrespective of the total score. A complete examination was defined as visualization of the hemorrhoidal plexus or documented capsule expulsion during the recording period.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± SD and compared using the Student t-test, whereas nonnormally distributed variables are reported as median (IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as a 2-tailed P-value <0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Ethical considerations

This study has been registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT07206329). The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board (code: Reference No. 65/2024), and all patients provided written informed consent.

RESULTS

A total of 220 patients were prospectively enrolled (Figure 1), with 110 assigned to the SPMC group and 110 to the PEG-Asc group. Seventeen patients did not attend their scheduled procedure, resulting in 203 patients completing the bowel preparation regimen (106 in the SPMC group and 97 in the PEG-Asc group).

Demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between the 2 groups (Table 1). The median age was 67 years (interquartile range [IQR] 20.25) in the SPMC group and 64 years (IQR 20) in the PEG-Asc group. Female patients comprised 67.0% of the SPMC group and 62.9% of the PEG-Asc group. Rates of chronic laxative use, presence of impaired mobility, antidepressant use, diagnosis of diabetes mellitus, and prior inadequate bowel preparation were similar between groups. A substantial proportion of patients had a history of abdominal surgery, with comparable distribution across both cohorts. Most procedures were CC (SPMC, 82.1%; PEG-Asc, 77.3%), and the remaining were panendoscopy procedures. The main referral indication was incomplete colonoscopy (SPMC, 82.1%; PEG-Asc, 77.3%), followed by inflammation assessment (14.2% vs 14.4%) and anemia workup (3.8% vs 8.2%). Referrals due to incomplete colonoscopy were predominantly due to technical limitations, most commonly severe looping or fixed colonic angulation preventing cecal intubation.

A total of 188 patients (92.6%) completed the questionnaire and were included in the tolerance analysis (SPMC, n = 98; PEG-Asc, n = 90). Fifteen patients (7.4%) did not provide evaluable responses. Tolerability scores differed between groups, with SPMC more often rated as easy (34.7% vs 21.1%; P = 0.039) and less frequently as very difficult (7.1% vs 16.7%; P = 0.042) (Table 2; Figure 2). Nausea (27.6% vs 42.2%; P = 0.035) and vomiting (9.2% vs 24.4%; P = 0.005) were also significantly less frequent with SPMC. Other adverse events, including headache (16.3% vs 13.3%; P = 0.565), abdominal pain (14.3% vs 14.4%; P = 0.975), and serious adverse events (0% vs 1%) did not differ between groups. Compliance was high in both arms, with complete intake achieved by 99.0% of patients in the SPMC group and 95.6% in the PEG-Asc group (P = 0.145).

In 11 cases, the capsule did not reach the cecum, and these patients were excluded from the bowel cleansing efficacy analysis (SPMC, n = 102; PEG-Asc, n = 90) (Table 3). Adequate overall bowel preparation was observed in 47.1% of patients in the SPMC group compared with 70.0% in the PEG-Asc group (P = 0.001) (Figure 3). Segmental analysis showed that PEG-Asc achieved significantly higher bowel cleansing adequacy rates in the right colon (77.9% vs 49.0%; P < 0.001), as well as higher proportions of excellent preparation in both the right colon (32.6% vs 9.4%; P < 0.001) and the transverse colon (38.8% vs 15.6%; P < 0.001). Prior inadequate bowel preparation at conventional colonoscopy was uncommon among patients with inadequate capsule cleansing (SPMC: 3/54, 5.6%; PEG-Asc: 2/27, 7.4%), with no significant association observed. Complete capsule examination rates were comparable between regimens (SPMC, 66.7% vs PEG-Asc, 68.9%; P = 0.742). Notably, completion rates in our cohort differed by indication because CC after incomplete conventional colonoscopy achieved a rate of 62.7%, compared with 87.2% for panendoscopy performed to evaluate inflammation or anemia (P = 0.004).

Clinically relevant findings were identified in 63.7% of patients in the SPMC group and 67.4% in the PEG-Asc group. The most frequent lesions were polyps (39.2% vs 41.4%) and diverticula (29.4% vs 35.6%), with no significant difference between both purgative regimens. Inflammatory alterations of the mucosa (10.8% vs 9.2%) and angioectasias (6.9% vs 4.6%) were less common and comparable between groups (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

In this randomized prospective study comparing 2 ultra-low-volume bowel preparations for CC and panendoscopy, PEG-Asc demonstrated superior overall cleansing efficacy, with significantly higher rates of excellent preparation in the right and transverse colon, whereas SPMC was associated with better patient-reported tolerability.

Tolerability remains a critical determinant of patient adherence to procedures requiring bowel preparation. In our study, both regimens achieved high compliance, but tolerability was superior with SPMC, as reflected by a greater proportion of patients reporting the preparation as easy and fewer nausea or vomiting episodes compared with PEG-Asc. Specifically, nausea and vomiting with PEG-Asc occurred after the second split dose and is consistent with rates previously reported for this product (16). Overall, these findings align with published data, which demonstrated significantly higher patient acceptance, willingness to repeat, and fewer gastrointestinal symptoms with SPMC (17). Similarly, others have confirmed that patient satisfaction again favored SPMC (18), and evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses reinforces this pattern (19,20). Taken together with our data, these findings suggest a superior tolerability profile of SPMC for CC and panendoscopy procedures.

Nonetheless, our findings suggest that tolerability is influenced not solely by the total ingested volume, but also by the composition, taste, and gastrointestinal effects of the purgative itself, because SPMC was better tolerated despite a higher recommended fluid intake compared with PEG-Asc. This observation may be clinically relevant when counseling patients, shifting the focus from volume reduction alone toward strategies aimed at improving palatability, minimizing nausea, and fractionating intake to enhance adherence (21,22).

Of those patients referred because of incomplete conventional colonoscopy, 41.8% had a history of abdominal or pelvic surgery, most commonly hysterectomy, which is a recognized risk factor for incomplete colonoscopy (23,24). Postsurgical anatomical changes may result in colonic angulations or rigidity that hinder passage of the colonoscope, particularly across the sigmoid colon. Moreover, diverticula were identified in 35.2% of patients referred because of incomplete colonoscopy, and advanced diverticular disease is likewise a well-established anatomical contributor to failure to reach the cecum (25). It is plausible that these same factors also contribute to the lower completion rates observed with CC (6,26–29). In our study, the overall capsule completion rate was 67.7%, with no significant differences between purgative regimens, but lower than the pooled completion rate of 83% reported in our recent meta-analysis (13). Interestingly, a subgroup analysis by referral indication revealed substantially higher completion rates in patients undergoing panendoscopy compared with those referred after incomplete colonoscopy (87.2% vs 62.7%). These findings support the hypothesis that the same anatomical factors predisposing to incomplete conventional colonoscopy, often related to prior abdominal or pelvic surgery, also negatively influence capsule completion rates.

Adequate overall bowel cleansing was markedly higher with PEG-Asc compared with SPMC, with a greater proportion of patients having excellent segmental bowel preparation rates. These results stand in contrast to evidence from conventional colonoscopy, where randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses have generally shown SPMC to be noninferior to PEG-based preparations (19,20). A multicenter trial comparing 1 L PEG-Asc with SPMC likewise demonstrated equivalent overall cleansing success (97.4% vs 97.3%) (18). The recent American Gastroenterological Association 2025 consensus guideline therefore endorses both PEG-Asc and SPMC as effective ultra-low-volume options for colonoscopy, noting a consistent preference for SPMC in tolerability (14). The discrepancy between these prior findings and the present results likely reflects the procedural differences between colonoscopy and CC, i.e., in colonoscopy, residual debris can be actively washed and aspirated, whereas in capsule endoscopy, even small amounts of fluid or stool may obscure the mucosa. From a clinical perspective, these observations suggest that bowel cleansing efficacy data from colonoscopy cannot be directly extrapolated to capsule-based procedures.

No standardized bowel preparation regimen has yet been established for capsule-based procedures, and there are currently no society-endorsed quality benchmarks for adequate cleansing or complete examination rates in CC and panendoscopy. In a recent meta-analysis from our group, higher-volume bowel preparations (4 L) were associated with superior rates of both adequate cleansing and complete examinations compared with lower-volume regimens, although ultra-low-volume strategies have not been tested (13). In this study, PEG-Asc achieved an adequate cleansing rate of 70%, representing the most effective ultra-low-volume regimen currently assessed in capsule-based procedures. Notably, this number (70%) closely approximates the pooled adequate preparation rate of 72.5% reported in our meta-analysis, suggesting that PEG-Asc may approach the performance of higher volume-based regimens.

Another important aspect is that bowel preparation for CC and panendoscopy requires a multimodal preparation incorporating a low-fiber diet, adjunctive laxatives, prokinetic administration before capsule ingestion, split-dose purgatives, and “boosters” (13). Despite this optimization, bowel cleansing quality remains suboptimal, highlighting a key limitation to widespread clinical adoption. Adjunctive measures may further improve performance. Antifoaming agents, such as simethicone, reduce intraluminal bubbles and, in randomized trials and meta-analyses of conventional colonoscopy, have been shown to enhance mucosal visualization and increase rates of adequate cleansing, although consistent improvements in lesion detection have not been demonstrated (14,30). We routinely administer simethicone in capsule procedures based on its established benefit in small-bowel capsule endoscopy; nonetheless, its specific role in CC has not been formally addressed (31). In addition, improved patient education, management of baseline constipation, temporary discontinuation of constipating medications, and intensified or extended preparation protocols may benefit patients with prior inadequate cleansing (14,32). Finally, the use of hyperosmolar “boosters” such as diatrizoate to enhance capsule propulsion and completion rates represents a promising strategy supported by evidence from our meta-analysis (13).

The diagnostic yield as judged by the prevalence of clinically relevant findings such as polyps, diverticula, inflammation, and angioectasias did not differ significantly between the 2 purgative regimens. The overall detection of polyps was similar with SPMC and PEG-Asc, with no differences in either small (≤5 mm) or larger (≥6 mm and ≥10 mm) lesions. Previous meta-analyses have shown that bowel preparation quality is paramount for polyp detection in colonoscopy (33), and data from CC similarly indicate that inadequate cleansing can reduce diagnostic yield, particularly for smaller or flat lesions (3,6,13). Nonetheless, this study was not powered for these diagnostic outcomes because 20.2% of examinations were performed for anemia investigation or Crohn's disease assessment, resulting in a heterogeneous population with lower expected neoplastic event rates. Consequently, the number of polyps ≥6 mm (18.8%) and ≥10 mm (7.3%) was limited, reducing the ability to detect between-group differences. It seems reasonable to consider that preparation quality plays a central role in capsule endoscopy diagnostic performance, but there is need for larger, more homogeneous studies to assess its impact on diagnostic yield in capsule-based procedures.

A limitation of our study is the absence of a higher-volume PEG-based comparator, which restricts direct assessment of whether either ultra-low-volume regimen performs comparably with traditional high-volume preparations. Consequently, relative efficacy can only be inferred indirectly from previous literature (13). However, high-volume regimens have been progressively abandoned in routine practice, and no previous study has evaluated ultra-low-volume purgatives in CC and/or panendoscopy (14). Given the limited eligible population and logistical constraints inherent to capsule endoscopy, we prioritized a powered comparison between 2 clinically relevant ultra-low-volume options.

Our randomized prospective study shows that despite SPMC better tolerability, PEG-Asc achieved superior bowel cleansing for both CC and panendoscopy. These findings suggest PEG-Asc as the preferred first-line ultra-low-volume preparation for capsule-based procedures.
