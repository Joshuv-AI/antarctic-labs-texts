Evaluating COVID-19 Response Within a Tribal Health Care Organization
Authors: Krista R Schaefer, Brianna Triplett, Olivia Lorenzo, Michelle Aregood, Donna Galbreath, Denise A Dillard, Ann Futterman Collier
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHealth care organizations of all types have adapted in response to COVID-19. Responding to the pandemic has varied across organizations and there are few standardized frameworks from which to evaluate an organizational response. This article reports COVID-19 responses by a tribal health care organization using a synthesized evaluation framework that integrated the organizational values and allowed for timely implementation of responses during this COVID-19 public health emergency.MethodsThe authors categorized each COVID-19 response into six domains, including leadership practices, patient engagement, operations management, teamwork and communication, public health, and external partnerships. Responses were analyzed by domain and across time.ResultsCOVID-19 responses included medical alerts, modifying delivery of care, establishing testing and vaccination sites, additional benefits for employees, as well as new processes for communication between employees and senior leadership. COVID-19 responses were most frequently categorized into the operation management domain (80%). Many responses, especially those that considered patients, employees, and the community, also fit other domains.DiscussionOperation management was the most frequent domain because the pandemic emerged rapidly and required swift, agile, action-oriented responses from the tribal health care organization. The majority of COVID-19 responses involved multiple, not single, domains.ConclusionOur evaluation approach captured areas of importance to the tribal health organization and may be pertinent for other indigenous organizations and rural communities. It is especially important to consider tribal perspectives in response to COVID-19 because of the prior history with Western medicine and infectious diseases, and health care limitations experienced due to rurality.

Introduction

At the onset of COVID-19, there were few public health guidelines and models to inform health care responses to a pandemic. Similarly, health care organizations had no guidance about how to evaluate the responses that were implemented. Many organizations likely faced similar COVID-19 challenges, as well as unique solutions that reflected regional and cultural special interests. Organizations had to identify funding for and procurement of supplies and engage their communities in new ways. In hindsight, there is much to be learned by sharing mistakes and successes to benefit future public health responses. Tribal health care organizations had to respond to the pandemic in culturally sensitive and appropriate ways for Alaska Native and American Indian (ANAI) people, who have experienced multigenerational, systematic disparities in medicine. 1–3 Little is known about how tribal health care organizations responded to the COVID-19 pandemic.

In 2018, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) published “Public Health Emergency Preparedness and Response Capabilities: National Standards for State, Local, Tribal, and Territorial Public Health” which includes six domains to prepare organizations for public health emergencies. 4 Although this is a concrete framework, it did not include a domain for organizational leadership or other decision-making structures needed to respond quickly to a pandemic. Other frameworks exist in the literature that have been adapted to guide planning and evaluating health care responses to COVID-19. 5–7 A diaspora of terms for a gold standard health care organization exists across these frameworks, including agile, adaptive, flexible, resilient, and innovative. 4–8 Considering the current literature, a critical limitation is the lack of a robust, standard evaluation framework for organizations to reflect on their pandemic responses.

Given the state of COVID-19, the necessity for health care organizations to rapidly adapt, and the unique setting and circumstances within tribal health care, the authors developed a synthesized evaluation framework to analyze what this tribal health care organization did to respond to the pandemic. This article first highlights six key domains that need to be considered for timely implementation of responses during a public health emergency. Then, the authors discuss a tribal health care organization’s COVID-19 responses categorized into domains and analyzed over time. This article helps establish a standard framework for organizations to reflect on their COVID-19 responses, provides a guide for other health care organizations, and elucidates key lessons learned.

Methods

Framework

Conceptual frameworks are not standardized for planning nor evaluating pandemic responses. There are many frameworks in the literature with analogous domains, but the frameworks do not have all of the same characteristics. Larger organizations such as the World Health Organization (WHO) have created resources for health care organizations to measure their performance or to guide planning to be ready for a future emergency, like the CDC. 4,8 Some literature provides conceptual frameworks for other pandemics like the flu. 7 Other literature puts forth lessons learned from the perspective of researching organization management and organization resilience. 5,6 The authors searched the literature for studies that provided a framework for how other organizations evaluated and analyzed their response to COVID-19, and synthesized these to create a robust framework with operationalizable domains. The following domains were included: leadership practices, patient engagement, operations management, teamwork and communication, public health, and external partnerships (Table 1).

The proposed evaluation framework encompasses the WHO and CDC’s domains but expands to include leadership, patient engagement, and external partners as distinct domains for organizations to consider when evaluating their COVID-19 responses. In addition, the domains align closely with Haldane and Nembhard’s work, 5,6 however the authors proposed terminology approachable to many organizations, including those outside health care. The domains aligned with the organization’s philosophy of care called the Nuka System of Care. 9,10 Nuka, an Alaska Native word that means strong, giant structures and living things, employs ANAI values of relationships to achieve the tribal health care organization’s goals. It is important when evaluating the COVID-19 responses in this setting to include the values that helped found and guide the organization. Although these values are specific to one organization, they can be applied to many other settings with similar missions.

Leadership Practices: This domain encompasses attributes of leaders at the health care organization and the decisions they make. Other frameworks also included governance and financing. Leadership decisions can help adapt the governance of the organization for rapid and flexible responses, create clear communication to employees, or show support by endorsing new initiatives.

Patient Engagement: The domain of patient engagement highlights how an organization focuses on patients and engages the community around the organization in health care initiatives. Other frameworks have a similar domain but focus on community resilience or simply putting people first.

Operations Management: The operations management domain is mandate driven. All of the existing frameworks include this domain as managing the operations creatively, including the facilities, products needed, and technology. The operations management domain focuses on the procedures that drive health care delivery, which include the physical space, guidelines for practice, facility closures, and employee mandates.

Teamwork and Communication: This domain spans from how service delivery is carried out by the clinical teams to how communication is quickly and clearly spread across an organization. Communication can include employee-focused messages, distribution of arising health information, or pertinent trainings. Other frameworks term this domain “health workforce.” Communication in other frameworks is termed “information management.”

Public Health: The public health domain focuses on the organization’s effort to align with the CDC, Department of Health and Social Services, and the State’s Department of Health. The domain includes health informatics and biosurveillance like community level data on vaccination efforts and herd immunity. Public health encompasses COVID-19 responses that improve population health like testing or vaccination.

External Partnerships: The external partnerships domain captures the importance of cross sector partnerships in responding to pandemics. These partnerships link health care organizations and their medical equipment suppliers, facilities managers, and external governing bodies to provide health care. In grouping other frameworks, this domain encompasses the macro-environment, surge management, and partnerships.

Site Description

Southcentral Foundation (SCF) was founded in 1982 and is a tribally-owned and operated health care organization, providing services for over 65,000 ANAI people, representing 229 Alaskan federally-recognized tribes living in southcentral Alaska. 11 Patients at SCF are referred to as customer-owners because they own their own health and drive their health care decisions, both individually and systemically. Health care is delivered through the Nuka System of Care, which is a relationship-based approach to transforming health care using integrated care teams. 11,12 SCF’s integrated care teams include behavioral health consultants, psychiatrists, midwives, and pharmacists. Medical specialists became advisory consultants to the integrated care team. The clinical and administrative workforce of more than 2300 people is 54% ANAI heritage. 13

In addition, SCF provides outpatient clinics in pediatrics, dental, optometry, complementary medicine, traditional healing, home-based services, and health education services. In partnership with the Alaska Native Tribal Health Consortium (ANTHC), SCF also co-owns and co-manages the Alaska Native Medical Center (ANMC), a 150-bed hospital in Anchorage, Alaska. Specialty services are offered at ANMC including an emergency department, internal medicine, oncology, orthopedics, ENT, surgery, and urology.

All of these operations changed dramatically during COVID-19, and rapid changes were possible because of the existing organizational structures, the relationship-based approach to work, and a newly and quickly created COVID Incident Command Team. This team met consistently throughout the pandemic to help guide organizational decisions and work with other tribal partners like ANTHC and state and federal entities. In addition to the multifaceted partnerships, the diversity of the organization was leveraged in creating the COVID Incident Command Team by including medical and public health professionals in addition to the quality assurance department, quality improvement specialists, data services staff, and researchers who were all ready to lend their expertise and inform SCF’s response to the pandemic.

The first case of COVID-19 in Alaska was March 12, 2020. Throughout the duration of the pandemic SCF and ANTHC provided a variety of testing locations and methods, to date amounting to over 360,000 tests. 13 Similarly, nearly 130,000 COVID-19 vaccinations have been administered. 13

Study Description

In this paper, the authors describe the tribal health care organization’s responses to COVID-19 using a synthesized evaluation framework. SCF COVID-19 responses were documented when announced through organization-wide emails. This list was reviewed and added to by clinical staff and administrative leadership for completeness. The authors did not document events that involved the withdrawal of services as the pandemic impact lessened, eg, closing of COVID-19 testing sites. The list of COVID-19 responses became our data source. This research was reviewed and deemed not research by the tribal research review committee.

The authors utilized a modified version of Kiger & Varpio’s (2020) thematic approach to analyze the data. 14 First, the authors familiarized themselves with all of the data, whereby two researchers (KS, AC) separately reviewed the entire list of SCF COVID-19 responses (N = 110 events). Second, initial codes were generated based on the synthesized domains listed in Table 1 and each response was categorized into one of the six domains. The authors found that many of the COVID-19 responses could fit into more than one domain. To address this issue, an additional coding scheme was created to identify which domain was primary, secondary, and tertiary. The primary domain was listed first, with secondary following. If a third domain was also applicable, it was noted in parentheses. For example, “Vaccine clinics are open to employees, customer-owners, and now household members of empaneled customer-owners” was coded primarily as patient engagement, secondarily as operations management, and then third as public health. Patient engagement was prioritized as the primary domain because this response focused on expanding vaccination eligibility to patient’s household members if they were not already a patient at SCF. The researchers met to review the categorization and build consensus about how to code each response. One researcher (KS) reviewed the entire response list again to ensure consistent application of the six domains. All coding was done in Microsoft Excel.

Results

Summary of SCF Responses to COVID-19

SCF created testing sites, vaccination clinics, and managed staffing shortages throughout the duration of the pandemic. The COVID-19 responses were quite varied and ranged from medical alerts to employee support. More specifically, COVID-19 responses related to health care changes included how medical and dental care would be provided safely, logistics of setting up testing and vaccination sites, and communication to and from senior leadership. Responses related to employee support included care of employees through pay, benefits, and extra paid leave. Responses to the pandemic were both process oriented, eg, leadership team was available for fireside chats, and functional, eg, personal protective equipment was provided and housing was available for employees to quarantine after workplace exposure if they chose not to return home. Some of the responses involved events that continued to evolve, such as the expansion of testing and vaccination sites and who was eligible from customer-owners to employees to the community.

Changes in Responses Over Time

In addition to categorizing the responses by the domains in the synthesized evaluation framework, the authors examined the timing of responses by domain in Figure 1. There were three peaks in cases in Alaska. 15 Similarly, there were three peaks in COVID-19 responses. During the first peak around April to June 2020, operational management and public health were the primary domains emphasized. During the second peak at the beginning of 2021, operations management, patient engagement, and public health were the primary domains. During the third peak toward the end of summer 2021, the overall number of responses appear to be lower and were dominated by operations management and leadership practices.

Discussion

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the most frequent type of responses that occurred at the tribal health care organization in Southcentral Alaska fell into the operations management domain, with over 80% of all activities involving operational decisions. This made sense given that the pandemic emerged rapidly and required swift, agile, action-oriented responses from the health care organization. Although the number of operational responses decreased over time, this domain still dominated in the categorization of responses. Because the pandemic evolved rapidly, clinical administrators and leadership needed to make tactical decisions so that they could be responsive. It is noteworthy that the majority of operational decisions involved multiple and not single domains, ie, operation management combined with either public health, external partners, teamwork and communication, or patient engagement. Thus, even when operational decisions were made by senior leadership they also involved employees, stakeholders, external partners, and the community at large.

Public health was the second most frequent (29%) domain of COVID-19 responses. Naturally with a pandemic, many responses were within the domain of public health as population-level prevention and harm reduction efforts were put in place against COVID-19 infection. Most of the public health responses were in combination with other domains like external partnerships, operations management, and patient engagement. This fits with the fundamentals of public health and facilitating population health efforts for a community.

External partnerships (16%), patient engagement (15%), and leadership practices (12%) were the next most common domains, which reflects many of the Nuka System of Care relationship-based approach at SCF. These values include responsiveness to patients, employees, and the surrounding community. At the health care organization, multiple representatives from different departments served on an emergently formed COVID Incident Command Team, and these members had the opportunity to voice concerns that reflected their own team members and constituents. Furthermore, in their leadership practices, the senior leadership team made decisions that could be considered beneficial to all employees, such as they corresponded daily and then weekly via emails, they provided lodging for employees working in high-risk areas, continued to compensate employees that could not perform their jobs during the peak of the pandemic, incentivized vaccines, and provided retention pay and COVID self-care leave.

It was initially surprising that the number of teamwork and communication responses was so low because this is a strong value in the Nuka System of Care. Yet when examined, the teamwork and communication responses were deeply responsive to employees, even if the quantity of responses in this domain was low. For example, housing and childcare stipends were provided for employees, and new communication channels were quickly developed that involved an employee-wide communication system utilizing SharePoint, a web-based organizational platform, and regular fireside chats with the leadership team. In these instances, the quality and duration of the responses need to be considered, not just the quantity of responses.

There was also a pattern of change in COVID-19 responses over the two years of observation. Operations management predominated but decreased in number of responses as the pandemic progressed. The authors saw three distinct peaks with all responses attenuating over time. In the first peak, operations management and public health were the most frequent responses. During the second peak, patient engagement was included in many COVID-19 responses. During the third peak, the number of responses included operations management and leadership practices. Leadership practices may have become more prominent later on to sustain the organization, bring employees together around a common goal, and re-emphasize the organizational values during a long pandemic response from SCF. Operations management is the most important and requires intense, immediate action. These initial responses should also align clearly with public health efforts. Over time, there was more time for clinical leadership to focus on patient engagement and leadership practices to best service the health care community. These patterns of responses over time have implications for the future health care organization planning.

Through this self-reflection on COVID-19 responses from a tribal health care organization, the authors utilized a synthesized evaluation framework to report SCF’s COVID-19 responses. The domains are not distinct, but still provide a structured way to reflect and support the interdisciplinary work needed during a pandemic. It is realistic that COVID-19 responses are multidimensional, and this reflects the complexity of organizational decisions. It may also reflect the relationship orientation of the Nuka System of Care: most decisions involved staff, patients, and community members. Another lesson learned was that senior leadership succeeded in taking swift, responsive action that benefited the lives of patients, employees, and the community in Anchorage at large. The categorization of COVID-19 responses into domains did not fit into or establish an expected distribution of responses across the domains, instead it highlighted the multidimensional and complex work a health care organization does to response to public health emergencies.

Some limitations exist. The pandemic is on-going and more recent COVID-19 responses have not been included. These may look different compared to the responses an organization first utilizes during a pandemic, but this evaluation of the start of a pandemic may be instrumental to future public health emergencies. In addition, the list of responses was most likely not a complete list, but it was verified by SCF leadership and extensive enough to analyze the responses in a framework that may be useful to other organizations.

Conclusions

This report describes SCF’s response to the COVID-19 pandemic. It highlights the importance of a decision-making structure that allows for timely implementation of responses during a public health emergency. The authors believe the strong philosophical structure of this tribal health care organization provided the building blocks for a rapid, flexible, and effective response to COVID-19 in each of the domains of the synthesized evaluation framework for health care response to COVID-19. The importance of self-reflection on COVID-19 responses can lead to continued improvements during this pandemic or applied lessons learned in the future. The authors hope that this paper and synthesized framework can be used as a guide for other communities and for future responses at health care organizations.
