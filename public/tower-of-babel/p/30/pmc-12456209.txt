Successes, lessons learned and recommendations for the future: Experiences from the first year of Ontario’s nirsevimab program for respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) prophylaxis in infants
Authors: Bosco Paes, Erin Fleischer, Jane Healey, Eddy Lau, Graeme N. Smith
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAt the conclusion of the 2024/2025 respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) season in Ontario, a panel of healthcare providers who were actively involved in the conduct of the province’s first RSV prophylaxis program with nirsevimab in infants, assembled to discuss their experiences. The discussion was a follow-up to a larger pre-RSV season meeting held in June 2024 to develop recommendations supporting a universal prophylaxis program for infants in Ontario. The principal objective of the 2025 meeting was to identify components of the province’s program that were successful, as well as those that posed barriers to nirsevimab uptake. A modified version of the World Health Organization’s analytical framework for health systems was used to structure the discussion around five key programmatic building blocks: (1) governance and leadership, (2) funding and reimbursement, (3) logistics, demand and distribution, (4) awareness and education, and (5) surveillance and monitoring. A consensus was reached on updated recommendations to enhance the success of Ontario’s nirsevimab program for future RSV seasons. These recommendations aim to ensure equitable access for all infants, achieve optimal uptake, and provide the best protection against RSV infection for Ontario’s infants.

Introduction

In April 2023, Health Canada approved a comprehensive respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) pediatric prophylaxis program with nirsevimab,1 an extended half-life human monoclonal antibody, for the prevention of respiratory syncytial virus lower respiratory tract infection (RSV LRTI). It marked the first time a product against the infectious pathogen became available for widespread use across the country. Approval of a maternal RSV vaccine (RSVpreF) followed shortly after for the same indication in infants.2 Given the significant economic and medical burden that RSV inflicts on the community, particularly in young children,3,4 the potential value of effective, all-inclusive prophylaxis in children aged <2 years was viewed as being highly beneficial. In 2024, Canada’s National Advisory Committee on Immunization (NACI) recommended the preferential adoption of universal RSV immunization programs with nirsevimab rather than RSVpreF.5 The recommendation was based on both the higher and possibly prolonged efficacy of nirsevimab against medically-attended RSV LRTI infection (MARI) and RSV hospitalization (RSVH) in healthy preterm and term infants throughout the RSV season, as reported in clinical trials.6–10

In June 2024, a panel of Ontario healthcare professionals (HCPs) who treat pregnant women and young children, developed recommendations to support a provincial nirsevimab program for infants11 based on the early experiences of three countries that implemented programs during the 2023/2024 RSV season. Prophylaxis in the United States, France and Spain resulted in a reduction of: 74–98% RSV-related hospitalizations,12–22 72–92% RSV-related intensive care unit (ICU) stays,13,14,16,18,20,23 77–87% RSV-associated emergency room (ER) visits,12,18 89% MARI episodes,12 and 78% outpatient visits for RSV-related bronchiolitis.24

Ontario RSV expert panel 2025

In July 2024, Ontario became one of five Canadian provinces and territories to announce a government-funded nirsevimab program for all infants during the 2024/2025 RSV season.25–29 At the season’s conclusion, five Ontario RSV experts – a neonatologist, two pediatricians, an obstetrician, and nurse practitioner – who were actively involved in the program’s implementation, assembled to discuss successes and barriers, and to update the previous recommendations to optimize the 2025/2026 RSV seasonal program. The discussion is summarized below, within a modified version of an analytical framework used by the World Health Organization30 to describe health systems through the lens of five building blocks: (1) governance and leadership, (2) funding and reimbursement, (3) logistics, demand and distribution, (4) awareness and education, and (5) surveillance and monitoring. Where appropriate, sections are further categorized into experiences related to nirsevimab administration for i) infants born during the RSV season (in-season cohort), and ii) for eligible infants born before the start of the season (catch-up cohort).

2024/2025 Nirsevimab program overview and discussion

Section I. Governance and leadership

This category refers to decision-makers and leaders who developed and conveyed policies defining the availability, accessibility, and delivery of nirsevimab.

Summary of the 2024/2025 RSV program

During the 2024/2025 RSV season, Ontario’s Ministry of Health (MOH) played a central role in the oversight of the province’s nirsevimab program, while hospitals made institution-level decisions regarding administration workflow. Public health units (PHUs) and primary care providers (PCPs) chose their own level of involvement in product administration (Figure 1). Early estimates indicate that the program from Nov 1, 2024 – March 31, 2025, resulted in a nirsevimab uptake of 67.7% for eligible in-season infants prior to discharge from the birth hospital.31 Figure 1.Governance and leadership of the nirsevimab program in infants.

Governance and leadership of the nirsevimab program in infants.

Nirsevimab administration for the in-season cohort

A publicly funded RSV immunization program was announced in July 2024, followed shortly thereafter with program guidance and eligibility criteria. Hospitals were tasked with rapidly developing their individual programs, including administration workflows, educating staff, and creating medical directives. Most hospitals launched their immunization programs by early November, though some hospitals experienced delays due to internal approval processes and challenges with effective staff collaboration.

There was uncertainty around the program’s end date, with HCPs expressing confusion about whether nirsevimab should be administered to infants born in March 2025, and how this might affect their eligibility in the next season, as well as what guidance to offer for infants born after the end of the season.

Nirsevimab administration for the catch-up cohort

The program was announced to a broad range of HCPs and locations that would potentially administer nirsevimab to the catch-up cohort and in-season infants who did not receive prophylaxis prior to hospital discharge. However, lack of primary responsibility resulted in a fragmented system where not all hospitals, PHUs and PCPs opted to administer nirsevimab to these cohorts, causing confusion and frustration for parents.

Key facilitators

Timely program announcements

The panel emphasized the importance of early guidance on well-defined program parameters – fixed start and end dates, and prophylaxis launch date at least three months before the program’s start date – to give hospitals adequate planning time, and allow community providers the opportunity to remind parents of young infants to return for nirsevimab at program onset.

Program leadership

The panel believed that the program’s success relied on the development and enforcement of mandates to ensure that all eligible infants, including the catch-up cohort, had well-defined access to receive nirsevimab, emphasizing the importance for the 20% of individuals in Ontario without a PCP.32 Given that PHUs distribute nirsevimab in Ontario and play an important role in immunization education and delivery, the panel reasoned that they are potentially well-positioned to identify eligible infants born prior to the season, conduct clinics, and facilitate program success, though additional administrative support and funding would be required.

Section II. Funding and reimbursement

This category identifies the bodies and organizations paying for nirsevimab, and the financial resources for implementation support.

Summary of the 2024/2025 RSV program

Nirsevimab was publicly funded by the provincial MOH for infants meeting eligibility criteria (Figure 2). Figure 2.Funding and reimbursement of the nirsevimab program in infants.*The MOH also provided public funding for RSVpreF33; prior to this announcement, individuals could opt to pay for the product out-of-pocket or through their private insurance.

Funding and reimbursement of the nirsevimab program in infants.

Physicians practicing in the community were paid a small fee to administer immunizations via reimbursement pathways, but due to payment regulations, this fee could not be claimed by hospital-based physicians and there was no specific nirsevimab billing code to track product receipt.34 Additionally, counseling parents about RSV and nirsevimab in hospitals added significantly to HCPs’ workload, but was uncompensated. Although mechanisms exist to pursue changes to Ontario’s physician payment structure, the process is complex. Any additional funding for RSV-related care would draw from existing resources, potentially impacting other services.

Key facilitators

Hospital funding

Implementing a nirsevimab program translates into hospital costs for creating program infrastructure and staff training. Advanced funding allocation is needed to optimize rollout and inform budgetary decisions in future years.

Nirsevimab billing codes

Billing codes specific to nirsevimab are critical to facilitate administration by PCPs and enable tracking of doses. In the United States, the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) quickly implemented specific billing codes for administration,35 which likely streamlined this process and enabled more accurate tracking of nirsevimab receipt within the community.

Section III. Logistics, demand and distribution

Logistics, demand and distribution of the nirsevimab program in infants.

Summary of the 2024/2025 RSV program

Nirsevimab administration for the in-season cohort

Each hospital was responsible for creating in-season protocols for their respective programs, including the development of medical directives to authorize additional HCP types to administer nirsevimab. This was time-consuming, but made more manageable through sharing of hospital templates. There was considerable variation in the development of directives, and approval delays occurred due to hierarchical administrative and nursing institutional policies.

The Ontario midwives’ scope of practice did not include nirsevimab administration, causing an uptake barrier given that midwives deliver 20% of the province’s births.36 Most hospitals navigated this obstacle by including midwives in the medical directive to administer nirsevimab within the hospital setting, while some directives extended to homebirths. In the latter’s absence, parents were instructed to have their infant immunized in hospital or by community HCPs, which posed an additional deterrent.

In-season, hospital-based administration of nirsevimab was successful when staff supported the program, and panelists remarked that most parents accepted nirsevimab, resulting in good uptake. Other families opted to wait and consider immunization at a later date.

Nirsevimab administration for the catch-up cohort

Panelists spoke of multiple barriers in implementing programs for eligible infants born before the season. Neither PHUs, hospitals nor PCPs accepted overall responsibility for nirsevimab administration, primarily attributed to a lack of funding. Not every hospital had the capacity to hold large public clinics, though some mounted smaller clinics specifically for second-season, high-risk children. Contacting families to alert them of clinic timings was a labor-intensive process.

Additional logistical challenges were related to dose distribution and allocation. This included timing and frequency of product delivery by PHUs, tailored, weight-banded dosing, availability of administration sites, and limited ability to predict supply needs for a novel program.

Key facilitators

Optimal and clearly communicated program start and end dates

The panel reasoned that a nirsevimab program start date of October 1st would likely precede the season, and would be logical given that once school begins, disease transmission escalates resulting in increased RSV risk for infant siblings. Panelists also discussed the feasibility of initiating the program earlier in September, to offer timely protection and minimize need to access catch-up clinics. This strategy is supported by nirsevimab’s long half-life (71 days) and clinical efficacy data extending beyond 150 days,37,38 even up to 8 months.5 Additional clinical data and formal endorsement from both NACI and Ontario’s MOH would strengthen the case for implementing this change.

The panel felt that the campaign’s end date should be flexible, based on Ontario’s regional RSV activity. Members agreed that regardless of the campaign’s start and end dates, specific announcements are critical to eliminate confusion for both HCPs and patients, clarify an infant’s eligibility in both the first and second season, and allow time for HCPs to plan and educate the community.

Program champions

Hospitals that were successful with nirsevimab implementation often had local leaders or an executive team comprising staff from both the maternal and infant health disciplines. These individuals and teams worked collaboratively with PHUs regionally to educate and motivate obstetricians, midwives, nurses, pediatricians and PCPs, through informal discussions, presentations, and dissemination of materials, serving as points of contact for questions and implementation support. Early engagement of hospital staff by a leading team was identified as a key facilitator for nirsevimab program success in the United States.39 The panel recommended extending the champion model to each PHU region, believing that it would be especially beneficial in facilitating nirsevimab uptake for the catch-up cohort. Such a model would be variable and reflective of the regional composition, needs, and available resources, and dependent on who in the community (potentially family health teams, or HCPs in hospital-based clinics, community health centers, designated private community offices or PHU-organized clinics) could optimally administer nirsevimab to the catch-up infants or newborns born in-season who did not receive nirsevimab in hospital.

Enhanced collaboration with local PHU

Whilst the nirsevimab program is becoming firmly established, enhanced collaboration with PHUs is critical to improving program awareness, administration, and access for future seasons. Key activities include instituting nirsevimab clinics, developing handouts of clinic schedules and RSV information, and addressing challenges in product ordering and delivery.

Opt-out policy

Panelists were in favor of implementing a standard of care, opt-out policy, for administering nirsevimab to in-season infants to optimize uptake. However, they acknowledged that such a policy may only be feasible once the program is firmly grounded.

Section IV. Awareness and education

This category focused on the various methods to guide nirsevimab acceptance and uptake by both HCPs and the public, through education.

Summary of the 2024/2025 RSV program

Nirsevimab education for the in-season cohort

HCPs adopted a range of approaches in the dissemination of RSV and nirsevimab information to expectant parents (Figure 4). Examples of patient and HCP-facing materials included those developed by the Ministry-affiliated Provincial Council for Maternal and Child Health (PCMCH), professional associations, and pharmaceutical industry (unbranded). In some hospitals, RSV program leaders collaborated with local PHUs to circulate local informational materials. Ideally, these were given to pregnant individuals at the time of tetanus, diphtheria and pertussis (Tdap) vaccination to facilitate education and receipt of RSVpreF vaccine at 32–26 weeks gestation, if they opted against nirsevimab for their infant. Although the Society of Obstetricians and Gynecologists of Canada recommends that prenatal HCPs should discuss both RSVpreF and nirsevimab with patients,40 some focused solely on RSVpreF and deferred nirsevimab discussions to pediatricians and family physicians, resulting in missed opportunities for early counseling. Figure 4.Awareness and education related to the nirsevimab program in infants.

Awareness and education related to the nirsevimab program in infants.

At the hospital level, different workflows involving various HCPs were adopted to enhance parent awareness of the program, whilst considering available resources and staff familiarity with nirsevimab. Despite recommendations for prenatal counseling on RSV prophylaxis, many families first received counseling at the time of childbirth. As a result, some hospitals opted to maximize time available for counseling by offering nirsevimab in the post-partum care unit rather than in the labor and delivery setting. Other hospitals designed workflows comprising multiple opportunities to support uptake, in which nurses offered nirsevimab alongside Vitamin K administration, and if declined, pediatricians would follow-up with families on the post-partum care unit.

Nirsevimab education for the catch-up cohort

The provincial MOH publicly announced the program and its parameters, but played a relatively small role in public education. In contrast, both HCPs and the pharmaceutical industry, led efforts to inform the public about RSV and nirsevimab. Notably, many HCPs had limited experience using monoclonal antibodies in a vaccine-like context and expressed uncertainty about how best to counsel parents on nirsevimab.

Key facilitators

Leadership on education efforts

The panel agreed that a key barrier to achieving optimal nirsevimab uptake is convincing families – often unfamiliar with RSV – to protect their infants using a monoclonal antibody, a type of product they may not recognize or understand. To optimize uptake, the provincial MOH should facilitate education about nirsevimab through public campaigns and implement a central resource hub, in collaboration with PHUs, where HCPs could direct parents for reliable information.

Accessible educational materials for parents

The panel expressed that the most effective resources were those that were available to families in different languages and literacy levels, posted through physical and digital media.

HCP education

The panel viewed the education of HCPs on RSV and nirsevimab as a weakness during the 2024/2025 season. Members proposed that PHUs should be more actively involved through hospital partnership. The panel also suggested integrating information about RSV and nirsevimab into both the Ontario Perinatal Record to facilitate prenatal discussion on RSV preventives, and the Rourke Baby Record, a knowledge translation tool that supports PCPs in delivering preventive care to infants and young children.

Early and consistent parent engagement

The panel proposed several strategies to escalate prophylaxis uptake by enhancing prenatal and postnatal parent education on RSV and nirsevimab. HCPs need timely information on product availability and eligibility well before the start of the season, so they can inform all families, including those in the catch-up cohort, on how and when to access nirsevimab.

Multiple opportunities for nirsevimab receipt

To optimize uptake, hospitals should continue to opt for nirsevimab administration workflows best suited for their institutions and routinely update protocols to maximize efficiency, while providing multiple opportunities for nirsevimab delivery.

Section V. Surveillance and monitoring

The ability to monitor nirsevimab administration and track relevant healthcare resource utilization-related outcomes is critical to evaluating the success of this new program.

Summary of the 2024/2025 RSV program

Ontario’s MOH is leading the province’s generation of real-world evidence (RWE) on nirsevimab coverage and impact (Figure 5). All nirsevimab administrative data are entered at the hospital level into Ontario’s Better Outcomes Registry & Network (BORN), a repository for every pregnancy and childbirth in Ontario. Community uptake measurement is limited given the lack of a nirsevimab-specific billing code; instead, an imprecise standard code for vaccinations and all other immunizations is currently used in non-birth settings. Figure 5.Surveillance and monitoring of the nirsevimab program for RSV in infants.

Surveillance and monitoring of the nirsevimab program for RSV in infants.

RWE on nirsevimab’s effectiveness and impact on MARI-related ER visits, hospitalization rates, and ICU admissions, is valuable in supporting uptake while facilitating program comparisons with other provinces, territories, and countries. Panel members discussed institution-level monitoring, including internal hospital reviews of nirsevimab administration, outcomes and incurred morbidities. Some hospitals opted to disseminate parental surveys to deepen understanding of experiences accessing nirsevimab.

Key facilitators

Comprehensive immunization records

While each child born in Ontario should receive a “Yellow Card,” the province’s paper-based personal immunization record, several challenges emerged with its use during the 2024/25 RSV season. Updated cards that included a section for nirsevimab were unavailable until mid-season. Some hospitals experienced card shortages, while others failed to distribute them. While some panelists advocated for the use of the CANImmunize application, given its benefits as an immunization record-keeper,41 the panel unanimously endorsed the development of centralized, individual-level electronic registries, akin to Spain and Chile,42,43 to support consistent documentation and province-wide tracking.

Conclusions

The 2025 panel meeting of Ontario HCP experts who were actively involved in the 2024/2025 infant prophylaxis program with nirsevimab resulted in a comprehensive discussion about experiences during the program’s initial year.
