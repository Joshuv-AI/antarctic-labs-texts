The Comparative Sensitivity to Ketamine-Induced Neuronal Death in Juvenile and Adult Rats
Authors: Sumit Sarkar, Gonçalo Gamboa da Costa, Kellie Woodling, Shiny Mathew, Ikram Elayan, John Talpos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKetamine, an N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist, was first approved by the US FDA as KETALAR® for induction and maintenance of general anesthesia. Ketamine has been used off-label for depression treatment, while its S-enantiomer, esketamine (SPRAVATO®), is approved for treatment-resistant depression and major depressive disorder with acute suicidal ideation or behavior. High doses of ketamine have been linked to neurotoxicity. The effects of acute ketamine exposure on brain development during childhood and adolescence in humans remain poorly understood. To better characterize the safety of ketamine, we treated juvenile (postnatal day (PND) 21, 30, 35) and adult rats (PND 90) with a single subcutaneous dose of ketamine (50, 75, and 100 mg/kg). Seventy-two hours later, tissue samples were collected. Tissue samples were processed, mounted as 5-µm-thick sections, and stained with H&E for neurohistopathological evaluation. Additionally, key pharmacokinetic parameters of ketamine were determined. Neuronal necrosis was detected in the retrosplenial cortex in adult female rats treated with ketamine (100 mg/kg), while no histological changes were observed in any other groups. These data are the first to demonstrate that acute ketamine can cause neuronal necrosis in adult animals and do not support juvenile rats as having an increased susceptibility to ketamine-induced neuronal death. Adult female rats showed a marked increase in norketamine, the primary metabolite of ketamine in serum. We hypothesize that the elevated levels of norketamine may contribute to these sex- and age-specific histopathological changes.Keywords: adolescence, sex differences, pharmacokinetics, age, NMDA receptor, neurotoxicity, MK-801

Introduction

Ketamine (KETALAR®), a potent N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist,1,2 was first approved for use as an injectable dissociative anesthetic in adults in 1970.3,4 Ketamine has gained popularity for treating depression after reports of its fast-acting antidepressant effects in humans after a single dose.5,6 S-ketamine, an enantiomer of ketamine, 7 is approved for treating treatment-resistant depression in adults and for short-term treatment of suicidal ideation or behavior under the brand name SPRAVATO®. 8

While ketamine has a long history of use, concerns exist over its safety. For example, an early study described intracytoplasmic vacuoles in degenerating neurons (commonly known as Olney lesions) that formed hours after the administration of NMDA receptor antagonists, 9 including ketamine, in the cingulate cortex of adult rats. These vacuoles are transient, appearing several hours after drug administration and largely disappearing by 18 hours post-exposure.9,10 While the implications of Olney lesions are still debated, it has since become apparent that some NMDA receptor antagonists can also cause neuronal necrosis in the retrosplenial/posterior cingulate cortex when administered at high doses.11-15 Interestingly, this phenomenon appears to be age dependent. No acute vacuole formation was observed after the administration of the prototypical NMDA receptor antagonist MK-801 until rats were at least 1.5 months old. 16 The finding that NMDA receptor antagonists induced vacuolization in an age-dependent fashion suggests that younger animals may have a different sensitivity or response to NMDA receptor antagonist-mediated neurotoxicity. This is supported by subsequent work14,15 showing an increased sensitivity to the effects of NMDA receptor antagonist-induced apoptosis in postnatal day 7 rats. 17 The neurotoxic potential of ketamine in very young animals was confirmed in the mouse, 18 rat, 19 and in the rhesus monkey.20,21 Importantly, in the rhesus monkey, neurotoxicity was observed during gestation, and at PND 5, but not at PND 35. 20 These data, taken in conjunction with findings of Olney lesions in adult animals, suggest there may be a U-shaped sensitivity to NMDA antagonists with very young and adult animals being vulnerable, but with a “protected” period occurring in juvenile animals. However, this is speculative and based on data from different species, different endpoints (ie, intracellular vacuole vs neuronal necrosis or apoptosis), and assessment methods (H&E, Fluoro-Jade C, cleaved caspase-3).

The lack of comprehensive data on the comparative neurotoxicity of ketamine in juvenile animals makes it difficult to describe the relationship between age and the relative threshold for ketamine-induced neurotoxicity. Understanding this relationship is important from a regulatory and brain ontogeny perspective. We addressed this data-gap by testing the effects of ketamine in rats on PND 21, 30, or 35. While the rate of rodent brain development does not perfectly mirror that of humans, PND 21 to 35 rats model brain development in children as young as 3 or 4,22-26 and as old as 12 years of age.23,27-30 Additionally, an adult cohort was included (PND 87-93) to determine if juvenile rats had differential sensitivity to the neurotoxic effects of ketamine when compared to adult rats. The doses used in this study were intentionally high, above those needed to induce a surgical plane of anesthesia 31 to increase the likelihood of observing neuronal necrosis, a necessity for determining the effects of age on ketamine-induced neurotoxicity.

When designing this study, we followed the 2018 draft guidance published by the FDA on developing drugs for the treatment of Major Depressive Disorder. 32 Five bilateral coronal sections were assessed per animal, covering the regions highlighted in the Society of Toxicologic Pathology-recommended best practices for sampling the nervous system in nonclinical toxicity studies, 33 but adapted to accommodate the small size of the PND 21-35 rat brain. 34 Five-µm-thick sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) 33 and evaluated by a board-certified pathologist. This approach is in line with the FDA draft guidance and has previously been shown to be sensitive 35 to the positive control used in this study (MK-801). To increase the translational impact of this work, we also performed a pharmacokinetic analysis of ketamine, and its primary metabolite norketamine, in each age group. Having information on plasma exposures at each age group will allow us to better understand if any observed interactions between age, sex, and neuropathological changes are driven by differences in plasma drug concentrations or are instead the result of differential responses to ketamine at the receptor level. These data will assist in determining safety margins for the use of ketamine in a pediatric population.

Material and Methods

Animals

All animal procedures were approved by the National Center for Toxicological Research’s (NCTR) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and conducted in full accordance with the Public Health Service Policy on Humane Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Sprague-Dawley rats (Crl:CD(SD); Charles River, Raleigh, N.C.) were used for this work. Separate cohorts of animals were used for histological assessments and determination of pharmacokinetic parameters. Animals had ad libitum access to food and water. Pregnant animals were fed NIH-07 open formula diet chow before and after giving birth (Envigo, Madison, WI) and adult and weaned animals were fed rodent NTP-2000 chow (Envigo, Madison, WI). Water was purified with an Avidity Reverse Osmosis system (Avidity Science LLC, Waterford, WI, USA). Alpha-dri bedding (Shepherd Specialty Paper, Richland, Mi) was used for all animals. Animals were maintained on a 12-hour light/dark cycle in a room maintained at 23.9°C (±1.1°C). Relative humidity was maintained between 30 and 70% with 10 air changes per hour.

Animals in the PND 90 cohort were not time-mated. Their age ranged from PND 87 to 93 at the time of treatment (PND 52-73 upon arrival). Animals in the PND 21-35 group were born in-house from pregnant animals purchased externally. Time-mated animals arrived on-site at gestational days 9, 10, or 11. Litters were culled to 4 animals per sex on PND 4. In instances where dams had fewer than 4 pups of either sex, cross-fostering occurred. On PND 21, animals were weaned and tattooed for identification. Pregnant animals were single housed upon arrival. Sample size was 11-12 animals per sex, per treatment, per age for histological assessments, and 7 animals per sex, per age for determination of key pharmacokinetic parameters.

Study Design

At weaning, the males from a litter were all assigned to one cage (n = 4), while the females were all assigned to another cage (n = 4). Pups from that litter would all be treated on the same PND. Treatments were assigned in a counterbalanced manner to ensure that each male from a litter received a treatment unique from all other males in the litter. The same was done with the females. Each treatment was assigned at least once per litter, but never more than twice. In instances where a treatment was assigned twice within a litter, it was assigned to animals of different sexes. Adult animals of the same age were housed 2 per cage. Each animal in a cage was assigned a different treatment. Animals in the adult cohort were treated on days that overlapped with the juvenile cohorts.

Experimental Treatment

The ketamine used in this study was pharmaceutical grade (ketamine HCl, Ketaset Injectable C IIIN; Patterson Veterinary, CO, USA) and came in a 100 mg/mL solution with sterile water. The solution contained not more than 0.1 mg/mL of benzethonium chloride as a preservative. As an FDA-approved, pharmaceutical-grade veterinary product, the ketamine solution meets established regulatory purity standards (≥99%) consistent with GLP requirements (21 CFR Part 58). This solution was diluted to the necessary concentration with sterile physiological saline.

For histopathological analysis, animals were treated with a single dose of racemic ketamine (50, 75, 100 mg/kg, s.c.), a positive control (2 or 3 mg/kg MK-801, i.p.; Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA, dissolved in sterile physiological saline), or a physiological saline vehicle (s.c.) on PND 21, 30, 35, or 90. All animals assigned to the MK-801 group were initially treated with 3 mg/kg of MK-801. However, female rats frequently failed to regain consciousness within 6 hours of administration of MK-801 (3 mg/kg), causing us to use a lower concentration (2 mg/kg) in females for the rest of the study (Supplemental Table 1). This is consistent with reported sex effects on pharmacokinetic parameters of MK-801. 36 Pharmacokinetic studies were performed in all experimental groups, but only with 100 mg/kg (s.c.) of ketamine.

After the treatment, time to unresponsiveness and time to normal ambulation were recorded in animals used for pathological analysis. Any animal still unconscious at the end of the day (at least 6 hours post-treatment) was euthanized via CO2 (confirmed via thoracotomy). Cage-side observations were made every day following exposure and no abnormalities were observed. All remaining animals were euthanized after 72 hours with Euthasol (i.p.) and brains were collected for histopathological evaluation. Pilot data suggested a sex-dependent effect on pharmacokinetic parameters. To optimize estimates of key pharmacokinetic parameters, different collection intervals were used for males and females, regardless of age. For the pharmacokinetic studies, samples were taken from males at 10, 20, 40, 60, 100, 200, and 300 min, and from females at 10, 30, 60, 90, 140, 200, 270, and 360 min post-injection. Serial sampling was performed via the tail vein of adult animals (n = 7), while a single sample was collected via cardiac puncture in PND 21-35 animals (n = 7 per time point) as part of the euthanasia process. Upon collection, blood samples were kept at room temperature and allowed to coagulate (30-60 mins). The samples were then centrifuged for 12 min at 9,000×g. The serum was then collected and placed into 500 µL tubes and stored at −80°C until processed.

Tissue Processing

Once the animal achieved a deep surgical plane of anesthesia (Euthasol, i.p.) and was unresponsive to pain (toe pinch confirmed by tail pinch), the chest cavity was opened. A blunted needle (14 gauge for adult and 20 gauge for juvenile animals) was inserted into the left cardiac ventricle and the tip of the needle was positioned in the aorta. Once the perfusion started, the right atrium was cut. Animals were perfused with room temperature heparinized (5000 units of heparin sulfate/liter) saline (0.9% NaCl) for 6 min, followed by neutral buffered formalin (NBF; 10%, recycled; PureForm 2010, Easton, MD, USA) for 12 min. A 10 mL/min flow rate was used for the PND 21-35 animals and 30 mL/min rate for the 90-day-old animals. After perfusion, animals were stored in 10% NBF at room temperature for about 48 hours without opening the calvarium. The brain was then removed, cut into 5 parts, and placed into cassettes. The cassettes were placed in ethanol of increasing concentrations at 40 min intervals (70%, 80%, 95%, 95%, 100%, 100%, 100%). After the last ethanol bath, cassettes were transferred to a series of 3 xylene baths (37 min each; Leica Biosystems, Buffalo Grove, IL, USA). When done, cassettes were placed in 3 baths of infiltrating media (Formula “R,” Leica Biosystems, Buffalo Grove, IL, USA), each lasting 40 min prior to being embedded in the same media. A microtome was used to collect 5-µm-thick sections at the desired levels. Sections were mounted on slides (StatLab, McKinny, TX, USA) and placed in an oven (60°C) for 2 hours. Slides were then bathed in xylene 3 times (1.5 min per). This was followed by 2 baths in 100% ethanol (1 min) and a bath in 95% ethanol (1.5 min). Slides were then rinsed in water (2 min) prior to being placed in Gill’s III Hematoxylin (Leica Biosystems, Buffalo Grove, IL, USA). Slides then underwent a brief acid wash (4 sec; 0.5% solution of acetic acid and deionized water; Pittsburgh, PA, USA) prior to being rinsed in tap water. Slides were then bathed in a mixture of 70% ethanol and ammonia (2 mins) before again being rinsed in water. Slides were bathed in 70% ethanol (1 mins) followed by eosin Y (Leica Biosystems, Buffalo Grove, IL, USA). Slides were then bathed in 95% ethanol twice (30 sec), followed by 2 washes in 100% ethanol (30 sec each). Slides were then bathed in 4 successive xylene baths (1 min each). Slides were then cover slipped. The H&E-stained tissue sections of brain mounted on glass slides were scanned with an Aperio Scanscope System (Leica Biosystems, Vista, Ca).

The brain was sectioned based on the recommendations for histopathological evaluations of the rodent nervous system by the National Toxicological Program (NTP) 37 and the Society of Toxicologic Pathology 33 (see Figure 1 for details). However, the PND 21 rat brain is substantially smaller than that of an adult, and more friable owing to incomplete myelination, high water content, underdeveloped extracellular matrix, and larger extracellular spaces. Practically, this caused difficulty in collecting the recommended 7 sections in these small animals. Instead, only 5 levels were collected as per current recommendations for performing neurohistopathological studies on animals with small brains from the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences. 34 Since this is a comparative study, the 5 equivalent sections were collected and analyzed throughout. Equivalents of National Toxicological Program levels 5 and 7 were not included in this study. While the FDA draft guidance on Major Depressive Disorder 8 does recommend using 7 sections based on the procedure of Bolon et al, 33 it states that alternative sections may be used based on the predictive sensitivity of the drug. Our approach includes areas of known sensitivity to NMDA receptor antagonist-mediated neuronal necrosis (retrosplenial cortex). Sections included the olfactory bulb, fronto-parietal cortex, mid-parietal cortex and thalamus, mid-brain, and cerebellum. Specifically, in the PND 21-35 rats, section 1 was mid-point of the olfactory bulbs, section 2 was approximately 2 mm rostral to the optic chiasm, section 3 approximately 2 mm rostral to the center of the infundibulum/pituitary stalk, section 4 about 1 mm rostral to the rear of the infundibulum/pituitary stalk, and section 5 (equivalent to NTP level 6) mid cerebellum through the widest portion of the cerebellum (Figure 1). In PND 90 animals, section 1 was mid-point of the olfactory bulbs, section 2 was approximately 1 mm rostral to the optic chiasm, section 3 was approximately 1 mm rostral to the center of the infundibulum/pituitary stalk, section 4 was directly behind and caudal to the infundibulum/pituitary stalk, and section 5 (equivalent to NTP level 6) was mid cerebellum through the widest portion of the cerebellum (Figure 1).

PND 21 (treated) and adult rat coronal sections at 5 different levels. Animals were treated on PND 21 and euthanized on PND 24; the image shown is representative of a PND 24 animal. Areas assessed were based on those proposed in reference 37 but modified due to the reduced size and friable nature of the PND 21–35 rat brain. Slides and corresponding high-resolution photomicrographs were read by a board-certified veterinary pathologist (American College of Veterinary Pathologists) who bilaterally scored the observed pathology (section level; structures involved; grade of neuronal necrosis) throughout each section. The pathologist was blinded to the treatment of each animal. Neuronal necrosis was scored on a 0-4 scale, where 0 represented an absence of lesions, 1 equaled a minimal presence of lesions impacting 1-25% of neurons, 2 equaled mild lesions impacting 26-50% of neurons, 3 equaled moderate lesions impacting 51-75% of neurons, and 4 equaled marked lesions impacting 76-100% of neurons.

In this study, we chose to use H&E as our sole marker of neuronal necrosis. The timeline of NMDA receptor antagonist toxicity has been well characterized using H&E in conjunction with MK-801. 35 Neuronal necrosis peaks approximately 72 hours after MK-801 exposure as assessed by H&E. 35 Therefore, using a protocol optimized for H&E and collecting tissue 72 hours post-exposure would maximize our ability to detect ketamine-induced neuronal necrosis, should it occur.

The inclusion of additional histopathological markers of neuronal necrosis would have required protocol modifications that could compromise conditions optimized for H&E detection. For example, the sensitivity of Fluoro-Jade C to injury peaks early and may be difficult to detect at 72 hours. Additionally, Fluoro-Jade C, TUNEL, and caspase-based stains typically require thicker sections than the 4- to 8-µm sections used for paraffin-embedded H&E analysis. Although these markers can be applied to thinner sections, interpretation and quantitative analysis become more challenging. The decision to focus exclusively on H&E was pragmatic and intended to optimize detection under conditions most likely to reveal ketamine-induced neuronal necrosis, if present.

Sample Preparation of Rat Serum for Drug Exposure Analysis

Rat serum (5 µL) was added to 250 µL of acetonitrile containing the internal standards ketamine-d4 and norketamine-d4. The samples were briefly vortexed and centrifuged at 21, 130×g for 3 min. The supernatant was placed in a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) total recovery vial (Waters Corporation, MA, USA) for liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC/MS/MS) analysis.

Analysis of Rat Serum Samples Using LC/MS/MS

Rat serum samples were analyzed using a Waters ACQUITY I-class system coupled to a Waters Xevo TQ-S triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Waters Corporation) equipped with an electrospray interface operating in positive ion mode. Separation was achieved using a Waters BEH C18 column (2.1 × 50 mm), using a 300 µL/min gradient consisting of 10 mM ammonium formate (A) and acetonitrile (B) as follows: 5-95% B in 3.5 min, held at 95% B for 0.5 min, and returned to 5% B in 0.1 min. The column was held at 40°C; the autosampler was maintained at 18°C; and injection volumes were between 0.2 and 2 µL. The total run time was 6.5 min.

Data were acquired in multiple reaction monitoring modes. The MRM transitions and MS source parameters were optimized for all compounds and were as follows: 237.93 > 125.06 for ketamine, 242.19 > 128.97 for ketamine-d4, 224.16 > 125.06 for norketamine, and 228.16 > 129.02 for norketamine-d4.

Data Analysis

To determine key pharmacokinetic values, the serum concentrations of ketamine and norketamine as a function of time following dosing were determined by LC/MS/MS and analyzed in Phoenix WinNonlin (Certara, Radnor, PA) using a non-compartmental model. For the adult animals (n = 7), the individual serum time-concentration curves of ketamine and norketamine were loaded into the model. For the PND 21, 30, and 35 animals, terminal blood concentrations obtained from 7 individuals per time point were used to compile a composite time-concentration curve.

All statistical analyses were performed using Statistica 64 version 13 (Dell Inc., OK, USA). Ambulation data was analyzed using a 3-way ANOVA and a Dunnett’s test. The effect of sex on pharmacokinetic endpoints in adult animals was analyzed with t-tests for independent samples.

Results

Time to Regain Normal Ambulation

After experimental treatment, all animals were continually monitored. The time from injection until animals were observed to be unconscious (lack of movement) was recorded. When animals were observed to be alert, they were placed in an upright position. If, upon release, an animal was observed to walk with a normal body posture they were considered to have regained normal ambulation. The time from observed unconsciousness to regaining normal ambulation was used for analysis. See Supplemental Table 2 for complete statistical results.

Main effects of age (P < 0.001), sex (P < 0.001), and ketamine treatment (P < 0.001) were observed. The interactions between sex and ketamine treatment (P = 0.65) and age and ketamine treatment (P = 0.65) were not statistically significant. The three-way interaction between age, sex, and ketamine treatment approached statistical significance (P = 0.060; Figure 2). Dunnett’s t-test indicated that the PND 90 animals had a longer duration to normal ambulation than all other groups after treatment with ketamine (P < 0.004 in all instances). These data show that younger animals are faster to regain normal ambulation. See Supplemental Table 2 for complete statistical results.

Pharmacokinetics of Ketamine and Norketamine

Pharmacokinetic studies were performed in a separate cohort of animals at PND 21, 30, 35, and 90. Serial samples were taken from the adult animals, whereas only a single sample was taken from each juvenile animal. Because of this, statistical comparisons cannot be performed on most endpoints in the PND 21, 30, and 35 animals. In most instances, only descriptive statistics are used for analysis of pharmacokinetic data.

Visual examination of data indicated that Cmax generally decreased with age, with the youngest animals (PND 21) having the highest group values for ketamine. These values were almost identical for males and females. No effect of sex was observed on ketamine Cmax, half-life, and area under the curve (AUC) (P = 0.08) in PND 90 rats. However, female rats (PND 90) had a higher peak plasma concentration (P < 0.001), half-life (P = 0.024), and AUC (P = 0.001) for norketamine (Figure 3A–C). Estimates could not be generated for several animals due to missed samples or outlying data points. The timepoints selected were not optimal for estimation of norketamine in females. Levels of norketamine in female animals were likely underestimated.

The effects of sex and age on key pharmacokinetic parameters of ketamine and its primary metabolite norketamine. Younger animals appeared to have a higher Cmax of ketamine and norketamine (A), although disparities in experimental approaches did not allow for a statistical comparison. Sex appeared to have little effect on ketamine levels. However, adult female rats had statistically significant higher levels of norketamine. Histopathology

After treatment with MK-801, shrunken neurons were observed in male and female rats in the PND 30, 35, and PND 90 groups (Table 1, Figure 4). These shrunken neurons had pyknotic or karyorrhectic nuclei, and hypereosinophilic cytoplasm indicative of neuronal necrosis. These neurons were often angular in shape. Infrequently, necrotic neurons had one to a few adjacent glial cells, with no apparent morphological change. This pattern of localization is consistent with the earliest stages of neuronophagia. No histological lesions were observed in the PND 21 group. Lesions were restricted to the retrosplenial cortex in the PND 30 male (1/8 displaying a lesion) and female rats (1/4 displaying a lesion). Female rats (n = 5) treated at PND 35 all showed shrunken neurons within the retrosplenial and piriform cortices (Level 2), while only some animals (2/5) displayed this pathology in the entorhinal cortex (Level 3, Figure 4E). However, these shrunken neurons were restricted to the retrosplenial cortex in male rats (Figure 4F) and were observed in all animals. Similarly, in PND 90 female rats shrunken neurons were observed in the retrosplenial (2/2, Levels 3 and 4), piriform (1/2, Level 2), and entorhinal (2/2, Level 4) cortices, but only in the retrosplenial cortex (8/8, Level 3 and 4) of male rats after treatment with MK-801. These affected neurons were most common in section 4 but were also observed in section 3. Affected neurons were typically found in layers II, III, and IV of the retrosplenial cortex, but in some instances included layer V. In most instances pathology was graded as “minimal,” impacting fewer than 25% of neurons. These data indicate that potent NMDA receptor antagonists, like MK-801, can induce neuronal death in young animals (PND 30 and 35) and support the sensitivity of this assay and animal model to characterize ketamine-induced neurotoxicity as assessed by histological analysis with H&E.

Discussion

All animals treated with ketamine, or the positive control, MK-801, became unconscious after administration. Additionally, ketamine (100 mg/kg, s.c.) induced neuronal necrosis in adult female rats. No neurohistopathological changes associated with ketamine treatment were observed in any other treatment group. The positive control, MK-801, caused necrotic neurons in all experimental conditions except in the youngest animals (PND21). The MK-801 data replicate previous findings and demonstrate that our procedure was appropriate for assessing NMDA receptor antagonist-induced neurotoxicity as measured by histopathology with H&E in all but the youngest animals used in this study.16,38 After treatment with MK-801, shrunken neurons with hypereosinophilic cytoplasm and pyknotic or karyorrhectic nuclei were observed in the retrosplenial, piriform, and entorhinal cortices of rats in the older age groups. In contrast, signs of neuronal death were limited to the retrosplenial cortex in adult female rats treated with ketamine. We believe that this is the first reported instance of neuronal necrosis observed in adult animals after a single administration of ketamine. Ketamine-induced neuronal necrosis was not observed in male animals treated with ketamine. An absence of neuronal necrosis in the juvenile and adult male rats makes it impossible to determine the relative sensitivity to ketamine-induced toxicity between these groups. Juvenile female rats were less sensitive to ketamine-induced neuronal death than adult female rats on an mg/kg basis. Adult rats (both sexes) also had a longer period to regain normal ambulation, suggesting they are more sensitive to the somnolent-inducing effects of ketamine.

For this work, we chose to treat animals via the s.c. route. There were several reasons for this. High serum levels of ketamine will cause death via respiratory depression. Administering ketamine via an i.v. bolus at a dose high enough to achieve an AUC sufficient to induce neuronal necrosis may be impossible without causing death via respiratory depression in the rat. Administration via the s.c. route results in a lower Cmax. Cmax was reached in around 30 mins. Additionally, when used off-label, ketamine is often administered as an extended i.v. infusion. Administering ketamine via the s.c. route allowed us to achieve a high AUC and to mimic many of the pharmacokinetic characteristics of these extended i.v. infusions.

While data are limited, reports suggest that a ketamine plasma concentration of around 3000 ng/mL is adequate to maintain a surgical plane of anesthesia in adult humans, 39 with recovery from anesthesia occurring at around 1000 ng/mL. 40 These values may be lower in children.41,42 Here, we found that treatment with 100 mg/kg ketamine resulted in a Cmax ranging from 10 000 ng/mL in adult PND 90 rats to 30 000 ng/mL in PND 21 rats (Figure 3A). The lowest doses used in this study likely achieved plasma levels of ketamine above those necessary to maintain a clinical plane of anesthesia in humans and appeared to induce a lasting plane of anesthesia in experimental animals (animals were not tested for a pain response, as such we cannot definitively comment on the presence of a surgical plane of anesthesia). Based on differences in plasma levels of ketamine between the youngest (PND 21) and adult animals (PND 90), a longer period of unconsciousness would be expected in the younger animals. Despite having a higher ketamine Cmax and AUC, their recovery times are similar to (males) or less than (females) adult animals (Figure 2).

Based on plasma exposures to ketamine we would anticipate a substantially longer time to regain normal ambulation in the PND 21 cohort, but this was not observed. This disparity suggests that the central nervous system of juvenile animals responds differently to ketamine, and potentially other NMDA receptor antagonists, at the receptor level. PND 21 female rats had a higher Cmax and AUC of ketamine than adult females but did not show signs of neuronal necrosis. Adult male and female rats had similar plasma levels of ketamine, but neuronal necrosis was not observed in male animals. A factor other than internal ketamine exposure is driving the neuropathology in this model. While NMDA receptor ontogeny is an important factor in antagonist-induced neurotoxicity, 43 by PND 21, NMDA receptors have assumed a pattern of expression similar to that observed in adult rats.44-46 We hypothesize that a contributing factor to the increased sensitivity to ketamine-induced neuronal death may be related to the substantially higher AUC of norketamine (Figures 3B and 6), the primary metabolite of ketamine, observed in adult female rats when compared to male rats. However other factors, like differences in anesthesia duration or hormone-based effects,47,48 might explain neuronal death only being observed in adult female rats.

Adult female rats have a higher norketamine Cmax and AUC than male rats when measured as a metabolite of ketamine. Published reports on sex differences in ketamine exposure and metabolism are inconsistent. Female Sprague-Dawley rats treated with ketamine (2.5 mg/kg, i.p.) had higher internal concentrations of ketamine and norketamine (2-fold) when compared to males.49,50 In contrast, female mice (CD-1) had lower internal concentrations of ketamine and norketamine after ketamine treatment (10 mg/kg, i.p.) when compared to males. 51 A single study in men and women (18-65 years old) indicated that men had modestly higher, but statistically significant, serum levels of ketamine and norketamine 51 after administration (0.5 mg/kg i.v. infusion over 40 min). The Cmax reported in these studies never reached 1000 ng/mL, a fraction of the levels observed here. We observed a fourfold increase in norketamine levels (AUC) between male and female rats (Figure 6). The exaggerated difference in norketamine levels between adult males and females in our study is likely caused by the increased half-life of norketamine observed in females in this study (Figure 3C). We hypothesize that high dose of ketamine used in this study caused the saturation of one or more of the cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYP) crucial for norketamine metabolism (CYP3A4, CYP3A5, CYP2B6) 52 in a sex-dependent manner. This slower rate of norketamine metabolism resulted in a longer half-life, likely due to enzyme saturation. Additional analysis of the relative abundance of the different norketamine metabolites would help to determine whether CYP saturation did occur. Importantly, the sex-dependent effects on norketamine levels occurred in the context of ketamine metabolism. Sex-dependent effects on metabolism of norketamine might not occur when administered on its own.

Norketamine shares many of the pharmacological properties of the parent compound, including acting as an NMDA receptor antagonist,7,53 albeit not as potent as ketamine. 7 In this study, the AUC of norketamine was 4 times higher than that of ketamine in female rats, compared to only 1.4 times higher in male rats. The additive effects of ketamine and norketamine on peak NMDA receptor occupancy, and the total duration of NMDA receptor antagonism, may be crucial in understanding ketamine-related neurotoxicity (Figures 3 and 5). This would suggest that AUC and Cmax are both key factors in ketamine/NMDA receptor antagonist induced neuronal death. Elevated levels of norketamine and continued NMDA receptor occupancy may continue to contribute to toxic processes even though animals had regained “normal” ambulation. Ketamine and norketamine do have differences in binding profiles to other neurotransmitter receptors, 53 and metabolites of norketamine may also bind to nicotinic receptors. 54 As such, non-NMDA receptor mechanisms cannot be ruled out as a contributing factor to the observed toxicity.

Due to the lack of observed neuronal death in male rats, we could not comment on the comparative toxicity of ketamine in adult and juvenile male rats. Neuropathological changes were not observed at doses that caused prolonged unconsciousness, and serum levels above those needed to create a surgical plane of anesthesia in the rat (Weisbroth and Fudens, 1972). However, toxicity was observed with the positive control MK-801, replicating a pattern of neuronal necrosis similar to that previously reported.11,35,55 Ostensibly, a reduced sensitivity to ketamine-related neuronal necrosis was observed in juvenile female rats when compared to adult female rats. However, this conclusion does not account for the increased levels of norketamine observed in adult female rats. We believe this sex- and age-dependent difference in neuronal necrosis is driven at least in part by increased levels of norketamine in adult female rats. Additional work with an NMDA receptor antagonist that does not show sex- and age-dependent effects on ADME properties (absorption, distribution, metabolism, elimination) is necessary to confirm the comparative sensitivity to neuronal necrosis. This would allow the determination of sex- and age-dependent sensitivity to NMDA receptor-mediated antagonism with similar levels of receptor occupancy.

We have no information on the effects of sex on plasma levels of MK-801 in juvenile rats. However, adult female rats were more sensitive to MK-801-mediated mortality in this study. It was necessary for us to use a lower dose of MK-801 in female rats (2 vs 3 mg/kg, i.p.) during the study. Interestingly, the distribution of the affected neurons showed sex specificity. Female rats treated with MK-801 also had a wider distribution of neuronal death involving the retrosplenial, piriform, and entorhinal cortices (PND 35 and 90), whereas MK-801-related neuronal death was localized to the retrosplenial cortex in males (Table 1). These data are consistent with past findings showing higher plasma levels of MK-801 36 and an increased sensitivity to MK-801-induced neuronal necrosis in female rats when compared to males. 11

We believe the observed neuronal necrosis results from ketamine’s NMDA receptor antagonism, an antagonism that is prolonged by the slow clearance of norketamine in female animals. The pharmacokinetic data from Morris et al 36 support this hypothesis: MK-801 has a longer half-life than ketamine, with females showing longer half-lives than males. The extended half-life of MK-801 may facilitate reaching a threshold for neurotoxic NMDA receptor antagonism while avoiding acute lethality—a threshold that may be difficult to reach with ketamine in adult animals (particularly males) without causing death. This pharmacokinetic difference would explain both the wider distribution of pathology and higher lethality observed in female rats treated with MK-801.

A shortcoming of these data is that several animals died or required euthanasia during the study after treatment with both drugs (Supplemental Table 1). Concerns about animal welfare and mortality caused the MK-801 dose to be lowered mid-study in females. Among the ketamine group, some animals did not reach the terminal time point because they expired shortly after the administration of test compound (unconsciousness followed by respiratory depression) or were euthanized when they had failed to recover consciousness within 6 hours of drug administration. Animals that died or required euthanasia might have disproportionately displayed signs of neurotoxicity. Additionally, we assessed neurodegeneration 72 hours after ketamine administration. This time point was selected based on the well-documented appearance of MK-801-related neuronal necrosis in adult rats at approximately 72 hours post-exposure. Related to the choice of time point, the decision to use H&E as the sole marker of neuronal necrosis was also intentional and pragmatic, as tissue collection and processing were optimized for H&E detection at the 72-hour time point. The inclusion of additional markers, such as Fluoro-Jade C, TUNEL, or caspase-based stains, would have required protocol modifications that could have compromised conditions optimized for H&E. For example, the sensitivity of Fluoro-Jade C peaks early after injury and may be difficult to detect at 72 hours, 56 and these markers typically require thicker sections than the 4-8 µm sections used for paraffin-embedded H&E analysis. While these markers can be applied to thinner sections, interpretation and quantitative analysis become more challenging. If neuronal necrosis were to occur through a different and more rapid mechanism in PND 21-35 rats, it is possible that the resulting pathology may have resolved by the 72-hour time point. However, signs of neuronophagia were not evident in the PND 21-35 cohorts, suggesting this did not occur. Future studies employing complementary histopathological markers at earlier time points, such as 24 hours post-exposure, would further strengthen our conclusion that ketamine caused neuronal necrosis in a sex- and age-dependent manner. Additionally, our assessment was limited to 5 levels per animal. It is possible that inclusion of additional levels and brain regions may have resulted in detection of neuronal necrosis in other cohorts. 57

Age and sex differences should be taken into consideration when appraising the comparative safety of ketamine in different clinical populations. While not conclusive, these data hint that the duration of NMDA receptor antagonism may be just as important as maximal antagonism in driving acute neurotoxicity. For example, a study found that repeated exposure to a subtherapeutic dose of ketamine induced neuronal necrosis in cynomolgus monkeys. 58 This may have implications for safe clinical use of NMDA receptor antagonists. Additional work should be performed to determine if sex-dependent effects on ketamine’s metabolic profile occur at clinically relevant doses in humans. Also, it would be valuable to determine the neurotoxic potential of norketamine in rats to confirm its importance in contributing to ketamine-induced neurotoxicity.

This work, when combined with other findings,36,49,59 counters a common belief that female rats are more sensitive to the effects of NMDA receptor antagonism at the neuronal level. Instead, it appears that 3 of the most commonly used compounds to study NMDA receptor antagonism (ketamine, MK-801, and phencyclidine) are metabolized in a sex-dependent fashion resulting in larger/longer periods of NMDA receptor antagonism in females. Differences in pharmacokinetics, and not necessarily neuro-sensitivity, appear to be the most likely explanation for the outsized impact of ketamine and MK-801 in adult female rats. Moreover, this study considered only the acute effects of ketamine on histopathological endpoints. Additional work with repeated dosing that considers the long-term impact of ketamine administration on neuropathology and behavior is necessary to fully compare the effects of ketamine at a young age and in adulthood.
