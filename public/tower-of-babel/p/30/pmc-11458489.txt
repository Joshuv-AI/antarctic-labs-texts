Reply to: SARS-CoV-2 Transmission Rate Depends on Infectivity of the Virus Strain, Closeness of Contact, and the Immune Competence of the Infected Person
Authors: Jiwoo Sim, Euncheol Son, Young June Choe
Published: 2024

Dear Editor:

We read with interest the correspondence by Finsterer regarding our recent study on the risk of SARS-CoV-2 transmission in Seoul, Korea [1,2]. We appreciate the author's insightful comments and would like to address the points raised.

Regarding the possibility of alternative infection routes for secondary cases, we acknowledge this limitation. While our study design focused on close contacts of confirmed cases, we cannot definitively exclude other sources of infection, as demonstrated in other outbreak investigations [3,4]. From original strain dominant periods to Delta dominant periods when 1,227 people among 1,286 people investigated were included, testing all relevant people was conducted, and classification and isolation of contacts were implemented within 24 hours following the diagnosis of the index case. The identification of contacts was conducted according to the World Health Organization (WHO)'s Interim Guidance on Contact Tracing in the Context of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) (published on February 1, 2021) and the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA)'s COVID-19 response guidelines for local governments. The classification of contacts was performed by epidemiological investigators. Moreover, as daily incidence rate was below 4 per 10,000 during the same period, we speculated the possibility of secondary infection from an unknown source was negligible.

The higher transmission rate in older individuals and long-term care facilities is likely multifactorial [5]. While we did not specifically investigate comorbidities or medication use, these factors could contribute to increased susceptibility. Additionally, age-related decline in immune function may play a role. Future studies could explore these factors in more detail.

We acknowledge the limitations of quantifying contact intensity as most contact level data was obtained based on patient and contact tracing statements. The contact categories applied in our study were based on the WHO guidelines and the KDCA's COVID-19 response guidelines for local governments. While our study used broad categories, we agree that a more nuanced assessment would be valuable. Future research could employ more detailed contact tracing methods to better understand the impact of contact intensity on transmission risk.

For the demographic characteristic 'nationality', the number of domestic cases should be corrected to 1,260 (97.98%) and overseas cases to 26 (2.02%). The majority of the study participants were local residents with domestic nationality.

We acknowledge that COVID-19 can cause various systemic symptoms. As ‘non-respiratory symptoms’ may not be a familiar term, it could lead to confusion. We categorized ‘non-respiratory symptoms’ to distinguish symptoms from respiratory symptoms like cough, sore throat, and so on.

Finally, we acknowledge the potential role of animal transmission, which was not addressed in our study [6], However, no cases of animal-to-human transmission have been reported in Korea. Therefore, we speculated the possibility of such transmission was extremely low.

We thank Finsterer for their valuable feedback and hope that our response clarifies the points raised. We believe that our study contributes to the growing body of knowledge on SARS-CoV-2 transmission and highlights the need for ongoing research and targeted interventions to mitigate the risk of infection.
