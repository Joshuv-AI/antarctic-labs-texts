Influence of a structured, 1-year-long dietary intervention regarding body composition and cardiovascular risk (ARCTIC) in coeliac disease: a protocol of a multicentre randomised controlled trial
Authors: Zsófia Vereczkei, Zsolt Szakács, Míra Zsófia Peresztegi, Klára Lemes, Krisztina Hagymási, Sarolta Dakó, Eszter Dakó, Szilvia Lada, Nándor Faluhelyi, Gábor Szekeres, Gyula Pásztor, Nelli Farkas, Gabriella Pár, Emese Mezősi, Judit Bajor

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCoeliac disease (CD) affects 1% of the population worldwide. The only available evidence-based treatment is a strict gluten-free diet (GFD), which can readily lead to weight gain and unfavourable metabolic changes (eg, dyslipidaemia, fatty liver disease and insulin resistance) if followed without adequate dietary control. That can lead to increased cardiovascular risk (CV). We planned a randomised controlled trial to test the effect of a group-based, structured, 1-year, advanced dietary education, per the proposal of a Mediterranean diet vs standard of care, regarding the most relevant CV risk factors (eg, metabolic parameters and body composition) in CD patients.Methods and analysisRandomisation will occur after the baseline dietary education and interview in a 1:1 allocation ratio. Outcomes include anthropometric parameters (body composition analysis including weight, Body Mass Index, fat mass, per cent body fat, skeletal muscle mass, visceral fat area and total body water) and CV risk-related metabolic parameters (eg, lipid profile, homocysteine, fasting glucose, haemoglobin A1c, Homeostatic Model Assessment Index, metabolic hormones, waist circumference, blood pressure, liver function tests, liver steatosis rate and diet composition). In this study, we aim to draw attention to a new aspect regarding managing CD: dietary education can lead to a better quality of the GFD, thereby reducing the risk of potential metabolic and CV complications.Ethics and disseminationThe study was approved by the Scientific and Research Ethics Committee of the Hungarian Medical Research Council (27521-5/2022/EÜIG). Findings will be disseminated at research conferences and in peer-reviewed journals.Trial registration number NCT05530070

Introduction

Coeliac disease (CD) is a chronic autoimmune disorder affecting approximately 1% of the population, for which the only available treatment is a lifelong strict gluten-free diet (GFD). Once adjusted to a well-managed diet, symptoms often disappear, the intestinal mucosa regenerates, CD-specific antibodies normalise and deficiency states resolve. Furthermore, the patients’ body weight and Body Mass Index (BMI) increase with GFD.1 2 There is a complex reason for this: as absorption improves, the nutrients are better utilised, but the hyperphagia, which is often characteristic, does not always disappear.3 Unfortunately, the quality of the GFD is unbalanced and generally does not meet the requirements of a healthy diet: the GFD is high in simple carbohydrates, fat, saturated fatty acids, meat and processed meat products and low in complex carbohydrates and fibres.47 According to a meta-analysis, GFD appears to increase body weight and per cent body fat (FM%) only in patients with CD, while this effect was not observed in individuals without CD following a GFD.8

Weight gain is desirable among the underweight; however, there is an increasing number of CD patients with normal or high body weight on diagnosis. Another problem is that weight gain is mainly due to an increase in body fat mass (FM) rather than fat-free mass (FFM), leading to an unfavourable change in body composition.9 The trend seen in the increasing proportion of obese individuals worldwide is also appearing among CD patients.2 The burden of metabolic and cardiovascular (CV) complications of obesity is expected to increase in the long term. Recent investigations suggest that individuals with CD, compared with the general population, may be at a higher risk of CV diseases. Moreover, CV risk has been reported to be higher in CD, although generally, these patients expressed fewer classical CV risk factors (eg, smoking, obesity and hypercholesterinaemia).10 11 In 2015, two systematic reviews and meta-analyses examined the association between CD and CV disease. Both studies suggested that CD was associated with a slightly increased risk of CV disease; however, data were heterogeneous, and the evidence strength had limitations.12 13

Potter et al studied the impact of the GFD on CV risk factors, reporting a significant increase in total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, fasting glucose level and BMI; whereas, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, triglycerides and blood pressure were not significantly changed.14 A meta-analysis examined changes in BMI and lipid parameters during GFD in CD patients. The results showed a statistically significant increase in BMI and HDL cholesterol during GFD; however total cholesterol and triglyceride were not changed significantly.15

Ciccone et al evaluated the metabolic changes that occur along a GFD in CD patients. The prevalence of metabolic syndrome and steatosis hepatis had significantly increased in patients with CD keeping a GFD.16 A similar conclusion was drawn by Tortora et al where newly diagnosed CD patients were studied at the diagnosis and 1 year after starting GFD: the prevalence of metabolic syndrome increased from an initial 2% to 29.5% after 12 months of GFD.17 A recent meta-analysis of 11 studies found a high prevalence of fatty liver disease and metabolic syndrome in patients with CD (18.2% and 4.3%), which was further worsened by GFD (to 28.2% and 21.3%).18

There is epidemiologic evidence that the Mediterranean diet is most suitable for reducing the risk of CV disease, both in primary and secondary prevention.19 20 A questionnaire survey showed that adherence to the Mediterranean diet among individuals with a longstanding GFD is far worse than that of the general population, as they consume more processed meat products, potatoes and less fruit.19 Insufficient dietary care leads to poor compliance and long-term adverse effects of an unbalanced diet. Most dietary counselling focuses only on strict adherence to the GFD while hardly emphasising the importance of a healthy, balanced diet and the disadvantages of gluten-free products. The ideal GFD is based on the pyramid of the GFD-Mediterranean diet: plenty of vegetables, fruits and pseudograin products. Additionally, consuming processed meat, gluten-free products and desserts should be reduced.20 21 The Mediterranean diet generally seems suitable for preventing obesity during a GFD.

A study in 2020 confirmed that dietary adherence and the quality of the GFD could significantly be enhanced through dietary counselling. Following a dietary intervention, adherence to the Mediterranean diet considerably increased, while the prevalence of obesity and the FM reduced and the FFM increased.22

The forms of education go together with different levels of effectiveness. Online dietary intervention has also been proven to be effective: it has led to a significant improvement in dietary adherence and diet-related knowledge.23 Online programmes have many advantages in our digital world—especially in the shadow of the COVID-19 pandemic. They are convenient for users, offer flexibility and reduce costs.24 In addition to providing detailed information, the advantages of a group-based dietary and lifestyle education include the ability for group members to exchange information, ask questions and generally demonstrate an increase in self-motivation.25 26 Group education focusing on healthy eating improves the quality of patients’ diet and nutritional status.22 Dietary education can even be integrated using digital platforms.27 No randomised controlled trials (RCT) are available to compare a repeated dietary intervention with basic dietary counselling and comprehensively investigate CV risk factors among CD patients.

Methods and analysis

Study design and setting

The study will be a multicentre RCT, including study sites at Pécs, Budapest and Szeged (involving three Hungarian university clinics). Sites will be provided with clinical trial infrastructure and skilled staff. All participants will be recruited consecutively in the outpatient clinics of the participating centres. The locations of recruitment will be the Division of Gastroenterology, First Department of Medicine, Medical School, University of Pécs (Pécs, Hungary); the Department of Surgery, Transplantation and Gastroenterology, Faculty of Medicine, Semmelweis University (Budapest, Hungary); and the First Department of Internal Medicine, Albert Szent-Györgyi Medical School, University of Szeged (Szeged, Hungary). The expected recruitment period is from September 2022 to December 2026.

Participants will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to one of the two study arms (‘standard of care’ and ‘dietary intervention’). This RCT recruits patients from other substudies of the ARCTIC study. The protocol of the observational studies has already been published.28 The newly diagnosed CD patients in the case-control study (Group A) following 1 year of strict GFD and CD patients who have been on a GFD for at least 1 year from the other case-control study (Group B) can be recruited to this RCT. Screening can be repeated if the patient can potentially become eligible for the study in both cases.

The study protocol was planned in conformity with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) 2013 Statement.29 For the WHO checklist, see table 1.

Intervention

Participants will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to dietary interventions, as follows:

A ‘standard of care’ arm in which patients will receive only baseline individual dietary education as a part of the standard of care.

A ‘standard of care’ arm in which patients will receive only baseline individual dietary education as a part of the standard of care.

A ‘dietary intervention’ arm in which groups containing 6–10 CD patients will be formed, and group-based consultations will be delivered. 1-hour/occasion consultations will occur using an online platform (via the Zoom application). Expert study dietitians will conduct these consultations and will ensure interactive learning with group discussion. These meetings allow the group members to ask questions and share experiences. Both groups will be advised to stay active with regular physical activity and to avoid a sedentary lifestyle. No study-related counselling will be given regarding physical activity.

Strategies have been developed to improve adherence to the interventions. Participation is supported through an optimal programme schedule and the possibility of interacting with experts and other participants. The use of online mediums offers convenience to participants. We provide participants with the flexibility to personalise the sequence of the modules. Productivity is addressed through realistic goal setting and precise action planning. Motivation is supported by sparking interest in the pursuit of a healthy lifestyle.

Participants will be encouraged to engage in all modules actively; however, we consider the programme completed if the participant has taken part in 5 out of 6 modules (the content of the missing module will be sent by e-mail to the patient). During the study, participants will receive regular e-mail reminders and phone calls regarding the programme’s schedule.

Participants are asked not to change their medication regimen during the study period unless it is indispensable.

Assessments

The patient’s physician will use a structured questionnaire to record medical data (medical history including comorbidities, physical status and current medication). The assessment and the outcomes are similar to our observational studies.28

CD-related symptoms will be assessed by the Coeliac Symptom Index (CSI) and a CD-specific. A quality of life questionnaire will also be used (Coeliac Disease Quality of Life (CD-QoL)) (by a team member with a medical degree).33 TTG IgA and IgG levels will be measured using a commercial test (Orgentec Diagnostika GmbH, Mainz, Germany). IgA and IgG antibodies and total IgA will be evaluated simultaneously.

A CV risk assessment will be performed using the European Society of Cardiology Systematic Coronary Evaluation 2 (ESC SCORE2) chart (by a team member with a medical degree) and by measuring blood pressure and waist circumference (by a healthcare practitioner) and laboratory parameters.34

Regarding anthropometric parameters, body height will be measured using a stadiometer. Body weight and body composition will be estimated with bioelectrical impedance analysis using the InBody 770 device (by a healthcare practitioner).

Sarcopenia will be assessed using body composition parameters and handgrip strength via handgrip dynamometer (by a healthcare practitioner).

Transabdominal ultrasonography will be used to assess the extent of fatty liver disease (based on non-alcoholic fatty liver disease-liver fat score, with a score range of 0–3) (a radiologist will determine scores).

Dietary adherence of CD patients will be determined by (1) a dietary interview provided by an expert dietitian with the CDAT, (2) coeliac-specific antibodies (tTG and EMA) and (3) urine GIP measurement.35 The composition of a GFD will be evaluated using the MDS as an indicator of adherence to the Mediterranean diet (by a dietitian).36

Routine laboratory measurements will be conducted by the laboratories of the study sites using venous blood. Blood samples for research will be collected in plastic tubes of 10 mL blood from each individual and centrifuged at 1500 G for 10 min after clotting. Midstream urine (at least 100 mL) will be collected in sterile urine sample containers. Serum and urine samples will undergo deep freezing at −80°C immediately following sampling and stored at this temperature until transfer. These samples will be transferred on dry ice to the Biobank at the Institute for Translational Medicine, Medical School, University of Pécs where samples will be stored at −80°C until processing. Following preparation, urine GIP detection will be performed with Biomedal (Spain) products.

Sample size estimation

The planned sample size has been calculated to ensure adequate statistical power. The chosen endpoint for sample size calculation was FM%. The estimated sample size was calculated for α=0.05, β=0.90, assuming normal distribution of data (10% drop-out rate, no interim analysis, assuming 5% point difference change between groups, calculated with the highest standard deviation (SD) identified from the papers listed above): at least 95 coeliac patients/group (based on papers of Capristo et al, Nunes-Silva et al, Newnham et al).37 37 38 38 39 39

Randomization and allocation concealment

Central randomisation will be done following the enrolment in a 1:1 allocation ratio with a randomly permuted block size using a computer-generated random sequence. Inclusion and exclusion criteria will be rechecked before computer-aided randomisation to ensure that only eligible patients are included in the trial. A team member who did not screen and consent the study subject will perform the randomisation procedure.

Patient and public involvement

Prior to initiating recruitment, three randomly selected CD patients reviewed the questionnaires. The education topics were discussed with the local organisation among coeliac patients before the trial registration. The method and timing of the education were discussed with the national organisation following the protocol registration, accomplishing the required corrections.

Data management

A subject identification (ID) number will be provided consecutively to every patient following inclusion. ID numbers with sensitive data of the patients (including the name, insurance number and date of enrolment) will be stored in a locked file separately from other data. De-identified data will be added to the source documentation and stored in locked cabinets. Source documentation will be entered in an electronic case report file (e-CRF). In the case of withdrawal, personal data will be deleted. The principal investigators will ensure that all collected data in an e-CRF are precise, complete and reliable (range checks for data values). E-CRFs will be stored on a secured research centre computer server. Access to data will be restricted through a password system to personnel involved in data management. The principal investigator will approve the final data to ensure data quality. To provide precise data collection, administrative and medical staff members will be invited to participate in training sessions to familiarise themselves with the study requirements, standardised data recording and biological specimen collection. The de-identified dataset will be delivered and shared on request.

Statistical analysis

An expert biostatistician will analyse all data, which will be curated and analysed using R statistical language. A descriptive analysis will be performed. In consideration of continuous variables, mean and SD or median and interquartile range (IQR) will be computed, dependent on the distribution of the data. Concerning categorical variables, proportions will be calculated. Through the analysis, the difference in the values from the baseline will be used, and t-tests or corresponding non-parametric tests will be applied. x² or Fisher’s tests, Spearman’s rank correlation and random effects regression models will be used to analyse connection. The threshold for statistical significance will be defined as p<0.05 and corrected for false discovery rate (FDR method). This study will not include an interim analysis. Subgroup analysis will be performed among diabetic patients. Intention-to-treat (all patients randomised) and per-protocol analyses (all patients completing at least 5 modules) are planned to handle protocol non-adherence. Statistical methods to handle missing data are not planned.

Discussion

The beneficial effect of improved adherence to the Mediterranean diet in CV prevention has already been demonstrated in patients without CD. The PREDIMED study provided strong evidence that adherence to Mediterranean dietary patterns could help to prevent CV disease.40 An inverse association was found between adherence to a Mediterranean diet and obesity indexes in adult subjects at high CV risk.41 The interim analysis of the ongoing PREDIMED-Plus trial showed that an energy-reduced Mediterranean diet and physical activity significantly reduced total and visceral fat in older adults with overweight or obesity and metabolic syndrome.42 In the PREDIMED-Reus study, the Mediterranean diet was effective without calorie restriction in preventing diabetes in patients at high CV risk. The risk reduction occurred without significant changes in body weight or physical activity.43 In the CORDIOPREV trial, the Mediterranean diet was superior to the low-fat diet in preventing major CV events in secondary prevention.44

Dietary education is effective in different patient groups to improve Mediterranean diet adherence and promote healthy eating. Nutrition intervention was effective among firefighters in a US study: in the intervention group, the MDS significantly increased.45 In the PREDIMED study, a 1-year-long dietary intervention conducted by dietitians successfully improved adherence to a Mediterranean diet in patients with high CV risk.46 CD patients have lower adherence to a Mediterranean diet; they consume less vegetables and fish.47 This eating habit is especially disadvantageous because of their proven increased CV risk.10 This highlights the importance of promoting the Mediterranean diet among these patients; furthermore, dietary education and counselling should not only focus on strict GFD.

Current coeliac guidelines do not give recommendations regarding CV risk assessment and prevention of metabolic diseases. The 2019 European guideline poses a strong recommendation (with moderate evidence) regarding dietary education assessment of nutritional status on diagnosis and highlights the importance of fibre intake yet neglects to advise for follow-up patient care.

CD guidelines should cover nutritional counselling, healthy lifestyle and patient follow-up, encouraging patients and dietitians to participate actively in their development.48 49 Moreover, patients should also be informed about the adverse changes that may occur with GFD, the increased CV risk and its prevention, and the importance of an adequate lifestyle.2 3 16 50

The effect of Mediterranean dietary education on body composition and CV risk-related metabolic parameters of CD were never studied. There is no ongoing or completed study on this topic. In this prospective RCT, we aim to test the effect of a group-based, structured, 1-year, advanced dietary education regarding the most crucial CV risk factors (eg, metabolic parameters, body composition) among CD patients. The dietary intervention aims to promote the Mediterranean diet and healthy menu choices to reduce the risk of unfavourable metabolic consequences associated with an unbalanced GFD. Early intervention is essential in cases where CV risk factors are observed among young individuals. The study will ensure contributions to the literature regarding the effectiveness of an online dietary intervention in CD.

We hypothesise that this dietary intervention favourably impacts CD patients’ body composition and cardiometabolic parameters. We hope these results will also contribute towards guideline developments.
