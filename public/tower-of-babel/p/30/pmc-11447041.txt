Activating transcription factor 4 plays a major role in shaping the transcriptional response to isoginkgetin in HCT116 cells
Authors: Erin van Zyl, John D H Stead, Claire Peneycad, Carole L Yauk, Bruce C McKay
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractActivating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) plays a central role in the integrated stress response (ISR) and one overlapping branch of the unfolded protein response (UPR). We recently reported that the splicing inhibitor isoginkgetin (IGG) induced ATF4 protein along with several known ATF4-regulated transcripts in a response that resembled the ISR and UPR. However, the contribution of ATF4-dependent and -independent transcriptional responses to IGG exposure was not known. Here we used RNA-sequencing in HCT116 colon cancer cells and an isogenic subline lacking ATF4 to investigate the contribution of ATF4 to IGG-induced changes in gene expression. Approximately 85% of the IGG-responsive DEGs in HCT116 cells were also differentially expressed in response to the ER stressor thapsigargin (Tg) and these were enriched for genes associated with the UPR and ISR. Most of these were positively regulated by IGG with impaired responses in the ATF4-deficient cells. Nonetheless, there were DEGs that responded similarly in both cell lines. The ATF4-independent IGG-induced DEGs included several metal responsive transcripts encoding metallothionines and a zinc transporter. Taken together, the predominant IGG response was ATF4-dependent in these cells and resembled the UPR and ISR while a second less prominent response involved the ATF4-independent regulation of metal responsive mRNAs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74391-8.Keywords: Isoginkgetin, Endoplasmic reticulum stress, ATF4, Thapsigargin, Unfolded protein response, Integrated stress responseSubject terms: RNA splicing, Cell biology, Genetics, Molecular biology

Introduction

Activating Transcription Factor 4 (ATF4) is a basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factor that can form heterodimers with other bZIP proteins to regulate gene expression1. ATF4 is preferentially translated under conditions of cellular stress through an adaptive cellular response called the integrated stress response (ISR). The ISR is activated by multiple cellular stresses that loosely fit into amino acid deprivation, accumulation of double stranded RNA, heme deficiency and/or oxidative stress, and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress2. These stresses are sensed by the general control nonderepressible 2 (GCN2), protein kinase RNA activated (PKR), heme-regulated inhibitor kinase (HRI) and protein kinase R-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK) kinases, respectively2. Each of these kinases can phosphorylate eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 (eIF2α), inhibiting cap-dependent translation while permitting the translational upregulation of ATF4 through a mechanism involving upstream open-reading frames (uORFs)2. ER-stress can also stimulate signaling through two other ER transmembrane proteins (ATF6 and IRE1α)3,4. Collectively PERK, ATF6, and IRE1α initiate separate branches of the unfolded protein response5. The PERK pathway contributes to both the UPR and ISR. Therefore, the ATF4 protein is central to all branches of the ISR, but it is only involved in 1 of the 3 branches of the UPR.

Gingko biloba extracts are widely used in traditional Chinese medicine and their effectiveness is under active investigation for a variety of conditions6–11. These extracts are rich in bioflavonoid compounds including isoginkgetin (IGG)12. IGG has potential anti-neoplastic activity that could be mediated through cell cycle checkpoints, the induction of apoptosis, inhibition of cell migration and inhibition of invasion13–16. IGG can affect gene expression directly because it is an inhibitor of pre-mRNA splicing in vitro and in vivo17,18. Furthermore, IGG can affect gene expression by causing the accumulation of RNA polymerase II in promoter proximal regions of genes19. In addition, it has been suggested that IGG is an inhibitor of carboxylesterase 220and cyclin-dependent kinase 612. Therefore, IGG may affect cell biology through a variety of mechanisms.

We previously used oligonucleotide microarrays to identify mRNAs that were differentially expressed in response to IGG21,22. We identified 53 upregulated and 26 downregulated transcripts in IGG-treated HCT116 cells. There were several readily identifiable ATF4-regulated transcripts among the upregulated mRNAs, and these led to a statistical over-representation of UPR- and ISR-related Reactome pathways21,22. Importantly, we targeted ATF4 with CRISPR-Cas9 to create an ATF4-deficient cell line (ATF4-def). The response of these ATF4-responsive genes to IGG was attenuated in this genetically modified cell line21. However, the ATF4-responsive mRNAs linked to these Reactome pathways represented only a small fraction of the differentially regulated mRNAs. Therefore, we hypothesized that IGG exposure would lead to changes in the expression of both ATF4-dependent and -independent genes.

To test this, we used RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) to characterize the transcriptional response of parental and ATF4-def cells to IGG. Thapsigargin (Tg) was included as a positive control because it is an inhibitor of the SERCA calcium transporter that should lead to decreased ER Ca2+, loss of ER chaperone activity and the accumulation of misfolded proteins in the ER23–25. Therefore, Tg was expected to activate all 3 branches of the UPR (Ire1α/XBP1, ATF6, and PERK/ATF4 pathways) and the PERK-dependent branch of the ISR23. We found that many IGG responsive transcripts exhibited an ATF4-dependent pattern of gene expression and they were also responsive to Tg. Interestingly, IGG treatment altered the expression of some transcripts in an ATF4-independent manner, including several metallothionines. These results highlight the central role of ATF4 in the response of HCT116 cells to IGG and expands our understanding of ATF4-independent responses to this drug.

Results

ATF4-deficient cells exhibited changes in the expression of genes encoding plasma membrane and extracellular matrix proteins

HCT116 and the isogenic ATF4-def strain21 were treated with IGG or Tg, and solvent DMSO controls alongside untreated cells to determine the extent to which ATF4 contributes to IGG responses. DEGs for all contrasts are found in Supplementary Tables S2–S4. First, we compared gene expression in untreated ATF4-def to untreated HCT116 cells to determine how ATF4 loss affects gene expression prior to drug treatment. Over 13,000 transcripts met our inclusion criteria and over 800 were differentially expressed between cell lines (Table 1). Approximately 60% of these DEGs were also differentially expressed between cell lines following exposure to the DMSO solvent controls (Table 1). We compared the list of 548 DEGs to a list of 234 ATF4-target genes compiled through a recent systematic review of ATF4 chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and ChIP-sequencing (ChIP-seq) data26. This list represents the most comprehensive compilation of ATF4-regulated transcripts to date. Only 3 of the DEGs (CHAC1,MKX and PCK2) in the untreated and DMSO-treated comparisons were among the direct targets of ATF4 reported in the systematic review26. Therefore, there were significant differences in basal gene expression between cell lines, but most were unlikely to be directly regulated by ATF4.

Reactome analysis of DEGs under untreated conditions revealed two groups of pathways that were over-represented among DEGS: extracellular matrix (ECM) and transcription factor pathways (Table 2). IGG affected the expression of more transcripts in the parental cells than the ATF4-def subline

The primary goal of the present work was to characterize the transcriptional response to IGG and determine to what extent ATF4 participates in this response. Tg was included as a positive control for UPR and ISR activation. The number of DEGs for each drug treatment is summarized in Table 4 and the complete gene lists are provided in Supplementary Tables S3 and S4. The number of DEGs following Tg treatment increased from 2 to 8 h in both cell lines and the number of DEGs was similar in parental and ATF4-def cells (Table 4). In contrast, IGG exposure led to half the number of DEGs in ATF4-def cells compared to the parental cell line (Table 4). Therefore, despite the fact that Tg is an ER stressor that activates the UPR, ATF4 and ATF4-target genes3, only IGG exposure produced an ATF4-dependent pattern of expression among both up-regulated and down-regulated transcripts. This is likely because Tg activates all 3 branches of the UPR, so the PERK- and ATF4-dependent component is only one branch5,28. Conversely, it is possible that IGG only activates the PERK-dependent branch or another ISR pathway.

Most IGG-responsive transcripts also responded to Tg

We compared DEGs between IGG- and Tg- treated HCT116 and ATF4-def cells. In both cell lines, the majority (> 80%) of IGG responsive transcripts also responded to Tg at 2 and/or 8 h (Fig. 1A). There were 119 and 75 DEGs identified across both IGG and Tg (2 and 8 h) treatment groups in parental and ATF4-def cells, respectively (Fig. 1A). There were more IGG-responsive genes in common with the Tg treatment groups in the parental compared to the ATF4-def cells (381 vs. 186 DEGs, respectively). There were also far more IGG responsive DEGs in common with the 8-h Tg treatment alone (IGG (8 h) ∩ Tg (8 h) – Tg (2 h)) compared to the 2-hour Tg treatment alone (IGG 8 h) ∩ Tg (2 h) – Tg (8 h)) in both HCT116 (253–9) and ATF4-def (106–5) cells (Fig. 1A). Therefore, many IGG-responsive mRNAs were differentially expressed in response to Tg in both cell lines. However, there were more DEGs common to the IGG and Tg responses in the parental cell line.

DEGs from each drug treatment were compared between parental HCT116 cells and the ATF4-def subline (Fig. 1B). The IGG response was unique in that there were far more DEGs specifically altered in the parental HCT116 cells compared to the ATF4-def subline (291–74, respectively) while only 152 DEGs were common to both lists. The same general patterns were present in the increased and decreased populations of DEGs except that downregulated transcripts were less common than up-regulated transcripts (Fig. 1C). Therefore, there appears to be an ATF4-dependent pattern of expression following IGG treatment in HCT116 cells that is not apparent in response to Tg.

IGG led to greater fold changes in gene expression in the parental cells

To better visualize the differences in the magnitude of transcriptional responses between cell lines, we plotted the fold change in expression of genes that were classified as DEGs in either cell line, for each of the three treatment conditions (IGG 8 h, Tg 2 h, and Tg 8 h). Exposure to IGG led to more induced than inhibited transcripts (Fig. 2A). Most IGG-responsive transcripts exhibited stronger fold changes (both up- and down-regulated) in the parental cells compared with ATF4-def cells (Fig. 2A). There were only a few transcripts that appeared to be preferentially induced or repressed by IGG in the ATF4-def cells (Fig. 2A). This pattern is consistent with a broad effect of ATF4 on IGG-induced gene expression. Similarly, many DEGs had higher fold-changes in parental cells than in ATF4-def cells following 8 h but not 2 h of Tg exposure (Fig. 2B,C). Following 2 h Tg, there was no clear ATF4-dependent pattern of expression (Fig. 2B), with most DEGs exhibiting higher fold changes in the ATF4-def cells compared with parental cells. Overall, IGG appeared to have the most prominent ATF4-dependent pattern of expression in these cell lines.

Reactome analysis identifies ATF4-dependent and -independent pathways activated by IGG

To determine what pathways were affected by IGG and Tg exposure, we performed Reactome pathway analysis of DEGs under all conditions (Supplementary Table S6A–F)29. IGG-treated cells showed 28 statistically overrepresented pathways (FDR < 0.05) in HCT116 cells but only 11 in the ATF4-deficient subline (Supplementary Table S6E,F). This is consistent with the larger number of DEGs detected in the parental cell line. Overrepresented pathways fell into several categories that were similar in both cell lines, including two groups of stress response pathways: Unfolded Protein Response (UPR) and Cellular response to stimuli (Tables 5 and 6).

Parental cells had 42 DEGs in the cellular response to stimuli pathway and these included two nested Reactome pathways: cellular response to stress and UPR/ISR (Fig. 3A,B). The 20 UPR- and/or ISR-related transcripts were all increased in response to IGG in the HCT116 parental cells. The other 18 transcripts in the cellular response to stress group (Fig. 3A) were not linked to the UPR or ISR in the Reactome database. Half of these transcripts were upregulated while the other half were downregulated (Fig. 3D). None of these transcripts were more responsive in the ATF4-deficient line and only 8 of these were differentially expressed in the ATF4-def cells (Fig. 3D). Six of the 9 induced transcripts in HCT116 cells (highlighted in red font) are direct targets of ATF426,30–35. There were no large differences between cell lines for downregulated transcripts (Fig. 3D, right side). Taken together, there is a clear ATF4-dependent effect of IGG on the expression of the known stress-responsive DEGs, including those that were not listed in the UPR and/or ISR Reactome pathways.

The 4 transcripts uniquely associated with the cellular response to stimuli were metallothionines (MT1X, MT1F, MT2A and MT1E) in the response to metal ions Reactome pathway. These transcripts are reportedly regulated by the metal regulatory transcription factor 1 (MTF1)36–39. We noted that another DEG, the SLC30A1 mRNA encoding the Zinc transporter 1 (ZNT1), is also known to be regulated by MTF1 and is metal responsive in mice37. We find that each of these 5 transcripts is up-regulated by 2- to 6-fold in response to IGG in both cell lines (Fig. 3E). We confirmed through qRT-PCR analysis that these metal responsive mRNAs were induced similarly by IGG in both cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S1). Therefore, these metal responsive transcripts appear to be regulated in an ATF4-independent manner. Taken together, ATF4 has a major influence on the magnitude of the transcriptional response to IGG but there are also some ATF4-independent DEGs, including metal responsive mRNAs.

The UPR response appeared broader in the Tg-treated cells at 2 and 8 h with all three branches of the UPR over-represented in the parental and ATF4-def cell line (Supplementary Table S6A–D). The over-represented UPR and ISR pathways were similar in Tg treated HCT116 and ATF4-def cells except that PERK regulates gene expression and ATF4 activates genes in response to endoplasmic reticulum stress were not over-represented among the DEGs detected in ATF4-def cells (Supplementary Table S5C–F). Unrelated pathways like NGF-stimulated transcription, Circadian Clock, and Toll Like Receptor 3 (TLR3) Cascade were also over-represented in both cell lines at 2 h. By 8 h, the Tg-response in parental cells included other Reactome pathways associated with normal ER function including Asparagine N-linked glycosylation, Cholesterol biosynthesis, ER to Golgi Anterograde Transport and N-glycan trimming in the ER and Calnexin/calreticulin cycle, a glycoprotein chaperone pathway in the ER40suggesting the ER function may be highly compromised by 8 h. Taken together, the UPR- and ISR- associated DEGs in Tg treated cells were similar in both cell lines at each time point but they differed in a limited number of pathways that involved the PERK/ATF4 pathway, consistent with loss of ATF4 protein expression in the targeted cell line21.

Identification of ATF4-regulated transcripts following IGG treatment

Five hundred and seventeen genes were differentially expressed in response to IGG in either parental cells, ATF4-def cells, or both (Fig. 1B). However, the fold change in expression of many of the cell line-specific DEGs was similar between cell lines because they were at or near the 1.5-fold threshold (see Fig. 2A). To more rigorously identify ATF4-dependent IGG-responsive transcripts, these 517 genes were filtered to include only genes that were induced to statistically higher levels following IGG exposure (p < 0.05 following FDR and > 1.5-fold in the ATF4-def_IGG_8h vs. HCT116_IGG_8h contrast in Supplementary Table S2F). All DEGs identified in this manner had greater fold change in parental cells compared with ATF4-def cells, indicating that all of these DEGs were ATF4-dependent (Fig. 4). Eighteen of these 68 transcripts, highlighted in red, are known to be direct targets of ATF426. Reactome analysis of these DEGs indicates that the over-represented pathways include UPR- and ISR-related pathways, as well as the more generic response to stress and stimuli discussed earlier (see Supplementary Table S6G). It is unclear if the fifty remaining genes are directly or indirectly regulated by ATF4. Taken together, this analysis indicates that IGG activates ATF4-dependent stress pathways that include the UPR and/or ISR.

Discussion

The regulation of gene expression is highly coordinated, with control mechanisms in place to prevent aberrant gene expression. For example, inhibitors of transcription are cytotoxic, and they can activate stress responses like the p53 tumour suppressor pathway to prevent the propagation of cells with widespread alterations in transcription41–43. Several years ago, we sought to determine if inhibitors of pre-mRNA splicing elicited a defined stress response including activation of the p53 response. We thus performed oligonucleotide microarray analysis of IGG-treated HCT116 and an isogenic p53 null subline21,22. This analysis suggested the involvement of PERK regulates gene expression and the unfolded protein response pathways but there was no evidence of a p53 transcriptional response22. These two pathways were only represented by 4 IGG-induced transcripts but they pointed us to the ISR and the PERK-dependent branch of the UPR involving ATF4, pursued here21.

Exposure to IGG led to more DEGs in parental HCT116 cells than the ATF4-deficient line and the DEGs tended to be induced to higher levels in the parental cells. The primary pathways were associated with cellular responses to stress including the UPR and response to metal ions. The former was largely ATF4-dependent in HCT116 cells while the metal response was ATF4-independent. At present there is no known target of IGG that predicts the activation of these pathways.

IGG is an inhibitor of pre-mRNA splicing that reportedly increases the time required to excise exons by approximately 2.5-fold17,18. It is conceivable that a subset of unspliced mRNAs is translated such that misfolded proteins accumulate in the ER and activate the UPR. This seems somewhat unlikely because the other branches of the UPR do not seem to be activated by IGG while they were in response to our positive control, Tg. The only transcripts linked to those pathways in our IGG data set are shared with the PERK-dependent branch of the UPR/ISR. Another splicing-dependent response could be through the accumulation of RNA products that activate the ISR through another ISR kinase like PKR.

We must also acknowledge that IGG has many effects on the cell and several proposed mechanisms of action that do not involve pre-mRNA splicing in vitro and in vivo17,18. IGG inhibits transcription by causing the accumulation of RNA polymerase II in promoter proximal regions of genes19. The accumulation of promoter proximal stalled RNA polymerase II is often associated with activation of the p53 pathway41,42. However, we didn’t detect a p53 signature here and the IGG response in HCT116 and an isogenic subline deleted of p53 are similar21,22. In addition, IGG has been reported to inhibit carboxylesterase 2 and cyclin-dependent kinase 612,20. The specific mechanism leading to the activation of the ISR or PERK-dependent UPR remains to be determined but ATF4 seems to play a prominent role downstream of these pathways in response to IGG. The contribution of other transcription factors to IGG response, including a hypothesized role for MTF1 in the response to metal ions warrants future study.

Intriguingly, comparison of the DEGs in untreated and DMSO treated controls highlighted alterations in the expression of many transcripts associated with extracellular and membrane associated proteins (Tables 2 and 3). Very few of these transcripts were altered by IGG or Tg exposure and only 3 were on the list of known targets of ATF426. Therefore, the role of ATF4 in the UPR/ISR appears to be distinct from its basal role in gene regulation in these cells. Nonetheless, secreted and membrane proteins are synthesized, processed and post-translationally modified in the ER and Golgi4,27 so the loss of ATF4 could affect the endomembrane system leading to feedback mechanisms that contribute to the deregulation of mRNAs encoding secreted and membrane-associated proteins. This hypothesis will require testing in the future.

In conclusion, the loss of ATF4 in HCT116 cells is associated with changes in the expression of mRNAs associated with secreted and membrane-associated proteins and this is at least partially dissociated from the role of ATF4 in the UPR and ISR. The response to IGG is dominated by an ATF4-dependent transcriptional response with a more modest ATF4-independent response that likely involves the metal-responsive MTF1 transcription factor and possibly other transcription factor(s) yet to be identified. Most IGG-responsive transcripts are similarly responsive to the ER stressor Tg establishing a firm link between IGG treatment and the UPR/ISR. At present, it is unclear if this occurs due to a splicing defect or another target of the compound. It is possible that alterations in pre-mRNA splicing either cause the accumulation of unfolded proteins in the ER or the accumulation of RNA intermediates. Either of these plausible effects could activate the UPR and/or ISR through PERK and/or PKR. Regardless of upstream pathways, the central role of ATF4 detected here provides insight into the effects of this natural compound on gene expression.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and drug treatment

HCT116 colon cancer cells were obtained from the American Tissue Type Collection (CAT #: CCL-247). HCT116 ATF4-deficient (ATF4-def) cells were generated through a tandem guide CRISPR-mediated strategy in our lab resulting in an in frame deletion in ATF4 (112–195), as described previously21. These cells do not express detectable levels of ATF4 protein even after Tg exposure21,44. HCT116 and the derived cell line were grown in McCoys medium (Cytvia). Both were supplemented with Newborn Calf Serum (NBCS) and Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) in a 3:1 ratio to give 12% serum, and 90 units/ml penicillin, 90 µg/ml streptomycin (Wisent). Cells were seeded 24 h prior to drug treatment in a 6-well tissue culture plate at 400,000 cells/well. DMSO, IGG and Tg were purchased from Millipore Sigma Canada Ltd (Oakville, ON). Stock solutions of IGG and Tg were dissolved in DMSO. An equal volume of DMSO was used as a solvent control in all experiments with IGG and Tg.

RNA-seq experimental design

HCT116 and the isogenic ATF4-def subline were treated with 30 µM IGG, 2 nM Tg or an equal volume of DMSO, alongside untreated cells, for 8 h and RNA was collected for RNA-seq to determine the extent to which ATF4 contributes to the IGG response. Tg is known to activate gene expression rapidly with downstream targets increasing within 2 h while IGG induces ATF4-dependent gene expression within 8 h21. Therefore, Tg-treated and DMSO control were also collected at 2 h and collected for analysis in parallel to ensure that we captured the early Tg response, for comparison. Four independent biological experiments were used for RNA sequencing. All contrasts involved pairwise comparisons between individual groups and the time matched DMSO controls.

RNA isolation

RNA was isolated as described previously21. Briefly, media were removed from the cells and cell monolayers were washed twice with PBS (pH 7.4). RNA was extracted using the EZ-10 DNA away RNA Miniprep kit from BioBasic according to manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was quantified using the Qubit 4 Fluorometer (ThermoFisher Scientific) and the Qubit RNA BR (broad range) Assay kit (ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality was confirmed using the 4200 TapeStation System (Agilent) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Library preparation

Library preparation was completed following the Illumina Stranded mRNA prep, Ligation Reference Guide (1000000124518). Briefly, 1 µg of RNA for each sample was loaded into a 96-well plate in a randomized well location. Illumina Poly(A) Capture (2004893), cDNA synthesis (2004895) and RNA PRE Ligation (20040897) kits were used to purify polyadenylated mRNA and synthesize first and second strand cDNA. Anchors were ligated using the IDT for Illumina RNA index Anchors (200040899), and samples were indexed using the IDT for Illumina DNA/RNA UD Indexes Set A (20026121). Sample quantity and quality was then measured using a Qubit 4 fluorometer, Qubit RNA BR Assay kit and 4200 TapeStation System as described above. Equal amounts of library for each sample were pooled and diluted to 750 pM. The pooled sample was loaded onto a NextSeq 2000 Reagent Cartridge (200 Cycles) (Illumina, 20040560). The samples were then sequenced using an Illumina NextSeq 2000. The data discussed in this publication have been deposited in NCBI’s Gene Expression Omnibus45 and are accessible through GEO Series accession number GSE249009 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE249009).

Data analysis

Data were processed using the Omics data analysis framework for regulatory application (R-ODAF) pipeline46 available at https://github.com/R-ODAF/R-ODAF_Health_Canada/. Briefly, raw paired-end sequence data were demultiplexed with bclfastq (v2.20) to generate sample-specific sequence files. Fastp (v0.23.2) processing removed adapter sequences, low-quality bases and reads shorter than 36 nt. Trimmed reads were aligned to the Genome Reference Consortium Human Build 38 reference genome using the STAR aligner (v2.7.10a)47, with annotations applied using version 107 of the corresponding GTF files. A count matrix was generated using RSEM (v1.3.3). Sample quality was assessed using MultiQC, with study-wide QC assessed with a series of metrics, standardized within the R-ODAF pipeline. Samples were removed if: (a) the mean Spearman’s correlation coefficient calculated against other samples was < 0.9; (b) the total number of mapped reads was < 2,500,000; (c) the fraction of reads that were successfully mapped to a genomic location was < 50%; (d) the number of genes with > 5 uniquely mapped reads was outside 3 times the interquartile range for the dataset; (e) the number of genes which together account for 80% of total reads was outside 3 times the interquartile range; or (f) the sample displayed a Gini coefficient > 0.95. All samples passed all QC thresholds.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the DESeq2 package48which applies the Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple testing. DEGs were defined by corrected p value < 0.05, and an absolute fold change > 1.5, following application of fold-change shrinkage by the ashr method49. DEGs also had to be expressed at > 1 CPM in > 75% of samples from at least one treatment group within the contrast.

Pathway analysis

Enrichment analysis of DEGs (up-regulated genes and down-regulated genes) was performed using the Reactome pathway and Gene Ontology databases accessed through the DAVID Bioinformatics Resource website (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/home.jsp)29. DEG lists were compared using an online multiple list comparator (https://molbiotools.com/listcompare.php).

Reverse transcription and qRT-PCR

RNA was isolated as described above. RNA concentration and quality was measured using a DeNovix DS-11 Spectrophotometer. Equal amounts of RNA were converted to cDNA using the ThermoFisher Scientific High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit. cDNA was diluted, and qPCR was completed using Bioline SensiFAST™ Probe HI-ROX Master Mix (FroggaBio Inc, Toronto, ON) with SYBR green or Bioline SensiFAST™ Probe NO-ROX with SYBR green (FroggaBio Inc, Toronto, ON) for analysis using an Applied Biosystems StepOnePlus (Thermo Fisher, Ottawa, ON) and CFX384 (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Saint-Laurent, QC) thermocyclers, respectively. Primers were generated by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) (Coralville, Ia). Primer sequences can be found in Supplementary Table S1.
