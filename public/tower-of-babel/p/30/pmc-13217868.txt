Functional sQTLs regulating PTK2B exon 31 splicing uncover an RNA-dependent modulation of its kinase activity and cellular phenotype
Authors: Masahide Kato, Ayaka Isazawa, Ai Ohki, Akari Fujita, Makoto Araki, Motoaki Yanaizu, Yoshihiro Kino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGenome-wide association studies have shown that polymorphisms in PTK2B (Protein Tyrosine Kinase 2 Beta), encoding the calcium-sensitive tyrosine kinase PYK2 (Proline-rich tyrosine kinase 2), are associated with an increased risk of Alzheimer’s disease (AD). However, it remains unclear which genetic variants of PTK2B are functionally important. A recent transcriptome-wide association study has identified alternative splicing of exon 31 in PTK2B as being associated with AD, and proposed rs2251430 as the relevant splicing quantitative trait locus (sQTL). Here, we aimed to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms and functional significance of exon 31 splicing.MethodsAlternative splicing of exon 31 was evaluated using publicly available RNA-seq data. To investigate the regulation of exon 31 splicing, we performed RT-PCR, RNA pull-down assays, Liquid Chromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry, and RNA immunoprecipitation. Western blotting, immunofluorescence, and co-immunoprecipitation analyses were conducted to examine the effects of exon 31 and its polymorphisms on PYK2 protein expression and function. The impact of exon 31 on cellular phenotypes was further assessed using antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) treatment in HeLa and HMC3 cells.ResultsWe detected exon 31 skipping in certain human cell types, but not in mice, and observed its increase in microglia during AD progression. We then identified rs751019 and rs751018, both located near rs2251430, as sQTLs that affect exon 31 splicing, with rs751019 having a greater impact. Among RNA-binding proteins that interact with the surrounding sequence of rs751019, PCBP1 and PCBP2 were found to redundantly regulate exon 31 inclusion. PYK2 lacking exon 31 exhibited reduced protein expression and kinase activity, which in turn resulted in diminished phosphorylation of its substrate, GSK3β, with little change in calmodulin binding and intracellular localization. Moreover, ASO-induced exon 31 skipping decreased cell migration in a wound healing assay. It also increased multinucleation in human microglia-like HMC3 cells, a process recently identified in protective microglia.ConclusionsOur study validates rs751019 and rs751018 as functional sQTLs that influence PTK2B exon 31 splicing. It also identifies species-specific alternative splicing of exon 31 as a modulator of PYK2 expression and kinase activity, thereby linking sQTL-associated genetic variants to molecular and cellular changes that may underlie AD pathogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02835-x.Keywords: PTK2B/PYK2, Alternative splicing, SQTL, Alzheimer’s disease, Antisense oligonucleotide

Background

The PTK2B (Protein Tyrosine Kinase 2 Beta) gene encodes PYK2 (Proline-rich tyrosine kinase 2), a non-receptor tyrosine kinase which is sensitive to intracellular Ca2⁺ levels [1]. PYK2 is expressed in the central nervous system and hematopoietic cells, and within the brain, it is present in both neurons and microglia [1, 2]. PYK2 participates in a variety of physiological and pathological processes, including cell adhesion and migration, inflammatory responses, tumor invasiveness, and synaptic plasticity [3–7]. Through these diverse functions, PTK2B has been implicated in a wide range of human disorders, such as cancer [6, 8, 9], neurological diseases [10], psychiatric disorders [11–13], bone disorders [14–17], cardiovascular diseases [18–20], and autoimmune diseases [21, 22].

Recent genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have repeatedly identified PTK2B as a genetic risk locus for late-onset Alzheimer’s disease [23–29]. To date, no coding variants in PTK2B have been linked to AD risk [10]; rather, most known variants appear to influence gene expression levels. For example, the AD risk allele rs2279590-C, located in an enhancer region near PTK2B, has been suggested to increase the expression of PTK2B and several neighboring genes [30]. Another variant, rs28834970-C, located upstream of the start codon in intron 6, has been associated with elevated PTK2B mRNA and protein expression in monocytes [31], and a recent study also reported increased PTK2B expression in the blood of AD patients carrying this allele [32]. Conversely, in macrophages and microglia, the rs28834970-C allele is predicted to reduce the binding affinity of the transcription factor CEBPβ, leading to decreased PTK2B mRNA levels [33, 34]. Thus, the effects of AD risk alleles on PTK2B expression remain inconsistent across studies, and their precise regulatory impact is yet to be determined.

The functional role of PTK2B in AD pathology also remains unknown. Studies using primary microglia and cultured cells have demonstrated that PYK2 becomes activated in response to amyloid-β (Aβ) stimulation [35]. Increased PYK2 activation has been observed in wild-type mouse brain slices treated with Aβ oligomers (Aβo) and in aged APPswe/PS1ΔE9 (APP/PS1) transgenic mouse brains [36, 37]. Furthermore, Aβo-induced dendritic spine loss in hippocampal neurons depends on PYK2 expression [38], and deletion of PYK2 in APP/PS1 mice attenuates Aβo-induced synaptic plasticity deficits and dendritic spine loss [39], suggesting that PYK2 mediates Aβ-induced neurotoxicity. In vitro studies have shown that PYK2 phosphorylates tau, either directly or indirectly through the activation of other kinases such as GSK3 and Fyn [40–42]. Consistent with this, overexpression of PYK2 in tau transgenic mice significantly increases tau phosphorylation at Tyr18 [41].

In contrast, some reports suggest a protective role of PYK2 in AD, as its overexpression improves synaptic markers and behavioral outcomes in 5XFAD mice [43], while PYK2 deletion in PS19 mice exacerbates tau hyperphosphorylation and behavioral deficits [42]. Collectively, these findings indicate that the impact of PTK2B variants and PYK2 activity on AD pathogenesis remains unresolved, and the potential of PYK2 as a therapeutic target for AD is still unclear.

Recently, a transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) integrating brain transcriptomic data from the ROS/MAP cohort (n ≈ 450) with large-scale GWAS data (> 70,000 individuals) identified rs2251430-G, located in intron 31 of PTK2B, as a variant potentially affecting alternative splicing of exon 31 and conferring AD risk [44]. However, no studies have yet investigated the regulation or functional significance of exon 31 splicing.

PYK2 comprises an N-terminal Four-point-one, Ezrin, Radixin, Moesin (FERM) domain, a kinase domain, and a focal adhesion targeting (FAT) domain [10]. Exon 31 encodes a region within the kinase–FAT linker (KFL) sequence. PYK2 activation is closely associated with its domain organization. Under basal conditions, PYK2 adopts an autoinhibited conformation, in which the FERM and kinase domains interact to form a closed structure that suppresses kinase activity [45]. Ca2⁺ induced self-association (dimerization or clustering) disrupts this autoinhibition and triggers kinase activation [10]. A key regulator of this process is calmodulin (CaM), which binds to sites in both the FERM [46] and kinase [47] domains to promote PYK2 dimerization and autophosphorylation at Y402, leading to activation. A recent study identified a novel CaM-binding region within the KFL. This region (KFL728–839) has been shown to function as a dimerization interface for PYK2, and CaM binding strengthens dimer formation through this region, thereby promoting PYK2 autophosphorylation [48]. Notably, exon 31 partially overlaps this CaM-binding region, suggesting that the presence or absence of exon 31 may influence PYK2 dimerization, enzymatic activity, and ultimately AD pathogenesis. However, the mechanisms regulating exon 31 alternative splicing and its functional consequences remain unknown.

In this study, we first identified exon 31 of PTK2B as a cell type- and species-specific alternative exon, whose splicing pattern changes in accordance with the progression of AD. To evaluate the impact of single nucleotide variants (SNVs) on exon 31 inclusion, we constructed splicing-reporter minigenes and sought to identify splicing quantitative trait loci (sQTLs) associated with exon 31 usage. We found that two SNVs, rs751019 and rs751018, influence exon 31 splicing, whereas rs2251430—previously reported by TWAS—does not. Among them, rs751019 exerted the stronger effect on splicing, and we identified PCBP1 and PCBP2 as RNA-binding proteins that associate with the sequence surrounding rs751019 and regulate exon 31 inclusion. Furthermore, PYK2 lacking exon 31 exhibited reduced protein stability as well as decreased kinase activity. In addition, inducing exon 31 skipping in HeLa and the human microglial cell line HMC3 resulted in reduced cell motility or increased multinucleation. These findings suggest that the splicing pattern of exon 31 directly determines the molecular and functional properties of PYK2, thereby influencing cellular phenotypes.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and cell line establishment

HEK293 cells (RCB1637, RIKEN BioResource Center, Ibaraki, Japan), Neuro2a (N2a) cells (#CCL-131, ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA), HeLa cells (#CCL-2, ATCC) and HepG2 cells (HB-8065, ATCC) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Nichirei Bioscience, Tokyo, Japan) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution (Wako, Osaka, Japan). THP-1 cells (RCB1189, RIKEN BRC) and Jurkat cells (RCB3052, RIKEN BRC) were grown in RPMI 1640 medium containing GlutaMAX (Invitrogen), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. The human microglia cell line HMC3 (EP-CL-0620, Elabscience, Houston, TX, USA) was cultured in Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (EMEM, Wako) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator under an atmosphere of 5% CO₂. HEK293-dCasRx cells were established as follows. PB-Tet-dCasRx-Azu-Puro and an expression vector for Super PiggyBac transposase (System Biosciences, Palo Alto, CA, USA) were co-transfected into HEK293 cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Transfected cells were selected with 0.125 μg/mL puromycin. After selection with puromycin, single cells were seeded into 96-well plates using a cell sorter MA900 (SONY, Tokyo, Japan), and multiple stable clones were established. Induction of HA expression after 24 h of treatment with 1 μg/mL doxycycline (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) was confirmed before cells were harvested for subsequent experiments.

Plasmids

All engineered plasmids were verified by Sanger sequencing (Fasmac, Kanagawa, Japan). The primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

PTK2B minigene constructs and its variants

The PTK2B Reference minigene fragment covering exons 30 to 32 was amplified from the genomic DNA of THP-1 cells by PCR using BglII-exon30-Fw and SalI-exon32-Rv. The fragment was inserted into the BglII/SalI site of the pEGFP-C1 vector (Clontech, San Jose, CA, USA). To construct allele-specific minigenes, this plasmid was used as a template for overlap-extension PCR. For the PTK2B-A (rs751018) minigene, two overlapping fragments were amplified using primer sets EGFP-C1-Fw/PTK2B-rs751018-A-Rv and PTK2B-rs751018-A-Fw/SV40-pA-seq-Rv. The fragments were joined by PCR, digested with BglII and SalI, and inserted into the corresponding sites of the vector. Using the same strategy, the following minigenes were constructed: PTK2B-C (rs751019) using primers EGFP-C1-Fw/PTK2B-rs751019-C-Rv and PTK2B-rs751019-C-Fw/SV40-pA-seq-Rv, PTK2B-T (rs2237819) using primers EGFP-C1-Fw/PTK2B-rs2237819-T-Rv and PTK2B-rs2237819-T-Fw/SV40-pA-seq-Rv, PTK2B-G (rs2251430) using primers EGFP-C1-Fw/PTK2B-rs2251430-G-Rv and PTK2B-rs2251430-G-Fw/SV40-pA-seq-Rv. Each pair of PCR products was assembled, digested with BglII and SalI, and cloned into the pEGFP-C1 vector.

CDS Constructs for PCBP1 and PCBP2

PCBP1 was amplified from a cDNA library of HMC3 cells and digested with BamHI and SalI. The fragment was inserted into the BglII/SalI site of pEGFP-C1. PCBP2 was also amplified from HMC3 cells and inserted into the BglII/SalI site of pEGFP-C1 using NEBuilder (New England BioLabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). GST fusion PCBP1 was generated by PCR using the primers BamHI-PCBP1-Fw and SalI-PCBP1-no-stop-Rv. The resulting DNA fragment was digested with BamHI and SalI, and inserted into the BamHI/SalI sites of the pGEX-6P-2(TH) vector, which contains a thrombin cleavage site and a His-tag inserted downstream of the original pGEX-6P-2 sequence (GE Healthcare Bio-science, Uppsala, Sweden, now Cytiva). For the negative control, pGEX-6P-2(TH) was digested with EcoRI and XhoI, blunt-ended using T4 DNA Polymerase (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan), and re-ligated. The resulting construct was designated as pGEX-6P-2(TH)-NC and used as a negative control.

Construction of PTK2B-gRNA

Seven different gRNAs targeting distinct sequences within exon 31 of the PTK2B gene were designed and constructed. To generate the insert DNA, gRNA-specific forward and reverse primers were annealed in Annealing Buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA) with nuclease-free water by incubation at 95 °C for 5 min. The resulting double-stranded oligonucleotides were then ligated into the BbsI-digested pXR003 vector (Addgene plasmid #109,053) [49].

CDS constructs for PTK2B and its variants

The CDS PTK2B (rs751019-C) construct was prepared by amplifying a PTK2B cDNA fragment from HMO6 cDNA [50], followed by digestion with BglII and XhoI, and insertion into the BglII/SalI site of pEGFP-C1. To introduce an A-to-C substitution at rs751019 in exon 31, the PTK2B cDNA was used as a template for overlap-extension PCR. Two fragments were amplified with primer sets PTK2B-mid-seq-Fw2/PTK2B-A-Rv and PTK2B-A-Fw/SV40-pA-seq-Rv, then fused by PCR and cloned into the vector after BglII and XhoI digestion. To generate the PTK2B-dE31 construct, exon 31 was deleted by PCR amplification of the 5′ region (EGFP-C1-Fw/PTK2B-dE31-Rv) and 3′ region (PTK2B-dE31-Fw/SV40-pA-seq-Rv), followed by PCR fusion and cloning into the vector after BglII and XhoI digestion. For all three constructs (PTK2B, PTK2B-A, PTK2B-dE31), the EGFP tag was removed by digestion with NheI and BglII, followed by blunt-end ligation using T4 DNA polymerase. To generate V5 and 3 × FLAG-tagged constructs for all PTK2B variants described above (PTK2B-A, PTK2B-C and PTK2B-dE31), each construct was used as a template for PCR amplification with the primer pair PTK2B-mid-seq-Fw2 and SmaI-EcoRI-SalI-PTK2B-no-stop-Rv. The amplified fragments were digested with BsmBI and SmaI, and inserted into the corresponding vector to remove the stop codon and introduce SalI and EcoRI restriction sites downstream of the coding sequence. Subsequently, DNA fragments encoding the 3 × FLAG or V5 epitope were amplified using primer pairs SalI-FLAG-Fw/EcoRI-FLAG-Rv and SalI-V5-Fw/EcoRI-V5-Rv, respectively. These fragments were digested with SalI and EcoRI and ligated into the corresponding PTK2B constructs, yielding 3 × FLAG- and V5-tagged PTK2B expression constructs.

KFL728–850 constructs

To investigate the effects of the rs751019 variant located in exon 31 and the presence or absence of exon 31 on PTK2B function, we constructed a KFL728–850 fragment, which extends the previously reported CaM-binding region (KFL728–839) to include the amino acid sequence up to exon 32. Each PTK2B construct served as a template for PCR amplification using the primers BamHI-KFL728–850-Fw and XhoI- KFL728–850-Rv. The resulting PCR fragments were digested with BamHI and XhoI, and cloned into the BglII/SalI sites of the pEGFP-C1 vector.

Recombinant tau protein expression construct

The MAPT gene encoding tau protein (0N4R isoform) was amplified from a cDNA library derived from SK-N-SH cells, which had been obtained in our previous study [51]. The amplified fragment was digested with BamHI and SalI and subsequently cloned into the BamHI/SalI sites of the pGEX-6P-2(TH) vector.

Construction of PB-tet-dCasRx-Azu-Puro

The dCasRx-HA coding sequence was amplified from pXR002 (Addgene, #109,050) [49] using the NheI-CasRx-Fw and BglII-CasRx(HA)-Rv primers. The PCR product was digested with NheI and BglII and subsequently inserted into the NheI–BamHI site of the PB-tet-mCherry-Azu-puro vector [52]. The resulting plasmid was referred to as PB-tet-dCasRx-Azu-Puro.

Cellular splicing assay

A cellular splicing assay was performed as previously described [53]. For this splicing assay, HEK293 cells were seeded on 12-well plates. A total of 0.02 µg of minigene expression vector and 0.48 µg of EGFP-fused protein expression vector or 30 pmol siRNAs (the sequences of siRNAs are listed in Supplementary Table S1) or an antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000. Cells were harvested 48 h after transfection for EGFP-fused protein overexpression experiments and 72 h after transfection for siRNA- or ASO-treated samples. THP-1, HMC3, and HeLa cells were seeded on 12-well plates and transfected with 25 pmol ASO or 30 pmol siRNA using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were collected 72 h after transfection. Total RNA was extracted using the NucleoSpin RNA kit (MACHEREY–NAGEL GmbH, Düren, Germany) or the RNA Basic Kit (NIPPON GENETICS, Tokyo, Japan), followed by DNase treatment using rDNase (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan). The cDNA derived from the human frontal cortex was obtained from a commercial source (Ambion, Austin, TX, USA). The primer sets used in the assay are listed in Supplementary Table S1. PCR products were analyzed using 10% polyacrylamide gels. The original gel images are provided in Supplementary Figure S8.

Splicing analysis using RNA-seq data

RNA-seq data were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus DataSets (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds). Read counts corresponding to each splicing pattern were determined using NCBI BLAST (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) with megablast as the program and SRA as the database. The following junctional sequences were used as queries: Human canonical form (exon 31 inclusion): GAAGTCCCTGGACCCCATGG, Human exon 31 skipping form: GAAGTCCCTGACGCCAGAGA, Mouse canonical form (exon 26 (corresponding to human exon 31) inclusion): GAGGAAAGATGCTTGGACCCTATGGTTTAT, Mouse exon 26 skipping form: GAGGAAAGATGCTTGACTCCAGAGAAGGAG. The SRA accession numbers used in this analysis are listed in Supplementary Table S3. The number of sequences matching each query was regarded as the read count for that corresponding splicing pattern. For each experiment, the proportion of each splicing pattern was calculated as the read count of that pattern divided by the total read count of all patterns, multiplied by 100. Samples with fewer than 10 total reads were excluded from the analysis. Finally, the average and standard deviation of the proportion of each splicing pattern were calculated for each cell type or condition.

RNA pull-down and LC–MS/MS analysis

Biotinylated RNAs corresponding to the PTK2B gene exon 31 containing either the adenine or cytosine allele of the rs751019 SNP (PTK2B-rs751019A-bio and PTK2B-rs751019C-bio, respectively) were synthesized by Ajinomoto Biopharma (Osaka, Japan). Each RNA was immobilized on Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin T1 (Invitrogen).

For the RNA pull-down, nuclear extracts were prepared from HEK293 cells cultured in two 10-cm dishes using the NE-PER Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and incubated with the RNA-conjugated beads. Prior to incubation, the nuclear extracts were pre-cleared by mixing with Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin T1 at 4 °C for 30 min with gentle rotation. Proteins bound to the RNA-conjugated beads were eluted by treatment with RNase, followed by boiling in RIPA buffer. SDS sample buffer was added to each eluate, and the samples were denatured by heating at 98 °C. Denatured proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE using 5–20% e-PAGEL gels (ATTO, Tokyo, Japan) and stained with CBB. Protein bands were excised from the gel and de-stained. The gel pieces were then reduced with 25 mM dithiothreitol and alkylated with 1% (w/v) iodoacetamide in 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate. In-gel digestion was carried out overnight at 37 °C in a digestion buffer containing 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate, 0.01% (w/v) ProteaseMax surfactant (Promega, Madison WI, USA), 1.33 μg/mL trypsin (Promega), and 1.33 μg/mL Lysyl Endopeptidase (Wako). Supernatants were collected, and additional peptides were extracted from the gel pieces using 2.5% trifluoroacetic acid with shaking for 15 min. Following digestion, the extracted peptides were desalted and concentrated using StageTips (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The peptides were analyzed using a quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Q Exactive; Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to an Easy-nLC 1000 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with an analytical column (C18, 0.1 × 125 mm; Nikkyo Technos, Tokyo, Japan). Liquid chromatography was performed using mobile phases A (0.1% formic acid in water) and B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile) under the following gradient: 0% B (0 min) – 30% B (50 min) – 100% B (51 min) – 100% B (60 min).

Raw LC–MS/MS data were processed using Proteome Discoverer version 2.4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). MS/MS spectra were searched using the Sequest HT search engine against the UniProt human protein database (release 2022_05) with a target-decoy strategy. Trypsin was specified as the proteolytic enzyme with up to two missed cleavages. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, and oxidation of methionine was set as a variable modification. The precursor ion mass tolerance was set to 10 ppm, and the fragment ion mass tolerance was set to 0.6 Da. Peptide-spectrum matches (PSMs), peptides, and proteins were filtered to a false discovery rate (FDR) of 1%. Label-free quantification (LFQ) was performed using the Minora Feature Detector with normalization based on the total peptide amount. Protein abundances were calculated from MS1 precursor ion feature intensities obtained by the Minora Feature Detector. Match-between-runs was enabled to transfer peptide identifications across LC–MS/MS runs based on accurate mass and retention time. Runs were aligned using nonlinear retention time alignment with a maximum RT shift of 10 min and a precursor mass tolerance of 10 ppm, enabling feature mapping across runs for LFQ. Each sample was analyzed in two technical replicates by LC–MS/MS.

Quantitative PCR

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was conducted following the MIQE guidelines. RNA quantity and purity (A260/A280 and A260/A230 ratios) were evaluated using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA was synthesized from 0.5 μg of total RNA. Primer sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1, and amplicon sizes ranged from 90 to 150 bp. Each qPCR reaction (20 μL total volume) consisted of 10 μL SYBR Green Master Mix (TOYOBO), 0.2 μL of each primer (50 μM), 2 μL of cDNA, and 7.6 μL of nuclease-free water. Amplification was carried out in LightCycler 8-Tube Strips (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) using a LightCycler 96 real-time PCR system (Roche). β-actin (ACTB) or GAPDH was used as the endogenous control, and gene expression levels were quantified using the standard curve methods. No-template controls were included to verify the absence of contamination and genomic DNA.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)

RIP was performed according to previously described methods [54]. To detect PTK2B RNA, RT-PCR was carried out using PTK2B-int30-Fw and PTK2B-ex31-Rv.

RNA pull-down assay

The RNA pull-down assay was performed based on a previously described method [54], with slight modifications. For the pull-down, Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin T1 (Invitrogen) conjugated with biotinylated PTK2B-rs751019A-bio or PTK2B-rs751019C-bio RNA was used. To prepare the cell lysates, HEK293 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and transfected with 1.0 µg of each EGFP-tagged PCBP expression construct using Lipofectamine 2000. After 48 h, cells from two wells were collected and lysed as described previously. The resulting lysates were subjected to the pull-down assay using the RNA-conjugated Dynabeads.

Purification of recombinant protein expressed in Escherichia coli

Purification of recombinant PCBP1 protein

pGEX-6P-2(TH)-NC and pGEX-6P-2(TH)-PCBP1 were transformed into BL21 (DE3) Competent cells and applied to LB agar medium containing ampicillin (100 µg/mL). The resulting colonies were inoculated into LB medium containing ampicillin and incubated overnight at 37 °C with shaking as overnight culture. The next day, 5.0 mL of the overnight culture was added to 200 mL of LB medium containing ampicillin and incubated at 37 °C for 1.5 h with shaking. Subsequently, 100 μL of 1 M IPTG was added and incubated at 24 °C for 5.5 h with shaking. E. coli cells were collected by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 20 min, then resuspended in 5 mL of binding buffer [50 mM NaCl, 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 8.0), 1% Triton X-100, 5 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, Protease Inhibitor EDTA(-) (Protease Inhibitor Cocktail Set VII DMSO Solution, Wako)], and were lysed by pulsed sonication (25% amplitude, 10 s ON/30 s OFF, 10 cycles on ice). All subsequent steps were performed on ice. After sonication, the lysate was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min and the supernatant was collected. A portion of the supernatant was saved as input. The remaining supernatant was mixed with 200 μL or 75 μL of Glutathione Sepharose beads (GE Healthcare Bio-science, now Cytiva) and rotated at 4 °C for 2 h. The beads were washed four times with 1 mL of wash buffer and eluted three times with 200 μL or 100 μL elution buffer [50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 8.2), 40 mM reduced glutathione (Wako), 180 mM NaCl]. After elution, the E1 and E2 fractions or E1 to E4 fractions were pooled and concentrated to a final volume of 100 μL using Amicon® Ultra Centrifugal Filter,10 kDa (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Subsequently, 300 μL of exchange buffer [50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 50 mM NaCl] was added, and buffer exchange was performed by concentrating the sample again to 100 μL using the same kit. After buffer exchange, SDS sample buffer was added and incubated at 95 °C for 5 min to prepare SDS-PAGE samples. These samples were electrophoresed on a 10% polyacrylamide gel and stained with CBB to confirm the presence of the purified proteins.

Purification of recombinant tau protein

pGEX-6P-2(TH)-MAPT was transformed into DynaCompetent Cells BL21 (DE3) pLysS (BioDynamics Laboratory, Tokyo, Japan). The transformed cells were cultured in LB medium containing ampicillin, and protein expression was induced with IPTG. After induction, cells were harvested and resuspended in 5 mL of binding buffer for His-tag purification [400 mM NaCl, 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), 1% Triton X-100, 5 mM imidazole, 5 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, EDTA-free protease inhibitor]. Cell lysis and recovery of the supernatant were performed as described above. A portion of the supernatant was saved as input, and the remaining supernatant was mixed with 200 μL of Talon Metal Affinity Resin (TaKaRa) and rotated at 4 °C for 2 h. The resin was washed five times with 1 mL of wash buffer [400 mM NaCl, 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), 1% Triton X-100, 5 mM imidazole], followed by elution with 250 μL of elution buffer [PBS, 400 mM imidazole, 0.005% Triton X-100]. The elution step was repeated a total of four times. All eluted fractions were pooled and subjected to further purification using 200 μL of glutathione Sepharose beads. Subsequent procedures were performed according to the GST-tagged protein purification protocol described above, and proteins were eluted using 200 μL of elution buffer.

Competitive inhibition assay

Purified PCBP1 protein was dissolved in RIPA buffer, and an equal volume of binding buffer [10 mM Tris, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 0.5% Triton X-100, 1 U/µL RNase inhibitor (Ambion), and protease inhibitor (cOmplete, Roche)] was added. The mixture was pre-cleared by incubation with unconjugated Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin T1 (Invitrogen) to reduce non-specific protein binding. Subsequently, the pre-cleared PCBP1 solution (corresponding to 300 ng of protein) was incubated with Dynabeads conjugated to biotinylated PTK2B-rs751019A-bio or PTK2B-rs751019C-bio RNA for 30 min at room temperature. After binding, non-biotinylated PTK2B-rs751019A or PTK2B-rs751019C RNAs were added at molar ratios equivalent to 0 × (none), 1 × , 5 × , or 10 × the amount of biotinylated RNA, followed by rotation overnight at 4 °C. The beads were then washed five times with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100, and bound proteins were eluted by adding 60 µL of 1 × SDS sample buffer, followed by heating at 95 °C for 5 min.

Drug administration

The cells were treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (Sigma) at a final concentration of 10 µM for 12 h. Cells were treated with bortezomib (Tokyo Chemical Industry, Tokyo, Japan) at a final concentration of 50 nM for 7 h and with epoxomicin (Wako) at a final concentration of 1 µM for 8 h. Autophagy inhibitor bafilomycin A1 (Merck, Rahway, NJ, USA) was applied at a final concentration of 100 nM for 3 h. The translation inhibitor cycloheximide (CHX; Wako) was applied at a concentration of 100 µg/mL for 4, 8, or 12 h. Calcium ion influx was induced using ionomycin (AdipoGen Life Sciences, San Diego, CA, USA) at a concentration of 1 μM for 90 s. The PYK2 inhibitor PF-431396 (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) was used at a final concentration of 1 µM for 24 h. Dimethyl sulfoxide (CultureSure DMSO; Wako) was used as the vehicle control.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence was performed according to methods described in our previous study [55]. Briefly, HEK293, N2a, HepG2 and HMC3 cells were seeded in an eight-well chamber slide (WATSON, Tokyo, Japan). Each PTK2B construct (0.12 μg) was transfected into HEK293 and N2a cells using Lipofectamine 2000, and into HepG2 cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Differentiation of N2a cells into neuron-like cells was induced with all-trans-retinoic acid (Wako) at 20 µM for 48 h, immediately prior to transfection. The cells were fixed with 4% Paraformaldehyde phosphate buffer (Wako) for 10 min and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min. SDS-PAGE and western blotting

SDS-PAGE and western blotting were performed as previously described [55]. The antibodies used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S2 and the corresponding gel images are shown in Supplementary Figure S8.

CaM pull-down assay

The CaM pull-down assay was performed as previously described [48], with minor modifications. Calmodulin-Agarose (Sigma-Aldrich) was equilibrated at room temperature for 1 h with gentle mixing, and 30 µL of the bead suspension was aliquoted into 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes. The beads were washed with lysis buffer [50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 100 mM NaCl, 2 mM CaCl₂, Protease Inhibitor EDTA(-) (Wako), PhosSTOP (Roche)]. Equal amounts of protein lysates (500 µg) derived from cells transfected with GFP-KFL constructs were added to the tubes containing CaM beads. The mixtures were gently agitated and incubated at 4 °C for 2.5 h. After incubation, the beads were collected by centrifugation at 5,000 × g for 1 min and washed three times with the lysis buffer. Bound proteins were then eluted by adding 50 µL of the lysis buffer supplemented with 10 µL of 100 mg/mL SDS (Wako) and gently agitating the samples at room temperature for 30 min. In parallel, aliquots of the transfected protein lysates were retained as input samples.

Pull-down assay with tandem ubiquitin binding entity (TUBE)

TUBE assays were performed as previously described [56], except that HEK293 cells transfected with three different PTK2B constructs were used. Cells were treated with 10 µM MG132 for 12 h prior to lysis.

Immunoprecipitation

Cells were lysed in the lysis buffer described in CaM pull-down assay. Equal amounts of protein lysates (500 µg) were used for each condition. For the PTK2B-dE31 construct, 750 µg of protein lysate was used to equalize the amount of immunoprecipitated 3 × FLAG-fused proteins. The lysates were incubated with 15 µL of anti-FLAG M2 affinity gel (Sigma) to perform immunoprecipitation. Normal mouse IgG-AC (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA) was used as a negative control. After incubation, the beads were washed three times with the lysis buffer. Following removal of the supernatant, the bound proteins were eluted with 30 µL of 1 M arginine-HCl (pH 3.5) by incubation on ice for 5 min. In parallel, aliquots of the transfected protein lysates were collected as input samples.

In vitro kinase assay

In vitro phosphorylation assays were performed using tau protein and FLAG-tagged PYK2. FLAG-tagged PYK2 was expressed in HEK293 cells and immunoprecipitated using anti-FLAG M2 affinity gel according to the immunoprecipitation protocols described above. Based on the molar ratio used in previous studies, 50 ng of immunoprecipitated PYK2 and 120 ng of recombinant tau were used for each reaction [41]. Kinase reactions were carried out in kinase incubation buffer containing 60 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 10 mM MgCl₂, 5.0 mM MnCl₂, 1.25 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), and 2.5 mM ATP. To assess whether tau phosphorylation was specifically mediated by PYK2, reactions were performed in the presence or absence of a PYK2 inhibitor (100 μM). All reactions were incubated at 30 °C for 1 h and terminated by the addition of SDS sample buffer. Reaction products were separated by SDS-PAGE and analyzed by western blotting using anti-phosphotyrosine antibodies to detect tyrosine phosphorylation of tau.

Wound-healing assay

For the wound-healing assay, cells were cultured in 12-well plates and scratched with a sterile pipette tip two days after transfection to create a wound in the cell monolayer. After removing debris by washing, the cells were further incubated in fresh medium containing 1% FBS for 24 h. The FBS concentration was set at 1% to reduce the contribution of cell proliferation in the wound healing assay. Cell migration was assessed by capturing two images per well. In each image, wound width was measured at five evenly spaced positions from the upper to the lower edge of the wound, yielding a total of ten measurements per well. During measurement, isolated protruding cells were excluded, and the distance between opposing cell fronts formed by contiguous cell layers was measured linearly in the horizontal direction. All images were blinded prior to measurement to minimize observer bias. The average of ten measurements per well was used as the representative value, and the wound healing distance was calculated by subtracting the representative value at 24 h from that at 0 h. Wound-healing distance values obtained from cells prepared on separate days were treated as independent biological replicates.

Quantification of multinucleated cells

Cells were stained with Phalloidin-iFluor 594 and Hoechst 33,342 as described above. For each sample, 2–3 fields of view were imaged, and all cells within each field in which nuclei and the cytoskeleton were clearly identifiable were counted (a total of 89–188 cells per sample). To minimize observer bias, all images were blinded prior to quantification. Cells containing two or more nuclei were defined as multinucleated cells, and the percentage of multinucleated cells was calculated relative to the total number of counted cells. Calculated data obtained from cells prepared on separate days were treated as independent biological replicates.

Results

Identification of single-nucleotide variants affecting exon 31 splicing

We first examined whether exon 31 of the PTK2B gene undergoes alternative splicing. RT-PCR using cDNA from multiple PTK2B-expressing cell lines and human cerebral cortex revealed transcripts lacking exon 31 in THP-1 cells, HMC3 cells, and human cerebral cortex (Fig. 1A). In addition, analysis of RNA-seq data from various cell lines derived from different tissues suggested that exon 31 skipping was particularly pronounced in bone and brain cancer–derived cells (Fig. S1A). Although a previous TWAS indicated a potential association between exon 31 splicing and AD risk, details in patient brains remained unclear. Analysis of RNA-seq data from microglia (GSE146639) isolated from healthy controls (CTR), non-demented individuals with Aβ and/or tau pathology (CTR +), and AD patients suggested that exon 31 skipping increases with AD progression. Consistently, beta-binomial regression analysis revealed a significant stage-dependent decline in exon 31 inclusion (β = − 0.64, OR per one-stage increase = 0.53, p = 0.016; Fig. 1B). In contrast, bulk RNA-seq data from the brains of AD patients and controls (GSE125583) revealed a significant association between disease status and a higher exon inclusion rate in AD (β = 0.84 ± 0.21, z = 3.98, p = 6.8 × 10⁻5; Fig. S1B). These findings suggest that the splicing pattern of exon 31 may change during AD progression, and that this tendency might differ among cell types. Analysis of RNA-seq data from wild-type and 5×FAD mouse brains revealed that skipping of exon 26 (corresponding to human exon 31) was scarcely observed in the cortex or hippocampus at any age (Fig. S1C, D), indicating that this exon is constitutively included in mouse brains. This difference may be attributable to species-specific sequence variations around exon 31 between humans and mice (Fig. S1E).

Next, we investigated the effects of rs2251430G and nearby SNVs on exon 31 splicing. A genomic fragment containing PTK2B exon 31 and its flanking introns was used to construct a reference minigene. Four common variants—rs2251430-G, rs751018-A (near the 5′ splice site), rs751019-C (near the 3′ splice site and within the coding sequence), and rs2237819-T (near the branch point)—were individually introduced into this construct, resulting in five distinct minigene variants (Fig. 1C). These constructs were transfected into HEK293 cells, and exon 31 splicing patterns were analyzed by RT-PCR. The results indicated that rs751018-A and rs751019-C significantly increased exon 31 inclusion (Fig. 1D), whereas rs2251430-G and rs2237819-T showed no detectable effect. Moreover, the minigene carrying both rs751018-A and rs751019-C exhibited a further increase in exon 31 inclusion compared with either variant alone, indicating that rs751018-A and rs751019-C independently and additively promote exon 31 splicing (Fig. 1E, F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that PTK2B exon 31 undergoes alternative splicing and that this process is influenced by two SNVs, particularly rs751019.

To elucidate the reason why rs2251430 was identified as an sQTL in the previous TWAS [44], linkage disequilibrium analysis was performed using LDlink (LDhap and LDpair; https://ldlink.nih.gov/?tab=home) [58]. Because the TWAS was based on genome and transcriptome data from non-Hispanic White populations, we restricted our analysis to European populations. Interestingly, samples carrying rs2251430-A lacked both rs751018-A and rs751019-C, whereas samples carrying rs2251430-G possessed either rs751018-A or rs751019-C (Fig. 1G). Thus, rs2251430-G consistently co-occurs with either rs751018-A or rs751019-C, both of which promote exon 31 inclusion, providing a plausible explanation for the observed association between rs2251430-G and increased exon 31 inclusion. Although we observed an additive effect of rs751018-A and rs751019-C, no samples with both variants were found (Fig. 1H).

Investigation of splicing regulatory factors acting on rs751019

We hypothesized that the marked change in exon 31 splicing caused by a single-nucleotide difference at rs751019 may result from differential binding of RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) to the surrounding sequences. To test this, we synthesized biotin-labeled RNAs from a region of exon 31 containing either rs751019-A (PTK2B-A-bio) or rs751019-C (PTK2B-C-bio) (Fig. 2A) and performed RNA pull-down assays using nuclear extracts from HEK293 cells. Proteins bound to the biotinylated RNAs were eluted by RNase treatment, separated by SDS-PAGE, and visualized by CBB staining. Four bands differing between PTK2B-A-bio and PTK2B-C-bio were observed (Fig. 2B): one protein band bound preferentially to PTK2B-C-bio (Band 1), while three others showed stronger binding to PTK2B-A-bio (Bands 2–4). These protein bands were excised and analyzed by LC–MS/MS (Fig. 2B). The presence of candidate RBPs identified by mass spectrometry was confirmed by immunoblotting with specific antibodies (Fig. 2C). To identify RBPs functionally involved in PTK2B exon 31 splicing, HEK293 cells were co-transfected with siRNAs targeting each candidate RBP and the reference minigene. Among these, knockdown of PCBP1 altered the splicing pattern of exon 31 (Fig. 2D). A similar effect was observed when PCBP1 was silenced in cells expressing the PTK2B-rs751019-C minigene (Fig. 2E). The knockdown efficiency of each siRNA was confirmed by qPCR (Fig. S2A, B). These results led us to focus on the PCBP protein family as potential regulators of PTK2B exon 31 splicing.

Alternative splicing of PTK2B exon 31 is regulated redundantly by PCBP1 and PCBP2

We next examined the role of PCBP proteins in the regulation of PTK2B exon 31 splicing. Constructs expressing EGFP-tagged PCBP1 or its paralog PCBP2 were co-transfected with the reference minigene into HEK293 cells. Overexpression of PCBP1 tended to promote exon 31 inclusion, while PCBP2 significantly enhanced it (Fig. 3A). However, these effects were not observed when co-expressed with the PTK2B-rs751019-C minigene (Fig. 3A). We then investigated the effects of PCBP1 and PCBP2 knockdown. Co-transfection of siRNAs targeting PCBP1 or PCBP2 with the reference minigene significantly reduced exon 31 inclusion, and simultaneous knockdown of both genes further enhanced this effect (Fig. 3B). The efficiency of siRNA-mediated knockdown was confirmed by qPCR (Fig. S3A, B). When using the PTK2B-rs751019-C minigene, knockdown of PCBP1 alone or double knockdown of PCBP1 and PCBP2 significantly decreased exon 31 inclusion, whereas PCBP2 knockdown alone showed a reduction trend without reaching statistical significance (Fig. 3B). In THP-1 cells, which harbor the rs751019-A allele as confirmed by Sanger sequencing, knockdown of PCBP1 or PCBP2 alone did not significantly alter exon 31 inclusion, although a decreasing trend was observed. Double knockdown, however, significantly reduced exon 31 inclusion (Fig. 3C). Together, these results indicate that PTK2B exon 31 splicing is redundantly regulated by PCBP1 and PCBP2.

To test whether PCBP proteins directly interact with PTK2B pre-mRNA, we performed RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays. HEK293 cells were co-transfected with EGFP, EGFP-PCBP1, or EGFP-PCBP2 together with either the reference or PTK2B-rs751019-C minigene. After immunoprecipitation with anti-GFP antibodies, RT-PCR using primers targeting intron 30 and exon 31 of PTK2B revealed that both PCBP1 and PCBP2 bound PTK2B pre-mRNA regardless of the rs751019 allele (Fig. 3D, S3C). Next, we compared the binding affinity of PCBP1 to rs751019 alleles using an RNA pull-down assay. PCBP1 showed stronger binding to the rs751019-C sequence than to the rs751019-A sequence (Fig. S3D). This is consistent with the above finding that higher amounts of PCBP1/2 were captured by C-bio RNA (Fig. 2C), despite their initial identification in the A-bio–preferential band 3 (Fig. 2B). To validate this, a competitive inhibition assay was performed using recombinant PCBP1 protein. Binding of recombinant PCBP1 to PTK2B-A-bio or PTK2B-C-bio was competitively inhibited by the addition of non-biotinylated RNA. The rs751019-C RNA exhibited stronger competitive inhibition than rs751019-A, confirming that PCBP1 has a higher affinity for the rs751019-C sequence (Fig. 3E, F).

Effect of exon 31 and its polymorphisms on PYK2 protein

To assess the effects of exon 31 polymorphisms and exon skipping on PTK2B (PYK2) protein, we focused on rs751019, which is located within exon 31 and its polymorphism (A or C) determines whether the amino acid at position 838 is lysine (K838) or threonine (T838). Expression constructs encoding PTK2B with rs751019-A (PTK2B-A), rs751019-C (PTK2B-C), or lacking exon 31 (PTK2B-dE31) were generated and transfected into HEK293 cells (Fig. 4A). Western blot analysis revealed that PTK2B-dE31 exhibited markedly reduced protein expression compared with the other isoforms (Fig. 4B). Quantitative PCR confirmed that mRNA levels were comparable among all constructs (Fig. 4C), suggesting that the reduction in PTK2B-dE31 was post-transcriptional.

To determine whether this reduction was due to proteasomal degradation, cells were treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132. PYK2 protein levels increased in all isoforms following MG132 treatment, with the most pronounced effect observed in PTK2B-dE31 (Fig. 4D). Similar results were obtained with bortezomib and epoxomicin (Fig. S4A, B). These findings suggest that exon 31 deletion accelerates proteasome-mediated degradation of PYK2. To examine whether this degradation was associated with enhanced polyubiquitination, we performed pull-down assays using tandem ubiquitin-binding entities (TUBE) fused to GST [59], which efficiently enriches polyubiquitinated proteins in the presence of the deubiquitinase inhibitor PR-619. All PYK2 isoforms were detected in the polyubiquitinated protein fractions, but PTK2B-dE31 displayed the strongest signal with a ladder pattern (Fig. 4E), indicating increased ubiquitination upon exon 31 loss. In contrast, treatment with the autophagy inhibitor bafilomycin A1 did not restore protein expression (Fig. S4C). Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays further confirmed accelerated degradation of PTK2B-dE31 compared with PTK2B-A and PTK2B-C, both of which showed gradual and time-dependent decreases (Fig. 4F). These results collectively suggest that PYK2 undergoes proteasome-mediated degradation irrespective of isoform, but the loss of exon 31 markedly enhances this susceptibility. We next analyzed the subcellular localization of PTK2B isoforms. HEK293, HepG2, and Neuro2A cells were transfected with the three constructs, and few differences in localization were observed among the isoforms in any cell type (Fig. 4G, S4D, E). Because Neuro2A cells can differentiate into neuron-like cells upon retinoic acid (RA) treatment [60], we also examined localization after RA-induced differentiation and following treatment with the calcium ionophore ionomycin. In both conditions, PYK2 localization remained unchanged (Fig. S4F).

Effects of exon 31 and its polymorphisms on PYK2 kinase activity

We next examined the impact of exon 31 and its polymorphisms on PYK2 kinase activity. HEK293 cells expressing the PTK2B constructs were analyzed for the ratio of phosphorylated (active) PYK2 to total PYK2. PTK2B-dE31 showed a significant decrease in the phosphorylated-to-total PYK2 ratio compared with PTK2B-A and PTK2B-C (Fig. 5A). Upon MG132 treatment, both PTK2B-A and PTK2B-C exhibited significant increases in the proportion of phosphorylated PYK2 in parallel with total protein restoration, whereas PTK2B-dE31 showed no change in its proportion (Fig. 5B). Similar results were observed following treatment with bortezomib or epoxomicin (Fig. S5A, B).

Because PYK2 phosphorylates GSK3β at Y216 to activate it [40], we evaluated the phosphorylation status of endogenous GSK3β in HEK293 cells expressing each PTK2B constructs. Expression of PTK2B-A or PTK2B-C significantly increased the ratio of phosphorylated GSK3β-to-total GSK3β (p-GSK3β/GSK3β), whereas PTK2B-dE31 had no effect (Fig. 5C).

Furthermore, to evaluate the kinase activity of PYK2 under conditions independent of the intracellular environment, we performed an in vitro kinase assay using recombinant tau protein as a known substrate [41]. PYK2 proteins were obtained by immunoprecipitation from HEK293 cells expressing PTK2B constructs fused at the C-terminus with 3 × FLAG (PTK2B-A/C/dE31–3 × FLAG), and bacterially expressed recombinant tau protein was used as the substrate. As a result, tyrosine phosphorylation of tau was detected in reactions containing PTK2B-A or PTK2B-C, whereas phosphorylation remained at a relatively low level in reactions containing PTK2B-dE31 (Fig. 5D). Notably, this phosphorylation signal was not detected in the absence of ATP (Fig. S5C). In addition, the signal was attenuated by treatment with the PYK2 inhibitor PF-431396, suggesting that the observed tyrosine phosphorylation of tau is dependent on PYK2 kinase activity (Fig. S5D). Together, these results indicate that exon 31 is essential for maintaining PYK2 kinase activity and the phosphorylation status of its downstream molecules.

Analysis of the mechanism of PYK2 activation mediated by exon 31

We next sought to elucidate the mechanism by which exon 31 influences PYK2 kinase activity. To examine isoform-dependent differences in Ca2⁺ responsiveness, each PTK2B construct was expressed in Neuro2A cells, followed by ionomycin treatment. The proportion of phosphorylated PYK2 significantly increased in PTK2B-A– and PTK2B-C-expressing cells after ionomycin stimulation, whereas no significant increase was observed in PTK2B-dE31 (Fig. 6A, B). Ca2⁺ sensing by PYK2 is at least partially mediated through calmodulin (CaM) [48]. During activation, PYK2 undergoes self-dimerization, and one of the essential CaM-binding regions (KFL728–839) partially overlaps with exon 31 (which encodes amino acids 830–841) [48]. To test whether this region contributes to CaM binding, we generated deletion constructs encompassing the kinase–FAT linker (KFL) region containing rs751019-A (KFL-A), rs751019-C (KFL-C), or lacking exon 31 (KFL-dE31) (Fig. 6C). When these constructs were expressed in HEK293 cells and subjected to pull-down assays using CaM-conjugated beads, no significant differences in CaM-binding affinity were detected among the isoforms (Fig. 6D).

Next, we investigated whether structural changes caused by exon 31 deletion might affect self-dimerization. Structural modeling using AlphaFold3 revealed that although PTK2B-A and PTK2B-C showed no major structural changes, differences were observed in the α-helical arrangement within the KFL region. Notably, PTK2B-dE31 displayed a shortened interdomain distance compared with the other isoforms (Fig. 6E). In predicted dimer models, PTK2B-C showed a slightly reduced interdomain distance relative to PTK2B-A, whereas PTK2B-dE31 exhibited a more expanded conformation (Fig. 6F). To experimentally verify the dimerization capacity of each isoform, we generated constructs expressing PTK2B fused at the C-terminus with either V5 or 3 × FLAG tags (PTK2B-A/C/dE31–V5 and PTK2B-A/C/dE31–3 × FLAG) and co-expressed them in HEK293 cells for co-immunoprecipitation (Fig. 6G). Prior to this, we confirmed that the addition of epitope tags did not affect protein expression or activation (Fig. S6A, B). Co-immunoprecipitation experiments revealed no marked difference in dimerization capacity among the isoforms (Fig. 6H). Thus, defective autophosphorylation in the absence of exon 31 was not attributed to an inability to bind CaM or to self-dimerize.

Functional regulation of PYK2 by an exon 31 skipping antisense oligonucleotide (ASO)

Thus far, we have demonstrated the impact of exon 31 on PYK2 protein function through overexpression studies. We next examined the functional relevance of exon 31 in the context of endogenous PYK2. To develop a tool capable of inducing exon 31 skipping, we utilized dCasRx to identify potential splicing regulatory regions on PTK2B pre-mRNA. dCasRx is a catalytically inactive mutant of CasRx that binds RNA in a guide RNA (gRNA)–dependent manner, thereby interfering with RBPs [49]. We established a HEK293 cell line expressing dCasRx under doxycycline (dox) addition (HEK293-dCasRx) and co-transfected it with a reference minigene and seven gRNAs targeting exon 31 or its flanking regions (Fig. 7A, B). Among them, gRx4 and gRx5 significantly promoted exon 31 skipping. Based on these findings, we designed an antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) corresponding to the gRx4 target region and introduced it into HEK293 cells together with the reference minigene or PTK2B-rs751019-C minigene. ASO treatment effectively induced exon 31 skipping for both rs751019 allelic variants (Fig. 7C, D). Furthermore, when the ASO was applied to THP-1 cells, it also successfully induced exon 31 skipping in endogenous PTK2B transcripts (Fig. 7E). In THP-1 cells treated with ASO, protein levels of both total and phosphorylated PYK2 (p-PYK2), as well as the phosphorylated GSK3β ratio (p-GSK3β/GSK3β), were decreased (Fig. 7F–I). In contrast, PTK2B mRNA levels increased following ASO treatment (Fig. S7A).

Similar results were observed in THP-1 cells (Fig. 8C). Recently, the PYK2 inhibitor PF-431396 was reported to induce multinucleation of microglia and exert protective effects against Aβ toxicity [61]. However, PF-431396 also inhibits FAK (Focal adhesion kinase), a member of the same kinase family as PYK2, making it unclear which target is responsible for this phenomenon. Therefore, we examined the effects of PYK2 and its exon 31 on microglial multinucleation. First, we examined whether PF-431396 induces multinucleation of HMC3 cells as previously reported. Consistent with prior observations, PF-431396 induced multinucleation in HMC3 cells (Fig. 8D). To determine whether this phenotype was specific to PYK2 inhibition, we performed siRNA-mediated knockdown of PTK2B or FAK in HMC3 cells. Knockdown of either PTK2B or FAK induced multinucleation, but the effect was significantly stronger with PTK2B knockdown (Fig. 8E). The efficiency of siRNA-mediated knockdown was confirmed by qPCR (Fig. S7B). Similarly, ASO treatment also induced multinucleation in HMC3 cells (Fig. 8F). Collectively, these findings suggest that exon 31 loss leads to reduced endogenous PYK2 function, which may in turn alter microglial cellular phenotypes such as multinucleation.

Discussion

In this study, we newly identified rs751018 and rs751019, rather than rs2251430, as SNVs that affect the splicing pattern of PTK2B exon 31. This finding suggests that rs2251430 detected in previous TWAS studies may serve merely as a marker, whereas rs751018 and rs751019 are likely the true causal variants. Notably, rs751019 has been reported to be associated with the risk of hypertension in Japanese males, with those carrying the AC or CC genotypes showing higher diastolic blood pressure compared to individuals with the AA genotype [62]. Considering that PYK2 is involved in hypertension-related pathways [63], it is intriguing to speculate that exon 31 inclusion driven by the rs751019-C allele may be involved in the development of hypertension. Furthermore, exon 31 skipping was not observed in mice, and the sequence conservation around the splice sites was lower in more evolutionarily distant species. These findings suggest that evolutionary sequence divergence may have contributed to the acquisition of exon 31 splicing regulation. It is also likely that previous studies on PTK2B in mice did not adequately account for the impact of exon 31 splicing.

Among RBPs predicted to bind near rs751019, PCBP1 and PCBP2 were found to redundantly regulate exon 31 splicing. The region containing rs751019 is thought to function as an exonic splicing enhancer, and PCBP1/2 act as trans-acting factors that promote exon 31 inclusion through this element. Notably, a competitive binding assay revealed that PCBP1 exhibits higher binding affinity to the rs751019-C sequence, consistent with the increased exon 31 inclusion observed for this allele. Because our proteomic identification of RBPs was not comprehensive, other RBPs may also bind to exon 31 in addition to PCBP1/2. Indeed, the magnitude of splicing changes induced by PCBP1/2 did not fully correspond to the difference observed between rs751019 alleles, suggesting that additional splicing regulatory factors are likely involved. Both PCBP1 and PCBP2 are known as iron chaperones in addition to RBPs [64]. Although the relationship between PCBP1 and AD remains unclear, increased PCBP2 expression has been reported in the brains of AD patients and mouse models [65, 66] and pharmacological reduction of PCBP2 improved AD-like phenotypes in a mouse model [66].

We also found that the absence of exon 31 leads to alterations in several molecular properties of PYK2. PYK2 lacking exon 31 showed enhanced proteasome-dependent degradation, likely due to its increased susceptibility to ubiquitination. PYK2 activity is known to be regulated by ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation mediated through interactions with SOCS2, Pin1, and the ubiquitin ligase CBL [67–70]. Additionally, T6BP recruits CBL to promote PYK2 degradation and suppresses PYK2 dimerization and activation by binding to its N-terminal region, functioning as a potent endogenous inhibitor [71]. Importantly, the decrease in phospho-Y402 levels in dE31 was not solely due to proteasomal degradation, as proteasome inhibition restored dE31 protein levels but not phospho-Y402 levels.

In general, PYK2 activation is initiated by CaM binding, which promotes dimer formation and autophosphorylation at Y402 [10]. This phosphorylation recruits Src family kinases, leading to subsequent phosphorylation at Y579, Y580, and Y881 within the FAT domain and enhanced PYK2 catalytic activity [10]. Our in vitro kinase assay revealed that exon 31 is essential for kinase activity, whereas PTK2B-A and PTK2B-C showed comparable activity. Interestingly, no marked differences were observed among the isoforms in terms of CaM-binding affinity or dimerization capacity. Taken together, these observations suggest that deletion of exon 31 does not impair CaM binding or self-dimerization per se, but may disrupt subsequent activation processes, particularly autophosphorylation and downstream tyrosine phosphorylation beyond Y402, thereby reducing kinase activity. Furthermore, AlphaFold3 predictions suggested that exon 31 deletion induces conformational changes in PYK2, potentially leading to enhanced degradation or reduced activity through altered interactions with binding partners.

Furthermore, we developed an ASO that induces exon 31 skipping, leading to reduced total and phosphorylated PYK2 levels and altered cellular phenotypes. In wound-healing assays, ASO treatment resulted in a reduction of wound closure in both HeLa and THP-1 cells. These findings suggest that exon 31 skipping may be associated with altered cell migration; however, contributions from cell proliferation, a known limitation of the wound-healing assay, cannot be fully excluded. Therefore, the relative contributions of cell migration and proliferation to the observed phenotype remain unclear in this study. A previous study reported that treatment with the PYK2 inhibitor PF-431396 induces multinucleation in microglia, enhances their survival, motility, and phagocytosis, and exerts neuroprotective effects against Aβ toxicity [61]. However, PF-431396 also inhibits FAK, a paralog sharing 65% sequence similarity with PYK2, at even lower concentrations than it inhibits PYK2 [61, 72–74], leaving the responsible target unclear. In our experiments, siRNA-mediated knockdown of PTK2B or FAK induced multinucleation, with a more pronounced effect observed following PTK2B knockdown. Similarly, induction of exon 31 skipping also led to multinucleation. These results suggest that multinucleation involves both PYK2 and FAK and can be induced by exon 31 skipping even without PTK2B knockdown. Thus, exon 31 skipping may influence cellular phenotypes, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. For example, aberrant γ-tubulin cleavage has been proposed to contribute to multinucleation induced by PYK2 inhibitors [61], but whether the multinucleation observed after PTK2B knockdown or exon 31 skipping involves the same mechanism remains unknown.

This study is based on cultured cells and in vitro experiments, and the in vivo function of exon 31 skipping remains to be elucidated. As our results may reflect cell-type-specific effects, our findings should be validated in disease-relevant cell types. Future work should explore the physiological role of exon 31 regulated splicing in AD and other diseases. Given that our RNA-seq analysis suggested increased exon 31 skipping in microglia during AD progression, determining whether this altered splicing affects microglial function and contributes to AD pathogenesis will be of considerable interest. The molecular mechanism by which exon 31 deletion suppresses PYK2 autophosphorylation also remains unclear, since neither CaM binding nor dimerization was affected. It is likely that alternative mechanisms are involved, and elucidating these mechanisms could facilitate the development of novel PYK2-specific inhibitory strategies.

Conclusions

We experimentally validated two SNVs, rs751019 and rs751018, as functional sQTLs that account for the observed association between rs2251430 and exon 31 splicing, a process that was found to be regulated by PCBP1/2. Our findings also provide a novel mechanism in which exon 31 deletion impairs PYK2 function and modulates cellular activity, and highlight splicing regulation as a promising therapeutic target for PYK2-related diseases.
