Development and Psychometric Validation of the Japanese Version of the Everyday Ageism Scale (EAS‐J) Among Older Adults
Authors: Kenjiro Kawaguchi, Chie Koga, Ayae Yamada, Isaku Kurotori, Hiroki Takeuchi, Lingling, Atsushi Nakagomi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimJapan lacks a validated tool to measure everyday ageism, leaving its prevalence and health impact unclear. This study aimed to develop and validate the Japanese version of the Everyday Ageism Scale (EAS‐J) in older adults.MethodsWe used a mixed‐methods design comprising forward and back translation, cognitive interviews, and a nationwide cross‐sectional online survey of 1500 adults ages 60–79 years. In September 2025, participants were recruited from an online panel using quota sampling to reflect national demographic distributions. We evaluated psychometric properties using confirmatory factor analysis, reliability coefficients, and validity indices. A Multiple Indicators Multiple Causes model was used to assess associations with loneliness, stress, well‐being, self‐esteem, and happiness.ResultsThe EAS‐J demonstrated a three‐factor structure—exposure to ageist messages, ageism in interpersonal interactions, and internalized ageism. Model fit was good on three of the four indices (comparative fit index = 0.971, Tucker–Lewis Index = 0.959, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation = 0.075, Standardized Root Mean Square Residual = 0.037). Internal consistency was good (Cronbach's α = 0.83), test–retest reliability was moderate (Intraclass Correlation Coefficient = 0.66), and convergent and discriminant validity were supported. The scale showed significant associations with perceived stress, loneliness, well‐being, and self‐esteem, supporting nomological validity.ConclusionsThe EAS‐J is a psychometrically supported, multidimensional instrument for measuring everyday ageism among older adults in Japan. Availability of the EAS‐J will facilitate research on the prevalence and health consequences of everyday ageism and inform development of interventions to promote healthy aging.

Introduction

Ageism—stereotyping, prejudice, and discrimination based on age—is a critical global public health challenge and an important social determinant of health [1, 2]. Empirical studies have consistently demonstrated that experiencing ageism is associated with poorer physical and mental health, reduced self‐esteem, and a lower quality of life among older adults [3, 4]. Addressing ageism is especially urgent in rapidly aging nations such as Japan.

Ageism affects people not just through overt discrimination in sectors such as employment and healthcare, but also through more subtle behaviors and attitudes encountered in daily life. Research in the United States indicates that over 90% of older adults report experiencing everyday ageism [5]. Such everyday ageism encompasses exposure to ageist messages, discriminatory interpersonal treatment, and internalization of negative age stereotypes [6], and its frequent occurrence produces cumulative negative effects [5], underscoring the public health importance of understanding everyday ageism.

Several instruments have been developed to measure ageism‐related constructs, each capturing different perspectives and dimensions. Scales such as the Fraboni Scale of Ageism (FSA) [7] and the Ambivalent Ageism Scale (AAS) [8] assess attitudes and prejudice toward older adults from an observer perspective; the AAS notably distinguishes both hostile and benevolent (patronizing) forms of ageism [8]. However, these scales do not capture the lived experiences of ageism targets. From the target perspective, Palmore's Ageism Survey [9] was among the first to document self‐reported experiences of ageism, but it lacks a formal factor structure and comprehensive psychometric evaluation [10]. The Everyday Discrimination Scale [11] measures general discrimination experiences but offers limited specificity for ageism since age is just one of several optional attributions. The Expectations Regarding Aging scale (ERA‐12) [12] was the only instrument meeting minimum COSMIN measurement‐quality standards [10], yet it captures only stereotype‐related expectations, not interpersonal or environmental ageism. Recent systematic reviews confirm that no existing measure achieves comprehensive content validity for everyday ageism [10, 13], highlighting the need for a dedicated tool.

To address this measurement gap, the Everyday Ageism Scale (EAS) was developed in the United States as a brief, multidimensional instrument that captures the complexity of daily ageist experiences from older adults' own perspective [6]. It comprises 10 items across three factors—exposure to ageist messages, ageism in interpersonal interactions, and internalized ageism. The EAS has since been adapted in Korean [14], Chinese [15], and Farsi [16], and applied in Ireland [17], Australia [18], the Philippines [19], and Canada [20]. However, no Japanese adaptation has been developed.

Japan's population is aging at an unprecedented rate. This demographic shift has created intergenerational tension and the potential for unconscious bias against older adults, despite Japan's cultural norm of respecting older adults [21]. Without a validated measure of everyday ageism, its prevalence and health impact in Japan remain largely unknown. This study, therefore, aimed to develop and validate the Japanese version of the EAS (EAS‐J). By examining the psychometric properties—specifically factor structure, internal consistency, and construct validity—we sought to confirm that the EAS‐J is a reliable and valid tool for assessing everyday ageism within the Japanese cultural context.

Methods

Study Design

A mixed‐methods design was used to translate, culturally adapt, and psychometrically validate the EAS‐J. The process involved forward and back translation, team consensus, and cognitive interviews, followed by a psychometric evaluation of the data collected via an online survey. The entire procedure adhered to the International Society of Pharmacoeconomics and Outcome Research guidelines for cross‐cultural adaptation and instrument validation [22].

Everyday Ageism Scale (EAS)

The 10‐item EAS comprises three domains: exposure to ageist messages (two items), ageism in interpersonal interactions (five items), and internalized ageism (three items) [6]. Seven items measure frequency of experiences (often/sometimes/rarely/never, scored 0–3), and three items assess agreement with age‐related beliefs (strongly agree/agree/disagree/strongly disagree, scored 0–3). Subscale and total scores (range 0–30) are computed as sums; higher values indicate greater everyday ageism. The original U.S. validation confirmed a stable three‐factor structure (Cronbach's α = 0.77) [6].

Translation and Cultural Adaptation

Permission to translate and use the EAS was obtained from Dr. Julie Ober Allen. Two bilingual translators independently translated the EAS into Japanese, focusing on conceptual equivalence. The research team compared the forward translations, resolved discrepancies through discussion, and synthesized a single draft. The draft EAS‐J was back‐translated into English by an independent bilingual translator who was unfamiliar with the original EAS. Discrepancies were identified, and each item was assessed for clarity, cultural relevance, and content validity.

Data Collection

Cognitive Interviews

To enhance wording and ensure cultural relevance, cognitive interviews were conducted with six members of the target population. Eligibility criteria included being 60–79 years of age, having no cognitive impairment, having no history of serious illness, being fluent in Japanese, and being able to provide informed consent. Participants were selected through the connections of the research team, ensuring diversity in age, sex, and educational levels. Before conducting the interviews, the research team held a consensus meeting to finalize the interview protocol. Using the think‐aloud method [23] and verbal probing [24], the interviews evaluated how well participants understood and interpreted the items, as well as any potential response issues. After each question, participants were prompted to explain their understanding. If their interpretation did not align with the intended meaning, any differences and suggestions for changes were recorded manually. Table S1 presents the final version of the EAS‐J.

Survey

A cross‐sectional, nationwide online survey was conducted between September 16 and 19, 2025, using the Rakuten Insight panel, one of Japan's largest online research panels. Quota sampling was applied to approximate the national distribution of Japanese adults 60–79 years of age by age decade, sex, and geographic region based on the 2020 Population Census. These variables were selected for quota sampling because they can influence internet access and ageism experiences. Eligible participants were enrolled until the target of 1500 adults 60–79 years of age was reached. As the number of panel members invited was not available from the panel provider, the response rate could not be calculated. A subsample (n = 150) completed the retest between October 16 and 17, 2025, sufficient for ICC calculation [25]. A 4‐week retest interval was selected to balance rehearsal and real‐change effects [26]. A mandatory‐response design ensured no item‐level missing data. Participants received Rakuten points—a loyalty program currency primarily redeemable for purchases at Rakuten Ichiba, Japan's largest online shopping marketplace—as compensation from Rakuten Insight.

Before participation, all respondents provided electronic informed consent after reviewing an information sheet that explained the study purpose, anonymity, and the right to withdraw at any time by closing the browser without penalty. Participation was entirely voluntary, and no personally identifying information was collected. The study protocol adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Chiba University Graduate School of Medicine (Reference No. M10843).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive Statistics

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1) with the lavaan package for structural equation modeling [27]. Score distributions were summarized using means, standard deviations (SDs), and the proportions of respondents scoring at the minimum and maximum values. Floor or ceiling effects exceeding 15% were considered potentially problematic [28].

Reliability

Item‐level performance was evaluated through corrected item–total correlations as indicators of discrimination. Internal consistency reliability was estimated using Cronbach's α [29] and interpreted as follows: ≥ 0.70 acceptable; ≥ 0.80 good [30]. Test–retest reliability was evaluated using a two‐way random‐effects, single‐measure, absolute‐agreement intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC (3, 1)] with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Values were interpreted as follows: > 0.90: excellent; 0.75–0.90: good; 0.50–0.75: moderate; and < 0.50: poor [31].

Factorial Validity

To confirm the three‐factor structure (i.e., exposure to ageist messages, ageism in interpersonal interactions, and internalized ageism), we performed confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using the robust diagonally weighted least squares estimator to account for the ordinal nature of the items. Global model fit was evaluated using the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) with its 90% CIs, and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). A good model fit was defined as CFI and TLI ≥ 0.95, RMSEA < 0.06, and SRMR < 0.08 [32].

Convergent and Discriminant Validity

Convergent validity was examined through Average Variance Extracted (AVE) and Composite Reliability (CR), with AVE ≥ 0.50 and CR ≥ 0.70 indicating satisfactory convergent validity [33]. Discriminant validity was evaluated using two complementary criteria: (1) the Fornell–Larcker criterion, which requires the square root of AVE for each factor to exceed its inter‐factor correlations (|φ|) [33]; and (2) the Heterotrait–Monotrait (HTMT) ratio, with thresholds of < 0.85 (strict) and < 0.90 (lenient) indicating adequate discriminant validity [34].

Nomological Validity

To examine theoretically expected relationships while accounting for the latent structure, a Multiple Indicators Multiple Causes (MIMIC) model [35] was estimated. In this single MIMIC model, the three latent EAS‐J factors were regressed simultaneously on external psychological variables. Loneliness was assessed using the three‐item University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) Loneliness Scale (UCLA‐3; range 3–9) [36, 37]. Perceived stress was measured using the 10‐item Perceived Stress Scale (PSS‐10; range 0–40) [38, 39]. Well‐being was assessed using the World Health Organization Five Well‐Being Index (WHO‐5; range 0–25) [40]. Self‐esteem was measured using the 10‐item Rosenberg Self‐Esteem Scale (RSES; range 10–50) [41, 42]. Happiness was assessed with a single item: “How happy are you currently?” rated from 0 (very unhappy) to 10 (very happy), a format adapted from the Cantril Self‐Anchoring Scale [43]. The multi‐item scales were administered in validated Japanese versions, and the happiness item in Japanese. Higher scores indicate greater loneliness and stress, and better well‐being, self‐esteem, and happiness. Age, gender, and employment status were included as control variables based on prior EAS research demonstrating their associations with everyday ageism [20, 44]. Employment was coded as employed (full‐ or part‐time) versus not employed. Standardized regression coefficients (β) and two‐tailed p values were reported.

Results

Cognitive Interviews

Cognitive interview participants (n = 6; age range, 62–75 years; 3 women; Table S2) found all items relevant to their daily experiences. Several linguistic adaptations ensured natural expression in Japanese. First, Items 3–7 (e.g., “People assume I have trouble using cell phones or computers”) were rephrased into a passive construction (i.e., “being assumed by people around me” rather than “people assume…”), reflecting the relational nature of stereotyping in Japanese. Second, Items 8–10 originally used an agreement–disagreement response format (“Strongly agree” to “Strongly disagree”), which was adapted to an applicability format (“very applicable” to “not at all applicable”), the standard self‐referential format in Japanese psychometric scales. Third, “unattractive” (Item 2) was rendered using two complementary Japanese expressions, one conveying “unattractive” and another conveying “unfavorable,” to capture both aesthetic and social dimensions. Fourth, “is part of getting older” (Items 8–10) was adapted to a phrase meaning “is natural/inevitable,” which aligns with how older Japanese adults typically express beliefs about aging.

Survey Participant Characteristics

In total, 1500 community‐dwelling older adults completed the online survey (Table 1). Women comprised 52.3%, with a mean age of 69.4 years (SD = 5.6); 42.5% held a university degree or higher, and 72.3% were married. The sample was substantially more educated than the general population of Japanese adults 60–79 years of age (Table S4).

Descriptive Statistics, Score Distributions, and Reliability

Descriptive results for the EAS‐J are summarized in Table 2. The mean total EAS‐J score was 9.8 (SD = 4.4) on the 0–30 scale, with observed scores ranging from 0 to 27. Floor and ceiling accumulation was minimal (1.9% and 0.0%, respectively). Subscale means were 2.2 (SD = 1.4) for exposure to ageist messages, 2.9 (SD = 2.7) for ageism in interpersonal interactions, and 4.6 (SD = 1.8) for internalized ageism. A notable floor effect was observed for ageism in interpersonal interactions (26.5%), modest for exposure to ageist messages (15.6%), and small for internalized ageism (3.5%), whereas ceiling effects were ≤ 2.3%.

Internal consistency reliability was good for the total scale (Cronbach's α = 0.826) and for the ageism in interpersonal interactions and internalized ageism subscales (α = 0.840 and 0.815, respectively), and acceptable for exposure to ageist messages (α = 0.779).

The retest subsample (n = 150) showed broadly similar distributions to the main sample on key demographics, including age, gender, education, marital status, employment, and household income (Table S3). Test–retest reliability over a 4‐week interval was moderate for the total scale (ICC [3, 1] = 0.66; 95% CI: 0.56–0.74), internalized ageism (0.69; 0.60–0.77), and interpersonal ageism (0.59; 0.47–0.68), and poor for exposure to ageist messages (0.47; 0.34–0.59).

Factorial Validity

The three‐factor CFA model showed good fit on three of the four indices (CFI = 0.971, TLI = 0.959, SRMR = 0.037), although the RMSEA (0.075; 90% CI = 0.063–0.088) slightly exceeded the cutoff of 0.06.

Convergent and Discriminant Validity

Convergent validity was supported by high CR and AVE values across three factors: AVE values (exposure to ageist messages: 0.73; interpersonal interactions: 0.65; internalized ageism: 0.72) exceeded 0.50, and CR values (0.85, 0.90, 0.88) exceeded 0.70. Discriminant validity was confirmed using the Fornell–Larcker criterion, with the square root of AVE values (0.85, 0.81, 0.85) exceeding all inter‐factor correlations, the largest of which was 0.54. Furthermore, HTMT ratios ranged from 0.35 to 0.54, falling below the strict threshold of 0.85.

Nomological Validity

Nomological validity was assessed using a MIMIC model, examining associations between each latent factor and psychological variables (Table 4). Exposure to ageist messages was positively associated with perceived stress (β = 0.07, p < 0.001) and loneliness (β = 0.09, p < 0.001). Ageism in interpersonal interactions was positively related to perceived stress (β = 0.05, p < 0.001) and loneliness (β = 0.09, p < 0.001), and inversely associated with self‐esteem (β = −0.05, p < 0.001). Internalized ageism was positively associated with loneliness (β = 0.16, p < 0.001) and perceived stress (β = 0.04, p < 0.001), and inversely associated with well‐being (β = −0.05, p < 0.001) and self‐esteem (β = −0.04, p < 0.001). No significant associations were observed for happiness across any factor.

Discussion

This study evaluated the psychometric properties of the EAS‐J. CFA confirmed the original three‐factor structure with good fit on three of the four indices, and convergent, discriminant, and nomological validity were supported. These results indicate that the EAS‐J is a valid instrument for assessing everyday ageism in the Japanese context.

The mean total EAS‐J score (9.8) was comparable to that reported in the original US fielding of the scale (10.2) [5, 6], but was lower than scores reported in Ireland (12.5) [17], Canada (11.5) [20], a subsequent US study (12.2) [44], and substantially lower than Iran (19.8) [16]. These differences may partly reflect sample characteristics, such as a higher proportion of clinically frail populations [17] and racial/ethnic minorities [44], as well as sociocultural factors. Some studies were conducted during or shortly after the COVID‐19 pandemic, when ageism was heightened globally [45, 46]. In the Japanese context, social norms emphasizing harmony and indirect communication may suppress overt interpersonal ageism in daily interactions [47, 48]. Consistent with this interpretation, marked floor effects were observed in the interpersonal ageism subscale, which suggests that overt experiences of ageism may be relatively uncommon in this population.

The three‐factor structure indicates that the concept of everyday ageism is applicable within the Japanese context. Although item‐level analyses generally showed strong loadings, Item 10 (“Having health problems is part of getting older.”) demonstrated a comparatively lower loading, possibly because Japanese respondents may interpret statements about health decline in older age as a natural inevitability rather than a self‐stigmatizing belief, thereby reducing its conceptual alignment with the other internalized ageism items. This pattern may reflect East Asian acceptance‐based cultural orientations [47, 48]. Notably, the exposure to ageist messages subscale demonstrated strong factor loadings (λ = 0.82–0.89) in the present study, possibly reflecting the salience of age‐related public discourse in Japan's society.

The MIMIC findings align with Stereotype Embodiment Theory, which holds that culturally internalized age stereotypes become self‐relevant in later life and adversely affect health [49]. Exposure to ageist messages and interpersonal ageism was positively associated with perceived stress and loneliness, whereas internalized ageism was positively associated with loneliness and perceived stress and inversely associated with well‐being and self‐esteem. Notably, internalized ageism was associated with a broader range of psychological variables than the other two factors and showed the largest association with loneliness. This pattern is consistent with Stereotype Embodiment Theory, although causal direction cannot be inferred from the cross‐sectional design. These patterns highlight the potential harms of internalizing negative age stereotypes [3, 50]. No significant associations were observed for happiness, which may reflect lower sensitivity of single‐item measures compared with multi‐item scales of related constructs [51, 52]. This pattern of larger associations with loneliness is consistent with findings from Iran, Ireland, and Australia [16, 17, 18].

The EAS‐J may support future evaluations of anti‐ageism initiatives, pending evidence on its responsiveness to change. At the societal level, media monitoring and intergenerational programs may help mitigate exposure‐based ageism [53]. At the individual level, psychoeducational [54] and stereotype‐reversal interventions [55] may address internalized ageism. It could also help researchers and policymakers track ageism over time.

This study had some limitations. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference; the MIMIC model treats psychological variables as predictors of latent ageism factors solely to establish nomological validity, not causal direction. Longitudinal research is needed to clarify how ageism influences mental health trajectories [50]. Second, data collection via an online panel may underrepresent the oldest‐old or individuals with limited digital access, potentially constraining generalizability [56]. Third, the test–retest reliability was moderate. Everyday ageism may fluctuate over short periods, and we did not screen for major life changes or salient ageism‐related events during the 4‐week interval that may have contributed to within‐person variability. Future studies should incorporate mixed‐methods approaches to further explore these temporal variations. Fourth, the mandatory‐response design may have caused differential attrition, and the response rate could not be calculated, preventing assessment of selection bias.

This study developed and validated the EAS‐J, the first psychometrically supported instrument for assessing everyday ageism among older adults in Japan. The scale demonstrated adequate structural validity and theoretically consistent associations with health‐related outcomes. The EAS‐J contributes to global gerontological research on fostering age‐inclusive environments.
