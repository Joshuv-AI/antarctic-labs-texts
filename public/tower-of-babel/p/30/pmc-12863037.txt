Implementation of Proton Radiation Treatment Exclusion Volume Because of Inconsistent Location of Breast Expander Titanium Port
Authors: Emily T Tran, Patrick Newbury, Mark Newpower, Heather Ortega, Timothy D Malouff, Christina Henson
Published: 2025

Introduction

While mortality has decreased because of the advancement of early detection techniques and efficient systemic therapies, breast cancer continues to be the most common malignant cancer in women.1 Following imaging studies, biopsy, staging, and analysis of tumor marker subtypes, treatment plans vary depending on disease characteristics and patient preference. For patients with sizable disease relative to breast size, the following may be considered: neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) to downsize tumor, mastectomy, adjuvant radiation therapy, and reconstruction.2

Proper timing of each step is vital in maintaining excellent outcomes. Reconstruction is a crucial step of treatment for many patients, especially because of the psychological burden experienced by women undergoing mastectomy. Silicone tissue expanders are an important element of the reconstruction process, particularly in patients expected to receive adjuvant therapy. They serve as spacers for the breast pocket and help account for the expected anatomic changes secondary to radiation before placement of the final implant. The devices gradually expand the chest wall by intermittent injection of saline via a metallic port (MP) to prepare for reconstruction.3 Special care must be taken when delivering proton radiation for these patients, as proton dosimetry is sensitive to heterogeneities in tissue densities. Here, we present a case of a patient in which the MP of the silicone expander shifted throughout proton treatment, causing critical and unacceptable uncertainties within the delivery of the postmastectomy radiation therapy (PMRT).

Case Presentation

A 22-year-old female with locally advanced, right-sided, T3N1, ER/PR positive, HER2-negative invasive breast cancer presented for adjuvant radiation therapy after NAC, mastectomy, and immediate reconstruction with expander placement. She first noticed a lump in her right breast 7 months before diagnosis, although immediate ultrasound failed to detect a mass. Subsequent mammogram detected 11 cm of segmental pleomorphic calcifications in the right breast at 7 o’clock, and ultrasound redemonstrated a duct correlating to the region, highly suggestive of malignancy. Around this time, she also began having nipple discharge and skin retraction. Bilateral breast magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a 4 cm lesion as well as an axillary lymph node 1.5 cm in greatest dimension, suspicious for nodal metastasis. A second right axillary lymph node showed irregular thickening. Biopsy of the primary breast mass revealed grade 3, ER/PR positive, HER2-negative invasive ductal carcinoma of no special type. A nearby axillary lymph node was positive for the same. Her clinical prognostic stage was therefore IIB (T3N1). Genetic testing was negative for any known high-risk mutations. Pre-NAC positron-emission tomography demonstrated only the known right breast lesion and as many as 3 mildly positron-emission tomography-avid axillary nodes. Oocyte cryopreservation was performed before starting chemotherapy. She completed 4 cycles of doxorubicsin/cyclophosphamide (AC) and 4 cycles of dose-dense paclitaxel, both with pegfilgrastim-bmez and goserelin. She tolerated NAC well with grade 1 fatigue, nausea, and headaches. Following completion of NAC, she underwent right-sided mastectomy and sentinel lymph node dissection approximately 6 months after diagnosis. Pathology revealed 2.1 cm grade 3 Ductal carcinoma in-situ but no invasive cancer in the breast. It also revealed 4 of 6 lymph nodes involved with invasive cancer with extensive lymphovascular invasion. Her pathologic stage was therefore IIB (yTisN2).

She was prescribed 50 Gy (RBE) in 25 fractions using a breath-hold technique to the chest wall and regional lymphatics, including internal mammary, axillary levels 1 to 3, and supraclavicular lymph node levels. Multiple photon plans were attempted; however, her heart and lung constraints were unable to be met despite multiple attempts. Because of her young age and high organ at risk dose on photon plans, the decision was made to proceed with intensity modulated proton therapy (IMPT). Kilovoltage posterior-anterior and lateral films were obtained each day. Her first week of treatment was uneventful; however, imaging before fraction 7 revealed that the titanium MP on her tissue expander had shifted 1 to 2 cm and rotated (Fig. 1B). Expander rotation is a rare but known complication, particularly when expanders are not anchored with sutures, as hers had not been. Treatment was aborted that day, and we hoped that there would be no further movement. Unfortunately, the expander continued to rotate unpredictably every day. Use of an IMPT treatment plan with the port in an incorrect rotation would have resulted in underdosing and overdosing in unpredictable ways. A surgical intervention would delay treatment for an unacceptably long time. Therefore, we made an exclusion volume that the treatment planning software used to prohibit spot placement. This resulted in a plan that was much less sensitive to the daily location of the MP. After the implementation of our solution, she completed radiation.

Discussion

The decision was made to use proton therapy for this patient because of her very young age and her anatomy, particularly the depth of her internal mammary nodes and the thickness and anterior location of her contralateral normal breast tissue (Fig. 1A). These features would have made it very difficult to deliver sufficient dose to the right-sided at-risk internal mammary nodes while adequately sparing her left breast from high doses of radiation. Proton therapy has been shown in phase 2 nonrandomized studies to have low toxicity rates and similar rates of disease control compared with historical data studying conventional photon RT.4 A consensus statement from the Particle Therapy Cooperative Group Breast Cancer Subcommittee recommends that the mean dose to all quadrants of the contralateral breast should be limited to <1 Gy, particularly in women <40 years old, because of higher doses being associated with a 2.5-fold increased risk of contralateral breast cancer.5 It is well documented that proton therapy is associated with a lower risk of second cancer compared to photon therapy.6,7

Reconstruction following mastectomy is very important to many women, with several methods developed to improve cosmetic outcomes. At our institution, women typically receive a 2-stage breast reconstruction, wherein a tissue expander is placed following mastectomy. Saline is then gradually added to the expander, and the expander is replaced by a permanent implant following maximal expansion and appropriate wait period following receipt of any adjuvant radiation. Most women receive PMRT while the tissue expander is in place.

It is critical to understand the components of tissue expanders when planning PMRT, especially when using protons. Tissue expanders are typically composed of an outer silicone shell with an MP, and they are gradually filled with saline.8 While the silicone shell and saline are typically water equivalent, the MP (and associated computed tomography [CT] artifact) can lead to large dose heterogeneities during planning and treatment delivery. Proton therapy dose distributions are highly sensitive to density changes along the beam path, and the nonuniform distribution of pencil beams in IMPT accentuates this concern. The presence of metal in a planning CT brings 2 concerns: accurate modeling of the relative stopping power (RSP) of the expander port’s metal components and image artifacts caused by the presence of metal. Although there have been some studies, there remain limited data to guide treatment decisions. Our report adds to a growing body of literature treating tissue expanders with proton therapy.

At our institution, we use the Mevion S250i Hyperscan compact proton therapy system. It is capable of IMPT. The treatment planning system (TPS) used for this plan was RayStation v11A.

To overcome the issue of imaging artifacts because of the presence of the MP, the entire expander was contoured and overridden as water. This process was applied for all plans. In the first treatment plan, a structure called “PORT_AVOID” was created by expanding the MP 5 mm in all directions, and the optimizer was forbidden from placing spots within that zone. Figure 1A shows the MP in the original planning scan.

On the patient’s 7th day of treatment, in-room imaging showed the MP had moved several cm toward the chest wall, and rotated approximately 180° (Fig. 1B). We resimulated and replanned the patient with the new port’s location.

On the first day of treatment for the replan, in-room imaging showed that the MP had moved significantly again (Fig. 1C), this time to a similar location as in the original planning scan shown in Fig. 1A. At this point, we decided to create a generous exclusion zone that would accommodate the movement of the expander port. In the avoidance zone, the TPS optimizer was forbidden from placing spots. This avoidance zone was approximately 11.0 × 4.1 × 4.0 cm3. After this second replan, the patient resumed treatment for 11 fractions. The MP’s position was relatively consistent thereafter. To improve plan quality, a final replan was created with a smaller exclusion zone (explained below) for the remaining 8 fractions. This final simulation CT is shown in Fig. 1D. This final avoidance zone was approximately 4.0 × 6.0 × 5.5 cm3 around the MP and attempted to account for the port’s different locations as seen on daily imaging.

Every plan and replan used the identical beam angles and number of beams. Because of the large size of the clinical target volume (CTV), the plan required 2 isocenters. Two beams were used on the upper isocenter and 3 beams on the lower isocenter. Beam geometry is shown in Fig. 2A.

To overcome daily motion of the MP, we created 2 structures: an avoidance zone and a CTV_EVAL volume. The CTV_EVAL volume comprised the original CTV minus the avoidance zone and the implant. By keeping spots out of the avoidance zone, any port movement would have minimal effects on the dose distribution. By only evaluating CTV_EVAL coverage for the dose prescription, we sought to consider only tissue that may harbor disease in the plan evaluation. The final plan was robustly optimized using multifield optimization (MFO). Robust optimization settings were an isotropic 5 mm in X, Y, and Z directions and a systematic density uncertainty of ±3.5%.

Although our method represents 1 solution, other institutions have reported on their solutions. In one of the earliest studies on proton therapy with immediate tissue expanders, Mutter et al describe the Mayo Clinic experience with treating 12 patients with IMPT. In this study, the authors created a planning strategy that created a template of the expander port, overlaid it onto the planning CT, and overrode the RSP values. The rest of the expander is contoured and the RSP is set to 1. They also assessed hypothetical movement of the port 1 cm in various directions and found that CTV coverage was clinically acceptable.9 Zhu et al presented a similar study of IMPT for PMRT in patients with expander ports. In their method, they used a 3-field technique. For each field, a block in the shape of the expander +5 mm was created, and the TPS was forbidden to place spots in these blocks. Because of the margin around the block, daily position variation of the port within that tolerance does not induce dose perturbation in the plan.10

Another approach is to avoid the port in general, as in the study by Kirk et al. In this study, a patient with left-sided breast cancer and an immediate tissue expander was planned for proton therapy using MFO and an anterior and left lateral beam arrangement to avoid the port while providing adequate coverage of the chest wall and regional nodes. For planning, the imaging artifact was given an RSP of 1.0, while the port was given an RSP of 1.4. A 5 mm margin around the port was also given. Even when avoiding the MP, the heart and ipsilateral lung doses were improved compared to a similar photon plan.11

DeCesaris et al also reported on their technique of avoiding the port, in which the authors used a hybrid treatment planning approach using pencil-beam scanning and combination single-field optimization and MFO techniques. For their study, the port was given a 5 mm expansion. A 2-field single-field optimization technique was used superior and inferior to the MP, with a 3 or 4-field MFO technique near the port, which was the port with a 1.5 cm superior and inferior margin.12 Additionally, the type of tissue expander may also be considered. Tissue expanders often contain high-Z MP, creating CT artifacts, such as streaking, that may limit the ability to accurately calculate and deliver dose with proton therapy. To counteract these effects, Howarth et al suggest the use of submuscular tissue expanders.13

With the creation of an exclusion volume around the unpredictable titanium port, we were able to provide proper proton radiation treatment to the breast area as needed. Moving forward, this planning approach may be a method to provide quality plans for patients with breast cancer with MP. However, plans will be more consistent and this issue may be avoided by suturing the expander port during surgery.

Conclusion

Sutured expander ports are often used during breast reconstruction following mastectomies; however, some cases may arise in which they are moving freely. By making a volume of exclusion, we were able to account for the inconsistency in the expander’s titanium port location and properly treat this patient with postmastectomy proton radiation therapy.
