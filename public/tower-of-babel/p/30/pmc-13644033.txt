Association between herpes zoster and cognitive impairment among Chinese community-dwelling elderly: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Bingbing Zheng, Wenting Cao, Linlin Zhang, Ping Zhang, Tianlei Song, Xiuru Wang, Jingxi Zhan, Yong You, Yi Ning, Jindong Ding Petersen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCognitive impairment (CI) and herpes zoster (HZ) are prevalent among elderly individuals, however, the association between these two conditions has not been studied.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study between April 1, 2023, to June 17, 2024, comparing nine communities in Haikou, Hainan Province, China. Participants were aged 60 and above and had resided in Haikou for at least six months. Participants were interviewed to obtain questionnaire data on age, sex, highest education level obtained, marital status, body weight and height, residence location, current smoker and daily alcohol drinker, history of HZ and antiviral treatment, and presence of other chronic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and depression). CI was assessed using the Chinese Mini-Mental Status (CMMS) with education-specific cut-off values. Associations between HZ and CI were examined using univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses in SPSS software (version 27.0).ResultsAmong 1233 participants (mean age ± SD: 72.4 ± 7.4 years; 41.8% males; 61.6% married; 66.0% with middle school education or less; 22.9% rural residence), 133 (10.8%) reported having an HZ history, of whom 37 (27.8%) received antiviral treatment. CI was identified in 430 participants (34.9%) based on education-specific CMMS scores. Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that a history of HZ was associated with an increased risk of CI (OR = 1.69, 95% CI: 1.14–2.51) after adjusting for age, sex, residence location, body mass index (BMI), and depression. Additionally, participants aged over 80 years (OR = 1.89, 95% CI: 1.31–2.73) and those living in rural areas (OR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.00-1.81) were found to have significantly increased odds of CI. Although antiviral treatment appeared to reduce the risk of CI (OR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.18–2.06), the result was not statistically significant.ConclusionsOur findings suggest a potential association between HZ and CI in the elderly population. Further large-scale cohort studies are needed to establish the causality and to assess the impact of antiviral treatment on the development of CI and dementia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28485-9.

Background

Cognitive impairment (CI) refers to the impairment of, often decline in, one or more cognitive domains, including memory, language, visuospatial, executive, calculation, and judgment [1], with ranges from mild to severe forms, often refer as dementia, which significantly affects an individual’s ability to live independently and the quality of life for both patients and their families.

Globally, the prevalence of CI among individuals aged 50 years and above varies widely, ranging from 5% to 41% in community settings [2]. In China, approximately 22.2% of the elderly population aged 60 years and above suffers from CI [3]. With the rapid aging of the population, it is projected that by 2050, around 40 million people in China will be living with dementia [4], which will have substantial socio-economic implications.

The risk factors for CI are multifactorial. In addition to age-related cognitive changes, various factors biological factors (such as genetics and sex), lifestyle factors (such as smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical activity), environmental factors (such as marital status, education, and occupation), and health conditions (including diabetes, hypertension, and hearing loss), have been identified are contributing to the development of CI [5, 6].

Herpes zoster (HZ), caused by the reactivation of varicella-zoster virus (VZV), is a neurocutaneous disease characterized by a strong neurotropic nature, infecting only humans [7]. Following primary infection, which causes varicella, VZV can remain dormant in locations such as the dorsal root and autonomic ganglia [8]. The global incidence of HZ is from 3 to 5 cases per 1000 person-years, with the incidence nearly doubled after the age of 50 [9]. In China, recent data from 2022 indicate that the incidence of HZ among individuals aged 60 and above is 12 per 1000 person-years [10].

The high prevalence of HZ and its persistent presence in nerve cells may contribute to neurodegenerative changes [11]. Some studies have reported a significantly increased risk of dementia among individuals with a history of HZ, particularly following the first diagnosis or recurrence, suggesting that HZ may double the risk of developing dementia. However, these findings are not universally consistent, as a recent study from South Korea found no increased risk [12–14]. Despite variations in study design that may account for these discrepancies, to our knowledge, no epidemiological study has examined the association between HZ and CI in the Chinese population. Therefore, we conducted this study to explore the potential association between these two conditions in our study population.

This study was reported following the Strengthening the Report of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines [15].

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a cross-sectional study involving community-dwelling individuals from nine communities across three of four districts in Haikou City, Hainan Province, China. Participants were selected using a convenience sampling method. The sample size was calculated based on the reported 22.2% prevalence of CI among individuals aged 60 years and above in China as of 2022 [3]. Using the standard formula for determining sample size in cross-sectional studies for quantitative variables [16], the required sample size was estimated to be 1390 participants.

The study population initially consisted of elderly individuals who had participated in the annual government-funded, free-of-charge health checkups at local community healthcare centers. The inclusion criteria for participants were: aged 60 years or older, residence in Haikou for at least six months prior to data collection, with no severe psychiatric illness, cancer, and hearing impairment.

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a structured questionnaire (Supplementary Appendix 1) between April 1, 2023, and June 17, 2024. To ensure accessibility for international readers, the questionnaire was translated into English corresponding to the original Chinese version (Supplementary Appendix 2).

Cognitive function and measurement

Cognitive function was assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) [17]. The MMSE is a 30-item tool that evaluates eight domains: time orientation, spatial orientation, instant memory, attention, calculation ability, delayed memory, language, and visuospatial skills. Given the significant influence of education on MMSE scores, we applied education-specified cut-offs to define CI based on the validated study of the Chinese population [18]: MMSE ≤ 17 points for those with no formal education, ≤ 20 points for those with 1 to 6 years of schooling, and ≤ 24 points for those with more than 6 years of education.

Additionally, for sensitivity analyses, we used an alternative MMSE cut-off score of 23 (MMSE ≤ 23), which is commonly employed in international studies to identify participants with CI [19].

Herpes zoster and measurement

Participants self-reported their history of HZ before data collection (Yes/No). For those who answered “Yes”, additional information was gathered regarding the age at the first onset of HZ, whether they received antiviral treatment within three days of onset, and if they experienced any complications associated with HZ. Due to the limited number of participants, the analysis focused on the first onset of HZ history and antiviral treatment.

Other covariates and measurements

Data on age (categorized as 60–69, 70–79, and ≥ 80 years), sex, highest education level, marital status, current smoker, daily alcohol drinker, residence location (urban or rural, based on home address), body weight, height, and health conditions hypertension (Yes/No), diabetes (Yes/No), dyslipidemia (Yes/No) were self-reported.

Given the generally low education levels among the elderly population in China [20], education was categorized into four groups: “Illiterate”, “Primary school”, “Middle school”, or “High school and above” (including College/University and Postgraduate). Marital status was recorded as “Married”, “Unmarried”, “Widowed”, or “Divorced”, and further grouped as “Married” and “Non-married” status (including those unmarried, widowed, or divorced). Body weight (in kilograms) and height (in meters) were measured by nurses at the healthcare centers, and BMI was calculated using the World Health Organization standard format (weight in kilograms by their height in meters squared, kg/m2). Participants were categorized according to “Chinese Adult Overweight and Obesity Prevention and Control Guidelines” as underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–24.0 kg/m2), overweight, and obesity (≥ 24.0 kg/m2) [18]. Smoking was dichotomized as “Yes” for participants who reported current smokers, and “No” for all others. Drinking was dichotomized as “Yes” for participants who self-reported had alcohol consumption every day in the past 12 months, and “No” for all others. Depression was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2) [21], a brief screening tool that evaluates the frequency of depressed mood or anhedonia (loss of interest or pleasure in routine activities) over the past 2 weeks. A total score of 3 or more was considered indicative of depression.

Quality control

To minimize measurement bias and ensure the quality and accuracy of data collection, all interviewers—comprising medical university students and community healthcare workers—received standardized training from two neurologists from a tertiary hospital before the survey began. The training covered communication skills, interpretation, and application of standardized cognitive assessment measurement MMSE. A video recording of a face-to-face MMSE interview by a neurologist with a patient was made available for review by the interviewers as needed.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of participants were described both in total and stratified by HZ history. Continuous variables were reported as mean with standard deviations (SD). Categorical variables were reported with count and prevalence.

Multivariable logistic regression models were applied to analyze the association between HZ history and CI, with adjustment for the significant covariates identified through univariable logistic regression. In addition, we conducted a subgroup analysis involving only individuals who experienced HZ, to estimate the association between antiviral treatment and CI. Stratification analyses on age group, sex, and residence location for the association were also conducted. Sensitivity analyses with an MMSE cut-off score of 23 as CI were performed. The results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). A statistical significance level was set at a P-value less than 0.05 (P < 0.05). All data analyses were conducted using the SPSS software (version 27.0).

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted by the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hainan Medical University on July 25th, 2022 (Approved number: HYLL-2022-301). All participants provided their written informed consent for study participation.

Results

Participants characteristics

Among the 1233 participants (mean age: 72.4 ± 7.4; 57.8% female), nearly two-thirds had an education level of middle school or lower. A majority (61.6%) were married, and 22.9% resided in rural areas. In terms of BMI, 45.4% were classified as overweight and obese. Regarding lifestyle habits, 12.8% of participants reported as a current smoker, and 16.4% consumed alcohol daily in the past 12 months. Among the chronic conditions, hypertension was the most prevalent (44.7%), followed by diabetes (18.2%), dyslipidemia (15.1%), and depression (11.8%) (Table 1).

A total of 133 (10.8%) participants self-reported HZ history, among them, 37 (27.8%) received antiviral treatment. The overall rates of HZ decreased from 60 to 69 to 70–79 and increased again after age 80. Participants in the urban areas (11.6%, 110/951) had more than the rate of HZ than those in rural areas (8.2%, 23/282).

Using MMSE education-specific cut-off values, 430 (34.9%) were identified with CI; Of whom 58 (13.5%) had HZ history. Additionally, using an MMSE cut-off score of 23, 491 (39.8%) participants were identified with CI; Of whom 59 (12.0%) had HZ history.

Association between HZ and CI

Univariate logistic regression analysis showed that individuals with a history of HZ had a 1.51-fold (95% CI: 1.05–2.18) increased risk of CI compared to those without an HZ history. Additionally, the participants aged over 80 years (OR = 2.09, 95% CI: 1.48–2.95), females (OR = 1.28, 95%CI: 1.01–1.62), those residing in rural areas (OR = 1.42, 95%CI: 1.08–1.86), and those with depression (OR = 1.59, 95%CI: 1.12–2.25) had an increased risk of CI. Conversely, being overweight and obesity was associated with a decreased risk of CI (OR = 0.72, 95%CI: 0.56–0.92) (Table 2).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis adjusted for age, sex, residence location, BMI, and depression, confirmed that increased risk of CI persisted for individuals with a history of HZ compared to those without (OR = 1.69, 95% CI: 1.14–2.51). Additionally, being over 80 years of age (OR = 1.89, 95% CI: 1.31–2.73) and having depression (OR = 1.75, 95%CI: 1.20–2.55) remained significant independent risk factors for CI. Residing in rural areas was on the borderline significant for increased CI risk (OR = 1.35, 95%CI: 1.00-1.81).

Stratification analysis revealed risk discrepancies by age group and sex in the association between HZ history and CI. A tendency of higher risk of CI was observed among the individuals aged 70–79 years (OR = 2.28, 95%CI: 1.20–4.34), males (OR = 1.84, 95%CI: 1.02–3.31), and those residing in rural areas (OR = 1.97, 95%CI: 0.81–4.81), compared to other age groups, females, and residing in urban areas, respectively, in multivariate logistic regression models adjusted for the confounders (Table 3).

Discussion

This study investigated the association between HZ history and CI among community-dweller elderly in Haikou, China. Among 1233 participants, the prevalence of CI and HZ was 34.9% and 10.8%, respectively. Multivariable logistic regression indicated that HZ history was significantly associated with a higher risk of CI after adjusting for the covariates. Increased odds of CI were also associated with age over 80 years, residing in rural areas, and depression. Conversely, lower odds were associated with being overweight and obesity. Moreover, we observed a tendency toward a lower risk of CI in those who received antiviral treatment.

Previous community-based studies in China have shown that the prevalence of CI in the elderly ranges from 4.2% to 44.2% [22]. Using education-specific MMSE cut-off values for defining CI, the 34.9% CI prevalence in our study population aligns with the previous report. Even with the commonly used MMSE cut-off value of 23 for CI, the 39.8% prevalence is close to the highest but still falls within the range. The high prevalence of CI in our study population may likely be due to the low education levels among the elderly population in Hainan Province, as 66.1% of our study population had a middle school education or less. Moreover, the 10.8% HZ prevalence among our study population is also consistent with other domestic cross-sectional study results [23–25]. These similar prevalences may indicate homogeneities of our study population with other regions of China.

Regarding the association between HZ and CI, previous studies have mainly focused on dementia. A systematic review published in 2019, encompassing 57 studies, found a tentative association between recent infection with, or reactivation of HZ virus and dementia [26]. Notably, one of the studies from Taiwan included in this report shows that HZ recurrence led to a 2.97-fold increased risk of developing dementia compared to the control group with 5 years of follow-up, indicating a strong association [27]. Another study showed that 5.6% of individuals aged 50 and above with HZ developed dementia through a six-year follow-up cohort study in Taiwan [28]. A study by Danish scholars also found that individuals carrying the varicella-zoster virus had a 2.4-fold increased risk of developing dementia after a 12-year follow-up compared to the control group [29]. In our study population, elderly with HZ history tended to a 1.69-fold increased risk of CI, lower than the risk estimations in the aforementioned studies, probably due to the outcome measurement of our study was CI which includes mild and severe forms of dementia.

The pathology of HZ and the development of CI has not been fully understood. Recent basic research has shown that mice infected with herpes simplex virus-1 (HSV-1) exhibited significant neurodegeneration and decreased cognitive abilities, and HSV-1 infections were independent predictors for dementia among older in northern Uganda in the epidemiology research conducted in the cross-sectional community study [30]. Further, research has indicated that HZ virus reactivation is a promoting factor for chronic neuronal damage, targeting brain areas that change in Alzheimer’s disease: the frontal and temporal cortices and the hippocampus [31]. Moreover, this virus is believed to contribute to the deposition of amyloid plaques in the brain [32].

Studies on HZ antiviral treatment and the risk of CI are scarce. A recently published systematic review identified only six cohort studies globally, including two studies from Taiwan, the United Kingdom, Denmark, Sweden, and South Korea, encompassing 538,531 patients [33]. The review showed a significantly increased risk (hazard ratio 1.15) of dementia in those who did not receive antiviral treatment for HZ and a significantly decreased risk (hazard ratio 0.68) in those who did. Our dataset was relatively small, with 133 participants reported with HZ history, only 27.8% received antiviral treatment within three days of their first HZ, regardless of antiviral medication types and forms, and those who received the treatment showed a lower risk tendency of CI than those who did not. The limitation of our cross-sectional study design calls for further cohort studies among the Chinese elderly population in mainland regions to provide evidence-based public health prevention and intervention strategies, including HZ vaccinations to prevent HZ and subsequent dementia.

Zoster vaccination has shown its effectiveness against almost 45.9% of HZ in adults alone; for those people with comorbidities, the effectiveness was even higher, around 50% [34]. The HZ vaccine was introduced in China in June 2020. However, a large proportion of older adults in China are unaware of the virus (33.2%), decline vaccination (30.2%), or are hesitant (26.8%) to be vaccinated [35, 36], calling for public strategies to increase disease awareness and vaccination acceptances.

With our dataset, we identified several risk factors for cognitive, including advanced age (older than 80 years), residence in rural areas, and depression—all consistent with previous research findings [3, 37]. Notably, our findings indicated significantly lower CI odds among overweight individuals (OR = 0.73, 95%CI: 0.56–0.95). This contrasts with the expression that overweight and obese are risk factors for health outcomes but aligns with the result published in 2021 of a community-based, 9-year prospective cohort study involving 12,027 Chinese elderly participants (mean age 81.2), which reported an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.75–0.99) for CI among the overweight group [38]. Additionally, a meta-analysis of prospective studies published up to May 2019, regardless of regions, also revealed that late-life overweight (pooled relative risk 0.78 (95%CI: 0.71–9.86)) and obesity (pooled relative risk 0.76 (95%CI: 0.66–0.87)) were associated lower risk for CI [39]. Due to limitations in sample size, we did not differentiate between the overweight and obese groups, which should be addressed in further studies.

Study limitations

Like other cross-sectional studies, ours is also subject to recall bias, measurement bias, and limitation in establishing causality between HZ and CI, or vice versa. Individuals with severe HZ often have severe pain and skin lesions, which are unlikely to be forgotten, whereas those with mild symptoms may underreport their experience. In addition, community-dwelling older adults with CI may have varying degrees of memory impairment, depending on disease severity, which could further compromise the accuracy of self-reported histories of HZ. To address these concerns, we are in the process of establishing a cohort study among community-dwelling older adults to enable more detailed and robust investigation. Furthermore, we use an education-specific MMSE cut-off value for CI identification without further categorizing it into mild to severe dementia, which may neutralize the association estimations. Additionally, due to sample size limitations, many variates that may associated with both HZ and CI were not included in the analysis, which may also lead to bias to some extent.

Conclusions

This is the first study to investigate the association between HZ and CI among the community-dwelling elderly population in China. Our findings suggest a potential link between these two prevalent conditions in the elderly. However, further large-scale cohort studies are necessary to establish a causal relationship and the effect of antiviral treatment on the development of dementia.
