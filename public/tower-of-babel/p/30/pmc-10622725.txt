Spontaneous Multiple Cervical Artery Dissections and Recurrent Deep Vein Thrombosis in a Young Adult
Authors: Maurizio Giorelli, Ruggiero Leone, Rosario Francesco Balzano
Published: 2023

Dear Editor,

Cervical artery dissections (CADs) are characterized by intramural hematomas arising from subintimal or subadventitial dissections of the arterial walls and clot formation.1 Spontaneous CADs account for 2% of all ischemic strokes occurring in young adults.2 The prevalence of CADs in the general population remains controversial, since some patients report only facial pain and the pathology may be underestimated.3 Multiple simultaneous, spontaneous CADs are even rarer, possibly accounting for 15% of all CADs.2

A 46-year-old male presented to our emergency room with headache, confusion, and mood fluctuations. A clinical examination revealed left Horner’s syndrome with no other neurological signs. The patient’s clinical history included recurrent deep vein thrombosis of the inferior limbs. The patient smoked 20 cigarettes daily. Familial occurrence of juvenile stroke was also reported. CT and MRI revealed brain lesions. Cranial tomography angiography showed the “flame sign” in the left internal carotid artery (ICA). Echo Doppler ultrasound and multiplanar reconstruction of fat-saturated T1-weighted MRI sequences confirmed dissections of the right and left ICAs and the left vertebral artery (VA) in the V3 segment (Fig. 1A-D). Echo Doppler ultrasound also demonstrated a “stump flow” profile in the left ICA and reductions in the blood flow and velocity in the right ICA and left VA (Fig. 1E-G). Complete screening for both common and juvenile vascular risk factors was performed, which revealed hyperhomocysteinemia (HHCy) (25.9 µg/L), reduced blood levels of folic acid (1.9 ng/mL) and cobalamin (130 pg/mL), and homozygosity for the C677T polymorphism in the methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase gene (MTHFR). The patient was admitted to the stroke unit and received dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin at 100 mg/day and clopidogrel at 75 mg/day). The patient was discharged and advised to continue dual antiplatelet therapy, discontinue smoking, and commence folate supplementation.

When we first evaluated the patient, we considered whether he could be affected by a single condition that would explain the multiple CADs and venous thrombosis. HHCy is a well-known risk factor for CADs that leads to arterial wall degradation.34 It is also associated with thrombophilia and deep vein thrombosis.5 MTHFR is the key enzyme involved in the metabolism of homocysteine (HCy).5 Three isoforms arise from different combinations of the C677T and T677T polymorphisms of MTHFR. The isoform derived from the homozygous C677T polymorphism shows a 50% reduction in enzymatic activity. The C677T polymorphism remains controversial as an independent risk factor for spontaneous CADs, with some studies finding an association67 whereas one study did not.8 Nevertheless, a correlation has been found between the C677T MTHFR polymorphism, plasma HCy levels, and the incidence of CADs.5 Notably, people harboring the C677T allele of MTHFR have HHCy, but the concentration of HCy varies from moderate to very high depending on the presence of either a homozygotic or a heterozygotic condition, smoking habits, and supplementation with specific nutrients such as folate.4 Further, blood HCy levels may depend on the effective activity of cystathionine β-synthase, which is an MTHFR-independent pathway for HCy scavenging.4 We believe that unequivocal results from studies on the C677T polymorphism of MTHFR as an independent risk factor for CADs are due not only to the genetic profile but also the net concentrations of HCy. Our patient had elevated blood HCy levels and low levels of both folate and cobalamin (well-known risk factors for CADs6) and venous thrombosis.9 The patient was also a heavy smoker. We believe that, in the present case, the interaction of the C677T MTHFR homozygous condition with cigarette smoking and scarcity of folate and cobalamin may have induced increased HHCy, ultimately leading to multiple spontaneous CADs and recurrent deep vein thrombosis of the limbs.

The main treatment decision for CADs is whether the patient should receive antiplatelet or anticoagulation therapy. Although there are no relevant treatment guidelines, some clinicians favor transitioning from anticoagulation therapy as a first-line stroke-preventing treatment to antiplatelet therapy after reaching clinical stability.10 Approximately 45% of patients included in the CADISS trial received dual antiplatelet therapy and did not show clear ischemic stroke occurrence during the 12-month follow-up.10 In the present case, we favored dual antiplatelet therapy owing to a lack of evidence of recurrent transient ischemic attacks/strokes in the patient’s recent history, the extension of occlusion to the intracranial tract of the left VA and right ICA, and the patient’s young age, preference, and occupation. Endovascular therapy was not considered suitable because of the lack of pseudoaneurysms and recurrent symptoms.
