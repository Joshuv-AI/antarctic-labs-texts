SNRPB2 promotes triple‐negative breast cancer progression by controlling alternative splicing of MDM4 pre‐mRNA
Authors: Shiyi Yu, Yue Si, Jianzhong Yu, Chengyang Jiang, Fei Cheng, Miao Xu, Zhehao Fan, Fangchen Liu, Chang Liu, Ying Wang, Ning Wang, Chenxu Liu, Caili Bi, Haibo Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlternative splicing generates cancer‐specific transcripts and is now recognized as a hallmark of cancer. However, the critical oncogenic spliceosome‐related proteins involved in triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC) remain elusive. Here, we explored the expression pattern of spliceosome‐related proteins in TNBC, non‐TNBC, and normal breast tissues from The Cancer Genome Atlas breast cancer (TCGA‐BRCA) cohort, revealing higher expression of nearly half of spliceosome‐related proteins in TNBC than their counterparts. Among these TNBC‐specific spliceosome‐related proteins, the expression of SNRPB2 was associated with poor prognosis in patients with TNBC. In TNBC cells, the knockdown of SNRPB2 strongly suppressed cell proliferation and invasion and induced cell cycle arrest. Mechanistically, transcriptome data showed that SNRPB2 knockdown inactivated E2F1 signaling, which regulated the cell cycle. We further validated the downregulation of several cell cycle genes in SNRPB2 knockdown cells. Moreover, the analysis showed that SNRPB2 knockdown triggered the alteration of many alternative splicing events, most of which were skipping of exon. In TNBC cells, it was found that SNRPB2 knockdown led to the skipping of exon 6 in MDM4 pre‐mRNA, generating MDM4‐S transcript and downregulating MDM4 protein expression. More importantly, downregulation of MDM4 decreased retinoblastoma 1 (Rb1) protein expression, which is a target of MDM4 and a regulator of E2F1 signaling. In summary, the current study revealed an SNRPB2/MDM4/Rb axis in promoting the progression of TNBC, providing novel insights and novel targets for combating TNBC.

INTRODUCTION

Breast cancer is the most prevalent cancer type for women worldwide. 1 , 2 Following the detection of estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and human epidermal growth factor receptor‐2 (Her2), breast cancer can be divided into luminal, Her2+, and triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC). 3 Triple‐negative breast cancer has the worst prognosis and strongest metastasis potential compared with other subtypes of breast cancer. 4 It is urgent to uncover mechanisms underlying the development of TNBC, providing novel biomarkers and therapeutic strategies to treat patients with TNBC.

More than 90% of human genes are regulated by alternative splicing (AS), which generates a diversity of transcripts and protein isoforms. 5 Alternative splicing events could be categorized into five major types, including skipped exon (SE), intron retention (IR), mutually exclusive exon (MXE), and selective use of alternative 5′‐or 3′‐splice sites (A5SS/A3SS). 6 The spliceosome is a multimegadalton RNA‐protein complex responsible for assembling, activating, and catalyzing pre‐mRNA splicing. 7 Aberrant activity of spliceosome produces cancer‐specific transcripts, which govern mRNA stability, translatability, and protein isoforms. 8 , 9 , 10 Dysregulation of several spliceosome components has been identified in breast cancer, which gave rise to oncogenic transcripts. 11 For example, hnRNPM, a spliceosome component, facilitates lung metastasis of TNBC cells by mediating splicing of CD44. 12 A recent study showed that spliceosome targeted therapy is a promising therapeutic approach for MYC‐driven TNBC by activating antitumor immune pathways. 13 However, the critical dysregulated proteins of the spliceosome in promoting TNBC remain uncertain and need further study.

The current study discovered that many spliceosome proteins were upregulated in TNBC compared with non‐TNBC and normal breast tissues. Among them, SNRPB2 was significantly upregulated in TNBC and its expression was associated with poor prognosis of patients with TNBC. We described that knockdown of SNRPB2 inhibited cell proliferation and induced cell cycle arrest in TNBC cells. Additionally, by analyzing transcriptome data and experimental validation, our data suggested that SNRPB2 directly bound to MDM4 pre‐mRNA and governed AS of MDM4 pre‐mRNA to activate E2F1 signaling. The study revealed a critical role of SNRPB2 in the progression of TNBC.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Human tissues

Eleven breast tumors and adjacent normal breast tissues were collected from patients diagnosed with TNBC in the Affiliated Hospital of Yangzhou University from 2021 to 2023. None of the patients received chemotherapy or radiotherapy before the surgery. Written informed consent was collected from patients enrolled in the study. All procedures were approved and carried out under the supervision of the Ethics Committee of Yangzhou University (YXYLL‐2021‐08). The samples were immediately transferred to liquid nitrogen for storage before extraction of RNA or protein.

Cell culture

293T, non‐TNBC cell lines (MCF7 and T47D), TNBC cell lines (BT549, MDA‐MB‐468, MDA‐MB‐231, and SUM159) and the immortalized breast epithelial cell line MCF10A were purchased from ATCC. These cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling and under the detection of mycoplasma contamination using MycoBlue Mycoplasma Detector (Vazyme) every month. Non‐TNBC cells were cultured in RPMI‐1640 (Hyclone) with the supplement of 10% FBS (Gibco). 293T and TNBC cells were cultured in DMEM (Hyclone) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco). MCF10A cells were cultured in MCF10A condition medium from Procell (#CM‐0525). These cells were kept in a humid incubator with 5% CO2.

Bioinformatic analysis

Expression matrix of breast tumors (774 nonbasal, 171 basal) and 114 normal breast tissues were acquired from The Cancer Genome Atlas breast cancer (TCGA‐BRCA) project using cBioportal software. 14 A list of spliceosome‐related proteins was retrieved from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). 15 The expression patterns of these spliceosome‐related proteins in normal breast tissues, nonbasal breast tumors, and basal breast tumors were compared. According to a previous study, basal breast tumors were divided into four major subtypes. 16 The expression data from the METABRIC dataset 17 was further downloaded using cBioportal software.

Kaplan–Meier Plotter software was used to study the association between the expression of genes and the overall survival of patients with basal breast cancer derived from transcriptome data deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository. 18 For further validation, survival information (overall survival, disease‐specific survival, and progression‐free survival) of patients with basal breast cancer from the TCGA‐BRCA cohort was acquired, and the association between SNRPB2 expression and the prognosis of patients with basal breast cancer was analyzed by the Kaplan–Meier method.

Genomic‐scale CRISPR screens of breast cancer cell lines were retrieved from a previous study. 19 A Bayes factor analysis was used to identify essential genes for the survival of breast cancer cells.

RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) data of SNRPB2 knockdown MDA‐MB‐231‐LM2 and the control group was acquired from the GEO database (GSE160952). 20 Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) software was used for enrichment analysis. The differentially expressed genes (fold change >2 and p value <0.05) were finally calculated using DeSeq2 software. rMATS software was selected to analyze AS events between siSNRPB2 and siControl groups (ΔPSI, Percent Spliced In of siSNRPB2 samples minus Percent Spliced in of siControl samples). Significantly altered AS events were defined as |ΔPSI| > 0.1 and false discovery rate <0.01. Genes involved in AS events were subjected to KEGG enrichment analysis using DAVID software (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/tools.jsp). The altered events were categorized according to their distribution and type of transcripts using the PAVIS annotation tool. 21 The IGV tool was used to visualize the Sashimi plot of MDM4 transcripts.

The abundance of RBX1 and FOXM1 transcripts in breast cancer and normal breast tissues was retrieved from the TCGA‐BRCA dataset using the ASCancer Atlas. 22

RNA interference

siRNAs targeting SNRPB2 and control siRNA were synthesized and purchased from GenePharma. siRNAs were transfected into indicated cells using GP‐transfect‐Mate (GenePharma) per the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, cells were seeded in 6‐well plates; on the next day, siRNAs were mixed with GP‐transfect‐Mate in OptiMEM (Invitrogen) and incubated for 15 min. The mixture was then added to the wells in the plates and cultured for an additional 2 days. Finally, the knockdown efficiency was confirmed by western blotting. The sequences of siRNAs were as follows: Control siRNA, 5′‐UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT‐3′; SNRPB2 si1, 5′‐GGUGGACAUUGUGGCUUUAAATT‐3′; and SNRPB2 si2, 5′‐GCUCAUCCACAAAUGCCUUGATT‐3′.

Lentivirus package

The sequences of control siRNA and SNRPB2 siRNAs were ligated into pLKO.1 vector to construct shRNA plasmids. pLKO.1‐shRNA was cotransfected with pMD2.G and psPAX2 plasmids into 293T cells to package lentivirus. MDA‐MB‐231 was infected with harvested lentivirus and screened with puromycin for 3 days. The knockdown efficiency of SNRPB2 shRNAs was determined by western blotting.

In vivo xenograft assay

The animal assays were carried out following the Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines of the Yangzhou University School of Medicine. Six nude mice (3 weeks old, female) were obtained from the Model Animal Research Center of Nanjing University and randomly divided into two groups (n = 3). MDA‐MB‐231 cells (5 × 106; Control sh or SNRPB2 sh1) were suspended in PBS/Matrigel (Corning Inc.) (1:1) and injected into the armpit of mice. When tumors were palpable (2 weeks after injection of MDA‐MB‐231 cells), we used a caliper to measure the size of tumors, and the tumor volumes (V) were calculated with the equation V = (L × W2)/2.

Reverse transcription‐PCR and quantitative PCR

Total RNA of cells was extracted using TRNzol reagent (Tiangen) according to the manufacturer's protocol. For tissues, samples were first cut into small pieces and homogenized with a homogenizer then subjected to RNA extraction with TRNzol. The RNA was reverse transcribed using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for quantitative PCR (qPCR; Vazyme). Reverse transcription‐qPCR was undertaken with the ChamQ SYRB qPCR Master Mix reagent (Vazyme) on a 7900HT Sequence Detection System (ABI). Polymerase chain reaction was carried out with the Rapid Taq Master Mix (Vazyme) on a 2720 Thermal Cycler (ABI). The PCR products were subjected to agarose gel electrophoresis and visualized with an imaging system. The primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Western blot analysis

Protein lysates of cells were prepared with the RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime). For tissues, samples were first cut into small pieces and homogenized with a homogenizer then subjected to protein extraction with RIPA lysis buffer. The BCA kit (Solarbio) was used to measure the concentration of lysates. Proteins (20 μg) were loaded in each lane of the SDS‐PAGE gel. After electrophoresis, protein transfer to the PVDF membrane, and blocking in 5% nonfat milk, the membrane was sequentially incubated with primary and secondary Abs. The membrane was finally developed with an ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The bands were quantified with ImageJ software. The immunogens for RBX1 and FOXM1 Abs were shared by the protein isoforms of RBX1 and FOXM1, respectively. The information of Abs was as follows: anti‐SNRPB2 (#13512‐1‐AP, Proteintech, 1:1000), anti‐β‐actin (#66009‐1‐Ig, Proteintech, 1:5000), anti‐MCM2 (#10513‐1‐AP, Proteintech, 1:1000), anti‐CDC20 (#AF6456, Beyotime, 1:1000), anti‐MDM4 (#28747‐1‐AP, Proteintech, 1:1000), anti‐RBX1 (#14895‐1‐AP, Proteintech, 1:1000), anti‐FOXM1 (#13147‐1‐AP, Proteintech, 1:1000), HRP‐conjugated goat anti‐rabbit (#SA00001‐2, Proteintech, 1:10000), and HRP‐conjugated goat anti‐mouse (#SA00001‐1, Proteintech, 1:10000).

Cell proliferation, cell invasion, and cell cycle assays

For detection of cell proliferation, cells were seeded in 96‐well plates. On days 0, 1, 2, and 3, 10 μL CCK‐8 solution (Solarbio) was mixed with 90 μL culture medium and maintained for 2 h. After that, the mixture was transferred to a new 96‐well plate and the absorbance at 450 nm was detected by a Microplate Reader to reflect cell proliferation ability.

To detect the invasive capacity of breast cancer cells, Boyden chambers (8 μm pore; BD Falcon) were first precoated with Matrigel (BD Falcon) in the upper chamber of a 24‐well plate. Complete medium was added to the lower chambers then DMEM with 1% FBS was added to the upper chambers. MDA‐MB‐231 or SUM159 cells were seeded in the upper chambers and cultured for 24 h in the incubator. On the next day, the cells in the upper chambers were fixed with paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. The images were captured with a microscope and subjected to cell number counting using ImageJ software.

To detect cell cycle distribution, cells were harvested and then fixed with 70% ethanol overnight at −20°C. Cells were then washed with PBS and stained with propidium iodide (Vazyme) for 30 min. Cells were then analyzed by flow cytometry (Becton Dickinson). The cell distribution of each group was calculated with ModFit LT software.

RNA immunoprecipitation

Radioimmunoprecipitation (RIP) was undertaken with a PureBinding RNA Immunoprecipitation Kit I (Geneseed) following the manufacturer's guidelines. Briefly, the anti‐SNRPB2 Ab was incubated with beads from the kit for 1 h. After that, cells were lysed with lysis buffer from the kit and the lysates were then incubated with Ab‐conjugated beads for an additional 2 h. RNA from the beads was collected using the buffers and spin columns provided by the kit. The concentration of RNA was determined with a NanoDrop 2000 (Invitrogen) and reverse transcribed to first‐stranded cDNA with HiScript II Q RT SuperMix followed by RT‐qPCR.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 8 and presented as mean ± SD. All experiments were repeated three times. Two groups were compared using Student's t‐test and multiple groups were compared with one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's test. p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Aberrant overexpression of spliceosome‐related proteins is a feature of TNBC

To investigate the role of spliceosome‐related proteins in TNBC, we retrieved expression data of basal breast tumors (most of them were TNBC), nonbasal breast tumors, and normal breast tissues from the TCGA‐BRCA cohort (Figure 1A). For 127 spliceosome‐related proteins, it was found that the largest proportion (54 out of 127) were upregulated in the basal group compared with the non‐basal group or normal group (Figure 1B). For example, SNRPB2, a component of the U2 spliceosome, was significantly upregulated in the basal group compared with the nonbasal or normal group (Figure 1C). Furthermore, in the METABRIC dataset, we also observed upregulation of SNRPB2 expression in basal and claudin‐low subtypes (most of them were TNBC) compared with breast tumors of other subtypes or normal breast tissues (Figure S1A). Moreover, by integrating RNA‐seq data from GEO datasets, it was found that many overexpressed spliceosome‐related proteins (14 out of 54) were associated with poor prognosis of basal breast cancer patients, and the prognostic difference between high‐ and low‐risk groups was the most significant according to the expression of SNRPB2 (Figure 1D,E). In the TCGA‐BRCA cohort, significant associations between high expression of SNRPB2 and poor prognosis were also observed (Figure S1B,D). We did not observe different expressions of SNRPB2 in subtypes of TNBC (Figure S1E), suggesting a critical role of SNRPB2 in all TNBC subtypes.

In a panel of breast cancer cell lines and the immortalized breast epithelial cell line MCF10A, it was found that SNRPB2 was overexpressed in all breast cancer cell lines examined compared with MCF10A, especially in TNBC cell lines (Figure 1F). Consistently, in our collected clinical samples, upregulation of SNRPB2 protein levels was detected in most TNBC tumors compared with the adjacent normal tissues (10 of 11) (Figure 1G).

Knockdown of SNRPB2 inhibits proliferation of TNBC cells

To evaluate the function of SNRPB2, we first analyzed a previous genomic‐scale CRISPR screen of breast cancer cells, the data showed that most breast cancer cell lines strongly rely on the expression of SNRPB2, especially MDA‐MB‐231, a TNBC cell line (Figure S2). For validation, we knocked down SNRPB2 in two TNBC cell lines, MDA‐MB‐231 and SUM159 (Figure 2A). The CCK‐8 assay showed that SNRPB2 knockdown strongly suppressed proliferation of MDA‐MB‐231 and SUM159 cells (Figure 2B,C). In addition, Transwell assays revealed that the knockdown of SNRPB2 inhibited the invasion ability of MDA‐MB‐231 and SUM159 cells (Figure 2D,E). Furthermore, using flow cytometry analysis, the data manifested that the proportion of cells in the G0/G1 phase was elevated following SNRPB2 knockdown in MDA‐MB‐231 and SUM159 cells, indicating SNRPB2 was critical for cells to pass the G0/G1 checkpoint (Figure 2F,G). To further investigate the function of SNRPB2 in vivo, we infected MDA‐MB‐231 cells with SNRPB2 shRNAs to downregulate SNRPB2 expression (Figure 2H). In nude mice, it was observed that SNRPB2 knockdown significantly repressed the growth of xenografts (Figure 2I,J), indicating SNRPB2 was critical for tumor formation of TNBC cells.

SNRPB2 activates E2F1 signaling in TNBC cells

To explore the molecular function of SNRPB2, we retrieved RNA‐seq data of SNRPB2 knockdown in MDA‐MB‐231‐LM2, a subline of MDA‐MB‐231 with strong lung metastasis potential. 20 The analysis showed that the expression of many genes was significantly altered following SNRPB2 knockdown in MDA‐MB‐231‐LM2 cells (Figure S3). Following GSEA, the data indicated that SNRPB2 knockdown inactivated several cell cycle‐related pathways, such as E2F_Targets, Mitotic_Spindle, and G2/M_Checkpoint (Figure 3A). Many targets of E2F1 signaling, most of which were also cell cycle regulators, were downregulated following SNRPB2 knockdown according to the RNA‐seq data (Figure 3B). For validation, we detected several E2F1 targets, revealing that CCNA2, MCM2, CDC20, and CDK4 mRNA levels were significantly decreased following SNRPB2 knockdown in both MDA‐MB‐231 and SUM159 cells (Figure 3C). Western blotting further confirmed significant downregulation of MCM2 and CDC20 protein levels following SNRPB2 knockdown in both MDA‐MB‐231 and SUM159 cells (Figure 3D).

SNRPB2 regulates many AS events in TNBC cells

We next investigated the impact of SNRPB2 on AS in TNBC cells. By analyzing RNA‐seq data of SNRPB2 knockdown in MDA‐MB‐231‐LM2, we found that SNRPB2 knockdown induced numerous significantly altered AS events (3004 events) in MDA‐MB‐231‐LM2 cells, most of which were SE (59%) (Figure 4A). The percentage between skipping and inclusion of exons was similar for SE, MXE, and A5SS. A higher percentage of skipping of exons and introns was found for A3SS and IR (Figure 4B). Overall, the siSNRPB2 group showed a higher percentage of exon skipping compared with the control group, with exon skipping of MDM4 ranked as the most significantly altered AS event (Figure 4C). Almost all significantly altered AS events occurred in mRNA (Figure 4D), which was located in the coding sequence region of the transcripts (Figure 4E). According to KEGG enrichment, the genes with significantly altered AS events were involved in several cancer‐related processes, such as ubiquitin‐mediated proteolysis (Figure 4F). Experimental validations with PCR were undertaken to examine the changes in AS events for RBX1 (an E3 ligase involved in the cell cycle) and FOXM1 (a transcriptional activator involved in the cell cycle), 23 , 24 two critical genes for the development of TNBC. Consistent with the rMATS analysis data, the results validated exon skipping of RBX1 and exon inclusion of FOXM1 following SNRPB2 knockdown in both MDA‐MB‐231 and SUM159 cells (Figure 4G,H). By analyzing the RBX1 transcript abundance in the data from the TCGA‐BRCA dataset, it was found that ENST00000216225 (RBX1‐FL, NM_014248) was the most frequently observed isoform and exon 4 skipping would decrease the expression of full‐length RBX1 protein by truncating RBX1 protein in the middle region (Figure 4I). As expected, western blot analysis showed that SNRPB2 knockdown slightly decreased RBX1 protein expression in MDA‐MB‐231 and SUM159 cells (Figure 4J). For FOXM1, ENST00000359643 (FOXM1 C, NM_021953) was the most frequently observed isoform, and exon 6 inclusion shifted FOXM1 B (ENST00000361953, NM_202003) to FOXM1 C, which was reported as a less potent isoform of FOXM1 proteins 25 (Figure 4K). SNRPB2 knockdown slightly increased FOXM1 protein expression in MDA‐MB‐231 and SUM159 cells (Figure 4L). As FOXM1 C is the most abundant isoform of FOXM1 proteins in breast cancer cells (Figure 4K), the western blotting data suggested the elevation of FOXM1 C in TNBC cells following SNRPB2 knockdown. These data indicated that SNRPB2 might promote the expression of oncogenes by controlling alternative splicing in TNBC cells.

SNRPB2 upregulates MDM4 expression by promoting inclusion of exon 6 in MDM4 pre‐mRNA

MDM4 is a well‐characterized oncogene in TNBC that promotes cancer cell proliferation and metastasis. 26 According to the Sashimi plot, it was observed that exon 6 of MDM4 was skipped following SNRPB2 knockdown in MDA‐MB‐231‐LM2 cells which generated MDM4‐S transcript and reduced MDM4‐FL transcript (Figure 5A). Using PCR, we confirmed the skipping of MDM4 exon 6 following SNRPB2 knockdown in both MDA‐MB‐231 and SUM159 cells (Figure 5B). Exon 6 skipping introduced a pretermination signal in MDM4 pre‐mRNA, which produces a short peptide that is unstable in the cells (Figure 5C). 27 Consistently, western blotting showed that MDM4 protein expression was significantly decreased in MDA‐MB‐231 and SUM159 cells with a knockdown of SNRPB2 (Figure 5D). MDM4 destabilized the retinoblastoma (Rb) protein and suppressed p53 activity, which are negative regulators of E2F1 signaling. 28 , 29 We found a significant upregulation of Rb protein expression following SNRPB2 knockdown in MDA‐MB‐231 and SUM159 cells while mutant p53 protein levels were not altered (Figure 5E), indicating an MDM4/Rb/E2F1 axis in driving TNBC cell proliferation. More importantly, using RIP‐qPCR, we showed that SNRPB2 was physically associated with MDM4 mRNA in MDA‐MB‐231 cells (Figure 5F), indicating a direct regulation of exon skipping in MDM4 pre‐mRNA by SNRPB2. Furthermore, in clinical samples, it was observed that Rb protein expression was decreased in most TNBC tumors (10 out of 11) (Figure 5G). In contrast, MDM4 protein expression was elevated in most samples (9 out of 11) (Figure 5G). Considering the upregulation of SNRPB2 in these tumors, the aberrant expression of Rb and MDM4 implies an SNRPB2/MDM4/Rb axis in the clinical setting.

MDM4 is critical for function of SNRPB2 in TNBC cells

To explore whether SNRPB2 exerts its function by regulating MDM4, we overexpressed MDM4 expression in SUM159 cells with a knockdown of SNRPB2 (Figure 6A). The CCK‐8 assay showed that overexpression of MDM4 largely rescued the growth inhibition effect of SNRPB2 si1 (Figure 6B). Moreover, MDM4 overexpression reversed the effects of SNRPB2 on cell invasion and cell cycle progression in SUM159 cells (Figure 6C,D). These data indicated that SNRPB2 mainly relies on regulating MDM4 to promote TNBC progression.

DISCUSSION

Spliceosome‐targeted therapies were promising strategies for cancer patients including patients with TNBC. 13 Further studies are needed to explore critical spliceosome components to develop specific treatments for TNBC. Currently, by analyzing the spliceosome‐related protein expression in clinical samples, our data uncovered that SNRPB2, a U2 small nuclear ribonucleoprotein (snRNP), had significantly higher expression levels in TNBC compared with non‐TNBC and normal breast tissues. SNRPB2 promoted cell proliferation and cell cycle progression in TNBC cells by mediating the inclusion of exon 6 of MDM4 to activate E2F1 signaling (Figure 7). As MDM4 is a well‐known oncogene in various cancer types, 27 we believe the current finding provides valuable knowledge for understanding cancer development.

Schematic of the proposed model. In triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells, aberrant upregulation of SNRPB2 physically binds to MDM4 pre‐mRNA and promotes the inclusion of exon 6 in the transcript. The MDM4 transcript produces MDM4 proteins destabilizing the retinoblastoma (Rb) protein through direct interaction. Loss of Rb releases E2F1 protein and facilitates transcription of E2F1 targets, leading to the progression of TNBC.

The U2 snRNP complex is a major part of the spliceosome. U2SURP has been reported as an upregulated U2 snRNP and could promote TNBC cell proliferation by regulating AS of SAT1, 30 indicating that U2 snRNP is pivotal for TNBC progression. SNRPB2 binds to stem‐loop IV of U2 small nuclear RNA to form the U2 snRNP complex. 31 Here, through bioinformatic analysis of several datasets, we revealed that SNRPB2 was upregulated in TNBC and these proteins were associated with poor prognosis of TNBC patients. The upregulation of SNRPB2 was validated in all TNBC cell lines tested and in almost all TNBC tumors, suggesting its importance in TNBC progression.

MDM4 is a well‐known oncogene and is upregulated in a variety of cancer types including breast cancer. 32 , 33 The upregulation of MDM4 is the consequence of multiple events, including gene amplification and AS. According to the METABRIC dataset, MDM4 amplification is only presented in 10% of basal and claudin‐low subtypes. 33 MDM4 exon 6 skipping converted MDM4‐FL to MDM4‐S, generating MDM4‐S protein, which is unstable and negatively regulates MDM4‐FL expression. 27 In breast cancer cell lines, a significantly negative correlation between MDM4 protein expression and exclusion of MDM4 exon 6 was observed. 34 In the current study, our RNA‐seq data showed that the inclusion of MDM4 exon 6 largely depended on SNRPB2 expression. Knockdown of SNRPB2 induced exclusion of MDM4 exon 6 and subsequent downregulation of MDM4 protein expression in TNBC cells. Previous studies discovered that MDM4 exon 6 skipping was negatively regulated by PRMT5, and PRPF19 and positively regulated by ZMAT3. 35 , 36 , 37 Our finding further revealed a novel spliceosome component that finely tuned the AS of MDM4 pre‐mRNA in TNBC.

The oncogenic function of MDM4 relies on its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity and substrate binding capacity. 38 A previous study found that MDM4 was pivotal for the metastasis of TNBC cells in vitro and in vivo. 39 Here, by RNA‐seq, we observed that E2F1 signaling was the main output of SNRPB2 in TNBC cells. More importantly, we found that SNRPB2 knockdown could upregulate Rb protein expression, a core regulator of E2F1 signaling and substrate of MDM4. 28 As E2F1 targets have been identified as major drivers of TNBC cell proliferation and metastasis, 40 , 41 these results implied that SNRPB2 could activate E2F1 signaling through MDM4 to facilitate cell proliferation and metastasis of TNBC cells.

In conclusion, our data revealed that SNRPB2 upregulated MDM4 protein expression by splicing, setting the stage for using these biomarkers to accurately carry out targeted therapy for TNBC. Further studies on how to use SNRPB2 as a therapeutic target are still needed to combat TNBC.
