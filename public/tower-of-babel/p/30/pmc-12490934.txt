The Case for Autoantibody Screening in Stone Benchtop Workers
Authors: Tiffany Lin, Jun Keng Khoo, Ryan Hoy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOccupational silica exposure is a recognised cause of respiratory diseases such as silicosis, with increasing evidence linking it to systemic autoimmune diseases. We report a case of a 40‐year‐old stone benchtop fabricator diagnosed with complicated silicosis through a screening program. He was initially asymptomatic apart from Raynaud's phenomenon but had an elevated antinuclear antibody (ANA) titre. Over time, he developed progressive autoimmune disease with multisystem involvement and respiratory decline. This case highlights the potential for silica exposure to trigger severe autoimmune disease and the importance of proactive autoimmune screening in high‐risk occupational groups. ANA positivity in otherwise asymptomatic individuals may signal underlying or future autoimmune disease. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for autoimmune conditions in workers exposed to silica, as early detection offers the potential to mitigate long‐term morbidity through earlier intervention.

Introduction

While occupational silica dust exposure is a well‐established risk factor for respiratory disease, its association with autoimmune disease has received comparatively less attention until recent years [1]. We present a case of complicated silicosis diagnosed in a previously asymptomatic patient, who was found to have detectable antinuclear antibodies (ANAs) and later developed multisystem autoimmune disease, resulting in progressive functional impairment.

Case Report

A 40‐year‐old male artificial stone benchtop worker with no prior medical or smoking history attended a statewide silicosis screening program in Victoria, Australia. He was born and educated in Pakistan before working for 9 years in the stone benchtop industry in Australia, where he had not undergone regular medical checkups. He predominantly dry cut high silica content artificial stone. He used respiratory protective equipment 90% of the time in the workshop, initially using a P2 paper mask for the first 3 years, followed by a P3 mask with filter in subsequent years.

Screening chest x‐ray according to International Labour Organisation (ILO) criteria showed scattered nodules in bilateral upper to midzones as well as enlarged hila (ILO classification 1/1). High‐resolution computerised tomography (HRCT) further revealed bilateral upper lobe predominant nodular opacities, coalescing in the right upper lobe up to 15 mm in diameter, with non‐calcified mediastinal lymphadenopathy, consistent with accelerated complicated silicosis (Figure 1). Lung function demonstrated normal spirometry (forced expiratory volume in 1 s 3.5 L [88% predicted], forced vital capacity 4.49 L [93% predicted], forced expiratory ratio 0.70) with moderately impaired diffusing capacity of carbon monoxide (18.30 mL/mmHg/min, 55% predicted).

Bronchoscopy was undertaken to confirm the diagnosis. Bronchial washings in the right upper lobe and endobronchial ultrasound‐guided fine needle aspiration of lymph node stations 4 and 7 revealed no evidence of infection or malignancy. Initial blood tests, including autoimmune screening (Table 1), were notable for ANA titre 1:320 (homogeneous pattern) and mildly elevated gamma‐glutamyl transferase (81 U/L). The patient had no respiratory symptoms, and examination revealed only Raynaud's phenomenon and finger clubbing.

Six months later, he developed symmetrical polyarthritis, gastroesophageal reflux symptoms, and peripheral oedema. On repeat autoimmune screening, ANA titre had increased to 1:640, and anti‐Scl 70 antibodies were detected. Following rheumatology evaluation, he was diagnosed with systemic sclerosis (SSc) and commenced on prednisolone and methotrexate. Transthoracic echocardiography excluded cardiac involvement. He subsequently exited the stone benchtop industry in accordance with medical recommendations, and a workers' compensation claim was accepted.

The patient returned to Pakistan during the COVID‐19 pandemic. He was admitted there to the intensive care unit in the context of acute renal failure. Renal biopsy after return to Australia confirmed myeloperoxidase‐positive ANCA vasculitis (AAV). He received two doses of rituximab, with other immunosuppressive treatments withheld.

From a respiratory perspective, the patient remained clinically and physiologically stable for 3 years. He then developed a dry cough, dyspnoea and a decline in forced vital capacity. Serial chest CTs showed progressive, basal, ground‐glass changes with architectural distortion (Figure 1). Multidisciplinary discussion favoured SSc‐associated non‐specific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP) superimposed on a stable background of upper‐lobe complicated silicosis. Mycophenolate was restarted and optimised, which improved his lung function parameters, but the extent of ground‐glass opacities worsened. Bronchial washings from the right lower lobe excluded bacterial, viral, fungal and tuberculosis infection contributing to the ground‐glass opacities. Cytology demonstrated 2% lymphocytes, but interpretation was limited by the absence of a dedicated bronchoalveolar lavage and possible suboptimal sampling. The patient then received two additional doses of rituximab with subsequent stabilisation of lung function test parameters. However, he continues to experience exertional dyspnoea and arthralgias resulting from manifestations of SSc and AAV.

Discussion

Links between occupational silica dust exposure and systemic autoimmune diseases have recently attracted more attention [1]. The present case study highlights both this important relationship and the utility of autoimmune screening for early disease identification.

With respect to autoimmune disease, current data suggest that silica dust exposure is most strongly linked to SSc, but is also associated with rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus and AAV [1]. Silica‐induced autoimmunity is thought to arise from alveolar macrophage ingestion, where phagocytic activity triggers an inflammatory cascade, ultimately leading to autoantibody production and sustained inflammation [2]. Those with occupational silica dust exposure are estimated to have a threefold increased risk of developing SSc compared to the general population and a dose‐dependent heightened risk of AAV [3, 4].

High silica exposure is an independent risk factor for autoantibody production [5]. A recent study of 1188 stone benchtop workers in the Victorian silicosis screening program showed that detectable autoantibodies without clinical autoimmune features were present in significantly higher rates compared to the general population [5]. This included the detection of elevated ANA (25%, titre > 1:160), as compared to a prevalence of < 6% in a healthy population aged 40–49. Rates of extractable nuclear antigen (4.6%) and rheumatoid factor (2.6%) were also elevated. The likelihood of positive autoantibodies increased with age, smoking, higher silica exposure and silicosis diagnosis. The present case study highlights that the detection of autoantibodies during screening of asymptomatic, high‐risk individuals may foreshadow the later development of SSc and AAV. However, we acknowledge that the natural history of ANA positivity in asymptomatic silica‐exposed individuals is unclear. Longitudinal data are needed to better quantify the proportion who ultimately develop clinically significant autoimmune disease and to guide risk stratification and surveillance strategies.

Active screening of silica‐exposed workers enables early detection of silicosis. In response to the increasing number of silicosis cases identified globally, the work, health and safety regulatory agency in Victoria, Australia, commissioned a free screening programme in May 2019 for all stone benchtop workers. In this patient, the respiratory symptom burden was driven by SSc‐associated NSIP rather than silicosis. This case adds to the body of evidence that silica exposure may predispose individuals to autoimmune diseases with severe pulmonary and extrapulmonary manifestations, in addition to silicosis, the more well‐recognised respiratory sequelae.

This case warrants comparison with Caplan syndrome, originally described in 1953 as pneumoconiotic nodules in coal miners with rheumatoid arthritis [1]. Both conditions reflect the overlap of dust exposure, pneumoconiosis and autoimmunity. Unlike the nodular pattern of Caplan syndrome, however, our patient developed SSc and ANCA‐associated vasculitis with progressive NSIP, demonstrating that silica can trigger a wider range of autoimmune diseases, including more than one condition in the same individual.

Clinicians should be vigilant of silica‐related autoimmune diseases in high‐risk occupational groups. Proactive autoimmune screening in this population is essential, as early ANA detection may indicate underlying autoimmune disease or serve as a predictor of future onset, providing an opportunity for early intervention.
