Evaluation of movable tight oil in different reservoir types in the Central Sichuan region
Authors: Haoqi Liao, Jinchuan Wu, Zhouhua Wang, Huang Liu, Ping Guo, Hanmin Tu, Xiuqing Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTight oil is abundant in the Central Sichuan area. Understanding the differences in tight oil movability among different types of formations is important for the development of tight oil reservoirs in Central Sichuan. The movability of tight oil in the shell limestone, argillaceous shell limestone, and shell shale reservoirs in Central Sichuan was evaluated by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) analyses. The results show that small pores are more common in the argillaceous shell limestone and shell shale, whereas large pores are more evenly distributed in the shell limestone. The average pore size of the core samples is linearly related to the ratio of permeability to porosity. The displacement pressure differential has little effect on the movable oil saturation in shell limestone but has a significant effect on argillaceous shell limestone and shell shale. Movable oil in argillaceous shell limestone is derived from both large and small pores, whereas in shell limestone, it primarily comes from large pores. In low-permeability shell shale, movable oil mainly originates from small pores, whereas in medium- to high-permeability samples, it primarily originates from large pores. The XRD results indicate that a lower clay mineral content and higher calcite content are associated with a higher movable oil saturation.Keywords: Tight oil, Displacement pressure differential, Movable oil saturation, NMR, XRDSubject terms: Crude oil, Natural gas

Introduction

With the rapid expansion of the global economy, the demand for energy has increased significantly. Tight oil, categorized as an unconventional oil and gas source, has increasingly become a focal point in the global exploration and development of unconventional energy resources. Its ability to assist in meeting the world’s energy needs is growing1. Unlike conventional oil and gas resources, tight oil has unique characteristics, such as low reservoir porosity, low permeability, intricate micropore structures, widespread oil source layer distribution, and tight and continuous contact between the reservoir and source rock, among other factors2,3. There is a close relationship between pore structure and movable oil saturation4–8, resulting in significant differences in movable oil saturation between tight reservoirs and conventional reservoirs9.

The evaluation of tight oil movability plays a pivotal role in accurately predicting reserves and production outcomes10,11. Numerous scholars have employed experimental techniques and methods, including mercury intrusion, casting thin sections, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and X-ray CT scanning, to assess tight oil movability12–15. However, each technique has inherent limitations. For example, the constant-speed mercury injection method faces challenges in accurately measuring pore throat radii that are smaller than 0.12 μm16. Obtaining quantitative pore size distribution parameters from cast thin sections and SEM can be difficult17–19. Additionally, CT scanning is not widely accessible because of its high cost. In contrast, NMR technology, which relies on fluid relaxation characteristics, has emerged as a valuable method for accurately characterizing the microstructure of rocks. It stands out as a crucial approach for evaluating fluid movability in tight oil reservoirs20,21. The distribution of the transverse relaxation time (T2) reflects the fluid microdistribution in porous rocks and provides petrophysical information such as porosity, saturation, permeability, and pore size distributions22–27. Different T2 values reflect different pore space sizes; a short T2 corresponds to small pores, and a long T2 corresponds to large pores. Compared with other methods, NMR technology can rapidly, nondestructively, and accurately measure movable oil saturation in rock samples and quantitatively map pore structure characteristics28–32.

The tight oil reservoirs in the Central Sichuan Basin have experienced a complex evolutionary process involving deposition, densification, and subsequent dissolution or structural changes. These reservoirs, which are often oil-bearing and highly heterogeneous, exhibit significant variability in tight oil movability across different reservoir types33–35. Many scholars have evaluated the movability of tight oil in the Central Sichuan Basin by NMR techniques. The reservoir permeability and the extent of intergranular pore development are crucial factors influencing movable oil saturations. Moreover, the movable oil saturation is positively correlated with the pore–throat radius and pore–throat connectivity. Using T2 spectra to analyze the smallest pore-throat radii of movable fluids and the contributions of different pore diameters to their distributions, it has been found that in the tight oil reservoirs of the Central Sichuan Basin, movable fluids are predominantly located in small pore spaces that are governed by pore throats with radii ranging from 0.1 to 0.5 micrometers36,37. Currently, the assessment of tight oil movability in Central Sichuan is in an exploratory stage. There is a lack of quantitative research that evaluates the tight oil movability across different reservoir types, hindering objective evaluations and an accurate understanding of the recovery factor.

In this study, the term ‘reservoir’ refers to a specific lithological reservoir type within the Da’anzhai Member—namely, the shell limestone, argillaceous shell limestone, or shell shale units; through NMR displacement experiments combined with X-ray diffractometer (XRD) analysis, this study quantitatively investigated the correlation between core permeability and the average pore throat radius. The effects of pore structure, displacement pressure differential, and mineral composition on the movable oil saturations in different types of reservoirs in central Sichuan were systematically analyzed, providing a scientific basis and technical support for the efficient development of various reservoir types.

Geological formation

Positioned as a significant oil and gas reservoir in China, the Central Sichuan Basin occupies the northern sector of the “Central Sichuan Gulongzhong oblique and gentle tectonic zone” within the central expanse of the Sichuan Basin. As shown in Fig. 1, the basin is bounded on the east and west by the Huayingshan and Longquan basement faults, respectively. This basin is recognized as the “Central Sichuan Land Core” in the realm of geotectonics. The Da’anzhai section, a primary oil formation in central Sichuan, exemplifies a classic continental inland lake from the Lower Jurassic. The strata within the Da’anzhai section primarily consist of shell limestone, argillaceous shell limestone, shell shale, and mudstone.

Geological map and general stratigraphy of the Da’anzhai section in the Central Sichuan Basin38.

The Da’anzhai section in the Central Sichuan area developed predominantly in shore-shallow lake environments. It is subdivided into three sub-members from top to bottom: Da1, Da2, and Da3. The Da1 and Da3 sub-members are characterized by high-energy shore-shallow lake shell beach deposits, with lithologies dominated by medium-thick bedded shell limestones and shell shales. In contrast, the Da2 sub-member primarily comprises shallow to semi-deep lake mud shale deposits, with lithology mainly consisting of thick-bedded mud shales intercalated with thin layers of argillaceous shell limestone.

Typically, these strata have thicknesses ranging from 60 to 110 m, gradually thinning from east to west. In this study, different types of reservoir cores from the Da’anzhai section were selected to evaluate the movability of tight oil in the central Sichuan area.

NMR measurements

An AniMR-150 NMR instrument was used for core scanning in this experiment. The acquisition parameters were set as follows: 2048 acquisition points, 128 scans, a waiting time of 3000 ms, and a gain of 100. Under consistent NMR conditions, the signal intensity is positively correlated with the number of protons or molecules. Prior to NMR testing, the core samples were degreased to ensure that the NMR signals originated solely from the introduced fluids. Additionally, heavy water was used as the displacing fluid to suppress the water-phase signal, ensuring that the detected signal intensity during the displacement process originated solely from the oil phase. The experimental procedure was as follows: first, the core was saturated with water at a pressure of 4 MPa for 72 h, followed by pore structure testing. The core was subsequently dried and saturated with heavy water. Next, simulated oil was injected at a rate of 0.1 mL/min to displace the heavy water until no more heavy water was produced at the outlet. Finally, heavy water was reinjected under different pressure differentials to displace the oil phase, and NMR scans were conducted at multiple positions along the core until the NMR signals no longer changed.

X-ray diffraction analysis

The experiments were conducted using a Bruker D8AA25 XRD. The instrument parameters were set as follows: step size of 0.0167°, voltage of 40 kV, current of 40 mA, and scanning rate of 0.13369°/s. Small fragments were cut from the experimental cores, crushed, and ground to extract samples of clay minerals with particle sizes less than 10 μm and less than 2 μm. The < 10 μm clay fraction was used to determine the total relative clay mineral content in the original rock, whereas the < 2 μm fraction was used to determine the relative proportions of individual clay mineral types.

Results and discussion

Pore size distribution

According to the microscopic pore–throat classification scheme for shale oil reservoirs42 and considering the characteristics of the tight oil reservoirs in the Central Sichuan Basin, we used a pore size threshold of 0.1 μm to distinguish between large and small pores. Pores larger than 0.1 μm were defined as large pores, whereas those smaller than 0.1 μm were classified as small pores. The NMR scanning results of the water-saturated core samples are shown in Fig. 2. The T2 spectra of the three types of reservoir cores all exhibit bimodal distributions: the first peak corresponds to small pores, and the second peak corresponds to large pores. The proportions of small pores in the shell limestone range from 8.5 to 23.7%; in the argillaceous shell limestone, they range from 48.6% to 73.1%; and in the shell shale, they range from 51.2% to 76.8%. For cores within the same reservoir type, higher permeabilities are associated with lower proportions of small pores.

As the displacement pressure increases, the average mobile oil saturation of the cores gradually increases. Specifically, as the displacement pressure increased from 1 MPa to 4 MPa, the average mobile oil saturations of the argillaceous shell limestone samples increased from 11.6% to 17.3%, representing a 5.7% increase. According to the pore size distribution results (Fig. 2), the argillaceous shell limestone is characterized by well-developed small pores, smaller average pore sizes, and stronger capillary forces. These factors result in lower movable oil saturations and limited variations under different displacement pressure gradients. In contrast, the shell limestone has a greater proportion of large pores and a larger average pore size, leading to higher movable oil saturations even at low pressures, with only slight increases as the pressure gradient increases. The shell shale also contains a significant number of small pores but with a relatively large average pore size, allowing more oil to be displaced as the pressure gradient increases, resulting in a marked increase in the movable oil saturation.

In argillaceous shell limestone reservoirs, the movable oil saturations of the core samples are relatively low (Fig. 5a). When the displacement pressure difference is 1 MPa, the movable oil saturation values of sample 1 (0.0585 mD), sample 2 (0.275 mD), and sample 3 (0.695 mD) are 12.57%, 1.82% and 20.61%, respectively. When the displacement pressure difference increases to 4 MPa, the movable oil saturation values increase by 2.67%, 5.35% and 8.9%, respectively. When the displacement pressure difference increases, the increases in the movable oil saturation values of the medium- and low-permeability cores are not high, but the degree of increase in the movable oil saturation of the high-permeability cores is obvious. In shell limestone reservoirs, the core samples with relatively high permeabilities present relatively high movable oil saturations, whereas those with relatively low permeabilities present relatively low saturations (Fig. 5b). At a displacement pressure of 1 MPa, the movable oil saturation values of Sample 6 (0.0172 mD), Sample 7 (0.519 mD), and Sample 8 (0.614 mD) were 12.21%, 46.18%, and 55.13%, respectively. When the pressure was increased to 4 MPa, the movable oil saturations increased by only 5.14%, 4.8%, and 1.96%, respectively. These results indicate that increasing the displacement pressure has a limited effect on improving the movable oil saturation in shell limestone. In the shell shale reservoirs, when the displacement pressure difference is 1 MPa, the movable oil saturations of sample 11 (0.0575 mD), sample 12 (0.208 mD), and sample 13 (0.806 mD) are 4.26%, 38.17% and 47.09%, respectively; when the difference increases to 4 MPa, the movable oil saturations increase by 24.25%, 10.81% and 14.76%, respectively (Fig. 5c). The movable oil saturations of the shell shale cores are strongly affected by the difference in displacement pressure. Increasing the difference in displacement pressure can greatly improve the movable oil saturation of such reservoirs.

Movable oils with different pore types

Table 5 summarizes the proportions of movable oil in the different types of pores under a displacement pressure difference of 4 MPa. As depicted in Fig. 6, the average movable oil content in the small pores of the argillaceous shell limestone samples is 39.7%, whereas the large pores account for 60.3%. Consequently, the movable oil originates from both small and large pores. In contrast, the average movable oil content in the small pores of the shell limestone samples is 17.9%, whereas the large pores contribute to 82.1% of the movable oil, indicating that the movable oil mainly originates from large pores. In the low-permeability core sample 11 (0.0574 mD), 93.8% of the movable oil is found in small pores, whereas only 6.2% is located in large pores. However, in samples 12 and 13, the average movable oil content in the small pores is 25%, with large pores contributing to 75% of the movable oil.

Proportions of movable oil in different types of pores.

In summary, the origin of movable oil in the core samples is significantly influenced by lithology. In argillaceous shell limestone, the movable oil is derived from both large and small pore systems. The shell limestone primarily hosts the movable oil in large pores. In shell shale, the movable oil in the low-permeability samples mainly originates from small pores (accounting for more than 90%), whereas in the medium- to high-permeability samples, large pores constitute the majority (up to 75%). This finding provides important guidance for developing targeted strategies for different lithologic reservoir types: both large and small pores should be considered in the development of argillaceous shell limestone; shell limestone development should focus on large pore systems; and the development strategies for shell shale should be tailored on the basis of permeability differences.

Influence of reservoir mineral composition on movable oil saturation

The pore structure and mineral composition of a reservoir jointly control the accumulation and mobility of tight oil33,43. The XRD analysis results in Table 6 show that the clay mineral contents of argillaceous shell limestone, shell shale, and shell limestone are 21.7–35.4%, 14.0–17.9%, and 8.0–13.4%, respectively. As shown in Fig. 7a, under a displacement pressure differential of 4 MPa, the movable oil saturation gradually decreases with increasing clay mineral content. This phenomenon is particularly evident in argillaceous shale. The main reasons are that a higher clay mineral content increases the oil adsorption capacity and that clay minerals swell upon hydration, blocking pore throats and thereby reducing oil mobility44. On the other hand, as shown in Fig. 7b, there is a positive correlation between the calcite content and the movable oil saturation. An increase in calcite content improves reservoir brittleness, promotes the development of fractures, and thus enhances fluid flow conditions. Moreover, as the calcite content increases, the oil saturation also increases, leading to improved fluid mobility. It should also be noted that total organic carbon (TOC) content is another important factor influencing movable oil in shale reservoirs. Higher TOC generally increases oil adsorption on kerogen and organic matter, thereby reducing movable oil saturation. Unfortunately, TOC data were not available for the samples analyzed in this study, which is a limitation of our work. Future studies will include TOC measurements to provide a more comprehensive assessment of movable tight oil behavior.

Conclusions

This study systematically evaluated the differences in movable oil saturation and its controlling factors among three types of reservoirs that are present in the Da’anzhai section of central Sichuan. The results indicate that small pores are more common in the argillaceous shell limestone and shell shale, whereas large pores are more common in the shell limestone. Core samples with lower clay mineral contents and higher calcite contents exhibit higher movable oil saturations. The oil mobility in shell shale reservoirs is highly sensitive to the pressure differential, making high-pressure displacement more suitable. In contrast, shell limestone can achieve relatively high movable oil saturations even under low pressures, making it suitable for low-energy development. For argillaceous shell limestone, increasing the permeability through reservoir stimulation is recommended to improve the oil recovery efficiency. This study is the first to compare the movable oil characteristics of different lithological combinations within the Da’anzhai section and expands the understanding of the enrichment mechanisms of tight oil. Future research will further consider the influence of TOC to enhance the completeness and accuracy of the evaluation.
