Evaluation of neo-innominate connection in bilateral bidirectional Glenn patients using computational flow modeling
Authors: Vikas Kannojiya, Neha Zacharia, Gianna Dafflisio, Akshita Sahni, Nicole St Clair, Rahul H. Rathod, Peter E. Hammer, David M. Hoganson, Pedro J. del Nido, Sitaram Emani, Vijay Govindarajan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:Patients with single ventricles undergoing bilateral bidirectional Glenn procedures present unique hemodynamic challenges due to distinct flow collisions compared with patients undergoing the unilateral Fontan. Flow-related changes in such patients may result in greater variation in power loss and hepatic flow differential (%) to branch pulmonary arteries that may result in pulmonary arteriovenous malformations and other Fontan-associated complications. This study aimed to analyze power loss and hepatic flow differential (%), along with its associated hemodynamics in patients undergoing the bilateral bidirectional Glenn under different physiological states and to evaluate the impact of a virtual neo-innominate vein connection on blood flow dynamics.Methods:Thirty patients who underwent the bilateral bidirectional Glenn were retrospectively analyzed, and patient-specific 3-dimensional models created using magnetic resonance imaging data were created. Computational fluid dynamic simulations were performed at rest and exercise states. In patients with significantly unbalanced hepatic flow differential (hepatic flow differential to the left pulmonary artery outside a 35% to 65% range), a virtual (neo-innominate) connection was introduced to assess its impact on overall hemodynamics.Results:Nineteen patients exhibited significantly unbalanced hepatic flow differential with median hepatic flow differential to the left pulmonary artery 24%, which improved to 32% after virtual neo-innominate connection, indicating considerable improvement in several patients, although the overall change was not statistically significant (P = .059). Notably, 37% of these showed substantial improvement after neo-innominate creation, highlighting patient specificity. This was accompanied by nonstatistically significant changes in power loss (P = .14), which remained higher than values typically reported for unilateral Fontan configurations.Conclusions:Our study showed that hemodynamics in patients undergoing the bilateral bidirectional Glenn are highly sensitive to anatomy and flow. A neo-innominate surgical modification can restore balanced hepatic flow differential (%) in select patients without a statistically significant increase in power loss but should be implemented selectively based on patient-specific anatomy and flow.

The Fontan procedure is the final stage of single ventricle palliation, and its success depends on optimal hemodynamics that minimize power loss (PL) and balance hepatic flow differential (HFD) to both lungs.1,2 Unlike patients with a unilateral superior vena cava (SVC), where a single caval inflow interacts with pulmonary artery (PA) flow,3,4 patients with bilateral SVCs pose a unique challenge with bilateral bidirectional Glenn (bBDG) connections creating distinct flow collision mechanisms that can alter both the energetics and HFD% compared with those with unilateral SVCs. This may consequently lead to higher rates of pulmonary arteriovenous malformations and other complications.5–7 An alternative to bBDG is the creation of a neo-innominate vein (NIV) that functionally recreates a single SVC-PA anastomosis.8,9 Given the increased inflow through the single SVC under the NIV configuration, whether NIV offers a hemodynamic advantage over the bBDG Fontan remains to be comprehensively evaluated.

Patient-specific computational fluid dynamics (CFD) has allowed predictions of Fontan hemodynamics based on individual anatomies, while enabling virtual surgical surgeries to optimize flow proposed interventions.10,11 Our recent studies have shown that caval flow patterns significantly influence PL and HFD (%) to the lungs, which are further influenced by anatomic variations and surgical configurations such as lateral tunnel and extracardiac conduit pathways.3,4 Although prior investigations have established the determinants of hemodynamic performance in unilateral SVC Fontan physiology,12–15 limited data exist for patients with bBDG and NIV connections.

We leverage patient-specific CFD to quantify key determinants of Fontan performance, including %HFD, and associated hemodynamic factors, followed by virtual NIV creation to evaluate whether this modification improves flow balance and at what energetic cost. We hypothesize that NIV creation provides more favorable HFD (%) and flow dynamics to certain patients compared with the native bBDG configuration.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Patient Selection and Exclusion Criteria

We conducted a retrospective review of patients who underwent the bBDG followed by Fontan surgery between 2003 and 2023. Patient-specific data were collected from Boston Children’s Hospital and the FORCE database,16 under Institutional Review Board approval (title: Utilizing Patient-Specific Computational Fluid Dynamic Modeling to Optimize Fontan Procedure; IRB-P00031903, approved 4/4/2019), with a waiver of informed consent. Patients were included if they received a bBDG, had available SVC and IVC flow data, and had closed fenestration (Table E1). Patients with evidence of thrombosis, obstructed conduit, baffle leak, single SVC, and interrupted IVC were excluded.

Reconstruction of Patient-Specific Three-Dimensional Model From Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance Imaging

Our patient-specific workflow extended for flow evaluation of Fontan hemodynamics in patients undergoing the bBDG (Figure 1).4,10,13,17 Following our inclusion and exclusion criteria, we selected 30 Fontan patients who had previously undergone the bBDG procedure. The imaging data of these patients were processed to create accurate patient-specific 3-dimensional models using our established protocol that included the Fontan, SVC, IVC, hepatic veins, and PAs.3,4,10,13 Branch vessels were carefully trimmed and elongated by 30 to 70 mm at the inlets and 100 mm at the PA outlets to allow for fully developed inflow ensuring numerical stability during flow modeling.3,4,10 The computational domains were discretized into approximately 1.0 to 1.5 million tetrahedral elements using ANSYS ICEM18 to capture hemodynamic features effectively, as described in our prior Fontan studies.4,17,18

Virtual Surgeries for Neo-Innominate Vein Creation

In the NIV configuration, the left SVC was virtually connected to the right SVC through an end-to-side anastomosis.19 The virtual NIV connection was created in patients with significantly unbalanced HFD, defined as HFD to the LPA outside a hypothetical 35% to 65% range (ie, ±15% from an ideal hepatic balance of 50%), to evaluate whether the introduction of NIV could improve hepatic flow balance. To avoid the geometric interference of the patient’s aorta, the aortic diameter was measured for each patient from their magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan, and the anastomosis was positioned approximately 2 mm superior to the aorta (Figure 1).

Computational Flow Simulations

Patient-specific 3D CFD simulations were carried out in ANSYS Fluent R2021 to assess postoperative hemodynamics in patients undergoing the bBDG.4,10,17 Blood was modeled using a non-Newtonian Quemada model with a density of 1060 kg/m3 to capture the effect of local shear rate and hematocrit that was fixed at 45%.4,17 Velocity calculated from MRI-measured flow rates was directly applied at the SVC and IVC inlets to ensure patient specificity. The hepatic inflow via hepatic veins was assumed to be 25% of the total caval flow based on previous studies20–22 to realistically represent hepatic flow dynamics. At the LPA and RPA outlets, a lumped parameter model was applied to calculate outlet pressures from pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR).3,4 PVR values were tuned for each patient to ensure that CFD-predicted pulmonary flow splits were consistent with cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) reported values within 5%.3,4 This allowed consistency between the clinically measured outflow and the model-predicted outflow. No slip conditions were applied at the Fontan vessel walls that were assumed to be rigid.

Hepatic flow balance was predicted using Lagrangian particle dynamics in which the massless particles were uniformly released at the hepatic inlets, carried with the local flow, and tracked at the LPA and RPA, where their distribution was quantified as the %HFD.4,17,18 The simulations were performed under both baseline MRI conditions (including both anesthetized and nonanesthetized patients) and exercise conditions. For patients scanned under anesthesia, inflows from the superior vena cava (SVC), inferior vena cava (IVC), and hepatic vessels were first scaled by a factor of 1.5 to approximate normal resting conditions.10 To simulate exercise, the SVC and IVC flows were then further increased by 1.5-fold to this adjusted baseline.10 In contrast, for nonanesthetized patients, the measured MRI flows were directly scaled by 1.5 in the SVC and IVC to represent the exercise state.10 We computed PL by accounting for the contributions of static pressure and kinetic energy across all inlets and outlets of the domain.4,17 Flow disturbance and the total energy dissipation within the domain were quantified by vorticity and viscous dissipation rate (VDR).17 Flow-weighted pressure gradients and momentum metrics were computed from CFD simulations as defined in equations 1 to 4 in the Online Data Supplement. Quasi-steady simulations were performed for each patient over a period of 10 seconds using a time step of 0.005 seconds. Convergence was achieved when the residuals for continuity and velocity fell below 10 −6.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted to assess differences between physiological states (baseline vs exercise) and across patient cohorts (bBDG vs NI). Data normality was evaluated with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Depending on the outcome, either a 1-tailed t test was applied for normally distributed data or a Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normal or mixed distributions to compare 2 groups.

RESULTS

A total of 86 patients met the inclusion and exclusion criteria of which 30 patients (20 female and 10 male) with high-quality imaging were suitable for CFD analysis. Five patients (4 male and 1 female) had dextrocardia with a left-sided IVC.

Evaluation of Hepatic Flow Differential Across Physiological States

Our CFD simulations predicted patient-specific variations in %HFD with changes in flow conditions corresponding to the physiological state (Figure 2). Although some patients showed considerable changes in %HFD, overall, the median %HFD to the LPA remained similar in our cohort, with values of 33.5% (IQR, 17–48) and 30% (IQR, 14–55) under baseline and exercise flow conditions, respectively. In our cohort of 30 patients, 11 patients had a balanced HFD (between the range of 35%−65% to the LPA) (Figures 2 and E1). In our prior study that focused on unilateral SVC Fontan cohort3 (n = 30), the median HFD% to the LPA was 35% for extracardiac conduit and 39% for lateral tunnel patients, with 11 extracardiac conduit and 12 lateral tunnel cases demonstrating balanced HFD%. Excluding the 5 patients with dextrocardia and left-sided IVC in the current cohort of 30 bBDG, the median HFD% to the LPA was 30% [IQR, 14.5–43] (baseline) and 27% [IQR, 63–82] (exercise). The 5 dextrocardiac patients demonstrated a reverse streaming of hepatic flow that resulted in higher HFD% to LPA, with median values of 61% [IQR, 51.5–67.5] (baseline) and 77% [IQR, 63–82] (exercise).

HFD (%) to the LPA for the bBDG cohort (n = 30) did not differ significantly between baseline and exercise (1-tailed t test, P = .495). These results suggest that despite patient-specific variations, hepatic flow remained largely consistent across baseline and exercise in our bBDG cohort. Figure E1 shows the HFD (%) at the baseline state for patients with balanced hepatic flow, in whom the neo-innominate virtual surgery was not performed.

Impact of Neo-Innominate Vein Creation on Hepatic Flow Differential

Virtual surgery was performed to introduce an NIV in a subset of patients whose HFD (%) to the LPA was outside a hypothetical 35% to 65% range to evaluate the impact of a NIV connection on HFD in patients undergoing the bBDG (Figure 2). With this sub-criterion, 19 patients underwent virtual NIV connection, including 1 patient with dextrocardia. Our model predicted changes in HFD (%) in these patients under both baseline and exercise conditions (Figures 3 and E2). NIV substantially improved %HFD (improvement of at least 20%) in 7 patients (patients 1, 6, 8, 9, 14, 17, and 19; ie, 37%), which remained consistent under exercise conditions as well (Figure E4). Patient 13 also showed improvement, although the HFD overshot the balanced range (35%−65%). The median HFD (%) to the LPA for bBDG patients (n = 19) at the baseline state was 24 [IQR, 7–33], which improved to 32 [IQR, 18–46] after the creation of the NIV. Although some patients showed substantial individual improvements, the differences did not reach statistical significance at the group level (Mann–Whitney U test between bBDG and NIV patients: baseline, P = .059; exercise, P = .13), highlighting interpatient variability in the hemodynamic response to the NIV connection.

Analysis of Predictors of Hepatic Flow Differential Improvement After Neo-Innominate Vein Creation

Pressure- and momentum-based analyses were performed to identify flow-based predictors associated with improvement in HFD (%) post-virtual NI.

Pressure-based predictors.

Pressure gradients across the superior cavopulmonary pathways were quantified after NIV creation (n = 19). Benefited patients showed a significantly lower median neo-innominate LPA pressure gradient, measuring 3.2 mm Hg compared with 5.84 mm Hg in nonbenefited patients (Figure 4 and Table E2). This finding suggests that reduced left-sided cavopulmonary resistance (neo-innominate LPA) after neo-innominate creation is associated with improvement in HFD%. In contrast, no significant difference in the right superior cavopulmonary pressure gradient (RSVC-RPA) was observed between benefited and nonbenefited patients.

To assess whether this relationship extended to the global Fontan circulation, flow-weighted pressure gradients across the entire Fontan conduit were also examined. Benefited patients demonstrated a lower median Fontan pressure gradient compared with nonbenefited patients (2.69 vs 4.16 mm Hg); however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Table E3 and Figure E3).

Momentum-based predictors.

To further evaluate flow-based predictors of HFD improvement, a momentum analysis was performed. Figure 5 summarizes momentum-based comparisons between benefited and nonbenefited patients across specific inflow and outflow regions, as well as at the global Fontan level. At the superior venous inflows, benefited patients demonstrated lower median momentum in both the LSVC (128.26 vs 145.75 kg/m2.s) and the RSVC (119.78 vs 180.73 kg/m2.s) compared with nonbenefited patients, although these differences were not statistically significant (Figure 5, A). Although superior cavopulmonary metrics showed no group-level differences, inferior venous inflow clearly distinguished the cohorts, with benefited patients exhibiting a significantly higher median IVC momentum (279.84 vs 123.49 kg/m2.s; Figure 5, B). This represented the only inflow region demonstrating a statistically significant difference between groups. Momentum associated with the hepatic veins showed smaller intergroup differences (109.18 vs 90.45 kg/m2.s) that were not statistically significant.

At the PA outlets, benefited patients demonstrated lower median momentum at the LPA (386.89 vs 535.14 kg/m2.s) and comparable momentum at the RPA (391.98 vs 403.41 kg/m2.s) relative to nonbenefited patients, with no statistically significant differences observed (Figure 5, C). Likewise, global momentum integrated over the Fontan volume tended to be higher in nonbenefited patients, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (Figure 5, D).

Quantitative Comparison of Power Loss Variations Pre- and Post-Virtual Neo-Innominate Vein Connection

NIV patients showed marginally higher PL than bBDG patients, with median power approximately 1.12-fold higher at baseline and approximately 1.53-fold higher under exercise (Figure 6). This pattern of marginal higher power loss (PL) was observed across most patients (Figure E4). Despite variability in PL among individual patients, differences between the bBDG and NIV cohorts were not statistically significant at either baseline or exercise state (Mann–Whitney test: baseline P = .14; exercise P = .14, Figure 6). The transition from baseline to exercise resulted in a significant increase in PL, with median values increasing 3.15-fold in bBDG patients (P = .0001) and 4.33-fold post-NIV (P = .0001; Figures 6 and E7).

Figure E6 presents the PL value and pressure gradients for the remaining 11 patients, and the PL trends for the full initial cohort of 30 bBDG patients (before NIV connection) are summarized in Figure E7.

Impact of Neo-Innominate Connection on the Flow Disturbance in Fontan

Vorticity and VDR were used to quantify local flow disturbances contributing to PL4,17 (Figure 7). Our model predicted regions of elevated vorticity and VDR near the anastomosis after NIV creation (Figure E8), corresponding to the modest increase in these hemodynamic metrics observed after NIV connection (Figure 7). This explains the marginally higher PL in the NIV cohort (Figure 6). Although both bBDG and NIV had a significant increase in vorticity and VDR as flow increased from baseline to exercise (vorticity P = .000078, VDR P = .0001) as well as NIV patients (vorticity P = .0001, VDR P = .00001; Figure 7), there were no significant differences in vorticity or VDR magnitudes between the bBDG and NIV cohorts at baseline or exercise state (t test; vorticity: P = .31, VDR: P = .24 at baseline and P = .16, VDR: P = .053 at exercise) (Figure 7).

DISCUSSION

Fontan outcomes are strongly influenced by patient-specific anatomy and flow patterns, because variations in vascular geometry can significantly alter hemodynamic efficiency and hepatic flow distribution. In this study, we examined how the bBDG anatomy affects Fontan flow due to the collision of its 2 caval inflows within the pulmonary arteries. We also assessed whether NIV connections could improve HFD balance and reduce energy loss in a subset of patients.

Our analysis showed that 63% (19 patients) of the bBDG patients (n = 30) have unbalanced HFD% (Figure 2). Virtual NIV reconstructions were performed to evaluate whether NIV could improve HFD in these patients and to assess the resulting impact on overall Fontan hemodynamics. Previous studies have explored the NIV connection8,9,23 as a potential surgical intervention in bBDG patients, with Talwar and colleagues8,9 providing a surgical precedent for creating an innominate conduit in bilateral SVC anatomy. Predictions from our cohort showed that NIV restored hepatic flow balance in 37% of patients, highlighting that its success in restoring HFD can be highly dependent on each patient’s anatomy and flow conditions (Figures 3 and E2). Thus, the decision to include an NIV is best guided by patient-specific evaluation of its hemodynamic benefit, considering anatomy, flow conditions, surgical complexity, and risk. Although modeling is not typically performed at the bidirectional Glenn stage, it may be especially useful for bilateral SVC patients, both early (4–6 months) and later in life, to guide revisions such as taking down the left SVC bidirectional Glenn and implementing an NIV connection.

Improvement in HFD after NIV creation was consistently associated with 2 complementary hemodynamic features: a lower left superior cavopulmonary (NI–LPA) pressure gradient and elevated IVC momentum (Figures 4, 5, and E3; Tables E2 and E3), identifying these quantities as key flow-based predictors of benefit. These 2 hemodynamic features might act in a complementary and synergistic manner. A lower NI–LPA pressure gradient can potentially reduce left–right resistance asymmetry in the superior pathway, preventing SVC inflow from being preferentially flowing to a single PA. Concurrently, elevated IVC momentum can strengthen the hepatic flow in the Fontan pathway, enabling it to more effectively interact and collide with the superior venous streams at the Fontan confluence. This combined effect may promote enhanced mixing resulting in a more symmetric hepatic flow to the PAs, explaining why these 2 predictors act synergistically to improve HFD.

Patient-specific computational modeling offers the opportunity to evolve from isolated hemodynamic assessment toward an integrated, feasibility-aware surgical planning framework for complex cavopulmonary anatomies. For example, in patients with bBDG, especially those with HLHS and neo-aorta, surgical decision-making is constrained by adjacent anatomy, conduit routing, and anastomotic tension, all of which must be assessed before hemodynamic optimization is considered. Three-dimensional anatomic modeling that incorporates surrounding structures24 may enable preoperative evaluation of whether a NIV connection is spatially feasible, whereas complementary structural or finite element analyses could be used to evaluate anastomotic tension or conduit kinking.25 Within such a multi-tool workflow, patient-specific CFD such as those used in our study would then serve to quantify the hemodynamics of a surgically feasible NIV configuration, including HFD and energy loss. This integrated approach has the potential to support intraoperative judgment by providing quantitative insight into tradeoffs among competing surgical options.

Cavopulmonary streaming can differ fundamentally in patients with dextrocardia and a left-sided IVC. In our cohort, 5 patients exhibited markedly higher HFD% to the LPA (61% under baseline and 77% under exercise) and slightly lower PL elevation during exercise compared with nondextrocardiac bBDG patients, underscoring how left-sided IVC routing and midline crossing may alter HFD and flow collisions. These predictions suggest that dextrocardiac bBDG patients may generate distinct inflow angles, helical flow structures, and collision dynamics that should be considered during intervention. Because our sample of dextrocardiac patients was small (n = 5), expanded analysis in a larger cohort, including systematic evaluation of IVC course, atrial position, and caval-PA alignment, is needed to delineate these mechanistic differences and determine their implications for surgical planning and long-term Fontan success.

As explored in this study, patient-specific computational simulations can provide a quantitative framework to evaluate how NIV placement may affect hemodynamic efficiency and HFD (%) before initial surgery or subsequent interventions. Our study showed that baseline PL (Figure E4) in the bBDG cohort (median 17.4 mW) was substantially higher than reported in previous unilateral SVC studies.4,26 After NIV reconstruction, LSVC flow is redirected through the NI, altering the collision dynamics at the Fontan-PA anastomosis potentially influencing PL.12 However, the PL loss after NIV connection did not change significantly in our cohort, suggesting that redistribution of flow through the NIV does not impose a substantial additional energetic burden. The marginal changes observed in flow metrics (vorticity and VDR) likely reflect localized alterations in flow collision without affecting overall hemodynamic efficiency (Figures E4 and E8). Notably, several patients exhibited substantial pre- to post-NIV variation in PL (Figures E4 and E5), underscoring the strong influence of individual anatomy and flow conditions. Taken together, our data suggests that NIV connection can potentially result in favorable HFD% in patients without significantly increasing the PL, but patient specificity should be considered.

Our data indicate that bBDG hemodynamics appear to be more energy demanding than those of the unilateral SVC4,26 but are equivalent to those of young I-IVC patients.17 Clinically, this is consistent with outcomes showing bBDG patients often maintain preserved short-term function but experience a higher long-term hemodynamic burden.27 ,28 Thus, we have focused on evaluating energetics under different flow conditions to predict the robustness of the surgical strategy.29 Staying consistent with this notion, evaluating bBDG and NIV under baseline and exercise conditions revealed that transitioning from baseline to exercise triggered a marked increase in PL (Figures 6 and E5), highlighting the sensitivity of these circulations to physiological stress. These findings underscore the importance of assessing hemodynamics across physiological and stress conditions, because they provide critical insight into robustness of surgical strategies and may help identify patients at risk for compromised Fontan performance during everyday exertion.

Limitations

Our study has the following limitations. All simulations were performed under the assumption of rigid vessel walls, which neglects the effects of vascular compliance. Although previous studies30 have reported that such assumptions minimally affect overall HFD (%) and PL, they may impact local flow near junctions. Furthermore, we used steady-state flow conditions that do not capture transient variations such as those caused by respiration, which could influence instantaneous pressure and velocity fields. Future studies incorporating transient flow profiles that represent both rest and exercise conditions will help improve simulation fidelity. Another limitation of the present study is the absence of direct in vivo or clinical outcome validation. To this end, multiple validation efforts are currently underway, including in vitro experimental validation, 4-dimensional flow CMR-based validation of hepatic flow distribution, and longitudinal clinical validation using postoperative follow-up data. These ongoing efforts are intended to establish the predictability of our computational framework and support its progression toward prospective surgical decision support. Finally, we could not identify consistent anatomic predictors of benefit within this cohort. It is thus challenging to rely on standard imaging features alone to determine which patients are most likely to benefit from an NIV connection, underscoring the need for integrated patient-specific hemodynamic modeling-based assessment. Future work will extend this analysis to a larger patient cohort and include additional geometric parameters to evaluate their influence on Fontan hemodynamics and identify optimal surgical configurations.

CONCLUSIONS

This study provides patient-specific insights into how bBDG influences Fontan hemodynamics, recognizing that the collision of 2 caval inflows creates distinct flow patterns that affect Fontan success. Using computational modeling, we evaluated PL, HFD (%), vorticity, and VDR across baseline and exercise states, showing that bBDG patients exhibit higher PL compared with previously reported uni-lateral SVC cohorts.4,17 Virtual NIV connections demonstrated patient-specific benefits, improving HFD (%) in select complex cases without significantly increasing PL, although localized increases in vorticity and VDR were observed near the anastomosis regions. Our findings underscore the importance of patient-specific modeling in assessing and personalizing surgical strategies for bBDG patients, highlighting the potential for case-specific improvements through NIV connections.
