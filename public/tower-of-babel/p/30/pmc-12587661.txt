Association between serum uric acid levels and primary aldosteronism
Authors: Xuying Zhao, Hui Jiao, Rongping Zhang, Hui Zhao, Limin Ma, Zhongzheng Jia, Zhuqi Tang, Yunjuan Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary aldosteronism (PA), the most common cause of secondary hypertension, is associated with increased cardiovascular risks compared to essential hypertension (EH). Although PA is frequently associated with metabolic abnormalities, the role of serum uric acid (SUA) remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate SUA as a potential biomarker for distinguishing PA from EH.MethodsA retrospective analysis of 152 PA patients and 280 age-matched EH patients hospitalized between January 2019 and February 2024 was conducted. Biochemical and clinical parameters, including SUA, aldosterone-renin ratio (ARR), potassium, and metabolic markers, were analyzed. Multivariate logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses assessed SUA’s diagnostic accuracy for PA. Subgroup analyses were conducted by sex and menopausal status. The disease severity of PA patients was further evaluated using the Primary Aldosteronism Severity Classification (PASC).ResultsPA patients exhibited significantly lower SUA levels compared to EH (P < 0.001), independent of age, BMI, and renal function. SUA levels showed a significant inverse correlation with PASC severity grades (Spearman’s rho = -0.42, P < 0.001) and no significant difference was observed between APA and IHA subtypes. SUA ≤ 361 µmol/L was strongly associated with PA, with an ROC AUC of 0.750. The ROC curve analysis revealed an AUC of 0.766 for SUA in screening PA among males (optimal cut-off: 364 µmol/L). For premenopausal female patients, the AUC was 0.725 (cut-off: 325 µmol/L), and for postmenopausal females, the AUC was 0.764 (cut-off: 358 µmol/L). The diagnostic performance of SUA surpassed that of the PFK score (AUC: 0.732).ConclusionReduced SUA levels are independently associated with PA, suggesting its potential utility as an auxiliary biomarker to raise suspicion for PA, particularly in settings with limited access to ARR testing. Prospective studies are required to validate its clinical relevance and mechanistic basis.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04504-6.

Introduction

Primary aldosteronism (PA), which is characterised by the excessive secretion of aldosterone, is the leading cause of secondary hypertension. It is also associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease compared to essential hypertension (EH) [1, 2]. The prevalence of PA increased with the severity of hypertension [3–5]. Compared to individuals with essential hypertension (EH) and a similar degree of blood pressure elevation, patients with PA have higher risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events, as well as adverse effects on renal function [6, 7]. Elevated aldosterone activates mineralocorticoid receptors (MR) in renal tubules, inducing sodium retention and potassium wasting. MR overstimulation also triggers proximal tubule injury [8], potentially enhancing urate excretion through impaired UA reabsorption transporters [9, 10]. However, the SUA-metabolic relationship exhibits subtype-specific patterns: KCNJ5-mutant aldosterone-producing adenomas (APA) demonstrate fewer metabolic derangements versus obesity-driven idiopathic hyperaldosteronism (IHA) [11, 12]. PA can be managed through surgery or the use of spirolactone, an aldosterone inhibitor. Hence, establishing an accurate differential diagnosis is crucial before patients develop resistant hypertension and other severe complications. Current guidelines recommend the use of the aldosterone/renin ratio (ARR) as a screening indicator for PA [1]. However, many hospitals face technical limitations that hinder the determination of ARR, leading to low rates of diagnosis and treatment. To address this challenge, endocrinologists have endeavored to develop predictive models for diagnosing PA using clinical and easily accessible biochemical features.

Metabolic syndrome is a concern for endocrinologists, and several clinical studies have found that metabolic abnormalities such as hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), and abdominal obesity are more frequent in patients with PA than in those with EH [13–15]. However, some studies have derived opposing conclusions [16–18]. Moreover, ethnic differences in study populations, heterogeneity in the diagnosis of metabolic abnormalities, and selection bias may influence the results. Consequently, the utility of metabolic abnormalities aforementioned as indicators of PA remains controversial. In addition to these common markers of glucose and lipid metabolism, hyperuricemia is frequently present in patients with metabolic syndrome [19, 20]. Abnormal serum uric acid (SUA) levels are closely related to hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and coronary heart disease [21]. Despite this, few studies have investigated SUA metabolism in patients with PA, and further investigation is required to establish the usefulness of SUA levels as an independent clinical indicator for guiding the diagnosis of PA. In this retrospective study, we aimed to compare metabolic markers between patients with PA and those with EH and investigate the independent association between SUA and PA.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective study included 614 hypertensive patients screened for secondary causes, who were hospitalized at the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University between January 2019 and February 2024. After excluding 182 patients per predefined criteria (other secondary hypertension, n = 91; medication interference, n = 47; comorbidities, n = 44), 152 PA and 280 EH patients underwent 1:1 age-matching via propensity score matching. Age was prioritized as the matching variable due to its dominant confounding effect on SUA metabolism. The primary factors prompting these patients’ consultations were the early onset of hypertension, refractoriness to conventional antihypertensive treatments, spontaneous hypokalemia or adrenal incidentalomas, and all were slated for secondary hypertension screening. The inclusion criteria were as follows. Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) age 18–75 years; (2) discontinuation of β-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and ARBs ≥ 2 weeks prior to screening; (3) confirmation of PA per 2016 Endocrine Society guidelines [1]. Screening for PA was based on a cutoff value of >3.8 for the upright plasma aldosterone concentration (PAC) (ng/dl)/direct renin concentration (DRC) (ng/L) ratio (ARR) in the presence of an aldosterone level of >15 ng/dl and suppressed DRC. Then, the diagnosis was confirmed if the saline infusion test (SIT) failed to suppress aldosterone to < 10 ng/dl. Fourthly, the term “patients with EH” refers to individuals characterized by a systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg and/or a diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg. To exclude other secondary hypertension, all subjects underwent comprehensive screening tests. Patients with recent infections, surgery, trauma, severe cardiopulmonary impairment, or chronic liver diseases; those with other types of secondary hypertension that had been clearly identified, such as renal parenchymal hypertension, pheochromocytoma, and Cushing’s disease; and those with a history of long-term or ongoing intake of potassium chloride or non-antihypertensive drugs affecting the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, such as glucocorticoids and contraceptives, were excluded. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University. All participating patients provided written informed consent for the utilization of their clinical data in scientific research. The flowchart of this study is shown in Fig. 1.

Subtype classification of primary aldosteronism

The subtype classification of PA into aldosterone-producing adenoma (APA) and idiopathic hyperaldosteronism (IHA) was based on a combination of adrenal venous sampling (AVS) findings, postoperative pathological confirmation, and clinical and biochemical outcomes after adrenalectomy, in accordance with the Endocrine Society guidelines [22]. Patients with confirmed unilateral aldosterone excess on AVS who subsequently underwent unilateral adrenalectomy and showed pathological confirmation of adenoma along with complete clinical and biochemical success (normalization of aldosterone-to-renin ratio and potassium levels, and improvement in hypertension) were classified as APA. Those with bilateral aldosterone hypersecretion on AVS or those who did not meet surgical criteria were classified as IHA [23, 24]. AVS was performed using sequential catheterization under cosyntropin stimulation, and lateralization was defined as a lateralization index ≥ 4 [22, 25].

Assessment of primary aldosteronism severity

The severity of primary aldosteronism was assessed using the Primary Aldosteronism Severity Classification (PASC) as recently proposed by Murakami et al. [26]. This classification integrates three key clinical and biochemical parameters at the time of diagnosis: serum potassium concentration, basal plasma aldosterone concentration (PAC), and blood pressure control status. Each parameter is scored, and the sum of these scores (ranging from 3 to 9) defines the overall PASC grade: a total score of 3–4 indicates Mild grade, 5–7 indicates Moderate grade, and 8–9 indicates Severe grade. This severity stratification was applied to our PA cohort to explore its relationship with serum uric acid levels.

Clinical measurements

Anthropometric, hemodynamic, and metabolic parameters were recorded. The body mass index (BMI) was calculated as the weight divided by the square of the height (kg/m2). Fasting blood glucose (FBG), total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), creatinine, and SUA levels were determined using a fully automatic biochemical analyzer (Hitachi 7600-020, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the following formula: 2374.78 × creatinine − 0.54753 × age − 0.25011(×0.8526126 for female patients). Urine samples collected in the morning were used to measure the urine pH and specific gravity.

Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricular wall thickness (LVPWT), interventricular septal thickness (IVST) and left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD) were measured by color Doppler ultrasonography. Left ventricular mass index (LVMI) was calculated according to Devereux formula: LVMI = left ventricular mass/body surface area; Left ventricular mass = 0.8 × 1.04×([LVEDD + IVST + LVPWT)3-LVEDD3] + 0.6; Body surface area (m2) = 0.0061× height (cm) + 0.0124× weight (kg)-0.0099 [27]. The criteria for diagnosing left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) were defined as follows: LVMI ≥ 115(g/m2) in men, ≥ 95(g/m2) in women [28].

After awakening in the morning, participants maintained a non-reclining posture for a minimum of two hours, followed by a 5–15 min seating period. Subsequently, blood samples were collected for hormone assays. PAC, DRC, and angiotensin II (ANG II) levels were measured by radioimmunoassays.

Finally, the PFK (urine pH, female sex, and serum K+) score, which is a parameter recommended for PA screening by Japanese researchers [29], was determined. A total of 3 points, 1 for female sex, 1 for a potassium level of < 3.5 mmol/L, and 1 for a urine pH of ≥ 7.0, was assigned as applicable.

Statistical analysis

We performed normality tests on continuous variables. Measurement data that met the normality distribution were analyzed using independent sample t-tests with homogeneity correction and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\stackrel{-}{x}\:$$\end{document}± SD). Data that did not meet the normality distribution are presented as M (P25, P75), where M represents the Median, and P25 and P75 represent the 25th and 75th Percentiles (interquartile range), respectively, and were analyzed using rank sum tests for intergroup comparisons. Categorical variables were analyzed using χ² tests with continuity correction and are presented as percentages. Given the well-established sexual dimorphism in serum uric acid levels, all primary analyses were stratified by sex to avoid confounding. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to determine specific factors that could be related to SUA levels. The accuracy of SUA screening for PA was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), and SUA levels were stratified according to the best cutoff value. Univariate logistic regression analysis was used to analyze the relationship between different levels of SUA and PA, and multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to detect independent correlations between different SUA levels and PA, with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) calculated. Analyses used SPSS 26.0, with P < 0.05 considered significant.

Results

Among the 152 patients in the PA group, 106 presented with APA, while 46 presented with IHA. Consistent with the pathophysiological features of PA, patients demonstrated characteristic biochemical abnormalities, including significantly increased PAC and ARR, decreased DRC, and lower serum potassium levels compared to the EH group, and these differences remained highly significant (P < 0.001) in both males and females upon stratified analysis (Table 1). PA patients also exhibited elevated urine pH and decreased urine specific gravity. Notably, DB) was significantly higher in the PA group, a finding that was consistent across both sexes. Stratification by sex confirmed that the key metabolic distinction of significantly lower SUA levels in PA patients was unequivocally present in both males and females (P < 0.001 for both, Table 1; Fig. 2A, B). FPG was also significantly lower in the PA group within each sex stratum. The decrease in urine specific gravity in the PA group further supports the mechanism of impaired proximal tubular function, potentially leading to enhanced uric acid excretion. No significant differences were observed in age, duration of hypertension, SBP, angiotensin II levels, lipid profiles, creatinine levels, eGFR, or echocardiographic parameters (LVEF, LVH, LVMI) between PA and EH patients within each gender subgroup (Table 1). Correlation analysis results showed that SUA was significantly positively correlated with BMI, DBP, DRC and creatinine levels, and significantly negatively correlated with female, ARR, PFK score, eGFR and HDL-C (Table S1). Multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to investigate the independent role of SUA in PA. Among 152 patients with PA (APA = 53, IHA = 99): APA SUA was 322.42 ± 43.95 µmol/L, and IHA SUA was 321.63 ± 52.48 µmol/L, both significantly lower than the EH group (369.3 ± 51.0 µmol/L), with no statistical difference between subtypes (Table 2). To further investigate the relationship between SUA and disease severity, we stratified the PA cohort according to the novel PASC. Based on serum potassium, aldosterone levels, and blood pressure control, 13.8% (21/152), 63.2% (96/152), and 23.0% (35/152) of patients were classified into mild, moderate, and severe PASC grades, respectively. As anticipated, the defining biochemical and hemodynamic parameters differed significantly across the severity spectrum (Table 3). Notably, serum uric acid levels exhibited a progressive and significant decrease with increasing PASC severity (median SUA: 355 µmol/L in mild, 325 µmol/L in moderate, and 280 µmol/L in severe grade; p for trend < 0.001). This inverse relationship was further confirmed by a significant negative correlation between SUA levels and the ordinal PASC grade (Spearman’s rho = -0.42, P < 0.001; Table Y). Consistent with the PASC construct, the proportion of APA subtypes was highest in the severe group (88.6%). Additionally, SUA demonstrated strong positive correlation with serum potassium (rho = + 0.55, P < 0.001) and negative correlations with systolic and diastolic blood pressure, but no significant correlation with plasma aldosterone concentration itself (Table 4).

As illustrated in Table 5, a significant correlation between SUA and PA persisted after adjusting for relevant variables. ROC curve was employed to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of SUA screening for PA, and the screening efficiency of SUA was compared to that of the PFK score. Although IHA-specific SUA analysis was precluded by limited samples, APA preserved diagnostic accuracy versus full cohort. As presented in Fig. 3 and Table S2, the AUC of SUA screening for PA was 0.750, with a specificity of 76.3% and a sensitivity of 63.9%, using an optimal cut-off value of 361 µmol/L. In comparison, the ROC curve for the PFK score yielded an area of 0.732, with a specificity of 79.6%, a sensitivity of 53.3%, and a best cut-off value of 1 point. Univariate regression analysis demonstrated that SUA levels ≤ 361.0 µmol/L were associated with PA (P < 0.001). To further address potential sex-specific effects, we performed stratified multivariate logistic regression analyses by gender. After adjusting for age, body mass index, diastolic blood pressure, total cholesterol, and fasting plasma glucose, the inverse association between SUA levels and PA remained statistically significant in both males and females. Every 10 µmol/L decrease in SUA was associated with a 5% increase in the odds of PA in males (OR 0.95, 95% CI 0.93–0.98) and a 3% increase in females (OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.95–0.99). The results of the sex-stratified analysis are presented in Table 6. Serum potassium-SUA correlation was nonsignificant, suggesting SUA reduction in PA is independent of hypokalemia. Given the strong correlation of SUA with PFK scores and gender-related factors within the PFK scores, both SUA and PFK scores were included in the multifactorial regression model. The results indicated that, even after controlling for the influence of PFK score, SUA levels ≤ 361.0 µmol/L remained significantly associated with PA (Table 7). We further explored these associations within sex-specific subgroups. The inverse association between low SUA and PA remained strong and significant in both males (adjusted OR 4.50) and females (adjusted OR 4.10). The association between higher PFK scores and PA was also consistent in the female subgroup. In males, who can only have a PFK score of 0 or 1 (as they do not score for ‘female sex’), a score of 1 (indicating hypokalemia and/or high urine pH) was also significantly associated with PA (adjusted OR 2.85). Detailed results of the sex-stratified analysis are provided in Table 8.

Discussion

To our knowledge, researchers have sought to develop predictive algorithms that incorporates a blend of clinical symptoms and/or laboratory findings to enhance the feasibility of predicting PA in healthcare facilities where plasma aldosterone and renin levels are not readily assessable. Yamashita et al. utilized data from 130 untreated hypertensive Japanese patients to construct a predictive scoring system, known as the PFK score, for PA [24]. A similar predictive score, dubbed the PSS, was developed based on data gathered from the Thai population [30]. The PFK score amalgamates factors such as urine pH, female gender, and serum potassium, while the PSS score considers age, Body Mass Index (BMI), diabetes, antihypertensive medication, and serum sodium and potassium. Notably, Wang’s nomogram for predicting PA also employed sex, serum potassium, serum calcium–phosphorus product and urine pH as predictors [31]. Despite ethnic and regional variations, these scoring methods share contentious elements like BMI and diabetes [16, 18, 32, 33]. Intriguingly, across numerous studies encompassing diverse regions and populations, SUA level discrepancies between PA and EH patients were observed to be alike [34–36], PA patients exhibited significantly lower SUA levels than EH. In accordance with the findings of this study, Nanbo et al. [35] observed that elevated uric acid levels follow adrenalectomy. This leads to aldosterone excess, proximal tubule damage, URAT1 inhibition and subsequent uricosuria. Due to inherent disparities in SUA levels among different sexes and between premenopausal and postmenopausal women [37, 38], we conducted a stratified analysis and found that the difference in SUA levels between PA and EH still existed within the subgroups. Additionally, studies have shown that patients with obesity-related IHA have a higher incidence of SUA than those with APA [11]. The reduced incidence of SUA in this cohort may be associated with the predominance of APA (accounting for 70% of PA). The multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed a significant, independent, inverse relationship between SUA levels and PA. As per the ROC curve analysis, SUA proved to be a beneficial screening parameter for PA. An additional multivariate logistic regression analysis inferred that lowered SUA levels correlated with PA independent of standard clinical features.

Elevated SUA levels are commonly observed in patients with metabolic syndrome and are widely accepted as risk factors for hypertension, gout, NAFLD, CKD, and cardiovascular diseases [39, 40]. In the present study, FPG levels were higher in EH patients than in PA patients. Nevertheless, a correlation analysis revealed no significant association between SUA levels and FPG levels. The underlying factors contributing to the disparity in SUA between the PA and EH groups necessitate further investigation. A previous case report demonstrated that a patient with PA exhibited elevated levels of uric acid excretion and urinary uric acid, which returned to normal following treatment with spironolactone [8]. Elevated aldosterone levels in PA patients contribute to water-sodium retention and negative feedback inhibition of renin, thereby effectively reflecting blood volume and renal perfusion [41, 42]. Sim et al. observed a gradual and significant decrease in serum uric acid levels as renin levels decreased, while eGFR exhibited an increasing trend [43]. It is speculated that the reduction in serum uric acid levels among PA patients may be attributed to an increase in blood volume, renal perfusion, and uric acid excretion rate resulting from elevated plasma aldosterone concentrations. Notably, urinary uric acid excretion is significantly higher in individuals with PA. A study revealed that after tumor resection and subsequent decrease in serum aldosterone levels, urinary uric acid declined while serum uric acid increased in patients with aldosterone-producing adenoma [35]. These findings indicate that diminished serum uric acid levels are correlated with augmented renal excretion. The process of reabsorption of uric acid is principally confined to the proximal convoluted tubules of the kidneys [9, 10]. In this study, reduced urine specific gravity in PA patients indicated impaired function of the proximal convoluted tubules, which could lead to increased uric acid excretion. A pivotal and novel finding of our study is the significant inverse correlation between SUA levels and PASC severity grades, indicating that more severe PA is associated with lower SUA. This phenomenon, though seemingly paradoxical given uric acid’s role in other metabolic diseases, is highly consistent with the pathophysiology of PA. We postulate that chronic aldosterone excess inflicts injury on the proximal tubules, impairing the function of uric acid reabsorption transporters (e.g., URAT1). This leads to increased urinary uric acid excretion and consequent hypouricemia, this is consistent with previous findings, the lower SUA in PA resulted from the suppression of reabsorption and/or an enhancement of secretion of uric acid in the proximal tubules, being related to the so-called escape phenomenon [44]. The strong positive correlation between SUA and serum potassium further supports this shared tubular mechanism, as both hypokalemia and hypouricemia are likely parallel manifestations of aldosterone-induced tubular dysfunction [45]. Thus, SUA level may not merely distinguish PA from EH but may serve as a biomarker reflecting the severity of aldosterone-mediated metabolic derangement and renal tubular injury.

This study has several limitations. First, its single-center, retrospective design and relatively small sample size preclude definitive conclusions about causality between low SUA levels and PA. Second, although we have reported quantitative SUA values for APA and IHA patients, the limited subgroup sample size reduced the statistical power for comparing subtypes, and SUA levels were not monitored post-treatment. Most importantly, although the observed decrease in urine specific gravity in the PA group supports the hypothesis of impaired proximal tubular function and increased uric acid excretion, urine specific gravity is a non-specific parameter influenced by multiple factors beyond uric acid concentration. The absence of direct measurements of urinary uric acid excretion (e.g., 24-hour urinary uric acid) is a notable limitation of our study, as it prevents a definitive conclusion on the precise renal handling of uric acid in PA.

The clinical utility of any single biomarker must be evaluated within the context of its inherent variability and the pathophysiological rationale underlying its association with disease. Serum uric acid levels are influenced by numerous factors, including renal function, dietary purine intake, and medication use. However, the strength, consistency, and independence of the inverse association between SUA and PA demonstrated in our study, across genders, menopausal status, and now, importantly, across grades of disease severity suggests a robust biological link that transcends this variability. This link is mechanistically grounded in the aldosterone-mediated proximal tubulopathy, which appears to promote uricosuria and consequently hypouricemia. Therefore, while SUA alone is not proposed as a standalone diagnostic tool, it emerges as a highly informative and readily available auxiliary biomarker. Its value likely lies in its integration into future multi-parametric predictive models. Combining SUA with other established features of PA, such as hypokalemia and hypertension severity, could yield a highly efficient pre-screening algorithm. This would be particularly valuable in resource-limited settings, helping to identify hypertensive patients at the highest risk for PA and prioritizing them for definitive testing.
