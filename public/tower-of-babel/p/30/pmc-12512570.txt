Hematogenous pulmonary abscess and septicemia due to infection with hypervirulent Pseudomonas aeruginosa in a nonimmunodeficient adolescent: a case report
Authors: Xinyu Zhu, Jie Yan, Xiyun Lai, Mengyuan Chen, Qiang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPseudomonas aeruginosa is a non-glucose-fermenting gram-negative bacillus and an opportunistic pathogen that typically causes localized or systemic infections in immunocompromised middle-aged and elderly individuals or children with congenital immune deficiencies. However, cases of pulmonary abscess with severe sepsis in normal individuals are rare and should be taken seriously.Case presentationA 15-year-old nonimmunodeficient adolescent was hospitalized due to high fever and other clinical symptoms. The patient was diagnosed with a pulmonary abscess with severe sepsis. Etiological Examination showed that sensitive Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Subsequent inspection revealed this clinical isolate produces virulence factors ExoA and Pyocyanin, which was highly virulent. The patient’s symptoms were improved after imipenem (IPM) treatment and discharged. At follow-up 3 months later, he had recovered well.ConclusionsWe report a case of a pulmonary abscess with severe sepsis caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa in an immunocompetent patient. Compared with the vast majority of infections caused by P. aeruginosa, this patient was younger and had no immunodeficiency or basic diseases, and the characteristics of the lesions were also unique. For this P. aeruginosa isolate, the testings of antimicrobial drugs were generally sensitive, and the virulence factors ExoA and Pyocyanin by quantitative metagenomics next-generation sequencing may be the main factors for the severity.

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa), a widespread opportunistic pathogen, belongs to aerobic and Gram-negative non-fermenting bacteria of the Pseudomonadaceae family. P. aeruginosa occurs in abiotic and biotic environments, from soil and aquatic environments to plant and animal tissues. It can be isolated from several nosocomial and life-threatening infections in patients with cystic fibrosis (CF), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and urinary tract infections (UTIs) [1, 2]. P. aeruginosa is considered one of the most important opportunistic pathogenic bacteria and a major cause of hospital-acquired infections (HAIs) in patients. It is well known to be associated with hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) along with ventilator-associated pneumonia, gastrointestinal infections and several other infections, especially in patients with severe burns, and immunocompromised patients, such as those with cancer or acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS) [3, 4].

In our study, we report a pulmonary abscess combined with severe sepsis caused by P. aeruginosa infection in an immunocompetent patient to increase clinical understanding of the emerging infectious manifestations of a hypervirulent phenotype of P. aeruginosa.

Case presentation

Clinical features

A 15-year-old adolescent had chest wall pain with no obvious predisposing causes, aggravated by breathing and exercise, radiated to the back, accompanied by a little dryness of the throat and dizziness. The patient had no symptoms such as cough or sputum, nausea and vomiting, The Computed tomography (CT) scan of the local hospital showed two upper lungs with ground-glass nodules, was given analgesia and hospitalization observing. Two days later, the patient developed a fever and the chest CT scan showed the pneumonia in the left upper lung, with a small amount of pneumothorax on the left side. So he came to our institute for treatment. The patient denied any history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or immunodeficiency syndrome.

Physical examination upon admission showed a body temperature of 40.0 °C, increased respiratory and heart rate, no chest wall tenderness, decreased breath sounds at the left upper lung, no rhonch or moist rales from both lungs, and both lower limbs had mild edema. Clinical and laboratory indicators of the patient

The patient was diagnosed with community-acquired pneumonia. On the first night after admission, the patient experienced significant dyspnea, productive cough with blood-tinged sputum, and a decrease in oxygen saturation to 77% despite receiving 3 L/min oxygen via nasal cannula. Blood cultures and immunology-related tests were carried out. Meanwhile, the patient was administered 40 mg of methylprednisolone intravenously, but the patient’s symptoms no improvement. A second dose of 40 mg methylprednisolone was then administered intravenously. The patient was subsequently transferred to the Respiratory Intensive Care Unit (RICU) and received high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) oxygen therapy (86–89% concentration, flow rate 15 L/min). The next morning, the patient’s oxygen saturation was 87%. Thus, the HFNC oxygen therapy was adjusted to 35 L/min with 55–60% concentration. Until 7 pm on the second day, the oxygenation fluctuations stabilized, and then nasal cannula oxygen inhalation at a flow rate of 2 L/min was provided. On the third day, he was transferred back to the Department of Respiratory Medicine.

CT showed a small amount of fluid density shadow in the left thorax, a mass lesion was seen in the upper lobe of the left lung, with cavities and air-fluid levels inside the lesion, and suggesting an infectious lesion and abscess. Immune-related tests indicated that the patient’s T cells and natural killer cells showed a slight decrease, while B cells increased. Human immunodeficiency virus antibody, hepatitis C virus antibody, and hepatitis B surface antigen were negative.

Computed tomography examination

CT showed a symmetrical thoracic cage, with a cavity in the left upper lobe measuring approximately 7.8 × 7.6 cm, containing an air-fluid level. Consider infectious lesions, abscesses probably. The texture of the right lung was clear, and there were no obvious abnormal density shadow. The structure of the two lung hilums was no abnormality. The trachea and bronchus were unobstructed.(Fig. 1A).

Etiological examination

Specimen processing and identification procedures were performed in accordance with laboratory protocols. The blood culture and alveolar lavage fluid were cultured, and observed on the blood agar plate revealed grey-green colonies as flat, moist, and irregularly edged (Fig. 2A). Gram staining and oil immersion microscopy (1000×) showed a large number of gram-negative bacilli (Fig. 2B). The colonies were identified as P. aeruginosa using Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization-time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS, Autof ms1500, Autobio), with a confidence level of 99.9% (Fig. 2C). Subsequently, further confirmed using 16 S rRNA sequencing.

Medication and prognosis

The patient was consolidated with severe sepsis, and empirical anti-infective therapy was started using meropenem (20 mg/kg/dose, administered intravenously every 8 h). Albumin supplementation and thoracic puncture drainage were performed at the same time. Alveolar lavage fluid and pleural fluid were sent for inspection. Pathogenic examination confirmed the presence of P. aeruginosa. Anti-infective treatment imipenem(10 mg/kg/dose, administered intravenously every 6 h) was administered. Repeat blood cultures reported negative results six days after starting antibiotics treatment. The patient was discharged when his symptoms improved and the immunological indices return to normal (Table 4) after four weeks of treatment. And he was treated with oral levofloxacin 0.5 g once daily for 4–8 weeks to target P. aeruginosa.

The patient was discharged and was alive and well at the 4-week follow-up. The CT re-examination result one month after the patient’s discharge showed a mass-like shadow in the upper lobe of the left lung, which had absorbed and improved compared with the previous imaging findings (Fig. 1B). Upon re-examination three months follow-up, only a small amount of fibrous cord-like shadows remained at the site of the original lesion (Fig. 1C), indicating that the patient had essentially recovered.

Discussion and conclusion

P. aeruginosa is a common cause of nosocomial infection, especially in patients with pneumonia, infection with low immune function and structural lung diseases such as cystic fibrosis [5]. It is reported to cause the highest mortality rate among common gram-negative bloodstream infections (BSI) [6, 7].

In a retrospective series of 39 cases of community acquired P. aeruginosa bacteraemia, Kang et al. [8] reported that the primary site of infection was the lung (23% of cases), followed by the biliary (20%) and urinary (12%) tracts. The initial presentation was associated with septic shock in 38% of cases, overall mortality rate was 38.5% and only 2 patients were immunocompetent. In children, The detection rates of various resistance and virulence genes were high, and the coexistence phenomenon was serious [9]. Fortunately, the P. aeruginosa isolated from this patient was susceptible to most antibiotics.

The virulence of P. aeruginosa is multifactorial. The clinical isolate in this case was confirmed to produce virulence factors ExoA and Pyocyanin. ExoA is an ADP-ribosyltransferase [10], it can inhibit the host protein synthesis and IL-18 secretion, reduce the production of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10, and induce the apoptosis of the host cells [11]. And water-soluble pyocyanin (phenazine) is expressed only by P. aeruginosa [12, 13]. Phenazines act by generating reactive oxygen species within bacterial cells, causing oxidative damage to proteins, lipids, and DNA, leading to cell lysis, severity of disease, and decreased lung function. In addition, it decreases the respiratory cilia movement, disrupts the function of epithelial cells, increases the secretion of respiratory mucus and colonization of bacteria, and increases the inflammation [14]. These virulence factors play a critical role in the pathogenesis of potential complications, including acute lung injury, bacteremia, sepsis and invasion of tissues.

In summary, we report a rare case of a particularly severe respiratory tract infection with septic shock due to a highly cytotoxic strain of P. aeruginosa. Compared with the vast majority of infections caused by P. aeruginosa invasion, the patient is younger and has no immunodeficiency or underlying diseases. For this P. aeruginosa isolate, Q-mNGS detected the toxA and phzM virulence genes, the antimicrobial drugs tested were generally sensitive, and the prognosis was ideal after combination antibiotic therapy. It should be emphasized that hypervirulent P. aeruginosa has gradually become a unique pathogen in patients with normal immune function. Antibiotics should be used quickly and accurately to avoid the generation of hypervirulent and multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa.
