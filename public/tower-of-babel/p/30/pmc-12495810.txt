RPL22L1-Myc positive feedback loop drives lung adenocarcinoma progression
Authors: Yahua Wu, Yingjiao Zhu, Rongqi Jiang, Weiwei Gu, Lihu Lu, Na Yao, Bin Du, Jinhuo Lai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRibosomal protein L22 like 1 (RPL22L1) plays an important role in some tumours. However, its role in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is less studied. This study aimed to analyse the molecular mechanism of RPL22L1 in LUAD and to identify new targets for LUAD treatment.MethodsReal-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and Western blot (WB) were employed to detect the mRNA and protein expression, respectively. The CCK8 assay, colony formation assay, and transwell migration and invasion assay were utilized to assess the effects of RPL22L1 on LUAD cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. A nude mouse subcutaneous graft tumor model was used to evaluate the effect of RPL22L1 on the proliferative capacity of LUAD cells in vivo. Dual fluorescent reporter gene assays, ubiquitination immunoprecipitation assays, and protein degradation assays were conducted to explore the potential mechanism of RPL22L1 in LUAD.ResultsThe study demonstrated that RPL22L1 could promote the proliferation, migration, invasion, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) of LUAD cells. Mechanistically, the upregulation of RPL22L1 in LUAD activated the MAPK/ERK/Myc signaling pathway, which subsequently promoted the proliferation, migration, invasion, and EMT of LUAD cells. Furthermore, RPL22L1 was found to influence the ubiquitination of Myc protein, inhibit its degradation, and maintain its stability. Additionally, Myc protein could directly bind to the promoter region (-653 to -664) of RPL22L1 and enhance its transcriptional expression, forming a positive feedback loop to promote the developmental process of LUAD.ConclusionsOur study revealed a positive feedback loop between RPL22L1 and Myc that drove LUAD progression.Keywords: RPL22L1, Lung adenocarcinoma, MAPK/ERK/Myc, Positive feedback loop, Progression

Introduction

Lung cancer is globally recognized as one of the most common malignant tumors [1]. According to statistics, the number of new lung cancer cases in China in 2022 exceeded one million, resulting in nearly 700,000 deaths [2]. Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is a common pathological type of lung cancer [3]. Despite significant advances in tumour therapies, the 5-year overall survival rate of LUAD patients remains unsatisfactory [4, 5]. Therefore, it is crucial to explore the mechanisms involved in the progression of LUAD.

Ribosomal protein (RP), in addition to its involvement in ribosome assembly and protein synthesis, is constantly being discovered for its extra-ribosomal function [6]. Studies have shown that a variety of RPs are closely associated with the development of lung cancer, such as RPS27, RPS15a, RPL19, RPL34, which are abnormally highly expressed in lung cancer tissues [7–9]. Ribosomal protein L22-like1 (RPL22L1) is an important member of the ribosomal protein family, and it has been found that RPL22L1 plays a key role in the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma [10], colorectal carcinoma [11], prostate carcinoma [12], and glioblastoma [13]. Although our previous study identified RPL22L1 as a possible potential oncogene in LUAD [14], its molecular regulatory network and functional characteristics in LUAD have not yet been fully elucidated.

Myc is a classical oncogene that is commonly deregulated in human cancers, a [15], and its product, the Myc protein, is a very unstable protein that is susceptible to degradation. It has been shown that ubiquitination of Myc is one of the key factors in maintaining Myc protein homeostasis [16]. In addition, MYC protein, as a transcription factor, is considered to be a powerful driver of human cancers, regulating the transcription of many genes that contribute to tumour development [17]. Studies have shown that Myc enhances transcription of many genes related to ribosomal biogenesis [18]. Hence, it is worth exploring whether there is a regulatory relationship between RPL22L1 and Myc protein.

Our previous study confirmed that RPL22L1 is highly expressed in LUAD and correlates with poor prognosis [14]. In this study, we will further explore the effects of RPL22L1 on the proliferation and metastasis of LUAD cells by in vivo and in vitro experiments, and will elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which RPL22L1 promotes LUAD progression, focusing on the interaction between RPL22L1 and Myc.

Methods

Cell culture

Human normal lung epithelial cell (BEAS-2B), human embryo kidney (HEK) 293T cell and LUAD cells (A549, H1975) were obtained from the Chinese Academy of Science Cell Bank. Cells were cultivated in RPMI-1640 or DMEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum, and 1% penicillin–streptomycin in 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling.

Functional enrichment analysis

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) database was used to obtain LUAD-LUAD RNA‐seq data. We used the “DEseq2” package to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in low- and high-RPL22L1 groups according to the median RPL22L1 expression in LUAD. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were performed based on DEGs.

Cell transfection

The RPL22L1 over-expressing cell lines were generated by lentivirus vector containing the full-length sequence of RPL22L1(Genechem, Shanghai, China). We used lentivirus-mediated shRNA (Genechem, Shanghai, China) to stably knock down RPL22L1 expression in LUAD cells. After lentiviral infection, puromycin screening was applied for at least 7 days to obtain stable knockdown or overexpressing cells.

The shRNA sequences were as follows:

shRPL22L1#1: CTTACCAAGAAATACCTTAAG;

shRPL22L1#2: CATTGAACGCTTCAAGAATAA;

shRPL22L1#3: GAGGTCAACCTGGAGGTTTAA.

Clone formation assay

Cells were inoculated in 6-well plates. After 14 days of cell culture, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and then stained with 1% crystal violet for 20 min. Finally, cell photographs were taken and cell colonies were counted.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8)

Slowly inoculate cells in 96-well plates. After culturing for a certain period of time, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h away from light to measure the optical absorption value at 450 nm.

Wound healing assays

Cells were inoculated in each of the 6-well plates, and after the cells were attached to the wall, the cell surface was scratched with the tip of 200 µL, and then washed twice with PBS to remove the dislodged cells. Images of each scratch at the same location were captured using a microscope at 0 h and 48 h and assessed for wound healing using ImageJ software.

Transwell migration and invasion assay

For transwell migration assays, cells resuspended in serum-free medium were slowly added to the upper chamber (Corning, NY, USA), while for transwell invasion assays, cells were added to the upper chamber pre-coated with a 1:8 dilution of Matrigel (Corning, NY, USA). The bottom chamber was added with 600 µl medium containing 15% FBS. After incubation for the indicated times, cells were paraformaldehyde fixed and stained with crystal violet dye. Finally, three randomly selected fields of view were photographed for subsequent analysis.

Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted according to the protocol of Eastep® Super Total RNA Extraction Kit (Promega). RNA was reverse transcribed using FastKing gDNA Dispelling RT SuperMix (TIANGEN). Real-time PCR was performed in Applied Biosystems 7500 Real Time PCR system using GoTaq® qPCR Master Mix (Promega). Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the internal control to normalize data. Primers used in this study were presented as follows:

RPL22L1-Forward: 5’-ACTGGAAATCTCGGGAATGTTGT-3’.

RPL22L1-Reverse: 5’-AGATGCAACCACTCGAAGCC-3’.

GAPDH-Forward: 5’-GGTGTGAACCATGAGAAGTATGA-3’.

GAPDH-Reverse: 5’-GAGTCCTTCCACGATACCAAAG-3’.

Myc-Forward: 5’-CCTACCCTCTCAACGACAGC-3’.

Myc-Forward: 5’-TTGTTCCTCCTCAGAGTCGC-3’.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors and was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China). Proteins were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked in 5% skim milk for 2 h at room temperature, and then incubated overnight at 4℃ with diluted primary antibody. Following washed with TBST, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibody for 2 h at room temperature. Finaly the protein bands were detected by enhanced chemiluminescence (BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA). The primary antibodies of p-MEK1/2 (cat No.9154), MEK1/2 (cat No.8727), p-ERK1/2 (cat No.4370), ERK1/2 (cat No.4695) and Vimentin (cat No.5741) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology, while E-cadherin (cat No.20874-1-AP), N-cadherin (cat No.22018-1-AP), Myc (cat No.10828-1-AP), Ubiquitin (80992-1-RR) and GAPDH (cat No.10494-1-AP) antibodies were purchased from Proteintech. RPL22L1 (cat No. YN1235) antibodies were purchased from Immunoway. The secondary antibody was goat anti-rabbit IgG HRP (cat No. SA00001-2) purchased from Proteintech.

Dual-luciferase reporter assays

293T cells were planted on 12-well plates. When the cell density was approximately 60%, the plasmid with the specified combination was transfected into 293 T cells for 24 h. The cells were lysed 48 h after transfection, and the lysate was analyzed using a Dual Luciferase Assay System kit (Promega). The renilla luciferase reporter was used as internal control.

Ubiquitination IP assay

Ubiquitination IP assay was performed using the Classic Magnetic Protein A/G IP/Co-IP Kit (YJ201, Epizyme Biomedical Technology Co, Ltd, China). The cell-permeable proteasome inhibitor MG132 (20 µM, GlpBio) was added to cellcultures for 6 h, followed by an immunoprecipitation lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors for 10 min. The centrifugation (12,000 rpm for 20 min) was performed to obtain the supernatant, a small amount of which was collected for western blotting analysis (input group). An appropriate amount of lysate was taken and immunoprecipitated with anti-Myc antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Then the antibody-antigen complex was incubated with protein-A/G-agarose beads for one hour at room temperature. Then the immunoprecipitated proteins were washed with lysis buffer and then denatured with loading buffer at 100℃ for 10 min. The immunoprecipitated proteins were quantified using western blotting.

Protein half-life assays

Cells were collected at 0, 30, 60, and 120 min after addition of cyclohexylamine (CHX; 50 µg/mL; GlpBio) to the cell culture medium. The cells were then lysed and boiled, and the target proteins were detected by western blotting.

Xenograft model

BALB/c nude mice (4 weeks, female, 16–20 g) were acquired from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co, Ltd. 100 µl of PBS buffer containing 5 × 106 A549 cells subjected to different interventions was injected into the right subcutaneous side of nude mice. Tumour volume was measured twice a week starting on the seventh day after cell injection and monitored for 3 weeks. Tumour volume (V) was determined by assessing the length (L) and width (W) of the tumour using a vernier caliper, calculated as follows: V = (L × W2)/2. Finally, the tumour tissue was subjected to immunohistochemistry to analyse Ki-67 expression. Animal studies were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical United Hospital and were conducted in strict accordance with the guidelines.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Mean ± SD was used to present experimental results. Differences between two groups were tested using the student’s t-test, while differences between multiple groups were compared using the analysis of variance (ANOVA) test. The P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Expression of RPL22L1 in LUAD cells

We used RT-qPCR and WB to detect RPL22L1 mRNA and protein expression levels in LUAD cells (A549, PC9, H1975) and human normal alveolar epithelial cells (BEAS-2B). The results showed that compared to BEAS-2B, RPL22L1 mRNA and protein exhibited significantly high expression in LUAD cells (A549, PC9, H1975) (Fig. 1A). Therefore, we selected A549 cells with relatively high expression of RPL22L1 to construct a stable knockdown cell model, and the H1975 cell with relatively low expression to construct a stable overexpression cell model. As shown in Fig. 1B, the LUAD cell model with stable overexpression and knockdown of RPL22L1 was successfully constructed.

(D) Cell clone formation assays were performed in A549 cells and H1975 cells. (E-F) Changes in volume and weight of subcutaneous tumours in control and knocked-down nude mice. (G) The protein expression of RPL22L1 and Ki67 in subcutaneous tumours were detected by immunohistochemistry assay. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001

RPL22L1 affected proliferation of LUAD cells

Firstly, we used CCK-8 and colony formation assay to explore the effect of RPL22L1 on the proliferation ability of LUAD cells. Compared to the control group, A549 cells with RPL22L1 knockdown showed significantly reduced proliferation, while H1975 cells with RPL22L1 overexpression exhibited significantly increased proliferation (Fig. 1C-D).

Next, we explored the effect of RPL22L1 on the proliferative capacity of LUAD cells in vivo. We established a nude mouse subcutaneous graft tumour model by injecting 5 × 10^6 RPL22L1 knockdown and control A549 cells into the right axilla of nude mice. Comparison of the volume and weight of the transplanted tumours between the two groups revealed that tumours in the RPL22L1 knockdown group exhibited significantly reduced growth, with lower volume and weight compared to the control group (Fig. 1E-F). Ki-67 is an important marker of proliferation. Finally, we performed immunohistochemical analysis of tumours from nude mice to analyse Ki-67 protein expression. The results demonstrated that Ki-67 protein expression in transplanted tumour tissues of the knockdown group was significantly lower than that in the control group (Fig. 1G), suggesting that knockdown of RPL22L1 could inhibit the proliferative ability of LUAD cells in vivo.

RPL22L1 affected migration, invasion and EMT of LUAD cells

We assessed the effect of RPL22L1 on LUAD cells’ migration and invasion using wound healing and transwell assays. The wound healing assay results showed that knockdown of RPL22L1 in A549 cells significantly reduced their migratory ability compared with the control group, while overexpression of RPL22L1 in H1975 cells significantly increased migration (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, transwell assays showed that the invasion and migration of LUAD cells were attenuated after RPL22L1 knockdown, and on the contrary, the invasion and migration of cells were enhanced after overexpression of RPL22L1 (Fig. 2B).

(B) Transwell assays were performed in in A549 cells and H1975 cells. (C) The effect of knocking down RPL22L1 in A549 cells on the expression levels of E-cadherin, Ncadherin and Vimentin. (D) The effect of overexpression of RPL22L1 in H1975 cells on the expression levels of E-cadherin, Ncadherin and Vimentin. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001

Epithelial mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a complex biological mechanism that mediates the progression of LUAD. We examined the effect of RPL22L1 on the expression levels of EMT-related proteins. The results showed that The results showed that RPL22L1 knockdown in A549 cells led to increased expression of E-cadherin and decreased expression of N-cadherin and Vimentin (Fig. 2C). Conversely, we found that in RPL22L1-overexpressing H1975 cells, E-cadherin expression was significantly down-regulated while N-cadherin and Vimentin expression was significantly up-regulated (Fig. 2D).

RPL22L1 activated the MAPK signalling pathway

Previously, we confirmed the effect of RPL22L1 on the malignant biological behaviour of LUAD at in vivo and in vitro levels. Further, to explore the specific mechanism of RPL22L1 in LUAD, we first performed differential analysis based on the high and low expression of RPL22L1, and identified 559 differential genes, of which 341 were down-regulated and 219 were up-regulated (Fig. 3A). We included the differential genes in GO and KEGG functional enrichment analyses, which showed that the upregulated differential genes were associated with the MAPK/ERK signalling pathway (Fig. 3B). Next, we detected the effect of RPL22L1 on the expression of related proteins on the MAPK/ERK signalling pathway. The results showed that compared with the control group, the protein expression of p-MEK1/2, p-ERK1/2 and Myc was significantly down-regulated in A549 cells with knockdown of RPL22L1 (Fig. 3C). In contrast, overexpression of RPL22L1 in H1975 cells resulted in up-regulation of p-MEK1/2, p-ERK1/2 and Myc proteins (Fig. 3D).

RPL22L1 affected LUAD cell proliferation, migration and invasion, and EMT by regulating the MAPK pathway

First, we added the MAPK pathway inhibitor PD98059 to H1975 cells overexpressing RPL22L1 and found that the up-regulation of p-ERK1/2, Myc protein expression induced by overexpression of RPL22L1 was reverted (Fig. 4A). Subsequently, we confirmed that the addition of PD98059 to H1975 cells overexpressing RPL22L1 reversed RPL22L1 overexpression-induced proliferation, migration, and invasion ability of LUAD cells (Fig. 4B-E). In addition, we found that blocking the MAPK/ERK signalling pathway in H1975 cells overexpressing RPL22L1 resulted in a decrease in the expression of up-regulated N-cadherin and Vimentin and an increase in the expression of down-regulated E-cadherin (Fig. 4F).

Transcription factor Myc activated RPL22L1 expression

Previous studies have indicated that transcription factor regulation is crucial in tumor progression. To investigate RPL22L1 upregulation, we screened potential transcription factors affecting its expression using five prediction websites, including Cistrome, hTFtarget, ENCODE, KnockTF, and ChEA3. The intersection of predicted transcription factors from five websites identified two potential regulators of RPL22L1: Myc and FOXA1 (Fig. 5A). Next, we analyzed the correlation of Myc and FOXA1 with RPL22L1 mRNA expression in the TCGA-LUAD dataset, finding a strong positive correlation between Myc and RPL22L1 (r = 0.326, P < 0.001) (Fig. 5B). Then, the dual luciferase reporter gene assay showed a significant increase in luciferase activity after Myc overexpression, indicating that Myc could promote RPL22L1 transcription (Fig. 5C).

(B) Pearson analysis of the correlation of RPL22L1 with the mRNA of Myc and FOXA1 based on the TCGA database. (C) Dual luciferase reporter gene assay to validate transcriptional activation of RPL22L1 by Myc in 293 T cells. (D) Prediction of binding sites based on the JASPAR database. (E, F) Dual-luciferase reporter assays were performed by co-transfection of the RPL22L1 promoter (WT or Mut 1/2/3) and Myc overexpression vector to determine the binding region of Myc to the RPL22L1 promoter. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001

Further, we predicted that Myc might have multiple binding sites in the RPL22L1 promoter region using the JASPAR database. In this regard, we selected the top three binding sites, including BS1 (−653~−664), BS2 (−166~−177), and BS3 (−44~−55) (Fig. 5D), and constructed dual-luciferase reporter gene plasmids (WT, Mut1, Mut2, and Mut3) for the full-length and binding site mutants of the RPL22L1 promoter, respectively. The results showed that introducing the full-length RPL22L1 promoter sequence vector along with the Myc overexpression vector into 293 T cells significantly enhanced luciferase activity (Fig. 6E). Secondly, when the promoter sequence of RPL22L1 was mutated at the BS1 site, its transcriptional expression activity was significantly decreased compared to the wild type. However, no significant change in transcriptional activity was observed when the RPL22L1 promoter was mutated at the BS2 and BS3 sites (Fig. 5F). These results suggest that Myc can promote the transcriptional expression of RPL22L1 by binding to the BS1 (−653~−664) site in its promoter region.

RPL22L1 affected Myc protein ubiquitination

In LUAD cells, RT-qPCR results showed no significant change in Myc mRNA expression after either knockdown or overexpression of RPL22L1 (Fig. 6A). However, knockdown of RPL22L1 caused a significant decrease in Myc protein expression, whereas overexpression led to a significant up-regulation of Myc protein (Fig. 6B). These results suggest that RPL22L1 regulates Myc at the post-translational level.

To verify whether RPL22L1 affects the post-translational stability of Myc protein, we performed protein half-life experiments using the protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide (CHX). The results showed that knockdown of RPL22L1 in A549 cell significantly accelerated Myc protein degradation (Fig. 6C). Conversely, in H1975 cells overexpressing RPL22L1, the degradation rate of Myc protein was significantly reduced (Fig. 6D). These results indicated that RPL22L1 stabilized Myc protein levels. Then, to further explore the degradation pathway of Myc protein, we blocked its ubiquitin-proteasome degradation pathway using the proteasome inhibitor MG132. The results showed that MG132 restored the down-regulated Myc protein level after knockdown of RPL22L1 (Fig. 6E). In contrast, chloroquine (CQ) had no significant effect on the down-regulated Myc protein level after RPL22L1 knockdown (Fig. 6F). These results demonstrate that RPL22L1 regulates Myc protein via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway rather than the autophagy-lysosome pathway. Finally, we used ubiquitin Co-IP assay to confirm that the ubiquitination level of Myc protein was increased after knockdown of RPL22L1 and decreased after overexpression of RPL22L1 (Fig. 6G), suggesting that RPL22L1 affects the ubiquitination level of Myc protein.

Discussion

The role of RL22L1 in cancer progression has been demonstrated in various types of cancer, including glioblastoma [13, 19], hepatocellular carcinoma [10], prostate cancer [12], colorectal cancer [11, 20], ovarian cancer [21]. Furthermore, our previous findings suggested that RPL22L1 was significantly upregulated in LUAD and associated with poor prognosis [14]. In this study, we found that RPL22L1 promoted LUAD progression and that this process was dependent on the activation of the MAPK/ERK pathway. In addition to this, we found that a positive feedback loop regulatory mechanism was formed between RPL22L1 and Myc, which helped to promote LUAD progression.

Mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK) are a group of evolutionarily conserved serine-threonine kinases involved in a wide range of cellular functions, including cell growth, proliferation, migration and apoptosis [22–24]. Numerous studies have shown that the MAPK pathway is involved in the development of malignant tumours [25, 26]. Most malignant tumours exhibit aberrant activation of MAPK/ERK signalling [27]. Our study revealed that RPL22L1 affected the activation of the MAPK/ERK signalling pathway, and when the expression of RPL22L1 was knocked down, key proteins on the MAPK/ERK pathway were inhibited, and on the contrary, the MAPK/ERK pathway was activated after overexpression of RPL22L1. In addition, our study also confirmed that RPL22L1 impacted the proliferation and invasion of LUAD cells through the MAPK/ERK pathway.

Epithelial-Mesenchymal transition (EMT) plays a key role in the metastatic process of cancer cells [28, 29], and is characterised by a decrease or loss of expression of epithelial cell markers (like E-calmodulin) and an increase in expression of mesenchymal cell markers (like N-calmodulin) [30, 31]. Several studies have shown that the MAPK/ERK signalling axis is closely involved in the regulation of EMT in tumour cells [32–34]. For example, a study by Wang et al. found that OVOL2 induced autophagy-mediated EMT via ERK1/2 MAPK signalling in LUAD [33]. Furthermore, Li et al. also demonstrated that TPI induced EMT in LUAD cells through the MAPK/ERK signalling pathway [34]. Our study found that RPL22L1 could affect the EMT of LUAD cells and reverse the EMT of LUAD cells when the MAPK pathway was blocked, suggesting that RPL22L1 may affect the EMT of LUAD cells through the MAPK/ERK signalling pathway.

Myc is one of the most widely studied oncogenes and plays an important role in almost all aspects of tumour development, including cell cycle, DNA replication, cell differentiation, apoptosis and metabolism [35, 36]. Studies show that Myc expression is dysregulated in many human cancers [37]. In our present study, we found that RPL22L1 regulated Myc protein expression but did not alter its mRNA expression. Therefore, we hypothesized that RPL22L1 regulated Myc at the level of protein post-translational modification. Ubiquitination is one of the most common post-translational modifications which is a multi-step enzymatic process involved in the regulation of protein homeostasis [38]. Myc is a very unstable protein with a short half-life, and the ubiquitin-proteasome system is one of the keys to regulating Myc protein stability [16, 39]. In this study, we demonstrated that RPL22L1 could maintain Myc protein homeostasis by protein half-life experiments at first. Furthermore, we blocked the ubiquitin-proteasome degradation pathway of Myc using the proteasome inhibitor MG132, and found that MG132 restored the down-regulated protein levels of Myc after RPL22L1 knockdown. Finally, we used the ubiquitin IP assay to confirm that RPL22L1 affected the ubiquitination level of Myc protein.

Transcription factors (TFs) can bind to specific regions of the promoter of a target gene to promote or repress its transcription, which are key molecules in the regulation of gene transcription [40]. Meanwhile, TFs also exert an important effect in the development of cancer [41]. Studies have shown that Myc is an important transcription factor which plays a pivotal role in driving the transcription of a diverse array of genes essential for various cellular processes and functions [17, 42]. Our study illustrated that Myc could directly bind to the promoter of RPL22L1, thereby facilitating the transcription of RPL22L1.

In conclusion, our study showed that upregulation of RPL22L1 in LUAD activated the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway, which subsequently promoted the proliferation, migration, invasion and EMT of LUAD cells. Besides, RPL22L1 also mediated Myc deubiquitination, thereby maintaining Myc stability. Furthermore, transcription factor Myc could directly bind to the transcriptional region of RPL22L1, thereby enhancing its transcription. This interaction established a positive feedback regulatory mechanism that contributed to the sustained activation of Myc, thereby initiating LUAD progression (Fig. 6H).
