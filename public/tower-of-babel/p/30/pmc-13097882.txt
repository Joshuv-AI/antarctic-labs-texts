Factors associated with participation in advance care planning among people with different degrees of cognitive impairment: a systematic review
Authors: Xing Wu, Wenqian Zhao, Xinyu Yao, Junhong Wu, Yutong Huang, Yuanxin Han, Huale Zhuang, Yayi Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCognitive function and decision-making ability are crucial for participation in advance care planning (ACP). People with different degrees of cognitive impairment face considerable difficulty and exhibit significant heterogeneity in their participation in ACP.MethodsSix English electronic databases (Embase, PubMed, Cochrane Library, PsycINFO, CINAHL, and Web of Science) and four Chinese databases (CNKI, CBM, Wanfang, and VIP) were searched from inception to February 18, 2024. The QualSyst tool was used to evaluate the study quality. Thematic synthesis was used to analyze the data.ResultsThirty-seven papers were included, comprising 21 qualitative, 9 quantitative, and 7 mixed studies. Factors related to ACP antecedents (sociodemographic characteristics, ACP perception, prior ACP experience, disease-related factors, and socio-cultural context) and ACP attributes (ACP procedural elements and ACP supportive mechanisms) influenced the participation of people with different degrees of cognitive impairment in ACP, but some differences were also found. Difficulty in accepting the disease and decision conflicts were not found to have an effect on mild cognitive impairment. Care network relationship was not found to influence mild to moderate dementia. Relying on surrogate decisions, decision conflicts, ACP data storage and informal social support were not found to influence moderate to severe dementia.ConclusionsThis study determined common and specific factors influencing the ACP participation of people with different degrees of cognitive impairment. The results could provide evidence for developing targeted interventions for promoting people with different degrees of cognitive impairment to participate in ACP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02050-y.

Background

Cognitive impairment is defined as the impairment of one or more cognitive domains due to various factors [1–4]. Based on severity, it encompasses mild cognitive impairment (MCI), along with mild, moderate and severe dementia. At present, over 50 million people globally are cognitively impaired, and the number of people with cognitive impairment (PwCI) is expected to reach 83 million by 2030 [5]. Given the large population, ensuring quality of long-term care services and quality of life of PwCI presents a significant challenge. Involving PwCI in their own medical and care decision-making is crucial for reflecting their will and improving quality of care and life. However, owing to the impairment of cognitive function and decision-making ability, PwCI experience extreme difficulty expressing their values and preferences when participating in future care decisions [6].

Advance care planning (ACP) is the process by which individuals understand and share their values, life goals, and preferences for future care before they become unable to make decisions, ensuring that they receive care aligned with their values and wishes once they are incapacitated [7]. ACP’s aim is to ensure that individuals who are incapacitated have access to the care of their choice [8]. ACP has been recognized for its effectiveness in promoting shared decision-making guided by the values and wishes of individuals, providing medical care consistent with their goals, and improving their quality of life [9–13].

Despite ACP’s many benefits, initiating this process can be difficult [2, 14], especially for PwCI. ACP for PwCI is a complex process influenced by multiple factors. Cognitive decline and the resulting gradual loss of decision-making capacity are the major barriers to PwCI’s participation in ACP. PwCI experience a decline in cognitive function and lose decision-making capacity earlier than people with other diseases. Moreover, PwCI face significant challenges in expressing their values and preferences when engaging in decision-making about their future medical care. ACP decision-making for PwCI is a complex process influenced by multiple factors, including those associated with their families, medical professionals, and the healthcare system [13, 15]. Fried et al. found that a lack of knowledge of cognitive impairment trajectories and related medical decision-making, focus on the present, refusal to think about the future, and reliance on family proxy decision-making hinder ACP decision-making in PwCI [16]. Chen et al. interviewed PwCI and family members and found that a lack of a supportive social environment, PwCI’s views on life and death, family members’ ethical considerations in surrogate decision-making, and absence of an ACP implementation model impede PwCI’s participation in ACP decision-making [17].

Reviews have also comprehensively described the factors affecting the participation of PwCI in ACP [18, 19]. However, these reviews only included studies until 2019; since then, new studies focusing on the factors of PwCI’s participation in ACP have been published extensively [16, 17, 20–32]. In addition, previous reviews have not focused on factors related to MCI. Owing to differences in cognitive function and decision-making ability, people at different degrees of cognitive impairment exhibit heterogeneity in participating in ACP. For instance, compared with people with dementia, those with MCI maintain comparatively preserved cognitive function and decision-making ability [33]. Their participation is often influenced by subjective willingness, family attitudes, and cultural factors [20]. As cognitive impairment progresses, people’s cognitive function and decision-making ability decline progressively [34], and their involvement in ACP thus becomes largely dependent on surrogate decision-makers (e.g., family members) [35]. Numerous factors that influence PwCI’s participation in ACP have been identified in previous studies, but the differences in these factors across different degrees of cognitive impairment remain unclear.

Therefore, this systematic review aims to identify factors associated with ACP participation among PwCI based on different degrees of cognitive impairment. The specific objectives are as follows: (1) to systematically summarize the factors influencing the participation of PwCI in ACP and (2) to clarify the similarities and differences in the factors influencing people with different degrees of cognitive impairment in participating in ACP, thereby providing evidence for subsequent targeted promotion of ACP participation.

Methods

Study design

A systematic review was conducted to provide a comprehensive perspective on understanding participation in ACP of people with different degrees of cognitive impairment. This review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024575178).

Search sources and strategies

Studies were systematically identified from six English databases (i.e., Embase, PubMed, Cochrane Library, PsycINFO, CINAHL, and Web of Science) and four Chinese databases (i.e., CNKI, CBM, Wanfang, and VIP) for eligible studies published from the inception of databases to February 18, 2024. A combination of subject headings, keywords, and free words was adopted with the search terms related to cognitive impairment (e.g., cognition disorders, dementia, Alzheimer’s disease) and ACP (e.g., advance care planning, advance directive, living will, power of attorney). The specific search strategy for each database is shown in Supplementary Table 1. The reference lists of included studies were also reviewed to ensure completeness. We also manually searched non-original studies (e.g., reviews, meta-analyses) to identify additional eligible original studies from their reference lists.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) study population: studies that include any of the following participant groups, namely PwCI, caregivers of PwCI, and medical professionals engaged in PwCI care; (2) study focus: studies investigating the factors influencing the participation of PwCI in ACP; (3) type of study: qualitative, quantitative, and mixed studies; and (4) language requirement: English or Chinese study. The exclusion criterion was unavailable full text.

Study screening and data extraction

After removing duplicates, two researchers (X.W and X.Y.Y) independently screened the studies according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Primary screening was performed by reading the titles and abstracts, followed by a secondary screening of full texts. For disagreements during the selection, final determination was made through mutual discussion or consultation with a third researcher (W.Q.Z). After thoroughly reading the article, a researcher (X.W) conducted data extraction using a pre-formulated data extraction form, which included title, authors, publication year, country/area, study design, study subject, age of PwCI, degree of cognitive impairment, and factors. In qualitative research, statements on the participation of PwCI in ACP were extracted. In quantitative research, factors with statistical significance influencing the participation of PwCI in ACP were extracted.

Critical appraisal

Study quality was assessed using the standardized QualSyst tool [36], which consists of qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods research scoring systems. Quality assessment for qualitative research includes 10 items: objective, design, context, connection to literature, sampling, data collection, analysis, credibility, conclusions, and reflexivity. Quality assessment for quantitative research includes 14 items: objective, design, subjective selection, subject characteristics, random allocation, blinding investigators, blinding subjects, outcomes, sample size, analysis, estimate of variance, confounding, results, and conclusion. Quality assessment for mixed-methods research includes three items: appropriate mixed-methods design, relevant integration of qualitative and quantitative data, and limitations of integration. Each item was scored depending on the degree to which a specific criterion was met (“yes” = 2; “partial” = 1; “no” = 0). Items not applicable to a particular study design were marked “n/a” and excluded from the calculation of the summary score. Moreover, assigning “n/a” was not permitted for any of the items, and the summary score for each paper was calculated by adding the total score obtained across the 10 items and dividing by 20 (total possible score) for the qualitative studies. Scores for the quantitative studies were calculated similarly, based on the scoring of 14 items. Each study was assigned a final score based on consensus. Aggregate scores were used to provide an overall measure of quality, with scores > 80%, 71%–80%, 51%–70%, and < 51% judged as “excellent,” “good,” “qualified,” and “limited,” respectively. Study quality was assessed independently by two researchers. Differences in opinion during the evaluation were resolved through mutual discussion or consultation with the third researcher.

Data analysis

The thematic analysis proposed by Braun and Clarke was used to analyze qualitative data [37]. The thematic analysis is particularly well-suited for identifying, analyzing, and reporting themes within complex datasets, especially when exploring participants’ experiences, perceptions, and factors influencing ACP participation across different stages of cognitive impairment. Given the heterogeneity in study designs (qualitative, quantitative, mixed-methods) and the nature of the factors identified (which included both objective, measurable variables and subjective, experiential insights), thematic analysis provided a flexible and comprehensive framework to synthesize findings from diverse sources into a coherent set of themes.

The specific steps of the thematic analysis were as follows: first, two researchers (X.W and X.Y.Y) carefully read the articles and then extracted and coded data and sentences reflecting factors associated with ACP of PwCI. The codes from two researchers were compared to find inconsistencies. Thereafter, the codes were summarized and compared with similar codes grouped into corresponding categories. Lastly, themes and sub-themes were determined. The degree of cognitive impairment was reflected among the identified factors. Different opinions during coding were resolved through mutual discussion or consultation with the third researcher (W.Q.Z). Meta-analysis was not conducted, given that the included quantitative studies mostly failed to clarify the degree of cognitive impairment, the heterogeneity of study factors was considerable, and the data descriptions in the results were inconsistent.

Results

Study characteristics

The initial search yielded 15,620 articles, and 37 studies were eventually included. The screening process and results are shown in Fig. 1. Included studies were published from 2008 to 2023. Eight studies were from the US [16, 21, 24, 30, 31, 38–40], six from the UK [41–46], five from China [18, 20, 32, 47, 48], four from Singapore [27, 49–51], three from Belgium [52–54], two from Australia [23, 55], two from the Netherlands [56, 57], two from Canada [26, 58], and one each from Germany [28], Israel [25], Norway [29], Finland [22], and Ireland [59]. A total of 21 studies were qualitative research [16, 17, 20, 22, 23, 25, 26, 28–31, 41–45, 52, 53, 55–57]. Eight quantitative studies were cross-sectional design [21, 24, 32, 38, 47–49, 59], one study was a retrospective cohort study [39], and seven were mixed studies [27, 40, 46, 50, 51, 54, 58]. Subjects included PwCI, caregivers of PwCI, medical professionals, and nursing home managers. The characteristics of the included studies are shown in Table 1.

Study quality

A total of 12, 13, and 12 studies were rated “excellent,” “good,” and “qualified,” respectively. The 21 qualitative studies described their objectives in detail. However, some studies did not provide detailed descriptions of other assessment items: 3 in design, 2 in context, 5 in connection to the literature, 1 in sampling, 13 in data collection, 4 in analysis, 11 in credibility, and 4 in conclusions. Reflexivity was not described in all studies. The nine quantitative studies provided detailed objectives, analyses, and results. However, some studies did not provide detailed descriptions of other assessment items: 6 in design, 1 in subject selection, 1 in subject characteristics, 2 in outcomes, 7 in sample size, 3 in conclusion. Two studies did not describe estimate of variance. Outcomes were not applicable in seven studies. Estimate of variance was not applicable in seven studies. Random allocation, blinding investigators, blinding subjects, and confounding were not applicable in evaluating the included quantitative studies. The seven mixed studies presented a detailed description of the appropriate mixed-method design and relevant integration of qualitative and quantitative data, but six did not consider the limitations of integration. The preceding results are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Associated factors

The analysis revealed two themes: ACP antecedents and ACP attributes. ACP antecedents refer to factors that influence individuals’ perceptions and experiences before they consider or initiate ACP. These include sub-themes of sociodemographic characteristics, ACP perception, prior ACP experience, disease-related factors, and socio-cultural context. On the other hand, ACP attributes encompass the key features and components that constitute the advance care planning process itself, which comprise ACP procedural elements and ACP supportive mechanisms (Table 2).

ACP antecedents

Sociodemographic characteristics

Education level

Two cross-sectional studies on people with MCI and mild dementia found a positive correlation between education level and ACP participation (p < 0.05) [32, 49]. A retrospective study on people with all stages of cognitive impairment also found that those with college degrees were more likely to participate in ACP than those with a high school education or below [38]. However, one prospective study on people with MCI and mild dementia showed no significant correlation between education level and ACP completion [51].

Age

The influence of age on ACP participation is controversial. Two cross-sectional studies [38, 47] on people with all stages of cognitive impairment found that the older people were, the more likely they would participate in ACP. However, a retrospective study on all stages of cognitive impairment [39] found that during a five-year follow-up, younger PwCI had a significantly higher rate of ACP completion compared with those who are older.

Race

For people with all stages of cognitive impairment, race was found closely related to ACP [24, 38]. In a retrospective cross-sectional study on 745 people with all stages of cognitive impairment, European Americans were found to be more likely to participate in ACP (adjusted odds ratio = 4.75; 95% CI, 2.40–9.38) than African Americans [38]. In a cross-sectional survey involving 431 racially diverse caregivers of persons with dementia, a higher proportion of whites completed ACP compared with African Americans (81% vs. 58%) [24].

Marital status

The influence of marital status on the participation of ACP was inconsistent [39, 48, 51]. A retrospective cross-sectional study on people with all stages of cognitive impairment [39, 48] found that married PwCI were likely to complete ACP. A cross-sectional study on 63 medical professionals [48] found that married medical professionals were markedly active in helping PwCI set up ACP. However, one prospective study [51] indicated that single (unmarried) PwCI were 8.88 times more likely to complete ACP than those married after receiving a psycho-education and counseling on the importance of ACP. A qualitative study on people with MCI also found that people without a partner appear to be considerably positive in terms of ACP participation [28].

Religious beliefs

Religious beliefs were confirmed to affect ACP participation for people with all stages of cognitive impairment. A cross-sectional survey among 143 nursing home dyads of residents with moderate and severe dementia and family caregivers found that the prevalence of ACP was considerably high among those with religious beliefs [47]. Medical professionals with religious beliefs were also found to likely assist with ACP [48]. However, findings from four qualitative studies of people with MCI, mild dementia and their caregivers, and medical professionals found that religious beliefs prevent PwCI from participating in ACP [25, 27, 50, 56].

ACP perception

Positive perceptions

The positive perception of ACP among people with MCI lay in the benefits of ACP discussion in maintaining quality of life and avoiding family conflict [20]. Positive perception of ACP among people with mild, moderate, and severe dementia suggested that ACP can prevent over-aggressive treatments [17, 41]. Positive perceptions of ACP among people with all degrees of cognitive impairment and caregivers described in the studies are as follows: noting the importance of early planning [22, 45], respecting PwCI’s autonomy [25], and enabling family members to prepare for PwCI’s future condition [25, 26, 53]. General practitioners (GPs) also expressed that ACP benefited the coordination among PwCI, nursing staff, and physicians’ goals [23].

Negative perceptions

The negative perception of ACP among people with MCI and mild dementia lay in questioning the value and usefulness of ACP [50]. Caregivers of individuals with severe dementia often did not perceive the utility of ACP leading to the conversations being delayed until it was too late [40]. Negative perceptions of ACP among people with all degrees of cognitive impairment and caregivers described in the studies were as follows: causing discomfort [27, 41], thinking about degradation was more challenging than thinking about death [22, 42], and no need to make judgments on PwCI’s future condition ahead of time [17, 40, 50, 51]. Medical professionals reflected that negative perceptions of ACP also included harming the doctor-patient relationship [23] and reservations about making formal assessments that would affect the implementation of legally binding instruments [44].

Unawareness of ACP

Lack of ACP knowledge commonly existed in PwCI, caregivers, and medical professionals. The lack of common understanding of ACP lay in the lack of awareness of ACP concepts, contents, and legal requirements [17, 30, 51, 55]; difficulty in planning for hypothetical future scenarios [17, 28, 42, 43, 45]; and concerning about PwCI’s willingness to change [17, 28, 44]. Moreover, PwCI and caregivers’ lack of ACP knowledge was reflected in fearing that ACP cannot be withdrawn once it was started [28].

Prior ACP experience

Experience from surroundings

Findings from two surveys on people with all stages of cognitive impairment indicated that those who had signed do-not-resuscitate consent were likely to participate in ACP (p < 0.05) [32, 47]. A cross-sectional study showed that medical professionals who experienced bereavement had markedly positive attitudes toward ACP [48]. Personal experiences of having loved ones or friends become ill or die from cognitive impairment promoted ACP in PwCI [22]. These experiences influenced PwCI and caregivers, particularly in the way they view where they want to live and be given care in the future [22]. Some caregivers reflected that they did not know the topics to discuss with their loved ones, mainly because they had no previous experiences with cognitive impairment [40].

Disease experience

An increased number of chronic diseases and a family history of cognitive impairment would increase the likelihood of people with all stages of cognitive impairment participating in ACP [21, 39]. Negative healthcare experiences, such as not being treated with respect when interacting with medical professionals and poor quality of care, presented challenges for PwCI and caregivers in ACP participation [26, 40].

Disease-related factors

Difficulty in accepting the disease

Some PwCI and caregivers had difficulty in accepting the diagnosis of cognitive impairment or even refused to recognize the diagnosis and their worsened condition [40, 45, 53]. They had a strong need to stay focused on the present. PwCI reportedly took years to accept a diagnosis and to begin to see beyond this situation [42]. Therefore, they were not ready to discuss ACP [41]. This finding also resulted in difficulty for medical professionals to carry out ACP [45, 52].

Cognitive decline

Cross-sectional studies have shown that the ability of PwCI to participate in ACP declines with disease progression, but people with mild dementia are associated with a significant likelihood of ACP engagement [24, 49, 50]. As the disease progressed, decline in cognitive function and lack of decision-making ability were given immense weight as barriers to initiating ACP for PwCI, caregivers, and medical professionals [27, 29, 53, 54]. Medical professionals expressed concern about PwCI’s ability to undertake ACP [43, 46, 52, 55].

Lack of cognitive impairment knowledge

Two quantitative studies focusing on people with all stages of cognitive impairment found that knowledge of cognitive impairment and life-sustaining treatments for PwCI and their caregivers could help future medical decisions [24, 32]. Lack of knowledge among PwCI, caregivers, and medical professionals about the diagnosis, developmental trajectory, and prognosis of cognitively impaired disorders affected participation in ACP [16, 20, 22, 30, 41, 42, 45, 46, 50, 52, 53]. Uncertainty about the future and various issues in the development of the disease among PwCI, caregivers, and medical professionals also hindered the implementation of ACP [16, 43].

Socio-cultural context

Traditional thoughts

In many cultures, talking about death is widely considered a taboo. As such, discussing end-of-life sensitivity discouraged PwCI and families from talking about ACP [16, 29, 45, 55]. In the Chinese culture, the life–death perspective of PwCI and social pressure influenced ACP practice [17]. Traditional family values, ageism, moral considerations, and other traditional thoughts were not conducive to ACP for all stages of cognitive impairment and caregivers [17, 20]. In different cultures, personal belief in God and different religious beliefs were found to make ACP difficult [25, 27, 56].

Relying on surrogate decisions

A cross-sectional study on MCI and mild dementia indicated that family members signing the do-not-resuscitate consent form at the end-of-life promoted participation in ACP (p < 0.05) [32]. Lack of autonomy made PwCI rely on others for decision-making and unable to make their own choices, thereby hindering ACP participation. This situation was concretely reflected by people with MCI and mild dementia and caregivers: insufficient autonomy [20], expecting close family members to handle problems when they arise [16, 27, 50, 51], and trusting spouses or children to make choices for them [22]. Relying on others for decision-making was also evident in people with cognitive impairment and caregivers, and caregivers perceived family’s support as preventing PwCI from thinking about the future [26]. However, medical professionals believed that family members override PwCI’s decisions [55].

ACP attributes

ACP procedural elements

Care network relationship

The relationship among PwCI, caregivers, and medical professionals played an important role in implementing ACP in people with severe dementia [41]. PwCI and medical professionals believed that good relationship among stakeholders was a facilitator of ACP [45, 56], but it was also an inhibiting factor [56]. Trust in professionals was considered by people with MCI to be key in drafting ACP [28].

Decision conflicts

Decision conflicts between PwCI, caregivers, and medical professionals were a barrier for ACP [30]. Medical professionals indicated that the presence of multiple family caregivers, balancing PwCI, and care partner involvement made ACP difficult [31, 56]. In emergency situations, ambulance workers reflected on the difficulty of adhering to PwCI’s expressed wishes and dealing with the tension between PwCI’s wishes and families’ preferences [44]. Non-confidence in congruent decision-making by family members and PwCI was identified as a caregiver-factor hindering the ACP implementation [27].

Uncertainty ACP implementation

Uncertainty regarding ACP implementation hindered its initiation and affected people with all stages of cognitive impairment [25, 27, 31, 40, 42, 44, 46, 52, 53, 55–59]. Uncertainty about the timing of ACP initiation was widely recognized by PwCI, their caregivers, and medical professionals as a significant impediment to ACP [27, 28, 31, 42, 44, 46, 52–54, 56, 58]. For medical professionals, the difficulty in diagnosing cognitive impairment in a timely manner and determining the decision-making ability of PwCI could lead to uncertainty about the onset of ACP [31, 55]. However, a change in health status for caregivers and physicians may be an opportunity to start discussing ACP [25, 40, 57]. The personnel involved in ACP and their responsibilities were also not clear [28, 31, 44, 53, 58]. Some GPs wanted all healthcare professionals involved in caring for people with dementia to participate in ACP [56]. However, no single professional group considered the issue of assessing people’s mental capacity to discuss ACP their responsibility [44]. In addition, the uncertain completion time of ACP discussions, frequent reviews of ACP, and unclear location of ACP implementation affected its implementation for medical professionals [44, 56].

ACP data storage

ACP data storage was an important factor for ACP of PwCI [22, 23, 25, 28, 43–45, 55, 56]. For medical professionals, the transfer and access of ACP documents [23, 44, 45, 55, 56], the extent to which ACP documents are likely to determine all possible individual cases [44], inconsistencies in the way ACP is documented [44], and uncertainty about how to determine the valid and applicable ACP documents [44] were identified as important factors for undertaking ACP. The complexity of ACP documents, expiration of the documents, and the need to repeat the process every five years (in Israel) were also considered obstacles to the behavior by PwCI and caregivers [22, 25]. MCI also indicated that having a pre-formulated template for an ACP would be beneficial [28].

ACP supportive mechanisms

Professionals’ capacity to implement ACP

The support of professional teams would affect the implementation of ACP in PwCI. People with all stages of cognitive impairment and their caregivers expressed a desire for support from healthcare professionals and multidisciplinary teams and considered that professional medical teams would start and proceed with ACP [17, 20, 51]. PwCI’s caregivers and GPs felt that medical professionals should take the initiative to discuss ACP [23, 57]. In addition, medical professionals and administrators believed that a lack of collaboration between key healthcare professionals and relevant information about treatment options may pose a threat to ACP participation [29, 46]. The lack of professional competence of medical professionals was not conducive to the implementation of ACP. Professionals could guide them understand and complete ACP. However, many medical professionals reported a lack of training in ACP-related knowledge and a lack of competence and confidence in implementing ACP [23, 43–45, 55, 56, 58, 59]. Some PwCI and caregivers did not know who to turn to for ACP-related help and questioned the competence of physicians [28, 42].

Informal social support

A mixed study of nursing home managers indicated that the level of peer support increases the likelihood of participation in ACP (p < 0.05) [46]. The support of family members was significant to ACP [25, 29, 50]. With their support, PwCI would be motivated to participate in ACP. People with MCI indicated they long for family support and felt that only with family support could ACP discussions be meaningful [20].

Social support

A supportive social environment was relatively important. People were likely to understand and accept ACP when in an environment where it is widely publicized. By contrast, lack of environment and resources to support ACP implementation was detrimental to ACP in all stages of cognitive impairment [17, 20, 43, 44, 53, 58]. GPs expressed that the increased availability of culturally and linguistically diverse education resources may assist with raising awareness about ACP in the current multicultural society [23].

Policy support

A cross-sectional study of PwCI and family surrogates suggested nursing homes with policies for ACP (p < 0.05) were a predictor of ACP [47]. PwCI, caregivers, and medical professionals reflected that the law affects PwCI’s participation in ACP discussions [17, 20, 44]. The lack of policy for professionals to conduct ACP was reflected in the fact that ACP is not in their agenda of work [29, 43]. Medical professionals were busy with clinical work, and the lack of time to participate in ACP was a concrete manifestation of the lack of policy support [31, 43–46, 54–56, 58]. In addition, the lack of funding for ACP implementation was a concern for PwCI and medical professionals [23, 43, 44, 50].

Discussion

This study provided a comprehensive review of the factors influencing ACP participation in people with different degrees of cognitive impairment, revealing that these factors vary across different degrees of cognitive impairment. We highlighted two key themes relevant to the research question: ACP antecedents and ACP attributes. Although there were many common factors in the participation of people with different degrees of cognitive impairment in ACP, some differing factors were noteworthy as well. This finding was significant for optimizing the implementation strategies of ACP. Additionally, this review identified discrepancies between quantitative and qualitative evidence regarding certain factors (e.g., positive perceptions, negative perceptions, unawareness of ACP). For instance, some factors were proposed by qualitative studies (e.g., positive perceptions of individuals with MCI toward ACP promoted their participation in it) but were not measured in corresponding quantitative research for specific cognitive stages.

The current study found that sociodemographic characteristics, ACP perception, prior ACP experience, disease-related factors, socio-cultural context, ACP procedural elements, and ACP supportive mechanisms could simultaneously affect ACP participation among people with different degrees of cognitive impairment. Sociodemographic characteristics included education level, age, race, marital status, and religious beliefs. ACP perception included positive perceptions, negative perceptions, and unawareness of ACP. Prior ACP experience included experiences from surroundings and disease experience; disease-related factors included cognitive decline and lack of cognitive impairment knowledge; socio-cultural context included traditional thoughts; ACP procedural elements included uncertainty ACP implementation; and ACP supportive mechanisms, included professionals’ capacity to implement ACP, social support, and policy support. Given that all of the factors have an impact on ACP participation for people with different degrees of cognitive impairment, this study suggested that these factors should be fully emphasized in future interventions to promote ACP participation for people with different degrees of cognitive impairment, regardless of their level of cognitive impairment.

Note that the difficulty in accepting the disease and decision conflicts may not affect the participation of people with MCI in ACP. The core diagnostic criteria for people with MCI, in which cognitive decline has not yet reached a level significantly affecting daily life, include “preservation of basic functional abilities” and “did not meet criteria for dementia” [60]. This preservation of functioning may enable people with MCI to perceive the condition with relative ambiguity and a lack of perceived urgency in the progression of the disease, thereby reducing the psychological resistance to accepting the condition. People with MCI, compared with the population with dementia, have mild cognitive decline, but most of them still have the ability to make decisions [61], making the mild cognitively impaired stage an ideal time to initiate ACP. Studies have shown that people with MCI are more receptive to ACP at this time and better able to express their wishes and needs [20]. Moreover, the core of ACP is to plan ahead for future medical wishes while the population still has the ability to make decisions on their own. The characteristics of this stage determined that decision-making conflict may not be a major obstacle for people with MCI to participate in ACP.

For people with mild dementia, care network relationship was not found to affect their participation. This study noted that some people with mild dementia may have impairment in understanding and reasoning but still retained decision-making abilities [62]. People with severe dementia have significantly reduced decision-making capacity [63], resulting in agents becoming the actual decision-making subjects, and power dynamics in care network relationships significantly affecting the ACP. By contrast, people with mild dementia may not need to be completely dependent on agents. Therefore, the care network relationship may not affect ACP.

Factors affecting participation in ACP among people with moderate to severe dementia did not include relying on surrogate decisions, decision conflicts, ACP data storage, and informal social support. In addition, factors influencing participation in ACP among people with moderate dementia did not include care network relationship. Studies have shown that even though people with moderate to severe dementia may still be able to express choices, their ability to understand and reason is drastically reduced [64]. When agentic decision-making becomes necessary, their own ability to participate in the ACP is already limited so reliance on agentic decision-making would not further affect their participation because they are no longer able to participate effectively. People with moderate to severe dementia were dominated by agents owing to their inability to make decisions, and conflict may be more at the level of the agents than themselves. ACP needs to be developed by individuals when they are in a position to make decisions, usually with the assistance of health professionals, and involved complex discussions and signing of documents. People with moderate to severe dementia have severely impaired cognitive function and decision-making capacity, and the processing of the ACP data storage no longer relied on individuals’ participation but was handled by agents. Hence, the ACP data storage may have no impact on their participation in ACP.

Participation in ACP in people with moderate to severe dementia may rely considerably on a plan that has been previously developed or on a family member’s complete agency in decision-making. Consequently, only a few studies have examined PwCI’s participation, leading to the possibility that the effect of informal social support and care network relationship may no longer be significant. In addition, cultural factors could be a reason. Asian families are more inclined to collective decision-making, which has a substantial impact on the mild stage. By contrast, in the moderate to severe stage, it may have defaulted to the family’s decision, so the impact is no longer significant.

Limitations

This study also has limitations. First, it did not include a search for grey literature, and only included literature published in Chinese or English, possibly resulting in an incomplete inclusion of studies, such as those published using other languages. Second, the included studies were mostly from high-income countries, low- and middle-income countries were underrepresented, and the representation of respondents may be limited. This aspect suggested that ACP has not yet been implemented in low- and middle-income countries. Hence, follow-up studies should focus on measures to promote ACP in low- and middle-income countries, particularly considering local socio-cultural characteristics. Third, the decision-making ability of PwCI would generally decline owing to cognitive function decline. However, this study did not consider the decision-making ability of people with cognitive impairment. Although most studies have concluded that a decline in cognitive function represents a decline in decision-making ability, some studies have also suggested a difference between the two. Thus, future studies need to further clarify the relationship between them and synthesize the effects of cognitive and decision-making functions. Fourth, some of the included studies did not specify the stage of cognitive impairment. As a result, certain factors influencing ACP participation could not be accurately integrated into specific cognitive impairment stages for analysis. Therefore, the interpretation of these results should be approached with caution. Future research should combine quantitative and qualitative methods to further explore factors influencing ACP participation across different degrees of cognitive impairment. Fifth, the outcome of ACP participation was not consistently defined in this study. Thus, meta-analysis was not considered in the quantitative study.

Conclusions

This study systematically summarized the factors influencing ACP participation among people with different degrees of cognitive impairment. The findings suggested consistency and differences in the factors influencing participation in ACP among people with different degrees of cognitive impairment. Future research can conduct targeted investigations or interventions based on the factors influencing the participation of people with different degrees of cognitive impairment in ACP, thereby promoting the implementation of ACP.
