Influence of Setup Uncertainties: Optimizing Clinical Target Volume-to-planning Target Volume Margins in Total Marrow and Lymphoid Irradiation
Authors: Henry Finlay Godson, Swathi Bapani, Tarun Jose, Retna Ponmalar, Ebenezer Suman Babu, Patricia Sebastian, Selvamani Backianathan, Rajesh Balakrishnan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Total Marrow and Lymphoid Irradiation (TMLI) is an advanced alternative to traditional Total Body Irradiation, aiming to reduce toxicities while maintaining treatment efficacy.Aim:This study evaluates setup uncertainties and their impact on clinical target volume (CTV) to planning target volume (PTV) in patients undergoing Total Marrow and Lymphoid Irradiation using a linear accelerator-based volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT).Materials and Methods:Setup deviations were analyzed for 15 patients using cone-beam CT image guidance across multiple isocenters. Systematic and random errors were computed for each anatomical region, and CTV-to-PTV margins were estimated using ICRU 62, Stroom’s, and van Herk’s formulas. Average shifts and 3D displacement vectors were quantified across the skull, thorax, abdomen, pelvis, and extremities.Results:Setup uncertainties were site dependent, with the thorax showing the largest systematic error (3.9 mm) and the leg the largest random error (2.6 mm). The mean 3D displacement vector ranged from 3.3 mm (pelvis) to 6.8 mm (leg). Based on systematic and random error analysis, differential PTV margins of 3 mm, for skull, 5 mm for thorax, abdomen and pelvis, and 10 mm for extremities were determined to maintain adequate dose coverage.Conclusion:VMAT-based TMLI requires region-specific margins to account for setup uncertainties, particularly in extremities. Image-guided adaptive strategies can optimize target coverage while minimizing normal tissue exposure. Precise image guidance and tailored margins are crucial for maximizing treatment efficacy and minimizing side effects in TMLI.

INTRODUCTION

Total body irradiation (TBI) has long been integral to conditioning regimens for hematopoietic cell transplantation, particularly in treating hematologic malignancies such as leukemia and multiple myeloma, as well as conditions such as thalassemia and Fanconi anemia.[1] Since its introduction by Nobel laureate E.D. Thomas in 1956,[2] TBI has played a vital role in myeloablative and immunosuppressive conditioning to facilitate donor cell engraftment.[3] Total marrow irradiation (TMI) was first attempted using helical tomotherapy (HT), and the results indicated a mean dose reduction in the organs at risk (OARs) of 35%–70% when compared to the standard TBI. TMI with large-field intensity modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) further reduces the dose to OARs by 29%–65% when compared with conventional TBI.[1] Total Marrow and Lymphoid irradiation (TMLI) have emerged as alternatives to TBI with the aim of reducing the toxicities induced by TBI. TMLI spares organs such as the lungs, heart, kidneys, liver, breast, parotids, oral cavity, and lens while targeting the entire skeleton and lymphoid tissues.[456] In addition, TMLI enables dose escalation, which has demonstrated reduction in probability of relapse, making it an appropriate treatment option for patients with a high risk of treatment failure.[78] Furthermore, the TMLI procedure is time-consuming and labor-intensive because of the limited availability of technological and human resources.[39]

TMLI is performed with intensity-modulated beams generated either from a linear accelerator or a tomotherapy unit. The simulation, planning, and treatment delivery of TMLI is challenging, as the entire skeleton needs to be irradiated while sparing nearby OARs.[39] The dose to OARs can potentially be reduced depending on the desired and appropriate clinical target volume (CTV) and planning target volume (PTV) coverage. For precise dose delivery in TMLI, proper immobilization, accurate patient positioning, and setup verification are of paramount importance.[10] It is often understood that the setup uncertainties are critical to the success of TMLI treatment. Therefore, it is imperative to carefully evaluate the margin between the CTV and PTV. The internal margin accounts for organ motion, whereas the setup margin accounts for patient setup and delivery uncertainties; hence, an appropriate CTV-to-PTV margin is necessary for adequate target coverage. To account for internal organ motions and setup errors, PTV is generated from the CTV with a margin of 5–10 mm.[911] Positional setup accuracy is vital during treatment because of the smaller CTV-to-PTV margin and the presence of steep dose gradients.[1213] Despite its advantages, TMLI presents considerable logistical and technical challenges due to its complexity, including extended treatment time, multiple isocentres, and stringent requirements for immobilization and setup accuracy. Accurate patient positioning is critical due to steep dose gradients and small margins between the CTV and PTV. While prior studies have demonstrated the dosimetric benefits of TMLI, limited data are available on real-world setup uncertainties and their implications for target coverage. Several studies have examined setup uncertainties specific to TMLI. Mancosu et al. reported translational errors typically within 2–3 mm using whole-body vacuum bag immobilization, emphasizing that robust immobilization significantly reduces setup deviations.[10] Chilukuri et al. highlighted the inherent difficulty in achieving reproducible positioning for extremities during TMI, particularly in the context of HT-based delivery.[3] Lambri et al. documented that extremity positioning variability in volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT)-based TMLI necessitated margins of up to 10 mm around the lower limbs.[14] These reports collectively underscore that setup uncertainties in TMLI are site-specific, region-dependent, and substantially larger in distal anatomical regions.

This study aims to quantify translational setup errors in VMAT based TMLI and evaluate the sufficiency of current CTV-to-PTV margins using established geometric uncertainty models.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient demographics

Simulation

All patients were immobilized in a supine neutral position in a whole-body vacuum bag (Klarity Medical Products, OH, USA) placed on an all-in-one board (Orfit Industries, Wijnegem, Belgium) and a 3-point thermoplastic mask (Orfit Industries, Wijnegem, Belgium) on a standard neck support to immobilize the head and shoulder. The arms, forearms, and hands were placed close to the lateral aspect of the body. The knees were placed in a comfortable position, and the height was adjusted using a vacuum bag. Two computed tomography (CT) scans with 5-mm slice thickness were acquired using a Somatom Definition AS Open CT simulator (Siemens Medical Solutions, Germany). The first scan, in head-first supine (HFS) orientation, extended from the skull vertex to the distal thighs. A second scan in feet-first supine (FFS) orientation was acquired for the lower limbs. A common reference marker was placed at the level of the greater trochanter on both HFS and FFS scans to enable image registration and treatment planning continuity. Target delineation

CT datasets were transferred to the Eclipse (v13.7) treatment planning system (TPS) (Varian Medical Systems Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA) to delineate the targets and OARs. The CTV included the entire bony skeleton, brain, spleen, and major lymphatic system. The mandible, hyoid bone, larynx, patella, and small bones of the hands/feet were excluded from the bony CTV. Lymphatics delineated included cervical (except level 1a, b), supraclavicular, infraclavicular, mediastinal, axillary, entire para-aortic chain, common iliac, internal, external iliac, obturator, presacral, and inguinal lymph nodes. The bony CTV was split into multiple segments (head and neck, thorax, abdomen and pelvis, and upper/lower limbs) to enable differential PTV margins. The generated individual PTVs were summed to create the final PTV according to the departmental protocol. OAR’s delineation included eyes, lens, parotids, thyroid, lungs, heart, liver, bowel, kidneys, and testes/ovaries.

Treatment planning and plan evaluation

Treatment plans were generated in the Eclipse TPS, using two different beam configurations. This process involved beam placement, treatment planning, and plan validation. The VMAT and three-dimensional-conformal radiation therapy (3D-CRT) plans were generated on both head-first and feet-first CT images, respectively. The 3D-CRT plan was first created to cover the target volume from the foot to the mid-thigh using two isocenters. VMAT planning was performed on the head-first CT images with either three or four isocenters for patients whose length between the skull and trochanter (CT origin) was ≤ 64 cm or > 64 cm, respectively. The isocenters were positioned at the pelvis, abdomen, thorax, head, and neck region, and further beams were fixed using arcs and asymmetric fields. The placement of isocenters at different anatomical locations has been detailed in our earlier publication.[9] VMAT plans were generated with the AcrurosXB algorithm using 6 MV photon beams in the Eclipse TPS. A prescription dose of 13.5 Gy in 9 fractions for 8 patients and 15 Gy in 10 fractions for 7 patients, with 1.5 Gy per fraction, was administered twice a day, with a minimum of 6–8 h interval between each fraction for 5 days from Monday to Friday.

Setup verification and treatment delivery

VMAT-based TMLI treatment was administered using a Clinac 2100 CD linear accelerator (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, USA). The patient was first positioned HFS on the couch to treat the target volume from the skull to the mid-thigh with either 3 or 4 isocenters, depending on the length of the patient. Initially, the isocenter corresponding to the head and neck region was positioned, and a kilovoltage cone-beam computed tomography (kV-CBCT) image was acquired and registered to the planning CT using automatic 3D rigid registration limited to translational degrees of freedom. The registration was subsequently reviewed and verified manually by the radiation therapist and the attending radiation oncologist to ensure accurate alignment. The 3D registration results were recorded, the shift values were applied to the couch only in translational directions (lateral, longitudinal, and vertical), and the rotational shift (yaw) was not accounted for. If the observed deviation was within the predefined tolerance limits, no shift was applied, else, the patient was repositioned, and the setup verification process was repeated before treatment delivery. After treatment, the patient was shifted longitudinally to the subsequent isocenter positions, and the corresponding treatment plans were delivered after kV-CBCT image verification to treat the adjacent regions, including the thorax, abdomen, and pelvis. In the FFS orientation, the patient was rotated by 180° with the same immobilization to irradiate the lower limbs, and kV imaging was performed before treatment. If the shifts in each isocenter were within acceptable limits, treatment was performed without repositioning the patient. The shift values obtained at each isocenter using kV-CBCT imaging were documented separately for head-first and feet-first CT orientations in the TMLI error margin shift template protocol sheet of our department. To ensure geometric accuracy of treatment delivery within the tolerance limits defined in the treatment plan, setup verification was performed using acquired images compared against reference images during the first three fractions. When the observed shifts along the three axes were within tolerance, treatment was delivered; else, the patient was repositioned, and treatment proceeded only after repeating image verification. The mean shift across the first three fractions for each isocenter was calculated and subsequently adopted as the reference value for the remaining fractions. From fractions 4 to 10, patient positioning was performed using this new mean shift, with treatment delivered only if the deviation remained within the predefined tolerance limits. Uncertainty analysis

Average shifts observed in lateral, longitudinal, and vertical axes at each isocenter, namely, Head and Neck, Thorax, Abdomen, Pelvis region, Thigh, and Leg, were noted for all fractions. During a treatment session, systematic or random deviations from the intended irradiation geometry may occur. Systematic errors emerge if the mean irradiation geometry deviates from the treatment plan geometry, whereas random errors vary from fraction to fraction around the mean deviation. The systematic and random errors[15] were calculated for each region.

The systematic error for the population (∑) was estimated by calculating the standard deviation of the average value of the individual mean setup error along the vertical, longitudinal, and lateral directions. Random error for the population (σ) was calculated by taking the root mean square value of individual standard deviations along the vertical, longitudinal, and lateral directions. In addition, the 3D displacement vector was calculated by combining the errors observed in each of the three axes. The square root of the sum of the squares of the PTV margins determined in each axis was used to compute the maximum 3D displacement vector. Further, the CTV to PTV margins in the lateral, longitudinal, and vertical directions were determined using international commission on radiation units (ICRU) 62 formula √(Σ2+ σ2), Stroom’s margin recipe (2Σ +0.7 σ), and van Herk’s formula (2.5Σ +0.7 σ).[161718] Stroom’s formula claims that, on average, >99% of the CTV takes at least 95% of the prescribed dose, whereas the van Herk formula signifies a minimum dose coverage of 95% for 90% of patients. However, given the extensive target volume in TMLI and the clinical imperative to minimize dose to critical organs, this 90% coverage criterion was considered to be appropriate. In the absence of specific target coverage guidelines for TMLI, this approach is also consistent with AAPM TG-29, which allows a ± 10% dose variation relative to the prescribed midline dose in TBI.[19]

DISCUSSION

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of setup uncertainties in VMAT-based TMLI, a technically demanding radiotherapy modality that requires the precise delivery of dose to extensive skeletal and lymphatic target volumes while sparing critical organs. The utilization of multi-arcs and multi-isocenters in VMAT necessitates meticulous attention to patient immobilization and setup accuracy. By quantifying translational setup errors and deriving CTV to PTV margins using established formulas, our findings offer actionable insights for optimizing treatment accuracy in TMLI.

Accurate patient setup in TMLI is critical due to the use of steep dose gradients and small margins around extensive target volumes. The setup errors presented in Figures 4-7 and used for systematic and random error calculations represent the initial positioning deviations detected by CBCT before any corrective repositioning was applied. These data reflect the inherent setup uncertainty of the immobilization system and positioning protocol. From the fourth fraction onward, the mean shift derived from the first three fractions was applied prospectively, verified using CBCT imaging, and corrected, if necessary, before treatment delivery. Despite the use of a whole-body vacuum bag and a three-point thermoplastic mask, our data showed residual setup errors, particularly in regions distant from the primary immobilization anchor. Although the observed mean setup corrections were generally minimal due to kV-CBCT verification before each fraction, they nonetheless highlight the inherent challenges in achieving perfect daily reproducibility in extensive, multi-isocentric treatments. Persistent mean shifts, particularly in the longitudinal and vertical axes across several regions, underscore the influence of factors such as patient movement, anatomical variations, and the limitations of sophisticated immobilization devices.[20] The legs demonstrated the largest 3D displacement vector (6.8 ± 2.7 mm), underscoring the challenges of maintaining immobilization fidelity over long anatomical spans. This increased uncertainty in the distal extremities could be attributed to indescribable shifts within the vacuum bag and the increased distance from the primary head and shoulder immobilization.[2122]

Challenges in patient setup for total marrow and lymphoid irradiation

Several logistical and anatomical challenges complicate patient setup in TMLI. First, the sheer length of the target volume, extending from the skull to the feet, necessitates multiple isocentres, which introduces additional opportunities for inter-isocenter misalignments.[23] Each isocenter shift and re-imaging prolongs the treatment duration, increasing the risk of intra-fractional movement, a source of geometric uncertainty that is discussed further below.[24] Second, immobilizing the lower extremities remains particularly difficult. While the upper body benefits from thermoplastic masks and closer proximity to image-guidance equipment, the legs often rest unsupported in the vacuum bag alone, which may allow subtle positional drift. Moreover, inconsistencies in patient compliance, variations in limb positioning, and the inability to apply rigid fixation to joints such as knees and ankles further increase uncertainty.[1425] Third, the VMAT technique itself involves steep dose gradients near critical organs, leaving little room for error. Even minor shifts can lead to underdosing of the CTV or overdosing of OARs. In our protocol, CBCT-based verification was used exclusively for translational positioning correction, and no adaptive re-planning was undertaken in this cohort. When residual deviations exceeded predefined tolerance limits following initial positioning, patients were repositioned and re-imaged prior to treatment delivery. This workflow ensured geometric accuracy within acceptable bounds while maintaining treatment efficiency. Rotational errors were not corrected as the treatment delivery platform did not support 6DOF couch corrections at the time of this study. Rotational deviations were therefore not systematically recorded and cannot be quantified. Passive rotational constraint was provided by the three-point thermoplastic mask for the head and shoulders, and by the whole-body vacuum bag for the trunk and lower extremities. However, residual rotational errors, particularly in the distal extremities that are furthest from the primary immobilization reference, cannot be excluded. This highlights the importance of routine image guidance and the need for an individualized margin strategy that reflects both the magnitude and source of setup error.

Sources of uncertainty

Geometric uncertainties in TMLI arise from multiple interacting sources. Inter-fractional patient positioning variability, the dominant source quantified in this study, was reflected in translational shifts ranging from sub-millimeter values to approximately 6–7 mm across anatomical regions, with the largest displacements observed in the distal lower extremities as detailed in the results section. Despite the use of whole-body vacuum bags and three-point thermoplastic masks, residual systematic deviations attributable to immobilization imprecision were most prominent in the thorax (Σ = 3.6 mm) and extremities (Σ = 3.3 mm for the leg). Intra-fractional motion, arising from patient fatigue, prolonged treatment sessions, and physiological factors such as respiratory excursion, bowel peristalsis, and bladder filling, was not directly measured in this study but represents a recognised source of additional geometric uncertainty, particularly given the twice-daily fractionation schedule employed. Mechanical uncertainties inherent to multi-isocenter VMAT delivery, including couch repositioning reproducibility and laser alignment precision, constitute further sources of error that are difficult to isolate and quantify independently. Finally, rotational deviations, which could not be systematically recorded in this study owing to the absence of six degrees-of-freedom couch capability, may contribute to residual geometric inaccuracy, particularly in anatomical regions distant from the primary immobilization reference point.

Error analysis and margin implications

The calculated systematic and random errors provided a deeper understanding of the nature of these geometric inaccuracies. Systematic errors (∑), representing consistent deviations across treatment sessions, were most prominent in the thorax (3.6 mm) and legs (3.3 mm). These likely reflect limitations in immobilization technique, persistent patient positioning tendencies, or internal anatomical variability.[26] Random errors (σ), capturing day-to-day fluctuations, were most evident in the abdomen and skull, possibly due to the influence of unpredictable factors, such as subtle variations in patient positioning on the treatment couch, or minor movements during the imaging and treatment process, or differences in patient relaxation or internal organ shifts.[27] The relatively larger random errors observed in certain regions, such as the longitudinal direction of the skull and the vertical direction of the leg, warrant further investigation into potential region-specific sources of variability.

In this study, established margin recipes such as ICRU 62, Stroom, and van Herk were applied to translate measured setup uncertainties into clinically applicable CTV-to-PTV margins. Among these, the van Herk formula, which ensures that 95% of the prescribed dose reaches the CTV in 90% of patients, produced the largest margins, particularly in anatomically challenging regions such as the thorax and lower extremities. This finding underscores the clinical trade-off between achieving sufficient target coverage and minimizing unnecessary radiation to adjacent healthy tissues.[18] Although this formula ensures adequate target coverage, it does not directly account for OAR constraints. Given the critical importance of organ-at-risk sparing in TMLI and the need to adhere to tolerance dose constraints, a target coverage of 90% near the critical organs was accepted. Considering the extensive irradiation volume inherent to TMLI and the associated long-term risks, including marrow suppression and secondary malignancies, a differential, region-specific approach to margin expansion was adopted in this study. Isotropic margins ranging from 3 mm to 10 mm were applied based on the degree of observed setup variability in each region. The margins selected reflect a pragmatic balance between theoretical margin considerations and practical clinical implementation in routine practice. The region-specific margins derived from this cohort can be regarded as conservative estimates. In TMLI, the clinical implementation of PTV margins must balance target coverage probability against the unavoidable increase in high-dose exposure to critical organs associated with larger margins. This tailored strategy reflects and aligns with the growing consensus in contemporary radiation oncology toward personalized margin design, with the goal of maximizing the therapeutic ratio by tailoring the margin to the specific geometric uncertainties of each treatment region. The smaller margins applied to the skull, where the setup was more consistent, and larger margins for the more variable extremities demonstrated the practical application of this principle. Moreover, daily CBCT-based image guidance with verification and correction of positioning errors before each fraction substantially reduces residual setup uncertainty, enabling the use of reduced PTV margins of 3 mm for the skull, 5 mm for the thorax, abdomen, and pelvis, and 10 mm for the extremities. Appropriate margin selection is particularly important in workflows without IGRT.

Our findings are in concordance with and build upon previous work in the domains of TBI and TMLI, offering both quantitative validation and clinical insight. Mancosu et al.[10] demonstrated that robust immobilization, such as the use of whole-body vacuum bags and thermoplastic masks, significantly reduces setup deviations, with reported translation errors typically within 2–3 mm. Our data similarly show reduced variability in the skull and pelvis regions, where mean shifts remained below 2 mm across axes, validating the effectiveness of our immobilization protocol. Chilukuri et al.[3] previously highlighted the difficulty in achieving a reproducible setup for the extremities during TMI, which aligns closely with our observation of the largest 3D displacement in the leg region (6.8 ± 2.7 mm). This reinforces the clinical need for either enhanced immobilization techniques or more generous PTV margins in distal anatomical areas.

Our findings align with and enhance existing TMLI research by offering a direct comparison with literature-reported margin values. For instance, Lambri et al.[14] noted that in their VMATbased TMLI protocol, margins of up to 10 mm around the extremities were employed to account for positional variability that closely matches our recommendation of 10 mm for legs to ensure consistent coverage. For localized treatment with VMAT technique in the thoracic and pelvic region, the typical van Herk derived margins range from 5.8 to 7.2 mm, with the thoracic region often requiring about 7.2 mm of craniocaudal expansion.[28] These values correlate well with our margin calculations, particularly the 5 mm margin recommended for the thorax, abdomen, and pelvis. The literature reports a notable reduction in skull and head-and-neck margins to 3–5 mm with robust immobilization and daily CBCT guidance; accordingly, a 3 mm margin was proposed for the skull.[29] By contextualizing our margin strategy within these concrete values, 10 mm for extremities and approximately 5 mm for torso regions, our study reinforces a practical, data-informed framework that is grounded in both TMLI-specific experiences and broader IGRT margin practices. In addition, the study by Anjanappa et al.[30] in nasopharyngeal carcinoma patients confirmed that setup errors are site-specific and not uniformly distributed, supporting our region-wise approach to PTV margin determination. Integrating measured setup errors with established margin recipes enables a practical, data-informed approach to tailor CTV-to-PTV margins for each anatomical region, maintaining dose coverage and reducing unnecessary organ exposure.

Limitations and strengths

The relatively small sample size (15 patients), while adequate for preliminary insights, limits the statistical power and generalizability of the results. Larger, multi-institutional datasets would help validate the observed patterns of setup error and margin adequacy. The study cohort included a broad age range (5–49 years), resulting in substantial variability in body size and anatomy. While this heterogeneity reflects real-world clinical practice in TMLI for hematologic malignancies, it may have contributed to the observed setup uncertainties. Future studies with larger patient populations are warranted to assess whether age-or size-specific margin adaptations could further improve treatment accuracy. In our institutional workflow, treatment commences approximately 7–10 days after CT simulation, as the intervening period is occupied by the sequential steps of target delineation, multi-isocenter treatment planning, plan evaluation, and pretreatment quality assurance. While this interval carries a theoretical risk of interim anatomical change, particularly in younger patients or those with rapidly responding disease, no clinically apparent volumetric or positional variation was documented in any patient in this cohort. Formal volumetric assessment comparing simulation CT with treatment-day CBCT was not performed in this retrospective study. A prospective study currently underway at our institution will systematically evaluate day-to-day anatomical variations in TMLI patients using serial CBCT-based volumetric assessment across the full simulation-to-treatment-completion timeline, the findings of which will be reported separately. In addition, this study relied exclusively on rigid registration for CBCT-based setup verification. For treatments extending from the skull to the feet, variations in spinal curvature, posture, and breathing-related deformations cannot be fully corrected using translational couch adjustments alone. The lack of deformable image registration limits the assessment of internal organ motion and deformation, especially in mobile regions such as the thorax and abdomen. Incorporating deformable image registration could provide valuable insights into internal anatomical changes and help further refine the required PTV margins.[31] Future studies incorporating 6DOF couch corrections and analyzing the magnitude and impact of rotational errors would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the overall geometric uncertainty in TMLI. The proposed margins account for inter-fractional setup variations corrected by pretreatment CBCT but do not capture intra-fractional motion. Future studies employing mid-treatment or posttreatment verification imaging are warranted to quantify these additional uncertainties.

A notable strength of this study lies in its detailed, region-specific analysis of setup uncertainties across multiple anatomical subsites in patients undergoing VMAT-based TMLI. Variations in patient positioning were assessed using daily CBCT imaging, allowing translational shifts to be calculated with high accuracy. The application of established margin recipes, namely, ICRU 62, Stroom, and van Herk, adds robustness to our methodology and enhances the generalizability of our recommendations. Furthermore, the use of a consistent immobilization protocol and standardized image registration approach allows for internal consistency across patients and treatment sites. The adoption of differential PTV margins based on observed region-specific set-up variability represents a significant step towards a more personalized and precise approach to TMLI delivery. Future research should focus on expanding the patient cohort, incorporating analysis of rotational errors, and investigating the role of internal organ motion to further enhance the accuracy and efficacy of this challenging but potentially curative treatment modality.

CONCLUSION

This study highlights the critical role of setup accuracy in the successful delivery of VMAT-based TMLI. Systematic analysis of translational setup errors across multiple anatomical regions demonstrated the feasibility and necessity of applying region-specific CTV-to-PTV margins. In clinical practice with daily CBCT-based image guidance and online correction, reduced margins may be appropriate based on observed displacements and residual uncertainties. These findings support the adoption of differential isotropic margin strategies in TMLI, ensuring adequate target coverage while preserving organ-at-risk sparing in this large-volume treatment. Future work should focus on integrating rotational error analysis, adaptive planning, and deformable image registration to further enhance precision and treatment outcomes in TMLI.
