Identification of an Anoikis-Related Gene Signature to Predict the Prognosis in Patients With Oral Squamous Cell Carcinoma
Authors: Xiaoqian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnoikis plays an important role in cancer metastasis. Nevertheless, research that focuses on the prognostic values of anoikis-related genes (ARGs) in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is still scarce.MethodsThe ARG-related gene signature was developed via LASSO regression using the TCGA-OSCC cohort. OSCC patients in the TCGA_OSCC and GSE41613 datasets were grouped into high- and low-ARG score (H-score, L-score) groups, and the overall survival (OS) between the 2 groups was analysed. Meanwhile, functional enrichment, CIBERSORT and drug sensitivity were conducted based on the data from the TCGA_OSCC cohort. Loss-of-function assay was employed to explore the biological roles of SPINK1 in OSCC.ResultsIn the current research, we constructed a novel prognostic model based on 5 ARGs (SPINK1, PLAU, VEGFA, PLK1 and EGF) for OSCC. The OS of OSCC patients in the H-score group was worse than that in the L-score group. Moreover, patients with the H-score had low immune and ESTIMATE scores, suggesting a declined immune status in these patients. Additionally, some tumour-promoting signalling pathways (e.g. focal adhesion, PI3K-Akt and WNT signalling pathways) were notably activated in the H-score group. Functionally, downregulation of SPINK1 notably suppressed OSCC cell viability, migration and invasion and induced ferroptosis through inactivation of the Nrf2/GPX4 signalling pathway.ConclusionWe have developed a novel 5-gene signature related to ARG, which may serve as a predictor for OS in patients with OSCC. Our findings may open a new possibility of providing personalised treatment for OSCC patients.

Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is the main type of malignant tumour in the oral and maxillofacial region, which accounted for about 90% of all oral tumours.1 Betel quid chewing, cigarette smoking and alcohol consumption are potential risk factors contributing to OSCC.2 It has been reported that OSCC arises from the oral epithelium tissues (e.g. specialised, keratinised, non-keratinised and tongue epithelium), with high rates of metastasis/recurrence.3 In recent years, surgery combined with adjuvant systemic therapies (e.g. chemotherapy, radiotherapy or immunotherapy) has been applied increasingly in the treatment of OSCC.4,5 Nevertheless, the prognosis of OSCC is still relatively poor, with a 5-year survival rate of 60%-80%.6 Thus, it is imperative to develop promising therapeutic targets for improving the prognosis of OSCC.

Anoikis is a form of programmed cell death induced by the loss of cell–cell connections or cell–extracellular matrix (ECM) adhesions.7,8 Evidence has shown that cell–cell and cell–ECM adhesions exert crucial roles in maintaining tissue integrity, promoting angiogenesis and facilitating cell migration.9 However, when the cell–ECM adhesion is disrupted, anoikis is increased.8 Anoikis plays an indispensable role in preventing cancer invasion and metastasis by inhibiting detached tumour cells from colonising other substrates.10 Unfortunately, cancer cells can develop anoikis resistance via several mechanisms (e.g. epithelial-mesenchymal transitions) to ensure growth and metastasis.11 Thus, anoikis resistance is a critical step contributing to tumour metastasis. Anoikis-resistant tumour cells have been associated with increased tumour burden and incidence, as well as a higher occurrence of metastatic lesions.12,13 Kamarajan et al. found that downregulation of sirtuin-3 expression could suppress anoikis resistance and prevent tumour growth in OSCC.12 Therefore, uncovering promising biomarkers may be beneficial in inhibiting OSCC progression.

Recently, anoikis-related genes (ARGs) have emerged as promising prognostic biomarkers for multiple cancers, including hepatocellular carcinoma14 and lung cancer.10 Nevertheless, research that focuses on the prognostic values of ARGs on OSCC is still scarce. Here, we aim to identify a novel ARG-based prognostic signature for OSCC. This research may provide a new basis for predicting OSCC patients’ prognosis.

Materials and methods

Dataset acquisition

Transcriptomic data and clinical profiles for patients with OSCC were acquired from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://tcga-data.nci.nih.gov/tcga/) database. The TCGA_OSCC cohort included 32 adjacent normal tissues and 331 OSCC tissues. These 331 OSCC patients all had the same whole survival information. The GSE41613 dataset contained 97 OSCC tissues from HPV-negative OSCC patients, which was acquired from the Gene Expression Omnibus (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Meanwhile, 338 anoikis-related genes (ARGs) were downloaded from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/). To screen ARGs from the GeneCards database, relevance score >1.0 was set as the threshold. These ARGs were listed in Table S1.10

Identification of differentially expressed ARGs (DE-ARGs)

The R package ‘limma’ (version 3.52.4) was used to conduct the differential analysis.15 The screening criterion was set as Log2(fold change) > 1 and p.adjust < 0.05.

Function and pathway analysis of DE-ARGs

The gene ontology (GO) analysis including biological process (BP), molecular function (MF) and cellular component (CC) terms and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed based on the R package ‘clusterProfiler’ (version 4.7.1.2).16 GO terms and KEGG pathways with p.adjust < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks

The string database (https://string-db.org/,version 11.0) was used to predict the interaction between each protein.17 Meanwhile, the Cytoscape software (version 3.7.2) was used for PPI network visualisation.18

Construction of the prognostic risk model

Univariate Cox analysis was conducted to identify DE-ARGs with prognostic value. Next, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis was used to narrow down the prognostic genes using the R package ‘glmnet’. The formula for ARG risk score calculation was: ARG risk score = ∑i=1nExpi*Coefi. n, Expi and Coef represented gene number, gene expression level and the regression coefficient of this gene, respectively. The ARG score of each patient was computed. The median ARG score was set as the cutoff point to divide OSCC patients into high- and low-ARG risk score (H-score and L-score) groups.

Survival analysis

The Kaplan–Meier survival plots were created using the R package ‘Survival’ (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=survival, version 3.5-5) and the R package ‘survminer’ (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=survminer, version 0.4.9). P values were evaluated using the log-rank test.

The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was created using the R package ‘timeROC’. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was then calculated.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

The enrichment of KEGG pathways between H- and L-score groups was analysed by GSEA. Pathways with P <.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Immune infiltration analysis

The CIBERSORT software was developed to assess the relative proportion of 22 immune cell types in each sample.19 For each sample, the proportion of all estimated immune cell types equalled 1. The ‘estimate’ package (https://R-Forge.R-project.org/projects/estimate/) was used to analyse the ImmuneScore, ESTIMATEScore, StromalScore and TumorPurity of each sample.

Drug sensitivity analysis

The Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/) contains a vast amount of information about drugs, genes and tumours. The ‘oncoPredict’ package (version 0.2) analysed the sensitivity of each patient in different groups to chemotherapy drugs based on the data in GDSC.20

Cell culture and transfection

Human oral epithelial cell line (HOEC) (YS1836C) and OSCC cell lines SCC-4 (BNCC340434) and CAL-27 (BNCC359527) were cultured in high-glucose DMEM medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution and were maintained in 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

SCC-4 cells were transfected with siRNA negative control (NC), SPINK1 siRNA1 (si-SPINK1-1), SPINK1 siRNA2 (si-SPINK1-2) and SPINK1 siRNA3 (si-SPINK1-3) using the transfection kit (C10511, RIBOBIO).

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) assay

Total RNA was isolated using the TriQuick reagent (R1100) from cells. Next, total RNA was subjected to reverse transcription to cDNA using the Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit with gDNA Clean for qPCR Ver.2 (AG11728). Subsequently, qPCR was conducted using the SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (AG11701). Relative mRNA levels were analysed using the 2−ΔΔCT method, and GAPDH was used as a control. Western blot assay

The concentration of the proteins was quantified using a BCA detection assay kit (CW0014S). Proteins were then separated using 8% SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis and then transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Next, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including anti-VEGFA (19003-1-AP), anti-PLK1 (10305-1-AP), anti-PLAU (17968-1-AP), anti-EGF (27141-1-AP), anti-SPINK1 (ab207302), anti-GPX4 (67763-1-lg), anti-Nrf2 (16396-1-AP), anti-SLC7A11 (26864-1-AP) and anti-GAPDH (60004-1-Ig). The membranes were then exposed to corresponding secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 hours. Subsequently, the protein bands were visualised using the BeyoECL Moon enhanced chemiluminescence kit (P0018FS).

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

SCC-4 cells were plated in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. After treatment, 10 µl of CCK-8 reagent (C0037) was introduced into each well, and the cells were further incubated for an additional 2 hours at 37 °C. Finally, the absorbance at 450 nm for each well was measured using a microplate reader (Infinite F50).

Transwell migration and invasion assays

To assess cell migration and invasion, Transwell inserts (TCS020024) were applied and placed in a 24-well plate. The upper chamber was seeded with 1×10⁴ cells in 100 μL of medium containing 0.1% FBS while the lower chamber contained 300 μL of complete medium (10% FBS). After 24 hours, migrated or invasive cells on the membrane's underside were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (P1110) and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (G1063). Cell visualisation was performed using an inverted microscope. For the cell invasion assay, the inserts were precoated with Matrigel matrix (356234).

ELISA

The levels of malondialdehyde (MDA), Fe²⁺ and lipid reactive oxygen species (ROS) were measured using commercial kits, following the manufacturers' protocols. The results were analysed using a microplate reader (Infinite50).

Statistical analyses

The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to compare the differences in immune cell infiltration between the 2 groups. Pearson correlation analysis was performed using the R package (Version 4.3.1). P values <.05 were considered significant.

All experimental data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from ≥3 independent replicates. Student's t-test or one-way ANOVA was employed for statistical analysis, with P values <.05 deemed statistically significant.

Results

Identification of DE-ARGs between normal and OSCC tissues

To screen DE-ARGs between normal and tumour samples in the TCGA_OSCC database, 338 genes were screened by using the R package ‘limma’, of which 61 ARGs were significantly upregulated and 29 ARGs were notably downregulated in tumour samples compared with normal controls (Figure 1A, 1B, Table S2). To observe the interaction among these 90 DE-ARGs, a PPI network was constructed using the STRING database (minimum required interaction score >0.4), and Cytoscape software was used for visualisation. To know the functional enrichment of these 90 ARGs, GO and KEGG analyses were conducted. A total of 1,237 GO terms including 1,137 BP (e.g. anoikis and regulation of anoikis), 64 MF (e.g. integrin binding and cytokine receptor binding) and 36 CC (e.g. collagen-containing extracellular matrix and protein complex involved in cell adhesion) were related to 90 DE-ARGs (Table S3 and Figure 1D). Next, LASSO Cox regression analysis was performed on these 5 ARGs to determine optimal genes, which were used for constructing the prognostic model (Figure 2B). The ARG score for each patient was then computed. According to the median ARG score, OSCC patients were grouped into H- and L-score groups in the TCGA_OSCC training cohort and GSE41613 validation cohort. In both cohorts, the ARG score was inversely related to the survival time of OSCC patients (Fig. 2, Fig. 2). Additionally, PCA analysis revealed that patients with L-score could be distinguished from patients with H-score both in the TCGA_OSCC and GSE41613 cohorts (Fig. 2, Fig. 2). Moreover, compared to the L-score group, patients with the H-score had significantly poorer overall survival (OS) both in the TCGA_OSCC and GSE41613 cohorts (Fig. 2, Fig. 2). Meanwhile, the AUC-ROC technique was used to observe the ARG score’s predictive accuracy. AUC values for the 1-, 3- and 5-year curves were, respectively, 0.63, 0.62 and 0.64 in the TCGA_OSCC cohort and 0.68, 0.70 and 0.67 in the GSE41613 cohort (Figure S1A and S1B), suggesting that the ARG score could predict OSCC patient prognosis.

Additionally, a heat map displayed the expression profiles of 5 genes in H- and L-score groups. Compared with the L-score group, the mRNA levels of SPINK1, PLAU, VEGFA, PLK1 and EGF were notably elevated in the H-score group in the TCGA_OSCC training cohort (Figure 2I). Meanwhile, in the GSE41613 validation cohort, the mRNA levels of PLK1, PLAU and VEGFA were remarkably elevated in the H-score group relative to the L-score group, whereas SPINK1 and EGF mRNA levels showed no obvious difference between the 2 score groups (Figure 2J). Furthermore, RT-qPCR and western blot results showed that, compared with HOEC cells, the mRNA and protein levels of PLAU, VEGFA, PLK1, EGF and SPINK1 were obviously elevated in OSCC cells (Fig. 2, Fig. 2).

Prognostic value of ARG score in OSCC

Next, based on the data in the TCGA_OSCC training cohort, the prognostic value of the ARG score for OSCC patients was tested in patients with different clinical features including age, gender and stage. As shown in Figure 3A and 3B, patients with H-score had an obvious worse OS than patients with L-score in the female and male subgroups. Meanwhile, when stratified into age ≥65 or age <65 subgroup, patients with H-score have a significantly poorer OS than patients with L-score (Fig. 3, Fig. 3). Furthermore, in the stage (II, III or IV) subgroups, patients with H-score also displayed worse OS relative to patients with L-score (Figure 3E-3G). We further investigated the relationship between ARG score and 18 immune checkpoints, including TNFSF14, IDO2, IDO1, CD40LG, PDCD1 (PD1), CD274 (PDL1), PDCD1LG2 (PDL2), CTLA4, LAG3, TIGIT, BTLA, LGALS9, LAIR1, TDO2, TMIGD2, NPR1, KIR3DL1 and VTCN1. Meanwhile, the ARG score was positively associated with TDO2 and TNFSF14 but negatively associated with LAG3, PD-1 (PDCD1) and TIGIT (Figure 5G). Additionally, we examined the connection between the ARG score and markers related to macrophages, specifically SIRP-α and Siglec-15. The findings indicated a positive correlation between the ARG score and both SIRP-α and Siglec-15 (Figure S3A and S3B).

Furthermore, the TIDE algorithm was used for assessing the potential efficacy of immunotherapy in the 2 score groups. Compared with the H-score group, L-score patients had higher dysfunction and IFNG scores and a lower exclusion score (Figure 5H).

Drugs susceptibility analysis

Next, the ‘oncoPredict’ package (version 0.2) was used for predicting patient sensitivity to anti-tumour drugs. The ARG score was negatively correlated with the IC50 values of 76 drugs such as Paclitaxel_1080, Cediranib_1922, Tozasertib_1096 and PD173074_1049 (Figure 6A and Table S5). Moreover, the IC50 values of Paclitaxel_1080, Cediranib_1922, Tozasertib_1096 and PD173074_1049 were markedly lower in the H-score group than in the L-score group (Figure 6B). Cellular redox homeostasis significantly influences various cell functions.21,22 Previous studies by Guo et al. demonstrated that SPINK1 maintains redox balance and promotes cell growth in lung cancer by functioning as an antioxidant mediator.23 Notably, glutathione metabolism, which is critically involved in redox regulation, represents one of the central mechanisms governing ferroptosis.24,25 Our GSEA analysis revealed significant enrichment of the glutathione metabolism pathway in the SPINK1 high-expression group compared with the SPINK1 low-expression group (Figure 7E), suggesting SPINK1′s potential involvement in glutathione-mediated redox homeostasis and ferroptosis regulation. Mechanistically, SPINK1 knockdown led to a substantial downregulation of Nrf2, GPX4 and SLC7A11 expression (Figure 7F). Concurrently, significant increases in MDA (an oxidative stress marker) content, Fe2+ levels and lipid ROS accumulation were observed in SPINK1-deficient SCC-4 cells (Figure 7G). These findings collectively indicate that SPINK1 inhibition promotes oxidative stress and ferroptosis in OSCC cells through the modulation of Nrf2/GPX4 signalling.

Discussion

OSCC is an aggressive tumour with a poor prognosis.26 Numerous studies have highlighted various potential biomarkers related to cancer prognosis, including TPPP3 and XPO1.27,28 TPPP3 has been identified as functioning as a tumour suppressor in OSCC and shows a correlation with favourable outcomes in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma.27 XPO1 has the ability to enhance OSCC progression via the AKT/MAPK signalling pathway and serves as a potential indicator of poor prognosis in OSCC.28 Recently, it has been shown that metastasis is a primary factor leading to poor prognosis in cancer patients.29 Anoikis has been recognised as a crucial modulator in tumorigenesis and metastasis.30 Nevertheless, the role of ARGs in the prognosis of OSCC has not yet been systematically uncovered. In the current research, we constructed an anoikis-related prognostic signature and found that this prognostic signature could evaluate the prognosis of OSCC patients.

By using the univariate Cox and LASSO regression analyses, we screened 5 valuable ARGs (SPINK1, PLAU, VEGFA, PLK1 and EGF) with prognostic significance based on the data in the TCGA_OSCC cohort. The relationship between anoikis and these signature genes has been reported by many researchers. For example, Mehner et al. also found that deficiency of SPINK1 was able to suppress the proliferation of ovarian clear cell carcinoma (OCCC) cells, and sensitise OCCC cells to anoikis, thereby suppressing peritoneal metastasis in a murine model of OCCC.31 Tan et al. found that PLAU could prevent anoikis and increase metastatic colonisation in mice bearing p53-mutant lung adenocarcinoma cells.32 PLK1 also plays an important role in anoikis resistance in human cancer.33 Lin et al. revealed that detachment-induced upregulation of PLK1 protected against anoikis in oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) cells through modulation of β-catenin.33 Furthermore, VEGFA, a key angiogenic factor, was found to be highly expressed in anoikis-resistant osteosarcoma (OS) cells than in parental OS cells.34 Similarly, EGF is also a pro-angiogenic growth factor, playing important roles in tumour angiogenesis and metastasis.35 Moreover, EGF also could induce anoikis resistance and then promote head and neck squamous cell carcinoma metastasis by upregulating ANGPTL4.36 These findings showed that all these ARGs could exert oncogenic roles in cancer progression and be related to anoikis resistance in cancer cells.

Additionally, these signature genes are involved in cancer patient prognosis. High SPINK1 level has been found to be related to poor clinical outcome in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).37 High EGF level was related to poor prognosis in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma patients.38 Meanwhile, high PLAU, VEGFA and PLK1 levels have been found to be related to poor prognosis in OSCC patients.39, 40, 41 These findings suggest that high levels of 5 signature genes may predict poor clinical outcomes in some cancer patients. Recently, the identification of gene signatures has been recognised as a promising approach for predicting the prognosis for cancer patients.42,43 Qiu et al. developed a 3-gene (CDKN2A, BIRC5 and PLAU) prognostic signature and found that this ARG signature could predict the prognosis of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma.43 Zhong et al. constructed a 9-ARG signature (PTRH2, ITGAV, ANXA5, BIRC5, BDNF, BSG, DAP3, SKP2 and EGF) and indicated that it could predict the prognosis of HCC patients.42 Thus, to evaluate the prognostic value of the ARG score established in the current research, the OS in OSCC patients between the L- and H-score groups were compared. Here, the expression levels of these 5 signature genes were all higher in the H-score group than in the L-score group, and OSCC patients in the H-score group had a remarkably worse OS compared with those in the L-score group. Collectively, the ARG score could predict the prognosis for OSCC patients.

Next, to explore the potential mechanism of the ARG score, GSEA analysis was conducted. The results showed that patients with H-score were positively associated with some tumour-promoting signalling pathways, such as focal adhesion, PI3K-Akt, TGF-β and WNT signalling pathways. EGF and VEGFA have been found to be related to focal adhesion, which links the ECM to the internal cell cytoskeleton to regulate cell adhesion and metastasis.44,45 Mechanically, VEGF and EGF could promote tumour progression through activation of focal adhesion kinase (FAK).46,47 Meanwhile, FAK could facilitate tumour progression by activating PI3K/Akt and WNT signalling.48,49 Moreover, TGF-β could increase the production of VEGFA in tumour cells to accelerate cell growth and vessel formation in breast cancer.50 These findings showed a relationship between ARGs (e.g. EGF or VEGFA) and these tumour-promoting signalling pathways. Furthermore, SPINK1 and PLAU have been found to be involved in the regulation of the PI3K/Akt or WNT signalling.51,52 These findings may further enhance the understanding of the role of the ARG score in OSCC.

Furthermore, different immune cells may play different roles in cancer progression.53 For example, M1 macrophages and CD8 T cells exhibit anti-tumour immunity, whereas M0 and M2 macrophages exhibit pro-tumour effects.53, 54, 55 In the present study, the aforementioned anti-tumour immune cells were highly infiltrated in the L-score group, whereas M0 macrophages were not well expressed in the L-score group. These data suggested that OSCC patients in the L-score group may have higher infiltration of tumour‐killing immune cells in the tumour-immune microenvironment (TIME). Meanwhile, patients with a low ARG score had higher immune and ESTIMATE scores. These results indicated that the prognosis of GC patients in the 2 score groups might be related to the composition of the TIME.

The entry of T cells into the tumour environment is essential for effective antitumor responses mediated by immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI).56 A cold tumour microenvironment (TME), characterised by low infiltration of effector T cells, results in a limited response to ICI therapy.57 Our findings indicate that the levels of CD8 T cells were decreased in the H-score group compared with the L-score group. Meanwhile, the levels of LAG3, PD-1 (PDCD1) and TIGIT were significantly reduced in the H-score group compared with those in the L-score group. These features are consistent with an immunological ‘cold tumour’ phenotype. Therefore, patients with tumours exhibiting an H-score are likely to derive limited benefit from specific ICI therapies, such as those targeting PD-1, LAG3 and TIGIT.58

Emerging evidence highlights SPINK1 as a key regulator in cancer progression.23 Previous studies indicate its involvement in promoting proliferation, autophagy, migration and invasion across various cancer types.23,59,60 Wang et al. demonstrated that the reduction of SPINK1 led to decreased proliferation, invasion and motility of oral tongue squamous cell carcinoma cells.59 In line with earlier research, our findings indicated that silencing SPINK1 markedly reduced the viability, migration and invasion abilities of OSCC cells. Furthermore, Guo et al. discovered that SPINK1 facilitated lung cancer cell proliferation by maintaining redox homeostasis via the regulation of the Nrf2 signalling.23 The Nrf2/GPX4 axis plays a pivotal role in tumorigenesis, and its inhibition can trigger ferroptosis, offering a potential therapeutic strategy.61,62 In this research we discovered that the downregulation of SPINK1 resulted in a significant decrease in the expression levels of Nrf2, GPX4 and SLC7A11, along with notable increases in MDA content, Fe2+ concentrations and lipid ROS accumulation within SCC-4 cells. Collectively, these results suggest that the inhibition of SPINK1 facilitates ferroptosis in OSCC cells by regulating the Nrf2/GPX4 signalling pathway.

However, this research has several limitations. In this study, we provide experimental evidence that SPINK1 inhibition promotes oxidative stress in OSCC cells, as demonstrated by increased levels of MDA and lipid ROS (Figure 7G). Additionally, 4 other genes in the ARG-related 5-gene signature have been reported to be involved in redox regulation. In a membranous nephropathy model, the absence of the Plau gene leads to increased renal ROS levels and exacerbates apoptosis,63 suggesting that PLAU may have a protective role in mitigating oxidative damage. VEGFA can influence oxidative status by regulating mitochondrial function, thereby modulating cell survival after radiation.64 PLK1 inhibitors can synergistically enhance mitochondrial ROS production, inducing apoptosis in lung cancer cells.65 Additionally, ROS are involved in EGF-induced breast cancer cell invasion,66 indicating a relationship between EGF signalling and ROS dynamics. However, in OSCC, it remains largely unclear whether PLAU, VEGFA, PLK1 and EGF influence disease progression by regulating cellular redox homeostasis, necessitating further research to clarify their roles in the future. Furthermore, while we have investigated the effects of SPINK1 on the biological behaviour of OSCC cells in vitro, future animal studies are necessary to further elucidate its oncogenic role in vivo.

Conclusion

We have developed a novel ARG-related 5-gene signature in OSCC to forecast the prognosis of OSCC patients. We found that the signature could not only predict the OS in OSCC but also reflect the immune conditions of OSCC patients. Our findings may open a new possibility of providing personalised treatment for OSCC patients.
