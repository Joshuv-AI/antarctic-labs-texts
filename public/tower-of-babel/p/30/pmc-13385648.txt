Layer-specific feature preference in a trained AlexNet model revealed by stylized image analysis
Authors: Nobuhiko Wagatsuma, Kazuma Ito, Akinori Hidaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deep convolutional neural networks (DCNNs) achieve high object classification performance; however, how representational preferences systematically evolve across hierarchical layers remains unclear. Although previous studies using stylized images have revealed texture biases at the output level, it remains unclear how representational preference changes across internal layers. Here, we quantitatively show a layer-wise transition along the AlexNet hierarchy, from organization dominated by content-related low- and mid-level structural cues in early and intermediate convolutional layers to style-related organization in higher fully connected layers. Using correlation analyses, uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), and quantitative neighborhood-based metrics, we identified distinct representational structures across layers. Early and intermediate convolutional layers showed high response similarity between natural and stylized images and were primarily organized according to content-related structural cues, including edges, contours, and coarse spatial layout. In contrast, higher fully connected layers formed clusters determined by texture and material properties derived from style images. These results provide a quantitative demonstration, within a trained AlexNet model, of a layer-wise transition from content-related encoding in early convolutional layers to style-related encoding in higher layers. Our findings clarify how hierarchical representations are reorganized across stages of AlexNet and provide a benchmark for future comparisons with more recent architectures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53152-9.

Introduction

Deep neural networks form the foundational framework of advanced computational algorithms used in artificial intelligence and computer vision1,2. Models based on deep convolutional neural networks (DCNNs) have recently achieved substantial improvements in task-specific performance, particularly in object classification when trained on large-scale datasets3,4. Notably, the AlexNet model (Fig. 1) is a prominent DCNN architecture that has significantly advanced the field of object classification in computer vision5,6. The architecture of DCNNs is generally inspired by the hierarchical organization of the primate ventral visual stream, which plays a central role in object recognition7,8. The analysis of the mechanisms developed through training these DCNN models may provide valuable insights into the perceptual processes of biological visual systems9.

In several studies, researchers have analyzed the characteristics and artificial representations of AlexNet models trained using the ImageNet dataset to understand the processes they use for classifying objects5,11,12. After an AlexNet model is trained on a large-scale dataset, its lower-layer neurons typically acquire a preference for both orientation and spatial frequency5. This is consistent with the response characteristics of neurons in the primary visual cortex (V1)13 and the properties of Gabor filters. In computational neuroscience, neuronal selectivity in V1 has frequently been modeled using Gabor filters14–16. A previous study reported that, in the trained AlexNet model, the artificial representations in the intermediate and higher layers corresponded to neural responses observed in the intermediate visual cortex (V4) and inferior temporal cortex (IT)11. Another study further demonstrated that the activity patterns of model neurons in the fully connected (FC) layers of a trained AlexNet model resembled those of neuronal responses in the human IT cortex12. These findings suggest that the obtained mechanisms of the trained AlexNet model may partially reflect those of the primate ventral visual stream involved in object recognition. However, the layer-specific characteristics of artificial representations and model neuron preference in trained AlexNet models remain poorly understood. Analyzing and understanding how a trained DCNN model classifies objects may also offer valuable insights into the neural processes underlying visual object perception9.

Biological neurons in intermediate-level visual cortices may integrate feedforward inputs from lower-level areas that encode fundamental visual features such as orientation and color13. This may enable them to encode more complex visual cues, such as figure-ground segregation17,18 and object shape representations within a scene19,20, thereby progressively contributing to the formation of object-based visual perception. In particular, neurons in areas V4 and IT are thought to play a role in the perception of surface properties, such as texture and material composition, in visual images21,22. Interestingly, transforming the textures and styles of an image to generate a stylized image can substantially modulate the responses of the output layer in a trained DCNN model23. The results of these physiological and computational studies imply that representations of textures and materials may serve as important cues for human object recognition in visual scenes, as well as for object categorization by trained DCNN models. Additionally, analyzing DCNN-based models using these image textures and materials may offer deeper insights into the relationship between the artificial processes underlying object categorization in a trained AlexNet model and the neural mechanisms involved in object recognition and identification.

In this study, we examined layer-specific model-neuron preference and artificial representations in a trained AlexNet model (Fig. 1). To this end, we compared layer-wise responses to natural images with those to stylized images generated from content images (natural images; Fig. 2a) and style images (natural object surface images; Fig. 2b), using correlation analysis as a measure of response similarity. We further analyzed the activity patterns evoked by the stylized images using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP)24,25 to characterize the representational organization in each layer. The stylized images were generated using a DCNN-based style-transfer model26, which progressively altered texture and surface appearance while retaining a degree of the original structural information. The natural object surface images used as style references were selected from visual stimuli employed in previous physiological studies27. Our analyses showed that responses in early and intermediate convolutional (Conv) layers were organized primarily according to content-related visual structure, whereas responses in higher layers were shaped more strongly by texture and style information derived from the surface images. These findings reveal a layer-wise transition in representational preference, from content-related encoding in early Conv layers to style-related encoding in higher FC layers, and provide insight into hierarchical processing within the trained AlexNet model, as well as its possible similarities to, and differences from, biological visual systems.

Methods

This study did not involve any human participants or human tissue samples. All images used in this study were obtained from the publicly available ImageNet dataset 10. The dataset consists of images collected and curated by the ImageNet project and does not involve any data collection performed by the authors. Therefore, ethical approval and informed consent were not required for this study.

AlexNet of the DCNN model for classifying objects

To understand the layer-specific preference of model neurons in an AlexNet model after training using the ImageNet dataset6, we visualized and analyzed the characteristics of the artificial representation and preference of model neurons in various layers of the model. A comparison of the responses of the AlexNet model to natural and stylized images (Fig. 2c) provides insights into the correspondences and distinctions between artificial object classification and the neural mechanisms underlying visual object perception, particularly regarding the layer-specific roles and preference of model neurons23. If the model neurons in a trained AlexNet preferentially respond to fundamental visual features of natural images, such as local edges, contours, contrast patterns, and coarse spatial layout, then similar patterns of neural responses would be expected between natural and stylized images. By contrast, if the neurons preferentially respond to surface-level features, such as styles and materials, their artificial representations are likely to differ markedly between the two image types.

The architecture of the AlexNet model used in this study is shown in Fig. 1. This model is adapted from that introduced by Krizhevsky6 and includes minor modifications relative to the original AlexNet used in the 2012 ImageNet Large-Scale Visual Recognition Challenge5. The AlexNet model in the present study consists of five Conv, three max pooling, one average pooling, and three FC layers. A nonlinear activation function, the ReLU28, was applied after each Conv layer to activate the model neurons. In layers 1, 2, and 5, the model neurons activated by ReLU were given to 3 × 3 max pooling layers with a stride of 2 pixels. The output layer, positioned after the third FC layer, provides the probability that the input image belongs to one of 1000 object classes. To avoid overfitting on the training data, two dropout layers are incorporated. The PyTorch framework on a ZOTAC GeForce RTX 2070 GPU was used to train the model and run simulations of the trained model29. The training code for the model can be accessed on GitHub (https://github.com/pytorch/examples/tree/master/imagenet).

Training the AlexNet model using the ImageNet dataset

We trained the AlexNet model using the ILSVRC 2012 ImageNet classification/localization dataset10, which is a standard 1,000-class subset of the full ImageNet database. This subset contains 1,281,167 training images from 1,000 object categories and 50,000 validation images. Thus, the term “subset” refers to the relationship between the ILSVRC 2012 dataset and the full ImageNet database, not to any additional exclusion of classes or images performed in the present study.

We trained the AlexNet model using stochastic gradient descent30 as the optimization algorithm, with cross-entropy loss31 as the objective function. We initially set the learning rate to 0.01, which we subsequently reduced three times before training was terminated. We trained the model for 90 epochs with a batch size of 256 images, following the learning protocol adopted in the original AlexNet5,6. To validate our analyses, we trained 10 independent AlexNet models with different random initializations across 10 trials11.

Generation of stylized images

To examine the characteristics of artificial representations and the feature preferences of model neurons in individual layers of the trained AlexNet model for object classification, we presented stylized images to the model. These images were generated using 64 natural images as content images (Fig. 2a) and eight natural object surface images as style images (Fig. 2b). The 64 content images were selected from the official validation set of the ILSVRC 2012 ImageNet classification/localization dataset, which is the standard 1,000-class subset of the full ImageNet database10. Specifically, we used files ILSVRC2012_val_00000001.JPG to ILSVRC2012_val_00000064.JPG; these images corresponded to 64 distinct ground-truth categories in the validation annotations. The eight style images were selected from visual stimuli previously used in physiological experiments that recorded neuronal responses to surface-related features and materials in monkey visual cortices27. No additional human image dataset was used in this study.

We generated the stylized images used in this study using AdaIN26 to transfer the style of the natural images. The corresponding source code is publicly available on GitHub (https://github.com/naoto0804/pytorch-AdaIN). To generate the stylized images, we set α, which is the dimensionless weighting parameter that controls the contribution of style-image–derived features to the visual appearance of the output, to 0.75. For the main analyses, we selected α = 0.75 as an intermediate stylization condition that preserved a measurable degree of content-related structure while introducing substantial style-derived texture and material information. This intermediate setting was intended to make potential layer-wise differences in representational preference more visible than would be expected under weaker stylization (more content-related) or stronger stylization (more style-related) conditions. Additional analyses were performed for α = 0.5 and 1.0 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Figure 2c presents examples of the stylized images used in our analysis. The stylized images retained a degree of the original structural information, although local contours and shape-related image structure were altered by the style-transfer procedure. By contrast, the textures and styles of the stylized images appeared to differ markedly from those of the original images. Notably, the results of psychophysical experiments demonstrated that, even after style transfer, human participants generally perceived objects in the natural and stylized images in a similar manner23.

We assumed that if the activities of model neurons in response to natural and stylized images are uncorrelated in a given layer, the neurons in this layer may contribute to artificial representations of visual features, such as styles and materials, that differ between natural and stylized images. To further examine whether the observed layer-wise trend depended on the use of a linear similarity measure, we additionally computed the Spearman rank correlation, a rank-based measure of monotonic association that does not assume linearity.

To further examine the characteristics of artificial representations and the response preferences of model neurons in individual layers of the trained AlexNet model, specifically for object classification, we used UMAP, which is a dimensionality reduction method that projects high-dimensional data into a low-dimensional space while preserving the local relationships and similarities among neighboring points24,25. This method allowed us to reduce the extremely high-dimensional responses of the model neuron population in each layer to a two-dimensional space, thereby enabling the visualization of the artificial representations and model neuron preference within that layer. We used the Python class “umap.UMAP” for the UMAP analyses with its default parameters, which we applied consistently across all analyses presented in this study. We used the following parameters: n_neighbors = 15, n_components = 2, min_dist = 0.1, metric = ‘euclidean’, and random_state = None. We used the same UMAP parameter settings for all layers to ensure consistency and comparability across layer-wise analyses. Because UMAP is a stochastic dimensionality-reduction method, the exact two-dimensional layouts may vary across runs when the random state is not fixed. However, our main conclusions were based not only on visual inspection of the embeddings but also on neighborhood-based quantitative analyses explained in the next paragraph. In addition, qualitatively similar layer-wise tendencies were observed with t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE)32, indicating that the main conclusions were not specific to UMAP.

To further elucidate the layer-dependent preferences of model neurons in the AlexNet model, we quantitatively evaluated the results of the UMAP analyses by examining the local distances between data points (Fig. 3). We randomly selected a point corresponding to the response of a model neuron from the two-dimensional map of UMAP (Fig. 3a). We further examined whether the selected model neuron shared common content or style with its five nearest neighboring model neurons (Fig. 3b and c). For each trained model, we estimated the intensity values among the nearest neighboring neurons with respect to shared image content or image style by averaging across 50 repeated trials of this procedure. We normalized the resulting intensity values by dividing them by 250 and used the normalized values as a metric to quantify the similarity in preference among neighboring model neurons. The similarity metric ranged from 0 to 1.0. A value of 1.0 indicated that the clusters in the UMAP embeddings were organized exclusively according to shared image content or image style. By contrast, the similarity value decreased when the model neuron responses to stylized images with differing contents or styles were mapped in close proximity to the response of the selected model neuron. In addition, we applied this neighborhood-based quantitative analysis to the previously obtained t-SNE results32.

Quantitative evaluation of structural preservation in stylized images

To quantify the degree of structural preservation in the stylized images, we performed image-based analyses using Canny edge overlap33, gradient-map correlation34, the structural similarity index (SSIM)35, and Fourier-based measures36. where EN and ES denote the binarized edge maps obtained from the natural and stylized images, respectively. For Canny edge detection, grayscale images were first smoothed using a 3 × 3 Gaussian kernel with σ = 0 in OpenCV, and edges were then detected using lower and upper hysteresis thresholds of 100 and 200, respectively. The resulting edge maps were binarized and compared using the Dice coefficient as the primary edge-overlap measure. Intersection over union and Pearson correlation between the binarized edge maps were also computed as supplementary edge-based measures.

Gradient-map correlation assessed the similarity of local intensity-change patterns34,36. In this study, gradient-map similarity was evaluated by computing Sobel gradients in the horizontal and vertical directions using a 3 × 3 kernel, calculating the gradient magnitude, and then computing the Pearson correlation between the gradient-magnitude maps of the natural and stylized images. Fourier-based measures were used to evaluate changes in spatial-frequency components, including low- and high-frequency information and the radial frequency profile36. These measures were computed for each natural–stylized image pair and summarized across all 512 image pairs for each α condition. For the Fourier-based analyses, two-dimensional Fourier amplitude spectra were computed from grayscale images, and the zero-frequency component was shifted to the center of the spectrum. Radial amplitude profiles were then obtained by averaging amplitudes within integer radial-frequency bins. Low- and high-frequency energies were defined as the mean amplitudes in the lowest 20% and highest 20% of radial-frequency bins, respectively. Low- and high-frequency differences were computed as absolute differences between the natural and stylized images, and radial-profile similarity was quantified using Pearson correlation.

Results

We analyzed model-neuron responses to stylized images generated with α = 0.75 (Fig. 2c). The 10 independently trained AlexNet models achieved a mean top-5 accuracy of 77.81 ± 0.04% on the 50,000 ImageNet validation images10.

To quantify the degree of structural preservation in the stylized images, we performed additional image-based analyses using Canny edge overlap33, gradient-map correlation34, SSIM35, and Fourier-based measures36 (Supplementary Table 1). These analyses revealed a monotonic decrease in structural similarity with increasing style-transfer strength. Specifically, the Dice coefficient, gradient-map correlation, and SSIM were highest at α = 0.5 and lowest at α = 1.0, indicating that stronger stylization progressively altered local edge configurations and overall image structure. In parallel, both low- and high-frequency differences increased with α, whereas radial-profile correlation remained high across conditions. These results indicate that stylization did not perfectly preserve contours or shape, but instead retained a progressively smaller degree of structural information as the contribution of style increased.

We observed a marked peak in the correlation coefficient rl in the lower-level layers, particularly in the Conv layer of layer 2. This result suggests that the artificial representations in the lower-level layers for the stylized images may share similar characteristics with those for the natural images. However, the magnitude of the correlation coefficient rl decreased as the network layer level increased. The reduced correlations that we observed in intermediate- and higher-level layers may reflect differences in visual style between the natural and stylized images. It is possible that model neurons in the higher-level layers of the trained AlexNet model may be more strongly influenced by artificial representations of image textures and material properties.

To test whether the observed layer-wise trend depended on the use of a linear similarity measure, we repeated the analysis using Spearman rank correlation (Supplementary Fig. 3). This analysis yielded a qualitatively similar pattern to that obtained with Pearson correlation (Fig. 4), with relatively higher similarity in earlier layers and an overall reduction toward higher layers, indicating that the main finding was not specific to Pearson correlation.

This dimensionality reduction technique aids the visualization of high-dimensional data while preserving the local and global structure and point-wise similarity relationships24,25. Quantitative analyses of UMAP embeddings for the layer-specific preferences of the trained AlexNet model based on local distances

To further elucidate the results of the UMAP analyses shown in Figs. 5, 6, and 7, we performed quantitative analyses of the UMAP embeddings based on local distance measures (see “Methods”). In the quantitative analyses of this study, we assessed whether each randomly selected model neuron shared common content features, derived from natural images, or style features, derived from natural object surface images, with its five nearest neighboring model neurons (Fig. 3). For each trained model, we computed the intensity values for the selected model neuron and its nearest neighbors, with respect to either content or style features, by repeating this procedure 50 times. We applied this quantitative analysis to the UMAP embeddings obtained from 10 independently trained AlexNet models and computed the mean intensity values of the layer-specific preferences across these models.

The quantitative results of model neuron responses to stylized images, which suggests the layer-specific preferences of the AlexNet model, are summarized in Fig. 8. In this analysis, a normalized intensity value of 1.0 indicated complete agreement between the response of the randomly selected model neuron and those of its five nearest neighbors in their preference for either content or style features. Under this normalized intensity condition, the chance rates for content and style features were approximately 0.014 and 0.123, respectively. In Fig. 8, white and black symbols denote intensity values corresponding to the preferences of model neurons in the layer for content features and style features, respectively. Across layers 1 to 5, the intensity values associated with content feature preference (white symbols) were significantly higher than those associated with style features (black symbols) (t-test, p < 0.01; Supplementary Tables 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6). By contrast, in layers 6 to 8, the intensity values associated with style features were significantly higher than those associated with content features (t-test, p < 0.01; Supplementary Tables 7, 8 and 9). These results quantitatively support the notion that, in the trained AlexNet model, responses in the FC layers are more strongly associated with artificial representations of image styles and materials relevant to object classification, whereas responses in the Conv layers remain more closely related to content-related structural cues of the images.

Effect of style-transfer strength on layer-wise feature preference

To assess whether our findings depended on the strength of style transfer, we repeated the neighborhood-based UMAP analysis for stylized images generated with α = 0.5 and α = 1.0 (Supplementary Fig. 6), in addition to the main condition of α = 0.75. At α = 0.5, the nearest-neighbor structure was dominated by shared content across nearly all layers (Supplementary Fig. 6a). At α = 1.0 (Supplementary Fig. 6b), however, the layer-wise pattern was not uniformly style-related across the entire network. Style-based organization remained relatively weak in the earliest layers, became mixed with content-based organization in the intermediate layers, and became dominant in the later FC layers. This graded pattern is consistent with our main interpretation that early and intermediate layers retain sensitivity to image structure shared between natural and stylized images, whereas higher layers are more strongly influenced by style- and material-related features. Thus, the results obtained across different style-transfer strengths indicate that the observed layer-wise transition is not an artifact of a single α value. Rather, the transition is most clearly observed at the intermediate stylization strength, where both content-related and style-related information are available in the input images.

As shown in Fig. 8, the intermediate condition (α = 0.75) yielded a mixed pattern, with content-related organization in the early and intermediate Conv layers and style-related organization in the FC layers. Thus, the intermediate condition provided the clearest visualization of the transition because both content-related and style-related information were available in the input images. In addition, although the same UMAP parameter settings were applied across layers to ensure comparability (see “Methods” section), qualitatively similar layer-wise tendencies were also observed in our t-SNE analyses32 and Supplementary Fig. 7, indicating that the main conclusions were not specific to a particular dimensionality-reduction method.

Discussion

To understand the characteristics of artificial representations and the feature preferences of model neurons at each layer of a trained AlexNet model, we analyzed their responses to stylized images (Fig. 2c) by calculating correlations and computing UMAP embeddings across the network layers. The stylized images were produced based on 64 natural images selected from the validation data of the ImageNet dataset10 as content images (Fig. 2a) and eight natural object surface images from visual stimuli used in physiological experiments27 as style images (Fig. 2b). We observed a marked correlation peak in the lower-level layers of the trained AlexNet model, particularly the Conv layer in layer 2 (Fig. 4). Moreover, the correlation between the model neuron responses to the natural and stylized images gradually decreased as the network layer level increased. These results imply that the artificial representations in the lower-level layers of the trained AlexNet model for stylized images exhibit characteristics similar to those for natural images, likely reflecting content-related structural cues such as edges, contours, contrast patterns, and coarse spatial layout. In contrast, the relatively low correlation magnitudes observed in higher-level layers suggest that responses in these layers are less constrained by the content-related structural cues shared between natural and stylized images and are more strongly influenced by texture- and material-related properties introduced by stylization.

The UMAP analyses demonstrated that model neuron responses to stylized images in the Conv layers tended to be mapped close to those elicited by the corresponding natural images used as content images (Figs. 5 and 7a). By contrast, in the FC layers, responses to stylized images formed distinct clusters according to the natural object surface images used as style images (Figs. 6 and 7b). These findings suggest that, within the trained AlexNet model, artificial representations in the early and intermediate Conv layers are organized differently from those in the FC layers. Specifically, responses in the Conv layers remained more closely related to content-related structural cues shared between natural and stylized images, such as edges, contours, contrast patterns, and coarse spatial layout. In contrast, responses in the FC layers were more strongly organized by style- and material-related properties derived from the natural object surface images. Together, these results indicate that the trained AlexNet model exhibits a layer-wise difference in representational organization, with early and intermediate Conv layers being more closely associated with content-related structural cues and FC layers being more strongly associated with style- and material-related properties.

In the present study, correlation analyses of model neuron responses to natural and stylized images (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 3) revealed a marked peak in similarity in Conv layer 2, followed by a gradual decrease in correlation with increasing network depth. Nevertheless, moderate correlations between responses to natural and stylized images were still observed in the FC layers. At first glance, this trend may appear inconsistent with the UMAP results, which suggest that content-based clustering becomes more apparent across the Conv layers (Figs. 5 and 8). However, these analyses capture different aspects of the representations. Correlation analysis quantifies the similarity between high-dimensional response vectors to natural and stylized images, whereas UMAP emphasizes the local neighborhood structure of the responses after dimensionality reduction. Therefore, a given layer can show reduced response-vector similarity while still preserving a neighborhood organization that is structured primarily by shared content. In this sense, the UMAP results do not contradict the correlation analysis, but rather complement it by showing that content-related organization remains relatively strong in earlier layers even as detailed response similarity gradually decreases with depth.

Importantly, the decrease in correlation across deeper layers should not be interpreted as evidence that AlexNet develops a fully shape-invariant or object-centered representation comparable to the biological ideal of hierarchical vision. If the higher layers of AlexNet robustly preserved object or shape information while filtering out texture-like perturbations, responses to natural images and their stylized counterparts would be expected to remain highly correlated in these layers. Instead, the present results show that this correspondence progressively weakens with depth, and that FC-layer responses are strongly organized by the style images used to generate the stylized inputs. This indicates that the trained AlexNet model does not simply transform local edge and texture signals into a style-invariant object representation. Rather, its higher layers remain substantially influenced by texture- and material-related image properties. In this sense, the observed hierarchy differs from an idealized biological hierarchy in which higher-level object representations would be expected to show greater tolerance to changes in surface style.

Our analyses suggest a marked difference in artificial representations across hierarchical layers. Responses in the early and intermediate Conv layers were more closely related to content-related structural cues that were partially shared between natural and stylized images. By contrast, responses in the higher FC layers appeared to be more strongly influenced by visual characteristics, such as style and material properties, that differed markedly between natural and stylized images. A previous analysis of DCNN models indicated that artificial representations in the early and intermediate layers of an ImageNet-trained AlexNet and VGG16 models may show partial similarities to response properties observed in V111,37. In primates, V1 neurons are selective for the orientation of stimuli presented within their receptive fields13. Despite the substantial changes in image appearance caused by style transfer26, some structural information, including coarse contour-related cues, remained in the stylized images, particularly at lower stylization strengths (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1). These results suggest that, after training, responses in the early and intermediate Conv layers of the AlexNet model are more closely related to content-related structural cues, such as contours and contrast patterns, rather than to style- or material-specific properties. This interpretation is broadly consistent with the response properties of orientation-selective neurons in V1, while not implying a direct one-to-one correspondence between AlexNet Conv layers and biological visual cortex.

Numerous studies have reported that model neurons in the intermediate and higher layers of an AlexNet model trained on the ImageNet dataset exhibit response characteristics that partially resemble those of biological neurons in intermediate visual area V4 and inferior temporal (IT) cortex11,12. In contrast to neurons in V1, neurons in V4 are known to process more complex visual cues, including figure-ground segregation17,18 and shape-related features of objects within a scene19,20. In addition, neurons in V4 and IT have been implicated in the representation of surface properties such as texture and material20–22, although these areas have also been shown to encode shape-related features and three-dimensional object structure38,39, and the relative contributions of shape-based and texture-related coding remain an active topic of investigation. In the current study, we found that the responses of model neurons in the FC layers of the trained AlexNet model formed clusters according to the natural object surface images used as style references for the stylized inputs (Figs. 6 and 7b). These findings suggest that FC-layer representations in the trained AlexNet model are more strongly influenced by texture- and style-related image properties. Thus, our results are broadly consistent with physiological observations that higher stages of the ventral visual pathway are sensitive to complex surface-related features, while not implying a one-to-one correspondence between FC-layer representations and the coding properties of V4 or IT.

Our analyses are broadly consistent with previous studies that investigated the relationship between DCNN-based object categorization and the neural mechanisms underlying visual object recognition5,11,12. After training, the AlexNet model appears to form artificial representations for object classification through its hierarchical network structure, which partially parallels the organization of the biological visual system involved in object perception40. In addition, our findings suggest that artificial representations of material and style in the FC layers may contribute, at least in part, to the object classification behavior of the trained AlexNet model. This raises the possibility that material- and style-related information, in addition to shape-related information, may contribute to object categorization in this model. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies20,23,41. However, in both monkeys and humans, contours, colors, figures, and shapes of objects in images also serve as important cues for visual recognition19,42. Thus, our results suggest characteristic object-classification mechanisms in an AlexNet model trained on the ILSVRC 2012 ImageNet dataset, while also highlighting both correspondences and distinctions between artificial object-classification mechanisms and biological visual perception. Furthermore, the representations acquired by DCNN models are likely to depend on the dataset used for training43. Analyses of AlexNet models trained on alternative datasets, such as CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-10044, may provide further insights into the dataset dependence of artificial representations underlying object classification.

Beyond the specific issue of AlexNet as a model of biological vision, the present findings may also be considered in relation to studies of perceptual image quality assessment (IQA), in which intermediate deep features have often been found to align well with human sensitivity to structural and textural image distortions45. Although the current study did not directly evaluate perceptual quality, our results suggest a related hierarchical distinction: earlier and intermediate Conv layers retained stronger organization by content-related structure, whereas higher FC layers were more strongly influenced by style and texture information. Together with the image-based analyses showing progressively reduced structural preservation at higher style-transfer strengths, these results suggest that different stages of the network may differentially reflect structure-related and style-related components of image representation. Further work will be needed to directly test how these layer-wise properties relate to perceptual IQA metrics.

The present study focused exclusively on ImageNet-trained AlexNet. Therefore, our conclusions should be interpreted as a quantitative characterization of a historically important and interpretable benchmark architecture, rather than as a general claim about all modern vision models. Although AlexNet has often been discussed in relation to the hierarchical organization of the primate ventral visual stream, this correspondence is necessarily limited. AlexNet is a simple feedforward architecture, whereas biological visual systems include recurrent, feedback, and horizontal connections18,40,46,47. Thus, the present finding that FC-layer responses are strongly organized by style- and material-related information highlights both the usefulness and the limitation of AlexNet as a model of hierarchical vision: the network develops layer-dependent representations for object classification, but its deeper layers do not appear to implement fully style-invariant, object-centered representations.

This interpretation is important for understanding the apparent discrepancy between the correlation (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 3) and UMAP analyses (Figs. 5, 6, 7 and 8). High response similarity in early layers should not be taken as evidence for abstract object-level content coding. Rather, it likely reflects the preservation of low- and mid-level structural cues, such as local edges, contours, contrast patterns, and coarse spatial layout, that remain partially shared between natural and stylized images. Conversely, the reduced correlation in deeper layers indicates that responses to natural and stylized counterparts become increasingly divergent, while the neighborhood-based analyses show that this divergence is systematically organized by style-image identity in the FC layers. In this sense, the two analyses are complementary: correlation measures pairwise response similarity between natural and stylized images, whereas UMAP-based neighborhood analysis reveals how stylized-image responses are organized relative to shared content or style factors.

Future work should examine whether the same layer-wise transition is observed in more recent architectures, including ResNet4, vision transformers48, ConvNeXt49, and multimodal models such as CLIP50. These models differ substantially in architecture, training objective, and reported shape–texture biases, and therefore may show different degrees of robustness to style manipulation51–53. Applying the present stylized-image analysis framework across such models would clarify whether the style-related organization observed in the higher layers of AlexNet reflects a general property of ImageNet-trained convolutional networks or a limitation specific to this classical architecture.

In summary, the present study demonstrates a layer-wise shift in representational preference within trained AlexNet. Early and intermediate convolutional layers retained stronger sensitivity to content-related structural cues shared between natural and stylized images, whereas higher fully connected layers were more strongly organized by style- and material-related properties. These findings provide a quantitative benchmark for understanding how stylization reshapes hierarchical representations in AlexNet, while also emphasizing that AlexNet should not be regarded as a fully style-invariant model of biological object recognition.
