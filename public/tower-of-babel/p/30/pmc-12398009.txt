Teamwork effectiveness in intensive care units: development and validation of a brief and reliable assessment tool (TES-ICU)
Authors: Murat Tümer, Zeynep Aycan, Banu Kılıçaslan, Elif Selva Oruç, Seda Banu Akıncı
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective teamwork is essential for patient safety and quality of care in intensive care units (ICUs), where complexity, high cognitive demands, and multidisciplinary collaboration prevail. Despite its critical role, there is a lack of validated instruments specifically designed to assess teamwork effectiveness in ICUs. This study aimed to develop and validate a context-specific tool—the Teamwork Effectiveness Scale in Intensive Care Units (TES-ICU).MethodsThe study was conducted in two phases. In Study 1, a comprehensive item pool was generated through literature review and expert evaluation. Items were tested for clarity and distributional properties, and an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was used to refine the pool. In Study 2, the psychometric properties of the resulting 30-item TES-ICU were evaluated using a nationwide sample of ICU professionals in Türkiye (N = 716). Validity was assessed by examining associations with job satisfaction, organizational commitment, burnout, and turnover intention. Reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha.ResultsEFA revealed a three-factor structure explaining 56.76% of the variance: (1) Teamwork Effectiveness (16 items), (2) Effective Communication and Error Management (11 items), and (3) Effective Leadership (3 items). All subscales demonstrated high internal consistency (α > 0.85). The TES-ICU showed strong construct validity; it was positively associated with job satisfaction and commitment and negatively associated with burnout and turnover intention.ConclusionsThe TES-ICU is a reliable and valid tool to assess teamwork effectiveness in ICU settings. It can guide interventions aimed at improving team dynamics, enhancing staff well-being, and ultimately contributing to better patient outcomes. The 16-item core scale may be used as a shorter form for research or quality improvement efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13432-y.Keywords: Teamwork, Intensive care units, Teamwork assessment tool, Effective communication, Effective leadership

Introduction

Teamwork in healthcare is a dynamic and multifaceted process that requires coordinated efforts and task integration among health professionals to ensure high-quality patient care [1]. Effective teamwork contributes significantly to several critical healthcare outcomes, including patient safety, quality of care, cost efficiency, employee satisfaction, and organizational commitment [2, 3].

Intensive Care Units (ICUs), in particular, are high-acuity environments characterized by complexity, uncertainty, and rapid decision-making. These settings demand strong interprofessional collaboration, effective communication, and seamless coordination among physicians, nurses, respiratory therapists, pharmacists, and other allied health professionals [4, 5]. Given the critical role of teamwork in ICUs, accurate assessment of team effectiveness is essential for evaluating and improving care processes.

Despite growing recognition of the importance of teamwork, there is a lack of validated tools specifically designed to measure teamwork in the ICU context. Existing instruments are either too general or fail to address the unique dynamics of ICU settings, such as the high cognitive workload, multidisciplinary interactions, and stress-related factors that influence team performance [6, 7]. Furthermore, definitions of teamwork vary across studies, resulting in inconsistency in measurement and limited comparability.

To address these gaps, we developed and validated a context-specific scale—the Teamwork Effectiveness Scale in Intensive Care Units (TES-ICU)—to measure teamwork perceptions among ICU healthcare professionals. The scale development process followed rigorous methodological and psychometric procedures. This study was conducted in two phases. Study 1 involved item generation and refinement based on literature review and expert panel evaluation. Study 2 focused on the psychometric validation of the instrument and examined its associations with key organizational outcomes such as job satisfaction, organizational commitment, burnout, and turnover intention.

Method

This study was conducted in two phases between April and December 2022. The research process complied with relevant ethical guidelines and regulations. Ethical approval was obtained from the Hacettepe University Ethics Board (IRB Number: GO22/226). Data were collected using online surveys. All participants gave informed consent and were assured of anonymity and confidentiality.

Study 1: scale development and item pool construction

Literature review and search strategy

To develop the Teamwork Effectiveness Scale for Intensive Care Units (TES-ICU), a comprehensive literature review was conducted using databases such as PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. The search strategy used the Boolean keywords: (“teamwork” OR “team effectiveness” OR “collaborative practice”) AND (“intensive care” OR “ICU”) AND (“scale” OR “questionnaire” OR “survey”).

Inclusion criteria comprised peer-reviewed articles published between 1990 and 2021, with a focus on teamwork in healthcare or ICU settings and including measurement or scale development components. Editorials, non-healthcare studies, or articles without empirical measures were excluded.

A total of 7 studies met the inclusion criteria and directly informed the generation of scale items [8–23]. A summary of the studies informing item generation and representative item examples is presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Item generation

Based on the reviewed literature and existing instruments, an initial item pool of 62 items was developed. Items were designed to capture key dimensions of teamwork such as workload distribution, communication, conflict resolution, leadership, and psychological safety.

Expert panel and face validity

The initial pool was evaluated by a multidisciplinary panel including ICU clinicians, researchers in intensive care systems, and an organizational psychology expert. The panel reviewed each item for clarity, relevance, and contextual fit. Items with redundancy or ambiguity were revised or eliminated. Feedback was used to enhance item wording and structure.

Content validity

Items were also reviewed for content validity by subject-matter experts who rated their relevance and representativeness for ICU teamwork. Items with low relevance were removed. The remaining 62 items were considered content-valid representations of teamwork constructs in ICUs.

Pilot testing and item reduction

The 62-item draft scale was administered to 251 ICU professionals across medical, surgical, and mixed ICUs at a tertiary academic hospital. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Almost never, 5 = Almost always). Items with poor distributional properties (e.g., skewness, kurtosis, high missing values, or “not applicable” responses) were excluded. A total of 32 items were removed. An initial exploratory factor analysis (EFA) further excluded 2 items with low factor loadings (< 0.45) or high cross-loadings. The remaining 30 items were retained for validation in Study 2. Items 2, 5, 7, 12, 15, 18, 19, 22, 25, 26, and 29 were reverse-coded prior to scoring.

Study 2: psychometric validation

Sample and data collection

A nationwide sample of ICU professionals (N = 716) participated in Study 2. Recruitment was conducted online through professional networks and hospital affiliations. Demographic and occupational variables were also collected.

Exploratory factor analysis

An EFA using principal component analysis with oblique (direct oblimin) rotation was performed using SPSS (v28). Factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 and under the elbow of the scree plot were retained. Three factors emerged: Teamwork Effectiveness (16 items; score range: 16–80), Effective Communication and Error Management (11 items; score range: 11–55), Effective Leadership (3 items; score range: 3–15). The total score, ranging from 30 to 150, represents the overall perception of teamwork effectiveness in intensive care units, with higher scores indicating stronger perceived effectiveness. These three factors explained a total of 56.76% of the variance. Demographic characteristics of study 1 and study 2 samples are presented in Table 1. Reliability

Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. The full 30-item scale had excellent reliability (α = 0.95). Each subscale also demonstrated strong internal consistency (Factor 1: α = 0.95, Factor 2: α = 0.89, Factor 3: α = 0.86). Results

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA)

A subsequent exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted with the sample of Study 2 using the statistical software package SPSS (version 28) to explore the factor structure of TES-ICU. As described in Study 1, the initial EFA was conducted with the aim of item elimination based on the Study 1 sample, whereas the second EFA reported in this section was conducted with the aim of factor identification based on the Study 2 sample.

The oblique rotation method, which allowed factors to be correlated, was employed (see, Table 2). Commonality and Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values were examined, and factors with eigenvalues above 1 and below the elbow in the scree plot were identified. This resulted in extraction of 3 factors explaining a total of 56.76% of variance. The first factor explaining 43.08% of variance was named as “Teamwork Effectiveness”. This factor had 16 items tapping domains such as goal-alignment, participative decision-making, harmonious work among members, effective stress management, and fair workload distribution. The second factor explaining 8.19% of the variance was named as “Effective communication and error management”. This factor had 11 items featuring the ways in which communication takes place (a sample item was “When there is conflict or disagreement among members within our unit, this causes interpersonal resentments”- reverse coded) and errors are management in teamwork (a sample item was “If one person on the team makes a mistake, it is used against that person” – reverse coded). The last factor had 3 items explaining 5.49% of variance. This factor captures effective leadership with a sample item of “The leader of the team highlights the success of the team, instead of standing out for his/her individual achievements”).

We recommend the use of the 16-item first factor in future research and practice. This factor encapsulates the essential elements of effective teamwork. Factors 2 and 3 can be used if there is a special emphasis on conflict and error management, and leadership, respectively.

Descriptive statistics for all variables are presented in Table 3. In Table 3, means, standard deviations and reliability estimates of the overall TES-ICU scale (i.e., 30 items) as well as its sub-scales derived from EFAs (i.e., 3 subscales) are presented. The validity of the TES-ICU scale was supported. It correlated with organizational outcomes in the expected way. Specifically, effective teamwork was correlated positively with job satisfaction and organizational commitment, whereas negatively with burnout and turnover intention.

Discussion

This study aimed to develop and validate the Teamwork Effectiveness Scale for Intensive Care Units (TES-ICU) and investigate its relationships with key organizational outcomes among healthcare professionals working in ICUs.

The TES-ICU comprised three factors. The first factor of the scale is Teamwork Effectiveness. Workload sharing, participation in decision-making, alignment with patient care goals, effective collaboration, stress management, and open communication about errors are key components supporting successful teamwork in intensive care settings. Understanding and enhancing these components can elevate the quality of intensive care services and positively impact patient outcomes. For that reason, the Teamwork Effectiveness scale pertains to workload sharing and coordination, active participation in decision-making and speaking up, alignment with patient care goals and competencies, effective collaboration among diverse groups, stress management, and the communication approach to errors.

Effective teamwork in the ICU requires efficient sharing of workload and coordination. “Information sharing and flow is at a good level.” and “There is harmony and coordination among the employees of our unit.” are sample survey questions of TES-ICU that measure efficient sharing of workload and coordination. Workload sharing involves balancing responsibilities and tasks among team members, while coordination ensures these tasks are executed seamlessly [28].

One fundamental aspect of teamwork involves active participation in decision-making processes and expressing opinions. This emphasizes each team member contributing to decisions by sharing their experience and expertise [29]. “Team members care about (listen and take seriously) each other’s ideas and suggestions.” and “Participation of team members in processes and decisions (in proportion to their knowledge and experience) is encouraged.” are the sample questions for this purpose.

The success of a team is measured by the alignment of team members with common patient care goals and competencies. Sharing a common vision and possessing the necessary skills to achieve this vision support effective patient care [30]. “There is a goal alignment among team members.” is a sample survey question for measuring the patient care goals.

Teams in ICU often are composed of individuals from diverse disciplines and specialties. Effective collaboration among team members requires leveraging these differences as an advantage and focusing on common goals [31]. “Different groups in our unit work in harmony with each other.” is one of the survey questions that measure the collaboration of different groups.

The intensive care environment can be stressful and dynamic. Teamwork effectiveness includes the ability of team members to cope with this stress. Stress management involves effective communication, support, and solution-oriented approaches [32]. “We are good at coping with stress.” and “We provide support to our colleagues who experience stress and fatigue” are the items that measure stress management in the scale.

Teamwork effectiveness incorporates openly communicating errors and learning from them. Open communication about errors supports continuous improvement and enhances hospital service quality. “Communication style among intensive care unit staff is courteous.” is the survey item about effective communication.

The other two factors of TES-ICU, which are “Effective communication and error management” and “Effective leadership,” consist of 11 items and 3 items, respectively. The items in these two factors measure communication, team leader, psychological safety, conflict, stress management, and approach to errors. Two sub-scales have high reliability scores. Therefore, these can also be used separately. The 16-item Teamwork Effectiveness scale, which is a subscale of the 30-item TES-ICU, also has high reliability. Therefore, we think that this subscale may be appropriate for researchers who want to use a compact questionnaire.

The Team Effectiveness Scale (TES) demonstrates a positive correlation with job satisfaction and organizational commitment. Simultaneously, its negative correlation with burnout and intention to leave the job, as indicated by surveys, highlights the scale’s effectiveness in measuring the positive effects of teamwork on job satisfaction and organizational commitment. Consequently, the developed teamwork scale proves to be a robust tool for assessing key factors such as job satisfaction, organizational commitment, burnout, and intention to leave the job. This scale can provide valuable insights to team leaders and human resources professionals, guiding them in better understanding and enhancing the organizational impacts of teamwork in the ICU.

This study introduces the first Turkish survey designed to evaluate team effectiveness. The lack of Turkish surveys focusing on the measurement of teamwork is noticeable in the literature. In this context, our developed survey stands out as the initial Turkish instrument dedicated to measuring teamwork specifically within an intensive care unit’s context. A substantial preparatory phase and expert evaluations were essential for the successful development of the questionnaire. Rigorous pilot testing ensured its reliability and validity. Positive feedback regarding the questionnaire’s clarity and consistency was gathered through comprehensive pilot studies.

The results of our study carry significant implications for both research and practice within the healthcare sector, particularly in the context of measuring teamwork effectiveness in Intensive Care Units (ICUs). The primary implication lies in the introduction of a specialized and comprehensive tool, the Teamwork Effectiveness Scale for ICUs (TES-ICU), designed for assessing teamwork within the unique setting of ICUs. The availability of a validated measurement tool provides healthcare organizations with a valuable resource to identify team strengths and areas for improvement. Given the critical link between effective teamwork in ICUs and patient safety, healthcare organizations can utilize this scale to implement targeted interventions, ultimately enhancing teamwork and improving patient outcomes. Moreover, this scale holds significance for researchers aiming to advance the understanding of teamwork in healthcare, specifically within ICUs. Additionally, educators can leverage the TES-ICU as a valuable training tool for healthcare professionals, offering effective strategies to enhance teamwork.

Another crucial implication of the study is its contribution to understanding the impact of teamwork in ICU on organizational outcomes, burnout, and turnover. The nuanced insights provided by the instrument enable targeted interventions to improve team performance. The negative correlations observed between TES-ICU scores and burnout, as well as turnover intention, underscore the pivotal role of teamwork in mitigating burnout and reducing turnover among healthcare professionals. By promoting teamwork in ICU, healthcare organizations may foster a healthier and more stable workforce, ultimately benefiting patient care.

Despite the strengths of this study, including a rigorous scale development process and a substantial sample for validation, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design of the study precludes the establishment of causality. Future research could adopt longitudinal designs to explore the dynamic relationships between teamwork, organizational outcomes, and changes over time. Additionally, reliance on self-reported measures introduces the potential for response bias. Subsequent research endeavors may consider incorporating observational or peer-assessment measures to complement self-reports. In the forthcoming period, conducting correlation tests with relatively objective performance or employee parameters (e.g., the number of patients survived or the turnover rate of doctors and nurses) through new studies would be beneficial for the scale.

Although the scale was rigorously validated in Turkish and professionally translated into English, it requires further psychometric evaluation in each language and cultural context to ensure cross-cultural validity. Its high reliability and uniqueness add significant value. The meticulous testing in Turkish, overseen by English professionals, provides a strong foundation for its reliability. However, language nuances and cultural variations necessitate a careful reevaluation of its validity for each application language, ensuring accuracy across diverse contexts. The scale’s reliability and originality enhance its perceived value, supported by trustworthy results in the Turkish context. Professional English translation further adds credibility for application in different languages. While proven reliable in Turkish and professionally translated into English, retesting is essential for each language, emphasizing its significance as a valuable tool across linguistic and cultural settings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the development and validation of the TES-ICU provide a valuable tool for assessing and improving teamwork effectiveness in ICU. The observed associations with key organizational outcomes highlight the importance of fostering effective teamwork in healthcare settings, ultimately benefiting both healthcare professionals and the patients they serve. Further research can continue to explore the nuances of teamwork dynamics and its impact on healthcare outcomes.
