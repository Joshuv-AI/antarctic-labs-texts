Harnessing DNA barcoding to enhance and sustain polyclonality in gene-edited hematopoietic stem cells
Authors: Isabel Ojeda-Perez, Andrés Bustos, Sridhar Selvaraj, Sara Fañanas-Baquero, Omaira Alberquilla-Fernandez, Luis-Javier Serrano, Jose Bonafont, Aida Garcia-Torralba, Raul Torres-Ruiz, Sandra Rodriguez-Perales, Valeri Lang, Cesar Trigueros, Rafael Mayo-Garcia, Oscar Quintana-Bustamante, Matthew Porteus, Rebeca Sánchez-Dominguez, Jose-Carlos Segovia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA challenge in gene editing for hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) is achieving efficient editing while preserving long-term engraftment and clonal diversity. Tracking edited clones with high resolution is essential to understand the impact of editing on hematopoiesis. We developed a barcoded AAV6 donor template (BC-AAV) to precisely monitor the fate of edited HSPCs following transplantation. Our findings reveal that, despite initial barcode diversity in vitro, human hematopoiesis generated by edited HSPCs transplanted in immunodeficient mice is driven by a limited number of dominant clones. The engraftment of gene-edited cells follows an oligo/polyclonal pattern, indicating that editing does not alter clonal dynamics in this model. Using BC-AAV, we optimized a gene editing protocol for correcting the PKLR gene, responsible for pyruvate kinase deficiency, a rare disorder that causes severe anemia due to red blood energy imbalance. We implemented key improvements. GMP-grade StemSpan AOF medium and StemRegenin-1 increased clonal diversity while maintaining hematopoietic potential. NHEJ inhibitor AZD-7648, significantly boosted editing efficiency in vitro, and a shorter transduction period enhanced engraftment and clonal balance without compromising editing outcomes. This refined strategy for gene editing in human HSPCs optimizes both efficiency and long-term polyclonal dynamics and has important implications for clinical applications.Keywords: Gene editing, DNA Barcode, Adenoassociated viral vector, Knock-in, HSC clones, CRISPR-Cas9

Introduction

Knock-in gene editing via homologous-directed repair (HDR) has emerged as an effective strategy for gene therapy. This strategy enables the insertion of full-length cDNA into its native locus under endogenous regulatory control while reducing the expression of the mutated protein.1 It avoids random integration, allows natural gene expression and reduces or even eliminates the expression of potential dominant negative proteins.2 One application of this strategy combines the use of ribonucleoprotein complexes (RNP), such as Cas9 protein paired with synthetic stabilized single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs), alongside a non-integrating adeno-associated virus (AAV) as a vector to deliver donor DNA templates.3,4 This approach has shown great promise in modifying therapeutically relevant primary human cells, such as hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs), T cells, and induced pluripotent stem cells. Preclinical studies suggest this technology could revolutionize treatments for a variety of genetic disorders, including pyruvate kinase deficiency (PKD),5 sickle cell disease,6 severe combined immunodeficiency-X-linked (SCID-X1),6,7,8 Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome (WAS),9 IL7R-SCID,10 mucopolysaccharidose type I (MPS I),11 chronic granulomatous disease,12 X-linked hyper IgM syndrome,13 and even certain cancers.14 Despite this promising approach, in clinical evaluations, gene editing presents challenges, such as inability to track individual gene-modified cells15 due to the specific genomic integration of the donor sequences and difficulties in achieving therapeutic levels.

The generation of genetically corrected grafts able to generate polyclonal engraftment is essential for optimal therapeutic outcomes. Clonal cell tracking and dynamic evaluation in gene editing cells have been achieved in experimental models by including a DNA barcode into donor sequences that are integrated into the cell genome.16,17 Follow-up of xenogeneic transplants have demonstrated that gene-edited cells typically engraft in an oligoclonal manner. Implementing donor DNA barcoding in the refinement of gene editing protocols is needed to monitor the maintenance of a polyclonal gene-edited hematopoietic stem cell repertoire.

A significant number of attempts to increase HDR-driven gene editing to reach therapeutic efficacies have been reported.6,7,9,11,17,18,19,20,21 Improving DNA access,22 inhibition of other DNA repair pathways different than HDR,21,23,24,25 and induction of HDR22,26 have been tested to facilitate HDR gene editing. Additional compounds to increase AAV delivery of the donor sequences have also been analyzed.27,28,29 Nevertheless, a robust protocol has not been achieved yet.

In this work, we have addressed both tracking of specific outcomes of individual gene-modified cells and achievement of therapeutic levels by developing a barcoded AAV6 donor (BC-AAV) library to track the contributions of gene-targeted HSPCs at the clonal level and optimize the gene-editing protocol for the treatment of PKD, a rare metabolic disorder that affects red blood cell survival, with the aim of achieving therapeutic outcomes.

Results

Development of a barcode AAV6 donor template targeting PKLR gene in human HSPCs

To enable clonal tracking of edited cells, we generated a barcoded AAV6 donor library based on the AAV6 donor template previously developed in our laboratory for PKLR gene knock-in editing.5 We incorporated a 27-bp degenerated heritable DNA barcode sequence30,31 within the repair template, positioned between the coRPK stop codon and the polyA tail sequence (Figure 1A). We subsequently generated high-complexity plasmid and AAV6 pools, ensuring barcode diversity remained consistent across both plasmid and viral libraries (Figure S1A). Deep sequencing confirmed the presence of a diverse barcode repertoire, with most clones (104 out of 226,906 or 307,651 reads) appearing only once. Nucleotide analysis further revealed clear distinctions between variable and non-variable regions, with no biases in nucleotide representation at any position (Figure S1B). Additionally, an analysis of the top 10 most abundant clones showed that barcode sequences differed between plasmid and AAV6 pools, with each contributing less than 0.5% to the total clone population. These results confirm that our barcode library enables the identification of single edited clones with high specificity (Figure S1C).

Following the establishment of the AAV6 barcode (BC-AAV) system, we validated its application in healthy cord blood-derived HSPCs (CB-HSPCs) maintained in vitro after gene editing. Cells were pre-stimulated for 48 h, then electroporated under standard conditions, and subsequently transduced with BC-AAV at viral concentrations ranging from 0 to 104 genome copies per microliter (gc/μl) (Figure 1B). Progenitor’s potential of gene-edited samples was assessed via colony growth in semisolid culture assay (CFU). CFU numbers and lineage distribution remained unaffected across multiplicities of infection (MOIs) from 0 to 2000 (Figure 1C). However, at MOI 3,000 and even more so at MOI 10,000, we observed a notable reduction in colony numbers and shifts in lineage distribution. To analyze the specific integration of the therapeutic transgene, we used PCR amplification targeting the left (LHA) and right (RHA) homology arm junctions in individual colonies derived from the CFU assay after 14 days of culture. At MOI 2000, ∼56% of CFUs showed successful integration of BC-AAV at both 5' (LHA) and 3' (RHA) ends (Figure 1D).

We then performed an in-depth analysis of barcode diversity in CB-CD34+ cells edited at different MOIs and cultured in liquid culture for 14 days. An in-house specific bioinformatics pipeline was developed (see materials and methods section). Samples from the cultures were collected on days 7 and 14 post-transduction. Due to observed toxicity, MOI 10,000 was excluded from further evaluation. Barcode analysis was performed with an average next-generation sequencing (NGS) depth of approximately 120,000 reads per sample. As shown in Figure 1E, the number of unique barcodes on day 14 was comparable among higher MOIs but significantly lower at MOI 300. Analysis of the top 10 represented clones revealed no major differences in higher MOIs on day 14. Notably, at MOI 2000, the top 10 clones constituted less than 5% of the total barcode population in all three CB samples, with two samples below 1%, indicating high clonal diversity in the edited cells at day 14 (Figure 1F). These percentages stabilized progressively throughout the culture period (Figure S1D). In contrast, at MOI 300 one sample showed nearly 50% of the total barcode representation concentrated in the top 10 clones, suggesting lower diversity. Given that MOI 2000 achieved optimal results for gene editing without significantly compromising barcode diversity, we further analyzed the dynamics of the most abundant clones over time in this condition. Comparing the top ten barcodes at days 7 and 14 revealed that the composition of the top clones differed completely between these time points, with no shared barcodes among the top ten in any sample (Figure S1E). Furthermore, tracking the dynamics of the five most abundant clones showed a clear alternation over time: dominant clones at day 7 declined in representation by day 14, while new clones that were initially less represented emerged as dominant at the later time point (Figure S1F).

To investigate the effect of clonal diversity on the engraftment of edited HSCs, we transplanted 3 × 105 BC-AAV-edited CD34+ CB cells into sublethally irradiated NBSGW mice via tail vein injection. Analysis of the animals two months post-transplant confirmed that our vector did not impair engraftment capacity and allowed efficient gene editing (Figures S1G–S1I). We performed clonal analysis on human cells isolated from mouse bone marrow (BM), initially focusing on the total number of detected barcodes. The total number of clones ranged from approximately 500 to 1,500 clones per mouse (Figure 1G). To complement this information and better characterize clonal diversity, we also calculated the Shannon diversity index, which accounts for both the number of clones and their relative abundances. A higher Shannon index indicates greater clonal variability. In our study, Shannon index values were around 2 at this MOI 2000, reflecting high clonal complexity within the CD45+ population (Figure 1H). However, despite this high diversity, the top 10 clones in each mouse accounted for over 90% of active hematopoiesis at this time point (Figure 1I), with the most prevalent clone reaching an average abundance of ∼47% (Figure 1J). The distribution of these top 10 dominant clones varied significantly across individual clones and mice, as shown in Figure 1K. Altogether, these analyses demonstrated that BC-AAV is a valuable approach for assessing the impact of gene editing on the clonal composition of transplanted cells.

Barcode donor helps the optimization of gene editing protocols to enhance clonal diversity and engraftment efficiency

To achieve therapeutic levels required for a potential clinical trial, we first evaluated the impact of growth media on the expansion and transduction processes. We compared two regulatory agency-approved media: stem cell growth medium (SCGM, CellGenix) and StemSpan AOF (AOF, Stemcell Technologies). Using our established protocol (Figure 1B), we edited CB-CD34+ cells with the BC-AAV. No differences were observed between media in terms of CFUs (Figure 2A) or in vitro editing efficiency (Figure 2B). However, when 3×105 BC-AAV-edited cells were transplanted into NBSGW mice, engraftment levels were significantly higher in cells cultured in AOF compared to SCGM media (Figure 2C). Moreover, the frequency of targeted-human alleles was markedly improved with AOF, reaching ∼29% at two months post-transplant (Figure 2D) measure by ddPCR. Regarding clonal diversity, SCGM-cultured cells showed a significant reduction in the total number of clones (∼1.5 × 102) compared to AOF (∼1.2 × 103) (Figure 2E), as well as a significant decrease in the Shannon diversity index (∼0.9 for SCGM versus ∼1.9 for AOF) (Figure 2F). Additionally, the abundance of the top 10 most represented clones was significantly higher in SCGM-treated cells (Figure 2G), with a trend toward increased dominance of the most prevalent clone (Figure 2H), as further shown in Figure S1J. These findings indicate that SCGM negatively impacted engrafted cells, reducing the total number of clones and affecting the polyclonality of hematopoiesis. Based on these results, we selected AOF as the base medium for subsequent experiments.

We took advantage of the BC setup to further evaluate the enhancement of gene-editing efficiency by modifications to our previously established protocol. We designed four experimental conditions, including variations in cell concentration, timings of the AAV transduction process, and inclusion of additional compounds, such as StemRegenin-1 (SR-1) (Figure 3A). This configuration enabled a direct side-by-side comparison of each factor’s impact on colony formation, editing efficiency, engraftment, and clonal diversity.

Both increased transduction cell concentration (condition D vs. A) and reduced post-electroporation incubation time (condition D vs. B) led to an increased number of multipotent CFU-GEMM colonies. Additionally, SR-1 supplementation (condition D vs. C) further enhanced overall CFU output, suggesting improved progenitor activity under these conditions (Figure 3B). Editing levels assessed after 5 days in culture by ddPCR were comparable between conditions with low vs. high cell concentration (A vs. D) and with or without SR-1 (C vs. D). However, extending post-electroporation incubation to 48 h (48 hpe; condition B) significantly increased editing efficiency compared to 24 h (24 hpe; condition D) (Figure 3C). Experiments performed using mobilized peripheral blood (mPB) CD34+ cells showed no significant differences in editing efficiency in vitro across conditions (Figure S2A).

Engraftment was significantly impaired when cells were cultured for 48 hpe (condition B), with reduced contribution to BM, spleen, and peripheral blood (PB) compared to 24 hpe conditions (conditions A, C, and D). In contrast, SR-1 supplementation (D vs. C) and increased cell concentration (D vs. A) were associated with a trend toward improved engraftment. Multilineage distribution remained consistent across all conditions (Figure S2B). In vivo editing levels ranged from 13% to 29%, corresponding to a 1.3- to 2.2-fold reduction compared to in vitro measurements. Total CD45+ clonal counts were similar across conditions (Figure 3F). However, clonal diversity, assessed using the Shannon diversity index, was significantly higher in condition D, indicating a more balanced clonal contribution across barcodes (Figure 3G). In contrast, cells cultured for 48 hpe (condition B) exhibited markedly reduced diversity and strong clonal dominance, with the top 10 clones contributing over 95% of total hematopoiesis (Figure 3H) and a single clone often accounting for the majority (Figure 3I).This pattern was even more evident in the CD34+ progenitor compartment, where shorter incubation times and SR-1 supplementation (condition D vs. B and C) significantly increased the number of detected clones (Figure 3J). The distribution of the top 10 clones is summarized in Figure 3K.

To further investigate the effect of incubation time on the most primitive hematopoietic compartment, we transduced CD34+ cells with a GFP-expressing AAV and analyzed the edited cells by flow cytometry. Although condition B yielded the highest overall editing percentages in CD34+CD38−CD90+ cells, cells cultured for only 24 h (condition D) exhibited a markedly higher proportion of these primitive cells and showed a higher mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) (Figures S2C–S2E). Importantly, freezing cells at 24 hpe did not compromise editing efficiency in mPB-CD34+ cells, which supported the feasibility of protocol shortening for clinical applications (Figure S2F).

Together, these results support condition D, increased cell concentration, shortened 24 h incubation post-electroporation, and SR-1 supplementation, as the optimal strategy. This combination preserved progenitor function, enhanced engraftment, and maintained robust gene-editing efficiency and clonal diversity, making it particularly suitable for clinical translation.

Evaluating chemical enhancers to improve CRISPR-Cas9-AAV6 gene editing in HSPCs

Then, we aimed to further enhance gene editing efficiency by identifying compounds that could efficiently increase gene editing while maintaining their in vivo properties without compromising engraftment or clonal repopulation. To this end, we tested several molecules known to enhance AAV entry, including bleomycin, teniposide, bortezomib, daunorubicin, polyvinyl alcohol, and cyclosporin H, as well as compounds involved in homologous recombination pathways, such as BRCA1, Rad51, Mre11A, and CtIP1. None of these compounds significantly improved our previous gene-editing efficiency in vitro (Figures S3A and S3B). All experiments in this section were performed independently and used condition D as the starting point, as this was our most optimized culture protocol. We maintained the same color code as in Figure 3 to indicate that the conditions are equivalent and to facilitate comparison, although the data shown derive from separate experimental replicates.

Inhibitors of DNA-PKc, a key component of the NHEJ repair pathway, have been described to enhance editing efficiency in hematopoietic and non-hematopoietic cells.21,25 Based on this, we tested the addition of AZD-7648 (AZD), a DNA-PKc inhibitor small molecule, alone or in combination with other potential enhancers to our optimized protocol. The addition of AZD during transduction period improved in vitro editing efficiency 1.5-fold in both CB-CD34+ and mPB-CD34+ cells (condition D vs. condition E; Figures 4A and S3C). Following a screening of AZD combined with multiple compounds at various concentrations (Figures S3D–S3F), we found that its combination with trichostatin A (TSA) at 0.05 μM, added during transduction, enhanced editing efficiency 2-fold compared to the optimized plus AZD condition (condition E vs. condition F; Figures 4A and S3C).

To validate these results in vivo, NBSGW mice were transplanted with CB-CD34+ edited cells from the three different conditions. Two months post-transplantation, AZD-treated cells exhibited reduced engraftment in BM, with no impact observed in the spleen or PB. The combination of AZD with TSA impaired the engraftment potential of HSPCs, as engraftment was nearly eliminated (Figure 4C). Multilineage differentiation was unaffected across all conditions (Figure S3F). AZD-treated cells showed an increasing trend on the percentage of targeted alleles compared to controls, though high variability limited the ability to discern significant differences (Figure 4D).

Due to TSA’s toxicity, further analyses focused on samples treated with AZD alone. Clonal analysis of the human CD45+ population revealed no significant differences in either the total number of clones or clonal diversity between the standard condition (condition D, ∼1.2×103 clones) and the AZD-treated group (condition E, ∼1.8×103 clones), both showing a Shannon diversity index of 1.9 (Figures 4E and 4F). The top 10 clones accounted for more than 88% of hematopoiesis in most cases, with variable dominance of the most prevalent one, ranging from 15% to 75% in the standard group and from 8% to 97% in the AZD-treated group (Figures 4G and 4H). Within the hCD34+ population, there was a slight increase in clone numbers in our standard condition without AZD (∼7.8 × 102 clones) compared to AZD-treated samples (∼5.6 × 102 clones), but this difference was not statistically significant (Figure 4I).

To define how the engrafted human progenitor’s population was contributing to mature cells, we compared our standard condition (condition D) with the one involving AZD treatment (condition E), focusing on the clonal overlap between CD45+ and CD34+ populations within the same mouse and among mice transplanted with cells from the same pool. Initially, we analyzed the percentage of shared clones within individual mice. While not statistically significant, mice transplanted with AZD-treated cells exhibited fewer shared clones between their CD45+ and CD34+ populations (∼2%) compared to ∼7% without the addition of AZD (Figure 4J). Next, we examined the relationship between the predominant clones shared between the CD45+ and CD34+ populations within individual mice (Figure 4K). In both conditions, a correlation was observed between the major clones in CD45+ and CD34+ cells, although the extent of overlap varied among mice. Some mice exhibited only one shared dominant clone, while others had more than five. Interestingly, in the AZD-treated group, we also noted clone similarities within the CD34+ population across different mice.

Finally, we studied whether clones present in one mouse were also shared across other mice transplanted with cells from the same pool. For the CD45+ population, the percentage of shared clones among different mice was consistently low, often reaching zero in both conditions. Conversely, in the CD34+ population, we observed a higher degree of shared clones across mice. This trend was slightly more pronounced in condition D, non-AZD-treated group (∼2% shared clones) compared to 0.4% in the condition E, AZD-treated group (Figure 4L). AZD improved gene editing efficiency in vitro, with a trend toward maintaining this enhancement in vivo, albeit with some impact on engraftment and clonal contribution.

Discussion

Knock-in gene editing via HDR has emerged as an effective strategy for gene therapy. The advent of CRISPR-Cas9-based HSC gene editing, which can control donor DNA integration, has revolutionized gene therapy and is being developed as a curative treatment for various congenital blood disorders, including PKD.5,32,33,34 PKD is considered a promising candidate for gene therapy due to its monogenic origin and the proven success of allogeneic HSCT in treating certain PKD patients.35 In fact, an ongoing international lentiviral-based clinical trial (NCT04105166) is showing therapeutic efficacy.36 Knocking in a full RPK cDNA in the ATG mRNA starting site restores gene functionality in vitro and in vivo, and maintains natural regulation.5,6 Despite advances in improving targeted integration in human HSCs, achieving therapeutic HDR levels remains a challenge, which limits clinical applications. To overcome this limitation and optimize the search for further improvements, we developed a single cell tracking tool based on DNA barcoding of exogenously delivered sequences. We defined a cellular clone by a unique barcode sequence. Additionally, we established a data analysis pipeline to differentiate sequencing artifacts from low abundance clones to detect minor represented clones. By employing these tools consistently throughout our studies, we demonstrate that the obtained grafts display an oligo/polyclonal pattern of engraftment in a xenogeneic human-to-mouse model. Our observations align with other reports,16,17,31 and indicate that in xenogeneic models, hematopoiesis is oligoclonal with given populations generated by few HSC-derived clones, supplemented by contributions from many minor clones. Moreover, we have observed that apparently rare clones in some mice significantly contribute to hematopoiesis in others, underscoring both the method’s sensitivity in detecting these clones and their biological importance in hematopoiesis. We also observed the same edited clones present in the CD45 and CD34 populations, indicating these clones as active stem clones at the time of sacrifice. Nevertheless, we observed that the vast majority of the clones are not shared between CD34 and CD45 populations, thus supporting the concept of dormant or hibernating HSCs, which has been suggested by several groups.37,38,39 We performed a comparative analysis using published data on lentiviral-based therapy of HSPCs targeting the PKLR locus,40,41 to further explore the clonal dynamics of genome-edited cells, an aspect not previously investigated in this context. Previous studies tracking LV integration sites (IS) in NSG mice indicated the presence of approximately 2 × 102 total clones, with strong variations in clonal composition across different mice. In some instances, a small number of these integrations contributed to a large number of sequence reads.40 Interestingly, our barcode system revealed over 1.5 × 103 clones per mouse. Some of these clones actively contributed to a substantial percentage of the chimeric hematopoiesis, suggesting that genome-edited human HSPCs engraft with comparable efficiency to lentiviral-engineered cells in this immunodeficient model. While clonality has been extensively studied using xenograft mouse models, including in our study and those based on lentiviral integration site analysis, it is important to recognize the inherent limitations of this system. Despite their widespread use, immunodeficient mouse models do not fully recapitulate the human BM niche, and differences in cytokine signaling, engraftment capacity, and hematopoietic dynamics can affect the behavior and output of transplanted human HSPCs.42,43 To better approximate human hematopoiesis, non-human primate (NHP) models have emerged as a valuable alternative. In rhesus macaques, barcoded lentiviral-transduced and HDR-edited HSPCs have been tracked longitudinally, revealing notable differences in clonal behavior: lentiviral-transduced cells led to highly polyclonal hematopoietic reconstitution, whereas HDR-edited HSPCs displayed a more oligoclonal pattern and a decline in clone numbers post-transplantation. These findings highlight the relevance of NHP studies to evaluate the safety and long-term behavior of genome-edited HSPCs in a physiologically more faithful context.44,45

Ex vivo activation of HSPCs is crucial for achieving high levels of genetic modification. The full progression of primitive cells through S/G2 phase is essential for successful HDR, although it can impact long-term HSC engraftment.46 Current protocols typically involve four to five days of culture, with varying pre-stimulation times ranging from 48 to 72 h5,6,7,9,11,17 Our protocol maintained 48 h pre-stimulation as previously determined.5 We focused here on reducing the post-electroporation/transduction period. Although the majority of gene editing protocols assign 48 h for optimal recovery of edited HSCs,5,6,7,9 by shortening the transduction period from 48 to 24 h we doubled engraftment levels without affecting the editing percentage, thus enhancing the number of clones in the primitive CD34+ population. Our data support that clonal diversity is best evaluated within the CD34+ compartment, which reflects long-term HSPC output more accurately than bulk CD45+ populations. While CD45+ cells include mature lineages and show higher clonal complexity, this likely results from transient progenitor activity.

Prolonged exposure to stimulation signals risks compromising stem cell function.47 Plating HSPCs at low densities has been shown to minimize these impacts,48,49 with greater effectiveness when combined with small molecules like UM171 and SR-1, which are known for their potential in expanding LT-HSCs.50,51,52 Existing protocols typically cultivate cells between 2.5 × 105 and 5 × 105 cells/ml during the whole process.9,17,18 We observed that low concentration was only necessary during pre-stimulation, as there were no differences in editing with more concentrated cells in the post-electroporation/transduction period. We even noticed a slight trend toward improved engraftment in this later case. We show here that an editing protocol combining both concentrations is beneficial in terms of the maintenance of long-term properties and editing efficacy.

Having already incorporated UM171 in our protocol, we further investigated the use of SR-1 in combination with high-cell-density culture. SR-1 addition increased the number of committed progenitors, enhanced the percentage of engraftment, and, most importantly, elevated the number of CD34+ clones in NBSGW mice without affecting the levels of gene editing. These results supported evidence previously described18,49,53 and confirmed the importance of including SR-1 in the gene editing procedure.

To better characterize clonal dynamics following gene editing, we evaluated clonal diversity using the Shannon diversity index.54 This metric is particularly informative within the bulk CD45+ population, where differences in clonal heterogeneity may not be fully captured by simple clone counts. Indeed, we observed that condition D, which includes reduced transduction time, higher viral concentration during transduction, and the addition of SR1, displayed a significantly higher Shannon index compared to other conditions, highlighting its greater clonal diversity. Notably, these differences were not evident when analyzing only the number of clones, underscoring the added value of diversity indices for assessing clonal heterogeneity in the CD45+ compartment.

The predominant pathway following DSBs is NHEJ.55 This fact impacts negatively on the success of HDR-dependent donor integration. Numerous HDR enhancers have been described to overcome this difficulty. However, despite their documented improvement in HDR,22,26,27,28,29 we have not observed any significant difference when they were included in our protocol. Likely, the inclusion of AZD-7648 (AZD), a small molecule described as an effective DNA-PKc inhibitor21,25, demonstrated a 1.5-fold increase in gene editing in both CB and mPB in vitro, without causing significant alterations to CFU or cell viability. However, the effects of AZD on hematopoiesis reconstitution in immunodeficient animals remained unreported. We have observed a decrease in the engraftment capacity of human cells compared to cells edited without adding AZD in the process. On the other hand, AZD resulted in a non-significant increase in gene editing. Editing remained consistent across all lineages, both in the control and AZD-edited groups, confirming edited cells’ ability to differentiate into various lineages. No clear difference in clonal diversity was observed with the use of AZD compared to the control. So, even though the use of AZD in vivo resulted in a reduction in engraftment percentage, the transplanted cells exhibit the capacity to differentiate and give rise to all lineages. Furthermore, AZD maintains in vivo editing at potentially therapeutic levels without significantly altering the composition of clones in either the CD45 or CD34 compartment.

To further enhance editing, we explored combinations of AZD with additional compounds, primarily aimed at improving DNA access and thereby increasing HDR percentages. Following screening and despite using molecules reported to enhance gene editing, only AZD and TSA combination significantly improved the in vitro editing percentage, showing a 1.3-fold increase compared to AZD and a 1.8-fold increase compared to the control. Despite these promising in vitro results, AZD/TSA combination demonstrated significant impairment in HSC in vivo repopulation capacity. This could be attributed to the crucial role of epigenetic regulation in maintaining the self-renewal and differentiation capacity of HSCs.56 Exposure to TSA can disrupt histone acetylation patterns, which influences gene expression, and ultimately, the function of HSCs. As documented in the literature, HDAC inhibitors like TSA can have pleiotropic effects, and specific outcomes may depend on factors such as dosage, duration of exposure, and cellular context.56,57,58

It is noteworthy that the decrease in editing efficiency was observed when changing from an in vitro to an in vivo setting. This reduction in editing is not an isolated case of a specific compound; rather, it recurrently occurs when transplanting human-edited HSPCs into NBSGW mouse model. Typically, a 30%–40% decrease compared to the in vitro observed values is obtained. This phenomenon is not unique to our editing protocol. Several studies have documented significantly lower efficiencies of genome editing in HSPCs after in vivo transplantation compared to those assayed in vitro.6,7,8,11,59,60,61 A lower gene editing efficiency of the long-term HSC subpopulation in the graft, or a specific cell death activation due to the editing process could account for this reproducible decrease. Understanding the factors involved in the process, such as innate cellular immunity, microenvironment of the NBSGW mouse model or potential off-target effects is crucial for further refining HDR gene editing approaches.

In conclusion, our optimized protocol enhanced editing efficiency 4.5-fold. Despite variability in engraftment, edited cells demonstrated robust repopulation and clonal diversity comparable to lentiviral-based genetic therapy for PKLR locus. Moreover, our barcode strategy provides valuable insights into the clonal contribution of genome-edited human HSPCs. This optimized strategy for gene editing in HSPCs has important implications for future clinical applications.

Materials and methods

Isolation of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells

Human HSPCs derived from umbilical CB from healthy donors were provided by Centro de Transfusiones de la Comunidad de Madrid and human HSPCs derived from mPB were provided by Hospital Virgen del Rocio, Fundación Jiménez Díaz (FJD), and Hospital La Fe de Valencia. All samples were collected under written consent from the mothers, Centro de Transfusiones de la Comunidad de Madrid and Ceim of FJD using an institutional review board agreement. Alternatively, HSPC either from CB or mPB from apheresis were purchased frozen from AllCells (Alameda, CA, USA) or acquired from donors under informed consent via the Binns Program for CB Research at Stanford University. Purified CD34+ cells were obtained by immunoselection with the CD34 Micro-Bead kit (MACS; Miltenyi Biotech). CD34+ cells were used fresh or frozen after purification. Cells were viably frozen in 10% dimethyl sulfoxide solution and stored in liquid nitrogen and thawed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

CD34+ HSPCs were cultured in StemSpan SFEM II (Stemcell Technologies, Vancouver, Canada), StemSpan AOF (AOF, Stemcell Technologies, Vancouver, Canada) or stem cell growth medium (SCGM, CellGenix, UK) depending on the experiment, supplemented with SCF (100 ng/mL), TPO (100 ng/mL), Flt3-Ligand (100 ng/mL), IL-6 (50 ng/mL), and UM171 (35 nM). Cells were cultured at 37°C, 5% CO2, and 5% O2.

Barcode AAV library generation

An oligonucleotide library with the following sequence: TAANNATCNNGATSSAAANNGGTNNAACNNT30,31 was purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). This library has a maximal theoretical complexity of 4,194,304 different combinations where N represents A, G, C, or T, and S represents G or C. The gene block library was generated from this single stranded oligonucleotide by PCR. Resulting PCR was digested using MluI and SpeI restriction enzymes and cloned into the therapeutic vector that already contained the sequence for the AAV ITRs and the optimized version of the RPK gene.5 The library was introduced between the codon stop of the codon-optimized version of the RPK (coRPK) gene and the poly-A tail. The ligation mixture was transformed by electroporation using MegaX DH10BTM T1R ElectrocompTM cells (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Diversity of the barcode library plasmid was then checked using high-throughput sequencing of the barcode sequence prior to AAV production. BC-AAV library was produced by Viralgen SL62 (San Sebastian, Spain), and diversity was also checked by NGS of the barcode amplicons.

Electroporation and transduction of cells

Chemically modified PKLR sgRNA used to edit CD34+ HSPCs was purchased from Synthego (San Diego, CA). The genomic sgRNA target sequence is: CTCATTCCATGGTCCCGCAG. Cas9 proteins (Alt-R HiFi Cas9 Nuclease V3) were purchased from IDT. RNPs were complexed at a Cas9/sgRNA molar ratio of 1:2.5 at room temperature (RT) for 10 min prior to electroporation. For nucleofection of RNP, the P3 Primary Cell 4D Nucleofector X Kit was used with the Amaxa 4D device (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) using the DZ-100 program. After the pulse, the nucleofected HSPCs were incubated for 10 min at 37°C. Cells were then transduced with the BC-AAV (MOI range 200–50,000) with a final concentration of 1 × 106cells/ml. After 48 h of transduction, cells were collected and either frozen or resuspended in medium for additional in vitro culture or in saline for transplantation into animals.

To improve gene editing efficiency, cells were treated with different enhancer compounds, tested at various concentrations and added at specific time points during the protocol. Table 1 summarizes all enhancers used, the concentrations tested, and the timing of their administration.

Transplantation of targeted HSPC into NBSGW mice

To conduct human gene editing studies, edited cells were transplanted through a tail vein to the NBSGW (NOD.Cg-KitW−41JTyr+PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1Wjl/ThomJ) mouse strain (The Jackson Laboratory, USA) which had been sublethally irradiated with an X-ray irradiator MG324 (Philips. Hamburg, Germany) the day before transplant with 1 Gy. All experimental procedures were carried out according to Spanish and European regulations at the CIEMAT animal facility (Protocol number PROEX 192.0/21). Mice used in the experiment were 6–12 weeks old (male and female), age-matched and randomized to experimental groups.

Analysis of human engraftment

Two months post-transplant mice were euthanized by CO2 and BM, PB, and spleen samples were collected. Total BM was obtained from the femur and tibia of mice by flushing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, USA) using a 25-gauge needle. Cells from BM, PB and spleen were filtered with a 40 μm nylon filter (BD/Becton, Dickinson and Company, New Jersey, USA) and incubated in ammonium chloride lysis buffer solution (NH4Cl, CO3HK 1M, EDTA 0.5 M; reagents from Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) for 5′ in darkness at room temperature. Thereafter, cells were centrifuged for 7′ at 1,400 rpm and washed with PBS. The engraftment capacity of edited human cells in transplanted mice was assessed by quantifying the percentage of hCD45 FITC (Beckman Coulter, clone J33) cells, with lineage analysis performed for hCD34 APC (BD clone 581), hCD33 PE (Beckman Coulter, clone: D3HL60.251), hCD19 PECY7 (Biolegend, clone: SL25C1), and hCD3 APCCY7 (Biolegend, clone: UCHT1) populations. Cells were stained with monoclonal antibodies and incubated at 4°C for 20 min in the dark. Following labeling, the cells were washed and resuspended in flow cytometry buffer (PBS with 0.5% BSA and 0.05% sodium azide), and 1 μg/mL of DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added for viability analysis. Flow cytometry was performed using an LSRFortessa Cell Analyzer (BD). Data was analyzed using FlowJo v10.5.0 (Tree Star).

Genomic editing frequencies by droplet digital PCR (ddPCR)

Genomic DNA was collected from CD34+ cells after editing and from distinct sorted populations in the BM using the QuickExtract method or DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (QIAGEN). Targeted gDNA was then digested with HindIII-HF following the manufacturer’s instructions (New England Biolabs). 50ng of gDNA sample was then used in 25 μL ddPCR Supermix for Probes (no dUTP) (Bio-Rad), following the manufacturer’s instructions with integration-specific and reference primer (WT Fw: TGTTCAAGGTACCATCTTGGG; WT Rv: CCCCAATCAGGATGGACTTT; coRPK Rv: CCACGCTAGCCCTTCTCA) and probes (coRPK probe (FAM): TCCGGCTGGTCCGCCAGGGG; WT probe (HEX): ACCTGGCCAGCTGCATGTTTTCGCC) at a primer: probe ratio of 3.6:1. Amplification was performed using in-out PCR (one primer binding to transgene insert in the genomic PKLR locus and the other immediately outside the homology arm). Droplet samples were prepared using 20 μL of the PCR mix, 70 μL of droplet generation oil, and 40 μL of the droplet sample. PCR cycling conditions were as follows: 98°C (10 min); 94°C (30 s), 59°C (30 s), 72°C (2 min) (40 cycles); 98°C (10 min). Finally, droplets were analyzed according to the manufacturer’s instructions using the QX200 Droplet Digital PCR System (Bio-Rad).

Barcode analysis by next-generation sequencing

NGS analysis of edited human cells was performed by Genewiz (Leipzig, Germany) generating between 10,000 and 200,000 sequencing reads per sample. The corresponding Fastq files, including total read counts and barcode sequences, are provided in Table S1 and S2. Genomic DNA was extracted from edited human cells between seven- and fifteen-days post-gene editing using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). DNA from the BM of engrafted immunodeficient mice was also collected and purified using the same kit. For each sorted population, we consistently used the same number of cells, typically a minimum of 100,000 cells per population. The sequencing depth was generally lower for these samples but remained comparable across conditions. Regarding the PCR, 100 ng of DNA per sample were used as input to ensure consistent barcode amplification across samples. PCR amplification targeted the genomic site of interest, incorporating Illumina adapters in the primers, and Herculase II Fusion DNA Polymerase (Agilent Technologies, California, USA) was used for amplification. For culture cells, a first PCR of 975 bp was carried out to amplify the region flanking the barcode sequence (In-OutBarcodeFw: TGTTCCCCCTGCTGTACCG and In-OutBarcodeRv: CACCTCTCTGGGTCTCCCTCT) following the manufacturer’s instructions with an annealing temperature of 60°C to prevent episomal AAV expression. This first PCR was followed by a second PCR of 280 bp (Table S3) specifically amplifying the barcode region (SeqBarcodeFw_ADAP: ACACTCTTTCCCTACACGACGCTCTTCCGATCTGGAAAGGACAGTGGGAGTGG and SeqBarcodeRv_ADAP: GACTGGAGTTCAGACGTGTGCTCTTCCGATCTTGCAGTTCGGCATCGAGAG, adaptor sequences are underlined). The PCR temperature protocol was as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 5 min, followed by 3 cycles of 95°C for 30 s, 55°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 45 s. This was followed by 1 cycle of 95°C for 30 s and 60°C for 30 s, and then 37 cycles of 72°C for 45 s. The final extension step was performed at 72°C for 2 min, with an indefinite hold at 4°C. Barcode variability was assessed using a custom bioinformatics tool designed to identify reads containing the barcode sequence, analyze the number of different barcodes, and determine the relative abundance of each barcode. This analysis allowed the study of clonality in engrafted mice and the tracking of clonal evolution during hematopoiesis. Sample quality was assessed using FastQC software, with Trimmomatic used to retain high-quality paired sequences. The custom pipeline analyzed amplicon quality and barcode integrity, identifying and quantifying the contribution and representativeness of each clone. Besides, the Shannon diversity index (H′) was calculated to assess clonal diversity within defined cell populations. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version v9.4.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego California USA). Normal distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilks test. Student’s t test or Mann-Whitney U tests were used based on the normality of data to compare two groups. For more than two groups, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison tests was used for normally distributed data, while Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparison test was employed for nonparametric analyses. Results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) or in a boxplot showing the median and the interquartile range. The significance is expressed as p < 0.0001 (∗∗∗∗), p < 0.001 (∗∗∗), p < 0.01(∗∗) or p < 0.05 (∗).
