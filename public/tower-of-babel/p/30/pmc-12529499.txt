Burden of non-COVID-19 lower respiratory infections and etiologies in China and globally: An analysis for the global burden of disease study 2021
Authors: Xueyan Zheng, Yongcheng Li, Dongxue Ruan, Lifeng Lin, Ruilin Meng, Dejian Zhao, Jiayin Yu, Xinyi Li, Hongjun Huang, Maigeng Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-COVID-19 lower respiratory infections (LRIs) represent a persistent public health concern in China and globally. However, comprehensive assessments of their long-term spatiotemporal trends remain limited. This study aimed to quantify the burden of LRIs by age, sex, and geographic region across China and globally from 1990 to 2021.MethodsUsing standardized methodologies from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, we estimated LRI incidence and mortality globally and across 33 provincial-level units in China by sex, age, and year. Attributable incidence and mortality were further analyzed by underlying etiologies and risk factors, with corresponding 95 % uncertainty intervals.ResultsIn 2021, LRI accounted for 343.6 (95% UI: 325.2–363.5) million episodes and 2.2 (95% UI: 2.0–2.4) million deaths worldwide, corresponding to age-standardized incidence and mortality rates of 4283.6 (95% UI: 4057.0–4524.9) episodes per 100,000 population and 28.7 (95% UI: 25.9–31.1) per 100,000 population, respectively. This reflected a decline of 32.8 % and 53.6 % since 1990. China contributed 44.7 (95% UI: 41.8–47.8) million episodes and 206,930.2 (95% UI: 171,260.9–251,990.5) deaths in 2021, with respective age-standardized rates of 2853.8 (95% UI: 2664.0–3067.6) episodes per 100,000 population and 14.0 (95% UI: 11.7–17.0) per 100,000 population. Between 1990 and 2021, the age-standardized incidence and mortality rates in China declined by 47.9 % and 76.9 % which were higher than the global average. This progress was primarily driven by reductions in children under 5 years. Older individuals ≥70 years exhibited the highest burden. In 2021, Guangdong reported the highest age-standardized incidence, while Guizhou had the highest mortality. Streptococcus pneumoniae was the leading cause of LRI-related deaths. Ambient particulate matter pollution, smoking, and low temperature were the primary risk factors, with notable sex-specific differences.ConclusionsDespite marked improvements, LRIs remain a major contributor to morbidity and mortality in China, particularly among children and older adults. Addressing the residual burden will require targeted strategies, including enhanced diagnostics, expanded vaccination, air quality control, and strengthened healthcare in high-burden provinces.Keywords: Lower respiratory infections, China, Mortality, Incidence, Risk factors

Introduction

Lower respiratory tract infections (LRIs), involving infections of the trachea, bronchi, and lungs, constitute a major global health concern. Key etiologic agents include bacterial pathogens such as Streptococcus pneumoniae (S. pneumoniae) and Haemophilus influenzae (H. influenzae) type b, as well as viral pathogens including influenza viruses and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV).1, 2, 3 The non-COVID-19 LRIs ranked as the fifth leading cause of death globally in 2021, accounting for approximately 2.18 million deaths, with the highest mortality rates observed among children under 5 years and adults over 70 years.4,5 Major contributing risk factors include air pollution, tobacco use, childhood malnutrition, and age-related comorbidities.2,6,7

S. pneumoniae remains the most prevalent LRI pathogen across regions with varying income levels, followed by H. influenzae.8 The global implementation of H. influenzae type b (Hib) vaccination has markedly reduced the incidence of H. influenzae pneumonia.5,9 However, non-vaccine-preventable pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and antibiotic-resistant organisms have emerged as significant contributors to the LRI burden.10,11 Mycoplasma pneumoniae, an atypical bacterium, is responsible for 4 % to 39 % of pediatric community-acquired pneumonia episodes and has become a leading cause of pneumonia among school-aged children.12,13 Viral infections, including influenza (14.1% of adult hospitalizations) and RSV (3.6 million pediatric hospitalizations in 2019), frequently precipitate secondary bacterial complications.14,15 Although non-pharmaceutical interventions during the COVID-19 pandemic temporarily suppressed RSV and influenza circulation,16, 17, 18 the extent of their post-pandemic resurgence in China remains unclear.

Previous studies in China have provided fragmented insights into the national LRI burden, but substantial gaps remain in spatiotemporal and demographic resolution. National-level analyses have largely relied on aggregated estimates, overlooking provincial heterogeneity in disease burden.19, 20, 21 Age-stratified investigations have been limited to discrete populations, such as older adults, without presenting continuous age-specific trends.22 Moreover, prior risk attribution studies have not included sex-specific population attributable fractions (PAFs) for modifiable risk factors,23 particularly among high-risk older individuals.

To address these gaps, we conducted a comprehensive assessment of non-COVID-19 LRIs in China and globally from 1990 to 2021. This study characterizes spatiotemporal patterns of incidence and mortality across 31 provinces, and provides age- and sex-specific estimates for 18 pathogen categories and key risk factors associated with non-COVID-19 LRIs.

Methods

Overview

This study evaluated the burden of non-COVID-19 LRIs at the provincial level across China using data and methodologies from the Global Burden of Disease 2021 (GBD 2021). The analysis included 31 provincial-level administrative units in the mainland of China, as well as the two special administrative regions of Hong Kong and Macao. GBD 2021 was designed to enable consistent comparisons of disease burden, associated risk factors, and etiologies over time from 1990 to 2021, disaggregated by locations, sex, and age. Detailed descriptions of the updated datasets and analytical methods applied in GBD 2021 have been published elsewhere.4 All procedures adhered to the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER) recommendations.24 This study utilized aggregate, anonymized data from the GBD 2021 study. As no individual-level data were involved, ethical approval was not required for this analysis.

Definitions

In this study, LRIs were defined as infections of the lower respiratory tract, including pneumonia or bronchiolitis, characterized clinically by symptoms such as cough, fever, and dyspnea.3 Although pathogens such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Bordetella pertussis, and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) can also infect the lower respiratory tract, these conditions were analyzed separately because of their distinct epidemiological and public health implications. Case definitions were based on standardized diagnostic coding using the International Classification of Diseases, with ICD-9 codes including 079.82, 466–469, 470.0, 480–481.9, 482.0–482.89, 483.0–483.9, 484.1–484.2, 484.6–484.7, 487–490.9, 510–511.9, 513.0–513.9, and ICD-10 codes for LRIs are A48.1, A70, B96.0–96.1, B97.21, B97.4–B97.6, J09–J11.89, J12–J13.9, J14–J14.0, J15–J15.8, J20–J21.9, J85.1, J91.0, P23.0–P23.4, U04–U04.9 (Supplementary Table 1).

Estimates of LRI mortality

Provincial-level mortality estimates for LRIs in China were derived primarily from the National Disease Surveillance Point System, Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention cause-of-death reporting system, and maternal and child health surveillance databases.25 Mortality was estimated using cause of death ensemble modeling methods (CODEm), a systematic approach that generates multiple statistical models and selects an ensemble of the best-performing models based on predictive validity and input data quality.3 Separate CODEm models were applied for each sex and two age groups (<5 years and ≥5 years) to ensure age-specific accuracy.

Risk factor and etiology estimation

The definitions and classification of risk factors—including household air pollution from solid fuels, ambient particulate matter pollution, non-optimal temperature, and lead exposure—were adopted from previously established frameworks used in GBD 2021.4 Attributable deaths and age-standardized DALY rates for each risk factor were estimated using a comparative risk assessment (CRA) methodology. This approach involved several key steps: (1) identifying causal risk–outcome pairs; (2) modeling relative risks as a function of exposure; (3) estimating population-level exposures disaggregated by age, sex, location, and year; (4) establishing the theoretical minimum risk exposure level (TMREL) to represent the counterfactual scenario; (5) calculating population attributable fractions (PAFs); and (6) quantifying deaths attributable to both individual and combined risk exposures. The PAF represents the proportion of disease burden that could theoretically be prevented if exposure was reduced to the TMREL. For harmful exposures, TMREL was set to zero. For risk–outcome associations with U- or J-shaped curves, the TMREL corresponded to the nadir of the risk function. The attributable burden for each risk–outcome pair was derived by multiplying the PAF by the total cause-specific disease burden.

Etiologic estimation was informed by multiple data sources, including cause-of-death registries, vital statistics, hospital discharge data, microbial laboratory records, and published literature.1 Mortality and morbidity were estimated for a comprehensive range of LRI-related pathogens, including Acinetobacter baumannii, Chlamydia species (spp.), Enterobacter spp., Escherichia coli, fungi, group B streptococcus, H. influenzae, influenza viruses, Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae), Legionella spp., Mycoplasma spp., polymicrobial infections, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, RSV, S. aureus, S. pneumoniae, and other viruses (excluding influenza and RSV), in addition to a residual category encompassing other bacterial pathogens.

Etiologic modeling followed a multistep process. First, incidence proportions were derived using a multinomial estimation within a network analysis model, though not all pathogen groups were simultaneously modeled.3,26 Second, these proportions were modeled as a function of age group, infection type, Hib and pneumococcal vaccination status, and the Healthcare Access and Quality (HAQ) Index, allowing generation of pathogen-specific predictions stratified by age, location, and year. Third, for data sources reporting only death, case-fatality rates (CFRs) were estimated using a Bayesian meta-regression tool (MR-BRT: Meta-Regression–Bayesian, Regularized, Trimmed), which enabled back-calculation of incidence using covariates such as age, pathogen type, and HAQ Index, incorporating random effects for data source heterogeneity.26, 27, 28 Fourth, for S. pneumoniae, a vaccine probe analysis was incorporated to address challenges in direct microbiological identification of the pathogen and enhance the robustness of incidence estimates.29 Modeled CFRs were then applied to convert case proportion into mortality proportion. All incident LRI episodes were distributed among the etiologies—detected and undetected—according to predicted age-, year-, and location-specific distributions. Finally, a multistep modeling procedure was implemented to adjust the incidence of influenza and RSV during the COVID-19 period (2020–2021), accounting for pandemic-related epidemiological shifts.

Statistical analysis

Age-standardized incidence, and mortality were computed using the GBD global standard population structure from 2021. To account for variability arising from both sampling and non-sampling sources, uncertainty intervals (UI) were computed following the standard GBD methodology. Specifically, 95 % UIs were generated by drawing 1000 samples from the posterior distributions at each modeling stage, with the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles reported as the lower and upper bounds of the estimates, respectively.4 Temporal trends in the burden of LRIs across China were assessed using Joinpoint regression models, which detect significant inflection points in trend trajectories over time. The average annual percent change (AAPC) with its 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated to summarize the trend over the entire study period. A P-value < 0.05 for the AAPC was considered statistically significant, leading to the rejection of the null hypothesis. To evaluate geographic heterogeneity, Poisson regression models were used to quantify regional differences in LRI burden across provinces. Forecasts of LRI incidence and mortality from 2022 to 2036 were generated using a hybrid modeling approach that integrated autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models with Bayesian structural time series (BSTS) methods, trained on historical data spanning 1990 to 2021 and stratified by age group. For the analysis of inter-provincial disparities, Poisson regression models were implemented for both incidence and mortality across Chinese provinces. Population size was included as an offset term to normalize for province-level population differences, and provincial-level unit was treated as a categorical predictor. The statistical significance of regional variations was assessed using likelihood ratio tests (LRTs), with a P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Consequently, provinces with non-significant results were interpreted as lacking substantial geographic heterogeneity in LRI incidence or mortality. All analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.0 (https://www.r-project.org/), in collaboration with the global GBD research network.

Results

Incidence and mortality of LRIs

In 2019, immediately prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, an estimated 50.0 (95% UI: 46.6–53.6) million episodes of LRIs occurred in China, corresponding to an age-standardized incidence rate of 3368.1 (95% UI: 3139.6–3641.7) episodes per 100,000 population. This represented a sustained decline from 50.9 (95% UI: 47.5–54.6) million episodes in 1990, with a further reduction to 44.7 (95% UI: 41.8–47.8) million episodes observed in 2021. Between 1990 and 2021, the age-standardized incidence rate declined by 47.9 % (95% UI: 45.4–50.3%), decreasing from 5481.1 (95% UI: 5149.1–5836.3) episodes per 100,000 population to 2853.8 (95% UI: 2664.0–3067.6) episodes per 100,000 population (Table 1, Fig. 1A). This corresponds to an AAPC of −2.1 % (P<0.0001), indicating a consistent downward trend in LRI incidence over the study period. Conversely, the global number of LRI episodes increased by 9.5 % (95% UI: 4.9–14.3%), from 313.9 (95% UI: 294.1–333.3) million in 1990 to 343.6 (95% UI: 325.2–363.5) million in 2021. However, the age-standardized incidence rate worldwide decreased by 32.8 % (95% UI: 30.6–34.8%), from 6373.2 (95% UI: 5993.5–6746.0) to 4283.6 (95% UI: 4057.0–4524.9) episodes per 100,000 population, with an AAPC of −1.3 % (P<0.0001) (Table 1). In China, the most substantial reduction in incidence was observed among children under 5 years, where rates fell from 16,638.3 (95% UI: 14,661.9–18,916.4) episodes per 100,000 population in 1990 to 3088.7 (95% UI: 2571.9–3649.6) episodes per 100,000 population in 2021, reflecting a decline of 81.4 % (95% UI: 79.2–83.45%). Age-specific reductions in other groups were as follows: 36.1 % (95% UI: 28.8–42.5%) in children aged 5–14 years, 17.9 % (95% UI: 13.5–22.0%) in individuals aged 15–49 years, 44.4 % (95% UI: 42.0–46.4%) in those aged 50–69 years, and 23.6 % (95% UI: 17.6–28.7%) in individuals aged ≥70 years (Fig. 1A). In 2021, the highest incidence rate was observed in adults ≥70 years (16,231.1 [95% UI: 14,550.4–17,980.8] episodes per 100,000 population), followed by adults aged 50–69 years (3149.8 [95 % UI: 2830.7–3445.4] episodes per 100,000 population), children under 5 years (3088.7 [95% UI: 2571.9–3649.6] episodes per 100,000 population), and children aged 5–14 years (2226.4 [95% UI: 1750.4–2781.5] episodes per 100,000 population) (Table 2). Globally, a similar pattern of highest burden among young children and older adults was observed. The age-specific incidence rates of LRIs worldwide in 1990 and 2021 could be found in Table 2 and Fig. 1B.

Regarding mortality, an estimated 198,750.5 (95% UI: 168,464.5–239,920.8) LRI-related deaths occurred in China in 2019, corresponding to an age-standardized mortality rate of 14.4 (95% UI: 12.4–17.3) per 100,000 population. This reflects a substantial long-term decline from 474,883.4 (95% UI: 414,630.7–532,254.6) deaths in 1990, although mortality increased to an estimated 206,930.2 (95% UI: 171,260.9–251,990.5) deaths in 2021. From 1990 to 2021, the age-standardized mortality rate declined by 76.9 % (95% UI: 71.2–80.9%), decreasing from 60.6 (95% UI: 53.0–66.7) deaths per 100,000 population to 14.0 (95% UI: 11.7–17.0) deaths per 100,000 population, with an AAPC of −4.8 % (P<0.0001), indicating a significant average annual decline in LRI mortality (Table 1, Fig. 2A). The greatest mortality rate reduction was observed in children under 5 years (95.4%, 95% UI: 93.9–96.6%), followed by those aged 5–14 years (86.1%, 95% UI: 81.6–88.8%), 50–69 years (72.2 %, 95 % UI: 62.0–78.7 %), 15–49 years (67.3 %, 95 % UI: 56.2–74.5 %), and adults aged ≥70 years (44.9 %, 95 % UI: 29.3–56.1 %). By 2021, globally death attributable to LRIs has declined by 27.6 % (95 % UI: 19.6–34.8 %), from 3.0 (95 % UI: 2.7–3.3) million in 1990 to 2.2 (95 % UI: 2.0–2.4) million in 2021. From 1990 to 2021, the age-standardized mortality rate worldwide also decreased by 53.6 % (95 % UI: 49.4–57.6 %), decreasing from 61.8 (95 % UI: 56.7–66.7) per 100,000 population to 28.7 (95 % UI: 25.9–31.1) per 100,000 population (Table 1), with an AAPC of −2.5 % (P < 0.0001). The greatest mortality rate reduction was observed in children under 5 years old (75.7 %, 95 % UI: 70.7–79.8 %), followed by those aged 5–14 years (59.3 %, 95 % UI: 52.9–63.9 %), 50–69 years (28.3 %, 21.8–34.0 %), ≥70 years (23.8 %, 95 % UI: 18.8–28.6 %), and adults aged 15–49 years (21.9 %, 95 % UI: 15.3–28.1 %) (Table 2, Fig. 2B).

Projections derived from ARIMA and BSTS models demonstrated excellent fit to historical data from 1990 to 2021, accurately replicating both long-term declining trends and pandemic-associated fluctuations in 2020 and 2021. Based on these models, LRI age-standardized incidence rate in China is projected to continue declining from 2803.2 episodes per 100,000 population in 2022 to 1812.8 episodes per 100,000 population by 2034. Age-standardized mortality rate in China is similarly projected to decrease from 12.6 per 100,000 population in 2022 to 0.5 per 100,000 population by 2034, indicating a sustained downward trajectory in both incidence and mortality throughout the forecast horizon. Globally, a continued decline is also projected. Model-based projections indicated that the global LRI age-standardized incidence rate is expected to decrease from an estimated 4253.7 episodes per 100,000 population in 2022 to approximately 3315.2 episodes per 100,000 population by 2034. Similarly, the global age-standardized mortality rate due to LRI is projected to decline from 28.1 to 15.2 per 100,000 population over the same period (Supplementary Table 2).

At the regional level, age-standardized incidence and mortality rates of LRIs showed significant downward trends from 1990 to 2021 across the 31 provincial-level units of the mainland of China and the special administrative regions of Hong Kong and Macao (Table 1). In 2021, the age-standardized incidence rate varied widely, ranging from 1477.6 (95 % UI: 1366.2–1588.0) episodes per 100,000 population in Shaanxi to 8637.1 (95 % UI: 7965.3–9382.0) episodes per 100,000 population in Guangdong (P < 0.0001). Similarly, age-standardized mortality ranged from 5.6 (95 % UI: 3.9–10.6) per 100,000 population in Shanghai to 38.1 (95 % UI: 26.8–48.0) in Guizhou (P < 0.0001). The highest incidence rates were observed in Guangdong, followed by Shandong, Jiangsu, Hong Kong, and Xizang, while the highest mortality rates occurred in Guizhou, followed by Xizang, Xinjiang, Qinghai, and Yunnan. Conversely, Shaanxi, Henan, Beijing, Hubei, and Anhui exhibited the lowest incidence rates, while Shanghai, Shandong, Henan, Jiangsu, and Anhui reported the lowest mortality. In general, southeastern coastal provinces demonstrated higher age-standardized incidence rates, whereas western provinces exhibited higher mortality rates, reflecting substantial geographic disparities in the LRI burden across China (Table 1).

Etiology of LRI-related mortality

In 2021, S. pneumoniae was the leading pathogen responsible for LRI-related mortality across all age groups in China, accounting for an estimated 51,179.8 (95 % UI: 42,055.4–62,158.1) deaths. This was followed by S. aureus (45,973.4 [95 % UI: 37,338.7–56,154.3] deaths), P. aeruginosa (15,459.9 [95 % UI: 12,648.8–18,796.1] deaths), K. pneumoniae (14,831.2 [95 % UI: 12,171.2–17,949.7] deaths], and a residual category of other bacterial pathogens (14,144.3 [95 % UI: 11,368.7–17,812.6] deaths) (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 3). The distribution of etiologies was broadly consistent between males and females. Across age groups, S. pneumoniae remained the most common cause of LRI mortality (except in the ≥70-year-old group) (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 3). In children under 5 years, the second most frequent etiology was other viral pathogens, whereas S. aureus was the second-leading cause in all older age groups (except in the ≥70-year-old group). The third-ranked pathogen varied by age: S. aureus in children under 5 years, other viral etiologies in individuals aged 5–49 years, and P. aeruginosa in adults aged 50–69 years.

Risk factors associated with LRI mortality

In 2021, an estimated 117,310.3 (95 % UI: 80,829.4–154,882.8) LRI deaths were attributable to all modifiable risk factors. Notable sex-based disparities were observed. Among men, the leading risk factors were smoking, ambient particulate matter pollution, low temperature, secondhand smoke exposure, and household air pollution from solid fuels. Among women, ambient particulate matter pollution ranked highest, followed by secondhand smoke, low temperature, household air pollution from solid fuels, and smoking (Fig. 5). Substantial age-related heterogeneity was also evident in both the attributable burden and PAFs. In children under 5 years, LRI risk was dominated by nutritional and environmental determinants. The top 10 contributors in this group included child underweight, ambient particulate matter pollution, secondhand smoke exposure, child stunting, low temperature, low birth weight, child wasting, non-exclusive breastfeeding, household air pollution from solid fuels, and lack of access to handwashing facilities. In contrast, among individuals aged 5 years and older, the dominant risk profile shifted toward pollution- and smoking-related exposures. These included ambient particulate matter pollution, smoking, secondhand smoke, household air pollution from solid fuels. Among the population ≥5 years old, the relative ranking of risk factors varied by age group (Fig. 5A–C, Supplementary Table 5).

Discussion

In this comprehensive analysis, we quantified the burden of LRIs in China and globally between 1990 and 2021, with stratification by age, sex, pathogen, risk factor, and geographic region. Our findings revealed a 47.9 % reduction (95 % UI: 45.4 %–50.3 %) in all-age incidence rate and a substantially greater 76.9 % decline (95 % UI: 71.2 %–80.9 %) in mortality rate over the study period in China. The decline in both metrics occurred at a significantly faster rate in China than the global average. This marked divergence suggests that public health interventions have been considerably more successful in reducing case fatality than in preventing the onset of new infections.

Despite these encouraging trends, LRIs continue to disproportionately affect children under 5 years and adults aged 70 years and older in China. In 2021, the highest incidence was observed in individuals ≥70 years (16,231.1 per 100,000), who also exhibited the slowest decline in mortality (44.9 %). Across all age groups, S.pneumoniae remained the dominant pathogen responsible for mortality, followed by S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, K. pneumoniae, and other bacterial pathogens. The leading risk factors including ambient particulate matter pollution, smoking, low temperature, secondhand smoke exposure, and household air pollution from solid fuels, varied significantly by sex, as reflected in the PAFs.

The more substantial decline in mortality compared with incidence (−76.9 % vs. −47.9 %) underscores progress in clinical management and secondary prevention. In children under 5 years, where mortality fell by 95.4 %, the reduction likely reflects the widespread adoption of Hib, pneumococcal conjugate (PCV), and influenza vaccines, as well as improvements in child nutrition, reductions in household air pollution, and mitigation of secondhand smoke exposure. Nevertheless, the PAFs for key risk factors—including ambient particulate matter pollution, high temperature, and smoking—rose between 1990 and 2021, indicating an evolving risk landscape. Among older adults (≥70 years), the incidence declined by only 23.6 %, likely as a result of immunosenescence, the age-related decline in immune function that increases susceptibility to LRIs and their complications.30,31 Addressing this vulnerability requires investment in more effective vaccines for older populations; however, vaccine coverage among older adults remains limited in China.32

Although overall trends in LRI incidence and mortality showed similar trajectories for male and female individuals, distinct sex-based differences in risk exposure were evident. These sex disparities are rooted in both social and behavioral determinants. Men are more likely to be employed in occupations associated with high exposure to outdoor air pollution (e.g., mining), while women are disproportionately exposed to indoor air pollutants, particularly from biomass-fuel use. Gendered caregiving responsibilities also increase women's exposure to respiratory pathogens via close contact with young children. Furthermore, the higher prevalence of smoking among men not only increases their direct risk but also contributes to secondhand smoke exposure among women. In addition, women often face barriers to timely healthcare-seeking, driven by caregiving obligations and financial constraints.33

Despite decades of progress, the burden of LRIs in China remains geographically heterogeneous and substantial. Industrialized eastern provinces such as Guangdong, Shandong, Jiangsu and Hong Kong reported the highest age-standardized incidence rates, whereas underdeveloped western provinces—including Guizhou, Xizang, Xinjiang, and Qinghai—experienced the highest age-standardized mortality. This east–west disparity likely reflects a convergence of socioeconomic, healthcare, and environmental inequalities.3,5,34 In western provinces, persistent poverty and low health literacy contribute to underutilization of preventive and curative services, exacerbating the burden of infectious diseases such as LRIs and meningitis.35 These regions also suffer from inadequate vaccine coverage for S. pneumoniae and H. influenzae type b, as well as workforce shortages in healthcare.9 Additionally, the continued reliance on solid fuels for household cooking and heating in western highlands further increases indoor air pollution and LRI risk. These findings are consistent with global evidence indicating that preventive, therapeutic and environmental disadvantages are more prevalent in low-resource settings, thereby amplifying the burden of preventable respiratory infections.36,37

Mechanistically, vaccination reduces LRI-related mortality by directly lowering the burden of vaccine-preventable pathogens such as S. pneumoniae and H. influenzae.38 Concurrently, air quality interventions mitigate LRI risk by preserving the integrity of respiratory epithelial barriers and reducing particulate matter (PM)-induced epithelial damage and macrophage dysfunction. To translate these mechanisms into effective public health practice, we propose a three-pronged implementation framework encompassing vaccine optimization, regionally tailored interventions, and comprehensive risk factor mitigation. First, vaccine optimization is critical to address dominant bacterial pathogens and age-related immune decline. Expanding the use of high-valency PCVs and adjuvanted influenza vaccines is essential to address coverage gaps in older adults, who remain particularly vulnerable as a result of immunosenescence. In parallel, accelerating the nationwide integration of PCV into the National Immunization Program for children would further reduce the burden of disease in younger populations. Second, region-specific precision interventions should be implemented to address the heterogeneity in disease burden and risk exposure. For example, mobile vaccination units and enhanced emission controls at coal-fired power plants are recommended for industrial regions. In high-mortality western provinces such as Guizhou and Xizang, financial subsidies for clean household energy alternatives are needed to reduce reliance on solid fuels. In high-incidence industrial zones such as Guangdong, targeted subsidies for adult PCV coverage and aggressive fine particulate matter (PM2.5) source control policies should be prioritized. Third, effective risk factor mitigation must involve life-course approaches. These should include scaling up child nutrition programs to address undernutrition and stunting, expanding enforcement of smoke-free legislation, and instituting more stringent industrial emissions standards to curb ambient pollution exposure.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, comparisons between different iterations of the GBD studies are inherently limited because of evolving methodologies, changes in data inputs, and refinements in analytical frameworks.39, 40 Second, data limitations—particularly in low-income provinces with the highest LRI burdens—may introduce bias into burden estimates. In these regions, underreporting and inconsistent data quality remain significant challenges.3 Third, variability in pathogen detection methods and diagnostic standards across regions and facilities may compromise the accuracy of etiological attribution and result in potential misclassification. Standardization of testing protocols is recommended for future studies to enhance comparability. Lastly, etiological proportions from the Global Burden of Antimicrobial Resistance study were applied to GBD LRI estimates, although minor discrepancies exist between the definitions of LRIs used in the two datasets, particularly in terms of disease classification granularity.

Despite these limitations, this study also offers notable strengths. To our knowledge, it represents the comprehensive assessment of LRI burden, etiology, and associated risk factors at the provincial level in China. The use of standardized GBD methodologies ensures analytical rigor and cross-provincial comparability, leveraging recent advancements in epidemiologic modeling and data science. Moreover, in regions lacking high-quality vital registration data, LRI mortality estimates were informed by verbal autopsy data—an approach that, while imperfect, provides a pragmatic alternative in data-scarce settings. Finally, we used covariate-based modeling and regional trend extrapolation to estimate LRI burden and etiological distributions in areas with limited direct data availability, thereby providing a more complete and geographically inclusive picture of LRI epidemiology across China.

Conclusions

Although substantial reductions in LRI-related incidence and mortality were achieved in China between 1990 and 2021, disproportionate burdens persist, particularly among children under 5 years and adults over 70 years. These disparities are largely driven by modifiable risk factors and pathogen-specific patterns. While children have experienced remarkable improvements in survival, they remain at elevated risk of LRI-related morbidity and mortality as a result of undernutrition and household air pollution, particularly in socioeconomically disadvantaged regions. Among older individuals, the highest incidence and slowest decline in mortality persist, driven in part by the continued predominance of S. pneumoniae and vulnerability to temperature fluctuations. The LRI burden remains widespread and unevenly distributed across China’s provinces. A clear mortality gap separates the high-burden western highlands (e.g., Guizhou) from low-mortality eastern coastal regions (e.g., Shanghai), while incidence rates continue to rise in heavily industrialized provinces such as Guangdong. Sustained progress will require a multifaceted strategy centered on vaccine optimization, geographically tailored interventions, targeted risk factor reduction, and expanded child nutrition and health security programs. Importantly, these efforts must be designed with affordability and scalability in mind to ensure equitable implementation and to reduce persistent disparities in LRI incidence and mortality across China.
