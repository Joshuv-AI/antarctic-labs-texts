Appropriateness of terlipressin use and its association with clinical efficacy and safety: A real-world cohort study of 240 hospitalized patients
Authors: Yifei Peng, Hongxia Duan, Meng Li, Ke Xiang, Jialin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to evaluate the appropriateness of terlipressin use and assess its effects on coagulation parameters in 240 hospitalized patients treated between January 2024 and January 2025 in a real-world, single-center retrospective cohort. Clinical appropriateness, encompassing diagnostic indication, initial dosing, and administration route, was rigorously evaluated against current clinical guidelines and the manufacturer’s prescribing information. Key coagulation parameters – prothrombin time, thrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, and fibrinogen – were analyzed before and after treatment using paired-sample t tests, and patients were stratified into short-course (≤5 days) and long-course (≥6 days) therapy groups to examine the impact of treatment duration on outcomes using independent-sample t tests. The analysis revealed a high degree of appropriateness: 231 patients (96.3%) received terlipressin for guideline-consistent indications, 143 patients (59.6%) were administered the recommended initial bolus dose of 2 mg, and 185 cases (77.1%) employed the advised slow intravenous infusion route. Treatment with terlipressin significantly improved coagulation, with prothrombin time decreasing by a mean of 1.43 seconds and fibrinogen increasing by 0.11 g/L (both P < .01). However, extending the treatment course beyond 5 days offered no additional coagulation benefit, as no parameters showed significant improvement in the long-course compared with the short-course group (all P > .05). These results underscore that terlipressin use was largely appropriate for indication and route, but extending therapy beyond 5 days provided no additional coagulation benefit. This finding supports stricter adherence to guideline-recommended treatment duration to promote more rational and effective use of terlipressin in clinical practice.

1. Introduction

Hepatitis-induced cirrhosis frequently leads to increased portal venous pressure, which can result in the rupture of esophageal and gastric varices, triggering upper gastrointestinal bleeding.[1,2] This condition severely impacts patients’ quality of life, and is characterized by acute onset, difficulty in achieving hemostasis, and a high short-term mortality rate. Without timely treatment, it can readily progress to hemorrhagic shock or hepatic encephalopathy, potentially leading to death.[3] Portal hypertension is the primary cause of esophageal and gastric variceal bleeding (EGVB).[4] Consequently, the use of drugs to reduce portal venous pressure is a common therapeutic approach for upper gastrointestinal bleeding.[5–7]

Terlipressin, a synthetic vasoactive agent, is a commonly used first-line clinical treatment. It constricts small veins in the splanchnic circulation, reducing blood flow and significantly decreasing portal venous pressure while improving hepatic tissue blood flow.[8] Additionally, it contracts esophageal wall smooth muscle, promotes intestinal peristalsis, reduces gastrointestinal stress responses, inhibits the release of inflammatory factors, and possesses effects against hemorrhagic shock, endotoxin, and histamine-induced shock.

Due to its favorable hemodynamic effects and reliable efficacy, terlipressin is widely used in the treatment of EGVB, other gastrointestinal bleeding, and related hemorrhagic shock, establishing itself as a core pharmacological strategy for managing cirrhosis-induced portal hypertension.[9,10] This study analyzes the appropriateness of terlipressin use in 240 inpatients at our hospital, aiming to provide a reference for rational clinical drug use.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and setting

This was a single-center, retrospective, observational cohort study conducted at the Department of Pharmacy, Leshan Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Affiliated to Chengdu University of Chinese Medicine, Leshan, China. The study period was from January 2024 to January 2025. The study protocol was approved by the hospital’s Ethics Committee (No: 2023-14).

2.2. Study population and eligibility criteria

Using the hospital information system, we retrospectively screened all inpatients who received at least 1 dose of terlipressin (specification: 1 mg) during the study period. A total of 240 patients were initially identified. The primary inclusion criterion was the administration of terlipressin for any indication. Patients were excluded from the efficacy analysis if pretreatment or posttreatment coagulation parameter data (prothrombin time [PT], thrombin time [TT], activated partial thromboplastin time [APTT], and fibrinogen [FIB]) were incomplete (n = 18). Thus, 222 patients were included in the final analysis of coagulation outcomes.

2.3. Data collection and variables

Patient data were extracted from electronic medical records and recorded in a standardized Excel spreadsheet. Collected variables included: basic demographics (age, gender); hospitalization details (clinical diagnosis, length of stay); medication information (dosage, duration of administration, route of administration, concomitant medications); and laboratory parameters (coagulation profile: PT, TT, APTT, FIB before and after treatment).

2.4. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk). Categorical data are presented as numbers (percentages) and were compared using the chi-square (χ2) test. The normality of continuous data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s). For comparisons of coagulation parameters before and after treatment (paired data), paired-sample t tests were employed as the data met normality assumptions. For comparisons between the short-course and long-course groups (independent data), independent-sample t tests were used. A two-sided P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

4. Discussion

In this study, 231 hospitalized patients (96.3%) had clinical diagnoses, including terms such as upper gastrointestinal bleeding, EGVB, portal hypertension, septic shock, and hepatorenal syndrome, which align with the clinical indications for terlipressin, indicating a high level of diagnostic appropriateness in patient selection. For the remaining 9 patients whose records lacked explicit mention of these conditions, pharmacist review attributed this to incomplete diagnostic documentation. Therefore, standardizing clinical diagnosis entries for terlipressin use is of paramount importance.

According to the package insert, the 1 mg terlipressin lyophilized powder should be reconstituted with the supplied 5 mL solvent for injection, and the solution should be used immediately after preparation. The recommended initial dose is 2 mg, administered by slow IV injection (over 1 minute). The maintenance dose is 1 to 2 mg intravenously every 4 to 6 hours until bleeding is controlled, with a recommended treatment duration of 24 to 48 hours. Consistent with guideline recommendations[11] for acute EGVB or portal hypertensive gastroenteropathy bleeding, terlipressin should be initiated promptly (2 mg every 4 hours for the first 48 hours, then 1 mg every 4 hours) and continued for 3 to 5 days. In the present study, 143 patients (59.58%) received the recommended initial dose of 2 mg, adhering to the specifications in both the package insert and guidelines. For drugs acting on the central nervous or cardiovascular systems, administration of the recommended initial dose ensures a rapid and safe pharmacological response at the receptor level. Thus, it is strongly advised that clinicians adhere to the dosage regimens outlined in the package insert and relevant guidelines.

This study preliminarily investigated changes in coagulation parameters – PT, TT, APTT, and FIB – before and after treatment. The results demonstrated statistically significant differences (P < .05), indicating that terlipressin significantly improves coagulation function in patients with cirrhosis-related upper gastrointestinal bleeding or EGVB.[12] The underlying mechanism involves the enzymatic cleavage of terlipressin into its active metabolite, lysine vasopressin, which acts on V1 receptors to induce splanchnic vasoconstriction, reduce portal pressure, and consequently improve blood flow velocity in the portal and splenic veins, thereby accelerating the coagulation process.[13–15]

However, comparison of coagulation parameters between the short-course (≤5 days) and long-course (≥6 days) groups revealed no statistically significant differences (P > .05). This suggests that extending terlipressin treatment beyond 5 days provides no additional benefit for managing cirrhosis-related EGVB, supporting the guideline recommendation of a treatment duration not exceeding 5 days.

Our finding that extended therapy (≥6 days) provided no additional coagulation benefit aligns with and reinforces the recommendations from major clinical guidelines,[11,12] which advise a treatment duration of 3 to 5 days for acute variceal bleeding. This consistency supports the external validity of our observation and underscores a key area for quality improvement in clinical practice – avoiding unnecessarily prolonged courses.

In this study, 185 patients (77.08%) received terlipressin via slow IV injection or slow micro-pump infusion. This adherence to the recommended route is important because research indicates that overly rapid infusion rates can lead to rapid metabolic clearance of the drug, potentially compromising efficacy and increasing the risk of adverse reactions.[16] Common adverse reactions associated with terlipressin include peripheral vasoconstriction, abdominal cramps, diarrhea, and other gastrointestinal symptoms, as well as reversible cardiac ischemia, atypical chest pain, and ventricular premature contractions. Serious adverse effects, such as hyponatremia and skin necrosis, also occur with notable frequency.[17,18] Although recent domestic case reports have documented several adverse events with terlipressin use, and direct evidence linking administration rate to these events is lacking, its pharmacological action – vasoconstriction of splanchnic vessels and smooth muscle leading to reduced hepatic blood flow and portal pressure – warrants caution. Adherence to the recommended slow administration rate helps avoid sudden peaks in drug concentration, minimizes vascular irritation, and may reduce the incidence of adverse reactions.[19]

Furthermore, 232 patients (96.67%) in this study received concomitant therapy with somatostatin for injection, reflecting the widespread clinical adoption of this combination to achieve synergistic hemostatic effects. Somatostatin, a tetradecapeptide hormone, acts by modulating insulin secretion, gastric pepsin production, and growth hormone release, promoting vasoconstriction. It mimics natural somatostatin effects, enhancing contraction of visceral vascular tissue, significantly reducing portal hypertension and splanchnic blood flow, ameliorating bleeding symptoms, and achieving effective hemostasis.[13] The combination of terlipressin and somatostatin in clinical practice leverages their complementary pharmacological effects, contributing to reduced mortality and facilitating rapid hemostasis.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, its single-center, retrospective observational design is susceptible to selection bias and unmeasured confounding factors. Second, the sample size, while adequate for overall analysis, limited subgroup comparisons. Third, our assessment of efficacy relied primarily on surrogate laboratory endpoints (coagulation parameters) rather than direct clinical outcomes such as rebleeding rates, transfusion requirements, or mortality. Fourth, despite adjusting for basic demographics, residual confounding from factors such as severity of liver disease, concurrent procedures, or precise timing of lab draws cannot be entirely ruled out. Future prospective, multi-center studies with standardized protocols and hard clinical endpoints are warranted to confirm our findings.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, our study provides insightful implications for the use of terlipressin, including dose and timing, in clinical practice. Optimal use of terlipressin requires close clinical vigilance, strict adherence to recommended dosing and administration, and avoidance of unnecessary prolongation of therapy. Limiting treatment to a maximum of 5 days, or stopping earlier once hemostasis is achieved, may help optimize outcomes while reducing the risk of adverse events.
