One-Year Real-World Outcomes of Intravitreal Faricimab for Previously Treated Neovascular Age-Related Macular Degeneration
Authors: Giuseppe Cancian, Arianna Paris, Lia Agliati, Angelica Rizzato, Michele Clerici, Giulio Volpe, Moreno Menghini, Gabriela Grimaldi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study assessed the efficacy, durability, and safety of faricimab in patients with neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD), previously treated with aflibercept or ranibizumab with unsatisfactory results.MethodsThis was a single-center, prospective cohort study of all consecutive patients with nAMD switched to intravitreally administered faricimab from traditional anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) treatments between September 2022 and April 2023 because of unsatisfactory response (maximal fluid-free interval ≤ 8 weeks). Faricimab was administered with a loading dose of four 4-weekly injections, followed by a treat-and-extend regimen. The primary outcome measures were maximum fluid-free interval after the switch and last assigned treatment interval. Secondary outcome measures included best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) and structural optical coherence tomography parameters.ResultsThirty-three eyes of 33 patients were included. Patients were followed for a median of 72 weeks [interquartile range 61, 76]. Median maximum fluid-free treatment interval after switch to faricimab and the last assigned interval were significantly longer than before the switch (7 vs. 4 weeks, p < 0.001 and 8 vs. 5 weeks, p < 0.001, respectively). Significant improvements in central subfield thickness (353 vs. 281 µm), macular volume (2.46 vs. 2.16 mm3), and pigment epithelial detachment height (198 vs. 150 µm) were observed (all p < 0.001). BCVA remained stable at 0.4 versus 0.3 logMAR before switch (p = 0.190). One eye (3%) developed intraocular inflammation and one eye (3%) developed a retinal pigment epithelium tear.ConclusionsFaricimab improved anatomical outcomes and allowed longer treatment intervals in patients with nAMD previously treated with other anti-VEGF therapies with unsatisfactory response, reducing treatment burden. A favorable safety profile was observed.Keywords: Age-related macular degeneration, Faricimab, Anti-VEGF, Switcher, Poor responder

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Short-term retrospective real-world evidence in patients with neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) switching to faricimab from traditional anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) treatment suggests potential to improve morphological outcomes and extend durability.The current study aims to determine whether the benefits offered by switching to faricimab are confirmed in a prospective study over a relatively longer follow-up time. What was learned from the study? After switch to faricimab and a median treatment of 72 weeks, patients achieved an improvement in durability (median extension of the treatment interval of 3 weeks), and morphology (median reduction of 72 µm), with stability of functional results (no improvement or worsening of visual acuity) and a good overall safety profile.Long-term prospective data confirm that intravitreally administered faricimab improves durability and morphological outcomes in patients switched from traditional anti-VEGF and treated with loading dose and treat-and-extend regimen. Long-term data confirm the excellent safety profile demonstrated in the pivotal trials, showing a low rate of adverse events.

Introduction

Neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) is the most common cause of visual impairment among older patients in developed countries [1]. Over the past 18 years, anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) therapies have revolutionized the treatment of nAMD, providing substantial benefits in disease management and prognosis. Despite these advancements, a subset of patients exhibits limited responses to anti-VEGF therapies, necessitating frequent re-treatments and imposing substantial burdens on healthcare systems and patients [1].

Faricimab (Vabysmo, Roche/Genentech), a novel agent that inhibits both angiopoietin-2 (Ang-2) and VEGF-A, has shown promising results in phase 3 trials (TENAYA and LUCERNE), demonstrating efficacy and durability in treatment-naïve patients with nAMD. These trials reported that over 70% of patients achieved extended dosing intervals of 12 or 16 weeks, underscoring faricimab’s potential for reducing treatment frequency in treatment-naïve patients with nAMD [2]. Additionally, these results were maintained at 2 years, further validating the long-term efficacy and durability of faricimab [3]. Since its introduction, an increasing amount of real-world evidence has been published on intravitreally administered faricimab, with special attention to patients previously treated with other anti-VEGF agents such as ranibizumab, aflibercept, and brolucizumab, who were not included in pivotal trials. These real-world case series suggest that the outcomes observed in pivotal trials are generally confirmed [4–20]. Nonetheless, most existing real-world studies only report short-term data and vary in terms of treatment regimens.

To fill this gap, we aimed to assess the efficacy, durability, and safety of faricimab in patients with nAMD who were previously treated with aflibercept or ranibizumab with unsatisfactory results. In this real-world study, we followed up patients over an extended observation period beyond 12 months to evaluate long-term outcomes of faricimab treatment, expanding the results published in a previous study by our group [14]. To our knowledge, this is the real-world study with the longest average follow-up that has ever been published in the literature.

Methods

This was an observational, prospective, single-center study conducted at the Ophthalmology Department of the Ospedale Regionale of Lugano in Switzerland. The study protocol was reviewed and approved locally by the ethics committee of Canton Ticino (CE 4340), with approval number 2023-00653. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. The research and data handling were conducted in compliance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and its amendments.

Population

All consecutive patients with nAMD who were switched from traditional anti-VEGF therapies (aflibercept or ranibizumab) to intravitreally administered faricimab because of unsatisfactory treatment response, between September 2022 and April 2023, were enrolled.

Inclusion criteria were a treatment duration of at least 6 months before the switch and a maximum fluid-free interval equal to or less than 8 weeks before the switch. Specifically, patients were divided into two subgroups based on treatment response to previous anti-VEGF therapy, using the nomenclature from a previous study from our group [14]: frequent flyers (FF, group 1), whose maximum fluid-free injection interval ranged between 4 and 8 weeks, and poor responders (PR, group 2), who were treated on a fixed 4-weekly injection interval and exhibited persistent disease activity. Persistent disease activity was defined as the presence of persistent intra- and/or subretinal fluid on macular optical coherence tomography (OCT) despite monthly aflibercept or ranibizumab intravitreal treatment (IVT) over at least 3 months.

Exclusion criteria were absence of clinical documentation prior to the switch, the inability to obtain high-quality imaging, and the refusal to provide informed consent. In case of bilateral treatment with faricimab, only one eye per patient was included. In cases where both eyes were eligible, the eye with the longer follow-up time was selected.

Study Protocol

At baseline, before the switch (T0), all patients underwent a comprehensive multimodal retinal imaging assessment to characterize the macular neovascularization. This included ultra-widefield (UWF) color fundus photography (Optos plc, Dunfermline, Scotland), fluorescein and indocyanine green angiography, spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT), and fundus autofluorescence (FAF) using the Heidelberg Spectralis® device (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). At each subsequent study visit, SD-OCT, FAF, and UWF color fundus photography were performed, alongside a morpho-functional assessment which included best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), intraocular pressure (IOP), and slit-lamp examination with dilated fundoscopy.

All patients were scheduled to receive a loading dose of four faricimab IVT every 4 weeks. Subsequently, at week 12, patients who showed a complete clinical response, consisting of a dry macular OCT, were extended to an 8-week interval, while those who did not exhibit a complete clinical response were maintained on a 4-week interval. A real-world treat-and-extend regimen was applied thereafter, as detailed in a previous study from our group [14].

Outcome Measures

Primary outcome measures were the maximum fluid-free interval between two injections achieved with faricimab, and the last intended interval, defined as the last treatment interval assigned to the patient at their last clinic assessment.

Secondary outcome measures included BCVA and structural SD-OCT parameters. Three SD-OCT parameters were selected to assess anatomical response: (1) central subfield thickness (CST), defined as the mean retinal thickness between the internal limiting membrane (ILM) and Bruch’s membrane (BM) within a 1-mm-diameter circular area around the center of the fovea; (2) central macular volume (MV), defined as the mean volume of the retina within a 3-mm-diameter circular area around the fovea; (3) pigment epithelial detachment (PED) height, defined as the longest distance between Bruch’s membrane and the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), using the automated segmentation of the Heidelberg Eye Explorer software V.2 (Heidelberg Engineering, V.2.4.1, Heidelberg, Germany). A manual correction of B-scans was conducted in case of automated segmentation errors. These morpho-functional parameters were measured at three timepoints: T0, at baseline; T1, at the end of the loading dose (week 12); and T2, at the last visit.

Additional secondary outcome measures were the ratio of eyes achieving at least one dry macular OCT scan and time between the first faricimab injection and the first dry macular OCT scan.

Statistical Analysis

The maximum fluid-free interval between two injections and the last intended interval achieved with faricimab treatment were compared to their corresponding intervals before the switch, using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The same statistical analysis was conducted for the PR and FF subgroups. For statistical analysis, PR eyes were attributed a maximum fluid-free interval of 4 weeks, despite not displaying any dry OCT scan before the switch. Differences in BCVA, CST, central MV, and PED height between timepoints (T0, T1, and T2) were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

Statistical analyses were performed using RStudio (version 4.4.0). Data distributions were summarized by median, interquartile range (Md [IQR]), and range (minimum–maximum), while proportions were summarized by number of eyes and percentage. BCVA was measured in decimals and for the purpose of this analysis converted to the logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR). P values inferior to 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Thirty-eight eyes with nAMD were switched to faricimab between September 2022 and April 2023 because of an unsatisfactory response to aflibercept or ranibizumab. Of these, five eyes were excluded: two because of lack of pre-switch clinical documentation and three in patients with bilateral disease, where the contralateral eye was included as a result of a longer follow-up period.

As a result, 33 eyes of 33 patients, with a median age of 82 years [IQR 77, 85] and a mild female predominance (58%), were included in the study. Patients were followed for a median of 72 weeks [IQR 61, 76]. Twelve eyes (36%) were classified as FF, while 21 eyes (64%) as PR. The most prevalent macular neovascularization (MNV) type at baseline was type 1 (67%), five patients (15%) had only one seeing eye and eight (24%) were active smokers. A significantly higher number of eyes displayed at least one dry macular OCT scan after switch to faricimab (N = 31; 94%) than before the switch (N = 12; 36%, p < 0.001, McNemar’s test). The median time to OCT dry scan after switch was 4.0 weeks [IQR 4, 12].

At the last observation, three patients (9%) had been switched from faricimab to an alternative medication, due to recurrence of fluid on macular SD-OCT upon extension to 5 weeks, requiring an excessively short treatment interval of 4 weeks. Notably, all three patients had a follow-up period longer than 52 weeks after the switch.

One patient (3%) with a known history of ischemic heart disease died from acute myocardial infarction after a follow-up period of 37 weeks. Ocular adverse events were observed in two eyes (6%). Of these, one eye experienced a retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) tear after two intravitreal injections of faricimab. At baseline, this eye exhibited a dome-shaped PED of 534 μm, which was the highest in the cohort. One eye had two episodes of anterior uveitis, both of which were diagnosed at least 10 weeks after the preceding injection (69 days after IVT #6, 75 days after IVT #8) and resolved without sequelae with topical steroidal treatment. No other systemic or ocular adverse events were reported during the study follow-up.

Discussion

The present study aimed to assess the efficacy, durability, and safety of IVT faricimab in a real-world cohort of patients with nAMD who previously exhibited unsatisfactory responses to IVT aflibercept or ranibizumab. This study extended follow-up to a median of 72 weeks, expanding 6-month data previously published by our group [14]. Notably, our research presents the most extended average follow-up period reported in the literature for such studies, underscoring the robustness and significance of our findings.

In real-world practice, it is well known that when a new anti-VEGF therapy receives regulatory approval, it is preferentially administered to patients currently undergoing treatment who exhibit an unsatisfactory response, such as those with persistent fluid or those requiring frequent dosing intervals of every 4–6 weeks [21]. Indeed, in recent months, numerous real-world studies on faricimab use in previously treated patients have been published [4–20]. The results have shown improved durability and favorable anatomical outcomes, with no proportional visual acuity improvement. It is important to note that the majority of these studies have short-term follow-ups, while the only three studies with 1-year follow-ups have a retrospective design [7, 18, 20].

The primary outcome of the present study was maximum fluid-free interval after the switch to faricimab. Our analysis showed that faricimab allowed longer treatment intervals in both the PR and FF groups, with median intervals increasing by 3 weeks (Fig. 3). The PR subgroup achieved higher statistical significance than the FF subgroup (p < 0.01 vs. p < 0.05). We believe that this difference in our sample can be attributed to the larger sample size of the first group (21 eyes vs. 12 eyes). Regarding the last intended interval, this parameter also showed a median improvement of 3 weeks, which reached statistical significance only in the PR subgroup and not in the FF subgroup (p = 0.109), likely due to the smaller size of the latter subgroup. At baseline, all patients had an intended treatment interval < 10 weeks, while, at the last visit, 36.4% of patients had reached an intended interval ≥ 10 weeks (Fig. 2). These parameters demonstrate increased durability and consequent reduction of the treatment burden. This finding aligns with existing literature, where studies with a 1-year follow-up showed similar results. Rush demonstrated that in a cohort of 54 eyes classified as PR before the switch to faricimab, 31.5% improved to an interval ≥ 8 weeks and 42.6% to an interval of 5-7 weeks [7]. Takahashi et al. showed that in 30 eyes with an interval ≤ 12 weeks before switching from brolucizumab to faricimab, the treatment interval lengthened by 2 weeks after 1 year of therapy [18]. Aljundi et al. showed that in 33 eyes, the number of yearly injections reduced from 8.6 ± 1.2 to 7.1 ± 2.4 IVT after switching to faricimab from an alternative medication [20].

Although faricimab has shown promise in providing greater durability for switched patients, in our study, three patients (9%) were switched to alternative anti-VEGF therapies because of unsatisfactory treatment intervals. Moreover, a subset of patients continued to require short treatment intervals even after 1 year of therapy, with 30.3% of study eyes being kept on a treatment interval of 4–6 weeks at the last follow-up (Fig. 2). However, 94% of these patients managed to achieve a dry OCT, suggesting that in the nAMD switcher population IVT faricimab might cause a shift of previously deemed poor responders to faricimab frequent flyers. In these patients, although faricimab did not offer an advantage in terms of durability, the anatomical benefit with fluid resolution might still be protective against further worsening over time.

Our study demonstrated that switching to faricimab in patients with unsatisfactory responses to aflibercept and ranibizumab led to significant morphological improvements (CST, central MV, and PED height), which were achieved after a complete loading dose of four monthly IVT faricimab and were maintained throughout the follow-up. The performance of faricimab in terms of anatomical outcomes is notable, considering that 94% of patients achieved at least one dry OCT scan after the switch, compared to only 36% before the switch. A potential explanation is a beneficial effect of faricimab’s dual inhibition mechanism, possibly combined with the overcoming of tachyphylaxis from the previous therapy. This finding aligns with published real-world studies, where 16 out of 17 series showed morphological improvements based on CST and PED height [4–20]. For example, Rush analyzed a cohort of 54 eyes and observed a decrease in CST from 395 to 350 µm after 52 weeks of follow-up [7]. Similarly, Aljundi et al. described a decrease in CST from 368 to 280 µm in a population of 33 eyes with refractory nAMD after a 52-week follow-up [20]. No data regarding changes in PED height and MV are reported in the available long-term studies.

Recently, OCT angiography (OCTA) has been employed to evaluate microvascular changes in previously treated patients with nAMD following a switch to faricimab, as reported by Brinkmann et al. [22]. It would be of particular interest to determine whether changes in OCTA parameters correlate with treatment durability, offering a potential biomarker for predicting the long-term efficacy of faricimab in maintaining disease control.

Despite the structural effect mentioned above, visual acuity in our sample remained unchanged during the follow-up. This discrepancy between morphological and functional improvement in switched patients has been previously observed in numerous studies [23]. A systematic review of 38 studies on patients with nAMD switching anti-VEGF therapies found that anatomical improvements were common, but visual acuity remained stable or only slightly improved [23]. This trend also appeared in real-world studies on faricimab in switched patients, where only a minority of studies (5/17) showed significant visual acuity improvements after switching to faricimab [4, 5, 7, 16, 17]. To explain this finding, Raimondi et al. suggested that long disease duration before switching might contribute to photoreceptor deterioration, limiting functional recovery [13]. In our study, patients had received a median of 27 injections before switching to faricimab, proving the significant chronicity of the disease in our cohort [13]. Another important factor to consider is the impact of retinal fibrosis, as highlighted by Romano et al. In nAMD switching cohorts, even when fluid reduction is successfully achieved, the presence of fibrosis could indeed limit the potential for visual acuity gains, contributing to the noted stability in visual acuity [24]. It can also be argued that BCVA might be an insufficiently sensitive parameter to monitor functional outcomes in long-standing AMD. Especially in switched patients with advanced nAMD or long disease duration, alternative functional outcome measures like low luminance visual acuity [25] or mean sensitivity in microperimetry [26] could be assessed to ameliorate sensitivity in evaluating functional improvements.

Regarding safety, our study found a low incidence of intraocular inflammation, with only one case (3%) developing anterior uveitis after IVT faricimab on two separate occasions. This finding is consistent with data from the pivotal trials [2] and other real-world studies, which reported a low rate (0.2–2.5%) of intraocular inflammatory adverse events [2, 4, 10, 27].

Additionally, one case (3%) of RPE tear was reported after two intravitreal injections. This complication is rare among nAMD switcher cohorts treated with faricimab, with only one case reported across 17 published studies [8]. Conversely, this complication appears to be more frequent in treatment-naïve cohorts, with higher incidences reported in pivotal trials and real-world studies: two cases each (2%) in TENAYA and LUCERNE, two cases (3.2%) by Mukai et al. [28], one case (4.3%) by Tanaka et al. [29], and one case (33%) by Stanga et al. [30] respectively. This difference in RPE tear incidence may be due to the lower PED height on average in switched populations versus treatment-naïve ones.

This study’s limitations include its single-center design and the relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Maximum fluid-free interval was selected as the preferred metric to evaluate treatment burden and drug durability owing to its objectivity and independence from variations in individual clinicians’ treatment decisions. Although this parameter may fluctuate during follow-up, we considered it a better indicator of disease control than treatment interval per se, as it is indeed common practice among retina specialists to tolerate fluid in selected cases and to extend intervals regardless of complete dryness on structural macular OCT. Moreover, in real-world studies, treatment intervals are often influenced by additional factors such as patient preferences and logistical constraints, leading to deviations from protocol-defined intervals despite established treatment criteria. The strengths of the study lie in the prospective design and the long-term follow-up, providing valuable insights into the real-world application of faricimab in switched patients with nAMD. Further studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate these findings.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that switching to faricimab significantly improved morphological outcomes and extended treatment intervals in patients with nAMD who were previously treated with aflibercept or ranibizumab with unsatisfactory results. The results confirm the findings of other real-world studies on switcher patients treated with faricimab, either with a larger sample size (n = 337) [4] or with a similar mean follow-up duration (52 weeks) [7, 20]. This study, featuring the longest follow-up available in the literature with a median duration of 72 weeks, adds to the existing body of evidence by providing robust support for faricimab’s long-term efficacy and safety. Despite the lack of significant visual acuity gains, the improved anatomical parameters and increased durability underscore faricimab’s potential to reduce treatment burden while maintaining a favorable safety profile.
