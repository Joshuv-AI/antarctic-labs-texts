Steindler flexorplasty in the management of elbow flexion deficits due to musculocutaneous nerve palsy: medium and long-term outcomes in two cases
Authors: Domenico Rodà, Giuseppe Bardellini, Federico Martinelli, Andrea Celli, Luigi Celli
Published: 2026

The musculocutaneous nerve (MCN) is essential for elbow flexion. MCN palsy induces elbow flexor paresis and involves significant functional impairment. The MCN originates from the C5 and C6 nerve roots and is primarily responsible for innervating the biceps brachii, brachialis, and coracobrachialis muscles. Associated C7 nerve root injury involves radial nerve innervation to the brachioradialis muscle and can result in complete elbow flexion palsy.9 Conditions giving rise to this lesion include traumatic or neonatal brachial plexus injuries, arthrogryposis, and prior poliomyelitis infection. Patients present with loss of active elbow flexion, reduced supination strength, and numbness or sensory loss on the lateral side of the forearm.4 Restoring elbow flexion is crucial for daily activities, hence quality of life. Steindler flexorplasty, first described by Steindler in 1918,20 is a surgical technique designed to restore elbow flexion in patients with MCN palsy.20 It involves detaching a local flap of the flexor–pronator mass from the medial epicondyle and transposing it proximal and anterior to the humerus to serve as an elbow flexor. We describe 2 patients who underwent Steindler flexorplasty to treat elbow flexor deficits due to different underlying conditions and report their mid- to long-term outcomes.

Case report 1

In 2014, a 29-year-old male patient presented with a right brachial plexus injury lesion due to trauma. He was treated in 2008 with a brachial plexus neurolysis without improvement of symptoms. He had signs of denervation of the MCN with an adducted shoulder, extended elbow, impaired in the abduction and external rotation of the shoulder, elbow-flexion deficit, atrophy of the deltoid, and biceps muscles. Electromyography study showed denervation with fibrillations and spontaneous activity of the biceps brachii, brachialis, deltoid, and infraspinatus muscles, confirming an upper brachial plexus trunk injury. He had normal activity of the wrist and finger extensors and pronators of the wrist and finger flexors. Pre-operatively, the patients presented a passive range of motion (ROM) of 10°-130° in flexion–extension and 90°-90° in pronation–supination. Measured biceps strength was M1,4 and elbow flexion was mainly achieved through brachioradialis activation. Pre-operative Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (DASH) score10 was 47.4 and Mayo Elbow Performance Score (MEPS)7 was 70. We used the DASH and MEPS scores to assess upper extremity function, particularly limitations in activities of daily living. We believe that biceps muscle paralysis significantly affects the function of the entire arm. Having functional flexor–pronator and brachioradialis muscles, a modified Steindler flexorplasty was planned.4 It was preferred to the triceps transfer to the biceps tendon to avoid any loss of extension strength, as the triceps was not involved in the injury.4 The patient was positioned supine with the arm on a table, and a tourniquet applied on the upper arm. A skin incision was made centered on the medial epicondyle, beginning 6-7 cm proximally and distally over the origin of the flexor–pronator mass insertion. The ulnar nerve (UN) was identified and dissected protecting its branches to the flexor carpi ulnaris and flexor digitorum profundus. The median nerve (MN) was identified and dissected distally protecting the branches to the pronator teres. Near the MN, the brachial artery and veins were identified (Fig. 1). The medial epicondyle was first predrilled with a 3.2-mm drill bit, taking care to preserve the flexor–pronator tendon insertion while separating it from the medial collateral ligament (MCL) insertion below the planned osteotomy line (Fig. 2). The size of the epicondylectomy was determined to maintain the integrity of the flexor–pronator insertion and the MCL. The medial epicondylectomy was then completed (Figs. 3 and 4). The anterior surface of the humerus was exposed detaching the brachialis (Fig. 5). The flexor–pronator group was elevated from the ulna taking care to preserve the elbow capsule and the anterior band of the MCL, then transferred proximally and anteriorly to the medial epicondyle just above the coronoid fossa carefully preserving the MN and UN branches (Fig. 6). The humeral cortex was drilled with a 3.2-mm drill bit, and the transferred fragment was fixed using a 3.5-mm compression screw with the elbow positioned at 120° of flexion (Fig. 7). The UN was transposed anteriorly to prevent contact with the osteotomized bone fragment. After wound closure, the elbow was immobilized in a posterior cast at 100° of flexion for 6 weeks. Then, the cast was removed and transitioned to a hinged elbow brace allowing full ROM. At 10 weeks post-operativly, the brace was discontinued. In 2025, after 10 years of follow-up (Fig. 8), the patient reported an improvement in DASH10 and MEPS7 score, 16.7 and 95 points, respectively. Elbow flexion strength improved to M4,4 and an active ROM of 20°-130° in flexion and extension with complete pronation and supination. Case report 2

In 2019, we evaluated a 40-year-old male patient with right biceps and brachialis paralysis due to poliomyelitis. The C5 and C6 nerve trunk were involved, as the patient had a stiff shoulder with deltoid, supraspinatus, and infraspinatus muscles deficit. Triceps, wrist extensors, and flexor–pronators were normal with intact strength. Electromyographic study confirmed the clinical findings. Passive elbow ROM was 0°-140° in flexion–extension and 90°-90° in pronation–supination. Active elbow flexion up to 30° was possible only in full pronation with wrist flexion and brachioradialis activity. Measured elbow flexion strength was M14 and pre-operative DASH10 and MEPS7 score were 59.2 and 80, respectively. A Steindler flexorplasty procedure was performed as described above, fixing the flexor–pronator mass proximally on the anterior surface of the humerus. Post-operative rehabilitation protocol was similar, with a long-arm cast for 6 weeks, transitioned to an elbow brace until the 10th week post-operative. After 5 years, at the last follow-up, the patient presented an active ROM of 20°-140° in flexion–extension and 70° to 90° in pronation–supination. Elbow flexion strength increased to M4 4 and post-operatively DASH score10 and MEPS7 improved to 19.2 and 95, respectively. The patient was satisfied with the results and regained adequate arm function, allowing independent performance of daily living activities.

Discussion

Steindler flexorplasty is a reliable surgical technique for restoring elbow flexion in patients with MCN palsy. Several studies have reported favorable outcomes with this procedure. After Steindler first description of his technique in 1918 in a poliomyelitic patient, a report in 1939 by the same author on 29 cases showed favorable functional outcomes in this population.20 Similarly, Nyholm17 demonstrated significant improvement in elbow flexion strength and ROM in 26 patients treated with Steindler flexorplasty, 22 of whom were polioparalysis of the elbow flexors. In his series, patients achieved a mean of 90° of elbow flexion with an extension deficit between 15° and 20° and a mean flexion strength of M3 to M4.17 He also found that patients who reached a better degree of elbow flexion had concomitant shoulder stabilization surgery, highlighting the value of a stable shoulder girdle for elbow flexion. Chen6 showed that modified Steindler flexorplasty is a simple, effective, and reliable procedure for restoring elbow flexion in patients with post-traumatic flail elbow, with all 8 patients achieving a stable and mobile elbow with marked improvement in upper limb function, even when associated with tendon transfer for wrist and finger extension and shoulder arthrodesis for flail shoulder treatment.6 Later, Brunelli et al3 used a modified Steindler flexorplasty effectively in 32 procedures in traumatic brachial plexus palsy, achieving very good results in 18 patients and good results in 8. Their technique involves avoiding the "Steindler effect" by carefully separating the flexor carpi ulnaris, the flexor carpi radialis, and the palmaris longus from the flexor digitorum superficialis, which remains in place avoiding flexion of the fingers and pronation. The authors stated that C5-C6 palsy remains the best indication for the procedure, although the length of the follow-up was not specified. Similarly, Monreal et al16 showed the reliability of Steindler flexorplasty in patients with brachial plexus palsy on 12 patients with concomitant wrist and fingers extensors weakness.16 They had very good and good results in 11 patients, associating wrist arthrodesis to the procedure. Another study13 highlighted the modified Steindler procedure efficacy in restoring elbow flexion in patients with brachial plexus injuries in whom neurotization was precluded due to the time elapsed since the injury. Post-operatively, the mean flexion isometric strength was 22% of the contralateral side, with the greatest strength at 45° of elbow flexion, and patient’s subjective scores were satisfying.13 Steindler flexorplasty provided reliable results even in children with obstetric brachial plexus palsy, further supporting its use in varied clinical scenarios.1 Eight of nine patients in the series achieved a mean active elbow flexion of 110° and a flexion contracture of 35°, with no pronation deformity recorded. Marinello et al14 reported a review of Steindler flexorplasty and presented a retrospective case series of 9 patients who underwent the procedure to restore elbow flexion. In their series, 8 of 9 patients achieved at least 90° of active antigravity (M3 or greater) elbow flexion. Two complications were reported, consisting of nonunion at the site of bone fixation, which required revision surgery. A major difference between the surgical technique described by Marinello et al14 and the technique used in the present study concerns the position of fixation of the osteotomized medial epicondyle on the anterior humeral cortex. Marinello et al fixed the medial epicondyle approximately 6 cm proximal to its native position, whereas fixation just proximal to the coronoid fossa is preferred in the present technique. Proximal fixation of the medial epicondyle may increase the flexion moment arm and improve elbow flexion strength; however, it may also increase tension on the flexor–pronator mass, potentially leading to increased traction on the MN, a higher risk of nonunion, fixation failure, and detachment of the bone fragment during forearm extension. For these reasons, a more distal fixation is favored. A second difference between the 2 techniques relates to management of the MN. Reduced tension on the flexor–pronator mass associated with a more distal bone insertion may decrease traction on the MN at the level of the 2 heads of the pronator teres. Consequently, extensive distal exposure and formal MN neurolysis are not routinely required. Comparative studies have evaluated Steindler flexorplasty against other surgical techniques such as triceps-to-biceps transposition, pectoralis major transposition, and latissimus dorsi transposition.4 Triceps-to-biceps transposition transfers the triceps muscle to the biceps tendon to restore elbow flexion.4 While effective, this procedure can lead to a weakened triceps muscle, compromising elbow extension. However, Ruhmann et al19 described the results of both triceps-to-biceps transfer and Steindler flexorplasty in 19 patients with post-traumatic brachial plexus lesion, and a slightly worse passive extension deficit remained after flexorplasty.19 In their algorithm, flexors transposition was reserved for paralysis of triceps muscle. Of note, patients who underwent triceps transfer to the biceps tendon had a loss of active extension, although the authors stated that this did not affect patient satisfaction. In our cases, we used flexors transfer even with functional triceps to avoid any loss of active elbow extension. Similarly, more recently, Rao et al18 used transfer of either the medial part of the conjoint triceps tendon or the long head of the triceps in 10 patients with brachial plexus injury after 12 months from injury, achieving grade 3-5 in 7.18 Nonetheless, a slight loss of triceps strength was found, with 7 patients having a Medical Research Council's scale4 grade 3 and 3 retaining a grade 2. While this technique can retain a degree of elbow extension and an independent control of elbow flexion, a certain degree of active extension loss has to be expected and can be an important factor to consider in patients with normal shoulder abduction. Moreover, flexorplasty can be a more straightforward solution both surgically and during the post-operative rehabilitation. The latissimus dorsi muscle has been efficiently used for transfer in restoring elbow flexion with restoration of 75% of elbow flexion between 60° and 140°.5,18 Eggers et al8 found that compared with Steindler flexorplasty, latissimus dorsi transfer (LD) transfer provided more powerful elbow flexion and a greater ROM at 12 months, including an 82% loss of strength in the flexor–pronators with only 56.4% of LD strength loss.8 Our patients experienced no loss of strength. For this reason, and due to its simplicity, reliability, and ability to spare the function of other major muscles, Steindler flexorplasty is our preferred technique. In addition, though effective as a unipolar as well as a bipolar procedure, LD transfer has some drawbacks, including poor cosmesis, risk of neurovascular structure injury, fibrofatty degeneration, and vascular pedicle thrombosis. Pre-operative evaluation, eg, differentiating the contribution of the teres major, is also difficult, both clinically and using nerve conduction studies or electrical stimulation.5 Satisfactory elbow flexion restoration has been described with pectoralis major transfer in post-traumatic brachial plexus lesions, with functional results ranging from 54% to 86%.12 However, the technique for partial/complete and unipolar/bipolar transfer is demanding and often needs extending by fascia or tendon graft to augment muscle length. It is generally employed in patients where the LD, flexor–pronator, or triceps are not suitable for transfer and has shown similar results to Steindler flexorplasty in terms of ROM, strength, and clinical scores.2,12 Nerve transfer procedures are also available to treat brachial plexus injury. Handog et al11 reported that nerve transfer and modified Steindler procedures both yielded satisfactory outcomes in terms of elbow flexion restoration, achieving similar ROM and DASH scores, as all flexorplasty patients achieved M3 or more in flexion strength compared with 74% in the nerve transfer group.11 Notably, the interval from injury to surgery was less than 12 months for the flexorplasty group and a median of 5.6 months for the nerve transfer group, confirming that nerve transfer yields the best results when performed within six months. The authors11 concluded that flexorplasty is effective in chronic conditions or as a salvage procedure after failed nerve reconstruction, requiring a shorter rehabilitation and involving faster flexion recovery. The interval from injury to surgery has also been explored.4 The highest rates of recovery ≥ M3 and the best pain relief and quality of life scores have been reported when surgery performed within 6 or, better within 3 months.15 Tendon transfer procedures, such as flexorplasty, can be considered when the interval exceeds 12 months, as in our cases. The choice of technique should be tailored to the individual patient, considering factors such as extent of the nerve injury, availability of donor muscles, and functional requirements. Steindler flexorplasty is particularly indicated for patients with an intact flexor–pronator mass and sufficient passive elbow flexion but weak or paralyzed biceps and brachialis muscles. Although we have described only 2 cases, their long follow-up demonstrates that the technique is reliable. Moreover, their different underlying conditions show that good results can be achieved in different scenarios. At a time when other tendon transfer techniques2,3,6,8,12,16, 17, 18, 19 are being popularized, these data demonstrate that Steindler flexorplasty20 retains an important role. Steindler flexorplasty is one of the most time-tested procedures and remains a powerful reconstructive option for the restoration of elbow flexion, ensuring durable and reliable outcomes and low morbidity while involving a relatively short learning curve.

Conclusion

Steindler flexorplasty is a well-established, dependable, and effective surgical technique for elbow flexion restoration in patients with MCN palsy of different origins, including brachial plexus injury and poliomyelitis. Several other surgical options are available to manage these patients, such as nerve transfer to the biceps, brachialis muscle transfer (unipolar or bipolar), and triceps-to-biceps transfer. Procedure selection should be based on the pattern of injury and the functional musculature, if extant. Besides the low morbidity and relatively short learning curve associated with Steindler flexorplasty, further advantages over other muscle transfer techniques2,3,6,8,12,16, 17, 18, 19 include preservation of triceps function, simplicity, and a less invasive approach, making it a useful option for active elbow flexion restoration. Future studies should further explore its long-term outcomes and potential refinements, to contribute to the continuous improvement of surgical interventions for nerve-related functional impairment.
