Assessment of PET drug adverse events in the U.S. since the implementation of 21 CFR part 212
Authors: Marianna Dakanali, Henry F. VanBrocklin, Steven S. Zigler, Peter J. H. Scott
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMultiple new Positron Emission Tomography (PET) radiopharmaceuticals (RPhs) have recently been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA). Despite the generally accepted safety profile of PET RPhs supported by millions of doses administered per year, the microdoses used in most injections, and the low incidence of adverse events (AEs), there appear to be elevated concerns from regulatory authorities regarding the safety of PET RPhs in recent years. There have been prior reports on the prevalence of AEs from RPhs, but most of them are outdated and do not reflect the current state-of-the-art and best practices. To address the gap, this retrospective study assesses risks associated with PET RPhs by reviewing AEs reported to the FDA Adverse Events Reporting System (FAERS) since the implementation of the PET good manufacturing practice (GMP) regulations in 21 CFR 212 in 2012. Data for AEs reported for approved PET RPhs were collected from the FAERS database. Data were collected during December 2025 for AEs reported between 2012 and 2024. FDA approved PET RPhs were searched using all known names for each drug. For each RPh, the following tables were downloaded from the database: (a) case count by received year; (b) cases by reaction; and (c) listing of cases. Tables downloaded for each radiopharmaceutical were combined, and duplicated entries removed. AE reports were then categorized based on the manufacturing regulatory oversight and, for the purposes of this report, we focused on AEs reported for 10 PET RPhs manufactured under 21 CFR 212.ResultsAlmost 24 million adverse event reports for all drug products were transmitted to FDA between 2012 and 2024. Of these, 932 (or 0.0039%) were AEs submitted to FAERS for FDA-approved PET RPhs manufactured in the US. Of these AEs, 451 were reported for PET RPhs manufactured under 21 CFR 212. This represents approximately 7.0 AEs per 105 RPh injections performed in 2024 for all PET RPhs or 4.3/105 for PET RPhs manufactured under the PET GMP regulations. At least one adverse reaction to the RPh was reported in 237 of the 451 cases recorded, and two-thirds of these (157, 66.2%) were characterized as non-serious.ConclusionsThe FAERS data confirm that PET RPhs can be generally recognized as safe and effective. The extremely low numbers of AEs for PET RPhs manufactured according to 21 CFR 212 and the lack of correlation between manufacturing issues and safety AEs overall support a low risk profile for this class of drugs. Moreover, these data do not appear to justify significant changes to the existing regulations, or more stringent regulation of PET drug manufacture beyond those currently in place.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41181-026-00482-8.

Introduction

Radiopharmaceuticals (RPhs) are drugs that carry a radioactive isotope and are widely used in the diagnosis as well as, increasingly, the treatment of diseases (Dhoundiyal et al. 2024; Brugarolas et al. 2020). Diagnostic radiopharmaceuticals have been used for years in nuclear medicine and enable visualization of molecular processes such as biomarker concentration, metabolism, blood flow, and oxygen consumption (Garg et al. 2025; Pantel et al. 2024). For example, since the 1970s, clinical studies using RPhs tagged with a positron-emitting radionuclide have found use in Positron Emission Tomography (PET) imaging, where they are used to diagnose disease and monitor patient response to therapy. PET is also increasingly used in drug development, where it can be used to measure receptor occupancy of investigational drugs, as well as to confirm target engagement and identify sites of off-target binding that could potentially cause unwanted side effects (Donnelly 2020). More recently, PET RPhs are also utilized in theranostics, where they are paired with a corresponding radiotherapeutic (Burkett et al. 2023; Deandreis et al. 2026; Karim et al. 2024). Reflecting these applications, PET RPhs have gained increased attention in the past decade, and multiple new PET drugs or new formulations, 18 since 2010, have been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2026a).

An important aspect of assessing the ongoing safety and efficacy of any investigational or approved drug, including PET RPhs, is routine surveillance of adverse events (AEs). According to the FDA, AEs are defined as “any untoward medical occurrence associated with the use of a drug in humans, whether or not considered drug related,” (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2026b) and AEs can include Adverse Drug Reactions (ADRs), drug misuse, drug withdrawal, over- or under-dose of the drug, or any failure of expected pharmacological action. An ADR is “any adverse event caused by a drug. Adverse reactions are a subset of all suspected adverse reactions where there is reason to conclude that the drug caused the event” (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2026b). Unlike AEs, where the relationship with the drug is not proven, ADRs are directly linked to the pharmaceutical.

AEs associated with PET RPhs are extremely rare, mainly due to the low pharmacologic and radioactive doses administered to patients (Silberstein 2001; Silberstein, 1998; Silindir-Gunay and Ozolmez 2024; Schreuder et al. 2019). Most PET RPhs, for example, are administered as sub-pharmacologic and toxicologic microdoses frequently 100× or even 1000× below no-observed-adverse-effect levels (NOAELs). Microdose is defined by the FDA as ≤ 100 µg for small molecules or ≤ 30 nmol for protein products (Food and Drug Administration Center for Drug Evaluation and Research, 2018). Despite the low incidence of AEs, there appear to be elevated concerns from the FDA regarding the safety of these drugs in recent years (Bunning et al. 2022, 2025). For example, there has been a steady push by agency officials during review of PET RPh submissions to reduce the mass dose further, below the established microdosing guidelines. Aseptic processes and microbial control during the manufacturing of PET RPhs have also received increased focus during FDA inspections and have been a main topic of discussion at recent workshops organized by the FDA and the PET drug manufacturing community (Bunning et al. 2022, 2025). During these workshops, FDA officials have referred to PET RPhs as “high risk” and have noted that the PET good manufacturing practice (GMP) regulations (21 CFR part 212) and related guidance documents are currently under revision, but the revisions have not appeared in the Federal Register or otherwise been made public to date.

Given these concerns, this analysis was undertaken to better understand the safety profile of PET RPhs both in our capacity as U.S.-based PET drug manufacturers and leaders at the Coalition of PET Drug Manufacturers, as an independent evaluation of the FDA concerns, and to identify any unforeseen issues from the patient perspective. There have been several prior reports in the literature that aim to assess the prevalence of AEs associated with radiopharmaceuticals (Silindir-Gunay and Ozolmez 2024; Schreuder et al. 2019; Silberstein 2001, 2014; Silberstein 1998; Silberstein and Ryan 1996; Mititelu et al. 2025; Karpuz and Aksoyalp 2026). Reviews have been published summarizing data from case studies in the literature (Schreuder et al. 2021) or from public databases such as the British Nuclear Medicine Society online database (Kennedy-Dixon et al. 2017), the national pharmacovigilance database of France (Meher et al. 2021), or data gathered by professional societies (Silberstein and Ryan, 1996; Hesslewood et al. 1997). Most recent published reports focus on therapeutic RPhs (Karpuz and Aksoyalp 2026) or present a narrative review of literature reported AEs for both diagnostic and therapeutic RPhs (Mititelu et al. 2025). The number of reports specifically focused on PET RPhs in the U.S. is very small and outdated (Silberstein 2001; Silberstein, 1998). Thus, the purpose of this work was to provide an updated assessment of PET RPhs that includes the many new agents currently in use, most of which were not FDA-approved when these earlier reviews were compiled.

The FDA Adverse Events Reporting System (FAERS) was established in 1969 and underwent a major revision in 1997 to increase reporting capabilities. The FAERS is an open-access database that collects AEs and other pharmaceutical errors reported by patients, healthcare providers, and drug manufacturers (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2026c). Although the number of reports in the FAERS database may underestimate the true frequency of AEs, the data represent a real-world collection of incidents related to the safety of drugs, and the FAERS has been widely used in pharmacovigilance studies (Wu et al. 2025; Tong et al. 2025; Shen et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2026). Recently, the FDA launched a new unified platform for analyzing adverse event reports named FDA Adverse Event Monitoring System (AEMS). This new system aims to combine databases used for different classes of drugs and allow easier access to data.

Our retrospective study aims to assess the risk associated with PET RPhs by reviewing adverse event reports in the FAERS since the implementation of 21 CFR part 212 in 2012 (Schwarz et al. 2014), and to identify an association between this risk and the increased regulatory concerns regarding the safety of PET RPhs.

Methods

Data for AEs reported to the FDA for approved PET RPhs were collected using FAERS. Data can be reported to the FAERS from the U.S. or other countries, and it is updated quarterly. For our analysis, data were collected during December 2025 for global AEs reported between 2012 and 2024. FDA-approved PET RPhs were searched using all known names for each drug, both generic and brand names. For each radiopharmaceutical, the following tables were downloaded from the database: (a) case count by received year; (b) cases by reaction; and (c) listing of cases. The latter provide, amongst other data, information on the type of adverse event, the date of the event, the date reported to the FDA, the date of the last update the FDA received for this event, and basic patient information such as sex, age, and weight. Furthermore, information regarding the country where the event occurred, the manufacturer, and the reporter type can also be extracted from these tables.

Tables downloaded for each radiopharmaceutical were combined, and duplicate entries were removed. AE reports were then categorized based on manufacturing compliance oversight. Radiopharmaceuticals labeled with either Ga-68 or Cu-64 are commonly prepared using FDA-approved kits. These kits are manufactured according to traditional GMP regulations (21 CFR part 211) and the radiopharmaceuticals are then prepared for use under the practice of pharmacy or medicine in nuclear pharmacies or nuclear medicine departments. Similarly, Rb-82 generators are manufactured under 21 CFR part 211 and typically used under the practice of medicine. All other PET RPhs (see Table 1) are manufactured according to the PET GMP regulations (21 CFR part 212) in academic or commercial manufacturing facilities. AEs for PET RPhs manufactured under part 212 were first separated into those reported in the U.S. or other countries, or those in which the country of the event was not specified. Following a conservative approach, if the country of manufacturing could not be identified, we hypothesized that those events occurred in the U.S. Subsequently, the reported AEs were segregated according to the following categories: (1) safety related, (2) efficacy related, (3) drug administration errors, (4) manufacturing errors, and (5) “other” which includes AEs not qualifying for the four previous categories. A limited number of cases reporting AEs belonging to either the safety or efficacy categories, were also reporting an AE belonging to one of the other categories. In such cases they were counted only once in our analysis as a safety or efficacy event that we considered higher risk for the patients. Finally, the AEs were separated based on the suspected active ingredient for the adverse reaction reported and further separated into three categories: (a) the adverse reaction was attributed to the PET RPh, (b) the adverse reaction was attributed to a different drug, or (c) the suspected drug was not specified, or more than one drug was suspected.

Results

Collectively, more than 23.9 million adverse event reports for all drug products were submitted to the FAERS between 2012 and 2024. A total of 1,090 reports were submitted for all FDA-approved PET RPhs. Of those, 7 cases were duplicates, bringing the total number of reports to 1,083, representing 0.0045% of AEs reported to the FDA between 2012 and 2024. Examination of the country where the event occurred revealed that 888 (82.0%) occurred in the U.S., 134 (12.4%) in other countries, and that the remaining 61 cases (5.6%) were not specified. Closer examination of the case data revealed that for 17 of the reports with unspecified country, the manufacturer’s control number belonged to a facility outside the US. This leaves only 44 cases (4.1%) where the country was not specified. Following a conservative approach, for the remainder of the analysis, we assumed all these cases occurred in the US, bringing the total of cases for further analysis to 932.

Of these 932 cases, 451 (48.4%) were associated with PET RPhs manufactured under 21 CFR part 212, and 348 (37.3%) were related to RPhs prepared under the practice of pharmacy or medicine from kits. These kits are manufactured following traditional GMP requirements under 21 CFR 211. The remaining 133 reports (14.3%) were submitted for Rb-82 generators manufactured under 21 CFR part 211 and used under the practice of medicine (Table 1).

Discussion

In the past 25 years there has been a drastic increase in the number of PET scans performed in the U.S., and the current estimate is more than 3,000,000 scans per year (IMV 2024). One of the factors contributing to this increase has been the approval of new PET RPhs by the FDA for myocardial perfusion imaging (Flurpiridaz F-18), imaging of Alzheimer’s disease such as Florbetaben F-18, Florbetapir F-18, Flutemetamol F-18 and Flortaucipir F-18, as well as for imaging of specific types of cancer such as Fluoroestradiol F-18 for breast cancer, and Choline C-11, Fluciclovine F-18, Flotufolastat F-18 and Piflufolastat F-18 for prostate cancer. Gozetotide Ga-68 for prostate cancer and DOTATATE Ga-68 for neuroendocrine tumors have also shown a growth in utilization because of theranostics and their use in tandem with the corresponding Pluvicto and Lutathera radiotherapies, respectively. Despite the increased number of PET scans, adverse events related to PET RPhs remain rare mainly due to the very small amount of mass administered, low radiation dose, and the low frequency of administration compared to non-radioactive drugs. Although reporting of AEs relies on healthcare providers, manufacturers, and patients, and can be under-reported, studies in the literature for AEs in the U.S. and other countries estimate a frequency of 1.63/106 (Schreuder et al. 2019), or 1.2 – 3.4/105 (Silberstein 2001, 2014; Silberstein, 1998; Silberstein and Ryan, 1996) for all diagnostic radiopharmaceuticals, both PET and non-PET.

Fludeoxyglucose F-18 has been used since the 1970s and first garnered FDA approval in 1994. Based on experience gained during this approval, the FDA Modernization Act of 1997 (FDAMA) required the FDA to create specific GMP regulations for PET RPhs. These GMPs were officially implemented in 2012 as 21 CFR part 212, and designed to ensure the safety and efficacy of PET RPhs while accommodating their unique characteristics such as the short half-life, the emission of high-energy gamma radiation, and need for distributed manufacturing in commercial facilities and academic medical centers. Since the initial approval of all PET manufacturing facilities in 2015, the FDA has slowly increased compliance expectations and demands through inspections (Bunning et al. 2022, 2025). Consequently, several workshops and meetings have been organized between the FDA and PET manufacturing stakeholders to discuss the evolution of compliance standards and concerns from PET manufacturers regarding inspection and review inconsistencies (Scott et al. 2026). During these workshops, the FDA often described PET RPhs as “high risk” products but has not elucidated the basis for this risk assessment (Bunning et al. 2022, 2025), and multiple times FDA officials have referred to the FAERS, stating that there is an increase in reported AEs for RPhs. Further, the Agency stated that 21 CFR part 212 will be revised to presumably address concerns regarding the safety of PET RPhs (Bunning et al. 2022, 2025). As of this report, the revisions to the PET GMP regulations have not appeared in the Federal Register or otherwise made public.

The FAERS Public Dashboard is an open-access database that collects ADR, performance concerns or pharmaceutical manufacturing errors reported by patients, providers and/or drug manufacturers (U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2026c). There are several limitations related to this dashboard, including: (a) underreporting of events since this is voluntary, or duplicate reports since the same event can be individually reported by the physician, the patient, or the facility; (b) categorization of the AEs is left to the party reporting the AE, and (c) lack of verification of the information reported to the FDA. Even though there are inevitably gaps in the reporting of such incidents, mainly due to a lack of familiarity with this reporting system, it provides a representative view of incidents related to the safety of drugs (including radiopharmaceuticals) and has been widely used in pharmacovigilance studies (Karpuz and Aksoyalp 2026; Wu et al. 2025; Tong et al. 2025; Shen et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2026).

With this study, we aimed to shed light on the frequency of AEs reported to the U.S. FDA for PET RPhs, to identify any correlation between the frequency of AEs and the safety profile of these drugs, as well as the perceived need to update the GMP regulations. For the purposes of this study we focused on AEs reported, since 2012 when the new 21 CFR part 212 was implemented, for FDA-approved PET RPhs (listed in Table 1) manufactured in cyclotron facilities. PET RPhs labeled with either Ga-68 or Cu-64 were not included in the most comprehensive analysis since they are usually prepared from FDA-approved kits under the practice of medicine or pharmacy. Rb-82 was also not included because it is produced from a generator manufactured under 21 CFR part 211 and administered directly to patients without further processing, in accordance with the practice of medicine.

Between January 2012 and 2024, more than 23.9 million AEs were transmitted to the FAERS database. These included cases for pharmaceuticals manufactured in the U.S. or abroad, reported by physicians, consumers, or manufacturers. Of these, we found 1,090 cases reported for FDA-approved PET RPhs. Seven (7) cases were duplicates resulting in a final count of 1,083 cases, representing 0.0045% of all reported cases to the FAERS. No AEs were reported for Choline C-11, Fluorodopa F-18 or Flurpiridaz F-18. There was 1 report for Flortaucipir F-18, but it was removed from further analysis since the AE occurred in Great Britain.

As mentioned previously, AEs can be reported to the FAERS regardless of whether the event occurred in the U.S. or a different country. Our first focus was to identify the incidents reported for PET RPhs manufactured in the U.S. Examination of the reported cases revealed that 888 AE cases were reported for PET RPhs manufactured in the U.S., while 134 cases were for PET RPhs manufactured in a different country. Although for 61 cases the country for the event was not specified, the drug manufacturer’s control number indicated that 17 reports involved drugs manufactured outside the U.S. Following a conservative approach, we decided to include the cases where the country of the event was not specified in our analysis. Combining these parameters, we concluded that 932 AEs were reported for events occurred in the US representing 86% of the cases.

Approximately half of the 932 cases (451) were reported for 10 PET RPhs manufactured under 21 CFR part 212 (Table 1), representing 48.4% of all reports for PET RPhs or 0.0019% of the reports for all drug products that were submitted to the FAERS between 2012 and 2024. The other half (481) were reported for 3 PET drugs prepared under practice of pharmacy, and for Rb-82 generators manufactured according to 21 CFR part 211 and used under the practice of medicine. The higher rate of reported AEs per RPh for these drugs can be attributed to either the administration of therapeutic radiopharmaceuticals before or after the PET scans, the formulation of the drug, or administration errors. More specifically, the RPhs Gozetotide Ga-68 and DOTATATE Ga-68 are used for confirming eligibility for treatment with Pluvicto or Lutathera respectively, and for therapy response assessment. Administration of the therapeutic RPhs close to the PET scans can be the cause of the reported AEs instead of the PET RPhs. Furthermore, both drugs have formulations with lower pH and higher salt content than a typical F-18 or C-11 labeled RPh, especially DOTATATE Ga-68 with pH 3.2 – 3.8. In 106 of the 160 reported cases for Gozetotide Ga-68, injection site pain and burning were reported as the ADR. For 76 of the 181 cases for DOTATATE Ga-68 nausea was reported as the ADR that is more usually associated with the administration of amino acids during the Lutathera treatment. Finally, for the Rb-82, the most common AE reported was the use of expired generators or a problem with the product preparation, that can be due to misuse of the generator.

The 451 AE cases reported for RPhs manufactured under 21 CFR part 212 were categorized based on the type of reported event according to the following categories: safety, efficacy, drug administration errors, and manufacturing errors. Reports that could not be assigned to any of these categories were characterized as “other” and were included in a separate group. As shown in Table 2, 237 cases were reported as safety related with at least one ADR. One third (80) of these cases were characterized as serious. The most common reported ADRs were flushing (50), nausea (31), dyspnoea (25), hyperhidrosis (25), dizziness (23) and back pain (20). There were also 4 cases where exposure to radiation was reported. One case filed for Florbetapir F-18 reported suspected transmission of an infectious agent via the product and was characterized as serious.

Notably, 15 cases reported death as the adverse event or the outcome of the event. One case of death was reported for Ammonia N-13 without providing other information regarding any observed ADR. The AE case included a long list of concomitant medication the patient was receiving at the time of the event. Two cases of death were reported for Fluciclovine F-18 with one of them being reported for a 44-year-old female, this indicates that the drug was probably used off label or the wrong drug was included in the report, since Fluciclovine F-18 is FDA approved for prostate cancer imaging. The remaining 12 cases were reported for Fludeoxyglucose F-18. For six of these cases more than one drug was the suspected PAI, while for three other cases Keytruda is listed as the suspected product name (pembrolizumab, a PD-1 inhibitor used in cancer immunotherapy). For another case, although no further information is provided for the cause of the death or the suspected PAI besides the RPh, the reported ADR was a contrast agent reaction.

Most of the cases (80) categorized as efficacy related were reported by healthcare providers. The most common reported events were “scan abnormal” (79) followed by “drug ineffective” (46). Both events were reported for 31 of the cases. For 20 other cases, the AE reported was “therapeutic response unexpected”. No further information was provided in the database regarding these events, and it is not clear what constitutes an abnormal scan or what it means for a diagnostic radiopharmaceutical to have an unexpected or less than expected therapeutic response. We speculate that these events were due to poor scan quality or unexpected diagnostic data.

We found 25 cases that reported an error during the administration of the drug with all of those characterized as non-serious. AEs in this category include extravasations (6), administration of the wrong product (4) or the wrong patient (4) or the wrong dose (9).

Further, 36 reports were related to manufacturing errors. These events included: dispensing errors (17), product preparation errors (10), and wrong label on the product (8). For 3 of these a sterility issue was also reported. For all these cases, no adverse drug reaction was reported, and all events were characterized as non-serious. Although accurately characterized as manufacturing errors, some of these events represent handling errors and are not related to product attributes such as purity, quality, and strength.

Finally, 34 cases could not match any of the above categories and were included in a different group. Of those, 19 reported either a false positive (5) or a false negative (14) investigation. Incidents that may require an investigation can include but are not limited to an abnormal scan, administration of the wrong radiopharmaceutical or dose, or a manufacturing or sterility problem. No further information was provided about the nature of the investigation for the majority of these reports (16), and only for 3 of these cases an abnormality of the scan or the effectiveness of the drug was also reported. Additionally in this category we included 13 cases for Florbetapir F-18 that reported amyloid related imaging abnormalities such as microhemorrhages and hemosiderin deposits. Lastly, there were 2 cases reporting “no adverse event” with no further information on why they were reported and included in FAERS.

All 451 cases reported for RPhs manufactured under 21 CFR 212 were categorized based on the suspected PAI. As mentioned, for 57 cases, at least one more drug besides the RPh was suspected to cause the adverse event. More specifically, for the 237 cases reporting an ADR and categorized as safety related, 42 had at least one more suspected PAI. Most common drugs listed as suspected to have caused the adverse reactions included chemotherapy drugs (e.g., doxorubicin, etoposide), antibodies (e.g., obinutuzumab, glofitamab), immunotherapy medications (e.g., pembrolizumab), and anti-amyloid agents (e.g., aducanumab) among others.

Combining all the different parameters used for our analysis, we found that over the past 12 years, there were 195 cases submitted to the FAERS database for PET radiopharmaceuticals manufactured in the U.S. and unspecified countries under 21 CFR part 212 that caused a safety-related adverse event for which the PET drug was the suspected product active ingredient. This represents 0.0008% of all reported AEs between 2012 and 2024, or 3.3 AEs/105 RPh administrations.

Despite the marked increase of PET scans performed each year, which has doubled over the past 12 years to ca. 3 million annually, the number of reported AEs remains very low and is estimated to be less than 7.0 AEs per 105 scans in 2024 for all PET RPhs, and 4.3/105 AEs for RPhs manufactured under the PET GMP regulations (Table 3). Dividing the rate of AEs/105 scans with the number of approved RPhs each year, it is estimated that there are 0.37 reported AEs per 100,000 administered doses for each approved RPh in 2024. This represents “real world” data for PET radiopharmaceuticals obtained only from the FAERS database, unlike former reports that used data reported to the authors from the manufacturers (Silberstein 2001; Silberstein, 1998), data from reports in the literature (Schreuder et al. 2019), or data for both PET and non-PET RPhs (Silberstein and Ryan, 1996; Silberstein 2014). Notably, our findings are within the same order of magnitude as those reported by Silberstein, 2.3 AEs per 105 (Silberstein 2001) and 2.1–2.3 AEs per 105 administered doses for PET or all PET and non-PET RPhs (Silberstein 2014), respectively, although our retrospective study design based on FAERS is completely different from these prospective studies. In our opinion, this indicates that AEs are consistent over time, reflecting the inherent safety and low risk of PET RPhs.

Based on our analysis, we cannot conclusively connect reported ADRs to PET RPh sterility or production failures. As shown in Table 2, there is no clear trend connecting the reported safety AEs to those related to manufacturing, distribution or sterility issues. The reported AEs for the radiopharmaceuticals manufactured under GMP requirements for PET drugs over the last 10 – 12 years (451) appear to be equal to those manufactured according to 21 CFR part 211 or prepared under the practice of pharmacy or medicine (481), and thus imply that the PET GMP regulations do not increase the risk of PET radiopharmaceuticals.

Lastly, it is estimated that there are approximately 4050 approved drugs in the FDA orange book and an additional 2055 biologics in the purple book (see Supporting Information for a list downloaded from FDA’s website). Assuming that approximately the same number of drugs were approved at the end of 2024, there were 2,110,318 AEs reported to FDA for those 6105 drugs, which corresponds to an average of 346 AE reports per drug. This is approximately 30 – 40 times more than the average for PET radiopharmaceuticals which is 216 AEs for all 19 PET RPhs (i.e. 12 AEs per drug), or 132 AEs for 14 PET RPhs manufactured under 21 CFR 212 (i.e. 9 AEs per drug) for 2024.

Conclusions

Despite the increased concerns regarding the safety of PET RPhs from regulators in recent years, our analysis of the AEs reported to the FAERS public dashboard revealed that the number of AEs remains extremely low (less than 7.0 AEs per 105 RPh administrations per year) compared to the annual number of doses administered (estimated at about 3,000,000) (IMV 2024). The findings confirm our assertion that PET radiopharmaceuticals can be generally recognized as a safe and effective drug class for which very low levels of adverse events are observed year to year, and which present low risk to patients. It is also evident that the manufacturing of PET RPhs under 21 CFR part 212 does not increase the frequency of AEs reported compared to part 211 or the practice of pharmacy. In addition, reports filed about sterility or production issues are not associated with any adverse events or ADRs. Taken together, it is challenging to conclusively link reported AEs to PET RPh sterility or production failures; thus, in our opinion, these data should not be used to justify any revision of manufacturing regulations. Finally, this analysis shows the importance of adverse event data in establishing the safety profile of PET RPhs. It is our hope that this study inspires the PET community to re-establish prospective studies and data collection around AEs to corroborate the data collected in the FAERS.
