MMP3C: an in-silico framework to depict cancer metabolic plasticity using gene expression profiles
Authors: Xingyu Chen, Min Deng, Zihan Wang, Chen Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic plasticity enables cancer cells to meet divergent demands for tumorigenesis, metastasis and drug resistance. Landscape analysis of tumor metabolic plasticity spanning different cancer types, in particular, metabolic crosstalk within cell subpopulations, remains scarce. Therefore, we proposed a new in-silico framework, termed as MMP3C (Modeling Metabolic Plasticity by Pathway Pairwise Comparison), to depict tumor metabolic plasticity based on transcriptome data. Next, we performed an extensive metabo-plastic analysis of over 6000 tumors comprising 13 cancer types. The metabolic plasticity within distinct cell subpopulations, particularly interplay with tumor microenvironment, were explored at single-cell resolution. Ultimately, the metabo-plastic events were screened out for multiple clinical applications via machine learning methods. The pilot research indicated that 6 out of 13 cancer types exhibited signs of the Warburg effect, implying its high reliability and robustness. Across 13 cancer types, high metabolic organized heterogeneity was found, and four metabo-plastic subtypes were determined, which link to distinct immune and metabolism patterns impacting prognosis. Moreover, MMP3C analysis of approximately 60 000 single cells of eight breast cancer patients unveiled several metabo-plastic events correlated to tumorigenesis, metastasis and immunosuppression. Notably, the metabolic features screened out by MMP3C are potential biomarkers for diagnosis, tumor classification and prognosis. MMP3C is a practical cross-platform tool to capture tumor metabolic plasticity, and our study unveiled a core set of metabo-plastic pairs among diverse cancer types, which provides bases toward improving response and overcoming resistance in cancer therapy.

INTRODUCTION

Metabolic reprogramming (MR) is considered the hallmark of malignancy and fulfills specific demands for energy, biomass and redox homeostasis [1]. MR has its roots in the observations of the Warburg effect that tumor cells preferred glycolysis for glucose metabolism rather than oxidized phosphorylation (OXPHOS) even in an oxygen-rich environment [2]. The Warburg effect could maximize biomass, fast provide energy and inhibit the function of tumor-infiltrating immune cells to promote tumor cell growth and proliferation [3, 4]. Many discoveries have been made in the field of MR since Warburg’s initial observations, including glutamine metabolism, macromolecular synthesis and redox homeostasis [5]. Among them, glutamine is the most consumed amino acid in cancer and used as the building block for proteins, lipids and nucleotides for tumor cell survival and proliferation [6]. Additionally, some studies have found MR in stromal and immune cells within the tumor microenvironment (TME). The reverse Warburg effect exhibited that tumor cells could fuel the metabolites generated by cancer-related fibroblasts and other tumor cells [7]. Additionally, MR in the TME can alter the immune phenotype of immune cells toward immunosuppression [4].

Currently, ‘wet lab’ biological methods, i.e. MS-based metabolomics, based on the composition of concentration of metabolites and computational biology methods, i.e. single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) [8] and flux balance analysis (FBA) [9] based on bulk or single-cell RNA-seq data could identify the aberrant metabolic flux for diseases, e.g. cancer [10] and Alzheimer’s disease [11]. Yet, the predictive power of the current approaches is limited due to many methodological issues. First, ‘wet lab’ biological methods cover only a small proportion of aberrant metabolisms correlated to tumorigenesis [12], even if many metabolic features have been identified. Second, majority of metabolic implications of tumors traditionally captured by high-throughput computational methods unilaterally consider dysregulation of one metabolism impacting tumorigenesis. These suggest that it lacks a solid bioinformatic basis to comprehensively uncover tumor metabolic switch between two metabloic pathways (MPs), herein referred to as ‘metabolic plasticity’, e.g. Warburg effect. Hence, we proposed a novel in-silico framework named MMP3C (Modeling Metabolic Plasticity by Pathway Pairwise Comparison) to depict it.

In brief, for the first time, a thorough investigation was conducted to unveil how metabolic plasticity communicated with tumor cells and the subsequent effects on TME and clinical outcomes in cancer. Preliminary inquisition using MMP3C on the Warburg effect across 13 cancer types demonstrates its availability and dependability. Thus, an extensive investigation of 3486 metabo-plastic pairs derived from 84 MPs within The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database [13] was conducted using bulk RNA-seq data from The Cancer Genome Atlas Program (TCGA) database [14], aiming at disclosure of potential metabolism plasticity implicated in tumorigenesis. Meanwhile, we verified MMP3C is applicable for single-cell RNA-seq data. The results suggested glucose-related and glutamine pathways exhibited increased fluxes compared with the ATP generation pathway. Furthermore, the MMP3C-derived metabolic features showed its great predictive potential in tumor diagnosis, staging and prognosis. In summary, our method provides an approach to broaden the horizon for insights into tumor metabolism, leading to a rational, optimized therapeutic strategy in cancer.

RESULTS

An in-silico framework depicting the tumor metabolic plasticity

The core concept of depicting metabolic plasticity is to decipher whether two specific MPs exhibit inverse state (a typical example refers to the Warburg effect, as illustrated in Figure 2A) between two conditions (herein referred as to carcinoma and adjacent normal tissues). We hypothesized that the expression of genes in a specific pathway could serve as a proxy for its metabolic activity, and the context of a large sample set (available in the TCGA pan-cancer cohort) coupled with strict criteria for hypotheses testing would provide an effective framework (Figure 1, see Methods for details) for inferring metabolic plasticity states between two MPs. To this end, we proposed the MMP3C framework for identifying the plastic metabolic pathway pair (PMPP) events, which integrates gene expression and prior knowledge of the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network of pathways to infer metabolic plasticity. In general, PPI network analysis was initially employed via the PageRank algorithm [15] toward assigning importance scores of genes within the specific MPs. Then, the activities of those MPs were assessed by taking the dot product between the gene expression value and the corresponding page rank score (Figure 1B), referred to as MP scores. Each score was then standardized into a value between 0 and 1, aiming at ensuring comparability between two MPs. We measure the relative difference of two MPs A and B within a sample as follows: DAB = MPA – MPB. Notably, the absolute value of DAB larger than 0.1 is considered for further analysis. For DAB < −0.1, the relative activity of MPA is lower than that of MPB, while DAB > 0.1, and vice versa. Based on this, the difference of each MP pair between carcinoma and adjacent tissue was evaluated using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test, and the MP pair exhibits significant difference representing PMPP events (Figure 1C).

To verify the reliability and robustness of MMP3C, a well-known PMPP event, the Warburg effect [16], was initially inquired in MMP3C in pan-cancer. Of note, we focused on 13 major cancer types with sufficient adjacent sample sizes (≥24), and then propensity score matching (PSM) [17], which is an important and widely used statistical approach [18], was employed to eliminate the bias induced by other factors (e.g. age, race, stage and tumor purity, Figure 1A). For each cancer type, samples with the similar propensity score were retained for MMP3C analysis. The results showed that 6 out of 13 cancer types exhibited a significant Warburg effect (Figure 2B). The remaining cancer types exhibiting unobvious signs of the Warburg effect might be interpreted by the fact that the tumor heterogeneity may lead to high metabolic heterogeneity [19, 20]. Furthermore, to validate the rationale of the MMP3C framework, the comparison between MMP3C and other existing counterparts such as ssGSEA and relative gene expression [10] was also performed. It suggested that the discovery of metabolic switch including glucose metabolism, fatty acid, glutathione and folate detected by MMP3C was validated by the computing counterparts (Supplementary Methods and Figure S1). These analyses indicated that MMP3C exhibited a decent discrimination power of metabolic plasticity.

The landscape of metabolic plasticity in pan-cancer

Next, all 3486 MP pairs (yielded by pairwise pairing between 84 KEGG MPs, see Supplementary Methods, Table S1) were subjected to MMP3C toward global PMPP events detection across 13 cancer types. The results showed that distinct cancer type exhibited a diverse number of significant PMPP events (Figure 2C). This suggested that metabolic plasticity might be characterized by organized heterogeneity [16]. Nonetheless, many significant PMPP events were detected in some specific cancers (Figure 2C and D). For instance, a PMPP event composed of beta-alanine metabolism and glutathione metabolism was consistently observed in BRCA, LUSC and THCA (Figure 2C and D). Several PMPP events consisting of the fatty acid biosynthesis pathway all exhibited the increase flux of fatty acid biosynthesis in KIRC, KIRP, LUAD, THCA and UCEC (Figure 2D). In LIHC, the TCA cycle significantly increased compared with one carbon pool by folate, beta-alanine metabolism and pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis. A relative higher activity of the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) than steroid hormone biosynthesis in tumor was observed in BRCA and PRAD. Incidentally, PPP versus folate biosynthesis increased in COAD and THCA; by contrast, it deceased in LIHC. These results revealed the presence of organized heterogeneity in metabolic plasticity and the increased flux of macromolecule synthesis promoting the tumor progression. Thereafter, to further identify consensus dysregulated MPs in the pan-cancer cohort, the top 200 significant PMPP events for each cancer type ordered by the adjusted P-value were selected to construct a metabo-plastic network (Figure 2E; Table S2). Subsequent network analysis via Gephi [21] disclosed many critical altered MPs (hub nodes in network) in the tumorigenesis, including the TCA cycle, nitrogen metabolism and tryptophan biosynthesis, implying their importance in tumorigenesis (Figure 2E).

Dynamic progressive roadmap of metabolic plasticity in pan-cancer

To illustrate the dynamic progressive roadmap of metabolic plasticity in pan-cancer, 482 significant PMPP signatures (referred to as the PMPP events screened out through previous global MMP3C exploration, adjusted P-value <10−8 as cutoff; Supplementary Methods) were subjected to an unsupervised clustering analysis on 6161 tumor patients across 13 cancer types using the non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) algorithm (Figure S2) [22]. The result showed that all the patients were stratified into four subgroups, referred to here as PMPP1–PMPP4 (with 2627, 2274, 769 and 491 patients). These four resultant tumor patient clusters displayed the apparent discrepancies (Figure 3A–G). Specifically, we observed that the fraction of different cancers was uniquely enriched among those four PMPP subgroups (Figure 3A and B). Besides, several significantly altered PMPP events among four PMPP subgroups were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (adjusted P < 0.05; Figure 3C, Figure S3 and Table S3). These four resultant tumor patient clusters can be characterized using a progressive activity profile of metabolic pathways, hallmark pathways and immune infiltration score, as well as many driver genes [23], immune checkpoint genes (ICGs) [24], epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) genes [25], etc. (Figure 3D, E and G). Remarkably, our analyses revealed that glucose metabolic pathways including glycolysis, pyruvate metabolism, citrate cycle (TCA cycle) and PPP descend in four PMPP stages (Figure 3E). The pan-cancer survival analysis depicted that those four PMPP subgroups are significantly different in overall survival (OS) rates (log-rank test P < 0.0001; Figure 3F), including KIRP and THCA (Figure S4). Additionally, four PMPP subgroups exhibit distinct immune cell populations (Figure 3G).

Multi-omics alterations link pan-cancer subgroups with PMPP signatures

Next, we seek to investigate the associations between PMPP subgroups and multi-omics alterations. To this end, comparative analyses of genomic variation, gene expression, DNA methylation and alternative polyadenylation (APA) profiles among four PMPP subgroups were performed, respectively. The results showed that the distribution of point mutations and copy number variations (CNVs) exhibits a unique pattern among the PMPP subgroups (Figure 4A and B). Figure 4A illustrates the distribution of point mutations in genomic regions among the PMPP subgroups. In Figure 4B, the CNV landscape of each PMPP subgroup highlighted the heterogeneity within the subgroups. Particularly, PMPP4 subgroups had higher tumor mutation burden (TMB) frequency, CNA burden and microsatellite instability (MSI) frequency (Figure 4C) and elevated glucose metabolic fluxes (Figure 3E) when compared with other PMPP subgroups. These findings suggested that genomic alterations might be associated with the distinct metabolic phenotypes of the four PMPP subgroups.

Next, we found that many tumor-associated genes exhibited a discrepant pattern spanning PMPP subgroups, including tumor driver genes, metabolic genes and proliferating genes (Figure 4D). Furthermore, many genes exhibit distinct patterns of correlation with the quantity of CD4+ Tcm cells and Th17 cells. CD4+ Tcm cells could repair or perform immune regulation [26]; by contrast, Th17 cells could promote tumor growth and angiogenesis [27] in the TME. This result implies the closed correlation between genomic alterations of PMPP subgroups and the TME (Figure 4D).

The metabolic plasticity of TME at single-cell resolution

Mounting evidence suggested that metabolic plasticity also occurs in immune cells, altering the immune phenotype of immune cells toward immunosuppression and promoting tumorigenesis [4]. Thus, to comprehensively illustrate the metabo-plastic architecture in the TME, we used MMP3C to analyze a 10x Genomics single-cell RNA-seq data, which contain four paired primary invasive breast tumors and metastatic axillary lymph nodes. The main reasons that we choose BRCA are as follows: (1) a significant Warburg effect (chi-square test P = 2.129155E-14) was observed in our previous MMP3C framework; (2) altered metabolism is a vital feature of BRCA, in particular, involved in treatment resistance [28]. After filtering low-quality cells, we established the scRNA-seq atlas consisting of 61 767 qualified cells (Supplementary Methods). The Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) clustering mainly generated epithelial cells, B cells, T cells, fibroblasts, macrophages and endothelial cells (Figure 5A and B; Figure S5).

Metabolic plasticity of intertumoral malignant tumor cells

Given that breast malignant cells originate from epithelial cells, then, CNV analysis was performed to distinguish malignant cells from non-malignant epithelial cells using the ‘infercnv’ R package [29]. The CNV level of all epithelial cells were evaluated and significantly clustered into three types compared with reference cells (Figure S6; see Methods for details). Among them, epithelial cells were obviously divided into malignant and non-malignant epithelial cells (Figure 5C). Subsequently, the MMP3C framework was applied for inferring PMPP events between those two types of epithelial cells. In line with the results of our previous MMP3C analysis in bulk RNA-seq data (Figure 2D), the Warburg effect was found at the epithelial cell population (Figure 5E). Additionally, we observed that pyruvate metabolism and pyrimidine metabolism has increased flux, yet valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis displayed decreased flux.

To illustrate the metabolic plasticity of various cells in the TME-implicated tumor metastasis, MMP3C was utilized to capture PMPP events in multiple cell types of the TME between primary breast tumors and the metastatic lymph node. As a result, the Warburg effect was observed in the metastatic immune cells (Figure 5D). The activity of glucose-related MPs increased and OXPHOS reversely decreased flux in B cells and T cells (Figure 5D). Although the flux of OXPHOS and glycolysis didn’t alter in macrophages, the OXPHOS in macrophages exhibited decreased flux compared with the TCA cycle (Figure 5D). In fibroblasts, the TCA cycle and PPP significantly increased compared with OXPHOS (Figure 5D). These results suggest that macromolecular production of immune cells decreases in tumor metastasis, and it might influence the normal function of immune cells. The enhancement of glucose-related pathways in fibroblasts might indicate the signs of the reversed Warburg effect, which could generate metabolites to fuel tumor cells.

Landscape of MR of T cells in TME

T cells play a pivotal role in immune response and show that they are highly correlated with metabolic alteration [4]. To investigate how metabolic plasticity affects T cells’ function, thereafter, all 24 925 T cells were subclustered again (Figure S7; Supplementary Methods). Six subclusters were identified, consisting of central memory T cells (Tcm), effector memory T cells (Tem), resident memory cells (Trm), helper T cells, naïve T cells and regulatory T cells (Treg) (Figure 5F and G). Then, to dissect the PMPP events in the evolutionary dynamics of T cell lineages, trajectory analysis of T cell subclusters was constructed, which generated a two-branch trajectory representing differentiation process of naïve T cells into either memory T cells or Treg cells (Figure 5G). Naïve T cells were obviously located at the starting point of the evolving trajectory curve. The memory T cells and Tregs were enriched in the endpoint of activated and exhausted pseudotime branches, respectively (Figure 5G). Subsequent comparative analysis of PMPP events demonstrated that the Warburg effect performed distinct differences in two cell fates (Figure 5H). During the process of cell fate 2, glycolysis and its downstream pathways were enhanced, while OXPHOS activity was decreased (Figure 5H) in Tregs. In contrast, memory T cells exhibited a reversal of the Warburg effect (Figure 5H). This phenomenon was consistently observed in other PMPP events, including fatty acid degradation–arginine and proline metabolism and pyruvate metabolism–arginine biosynthesis (Figures S8 and S9). These results suggested that exhausted T cells might be coupled with tumor cells in glucose, fatty acid, etc., potentially competing with activated T cells for these nutrients within the TME, thus promoting immune evasion.

Leveraging PMPP signatures for modeling the diagnosis, stages and survival of tumor patients

The previous study suggested that MR events could infer tumor occurrence [16]. Consequently, the PMPP signature score was leveraged to construct the tumor diagnosis model. A deep learning method ‘Deep autoencoder’ is employed to screen-out 23 key features from 428 candidate signatures (Table 1 and Figure 6A). The pan-cancer data were randomly compartmentalized into train and test cohorts by a ratio of 7:3. Then, multiple machine learning (ML) methods including random forest (RF), multi-layered perceptron (MLP), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), logistic regression, and support vector machine (SVM) were utilized for training the tumor diagnosis model, respectively. These ML methods performed good predictive ability except logistic regression (Figure 6B). Specifically, the tumor diagnosis model based on XGBoost, referred to as the PMPP-XGB model, was found to achieve superior performance for tumor diagnosis than that of other ML methods (Figure 6B). Receiver operating characteristic curves (ROCs) results demonstrated that the PMPP-XGB model had outstanding performance on these five external datasets (Figure 3C and Table S4).

Furthermore, to endow our PMPP subgrouping with clinical application, we utilized those 23 PMPP signatures identified from ‘Deep autoencoder’ to predict the hierarchical tumor metabolic stages divided by NMF clustering. To this end, five classical ML methods consisting of RF, XGBoost, SVM, MLP and multi-logistic were used for training, all of which exhibit satisfactory performance for the tumor metabolic stage prediction (Figure 6D and E). The ROC curves indicated that the multi-logistic method had better sensitivity and specificity of identification of tumor metabolic stages (Figure 6D and E). The 482 PMPP events displayed a continuous spectrum of transcriptional states in patients among the different PMPP subgroups (Figure 6F). Moreover, the predicted PMPP subgroups also exhibited a distinct difference on the several important metabolic pathways including fatty acid degradation, glycolysis and OXPHOS (Figure 6G). We subsequently evaluated clinical outcomes of the four PMPP subgroups and found that the OS in each PMPP subgroup exhibited statistically different (Figure 6H). We next inspected the potential of those 428 PMPP signatures for predicting prognosis. The PMPP signature LASSO cox model was built and had good predicting performance (AUC > 0.7; Figure S10) in the train and test data. We also compared the LASSO cox model with other ML methods (RF, XGBoost, SVM, MLP and logistic regression). The ROC curves suggested that the LASSO cox model outperformed other state-of-the-art models (Table S5). In conclusion, our analysis demonstrated that the PMPP signatures have great potential to predict the diagnosis, tumor classification and prognosis for cancer.

DISCUSSION

Accumulating evidence suggests that metabolic dysfunction of tumor cells is a key feature of malignancy. This dysfunction provides malignant cells with a selective advantage in the TME during the process of tumorigenesis and metastasis [30]. Therefore, understanding the metabolic dysfunction within the TME within the same cancer type or different cancer types is key to depict the tumor metabolic mechanism and heterogeneity. Additionally, it could overcome the barrier of tumor diagnosis and treatment in clinic. In the present study, we developed a new in-silico framework based on the context of a large sample set and strict criteria for hypotheses testing to characterize the metabolic switch between two MPs using bulk and single-cell gene expression profiles in the tumor progression. The results suggest that tumor metabolic plasticity can exhibit high heterogeneity within one specific cancer type and among different cancer types. This heterogeneity may result from a variety of factors, including the originated tissues, intracellular and extracellular factors such as mutations, methylation, nutrient availability and the interplay of the tumor with components of its TME.

There are several interesting findings around the tumor metabolic plasticity in our pan-cancer analysis. In bulk tumor samples, 6 out of 13 cancer types significantly exhibited the Warburg effect. Among them, BRCA, THCA and PRAD also exhibited a Warburg-like effect between PPP and steroid hormone biosynthesis. Steroid hormone biosynthesis is the important metabolic pathway to generate sex hormone [31]. The reduced fluxes of steroid hormone biosynthesis might switch to macromolecular biosynthesis. The TCA cycle, PPP and pyruvate increased in three tumors at various extents (Figure 2D). In terms of specific mechanisms, it may not be the same as the Warburg effect, but it still reflects the heterogeneity of tumor MR. The shift toward anabolic metabolism allows the majority of carbon surplus to be allocated to biosynthetic pathways. Therefore, glucose-6-phosphate, a byproduct of glycolysis, can be transferred to the pentose phosphate pathway to support the production of ribose for nucleotide biosynthesis [32]. It should be noted that we have selected the top six cancers exhibiting the Warburg effect through strict testing, which included all three cancers (BRCA, THCA and PRAD) mentioned above. The increase of the Warburg effect is likely to provide more substrate sources for the pentose phosphate pathway, thus greatly increasing the likelihood of Warburg effect metabolic pairs formed between the pentose phosphate pathway and other metabolic pathways. The decrease of steroid hormone biosynthesis in BRCA, THCA and PRAD reflects that tumors heterogeneity can still be classified based on heterogeneity. Previous studies have shown that cancers such as BRCA and PRAD are hormone dependent but have not conducted bioinformatics analysis from the perspective of metabolic plasticity [33, 34]. The metabolic pairs formed with the pentose phosphate pathway appropriately reflect the emergence of a Warburg-like effect.

Our research shows that BRCA has the most pronounced Warburg-like effect. At the single-cell resolution, the glycolysis and downstream pathways also increased compared with OXPHOS. We also found that the flux of valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis pathway enhanced in the malignant cells, which could fuel as the material of protein production (Figure 4E). These results suggested that glucose metabolism switches into macromolecular biosynthesis in tumorigenesis. Meanwhile, in the TME, Treg cells also exhibited the Warburg effect (Figure 4H). The Warburg effect entailed malignant cells and Treg cells to metabolizing glucose rapidly and generating lactate, which provided competing advantages with immune activated cells. In addition, some studies reported that lactate could inhibit the function of immune activated cells [4]. Besides, metastatic fibroblasts exhibited an increased Warburg effect, which coupled with the reversed Warburg effect that tumor cells fuel the lactate generated from fibroblasts and thus promote immune evasion.

Glucose, glutamine and fatty acid are the main fuels that power cells [35]. Glutamine dysfunction has been observed in BRCA, LUSC, PRAD and THCA. BRCA, PRAD and THCA also exhibited increased flux of the TCA cycle (Figure 2C). Glutamine could generate glutamate, which is then converted to α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) and fed into the TCA cycle [36]. Glutamine, which fuels the TCA cycle, replaces glucose to provide energy in tumor cells avoiding the OXPHOS generating reactive oxygen species (ROS). Meanwhile, the FA oxidation pathway increased in BRCA, but it decreased in THCA (Figure 2D). This revealed that BRCA fueled FA and glutamine instead of glucose to provide energy for cell proliferation. However, THCA fueled glutamine instead of glucose. The FA biosynthesis pathway decreased in KIRC, KIRP, LUAD, THCA and UCEC but increased in LIHC and LUSC.

However, several limitations exist and should be noted. First, although metabolic gene expression can be to some, if not a large, extent predictive of metabolic fluxes and metabolic concentrations [37, 38], metabolic gene expression levels are not completely equivalent to metabolic fluxes or metabolite abundance. Further molecular experiments should be conducted to investigate the metabolic dysfunction of candidates identified using the MMP3C framework. Second, five ML methods were used in our model, but the ensemble learning method might be an alternative approach to achieve more precise accuracy when compared with a sole ML method. Third, combining with other omics datasets could determine the complex interplay among metabolic pathways. Additionally, we applied the MMP3C framework to analyze 13 cancer types from the TCGA database due to sample size limitation. The MMP3C framework in our research should be tested and validated in the prospective studies with more cancer types.

To summarize, this paper offers a global landscape of metabolic plasticity in the pan-cancer cohort at bulk and single-cell levels. Tumors originated from various tissues exhibited high tissue heterogeneity at the bulk level. At the single-cell resolution, comprehensive characterization of the metabolic plasticity and activity of malignant cells is performed. In addition, the metabolic plasticity of different types of cells from TME are also identified in the process of metastasis. Notably, the metabo-plastic events screened out by MMP3C are potential biomarkers for diagnosis, tumor classification and prognosis. With the rapid advancement of omics techniques and the increasing accumulation of data across various tumor types and patients, we are highly optimistic that a comprehensive understanding of the metabolic switch in each tumor type will emerge in the future.

METHODS

The construction of MMP3C in-silico framework

The pan-cancer bulk RNA-seq datasets (6161 tumor and 643 adjacent normal samples) including 13 cancer types were downloaded from the UCSC Xena Database. The single-cell RNA-seq data (GSE225600) was downloaded from gene expression omnibus (GEO) database [39]. All the data with details within this study can be found in Supplementary Methods.

Filtering patients using propensity score matching method

At first, we utilized the propensity score matching (PSM) based on the ‘MatchIt’ [17] V4.5.0 R package to select tumor and adjacent normal groups with enough sample number (≥24; Figure 1A). Then, samples that had consistent propensity score would match for the further downstream analysis. Consequently, we selected 13 cancer types from the TCGA database due to the sample number (Table S5). We next normalized the pan-cancer transcriptome data as log2(TPM + 1) to reduce the effect of confound factors such as gene length and sequence depth. Single-cell RNA-seq data were normalized by the ‘LogNormalize’ method. Key Points

In this paper, an in-silico framework to depict the switch between two metabolic pathways in cancer was developed, which coupled a large sample set from the pan-cancer cohort with hypothesis testing.

Across various cancer types, high metabolic organized heterogeneity was found in the pan-cancer cohort, and four metabolic subtypes were determined, linking to distinct immune and metabolism patterns impacting prognosis. Additionally, the cell type–specific metabolic switch in the tumor microenvironment has also been identified.
