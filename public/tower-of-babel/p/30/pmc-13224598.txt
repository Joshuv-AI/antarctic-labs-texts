Clinical and radiographic outcomes of three calcium silicate-based cements in primary tooth pulpotomy: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Rabia Yucel, Fatih Sengul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdvancements in technology have enabled successful outcomes to be achieved in primary tooth pulpotomy treatments using various materials. This randomized clinical trial aimed to compare the 12-month clinical and radiographic success rates of three calcium silicate-based cements with different physical forms in primary tooth pulpotomy.MethodsA total of 112 primary second molars from 70 children were block-randomized into three groups: ProRoot® MTA (powder–liquid form, n = 38), NeoPutty® (ready-to-use putty form, n = 36), and TheraCal PT® (light-cured, resin-modified calcium silicate, n = 38). Teeth underwent pulpotomy followed by stainless steel crown restoration. Clinical and radiographic success rates were evaluated at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months. Statistical analysis was performed at a significance level of 0.05.ResultsAt the 12-month follow-up, the clinical and radiographic success rates were 97.2% and 91.7% for NeoPutty® MTA, 97.4% and 84.2% for ProRoot® MTA, and 91.9% and 67.6% for TheraCal PT®, respectively. Radiographically, TheraCal PT® demonstrated significantly lower success compared to the other materials (p = 0.026).ConclusionAlthough the light-cured TheraCal PT® demonstrated clinical outcomes comparable to those of MTA-based materials, its inferior radiographic performance raises concerns regarding its long-term effectiveness.Trial registrationThe study was registered retrospectively at ClinicalTrials.gov (https://clinicaltrials.gov) with the ID NCT06874062 (2025-03-03).Keywords: Calcium silicate, Deciduous tooth, Mineral trioxide aggregate, Neoputty, Proroot MTA, Pulpotomy, TheraCal

Background

Dental caries is one of the most prevalent chronic diseases of childhood. In asymptomatic primary molars with carious pulp exposure, pulpotomy is a commonly preferred treatment option in clinical practice. The aim of the treatment is to maintain the vitality of the radicular pulp, control pain and inflammation, and preserve the tooth until its natural exfoliation [1]. The choice of an appropriate material and technique is critical for the success of the procedure. In addition to biocompatibility and clinical effectiveness, factors such as ease of application and cost should be considered when selection a pulpotomy material [2]. Although numerous studies have evaluated the success of various pulpotomy materials and techniques, the quest for an ideal material remains ongoing.

Formocresol has long been used as the primary agent in pulpotomy procedures. However, due to its potential toxic and mutagenic properties, the European Academy of Paediatric Dentistry recommends using alternatives such as ferric sulfate or mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) [3]. The main concern regarding ferric sulfate is the high incidence of internal resorption observed radiographically, which may lead to early tooth loss [4]. Conversely, MTA, first developed by Torabinejad in 1993 as a root canal filling material, is a calcium silicate-based cement with various applications in dentistry, including pulpotomy treatment of primary teeth [5]. It consists of fine hydrophilic particles containing tricalcium silicate, tricalcium aluminate, tricalcium oxide, and silicate oxide, with bismuth oxide added to provide radiopacity [6]. The main advantages of MTA include its biocompatibility, bactericidal properties (pH: 12.5), ability to induce cementum-like tissue formation, osteoblastic adhesion, and bone regeneration [7]. Moreover, its sealing ability, mineralization potential, and dentinogenic and osteogenic capabilities make it a preferred material in various clinical applications [8]. However, its disadvantages include a long setting time, potential for tooth discoloration, high cost, handling difficulties, and concerns regarding trace heavy metal impurities such as arsenic [8–10].

Powder-liquid materials such as MTA require technical precision during mixing and handling; improper mixing may result in excessive material waste, and the preparation process is time-consuming [11]. To overcome the drawbacks of MTA, manufacturers have developed a series of bioactive endodontic cements that possess similar properties but are designed to minimize its disadvantages [12]. Pre-mixed bioceramic materials are available as syringeable pastes or condensable putties [11]. These materials offer advantages such as homogeneous consistency, reduced material waste, and quick, accurate application [11].

NeoPutty® is an MTA-based pulpotomy material approved by the FDA in 2020. It contains tantalum oxide as a radiopacifying agent [1]. Supplied as a pre-mixed putty, NeoPutty® allows for immediate application. This feature eliminates the material waste associated with the mixing process and significantly shortens chairside treatment time [13]. In vitro studies have demonstrated that NeoPutty® exhibits biocompatibility and mineralization-promoting properties comparable to other MTA formulations [14, 15]; however, there is a scarcity of randomized clinical trials evaluating its long-term clinical performance in primary teeth.

Introduced in 2011 as a light-curable tricalcium silicate, TheraCal LC® was presented as an alternative to MTA [16]. Although it has demonstrated higher calcium ion release and lower solubility than MTA, TheraCal LC® is not recommended for direct contact with the pulp tissue due to its reported cytotoxicity [17]. To address these limitations, TheraCal PT® was introduced in 2019 as a dual-cured, resin-modified calcium silicate material specifically designed for pulpotomy procedures. Supplied as a pre-mixed, syringeable paste, it offers a simplified application process compared to traditional powder-liquid systems. Nevertheless, previous in vitro studies have yielded conflicting results regarding its biocompatibility due to its resin content, necessitating further clinical investigation [18, 19].

ProRoot® MTA is widely considered the gold standard for pulpotomy treatments; however, its manual mixing requirement and handling difficulties have led to the development of these newer formulations. Although ProRoot® MTA, NeoPutty®, and TheraCal PT® all belong to the calcium silicate family, they differ significantly in their delivery systems and physical forms. ProRoot® MTA represents the traditional powder-liquid system requiring manual mixing; NeoPutty® is a pre-mixed, moldable putty; and TheraCal PT® is a pre-mixed, syringeable paste. While in vitro studies have evaluated the biological properties of these materials individually, there is a lack of clinical data comparing these distinct forms side-by-side. To the best of our knowledge, no study has yet compared the clinical and radiographic success of these three materials simultaneously in a randomized clinical trial. Therefore, this study aims to bridge this gap by evaluating the 12-month outcomes of these three bioactive materials in primary molar pulpotomies.

This study aimed to test the null hypothesis that there is no significant difference in the 12-month clinical and radiographic success rates of three calcium silicate-based materials with different physical forms – traditional powder-liquid (ProRoot® MTA), putty (NeoPutty® MTA), and light-cured syringeable paste (TheraCal PT®) – when used for pulpotomy in primary molars.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted with the approval of the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Ataturk University Faculty of Medicine (Date: 03.2023 / Session No: 02 / Decision No: 04). The study was registered as a randomized, three-arm parallel-group trial in a public trial registry (www.clinicaltrials.gov, #NCT06874062) and has been reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines for randomized trials [20]. Detailed information regarding the treatment and follow-up procedures was provided to the parents or legal guardians of all participants, and written informed consent was obtained from them prior to enrollment in the study.

Design of the study

The study was designed as a three-arm parallel-group trial. In the study, teeth requiring treatment were assigned using block randomization via R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). To ensure balanced distribution within subjects, when multiple teeth from the same participant were included, a specific allocation rule was applied: each subsequent tooth was treated with a different material following the randomization sequence.

Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. The envelopes were opened by a dental assistant only after the participant was seated and anesthesia was administered, ensuring that the operator was blinded to the allocation sequence until the moment of treatment.

Patient selection, examinations, and treatments were performed by a single pediatric dentistry specialist. Due to the distinct physical characteristics of the materials, operator blinding was not feasible. However, the patients, their parents/legal guardians, and the independent evaluator assessing the clinical and radiographic success of the tooth during follow-up visits were blinded to the materials used.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.2 software (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) based on the primary outcome of clinical and radiographic success rates. A z-test (Proportions: Difference between two independent proportions) was applied.

The calculation was based on reference success rates derived from a similar previous study [21], which reported success rates of 100% and 60%. To be conservative, we adopted anticipated success rates of 90% and 60%. With a significance level α of 0.05, and a power (1-β) of 0.80, the calculation indicated a minimum of 32 teeth per group. However, since multiple teeth from single participants were included, the sample size was adjusted using the Design Effect (DE) to account for the clustering effect (within-subject correlation). The DE was calculated using the formula DE = 1 + (m-1) x ICC, where m is the average number of teeth per participant (112/70 = 1.6) and the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was estimated at 0.1. The resulting DE was 1.06.

The required sample size was inflated by this factor (32 × 1.06 ≈ 34). To account for potential dropouts, the target was set at 36 teeth per group. Ultimately, a total of 112 teeth (36 in the NeoPutty group, 38 in the ProRoot MTA group, and 38 in the TheraCal PT group) obtained from 70 children were included in the study, satisfying the power requirements adjusted for the clustering effect. The unit of analysis was defined as the tooth.

Participants and settings

This study included systemically healthy children (ASA I or II) aged between 4 and 9 years who presented for dental treatment at the Department of Pediatric Dentistry, Faculty of Dentistry, Ataturk University, between June and October 2023. To ensure standardization of the study sample, only primary second molars were included. This approach was chosen to minimize anatomical variations in pulp chamber configuration and to provide a uniform model for evaluating the materials under similar masticatory forces. Treatment procedures

All procedures performed prior to the application of the pulpotomy material were standardized across the three groups. First, following topical anesthesia, local anesthesia was administered to the treatment area using an Ultracain D-S ® ampoule (Aventis Pharma, Istanbul, Turkey). Under rubber dam isolation, carious enamel was removed using a diamond round bur (#21, Dimei, Piranha, China) in a high-speed air rotor with water cooling, and carious dentin was excavated using a tungsten carbide bur (#18, Wave Dental, China). Next, the access cavity was enlarged, and the roof of the pulp chamber was removed using a fissure bur (Dimei, Piranha, China). The coronal pulp tissue was then removed using a sterile tungsten carbide bur and a sharp excavator. Subsequently, hemostasis was assessed by applying a saline-moistened sterile cotton pellet to the pulp stumps for up to 5 min; teeth that failed to achieve complete hemostasis within this period were excluded. Following successful hemostasis, the teeth were randomly assigned to one of the following groups:

Group 1 (NeoPutty® MTA): Ready-to-use, putty-form NeoPutty® MTA was dispensed from the syringe and placed over the canal orifices and pulpal floor to a thickness of approximately 2–3 mm using a plastic instrument, followed by gentle adaptation with a moist cotton pellet (Fig. 1).

Group 2 (ProRoot® MTA): ProRoot® MTA powder and liquid were mixed on a sterile glass slab with a spatula for approximately one minute, following the manufacturer’s instructions, until a homogeneous consistency was obtained. The mixture was carried to the pulp chamber and placed over the canal orifices to a thickness of approximately 2–3 mm (Fig. 1).

The mixture was carried to the pulp chamber and placed over the canal orifices to a thickness of approximately 2–3 mm (Fig. 1).

Group 3 (TheraCal PT®): The material was applied directly from the dual-barrel syringe onto the canal orifices and pulpal floor. A layer of approximately 2–3 mm thickness was placed. Care was taken to allow the material to flow over the pulp stumps naturally without probing or forcing it into the root canals. Finally, in all groups, the pulp chamber was covered with glass ionomer cement (EQUIA Forte®, GC, Tokyo, Japan), and the teeth were permanently restored with an SSC (3 M ESPE ®, Seefeld, Germany) in the same visit.

Follow-ups and outcome evaluations

Patients were clinically evaluated at the 1-month follow-up and both clinically and radiographically at the 3-, 6-, and 12-month intervals. For each tooth, periapical radiographs were obtained at baseline (preoperative) for diagnosis and immediately following the permanent restoration (postoperative) to serve as a reference. All subsequent follow-up radiographs were compared against these baseline images to ensure a consistent longitudinal evaluation.

The primary outcome of this study was the radiographic success of the pulpotomy treatments at the 12-month follow-up, specifically the absence of internal/external resorption and periapical/inter-radicular pathology. The secondary outcomes included clinical success (absence of pain, swelling, or mobility) and the comparative performance of the three materials (NeoPutty®, ProRoot® MTA, and TheraCal PT®) at the 3- and 6-month intermediate intervals.

To ensure consistency of radiographic assessments, a random subset of 40 radiographs representing different follow-up periods was selected for reliability testing. These images were reassessed three weeks later by two calibrated evaluators specialized in pediatric dentistry. Inter- and intra-examiner agreement were analyzed using the Kappa statistics with values of 0.863 and 0.96, respectively, indicating excellent agreement. In cases where the evaluators disagreed on the clinical or radiographic outcomes, the cases were re-evaluated together to reach a consensus. If a consensus could not be reached, a third experienced pediatric dentist was consulted to make the final decision.

Clinical and radiographic outcomes were evaluated based on specific success criteria. Clinical success was defined as the absence of spontaneous, provoked, or nocturnal pain; sensitivity to percussion or palpation; pathological mobility; and abscess or fistula. Radiographic success was defined as the maintenance of a normal periodontal ligament space and the absence of internal or external root resorption, and periapical or inter-radicular lesions. For the purpose of statistical analysis, the outcomes were recorded as dichotomous variables (success vs. failure). Any case not meeting these criteria was recorded as a failure.

Statistical data analysis

For the comparison of three or more independent groups with normal distribution, a one-way ANOVA test was used; when normality was not satisfied, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied. To examine the relationships between categorical variables, Pearson’s Chi-square test was used when the sample size assumption (expected value > 5) was met; otherwise, Fisher’s Exact test was employed. To account for the clustering effect caused by treating multiple teeth within the same subject, clinical and radiographic failure rates were analyzed using Generalized Estimating Equations, with age and gender included in the model as covariates. Inter- and intra-examiner agreement was assessed using Cohen’s Kappa statistics. Kaplan-Meier analysis was performed to calculate survival times. To compare survival times between groups, the Log-rank test was used to weight observations equally across the entire follow-up period, Breslow (Generalized Wilcoxon) analysis was used to compare early survival, and Tarone-Ware analysis was applied to compare mid-term survival. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics® version 25 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA), with the significance level set at 0.05.

Discussion

Pulpotomy is a vital pulp therapy used in primary teeth, aiming to preserve the vitality and function of the tooth by removing infected coronal pulp tissue. The success of the procedure is largely influenced by the biological properties of the applied material. In this randomized clinical study, the clinical and radiographic success of three different pulpotomy materials—NeoPutty® MTA, ProRoot® MTA, and TheraCal PT®—was evaluated over a 12-month period. While all materials exhibited comparable and high clinical success rates, a notable divergence was observed in their radiographic performance. Specifically, TheraCal PT® demonstrated a significantly lower radiographic success rate compared to the MTA-based groups. Our findings indicate that the risk of radiographic failure was substantially higher for TheraCal PT® than for NeoPutty® MTA, suggesting that the formulation of the material plays a critical role in maintaining long-term periapical and furcal health. Consequently, the null hypothesis—that no significant difference exists among the materials regarding clinical and radiographic success—was rejected.

Several meta-analyses on pulpotomy materials in primary teeth have reported MTA as the most effective option in terms of clinical and radiographic outcomes [22, 23]. The clinical and radiographic success rates of pulpotomy procedures in primary teeth using ProRoot® MTA have been reported to range from 82% to 98% in previous studies [24–26]. In the present study, ProRoot® MTA revealed comparable outcomes, with a clinical success rate of 97.4% and a radiographic success rate of 84.2% at the 12-month follow-up.

Earlier studies evaluating pulpotomy treatments in primary teeth using NeoPutty® have shown clinical outcomes between 96% and 97.4%, and radiographic outcomes between 92% and 97.4% [1, 13, 27]. In line with the existing literature, NeoPutty® MTA achieved consistent clinical and radiographic success rates in this study and yielded similar performance to ProRoot® MTA.

As for TheraCal PT®, Wassel et al. [28] documented 100% clinical and 96.15% radiographic efficacy at the 12-month follow-up in primary molar pulpotomies. However, these conflicting findings should be interpreted with caution due to significant methodological differences. Wassel et al.’s study was designed as a non-randomized clinical trial, a methodology prone to selection bias where treatment allocation may be influenced by the operator’s judgment of the tooth’s prognosis. Such bias can inadvertently inflate success rates for the experimental material. In contrast, our study employed a strictly randomized design to ensure an unbiased distribution of baseline risks. Our results align more closely with those of Gaber et al. [29], who reported a considerably lower radiographic success rate of 40% in a randomized pilot study. In the current study, TheraCal PT® achieved a clinical success rate of 91.9%, while the radiographic success rate was significantly lower at 67.6%, suggesting that the high efficacy reported in non-randomized settings may not be replicable under the rigorous controls of a randomized trial.

Studies assessing the clinical and radiographic survival of pulpotomy treatments utilizing various types of MTA have reported no significant differences throughout the entire follow-up period [25, 30]. Conversely, in our study, significant differences were observed in the early, mid, and overall follow-up periods due to failures associated with TheraCal PT®.

Several factors may have contributed to the lower success rate observed in the TheraCal PT® group in our study. First, the lack of a strong correlation between histological inflammatory changes and clinical findings may have led to incorrect treatment indications at the outset. If chairside cytokine test kits had been available to assess the inflammatory status of each tooth prior to treatment, the indication for pulpotomy could have been established more accurately [31]. However, since all teeth in our study were included based on the same inclusion criteria and randomized, uncertainty regarding inflammatory status alone does not sufficiently explain the relatively lower success rate of TheraCal PT®. Second, it has been reported that TheraCal PT® releases higher amounts of triethylene glycol dimethacrylate (TEGDMA) monomer, which is known to be toxic and induce apoptosis in human pulp cells [32]. The release of TEGDMA may have triggered an inflammatory response in the pulp, contributing to treatment failure. Furthermore, similar to TheraCal LC®, which has been shown to increase the proinflammatory cytokine IL-8 [33], further investigation is warranted to determine whether TheraCal PT®, a subsequent product from the same manufacturer, exhibits similar proinflammatory effects. Third, during the polymerization of TheraCal LC®, a temperature increase of 7.53 °C ± 1.34 °C in the pulp chamber was observed [34], exceeding the critical threshold of 5.6 °C reported by Zach and Cohen [35] as necessary for the maintenance of pulp vitality. Therefore, despite being dual-cured, TheraCal PT® may have caused irreversible pulp damage due to heat generation during polymerization, similar to its predecessor, TheraCal LC®.

Internal resorption represents a strictly inflammation-dependent pathological process, driven by the activation of odontoclasts within continuously inflamed pulp tissue [24, 36]. In our study, internal root resorption was the predominant failure mode, observed significantly more frequently in the TheraCal PT® group (9 cases) compared to the other materials tested. This distinct failure pattern suggests a potential link between the material’s properties and the biological response. TheraCal PT®, as a resin-modified calcium silicate cement, is known to release resin monomers (e.g., TEGDMA), and cytotoxicity in resin-containing calcium silicate materials has been attributed to this resin content [18, 32]. These chemical irritants—potentially combined with exothermic polymerization heat—may sustain chronic low-grade inflammation in clinical settings. Persistent pulpal inflammation is well-documented to compromise dentine bridge continuity and reparative outcomes [37] and such an inflammatory environment could theoretically stimulate odontoclastic activity. The significantly elevated rate of internal resorption observed with TheraCal PT® in our study likely reflects this inflammation-driven resorptive process, warranting further investigation into the material’s long-term biological interactions.

From a clinical perspective, although a formal cost-effectiveness analysis was beyond the scope of this study, the economic aspect of material selection is highly relevant. While pre-mixed bioceramic materials may have a higher initial purchase cost compared to traditional powder-liquid systems, their ability to eliminate mixing errors, reduce material waste, and significantly shorten chairside time suggests a potential cost-efficiency in clinical practice. This is particularly advantageous in pediatric dentistry, where reducing treatment duration is critical for behavior management.

To obtain reliable outcomes in the evaluation of pulp therapies, a minimum follow-up period of 24 months is recommended [38]. The 12-month follow-up duration in the present study may be considered a limitation. Nevertheless, the lower rate of patient dropout within a 12-month follow-up period may provide an advantage for the study outcomes. Another limitation of this study is the inclusion of only primary second molars, due to the earlier onset of physiological root resorption in primary first molars.

Conclusion

In conclusion, ProRoot® MTA and NeoPutty® MTA demonstrated high clinical and radiographic success over 12 months, confirming their reliability as gold-standard materials for primary tooth pulpotomy. However, TheraCal PT® showed a significantly higher risk of radiographic failure, specifically characterized by internal resorption, despite its acceptable clinical performance. These findings highlight that clinical success alone may mask underlying pathological processes. Therefore, caution is warranted when using TheraCal PT®, and it should not be considered a primary alternative to MTA-based materials in pediatric endodontics until further long-term clinical trials validate its safety and efficacy.
