Predicting reduction loss risk after acromioclavicular joint dislocation treated with the endobutton device
Authors: Yuqin Fang, Jinwu Wang, Junlei Lv, Zeming Zhang, Miaolong Zhao, Yaosen Wu, Xiaolei Zhang, Naifeng Tian, Huamei Zhu, Wenhao Zheng, Liaojun Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe objective of this study was to evaluate risk factors and clinical outcomes in patients with reduction loss after acromioclavicular joint dislocation treated with the Endobutton device and to develop a nomogram prediction model.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective examination of the medical records of 250 patients who had undergone ligament reconstruction using the Endobutton device. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were utilized to identify the risk factors. Subsequently, a nomogram model was constructed to forecast the probability of reduction loss. The model’s discrimination and calibration were assessed using a calibration plot, receiver operating characteristic, and decision plot. To evaluate the clinical outcomes of patients experiencing reduction loss, both the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score and Constant score were assessed both preoperatively and at the final follow-up.ResultsReduction loss occurred in 25.6% (64/250) of enrolled patients during the study period. In the multivariate logistic regression analysis, independent risk factors for postoperative reduction loss were identified, including the conoid tunnel ratio (P = 0.002), trapezoid tunnel ratio (P = 0.009), coracoid button position (P < 0.001), additional pin fixation (P < 0.001), and weight-bearing time (P < 0.001). The contour map demonstrated a predictive accuracy of 0.896 in assessing the risk of reduction loss. Patients with displacement exceeding 100% of the thickness of the distal clavicle exhibited significantly worse results at the final follow-up in terms of the VAS score (P < 0.001), Constant score (P < 0.001), and subjective outcomes (P < 0.001).ConclusionsPatients with displacement greater than 100% of the thickness of the distal clavicle had poorer postoperative clinical outcomes. We found a correlation between reduction loss and tunnel malposition, no additional pin fixation and premature weight-bearing of the affected limb (< 5 weeks). Our nomogram helped to identify high-risk patients and provide them with close follow-up and even surgical intervention to reduce the rate of reduction loss. It also provided us with guidance on surgical treatment for such injuries in the future.Keywords: Acromioclavicular joint dislocation, Complication, Reduction loss, Risk factor, Prediction model

Background

Both biomechanical and clinical studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of anatomic ligament reconstruction using the Endobutton device for managing acute acromioclavicular joint dislocations [1–3]. Nevertheless, postoperative complications still occur frequently. Complications such as residual shoulder pain, calcification of the coracoclavicular ligament, nerve injury, iatrogenic fractures of the coracoid or clavicle, heterotopic ossification, infection, reduction loss, etc. have been reported [1–3]. One of the most frequent complications among them is reduction loss, occurring at an incidence rate ranging from 12 to 28% [4–7]. This complication results in reduced range of motion, heightened pain levels, and poor subjective satisfaction [5–7].

Multiple studies have attempted to identify risk factors associated with reduction loss following acromioclavicular joint dislocation treated with the Endobutton device. Reported contributing factors include inappropriate surgical indications, technical errors, and improper loop length selection [6–8]. However, these studies are generally limited by small sample sizes and a narrow range of selected variables [7, 9, 10]. Some predictive factors are based on surgeons’ subjective experience or biomechanical assumptions, which may compromise the reliability of the conclusions [11, 12]. Moreover, the clinical consequences of reduction loss remain controversial, and current evidence regarding its impact on postoperative pain and shoulder function is still limited [4, 13]. In recent years, nomogram prediction models have gained widespread attention in medical research due to their ability to visually integrate multiple risk factors and provide individualized risk assessments. As an intuitive and practical clinical tool, nomograms assist clinicians in identifying high-risk patients preoperatively and in formulating personalized intervention strategies. Nevertheless, studies on visual predictive models specifically for reduction loss after acromioclavicular joint dislocation remain scarce. Therefore, developing a scientifically sound and practical predictive model is of great significance for guiding early intervention, and optimizing postoperative recovery in patients.

To address the above issues, we designed this study. First, the purpose of this study was to identify risk factors for reduction loss in patients treated with the Endobutton device for acromioclavicular joint dislocation. Second, we wanted to build a nomogram prediction model. This approach facilitated the identification of high-risk patients and the implementation of early interventions to reduce the occurrence of reduction loss. Third, we assessed shoulder joint function and pain scores of patients with reduction loss to see if all reduction loss adversely affected the patients’ postoperative outcomes.

Materials and methods

Patients

The Ethics Committee approved the study, and informed consent was acquired from all patients prior to the operation. Diagnosis of complete acromioclavicular joint dislocation relied on both clinical and radiographic assessments. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age from 18 to 55 years, (2) fresh dislocation (defined as surgical intervention performed within 14 days of injury), (3) a minimum period of 12 months follow-up, (4) Rockwood type III, IV or V dislocations. Patients meeting the following criteria were excluded: (1) open dislocations, (2) history of ipsilateral upper extremity injury or surgery, (3) combined with nerve or vascular injury, (4) lost follow-up, (5) pathological fracture. Methods

Potential risk factors and radiographic measurements

The potential risk factors include age, BMI, gender, time from injury to surgery, site of injury, mechanism of injury (low or high energy), Rockwood’s classification (type III, IV or V), open or arthroscopic reconstruction, conoid tunnel ratio (< 0.2, 0.20–0.25 or > 0.25), trapezoid tunnel ratio (< 0.13, 0.13–0.16 or > 0.16), coracoid button position (central, medial and lateral areas), tunnel diameter (3.5 mm, 4.0–4.5 mm diameter of the drilling bit), different fixation modes (double, triple or quatra), button alignment (perpendicular or parallel), additional temporary pin fixation (yes or no), acromioclavicular ligament repair (yes or no), operation time (duration from skin incision to skin closure), postoperative limb immobilization (triangular bandage or neck and wrist sling), weight-bearing time of the affected upper limb, heterotopic ossification. In this study, surgeries were performed by different surgeons based on the actual clinical situation (open or arthroscopic approach). In the open approach, a standard incision was made to directly visualize and reduce the fracture, followed by internal fixation. In the arthroscopic approach, small portals were created to allow minimally invasive visualization, fracture reduction, and fixation under arthroscopic guidance. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the yellow line labeled as “a” represented the measurement from the lateral edge of the clavicle to the midpoint of the conoid tunnel, while the red line labeled as “c” indicated the total length of the clavicle. The conoid tunnel ratio was computed as the ratio of “a” to “c”. Likewise, the trapezoid tunnel ratio was derived from the ratio of “b” to “c”. The location of the coracoid buttons were divided into central, medial and lateral areas as described in Fig. 3. Different fixation modes meant to use 2 buttons (double), 3 buttons (triple), or 4 buttons (quatra) for reconstruction of the coracoclavicular ligament. The alignment of buttons (one positioned above the clavicle and the other below the coracoid process) was assessed using surgical records or intraoperative printed X-ray images. Alignment was considered parallel when the long axes of the two buttons were at 0° and perpendicular when they intersected at 90°. Additional temporary pin fixation meant to use 2 Kirschner wires to fix the acromioclavicular joint for 6 weeks in addition to using the buttons to reconstruct the coracoclavicular ligament. Heterotopic ossification was detected based on anteroposterior views of the injured shoulder obtained during the postoperative period or at the final follow-up. Two authors collected all demographic and outcome data from the patients and stored it anonymously in a database. Radiographic and clinical follow-up occurred at 2, 4, 6, 8, and 12 weeks, followed by evaluations at 6 and 12 months, and ultimately at the last follow-up. Radiographic views, including anteroposterior, Zanca, and axillary were captured for both shoulders. All radiographs were obtained with the patient in the supine position, bilateral forearms in neutral position, adducted to the chest. To ensure consistency across individuals, the position of each patient was evaluated by an author. Additionally, all Endobutton devices used were non-adjustable double-button systems supplied by Smith & Nephew.

Zonation of the coracoid button location (lateral, central, medial) was delineated using a ratio-based measurement. Surgical procedures and fixation techniques

After routine disinfection and draping, a 4-cm incision was made over the lateral edge of the clavicle, and a 3-cm vertical incision was made at the coracoid process, perpendicular to the clavicle. One assistant pressed down on the distal end of the clavicle while another lifted the elbow upward. Following manual reduction of the acromioclavicular joint, a 2.0 mm Kirschner wire was used for temporary fixation. A 2.0 mm guide pin was then inserted to localize the bone tunnel, which was carefully positioned through the central portion of the coracoid process. A 3.5 mm drill was used along the guide pin to create the coracoclavicular tunnel. The channel length from the superior surface of the clavicle to the inferior surface of the coracoid is measured with a depth gauge, and the loop size is determined in 5-mm increments. If the measurement is within ± 1 mm, a standard EndoButton (Smith & Nephew) is used; if it exceeds this range, a larger loop combined with a thicker button is selected to ensure reduction accuracy within 1 mm. Two No. 5 braided polyethylene sutures are then threaded through the first and fourth holes of the button. The button is first passed through the clavicle, after which a 3.2-mm smooth cylindrical dilator is introduced into the coracoid tunnel. By pulling the sutures, the inferior button is secured beneath the coracoid, while the superior button is fixed on the clavicle. If required, an additional 2.0-mm drill hole is made on the superior clavicle, approximately 2 cm medial to the acromioclavicular joint, to create a second tunnel for placement of a third button to reconstruct the trapezoid ligament. Under fluoroscopic guidance, satisfactory implant positioning and acromioclavicular joint reduction are confirmed, after which the sutures are tied to complete fixation. Radiographic evaluation and clinical outcomes

The X-ray images were interpreted by two blinded observers to prevent any potential confusion. Our measurements were conducted using a picture archiving and communication system (PACS). The two blind observers, who were not involved in the study, conducted measurements twice, and the average values of four measurements were recorded and analyzed. To assess the inter-observer reliability of radiological measurements, we introduced a consistency evaluation using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for continuous variables and Cohen’s Kappa coefficient for categorical variables. ICC values > 0.75 were considered to indicate excellent agreement, and kappa values > 0.80 indicated strong agreement [14]. Reduction loss was characterized as a reduction loss during surgery of at least half the thickness of the distal clavicle [2]. According to Rockwood’s original classification, we classified postoperative reduction loss into two categories: less than or equal to 100% displacement of the thickness of the distal clavicle (Group B) and greater than 100% displacement of the thickness of the distal clavicle (Group C). At the final follow-up, the clinical outcomes of patients with reduction loss were assessed using the original Constant score [15] and Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score. Additionally, patients were asked to subjectively rate the condition of their injured shoulder as excellent, good, fair, or poor.

Statistical analysis

For comparing continuous variables, the Student’s t-test was employed, while the Mann-Whitney U test was utilized for other comparisons between groups. The chi-square test was used to compare qualitative variables. Univariate analysis assessed the relationship between reduction loss and potential risk factors, followed by multivariate logistic regression analysis to predict risk factors. The inclusion process was assessed using a P threshold of < 0.10. After establishing regression coefficients for independent variables, we developed a nomogram prediction model for postoperative reduction loss. Assessment of dichotomous outcomes generally entailed the examination of the area under the curve (AUC) on the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. An AUC ranging from 0.5 to 0.75 was considered acceptable, whereas an AUC exceeding 0.75 indicated excellent discriminative capability within the prediction model. Furthermore, a decision curve analysis was conducted to assess the net benefit of the nomogram in decision-making. The analyses were conducted utilizing SPSS software, version 21.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and R software, version 3.6.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The predetermined significance level was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Between March 2014 and May 2021, two hundred and seventy-six consecutive patients with AC joint dislocation underwent treatment with the Endobutton device at our institution. Among them, 26 cases were excluded, 3 cases were open dislocation, 4 cases were complicated by nerve or blood vessel injury, 6 cases had a history of ipsilateral upper limb injury or surgery, 1 case was a pathological fracture, and 12 cases were lost to follow-up. In conclusion, a total of 250 patients were included, with an average follow-up time in our group of 20.4 months (ranging from 12 to 35 months). In 64 out of 250 cases (25.6%), reduction loss was observed. Detailed baseline characteristics of all patients are provided in Table 1. In the univariate analysis, statistically significant associations with reduction loss were observed for the conoid tunnel ratio, trapezoid tunnel ratio, coracoid button position, additional pin fixation, acromioclavicular ligament repair, weight-bearing time, and button alignment (P = 0.026, P = 0.014, P < 0.001, P < 0.001, P = 0.030, P < 0.001, and P = 0.042, respectively) (Table 2). The final multivariable binary logistic regression model showed that conoid tunnel ratio (P = 0.002), trapezoid tunnel ratio (P = 0.009), coracoid button position (P < 0.001), additional pin fixation (P < 0.001) and weight-bearing time (P < 0.001) were independent risk factors of postoperative reduction loss (Table 3). The inter‑observer reliability for continuous measures was excellent: the ICC for the conoid tunnel ratio was 0.87, for the trapezoid tunnel ratio was 0.84, and for weight-bearing time was 0.90. For categorical variables, the kappa value for coracoid button position classification was 0.82, and for additional Kirschner wire fixation usage was 1.00, indicating strong agreement.

Utilizing the multivariable model, we developed a nomogram (Fig. 5) integrating independent predictors to forecast postoperative reduction loss. The probability of reduction loss was determined by calculating the total score, which was obtained by summing the points assigned to each variable. The calibration curve demonstrated notable concordance between predicted and actual values, with an average absolute error of 0.03 (Fig. 6). This close alignment between predicted and actual risk values underscores significant agreement. ROC analysis assessed the predictive efficacy of the nomogram model. The calculated area under the curve (AUC) value was 0.896, indicating outstanding discriminatory ability in predicting the risk of postoperative dislocation (Fig. 7). To assess the clinical utility of the nomogram, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA), an advanced method for assessing the net clinical benefits of predictive models, suggested that the optimal threshold probability for predicting reduction loss using this nomogram fell between 0.03 and 0.98. Favorable threshold probabilities suggest that the nomogram can aid clinicians in accurately assessing the risk of postoperative reduction loss in patients with AC joint dislocation (Fig. 8). For example, a 30-year-old male patient developed a right AC joint dislocation (Fig. 9A). Postoperative radiograph on the second day after surgery showed that the AC joint had achieved satisfactory reduction (Fig. 9B). The patient’s score was determined using the nomogram model: conoid tunnel ratio > 0.25 (30 points), trapezoid tunnel ratio between 0.13 and 0.16 (0 points), non-central positioning of the coracoid button (80 points), absence of supplementary pin fixation (77 points), and starting weight-bearing activities 4 weeks after surgery (75 points). The cumulative scores amounted to approximately 262 points, suggesting a reduction loss likelihood of around 75%. In the sixth week of postoperative follow-up, we found that the patient experienced reduction loss (Fig. 9C).

Discussion

Previous studies had a small sample size and included too few potential risk factors [9, 10]. Furthermore, simple correlation analysis in the past did not accurately reflect the situation and often led to erroneous conclusions. The current research improved the shortcomings of the previous studies. Additionally, through the utilization of the nomogram derived from the multivariate model in clinical practice, we enabled individual risk prediction for reduction loss. The variables significantly and independently influencing reduction loss were the conoid tunnel ratio, trapezoid tunnel ratio, coracoid button position, additional pin fixation, and weight-bearing time, as revealed by the findings of the study. Additionally, the accuracy of the contour map in predicting the risk of reduction loss was 0.896.

In our collective, the rate of reduction loss was 25.6%, within the range reported in the previous studies [9, 10, 16].Consistent with prior studies, our findings indicated that the misalignment of tunnels correlated with a heightened risk of reduction loss [7–10, 17, 18]. The establishment of clavicle tunnel and coracoid tunnel was the most critical factor for a successful surgery [6, 9, 10, 17–19]. In Eisenstein ED’s study [17], it was suggested that the ideal conoid tunnel ratio falls within the range of 0.20 to 0.25, while the trapezoid tunnel ratio should be under 0.16. Deviating from these ranges, with a conoid tunnel ratio below 0.20 or above 0.25, or a trapezoid tunnel ratio exceeding 0.16, was associated with a notable increase in the likelihood of reduction loss. Cook JB et al. [8] corroborated that positioning the conoid tunnel approximately 7 to 9 mm laterally from previous norms, or at 25% of the clavicular length, was linked to a decreased incidence of reduction loss. Our research also reached the same conclusion. Our prior investigation introduced a straightforward and pragmatic approach to precisely determine the position of the coracoid tunnel [18]. The placement of the coracoid tunnel could be inferred indirectly by assessing the position of the button beneath the coracoid process [18]. This study revealed that both medial and lateral placement of the coracoid button contributed to an increased rate of reduction loss. Biomechanical research further indicated that when the coracoid tunnel was positioned centrally, fixations exhibited superior strength, a reduced rate of reduction loss, and fewer failures [11, 12]. Hence, it became imperative to adjust the guide pin’s position if it deviated too far from the central region, as confirmed by intraoperative fluoroscopy.

Our study identified the absence of additional pin fixation as another notable predictor for an increased risk of reduction loss, a finding consistent with the observations made by Cho CH et al. [20]. They used additional Kirschner wires to fix the acromioclavicular joint for 6 weeks. The findings from their study endorsed the notion that temporary pin fixation for the acromioclavicular joint could aid in safeguarding the reduction during the initial healing phase and the formation of rigid scar tissue when utilizing a suture button device for coracoclavicular reconstruction. In our series, using two Kirschner wires was mostly seen in cases where 2 buttons are used for fixation. Two buttons, with their provision of only single-point vertical fixation, were insufficient for restoring the horizontal stability of the AC joint, particularly among patients unable to undergo acromioclavicular ligament repair. Furthermore, the previous biomechanical studie [21] has shown that the gradual loss of AC joint reduction stems from excessive stress concentrated at the interface between the bone and the metal button, leading to clavicle osteolysis or the metal button pulling through the coracoid process. If additional fixation (using two Kirschner wires) was used, some stress could be dispersed. In clinical practice, we have observed that loss of reduction frequently occurs during the early postoperative period (< 6 weeks). Nevertheless, we acknowledge that modern techniques such as non-absorbable suture or tape augmentation offer superior dynamic stability without the complications related to K-wire fixation (e.g., migration or breakage) [22, 23]. While our findings suggest that K-wire fixation remains a feasible alternative in cases where AC ligament repair is not possible, current best practices should favor suture/tape augmentation when conditions permit.

Within our study, a notable increase in the risk of reduction loss was observed when weight bearing on the affected limb commenced earlier than 5 weeks post-surgery, with an average initiation time of 4.78 weeks noted in the reduction loss group. Due to social and family factors, some patients must return to their original jobs as soon as possible. Hence, these patients, often engaged in physically demanding occupations, tended to disregard the doctor’s discharge instructions. Early stress stimulation might lead to difficulty in ligament healing. A study on ligament characteristics further illustrated a notable increase in laxity three weeks post-injury, with functional strength returning to normal levels after six weeks [24]. The average weight-bearing time of the affected limb in the remaining reduction group was 6.4 weeks. This time met the requirements for ligament healing mentioned above.

While several studies have reported various predictors of reduction loss following acromioclavicular joint dislocation treatment with the Endobutton device, none have investigated the visual representation of these risk factors in nomograms. Nomograms offer the advantage over traditional multiple regression models in that they can graphically display all key predictors. Taking into account patient, surgeon, and injury-derived parameters, it facilitates straightforward risk assessment for reduction loss. According to the DCA curve, our ROC curve predicts that the probability range of 0.1–0.9 is within the optimal threshold range. Interventions within this range can achieve better net benefits. Based on the predicted risk of reduction loss, we classified patients into three risk levels: low risk (probability < 10%), moderate risk (10–50%), and high risk (> 50%). A corresponding graded management strategy was developed: the low-risk group undergoes routine follow-up and health education; the moderate-risk group requires enhanced clinical monitoring and preventive interventions; and the high-risk group is advised to initiate individualized treatment early and undergo surgical intervention if necessary. The nomogram we present serves as a valuable tool for clinicians to identify high-risk patients, enabling them to offer close follow-up and, if necessary, surgical intervention to mitigate the rate of reduction loss.

At the last follow-up, we also conducted a clinical evaluation of the patients with reduction loss to see if it had a significant impact on their final function. Our results indicated that compared with patients without reduction loss, patients with less than or equal to 100% displacement of the thickness of the distal clavicle did not show significant differences in pain and functional scores at the last follow-up. However, patients with displacement greater than 100% of the thickness of the distal clavicle had significantly poorer pain and functional scores. Çarkçı E et al. [13] suggested that a deviation exceeding 3 mm in the coracoclavicular (CC) distance between the last follow-up and early post-operation indicated reduction loss. While their findings revealed no statistically significant disparity in Constant scores due to this loss, subjective evaluation and aesthetic satisfaction values were notably compromised.Our grouping method was different from theirs. The degree of reduction loss in group C was greater than that in their cases. This might be the main reason for explaining the different functional outcomes of patients in the two studies.

The present study had limitations: (1) It employed a retrospective design, necessitating reliance on accurate and thorough past data documentation. (2) Despite repeated measurements of relevant indicators and evaluation by two independent observers, measurement errors were inevitable. In addition, it was extremely difficult to ensure that the position of each patient remained exactly the same when taking X-rays. (3) Although osteoporosis may influence reduction loss, our cohort consisted predominantly of young male patients, limiting our ability to assess bone quality as an independent risk factor. (4) Radiographs alone are insufficient to quantify horizontal instability; future studies should incorporate CT imaging to evaluate lateral displacement. (5) One limitation of this study is the heterogeneity in injury types and surgical techniques, which future studies can address by focusing on acute Rockwood V cases with standardized procedures. (6) Perhaps there are still some risk factors that have not been discovered yet. For example, tunnel widening was still a controversial risk factor. Some authors believed it is a risk factor for reduction loss while others believed it is not [25, 26].

Conclusion

In conclusion, patients with displacement greater than 100% of the thickness of the distal clavicle had poorer postoperative clinical outcomes. Further surgical intervention may be needed for this group of patients. For patients with less than or equal to 100% displacement of the thickness of the distal clavicle, no special treatment was required. We found a correlation between reduction loss and tunnel malposition, no additional pin fixation and premature weight-bearing of the affected limb (< 5 weeks). Our nomogram helped to identify high-risk patients and provide them with close follow-up and even surgical intervention to reduce the rate of reduction loss. It also provided us with guidance on surgical treatment for such injuries in the future.
