Engineered vesicles facilitate the programmed transdermal-intestinal delivery of cinnabar for insomnia mitigation by modulating serotonin-Htr1d-cAMP
Authors: Yunhao Ren, Fengyuan Song, Yuwen Li, Hui Li, Yuling Liu, Maobo Du, Lihua Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Insomnia is a prevalent sleep disorder and it affects brain development, with pediatric populations being particularly vulnerable. Cinnabar, a mineral drug composed of HgS, has demonstrated efficacy in alleviating insomnia through suppressing the overactivity of glutamate receptors (NMDA/AMPA), etc. However, oral administration of cinnabar poses risks, including binding with hemoglobin and accumulation in tissues and organs, resulting in neurotoxicity. To overcome these limitations, we developed a novel transdermal and intestinal targeting programmed nanoplatform for cinnabar delivery (TAT/CSK-cinnabar vesicle, TCCV), of which, cinnabar was encapsulated within a lipid vesicle, which was then co-engineered with cell-penetrating peptide TAT and the intestinal-targeting CSK ligand. Accordingly, TCCV showed exceptional sequential penetration through the stratum corneum (SC) and intestinal barriers, and also actively targeted intestinal goblet cells with a 15-fold increase in efficiency compared to non-engineered vesicles. Furthermore, TCCV forms a reservoir releasing cinnabar at the intestinal site with controlled manner, significantly reducing fluctuations in cinnabar concentration in blood and organs, thereby reducing toxicity. In current anti-insomnia studies, TCCV exhibited predominantly enhanced therapeutic efficiency compared to the oral control group, with drug efficiency increased by 1.5 to 2.5-fold. With the analysis of RNA sequencing and 16S rRNA, the regulation of the serotonin (5-HT) production in gut microbes and activating the Htr1d-cAMP pathway of cerebral cortex through the "brain-gut axis" by TCCV is identified as the novel mechanism for the insomnia mitigation effect of cinnabar. This study offers a novel non-invasive transdermal and targeted nanoplatform that significantly improves the efficacy and biosafety of cinnabar delivery and highlights a new gut-brain axis-mediated mechanism in insomnia mitigation.

Introduction

Insomnia is the most common sleep problem among all ages, affecting up to one-third of the global population [1]. Given the critical role of adequate sleep in brain development, pediatric populations are particularly vulnerable to the negative effects of insomnia [[2], [3]]. However, the problem of insomnia, especially among children and adolescents, is often under-addressed in clinical practice. Current clinical treatments for insomnia include cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and pharmacological interventions. Although CBT is psychotherapy advocated by the industry, it faces limitations, such as shortages of relevant talents and high costs [4]. Therefore, pharmacological interventions, including benzodiazepines and non-benzodiazepines, remain the most commonly used options due to their convenience and effectiveness [5]. However, these medications are not without drawbacks, as they are associated with the risk of drug dependence and potential negative effects on neurological function [6,7].

Cinnabar, a commonly used mineral drug which is composed of mercury sulfide (HgS), has been employed as a sedative for over 2000 years and remains prevalent in clinical practice across Asia [8]. It is traditionally used to treat insomnia, epilepsy, excessive dreaming, and infantile convulsions [9]. In China, approximately 40 traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) formulations currently incorporate cinnabar [10]. One notable example is Zhu Sha An Shen Wan (ZSASW), a TCM formula containing 20 % cinnabar, which is widely prescribed for sedation, insomnia, and palpitations [9]. Patients with insomnia disorder (ID) exhibit elevated serum levels of glutamate (Glu), glutamine (Gln), and glutamate dehydrogenase (GAD), along with increased mRNA expression of the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) NR1 subunit in peripheral blood. Notably, the upregulation of NMDAR NR1 subunit mRNA may serve as a potential clinical biomarker for ID [11]. Cinnabar demonstrates both hypnotic and anxiolytic properties. It normalizes kainic acid (KA)-induced hippocampal neurotransmission imbalances by simultaneously suppressing the overactivity of glutamate receptors (NMDA/AMPA) and modulating gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) signaling. Through mechanisms involving the restoration of hippocampal excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) balance, cinnabar exerts neuroprotective effects, alleviating insomnia and comorbid anxiety-like behaviors, thus emerging as a potential therapeutic agent for seizure management [12]. Additionally, cinnabar treatment prolongs pentobarbital-mediated sleep duration in mice and may delay or prevent pentobarbital tolerance [13]. Its sustained hypnotic effects (without altering pentobarbital metabolism) suggest intrinsic sedative properties [14], possibly linked to reduced activity of Na+/K+-ATPase and Ca2+-ATPase [14]. Moreover, Connectivity Map (CMap) analysis predicts that leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2) may be a therapeutic target of cinnabar for insomnia relief, while farnesyltransferase (FTase) could be a potential toxicological target of cinnabar [15], and the modulation of brain serotonin (5-HT) levels by cinnabar likely contributes to its anxiolytic effects [[8], [16]]. Furthermore, insomnia severity correlates with lower plasma concentrations of key antioxidant metabolites, which are critical for mitigating oxidative stress and neurodegeneration [17]. In cinnabar-treated mice, the expression of pro-inflammatory enzymes (COX-2, iNOS) and mediators (IL-1β, TNF-α, PGE2, NO) in the liver and kidneys is consistently downregulated [18], indicating that cinnabar may also alleviate insomnia by reducing systemic inflammation.

However, when administered orally, cinnabar undergoes oxidation in the gastrointestinal tract, resulting in the formation of toxic inorganic mercury ions (Hg2+ and Hg4+) [19]. These accumulated ions exhibit toxicity through binding to macromolecules, particularly hemoglobin and plasma proteins, leading to organ-specific damage, including hepatic and renal tubular lesions [20]. Therefore, improving the delivery method for cinnabar, reducing its toxicity, and enhancing its bioavailability are crucial for advancing insomnia treatment. Transdermal drugs delivery systems (TDDS) have emerged as an attractive alternative solution for pediatrics drug delivery. Several methods have been developed to facilitate the transdermal delivery of both small molecules and macromolecules for various clinical applications, such as physical, chemical and biological penetration enhancement methods, etc [21]. Compared to oral delivery, transdermal administration of cinnabar bypasses the gastrointestinal metabolism, thereby reducing the formation of mercury ions and significantly mitigating its toxicity, while maintaining therapeutic efficacy [22,23]. However, the barrier effect of stratum corneum (SC) limits the skin penetration of high polar crystalline drugs [24,25]. Liposomes or vesicles, composed of a lipid bilayer and an aqueous core, have shown promise in improving transdermal drug delivery [26,27]. These nanoparticles can encapsulate both hydrophobic and hydrophilic drugs, and integrate with the skin lipids, maintaining optimal hydration conditions, thereby improving drug penetration in the skin layers. Moreover, after penetrating the SC, cinnabar can be transported across the dermal layer and intestinal wall tissues, potentially serving as a depot for sustained drug release at the intestinal site. This approach helps to control cinnabar concentration fluctuations in systemic circulation, thereby reducing its toxicity [28].

Although liposomes or vesicles demonstrate transdermal delivery potential, further engineering of them is suggested to achieve higher transdermal efficiency and reduce skin retention. Cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) are emerging as promising tools due to their ability to facilitate the translocation of nanoparticles across cellular membranes [29]. Transactivating transcriptional activator peptide (TAT, YGRKKRRQRRR), a well-known CPP, has been demonstrated to enhance the transdermal delivery through electrostatic interactions with cell membranes, utilizing its hydrophilic and cationic properties [30]. Additionally, to improve the accumulation of encapsulated compounds in target areas, liposomes or vesicles can be functionalized with targeting moieties that promote specific interactions with target cells. CSK (CSKSSDYQC), a short peptide with a stable cyclic structure [31], and potential biological activity that specifically targets intestinal goblet cells (mucus-producing cells) [32], facilitating traversal of mucosal barrier [33].

In this study, for the first time, we present the peptide-modified cinnabar-loaded vesicles therapeutic for the treatment of insomnia. To enable transdermal delivery of cinnabar while minimizing potential toxicity, we initially developed a cinnabar-loaded lipid vesicle (CV) using polyoxyl 40 stearate and cholesterol, which was then engineered by two functional peptides to form TAT and CSK-modified cinnabar-loaded lipid vesicle (TCCV). Upon application to the abdominal skin, TCCV utilized TAT-mediated alterations in cell membrane permeability to facilitate efficient transdermal delivery through the transcellular pathway. Guided by CSK peptide, TCCV could accurately recognize intestinal goblet cells and fulfill the anchoring of TCCV to intestinal wall, where cinnabar was released in a sustained manner. Furthermore, considering the prolonged intestinal retention of cinnabar after administration, 16S rRNA sequencing was performed to investigate the novel mechanism by which cinnabar regulates the gut microbiota to exert its anti-insomnia effects (Fig. 1). Overall, this platform greatly improves the utilization of cinnabar, reduces its toxicity, and extends its duration of action appropriately. Materials and methods

Materials

Cinnabar was purchased from TOP Biotechnoligy Co., Ltd (Hangzhou, China). Polyoxyl [34] stearate and cholesterol were purchased Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was bought from Mingte Co., Ltd (Hangzhou, China). Elisa kit (SOD, LDH, MDA, GSH, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) were obtained from Bosch Biotechnology (Wuhan, China). The apoptosis and ROS kit were obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Trizol, cDNA Synthesis Mix kit, real-time quantitative PCR kit were obtained from Novoprotein Scientific Inc (Suzhou, China). DMEM medium and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were brought from Tangling Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Hangzhou, China). DiO was purchased from Yeasen Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). DCFH-DA was brought from AAT Bioquest (USA). P-chlorophenylalanine (PCPA) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (USA).

Preparation and characterization of TCCV

Preparation of cinnabar vesicles (CV)

1.8 g polyoxyl [34] stearate and 1.5 g cholesterol were placed in a 100 mL round bottom flask, and 25 mL ethanol was added and shaken until thoroughly dissolved. The mixture was then placed on a rotating evaporator at 55 °C under reduced pressure to remove ethanol. Then, 1.5 g cinnabar suspension (20 mL PBS, pH 7.4) was added to this flask and removed PBS (reduced pressure). The round bottom flask was dried overnight in a vacuum drying oven, 20 mL PBS (pH 7.4) was added, stirred at 55 °C for 30 min, and sonicated for 30 min. Finally, cinnabar vesicles (CV) were preserved at −20 °C.

Surface modification of CV with TAT and CSK

TAT (YGRKKRRQRRR) and CSK (CSKSSDYQC) peptides (synthesized from Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd.) were incubated with CV at a mass ratio of 1:3 for 1 h at 37 °C. After incubation, the mixture was centrifuged at 3000 g for 30 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the CV that retained on the filter membrane was carefully collected. Finally, TCCV were preserved at −20 °C.

Identification of TAT/CSK grafts to CV

The grafting rate of the targeted peptides onto the CV was determined by an Infinite F50 absorbance microplate reader (TECAN, Austria). Moreover, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Bruker Corporation, Billerica, MA, US) and ultraviolet spectrophotometer (US, Bruker Corporation, Billerica, MA, US) were used to detect the absorption peaks of TAT/CSK peptides. FTIR measurements was performed in transmission mode over a wavenumber range of 4000-400 cm−1. The working parameters: resolution of 4 cm−1, 32 scans. In addition, UV–vis absorption spectra were recorded.

Nanoparticle size and zeta potential analysis

The particle size, and zeta potential of the CV and TCCV were measured in triplicate using a nano-particle size and potential analyzer (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Malvern, UK). The vesicles were diluted with PBS (pH 7.0) to an appropriate concentration to avoid multiple scattering effects. Nano-particle size analysis: The detection angle is 90°, the measurement time is 60–120 s/time. Zeta potential analysis: Electric field strength is 10–20 V/cm, measure 10 cycles.

TEM observation

For CV and TCCV, 20 μL TCCV was diluted and dropwise added onto a Formvar/carbon copper mesh (Merck, Germany), and sat for 5 min to allow it to adsorb. After excess liquid was removed with filter paper, 2 % phosphotungstic acid (pH 7.0) was immediately added for negative staining for 30 s, after excess liquid was removed with filter paper, and dried at room temperature for 5 min and waited for observation (TEM, Tecnai G2 Spirit).

For skin, TCCV treated skin samples (2 × 2 mm2) were excised and immediately immersed in 2.5 % glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h for fixation. Samples were then rinsed 3 times with buffer solution (0.1 M PBS) for 15 min each to remove residual glutaraldehyde. Samples were then transferred to 1 % osmium acid (OsO4) solution and fixed at 4 °C for 2 h. Then, the sample was immersed in 30 %, 50 %, 70 %, 90 %, and 100 % ethanol for 15 min, respectively, and soaked in acetone for 10 min, it was then immersed in the epoxy resin and acetone (1:1, mixed at room temperature) for 2 h, and immersed in the pure resin overnight. Finally, the tissue was placed in a mold, fresh resin was injected, polymerized at 60 °C for 48 h, cut into 70 nm sections by an ultra-thin slicer (Leica EM UC7, Germany), stained with a saturated ethanol solution of uranyl acetate for 30 min, stained with lead citrate for 10 min, and waited for observation (TEM, Tecnai G2 Spirit).

Drug loading capacity and encapsulation rate

The prepared vesicles were sonicated for 30 min to make cinnabar release, and the concentration of Hg in the supernatant was detected by Inductively Coupled Plasma (ICP) spectroscopy (PerkinElmer, USA), denoted as C1. Encapsulation Rate = (C1/C total) × 100 %, Drug Loading Capacity =(C1 × V)/(C vesicle × V vesicle) × 100 %.

To prepare the samples for testing, 1 mL supernatant or suspension was placed into a clean PTFE digestion tube, 2 mL hydrochloric acid and 6 mL nitric acid was added to the tube gradually. The inner and outer lids was screwed tightly and placed the digestion tube inside a microwave digestion oven. The digestion procedure included heating up time, control temperature and hold time, and the first steps are 10, 120 and 10 min, and the second steps are 10, 180 and 60 min, respectively. Then, the solution was cooled to room temperature. The digested solution was transferred from the tube into a volumetric flask and diluted it with ultrapure water up to the mark (50 mL), ensuring mixing thoroughly. Finally, this mixture was diluted by 100-fold. Detection of angle of repose and bulk density

Detection of angle of repose: Firstly, the funnel was fixed height of 5 cm, so that the cinnabar powder, as well as the lyophilized CV and TCCV powders, respectively, flowed out freely to form a cone-shaped accumulation on the horizontal substrate. Finally, the cone height (h) and bottom radius (r) are measured to calculate the angle of repose: tan θ = h/r.

Detection of bulk density: Firstly, 20 g cinnabar powder and lyophilized CV and TCCV powder were taken, respectively, slowly pour a graduated cylinder of known volume (V0) through a funnel (to avoid vibration). Finally, the surface was scraped and the powder mass (m) was weighed. The density of loose packing was calculated: ρ loose packing = m/V0.

Raman spectroscopy (RS) detection

The 5 mg sample was placed on a Raman optical detection platform (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), respectively, point scanning technology was used, and spectra was collected within the range of 400∼4000 nm−1; laser power of 10 mV; integral time: 5 s; excitation wavelength: 532 nm; X50 calcium fluoride eyepiece.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

The 5 mg sample was accurately weighed and placed it in an alumina crucible, respectively, to avoid over-accumulation and heat conduction. The TGA test (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was carried out under an inert gas (N2), the heating program (10 °C/min) is set to the 500 °C, and the quality change is recorded in real time.

Release and skin penetration tests of TCCV in vitro

Release tests: The in vitro release of Hg was studied by a Franz diffusion cell (LOGAN, USA). CV and TCCV suspension (500 μL, 100 mg/mL) were added to the donor compartment, respectively, with a cellulose membrane (MW 12,000, Membra-Cel®, Viskase, USA) sandwiched between the donor and receptor chambers. The temperature was maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C, 1 mL aliquots were withdrawn from the receptor chamber at 0, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h, and replaced with 1 mL fresh medium to maintain constant sink conditions. To analyze release of Hg, samples were prepared for Inductively Coupled Plasma detection as described in Table 1.

Skin penetration tests: Franz diffusion cells (LOGAN, USA) were used for in vitro skin penetration. Skin (from 4-week-old female Sprague-Dawley rats) was mounted between the donor and receptor chambers. CV (500 μL, 100 mg/mL) was applied to the donor compartment. The temperature was maintained at 32 ± 0.5 °C with constant stirring at 300 rpm using a magnetic stir bar. Each experiment was performed in triplicate. To analyze skin penetration of Hg, samples were prepared for ICP detection as described in Table 1.

Interaction of keratin and vesicles matrix

ATR-FITR detection: The FTIR spectrometer (Bruker Corporation, US) was used for infrared analysis of skin samples. Approximately 2 cm2 skin samples were rinsed with distilled water and dried. The treated skin samples were examined at transmission mode (wavenumber: 4000-400 cm−1). The working parameters: 4 cm−1, 32 scans, and each sample was analyzed in triplicate.

Molecular docking: The amino acid sequence of skin keratin (PDB ID: 8ZUG) was obtained from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB) website. The structural formulas of polyoxyl [34] stearate and cholesterol were generated using ChemDraw software. These 2D structures were then converted into optimized 3D molecular conformations using Chem3D. Keratins and small molecule compounds were exported, they were imported into the DeepMice website, respectively, and molecular docking model diagrams and scores were generated.

Cells experiments

Cells culture

The rat adrenal medullary chromaffin tumor cells (PC12) and intestinal goblet cells (GC) were derived from Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). The cells were maintained in DMEM medium (with 10 % FBS and 1 % Penicillin-Streptomycin solution) and cultured in a standard cell incubator at 37 °C with a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO2.

Cell viability assays

The CCK-8 was used to assess the effect of CV and TCCV on the proliferation of PC12 cells and intestinal goblet cells, respectively. They were reached 90 % density in the medium, then the cells were harvested and plated into a 96-well plate (5 × 103 cells/well). The cells were treated with CV/TCCV at concentrations (100 to 1000 μg/mL) after 24 h. Then, 10 μL CCK-8 solution was added after 24 h, and the cells were further incubated for 4 h. Subsequently, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured by an Infinite F50 absorbance microplate reader (TECAN, Austria).

Furthermore, the influence of CV and TCCV (200 μg/mL) on H2O2 (5 μg/mL)-induced PC12 cells were investigated. The cells were cultured in 96-well plates and the cells were treated with H2O2 (5 μg/mL), CV and TCCV (200 μg/mL), respectively, for an additional 24 h, the absorbance of the resulting solution was measured using an Infinite F50 absorbance microplate reader (TECAN, Austria).

Cellular uptake tests

To evaluate cellular internalization of CV and TCCV. They were first labeled with DiO and then centrifuged at 150,000 g to remove unbound dye. Following this, the DiO-CV and DiO-TCCV were incubated with PC12 and intestinal goblet cells (200 μg/mL), respectively, for various durations (2, 4, 8, 16, and 24 h). Then, the cells were washed with PBS and analyzed by flow cytometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Leica, Germany) to assess the impact of PC12 and intestinal goblet cells on the uptake of CV and TCCV, respectively.

Detection of cell apoptosis

The effects of CV and TCCV on apoptosis in PC12 cells were evaluated by a flow cytometric detection. PC12 cells were seeded into 6-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well). These cells were then cultured at 37 °C for 24 h, and the cells were treated with H2O2 (5 μg/mL) and CV/TCCV (100 μg/mL), respectively, at 37 °C for an additional 24 h. The PC12 cells were harvested by trypsin digestion, and centrifuged at 1, 200 g for 5 min. After the cells were washed 3 times with PBS at 4 °C, they were stained with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled Annexin V and propidium iodide (PI) for 15 min. Finally, the cells were undergone analysis immediately by flow cytometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Measurement of ROS

The effects of CV and TCCV (100 μg/mL) on the release of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in PC12 cells were investigated using a flow cytometric detection (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The cells were cultured in 6-well plates (5 × 105 cells/well), treated with H2O2 (5 μg/mL) and CV/TCCV (100 μg/mL), respectively, at 37 °C for an additional 24 h. Then, the cells were washed 3 times with PBS and stained with 10 μM DCFH-DA. The fluorescence intensity of the cells was measured by flow cytometric after an incubation period of 20 min.

Cell migration and invasion assays

To conduct the scratch assay, a smooth tip was used to create scratches between PC12 cells in each well of a 24-well plate (1 × 105 cells/well). The plate was then incubated for 24 h at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 conditions. Following a 24 h treatment with H2O2 (5 μg/mL), the PC12 cells were treated with CV/TCCV (100 μg/mL), respectively. Images of the cells migrating into the scratch gap were captured using a MShot Image Analysis System (TWS Technology, Guangzhou) and quantified using ImageJ software. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

For the cell invasion assay, modified Boyden chambers (Transwell®, Corning Incorporated, NY, USA) with 8-μm pore membrane filters coated with Matrigel were used in 24-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well), which were loaded into the upper chamber and incubated for 24 h. Following a 24 h treatment with H2O2 (5 μg/mL), and CV and TCCV (100 μg/mL), respectively. After incubation, the non-migratory cells on the upper surface of the membrane filter were gently removed, the migrating PC12 cells were fixed, and stained with a crystal violet solution (1 %) for 30 min. The displacement of migrating cells was determined microscopically by counting 3 random visual fields per well. Images were captured using the MShot Image Analysis System (TWS Technology, Guangzhou) and quantified using ImageJ software.

Indicators of oxidative stress and inflammatory factors

The PC12 cells were treated with CV and TCCV (100 μg/mL), respectively, after a 24 h treatment with H2O2 (5 μg/mL), the cells were digested with trypsin and centrifuged at 1, 200 rpm for 5 min. The cells supernatant was collected for detecting oxidative stress indicator (SOD, MDA, GSH, and LDH), as well as inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) were tested according to instructions of ELISA kits.

Animal experiments

Animals

C57BL/6 mice (SPF grade, female, 6-week-old, 20 ± 5 g) were purchased from Shanghai Slac Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The experimental procedures were carried out in strict accordance with the guidelines for animal experimentation of Zhejiang University and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care Committee of the College (Approval No. ZJU20170733).

Distribution tests experiments of TCCV

Qualitative tests: C57BL/6 mice were administered DiO-labeled CV/TCCV (100 μL, 100 mg/mL) orally or transdermally, respectively. The mice were sacrificed and the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and brain were extracted after 2, 6, 12, and 24 h. The in vivo imaging system (IVIS Spectrum, PerkinElmer, USA) was utilized to monitor the enrichment of CV/TCCV in different organs.

Quantitative tests: Thirty-six C57BL/6 mice were divided into 6 groups, with 6 mice in each group. The groups included a blank, Cin-or, TCCV-or, Cin-tr, TCCV-or, and TCCV-tr group. Each group was administered drugs (a dose of 500 mg/mL) daily for 3 d. Then, the mice were fasted for 12 h after 3 d, and were euthanized (cervical dislocation) and dissected. Finally, their hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, kidneys, large intestines, small intestines, brains, and blood were collected and homogenized. The Hg content was determined by Inductively Coupled Plasma (ICP).

Toxicity tests: Eighteen C57BL/6 mice were divided into 3 groups, with 6 mice in each group, included 1 d, 30 d, and 60 d group. Each group was administered drugs (a dose of 50 mg/mL) daily. Then, the mice were euthanized (cervical dislocation) and dissected. Finally, their hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, kidneys, large intestines, small intestines, brains, and blood were collected and homogenized. The Hg content was determined by ICP.

Animal model of sleep deprivation (SD)

Chronic restraint stress combined with intraperitoneal injection of p-chlorophenylalanine (PCPA) was employed to induce sleep deprivation (SD) in mice. A total of 42 mice were divided into 7 groups: a blank group (blank), a model group (model), and 5 treatment groups (Cin-or, TCCV-or, PV-tr, Cin-tr, and TCCV-tr). C57BL/6 mice were administered 100 mg/kg PCPA suspension (dissolved in physiological saline) by intraperitoneal injection from 1 to 6 d. Concurrently, a sleep deprivation apparatus was used to prevent sleep for 18 h/d (10 d), thereby establishing the SD model.

Administration

Cinnabar/CV/TCCV suspension with 50 mg/kg/d was administrated by gavage during 10–20 d, and the blank group was administrated with the same dose of PBS. After completion of the dosage regimen on the 20th day, the mice in all groups were fasted for 16 h.

Animal behavioral experiments

Forced swim test (FST): Mice were placed in a cylindrical container (34 cm diameter × 37 cm height) filled with 10 cm of water (25 °C) for 6 min. Immobility time (floating without escape attempts) was recorded during the first 4 min as an indicator of depressive-like behavior or sedation.

Tail suspension tests (TST): Mice were suspended by the tail (one-third from the base) on a custom stand for 6 min. Immobility time (complete relaxation without movement) was measured during the last 4 min.

Sleep-modulating synergy: Sleep latency (SL) and sleep time (ST) were assessed after intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium or chloral hydrate (33 mg/kg, the minimal sleep-inducing dose). Sleep onset was defined as loss of the righting reflex, and awakening as its recovery. SL was measured from injection to sleep onset; ST from sleep onset to awakening.

Weight and organs index

The body weights of the mice were measured. The mice were euthanized (cervical dislocation) and dissected, their hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, and kidneys were collected, and individually weighed. Subsequently, the organ index was calculated.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from brainstem, cerebral cortex, and hippocampal tissues using standard protocols. RNA samples meeting quality thresholds were converted into cDNA libraries and sequenced on Illumina platforms (Lianchuan Biotech, Hangzhou). Raw sequencing data quality was evaluated with FastQC (v0.11.8), followed by adapter trimming and quality filtering using FASTP (v0.23.1). Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome with STAR aligner (v2.7.0f), and gene expression levels were quantified using FeatureCounts (v1.6.3). Differential gene expression analysis (thresholds: |log2FC| ≥ 1, FDR <0.05) was performed with limma, and significantly dysregulated genes were subjected to functional Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis (p < 0.05).

After dehydration (95 % and 100 % ethanol) and clearing (xylene), sections were mounted with neutral resin for microscopic analysis.

In addition, the modified vesicles (TCCV) exhibited a new UV absorption peak at 280 nm (Fig. 2F) and an infrared (IR) detection peak at 1651 cm−1 (Fig. 2E), indicated presence of the C=O amide bonds, consistent with the existence of the grafted peptides that have been studied [35,36]. Furthermore, multiorgan index profiling revealed the distinctive anti-inflammatory capacity of transdermal cinnabar, with marked reductions in TNF-α (Fig. 7F1), IL-1β (Fig. 7F2), and IL-6 (Fig. 7F3) in the hepatic, renal, cerebral, and blood of SD mice. Collectively, these multimodal findings position transdermal cinnabar as a sophisticated administration method combining neuromodulation, organ protection, and systemic inflammation control.

TCCV's gut microbiota-5-HT axis: a novel pathway for insomnia relief

Recent studies have revealed the importance of gut microbiota in serving as a critical regulator of systemic homeostasis, emerging roles in neurological disorders [58,59], and sleep-related disorders through brain-gut axis crosstalk [60,61], while regulating brain activity [62,63]. To investigate the regulatory effect of TCCV-or on intestinal microbiota, the intestinal flora composition was analyzed by performing 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing in the fecal sample.

Bacterial species annotation data showed that sleep deprivation altered the abundance of gut microbes at the phylum level, particularly within the Bacteroidota phylum (Fig. 10A). Moreover, α-diversity analysis confirmed distinct differences in the abundance of microbial species between the cinnabar administration and the model groups (Fig. 10C). Notably, γ-Proteobacteria were prominently enriched (LEfSe and phylogenetic branch plot, Fig. 10H and I). These bacteria possess broad organic matter decomposition capabilities, generating bioactive compounds beneficial to human health. Their presence in the gut contributes to intestinal microenvironment stability. Functional profiling further demonstrated their potential for neuroactive metabolite biosynthesis.

As shown in Fig. 10J, within the range of proteobacteria, the most prominent effect was shown in bacterial genera such as γ-proteobacteria and Lactobacillus in the transdermal administration group. Crucially, these microorganisms demonstrated dual biosynthetic pathways: the production of 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) and glycine, both of which are implicated in brain-gut axis signaling (Fig. 10K) [66]. In addition, they were capable of generating barrier-protective short-chain fatty acids, which synergistically reinforced intestinal immunity [67]. The microbiota-derived 5-HT specifically engages activation of 5-HT1AR receptors, establishing a direct molecular interface between gut microbial ecology and central neuronal regulation. These findings mechanistically corroborate RNA-seq data, further implicating gut microbiota-generated 5-HT as a key regulator in gut-to-brain signaling, wherein Htr1d activation contributes to alleviate insomnia.

Biosafety evaluation of TCCV

TCCV relieves the PCPA-induced intestinal damage

Mercury absorption and its methylation/demethylation processes in the intestine allow both inorganic mercury (IHg) and methylmercury (MeHg) to induce intestinal toxicity, manifesting as microbiota dysbiosis, aberrant metabolite profiles, tight junction disruption, and immune activation [68,69]. Toxicity tests of TCCV

Furthermore, in order to further explore the safety of TCCV, we found that compared to oral administration of cinnabar (Cinnabar-or), at the same dosage (50 mg/mL/d), after 1, 30, and 60 d, the transdermal administration mediated by TCCV (TCCV-tr) resulted in lower Hg accumulation in the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, small intestine, large intestine, brain, and blood. However, due to the presence of the intestinal-targeting peptide (CSK) in TCCV, although the overall Hg accumulation of small intestine was lower than in the Cinnabar-or group, the Hg accumulation multiples at 30 and 60 d were higher than those in the Cinnabar-or group (Table S2).

Specifically, in the oral administration of cinnabar (Cinnabar-or), Hg accumulation in all organs increased significantly over time (1d→60d), reaching 5.4- to 30-fold by day 60 (with the highest accumulation observed in brain tissue). The kidneys, liver, and brain showed the most pronounced accumulation (reaching 28.77 μg/g, 15.20 μg/g, and 1.20 μg/g at 60 days, respectively). This suggests that orally administered cinnabar is likely absorbed through the digestive tract, widely distributed via systemic circulation, and subsequently accumulates long-term in metabolic/excretory organs (liver and kidneys) and neural tissues (brain). In contrast, transdermal administration of TCCV (TCCV-tr) resulted in significantly lower Hg accumulation compared to the oral group (p < 0.05), with only 1.8- to 20.6-fold accumulation by day 60. The accumulation multiples in the liver, kidneys, and brain were 10.4 × , 18.5 × , and 14.0 × , respectively - significantly lower than the oral group's 21.7 × , 23.5 × , and 30.0 × . The study demonstrate that transdermal administration effectively reduces systemic mercury exposure. These findings suggest that TCCV-mediated transdermal delivery significantly suppresses Hg accumulation, supporting its potential for clinical applications.

Discussion

Conventional drug delivery methods face inherent limitations, systemic administration suffers from metabolic clearance rapidly and dose-dependent toxicity [74,75]. Therefore, to leverage potential of TDDS and avoid these problems, we developed a multifunctional nanoplatform wrapped in cinnabar (TAT/CSK-cinnabar vesicles, TCCV), which is modified with TAT and CSK peptides. This platform enables programmed transdermal delivery, penetrating lipid bilayer barriers to achieve precise accumulation in the intestine. The nanoplatform exhibited robust encapsulation efficiency and drug loading capacity (Fig. 2G1, 2G2). In vitro assays revealed that TAT and CSK synergistically enhanced intestine goblet cells (GC) targeting, increasing cellular uptake by 1.69-fold (Fig. 3E1-3E3). In vivo studies demonstrated 15-fold cinnabar accumulation of intestine at 24 h post-administration, coupled with over 50 % reduction in systemic organ retention (brain, liver, kidneys) (Fig. 4). Given the intestine's role as a barrier and Hg's propensity for intestinal methylation [76], this targeted approach aligns with emerging peptide-based strategies in oncology [77] and atherosclerosis [78,79].

In addition, TCCV alleviates insomnia via “gut-brain axis”. Gut microbiota-derived 5-HT (Fig. 10), glutamic acid, dopamine, and GABA [[80], [81]] enter systemic circulation through intestinal epithelia, via the enteric nervous system (ENS) [82,[83], [84]], influencing Central Nervous System (CNS) activity (Fig. 11). While cinnabar's intestinal interactions remain underexplored, its potential to modulate the “gut-brain axis” offers transformative insights. Notably, cinnabar's therapeutic scope extends to metabolic disorders (obesity, gout) [[85], [86], [87], [88], [89]], genetic syndromes (Prader-Willi) [90], and skeletal pathologies [91,92], positioning it as a multitarget agent for systemic conditions. Moreover, gut dysbiosis can trigger neuroinflammation, disrupt blood-brain barrier integrity, and promote pathological protein aggregation (α-synuclein, β-amyloid), this mechanisms implicated in Parkinson's and Alzheimer's diseases [93,94].

In summary, the TDDS nanoplatform (TCCV) based on targeted modifications (TAT/CSK) represents a paradigm shift in cinnabar-based therapeutics. TCCV shows significant research potential and clinical promise. With advancements in precision medicine, personalized adjustments to the composition of TCCV and peptide selection, based on patient-specific conditions and biomarkers, could further enhance therapeutic outcomes. This innovative drug platform and targeted therapy also have considerable potential in treating neurological diseases, such as insomnia. Future studies could explore combining this nanoplatform with other treatments, like chemotherapy or immunotherapy, to achieve synergistic multi-target effects. Translating these findings into clinical applications will require well-designed trials to assess efficacy and safety in real patient populations.

Conclusion

We developed a transdermal and intestinal targeting nanoplatform (TAT/CSK-cinnabar vesicles, TCCV) for the programmed delivery of cinnabar to reduce Hg2+ accumulation in organs. We found that TCCV regulates the production of serotonin (5-HT) by gut microbiome, and activates Htr1d-cAMP pathway through the "brain-gut axis" to alleviate insomnia. This nanoplatform offers a novel therapeutic strategy for insomnia, with broader implications for optimizing the efficacy and biosafety of cinnabar.
