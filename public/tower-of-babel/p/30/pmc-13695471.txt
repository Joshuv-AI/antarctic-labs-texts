A 23‐year‐old female was diagnosed with Ebstein‐Barr Virus (EBV) encephalitis during infectious mononucleosis. Non‐convulsive status epilepticus (NCSE) was suspected due to the presence of fluctuating aphasia. EEG findings were supported by corresponding MRI hyperperfusion.

She presented to our hospital after 5 days of severe headache, associated with neck pain, nausea and repeated vomiting. Ten days prior, she developed fever and chills. Her previous history was notable for tonsillectomy following tonsillitis 2 years earlier and adjustment disorder with depression and anxiety. Ongoing treatment included mirtazapine and combined oral contraception. Neurological evaluation was unremarkable, whereas general physical examination revealed splenomegaly. Blood tests, PCR testing for influenza A, influenza B, RSV and SARS‐CoV‐2 and a mononucleosis rapid test were performed; she was diagnosed with infectious mononucleosis and discharged. On the following evening, however, she developed aphasia with paraphasias and anomias and difficulties in speech comprehension, for which she was readmitted. She was awake, but unable to answer questions appropriately or follow commands. The neurological examination was otherwise unremarkable. Neck stiffness was absent. CSF analysis revealed white blood cell count elevation (41/μL, normal range 0–4), mainly lymphocytes and monocytes, elevated protein levels (120 mg/dL, normal range 3–50), elevated lactate (3.0 mmol/L, normal < 2.2), and normal glucose levels. EBV‐DNA was detected on CSF (4086 UI/mL), while tests for other infectious and toxicological agents were negative. EBV primary infection was confirmed by serum analysis: EBV VCA‐IgG and IgM positivity; EBNA‐1‐IgG negativity; EBV‐DNA detection (1099 IU/mL). The patient was therefore diagnosed with EBV encephalitis. Intravenous aciclovir 750 mg every 8 h had been empirically started at admission.

EEG showed intermittent slowing of the background rhythm on the left hemisphere, with superimposed frequent runs, lasting 6–12 s, of lateralized rhythmic delta activity fluctuating between 2.5 and 5 Hz in the left temporal region. On some occasions, the rhythmic activity showed spatial evolution to the contralateral electrodes (Figure 1A). Considering the diagnosis of aphasic NCSE, 1 intravenous levetiracetam (2000 mg) was administered, followed by maintenance with levetiracetam 750 mg twice a day. A brain MRI – including T1‐weighted, T2‐weighted, FLAIR, DWI, SWI and arterial spin labeling (ASL) images – performed 3 h later detected increased perfusion in the left lateral temporoparietal region (Figure 1B,C), without any abnormality in FLAIR, DWI, or SWI. The finding was compatible with peri‐ictal MRI abnormalities (PMA) – which include hyperperfusion, FLAIR hyperintensity and DWI abnormalities and manifest in approximately 50% of patients with SE during the first 48 hours. 2 The following day, the EEG showed frequent intermittent rhythmic delta‐theta groups in the left middle‐to‐posterior temporal region, no longer presenting frequency fluctuations or spatial evolution. Based on this EEG finding and the resolution of aphasia, NCSE was deemed to be terminated.

(A) EEG performed on 2nd day of admission. It shows slowing of the background rhythm on the left hemisphere, together with superimposed runs of rhythmic delta activity (underlined) in the left temporal region. On some occasions the rhythmic activity showed spatial evolution, spreading to the contralateral electrodes (circled). The EEG pattern, anatomically consistent with the aphasia, was interpreted as Non‐Convulsive Status Epilepticus originating in the left temporal lobe. (B and C) perfusion‐MRI (arterial spin labeling) performed 3 hours after the above EEG showed increased perfusion in the left lateral temporoparietal region (circled).

Seven days after admission, she again experienced fluctuating aphasia. She was oriented and correctly performed commands, but occasionally provided incongruous answers with phonemic paraphasia and neologisms. The EEG revealed rhythmic 1.5 Hz activity over the left centrotemporal region and superimposed sharp waves (Figure S1A). The electrographic pattern was compatible with an ictal‐interictal continuum (IIC). 1 The patient received 1 mg of lorazepam, followed by 200 mg of intravenous lacosamide. The therapeutic trial achieved EEG improvement and resolution of the aphasia, leading to a diagnosis of electroclinical aphasic NCSE originating from the left temporal lobe. Levetiracetam 1000 mg and lacosamide 100 mg twice daily were prescribed. MRI (limited to DWI and ASL due to the patient's lack of tolerance) was repeated later the same day. Hyperperfusion in the left temporo‐lateral region persisted, with further extension to the left temporo‐dorsal, insular and fronto‐opercular regions (Figure S1B,C). No abnormalities were detected on DWI. Continuous EEG monitoring during the following 24 h showed symmetric slow background activity at 6–7 Hz, with intermittent delta‐theta activity in the left fronto‐temporal region, but no IIC pattern. Neuropsychological testing performed during the hospital stay revealed mild psychomotor slowing, with an otherwise age‐appropriate profile. During the hospital stay, a bilateral abducens nerve palsy with diplopia emerged, which had fully resolved at the follow‐up visit 6 weeks later, at which time she was clinically asymptomatic. Follow‐up MRI performed 3 weeks later did not show any perfusion nor structural abnormality.

EBV encephalitis is an uncommon manifestation of EBV infection. 3 , 4 To our knowledge, only six cases of SE in the context of confirmed EBV encephalitis have been reported: three adults, 5 , 6 , 7 one adolescent 8 and two children. 9 , 10 Only one report documented hyperperfusion—in the right temporal region, with ASL‐MRI and SPECT 7 —compatible with peri‐ictal MRI abnormalities. 2 In our case, NCSE was diagnosed based on concordance between symptom, EEG pattern and electroclinical response to antiseizure medications and further supported by anatomically concordant hyperperfusion in the left temporal cortex.
