Diversity and antimicrobial resistance among bacterial isolates from finfish aquaculture in Thailand
Authors: Partho Pratim Debnath, Putita Chokmangmeepisarn, Athina Papadopoulou, Nicola M Coyle, Craig Baker-Austin, Ronny van Aerle, David Bass, Charles R Tyler, Channarong Rodkhum
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobal aquaculture, has expanded rapidly, especially in Southeast Asia, with Thailand emerging as a leading producer. However, the sector faces economic losses from disease outbreaks and has problems with escalating antimicrobial resistance (AMR).ResultsThis study examined bacterial diversity and AMR in 695 moribund fish samples collected from regions across Thailand between 2018 and 2024, and spanning eight key finfish aquaculture species such as tilapia, Asian sea bass, snakeskin gourami, snakehead, walking catfish and carp species. Gram-negative bacteria (86.62% of isolates) were dominated by Vibrionaceae and Aeromonadaceae, while Gram-positive bacteria were primarily from Streptococcaceae, with notable species including A. veronii, V. vulnificus, S. agalactiae and S. suis. Antibiotic susceptibility testing was conducted with 29 antibiotics across nine different classes and the isolates were classified as wild-type (WT) or non-wild-type (NWT) based on their inhibition zone diameters. The results indicated high resistance levels, particularly against metronidazole, streptomycin, clindamycin, sulfonamide, and kanamycin. Multidrug resistance (MDR) was notably high in Aeromonas and Vibrio species. Antimicrobial resistance gene (ARG) analysis showed a high prevalence of beta-lactam, tetracycline, and fluoroquinolone resistance in Gram-negative bacteria, and resistance to beta-lactams, macrolides, fluoroquinolones, and peptides in Gram-positive bacteria. Antibiotic efflux was the predominant putatively detected resistance mechanism, accounting for 50-60% of ARGs. Identification of unique resistance gene families in Aeromonas spp. and V. vulnificus, including the SMR efflux pump and OXA beta-lactamase, emphasizes the adaptive strategies of these bacteria and the discovery of host-specific resistance mechanisms in S. suis, such as nutrient acquisition pathways, underscores the challenges of controlling and managing AMR in aquaculture systems. Mobile genetic elements (MGEs), particularly IS elements, were found to be widespread in all species, underscoring the significant role of horizontal gene transfer in the dissemination of resistance.ConclusionsThis study advocates for enhanced AMR surveillance, responsible antibiotic use, and species-specific monitoring to safeguard aquaculture and public health utilizing the one health approach.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-04942-2.Keywords: Aquaculture, Antimicrobial resistant, Multidrug resistant, Antimicrobial susceptibility test, One health approach

Background

Global aquaculture experienced significant growth from 2005 to 2019, with an average increase in production of approximately 4.35 million metric tons per year, equating to an annual growth rate of 5.21% [1]. Southeast Asia, including Indonesia, Vietnam, Thailand, and Malaysia, contribute around 21% of the global aquaculture production [2]. In this region aquaculture plays a crucial role in protein provision, enhancing food security, supporting livelihoods, generating income and employment, and driving economic growth. Thailand produces and exports both freshwater and marine fish species [3] and in 2020, Thailand’s freshwater fish production was 471,238 tons, generating revenue of USD 802 million [4]. Tilapia (Oreochromis sp.), walking catfish (Clarias macrocephalus), Asian sea bass (Lates calcarifer), and snakehead (Channa striata) accounted for 80% of this production. Most of the freshwater fish produced is destined for human consumption, and many small-scale farmers depend on fish farming as a primary source of income [5].

However, disease is a major threat to profitable aquaculture and can result in high mortality rates [6]. The high stocking density and stressful conditions in aquaculture make fish more susceptible to bacterial infections [7, 8]. According to the Department of Fisheries (DoF), economic losses due to diseases in aquaculture species in Thailand amount to approximately USD 300 million annually [5]. Several bacterial diseases have been reported in Thailand’s aquaculture sector, including Motile Aeromonas Septicemia (MAS) caused by Aeromonas spp. including A. hydrophila, A. sobria, and A. jandaei [4]. Species including Vibrio vulnificus, V. parahaemolyticus, and V. alginolyticus are responsible for vibriosis, a significant disease affecting various aquaculture species [9]. Streptococcosis is another critical disease caused by S. agalactiae, S. iniae that hampers the development and progress of aquaculture in Thailand [10]. Infections caused by Francisella spp. and columnaris disease have also been reported in several aquaculture species [11, 12]. To combat these disease outbreaks, farmers often rely on the prophylactic and therapeutic use of antimicrobials. In Thailand, 14 antibiotic compounds were recently identified in aquaculture, a similar number (13) reported to be used between 1990 and 2007. Even though eight antibiotics (amoxicillin, enrofloxacin, trimethoprim, ormethoprim, oxolinic acid, sulfadiazine, sulfadimethoxine, sulfamonothoxine) are officially permitted by the Thai FDA. Despite a ban on the use of certain antibiotics by the DoF in 2002, unauthorized use—such as penicillin in Nile tilapia farming—has still been reported [13].

The widespread occurrence of bacterial infections in farmed fish has led to the frequent use of antibiotics [14]. The overuse and improper application of antibiotics are considered primary factors driving the high levels of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) now observed widely in aquaculture [14–16]. A large portion of the antibiotics used in animals and humans are excreted to the environment unchanged generating antibiotic residues that can contribute to AMR [17–19] The increasing resistance of foodborne pathogens in aquaculture to critical antimicrobials is especially concerning [20]. Through horizontal gene transfer, AMR in aquaculture can spread to environmental bacterial strains, impacting the broader ecosystem [21] and as a result, many farmed fish are likely infected with pathogens resistant to multiple antibiotics [22, 23]. The declining effectiveness of antibiotics in treating aquaculture infections due to AMR poses a growing global health threat [24]. Isolates with a multiple antibiotic resistance (MAR) index greater than 0.20 are considered high-risk and warrant proper attention with research in Southeast Asian countries has shown a high incidence of MAR, with 74.7% of bacterial isolates exhibiting MAR values above 0.2 [25, 26]. Bacteria such as Aeromonas spp., Escherichia coli, Salmonella spp., and Vibrio spp. have been found resistant to three or more classes of antibiotics, qualifying them as multidrug-resistant strains [25, 26]. These resistant organisms can persist and spread to other environmental matrices, posing an escalating threat to human and others animal health [27, 28]. Recognizing food animals as key reservoirs for the development and dissemination of AMR, several countries, including those in Europe, the United States, Mexico, and China, have implemented varying degrees of restrictions on antibiotic use in aquaculture, along with surveillance systems to monitor AMR [29]. However, in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), initiatives to track antimicrobial use in aquaculture are limited [30–32], complicating efforts to assess their role in fostering AMR due to inadequate surveillance.

The prevalence and trends of AMR in Thai aquaculture remain largely uncharted, with existing data on AMR being notably limited. Most available information is fragmented, with only a few reports on individual bacterial infections and minimal insights into the broader context of bacterial infections, antibiotic contamination, and AMR patterns in aquatic environments. Given Thailand’s significant role in global aquaculture and its wide ranging geographic and aquaculture conditions, there is an urgent need for a comprehensive study on the occurrence and development of AMR in Thai aquaculture to inform, as and where required, on possible AMR mitigation strategies. This study aims to document the distribution of bacterial infections in key aquaculture species and assess their antibiotic susceptibility profiles, with the goal of contributing to a better understanding of AMR patterns that could support future risk assessments and inform discussions around mitigation strategies where appropriate.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

A total of 695 samples from eight commercially important cultured fish species, diseased and moribund, were collected or submitted by farm owners from sites across Thailand to the Center of Excellence in Fish Infectious Diseases (CE FID) research unit between 2018 and 2024 (Supplementary Table S1). A total of 662 of these samples were derived from 25 provinces across Thailand, while 33 samples were submitted by farmers who did not disclose their locations (Fig. 1). Both diseased and moribund fish from various provinces were retrieved and transported alive in tanks to the laboratory, where necropsy and bacterial isolation were performed on the same day. Prior to necropsy, the fish were euthanized using clove oil (1 g/L) [33], and dissections were carried out under sterile conditions. Body tissue samples were collected from the liver, kidney, spleen, and brain of all fish and steaked onto culture media.

Bacterial isolation and identification

Bacterial isolates were obtained from goldfish (Carassius auratus) (n = 12), koi carp (Cyprinus rubrofuscus) (n = 19), Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) (n = 8), red tilapia (Oreochromis spp.) (n = 185), Asian sea bass (n = 364), snakehead fish (n = 48), snakeskin gourami (Trichopodus pectoralis) (n = 31), and walking catfish (n = 28). Bacterial isolation was performed by streaking tissues collected from fish with clinical signs. Samples, including the liver, spleen, kidney, and brain, were aseptically obtained and streaked onto tryptic soy agar (TSA) (Bacto™) supplemented with 5% sheep blood, specifically for freshwater species. For marine species, thiosulfate citrate bile salts sucrose agar (TCBS) (Difco™, New Jersey, USA ) was additionally used along with TSA supplemented with 2% NaCl. The bacterial culture plates were incubated at 28 °C for 24 h [4, 9], and bacterial colonies were observed. The obtained colonies were differentiated based on colony morphology and colour (e.g., green, yellow, yellow-greenish) for further identification and purification. Suspected bacterial colonies resembling Aeromonas spp., which produced dominant white colonies with hemolysis, were picked and subcultured on selective Rimler Shotts (RS) medium (HIMEDIA, India). Pure yellow colonies from RS medium were then streaked onto TSA for bacterial isolation and incubated at 28 °C for 24 h. For Vibrio spp., the pure colony was picked and subcultured on TCBS selective media and incubated at 28 °C for 24 h. Flavobacterium spp. were isolated using the selective Anacker and Ordal (AO) medium (Bacto™, USA), with incubation at 28 °C for 48 h. The pure isolated bacteria were subsequently stored at − 80 °C in 20% (v/v) glycerol for further investigation. The primary identification of pure bacterial isolates was conducted using the Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometer (MALDI– TOF MS) Biotyper system v3.1 software and library, which analyzed spectra with mass-to-charge ratios (m/z) between 2000 and 20,000, adhering to the standard protocol [34]. The Bruker database was employed for the MALDI-TOF MS analysis in this study. Our study also identified Flavobacterium spp, a brief summary for which results are provided in Sect. Bacterial diversity, with a full dataset available in Supplementary Table S5. The Flavobacterium spp. data are excluded from the analysis as they are presented in another manuscript which is currently under preparation for submission.

Molecular-level species confirmation

The dominant species identified through MALDI-TOF MS screening were selected for species-specific identification and a total of 272 Vibrio spp., 222 Aeromonas spp., 52 Streptococcus spp., and 35 Photobacterium spp. isolates were confirmed through PCR and sequence analysis. Genomic DNA was extracted from the major bacterial isolates using Nucleospin® Tissue genomic DNA extraction kit (Macherey-Nagel, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The quality and quantity of the extracted DNA were evaluated using a nano spectrophotometer (Nanodrop 1000, Thermo Scientific). All samples had a purity ratio (260/280) of approximately 1.8, indicating high-quality DNA. The DNA was then stored at − 20 °C until further use.

Details regarding the primers, PCR conditions, and specific methods used for each species are provided in Supplementary Table S2. The negative control for PCR consisted of a GoTaq® Green Mastermix (Promega) with ultrapure water, while the positive control used a PCR cocktail containing mixed DNA for multiplex PCR and single DNA for conventional PCR. The bacterial DNA used as a positive control was obtained from the CE FID collection, which had been previously confirmed by sequencing. PCR products were detected by 1.5% (w/v) agarose gel electrophoresis (Applichem, Germany, GmbH) run for 45 min at 100 V in Tris-Borate-EDTA (TBE) buffer and visualized under UV light using RedSafe™ (Intron, Gyeonggi-do, Korea). Identification of Aeromonas spp. at the species level was conducted based on gyrB sequencing and phylogenetic analysis, as described in our previous studies [4, 35].

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The antimicrobial susceptibility test (AST) was conducted for the bacterial species as shown in Table 2 using a disc diffusion assay following the guidelines of the Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute [36, 37]. Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 served as the quality control strain. A total of 29 antimicrobials (Oxoid™, Basingstoke, United Kingdom) from nine classes, considered critical for both aquaculture and human medicine, were tested (Supplementary Table S3). The disc diffusion method was performed on standard Mueller–Hinton agar medium (MHA) (with 1% NaCl supplementation for Vibrio spp.). The bacterial suspension was standardized to a 0.5 McFarland turbidity from a single pure colony of a fresh culture. The inoculum was evenly spread onto MHA plates using a sterile spreader. Antimicrobial discs were then placed on the MHA surface, followed by their incubation at 28 °C for 24 h. Moreover, the broth microdilution method was applied specially for a susceptibility test for colistin sulphate (CT), as described in our previous study [21]. Due to the lack of interpretive criteria for aquaculture related bacterial species, the wild type cutoff values (COwt) for categorizing wild-type (WT) and non-wild-type (NWT) isolates of Aeromonas spp. against ceftaxidime (CAZ), cefotaxime (CTX), cefotaxime + clavulanic acid (CTX + CLA), gentamicin (CN), oxolinic acid (OA), oxytetracycline (OT), tetracycline (TE), chloramphenicol (C), florfenicol (FFC), and trimethoprim/Sulfamethoxazole (SXT) were determined using reference zone diameter values from published studies [38–40]. Similarly, COwt values for Vibrio spp. against Amikacin (AK), Streptomycin (S), CN, CAZ, cephalexin (CL), ciprofloxacin (CIP), enrofloxacin (ENR), OA, TE, FFC, and SXT were derived from reference values in previous publications [41–46]. Additionally, COwt values were determined for Streptococcus spp. and Lactococcus spp. against amoxicillin (AML), amoxicillin + clavulanic acid (AMC), Penicilin G (P), ampicillin (AMP), CN, CTX, ceftiofur (EFT), ENR, norfloxacin (NOR), OT, doxycycline (DO), TE, C, FFC, and clindamycin (DA); for Edwardsiella spp. against AML, P, CN, ENR, NOR, OA, OT, C, FFC, SXT, and sulfonamide (S3); and for Pseudomonas spp. against CN, CAZ, cefepime (FEP), CIP, OT, and C using referenced values from published studies [42, 43, 47–50]. Provisional COwt for the rest of the tested antimicrobials for Aeromonas spp., Vibrio spp. and other bacterial species were determined using the normalized resistance interpretation (NRI) methodology [51, 52]. The NRI analysis was conducted using a free automatic Excel tool available at http://www.bioscand.se/nri/. When the number of observations was too small (fewer than 15 isolates) and no established ECVs-COwt values were available for specific species-antibiotic combinations—such as Aeromonas spp. against EFT, AK, NOR, DA, and CT, or Vibrio spp. against EFT and DO—the isolates were categorized as NWT solely based on the absence of an inhibition zone in the disc diffusion test. The definition of WT and NWT isolates were used as defined by [53]: a microorganism is classified as WT for a species if it lacks acquired or mutational resistance mechanisms to the drug in question. Conversely, it is classified as NWT if it possesses an acquired or mutational resistance mechanism to the drug. The MAR indexing is a valuable tool for aiding in the identification of high-risk sources or environmental contamination for risk assessment. Resistome analysis

Selection of bacterial isolates and whole genome sequencing (WGS)

A total of 26 bacterial isolates, representing nine species of both Gram-negative and Gram-positive fish pathogens, were selected for whole-genome sequencing (WGS) considering their high antibiotic resistant profiles in phenotypic AMR analysis. This included seven isolates from previous studies and 19 from this study (Supplementary Table S4). The genomic DNA was extracted using Nucleospin® Tissue genomic DNA extraction kit (Macherey-Nagel, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The DNA concentration was determined using Qubit™ 4 Fluorometer (Invitrogen™, Singapore) prior to submission to a sequencing service where they were sequenced using Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform with 150 paired-end mode (GENEWIZ Biotechnology, Suzhou, China). Sequencing libraries were prepared using NEBNext® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, Massachusetts, USA).

Genome assembly and annotation

Raw sequence reads were filtered and trimmed to remove low-quality reads (Q < 20) and adapter sequences using Trimmomatic v0.39 [55]. The clean reads were then assembled de novo using SPAdes v4.0.0 [56], and contigs shorter than 200 bp were excluded. Draft genomes were annotated using Prokka v1.14.5 [57] and deposited in the NCBI Genbank database (Supplementary Table S4).

Whole genome-based identification

Pairwise average nucleotide identity (ANI) values were determined based on MUMer software implemented in JSpeciesWS web server (https://jspecies.ribohost.com/jspeciesws) [58]. Digital DNA-DNA hybridization (dDDH) values were calculated using genome-to-genome distance calculation (fomula d6) in the Type Strain Genome Server web server (https://tygs.dsmz.de). A maximum likelihood trees were constrcted based on WGS using the Genome BLAST Distance Phylogeny (GBDP) distances approach on the TYGS web server [59].

Antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) and mobile genetic elements (MGEs) prediction

Antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) and chromosomal point mutations were identified using the Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI) tool from the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD), applying perfect, strict, and loose criteria [60] (accessed in September 2024). Additionally, ARGs and chromosomal point mutations were analyzed with ResFinder v4.6.0 (accessed in September 2024), using a threshold of 80% nucleotide identity and 60% nucleotide coverage [61, 62]. ARGs identified using the Perfect and Strict hit criteria in the CARD database, along with those predicted by ResFinder, were analyzed and compared to ensure the reliability and accuracy of potential ARG predictions. Mobile genetic elements (MGEs) were identified using MobileElementFinder v1.1.2 (accessed in September 2024) [63].

Statistical analysis

Data processing and visualization were carried out using Excel MS Office 2019 and Rstudio v4.2.2 [64]. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics 22). To avoid small group sizes in the analyses, bacterial genera with fewer than five occurrences were grouped together as ‘others’. Similarly, bacterial genera included in the AST analysis were only those with more than one isolate tested; genera with only a single isolate were excluded from the analysis such as Acinetobacter, Exiguobacterium and Micrococcus. The Pearson Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were used to assess statistical differences between bacterial species that were reported as WT and NWT. The Kruskal-Wallis, Mann-Whitney U test, and Fisher’s exact test were used to assess statistical differences between bacterial species and MAR values. A p-value of 0.05 was used as the criterion to determine whether the observed differences were statistically significant.

Results

Host species and gross clinical signs

A total of 695 fish samples were collected between 2018 and 2024, with the majority of samples, (52.37%, n = 364) obtained from Asian sea bass, followed by red tilapia ( 26.62%, n = 185), and with the lowest number of samples, (1.15%, n = 8) collected from Nile tilapia (Supplementary Table S1). In terms of yearly collection, the highest number of samples, 33.38% (n = 232), was collected in 2021, with 23.45% (n = 163) in 2019, 14.82% (n = 103) in 2018, and 2.88% (n = 20) in 2024 (Supplementary Table S1). Regarding sampling locations, Asian sea bass samples were collected from the highest number of provinces, totaling 12, followed by red tilapia from seven provinces (Fig. 1). Statistical analysis revealed that the number of host species differed significantly by year (p < 0.001) and collection location (p < 0.001).

Infected fish exhibited lethargy, pale gills, they were gasping at the water surface, and carried skin hemorrhages, skin discoloration, ulcers, gill necrosis, and/or erosion of the fins and tail. In addition to these common signs, some cases were presented with more specific signs such as exophthalmia, cloudy or sunken eyes, abdominal distension, scale detachment, deep ulcerative lesions, muscle necrosis, and saddleback lesions. Gross pathological examination of internal organs revealed enlargement of the liver, spleen, and kidney, as well as brittle liver, multifocal hemorrhages. Necrotic foci in the liver were commonly found while more distinct lesions such as white nodules in the liver and spleen were more occasional. Specific signs included exophthalmia, scale drop and muscle necrosis in Asian sea bass, white nodules in striped snakehead, deep ulcers in walking catfish, hemorrhagic operculum with scale loss in Nile tilapia, and enlarged, hemorrhagic liver in red tilapia.

Bacterial diversity

The Gram staining of 695 bacterial isolates showed that 86.6% (n = 602) were Gram-negative, while 13.4% (n = 93) were Gram-positive. In total, 83 bacterial species from 31 genera and 19 families were identified from the fish samples collected by MALDI-TOF MS, PCR, and WGS. For results obtained from MALDI-TOF MS, the identification results were reported at the genus or species level according to the confidence level. High-confidence identification, with scores from 2.00 to 3.00, indicates reliable identification at the species level. Low-confidence identification, with scores from 1.70 to 1.99, is accepted at the genus level. Scores below 1.70 are considered unreliable for identification at any level (Supplementary Table S8). The Vibrionaceae family was the most abundant, comprising 44.2% (n = 307) of the isolates, followed by Aeromonadaceae at 32.2% (n = 224) and Streptococcaceae at 8.5% (n = 59) (Table 1). Of the 31 bacterial genera identified, Vibrio was the most prevalent, representing 39.1% (n = 272) of isolates, followed by Aeromonas (32.2%, n = 224) and Streptococcus (7.5%, n = 52). The Bacillaceae family showed the highest diversity with six genera, and the Enterobacteriaceae contained five genera (Table 1). Regarding species, Vibrio had the greatest diversity with 20 species, followed by Aeromonas (nine species), Pseudomonas (six species), and Staphylococcus (five species). The most frequently identified species was Aeromonas veronii, accounting for 20.7% (n = 144) of isolates, followed by Vibrio harveyi (9.5%, n = 66), Vibrio spp. (7.8%, n = 52), Vibrio vulnificus (5.3%, n = 37), Photobacterium damselae (5.0%, n = 35), Streptococcus agalactiae (4.3%, n = 30), and Aeromonas jandaei (4.6%, n = 32) (Table 1). Additionally, 94 Flavobacterium spp. isolates were identified, comprising 42 isolates of F. oreochromis, nine isolates of F. covae, one isolate of F. davisii, and 42 unidentified Flavobacterium spp. (Supplementary Table S5).

Analysis over the sample collection years revealed the highest bacterial diversity in 2018 and 2019, with 12 genera identified each year. The largest number of isolates was recorded in 2021 (n = 232), followed by 163 isolates in 2019 and 103 in 2018 (Fig. 2). From 2018 to 2021, Aeromonas and Vibrio were the most frequently identified genera, with a maximum of 81 Aeromonas isolates in 2019 and 171 Vibrio isolates in 2021. In 2022, Streptococcus predominated for 26 isolates. Nevertheless, Aeromonas was consistently found each year (Fig. 2).

Annual distribution and diversity of bacterial genera collected from diseased fish from 2018 to 2024. Number of bacterial isolates are represented according to the green colour gradient legend shown in the right-hand panel

Seasonally, bacterial abundance was highest in June (20.58%, n = 143), followed by March (20.29%, n = 141) and October (10.79%, n = 75) (Fig. 2). Notably, no samples were collected or received in November across the six years. Aeromonas was present in all months except January, with its highest occurrence in July (n = 59) and lowest in April (n = 7). Vibrio was most frequently identified in January (n = 21), February (n = 55), March (n = 84), and June (n = 102). Streptococcus appeared between May and October, peaking in August (n = 15), with additional peaks in July and October (n = 8 each).

Geographically, the highest bacterial diversity was seen in the Kanchanaburi province, with ten genera identified, followed by Chonburi and Samut-Prakan, each with eight genera (Fig. 3). Samples from Kanchanaburi also recorded the most bacterial isolates (15.7%, n = 109), followed by Phang-Nga (7.3%, n = 51), Phuket (7.3%, n = 51), Samut Songkhram (6.8%, n = 47), Chonburi (6.1%, n = 42), and Nakhon Pathom (6.1%, n = 42). Aeromonas was found in 17 provinces, Vibrio in 16, and Streptococcus in eight (Fig. 3).

Geographic distribution and bacterial diversity identified in aquaculture species

In terms of host-bacterial diversity and distribution, red tilapia and Asian sea bass showed the highest bacterial diversity, each with 12 genera, and snakehead fish had the lowest with only two genera (Fig. 4). Aeromonas was present in all eight host species, while Vibrio and Streptococcus were found in six species. Aeromonas was most frequently isolated from red tilapia (16.0%, n = 111), followed by snakehead fish (5.8%, n = 40) and Asian sea bass (5.2%, n = 36). Vibrio was predominantly found in Asian sea bass (38.1%, n = 265), while Streptococcus was most common in snakeskin gourami (3.9%, n = 27), followed by red tilapia (1.7%, n = 12) and snakehead fish (1.2%, n = 8) (Fig. 4). Statistical analysis showed a highly significant difference (p < 0.001) in bacterial abundance and diversity across year of collection, season, location, and host species.

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) pattern

In this study, AST was performed using 29 antibiotics, with 21 specifically tested on Gram-positive bacteria. All Gram-positive isolates (100%) exhibited NWT to CN, OA, CT, and MTZ, while they were 100% WT to P, AMP, cephalexin (CL), EFT, CTX + CLA, CIP, C, and FFC (Fig. 5). For Gram-negative bacteria, all 29 antibiotics were tested, with 100% NWT to P, followed by MTZ (98.1%), S (88.3%), DA (85.3%), kanamycin (K) (83.3%), S3 (83.0%), and AMP (80.4%) (Fig. 5). In contrast, all Gram-negative isolates were fully WT to EFT, CTX + CLA, and C. Significant differences (p < 0.05) in WT and NWT bacteria were observed between Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria for eight antibiotics, including AML, AMC, P, AMP, CN, OA, CT and OT.

Antibiotic susceptibility profiles of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. NWT = non-wild type, WT = wild type

Key trends in AMR were noted among prominent bacterial genera in aquaculture (Table 2). High percentage of NWT were identified for MTZ, DA, AML, ENR, and OT. Among common pathogens, Aeromonas exhibited fully NWT (100%) to K and DA, with substantial NWT observed against AML (84.5%), AMC (83.7%), AMP (82.4%), MTZ (81.8%) and OA (81.7%). However, Aeromonas showed fully WT (100%) to amikacin (AK), EFT, CTX + CLA, NOR, CT and C (Table 2). NWT Aeromonas species was high (50-100%) for AML, AMC, AMP, and MTZ (Fig. 6A). A. punctata demonstrated 100% NWT to AML, OA, nalidixic acid (NA), and OT, while A. veronii showed NWT of 83.3%, 84.7%, 66.7% and 63.9%, respectively, to these antibiotics. Additionally, A. jandaei displayed 71.4%, 76.9%, 50.0%, and 52.2% resistance to these antibiotics (Fig. 6A). Both A. schubertii and A. veronii were completely NWT to DA, with A. veronii also exhibiting full NWT to K and CTX.

Percentage of NWT isolates among bacterial species in this study. Percentages of non-wildtype (NWT) are represented according to the green colour gradient legend shown in the left-hand panel. White cells represent undetermined data

Among the Vibrio species, which are abundant in both freshwater and brackish aquaculture, high percentage of NWT were seen for MTZ (99.6%), S (89.1%), DA (85.5%), and S3 (80.6%). Conversely, all isolates were fully WT to AK, CAZ, EFT, CIP, TE, FFC, and DO, with AMC (97.6%) and OA (96.8%) also demonstrating high susceptibility (WT) (Table 2). NWT to S, DA, and MTZ was universal (90–100%) across 19 different Vibrio species (Fig. 6C). Additionally, all isolates of V. alginolyticus, V. campbellii, V. furnissii, V. owensii, V. albicans, and V. harveyi were 100% resistant to AML (Fig. 6C). All isolates of V. alginolyticus, V. brasiliensis, V. diazotrophicus, V. fluvialis, V. furnissii, V. natriegens, V. navarrensis, V. ostreicida, and V. harveyi were similarly 100% NWT to S3 (Fig. 6C) and V. campbellii, V. mediterranei, V. ostreicida, V. rotiferianus, V. albensis, V. harveyi, and V. vulnificus exhibited 90–100% NWT to CT, while V. furnissii and V. albensis were entirely NWT to cefepime (FEP) (Fig. 6C).

Isolates of Streptococcus, another significant genus in aquaculture, were 100% NWT to CN, OA, CT, and MTZ, but showed full WT to various other antibiotics (Table 2). Specifically, all isolates of S. agalactiae and S. suis demonstrated complete NWT to CN and OA. Additionally, S. suis showed full NWT to DO, and CT while S. agalactiae was fully NWT to MTZ (Fig. 6B). Edwardsiella spp. isolates also presented 100% NWT to AML, FFC, TE, SXT, and OT, while being fully WT to CL, ENR, and OA (Table 2). Specifically, all E. tarda and E. ictaluri isolates were 100% NWT to AML, OT, and SXT, with E. ictaluri also demonstrating complete NWT to TE, FFC, and MTZ (Fig. 6D). Resistance patterns were not limited to these genera; Photobacterium showed complete NWT to AML, S3, and MTZ, while Plesiomonas exhibited full NWT to NA and MTZ (Table 2; Fig. 6D).

Analysis of NWT trends (across all years, from 2018 to 2024) revealed different patterns across the antibiotics. The highest average NWT rates were recorded for MTZ (93.5%), while the NWT to CTX was the lowest (3.6%) (Fig. 7). NWT isolates to MTZ and K was consistently high across all years, except for a brief lower level recorded in 2022. A notable greater NWT was observed for S, OA, DA, and CT over the successive years (Fig. 7). AML also consistently showed NWT in over 50% of bacterial isolates throughout the study period. Six antibiotics (S, K, NA, DA, S3, and MTZ) exhibited average NWT isolate rates of 71-94%, while AML, AMP, and OA had NWT isolate rates between 40 and 60% (Fig. 7).

Annual trend of NWT isolates across different antibiotic groups

Regional analysis revealed significant (p < 0.05) variation in NWT isolates rates for 16 antibiotics, including AML, AMC, AMP, S, CN, FEP, CTX, ENR, OA, OT, FFC, DA, CT, SXT, S3, and MTZ, across Thai provinces. NWT isolates against MTZ was observed at 100% in 14 provinces, while there was 100% NWT to AMP in Chachoengsao, Chonburi, Kalasin, Nakhon Pathom, Nonthaburi, Uttaradit and Ratchaburi provinces (Fig. 8). NWT isolates against Streptomycin ranged from 50 to 100% across provinces, with OA exhibiting 100% NWT in seven provinces and AMC reaching 100% NWT in four different provinces.

Multidrug resistance (MDR) patterns and MAR index

MDR is commonly associated with bacterial pathogens and is defined as resistance to three or more antimicrobial classes. Bacterial isolates from aquaculture species exhibited a wide range of MDR patterns. Vibrio showed the greatest diversity, with 100 distinct MDR patterns, followed by Aeromonas with 43 MDR patterns. Among Aeromonas species, A. veronii exhibited the most diverse resistance patterns, with 24 profiles showing to 3 to 10 drugs across 3 to 6 different antibiotic classes. A. jandaei exhibited 11 MDR patterns, resistant against three to six drugs, while A. schubertii had seven patterns, resistant against three to five different antibiotic classes (Table 3). In Vibrio species, V. parahaemolyticus demonstrated the highest resistance, showing MDR against 13 commonly used drugs across eight different antibiotic classes used in aquaculture. Vibrio spp. had the highest number of MDR patterns (n = 27), followed V. vulnificus (n = 19), V. parahaemolyticus (n = 14) and V. navarrensis (n = 10) (Table 4). Also, V. vulnificus demonstrated the highest multidrug resistance (MDR) pattern, exhibiting resistance to eight different classes of antibiotics. Among other bacterial groups, P. shigelloides exhibited the most MDR patterns (n = 5), followed by Pseudomonas spp. (n = 3), L. garvieae (n = 2) and S. suis (2) (Table 5). P. shigelloides and Pseudomonas spp. were resistant to six different antibiotic classes, while Shewanella decolorationis, E. ictaluri, Pseudomonas aerugionosa and P. shigelloides showed resistance to five different antibiotic classes each (Table 5).

Analysis of bacterial genomes

Genome features

WGS and resistome analysis were conducted on nine fish pathogen species, including seven Gram-negative bacteria (A. veronii, A. jandaei, A. dhakensis, A. schubertii, A. sanarellii, A. diversa, and V. vulnificus) and two Gram-positive bacteria (S. agalactiae and S. suis). Genome sizes varied by genus, with Aeromonas species ranging from 4.2 to 4.9 Mbp, V. vulnificus from 4.9 to 5.1 Mbp, and Streptococcus spp. from 2.0 to 2.1 Mbp. The G + C content also differed by genus: Aeromonas species ranged from 58 to 63%, V. vulnificus from 46.6 to 46.8%, and Streptococcus spp. from 35.0 to 41.3% (Supplementary Table S4).

Whole genome-based species identification

Taxonomic thresholds, including average nucleotide identity (ANI) and digital DNA–DNA hybridization (dDDH) values, were determined for the bacterial whole genomes analyzed in this study. These included A. veronii (PLRT09, KCRT12, CBSB03, CCSB46, NBCF28, and NPSF15), A. jandaei (CN01), A. dhakensis (UD09), A. schubertii (A40), A. sanarellii (CCSB44), A. diversa (CCSB51), S. suis (CEFID-SS01 to SS07), and S. agalactiae (CEFID-SA14 to SA16). All Aeromonas isolates exhibited ANI values ranging from 96.36 to 99.1% and dDDH values from 81.6 to 95.8% when compared with the reference genomes of their respective species. S. suis isolates showed ANI values of 96.67–96.71% and a dDDH value of 83.4% relative to the S. suis reference genome. Similarly, S. agalactiae isolates displayed ANI values of 98.89–98.90% and a dDDH value of 91% in comparison with the S. agalactiae reference genome. Phylogenetic analysis based on whole-genome sequences supported the ANI and dDDH results, with all isolates clustering within the same clade as the reference genomes of their assigned species. For strains derived from our previous studies, including A. veronii (NK01, NK02, NK07) and all V. vulnificus isolates, species identification was also confirmed using multiple genomic criteria: ANI, dDDH, multilocus sequence typing (MLST), and whole-genome-based phylogenetic analysis [4, 21, 35].

Prediction of ARGs and MGEs

ARGs were identified in bacterial isolates and categorized into 35 drug classes based on the CARD database. For comparative analysis, the top 20 drug classes were selected and visualized in a bar chart (Fig. 10). In Gram-negative bacteria, the predominant ARG classes were beta-lactams, tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, penams, and macrolides. In contrast, Gram-positive bacteria predominantly displayed ARGs related to beta-lactams, macrolides, fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines, and peptide antibiotics. ARGs were classified into seven resistance mechanisms: antibiotic efflux, target alteration, inactivation, reduced permeability, target protection, target replacement, and host-dependent nutrient acquisition (Fig. 11). Antibiotic efflux was the most common mechanism, representing 50–60% of ARGs across all isolates. The host-dependent nutrient acquisition mechanism was found exclusively in S. suis and was the least represented among all mechanisms. S. agalactiae (CEFID-SA16) showed a unique profile with a higher prevalence of antibiotic inactivation mechanisms than other S. agalactiae isolates.

Stacked bar plot showing the proportion of drug classes of antimicrobial resistance genes

Stacked bar plots showing the proportion of resistance mechanisms of antimicrobial resistance genes

A total of 131 AMR gene families were identified from ARGs based on the CARD database with perfect, strict, and loose hits for screening a putative ARGs. Shared and unique AMR gene families across bacterial isolates were visualized using an UpSetR plot (Fig. 12, Supplementary Table S7). Thirty gene families were shared among all bacterial isolates while seven gene families were shared only in Gram-negative bacteria. Six gene families were uniquely found in Vibrio species, and three were unique to Aeromonas species. Among Gram-positive bacteria, six gene families were unique to S. suis, and four to S. agalactiae. Additionally, four gene families were absent in S. suis and another four in S. agalactiae. Excluding ARGs predicted using the loose hit criteria in CARD, 48 genes were identified across 14 isolates (Table 6). Using the Resfinder web tool, 23 ARGs associated with eight drug classes were identified in 15 isolates (Table 6). No ARGs were predicted in any S. suis isolates by ResFinder. Overall, high-coverage and high-identity ARG predictions from CARD and ResFinder identified a total of 151 ARGs linked to resistance against 13 drug classes. Among these, beta-lactam resistance genes were the most prevalent (38 genes), followed by fluoroquinolone resistance genes (28 genes) and tetracycline resistance genes (13 genes).

Discussion

This paper set out to establish the bacterial diversity and antimicrobial resistance patterns in major cultured fish species in Thailand for the purpose of understanding potential disease and AMR risks for aquaculture systems and also considering human health. A total of eight economically important freshwater and brackish-water fish species were investigated for bacterial isolation and AMR analysis. The high representation of Asian sea bass and red tilapia samples from across wide geographical regions reflects their significant economic value and high demand in Thailand’s aquaculture industry and the associated health management demands nationwide. Alongside food fish, koi carp and goldfish also hold importance in the country’s aquarium sector as ornamental fish [65]. The sample size variations across years and locations likely result from environmental, logistical, and disease-specific factors, along with the influence of farmer requests. For example, the high sampling years of 2021 and 2019 may reflect periods of elevated disease risk or targeted surveillance initiatives.

This investigation highlights the prevalence and diversity of bacterial species associated with aquaculture in Thailand, with a predominance of Gram-negative bacteria, particularly from the families Vibrionaceae and Aeromonadaceae, while Gram-positive bacteria, such as those in the family Streptococcaceae, were also identified. Findings from previous studies support the majority of bacterial pathogens in aquatic animals being aerobic, Gram-negative rods [66]. Yearly and seasonal analyses reveal trends in bacterial diversity dynamics within aquaculture environments, with the greatest diversity seen in 2018–2019, and with the largest number of isolates in 2021. There was a general predominance of Aeromonas and Vibrio across years 2019 to 2021, respectively, while Streptococcus dominated in 2022, suggesting changeable bacterial communities. The abundance peaks of Vibrio (peaking in the warmer months) and Streptococcus (appearing mainly in August) are likely influenced by specific seasonal environment (i.e. temperature) and/or host factors [67]. Aeromonas was present year-round. The high bacterial diversity in the Kanchanaburi province may indicate a natural diversity hotspot, but equally it may have been as a result of the more intensive sampling in this region. The greater microbial diversity harboured by Red tilapia and Asian sea bass is likely reflective of their very extensive production across the wide geographical range with an associated wide range of environmnental exposure conditions. This may underscore the need for species-specific monitoring. There was a possible indication of host-specific pathogen relationships with Vibrio prevalent in Asian sea bass and Streptococcus in snakeskin gourami, whereas Aeromonas was widespread in all hosts, supporting the importance of targeted health management by region and species.

The Vibrionaceae family significantly impacts the aquaculture industry, with numerous pathogenic bacteria species that cause diseases and contribute to the spread of antimicrobial resistance [68]. In this study, the highest bacterial diversity was found within Vibrionaceae, with more than 20 species identified from the genera Photobacterium and Vibrio, particulary in Asian sea bass. Notable species identified from the Vibrio genera include V. harveyi, V. vulnificus, V. parahaemolyticus, V. navarrensis, and V. alginolyticus, known for causing vibriosis, a significant disease in aquaculture [69, 70]. Furthermore, non-pathogenic Vibrionaceae species, while not causing disease independently, may act as opportunistic agents, potentially intensifying infections when interacting with V. harveyi or other pathogens [9]. Species within the Vibrio genus are of particular concern due to their zoonotic potential; they can infect humans through contaminated seafood or seawater exposure, causing gastrointestinal illnesses, wound infections, and severe systemic infections [71]. Some of Vibrio species remain unidentified, necessitating further investigation to determine their identities. Another prominent species identified was P. damselae, within the family Vibrionaceae, that is known to cause disease in various species, particularly marine fish, including Asian sea bass [72, 73]. Multilocus Sequence Typing (MLST) and Multilocus Phylogenetic Analysis (MLPA) of V. vulnificus Thai isolates in this study revealed distinct phylogenetic patterns. One isolate (CUVETSS48) exhibited close phylogenetic proximity to two sequence types (STs) previously identified from strains originating in China and India [21]. Other isolates (CUVETCC1, CUVETCC2, and CUVETSS2) clustered more closely with single STs (ST209, ST426, and ST372, respectively) predominantly found in China [21]. Importantly, none of the V. vulnificus isolates from the current study showed close genetic relationships with strains previously reported from Thailand within the pubMLST database. The findings support the idea that V. vulnificus ancestors emerged in East Asia, then evolved into diverse strains that dispersed worldwide. Genetic analyses indicate significant sequence type variations among V. vulnificus in East Asia, without any single type predominating. This inherent diversity within the presumed ancestral region could explain why the Thai isolates in this study clustered with various STs from China and India, rather than forming distinct local clades, and underscores the dynamic nature of V. vulnificus population structure. The lack of geographically distinct Thai STs in this dataset may also reflect the current limited representation of Thai isolates in global databases, emphasizing the need for more comprehensive local genomic surveillance to fully elucidate the phylogeography of V. vulnificus in Thailand.

Aeromonas, a Gram-negative bacterium commonly found in the environment, is frequently associated with motile Aeromonas septicemia (MAS) in fish, affecting species like tilapia, catfish, snakehead, and tropical fish, sometimes leading to high mortality rates [74, 75]. We identified a diverse range of species within the family Aeromonadaceae, notably A. veronii, A. jandaei, A. schubertii, and A. hydrophila, with A. veronii, being the most prevalent, suporting another recent study carried out in Thailand [4]. Reports vary on which Aeromonas is the most associated with MAS in cultured freshwater fish globally but they include A. hydrophila [76, 77] and A. veronii [4]. In the study of [78] suggest that in cyprinid fish A. veronii may be more virulent than A. hydrophila due to differing aerolysin expression and higher efficacy of intestinal invasion. There is a clear need for continued monitoring and a deeper understanding of the pathogenicity of diverse Aeromonas species. From our previous studies, phylogenetic analyses were carried out using single gene, multilocus, and SNP-based approaches, which demonstrated high genetic diversity among Aeromonas spp. isolated from diverse fish species and geographical origins [4, 35]. Notably, the observed genetic relationships appeared to be independent of the geographical origin, host species, and antimicrobial resistance phenotypes of the analyzed taxa. This complex pattern is likely due to horizontal gene transfer, which allows Aeromonas to acquire new genes from unrelated or distant bacteria. For example, some Aeromonas isolates from fish in Thailand were grouped into separate clades along with isolates from other countries such as A. veronii NK02, NK07, and CNRT12. In contrast, isolates from different host species often clustered in the same clade and were closely related. These findings indicate a complex population structure in Aeromonas spp., where genetic variation is not strictly determined by location, host species, or resistance traits.

Multiple species within the Streptococcus and Lactococcus genera, were identified including, L. garvieae, S. agalactiae, and S. suis. S. agalactiae and S. iniae, that are established as causes of streptococcosis in fish and known zoonotic pathogens, and L. garvieae is emerging as a significant pathogen in both freshwater and marine fish, often causing fatal hemorrhagic septicemia and meningoencephalitis [79–81]. There has been a recent outbreak of piscine S. suis in farmed snakeskin gourami in Thailand [82], and as primarily a zoonotic swine pathogen linked to economic losses in the pig industry, S. suis, poses a growing health concern. Furthermore, increasing human infections with S. suis in Southeast Asia highlight its zoonotic risks to aquaculture and public health [83–85].

Other bacteria identified in this study, including species from the genera Edwardsiella, Serratia, Plesiomonas, Acinetobacter, Pseudomonas, and Staphylococcus, are known for their potential to infect fish under certain conditions. While infections by these bacteria in fish are rarely reported [86–88], some species are well-documented human pathogens and are notable for their multidrug-resistant profiles, including P. aeruginosa, Staphylococcus epidermidis, C. freundii, Serratia spp., and S. maltophilia [89, 90]. These findings underscore the potential zoonotic and antimicrobial resistance risks posed by these bacterial species in aquaculture, highlighting the need for stringent monitoring and control measures to safeguard both animal and human health.

The finding of significant resistance patterns via AST analyses of the bacterial isolates and for multiple drugs classes, poses a major potential treatment challenge in aquaculture. Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria differ structurally, influencing their drug resistance mechanisms. Gram-positive bacteria lack an outer lipopolysaccharide (LPS) membrane and are less reliant on restricting drug uptake and have limited efflux capabilities [91, 92], whereas Gram-negative bacteria employ all four major drug resistance mechanisms [89]. We found high percentage of NWT isolates among Gram-negative bacteria, especially to beta-lactams, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, lincosamides, polymyxins, and nitroimidazoles, consistent with other regional findings in Southeast Asia [93] for E. coli, Aeromonas, and Vibrio. Earlier research reported that certain Aeromonas species show intrinsic resistance to beta-lactam agents via chromosomally encoded beta-lactamases, but species-level data on their prevalence and correlation with resistance phenotypes are limited [94]. Also, recent studies suggest that some Aeromonas spp. may harbor chromosomal colistin resistance genes, potentially associated with plasmid-mediated mcr-3 and mcr-7 in Enterobacterales [95–97]. Notably this study identified that Aeromonas had a distinct resistance pattern, with 70–100% resistance to beta-lactams like AML, AMC, and AMP, attributed to beta-lactamase enzymes as shown in other studies [4, 38, 98, 99]. A. veronii, A. jandaei, and A. schubertii showed significant resistance to fluoroquinolones and tetracyclines. Previous studies have indicated multidrug resistant Aeromonas species are also resistant to tetracycline, neomycin, and colistin, likely due to the widespread availability over the counter and administered via feeds or baths [68, 100–102]. An earlier study in Thailand investigated antibiotic use in tilapia cage farming in the Tha Chin and Mun rivers, revealing that all surveyed farmers routinely used antibiotics, with OTC and ENR being the most commonly applied compounds. Environmental monitoring showed peak ENR concentrations of 1.6 µg/L in water and up to 2339 µg/kg dry weight in sediment, indicating substantial environmental accumulation. This persistent presence of antibiotics in aquatic environments may promote the development and dissemination of AMR in aquaculture settings [103]. While ENR and FFC have been reported as the most effective treatments for Aeromonas, our research indicated a significant percentage of isolates from A. caviae, A. jandaei, and A. veronii showed resistance to ENR, suggesting an emerging pattern of resistance against previously effective treatments [104–106]. Our analyses also found a consistent resistance pattern across Vibrio species, distinct from that of Aeromonas. All Vibrio species showed high resistance to beta-lactams, especially AML, as well as resistance to aminoglycosides, tetracycline, lincosamides, polymyxins, sulfanomides and nitroimidazoles. Some species including P. damselae, V. alginolyticus, V. navarrensis, and V. albensis also showed varied resistance to S3. While resistance across most drug classes has been reported in Thai aquaculture, all tested species remain susceptible to fluoroquinolones, and phenicols. However, V. parahaemolyticus has been reported to have intrinsic resistance to penicillin, while V. parahaemolyticus, V. alginolyticus, V. antiquarius, and V. diabolicus exhibit intrinsic resistance to chloramphenicol [107, 108]. These findings emphasize the need for intensified AMR surveillance to map species distribution and manage AMR risks effectively [2]. Edwardsiella spp. and opportunistic Pseudomonas spp. showed multi-drug resistance, highlighting a need for further research into their roles in AMR spread and potential human health risks. Additionally, the zoonotic P. shigelloides, known to cause diarrhea in humans, exhibited high resistance to beta-lactams, fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines, and nitroimidazoles. Previous reports of resistance in this species emphasize again the need for further investigation due to its public health implications, especially given its prevalence in avian wildlife, such as great cormorants [109].

Among Gram-positive bacteria, high percentage of NWT isolates was noted for aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, polymyxins, and nitroimidazoles. Consistent with other studies, Streptococcus isolates from aquaculture showed varied resistance to macrolides, beta-lactams, lincosamides, fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines, and aminoglycosides [110, 111]. The intrinsic cell wall structure of Gram-positive bacteria limits aminoglycoside permeability, explaining their lower susceptibility at typical concentrations [112]. In contrast, Lactococcus species, exhibited distinct resistance patterns, with high resistance to nitroimidazoles and significant resistance to beta-lactams, and fluoroquinolones, suggesting species-specific adaptations. Prior research has highlighted L. garvieae resistance to macrolides, lincosamides, fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines, and sulfonamides [113, 114] and our findings indicate emerging resistance to beta-lactams and nitroimidazoles. These findings again underscore the importance of species-specific AMR monitoring in aquaculture.

AMR in aquaculture across Southeast Asia reveals significant challenges, with resistance identified in 17 drug classes [115]. The most common classes include aminoglycosides, beta-lactams, quinolones, tetracyclines, sulfonamides, and MDR [116]. MDR profiling and MAR indexing are valuable techniques for enhancing antibiotic risk assessment by identifying high-risk sources and environmental contamination. MDR prevalence is notably high in Malaysia (80% of studies mentioned), followed by the Philippines (60%), Singapore (50%), Vietnam (32%), and Thailand (27%) [115]. In this study, Gram-negative bacteria exhibited highly diverse MDR patterns, with Vibrio spp. displaying 100 distinct MDR patterns with resistant against three to eight different drug classes and Aeromonas spp. showing 43 with resistant against three to six drug classes. This diversity illustrates the increasing challenges of treating aquaculture diseases with antibiotics. MDR and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) Aeromonas spp. were found in seawater and wild mullet in a recent investigation conducted in Egypt [117]. Similarly, 14 Aeromonas spp. isolates from seawater were reported 100% MDR, specifically exhibited complete resistance to b-lactamases and sulfonamides [118]. Consistent with previous research, our study identified frequent MDR in aeromonads, with A. veronii showing the highest diversity with 24 distinct MDR patterns, resistant to three to six drug classes, and A. jandaei exhibiting 11 different MDR patterns, resistant to three to six drug classes [119–121]. This is concerning given their ubiquity and potential role as reservoirs of ARGs. The diverse MDR patterns among Vibrio isolates in our study, with V. vulnificus showing 19 distinct MDR profiles against three to eight different class of antibiotics and V. parahaemolyticus exhibiting the highest resistance, with resistance to 13 different antibiotics, serves to further highlight the emergence of MDR in Vibrio reported in several countries, including Malaysia, Thailand, and China [9, 122–124]. The presence of unidentified Vibrio with 27 distinct MDR patterns further emphasizes the need for species identification to better understand and address MDR risks associated with Vibrio spp. in aquaculture. The emergence of MDR poses significant risks to human health, as pathogens like Aeromonas spp., Vibrio spp., and Shewanella spp. have been implicated in severe infections, including necrotizing fasciitis, following the consumption of raw or undercooked seafood or exposure to seawater [125]. The widespread distribution of antibiotic resistance genes across livestock, aquaculture, and crops facilitates their transmission to humans through contaminated food or direct contact, heightening the public health challenge [126].

In this study, 99% of Aeromonas isolates and 94% of Vibrio isolates showed MAR index values above 0.2, a score indicating high risk bacterial contamination sources for AMR [54, 127–129]. The MAR index for Aeromonas spp. ranged between 0.2 and 1.0, with A. veronii, A. jandaei, and A. schubertii reaching the highest index of 1.0, indicating resistance to all tested antibiotics. These findings suggest that the isolates may have originated from high-risk sources, such as intensive aquaculture systems or environments with frequent and prolonged antibiotic exposure [130, 131]. Similarly, the MAR index for Vibrio spp. ranged between 0.13 and 0.87, with V. parahaemolyticus showing the highest index of 0.87. This value is notably higher than previously reported MAR indices of 0.3 to 0.4 in V. parahaemolyticus from marketed seafood [132] and 0.13 to 0.66 in farmed Asian sea bass [9] raising concerns regarding prudent antibiotic use.

Seven unique MDR patterns were identified among Gram-positive bacteria, with each resistant against three to four classes of antibiotics with MAR indices from 0.20 to 0.50. Both L. garvieae, and S. suis each had distinct MDR profiles, highlighting the complexity of treatment strategies. Although moderate compared to broader MDR pathogens, resistance to even three antibiotic classes limit treatment options, especially for commonly used drugs. Even though S. suis has recently been identified in aquaculture species, isolates from pigs exhibit a higher MDR pattern, with resistance observed against up to seven drug classes, therefore, posing a significant challenge to global public health [133]. Furthermore, despite the fact that the MAR index did not exceed 0.3, any value above 0.2 is considered high-risk and warrants monitoring [54]. This study identified significant regional variations in MAR indices across Thai aquaculture locations, highlighting differences in antimicrobial use across fish culture systems [26, 122, 134], but noting also that factors including the physicochemical environment and disease pressures can also affect the MAR indices.

ARGs can be spread between bacterial species and genera through horizontal gene transfer via plasmids and other mobile genetic elements [135] and have emerged as a major challenge to global public health security in the 21st century, with aquaculture environments a significant source of them [133, 136, 137]. The resistome analysis in this study revealed the ARG pattern of Gram-negative bacteria, conferring resistance to multiple drug classes including beta-lactams, tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, penams, sulfonamides, aminoglycosides, and macrolides, reflecting a broad resistome profile, as shown through AST analysis. The most detected ARGs in other studies of resistance in aquaculture have been associated with beta-lactams, tetracyclines, sulfonamides, and aminoglycosides [2, 138]. The different bacterial species sequenced here each displayed different ARG profiles with those for beta-lactam resistance most associated with Aeromonas spp. and those ARGs related to fluoroquinolones and sulfonamides most frequently associated with V. vulnificus. Notably, the tet(59) gene, a chromosome-encoded tetracycline efflux pump rarely reported in aquaculture, was detected in V. vulnificus. Additionally, a multidrug resistance gene, aac(6’)-Ib-cr, known for antibiotic inactivation, was identified in A. veronii, consistent with other very recent research findings [2]. Moreover, the blaOXA−12 gene, a class D beta-lactamase naturally produced by A. jandaei, confers resistance to ampicillin and suggests the potential for gene transfer among other Aeromonas species [139]. Our findings also revealed the coexistence of sulfonamide-resistant sul genes and trimethoprim-resistant dfr family genes in some Aeromonas spp. and V. vulnificus isolates. Notably, the sul1 gene, commonly found in Gram-negative clinical isolates, is frequently associated with Pseudomonas spp [140]. Furthermore, sul1, sul2, and the class 1 integrase gene (intI1) have been detected in urban wetland sediments, suggesting their potential as indicators of environmental contamination with AMR genes [141].

Furthermore, genome sequence analysis for predicting resistome pattern of Gram-positive bacteria, indicate that resistance in S. suis and S. agalactiae isolates of this study, was most prevalent against glycopeptides, macrolides, fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines, and peptide antibiotics, suggesting a broader resistance profile than identified through traditional AST analysis. In S. agalactiae, this study detected one tet gene, tetM, encoding ribosomal protection proteins [142, 143] and one macrolide resistance efflux gene, mre(A), though mre(A) gene has been reported as linked to macrolide resistance in Group B Streptococcus [144]. However, several studies question its role as a definitive cause of macrolide resistance, illustrating its resistance mechanisms operate via efflux pumps and ribosomal protection proteins [145, 146]. Some of the ARGs identified among Streptococcus isolates may be intrinsic, or these isolates could represent closely related strains due to their highly similar ARG patterns. To better understand the prevalence and distribution of resistance mechanisms within these species, future studies should incorporate both genomic and phenotypic analyses of a broader range of Streptococcus isolates collected from diverse geographical regions. Such surveillance could help distinguish genes that are intrinsic to the species, genus, or specific lineages from those that are mobile and may have been acquired in the context of this study.

While the resistance mechanisms detected above describe some of the ARGs present in these genomes, further studies are needed to help provide more comprehensive and accurate detection of the resistance mechanisms within each species associated with the aquaculture industry in Thailand. This would require population level analyses of important species to identify and describe intrinsic and mobile resistance mechanisms [147]. Large population wide studies are also needed to discover and describe resistance mechanisms present within these important fish pathogens that are not currently defined and included in available resistance gene databases [148–150]. Thus, in this study we begin to explore the broader potential for resistance for each isolate by searching for genes with lower similarity to known genes in the CARD database. By focusing on the antibiotic resistance ontology (ARO) gene family definitions of these putative gene hits, we note that thirty putative gene families were present across all isolates. The presence of seven unique gene families in Aeromonas spp. and V. vulnificus, demonstrates potential for resistance mechanisms through target al.teration, reduced permeability, and antibiotic inactivation in these species. In V. vulnificus six unique gene families primarily focused on antibiotic inactivation and target al.teration, and in Aeromonas spp., three distinct gene families, including the small multidrug resistance antibiotic efflux pump and OXA beta-lactamase, both associated with broad-spectrum multidrug resistance, illustrates the capacity for adaptive variation between these species.

S. suis and S. agalactiae each displayed unique putative gene families S. suis strains were shown to carry sequences with some similarity to P. aeruginosa carbapenemase (PAC) beta-lactamase (enzyme that hydrolyzes beta-lactam antibiotics), VanZ (protein that modifies cell wall precursors, conferring resistance to vancomycin), PNGM beta-lactamase (enzyme that hydrolyzes various beta-lactam antibiotics, contributing to multidrug resistance), ECF transporter S component (part of the ECF transport system that facilitates the uptake of essential nutrients from host), Edeine acetyltransferase (enzyme that modifies and inactivates the peptide antibiotic), GOB beta-lactamase (enzyme that provides resistance to a broad range of beta-lactam antibiotics by hydrolyzing their structure). Notably, S. suis exhibited a distinct host-dependent nutrient acquisition resistance mechanism, that has also been observed in pathogenic S. suis isolates from pigs and group A Streptococcus, which allows the uptake of extracellular reduced folate compounds [151, 152]. Since this reduced susceptibility relies on the presence of host folate compounds, it is undetectable by phenotypic testing in the absence of folate compounds. This highlights one important role of whole genome sequencing for detecting known resistance determinants and the need for continued investigations into the molecular mechanisms of resistance in vivo and in vitro. S. agalactiae strains possessed sequences with some similarity to ANT(6) (enzymes inactivate aminoglycoside antibiotics), BlaB beta-lactamase (which confers resistance to beta-lactam antibiotics), antibiotic-resistant gidB (a mutated gene associated with aminoglycoside resistance), and VanK (an enzyme involved in vancomycin resistance). The identification of these candidate gene families may be used to investigate novel resistances within these species that have been acquired through various routes and which pose significant challenge for treating infections with antibiotics in aquaculture settings.

The capture, accumulation, and spread of resistance genes are primarily driven by MGEs, which facilitate DNA movement within and between cells, often linking ARGs and enabling their transfer across bacterial species [153, 154]. Exposure to antibiotics has revealed an increased association between insertion sequences (IS), small mobile genetic elements found in most bacterial genomes that play a crucial role in molecular adaptation and evolution by promoting gene activation and other genomic rearrangements [155] and ARGs on bacterial genomes, highlighting their relationship [156]. In this study, MGEs, particularly IS elements, were found to be widespread in all species, underscoring the significant role of horizontal gene transfer in the dissemination of resistance. In addition, integrative and conjugative elements (ICEs), mobile genetic elements carrying genes essential for conjugation; [157] and cis-mobilizable elements (CIMEs) were present exclusively in S. agalactiae, potentially supporting its unique MDR profile. In S. agalactiae, ICEs and related elements have been reported to contribute significantly to genome diversity and likely play a role in horizontal gene transfer with other species [158]. CIMEs are reported as prevalent in S. agalactiae genomes and originate from ICEs or IMEs through deletion, lacking autonomy for integration, excision, or transfer [159].

Conclusion

Through a wide geographical study, we have identified several AMR hotspots in aquaculture systems in Thailand that could be prioritized in a national AMR surveillance program. The finding of complete (100%) resistance to several antibiotics in various high-risk disease-causing pathogens including Vibrio spp., Aeromonas spp., and emerging pathogenic strains like S. suis, underscores the need for targeted, species-specific AMR monitoring to capture resistance patterns. Additionally, our study highlights a significant gap in information on antimicrobial use (AMU) in aquaculture, indicating a need for national AMR/AMU surveillance systems to collect these data. The identification of zoonotic, multidrug-resistant pathogens, including A. veronii, V. vulnificus, and S. suis, furthermore highlights the urgent need for an integrated One Health approach to combat AMR in aquaculture. The evaluation of Thailand’s National Strategic Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance (2017–2021) also recommended integrating the surveillance of antimicrobial residues and consumption into the national antimicrobial resistance surveillance system under the one health approach [160]. Such an approach should encompass enhanced surveillance, regulatory measures, and responsible antibiotic stewardship. Specific antibiotics, such as MTZ, S, DA, S3, K, and AML, which have shown high resistance rates among both Gram-positive and Gram-negative isolates, should be prioritized for restriction. Strengthening antibiotic usage regulations in aquaculture, along with promoting non-antibiotic alternatives such as probiotics, vaccines, and immunostimulants, will help reduce antibiotic pressure and the selection for resistance. Expanding environmental monitoring, particularly in water and sediment, will aid in tracking the spread of resistance genes and identifying key reservoirs. Collaborative efforts among aquaculture stakeholders, public health authorities, and environmental agencies are critical for establishing sustainable biosecurity frameworks. Finally, enhancing research on the mechanisms of resistance transfer in aquaculture, particularly the role of horizontal gene transfer, will improve our capacity to develop targeted interventions to mitigate AMR, thereby ensuring the long-term resilience of both aquaculture and public health. Overall, this research advocates for sustainable aquaculture practices that protect both fish and human health while building resilience against AMR.
