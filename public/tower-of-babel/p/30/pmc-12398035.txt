Comparison of the accuracy of predictive models in early detection of clinically relevant posthepatectomy liver failure
Authors: Ying Li, Yu-Meng Liu, Yu-Lin Gao, Zun-Qiang Xiao, Lei Jin, Jun-Wei Liu, Xiao-Dong Sun, Yi Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPost-hepatectomy liver failure (PHLF) is a leading cause of perioperative mortality following liver resection. Early detection and prediction of clinically relevant post-hepatectomy liver failure (CR-PHLF) remain critical but challenging. Lactate has shown promise as a biomarker, but its predictive power when combined with other factors remains unclear.MethodsThis study retrospectively analyzed 915 patients who underwent liver resection at Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital. Variables including demographics, liver function markers, intraoperative blood loss, and postoperative lactate levels were assessed. Multivariate logistic regression identified significant predictors for CR-PHLF, and a nomogram was created. The model’s performance was evaluated using ROC curves and decision curve analysis.ResultsIn this study, Multivariate logistic regression was applied to select 6 predictors from the relevant variables, which were gender, ICGR-15, intraoperative blood loss, transfusion, resection extent, and lactate. In the training set, the AUC of the model was 0.781, significantly outperforming traditional models like ALBI and APRI. In the validation set, the model’s AUC was 0.812, indicating robust predictive accuracy.ConclusionsThe integrated model combining lactate and intraoperative factors provides a more accurate prediction of CR-PHLF risk. It outperforms existing models and has significant potential for improving preoperative risk assessment and intraoperative decision-making.

Introduction

Liver tumors encompass malignant neoplasms such as hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), and gastrointestinal liver metastases, as well as various benign tumors. Advances in medical technology have improved the short-term prognosis for patients undergoing liver resection for hepatic tumors. These improvements include lower postoperative complication rates, faster recovery, and better quality of life [1, 2]. As a result, surgical resection has become the preferred treatment for both benign and malignant hepatobiliary diseases with surgical indications.A study by Michelle et al. examined trends in liver surgery over a decade in the Netherlands, revealing decreases in mortality rates, failure-to-rescue (FTR) rates, and length of hospital stay [3]. These improvements can be attributed to factors such as increased reintervention rates, which reflect earlier recognition and more effective management of complications.However, PHLF remains a major cause of perioperative mortality following liver resection, with incidence rates ranging from 1.2–32%4,and accounting for 41–62% of postoperative deaths. Once PHLF occurs, it progresses rapidly, leading to irreversible liver dysfunction. As the most common and critical factor affecting short-term clinical outcomes, identifying high-risk populations, along with early prediction and timely intervention, is crucial for preventing and managing PHLF.

The International Study Group of Liver Surgery (ISGLS) defines PHLF as an increase in INR and serum bilirubin levels on or after postoperative day 5, compared to preoperative values. The severity of PHLF is classified based on the need for specific treatments. Grade A represents mild liver dysfunction that requires no special treatment, while Grade B involves deviations from the normal clinical course without invasive interventions. Grade C requires invasive treatments. Grades B and C are collectively referred to as clinically relevant post-hepatectomy liver failure (CR-PHLF). Studies have shown that Grade A liver failure has minimal impact on patient prognosis [4]. In contrast, patients with clinically relevant PHLF (ISGLS Grades B and C) often experience poorer outcomes. Therefore, early identification of Grade B/C PHLF is essential for improving patient prognosis.

Currently, no widely accepted or universally used predictive model for PHLF exists. Composite indicators like the Albumin-Bilirubin grade (ALBI) and the Aspartate Aminotransferase to Platelet Ratio (APRI) are commonly used to assess PHLF. However, these primarily focus on preoperative liver function evaluation. The management of intraoperative factors, such as surgical injury (e.g., ischemia-reperfusion injury, intraoperative bleeding), remnant liver volume, hepatic portal vein occlusion, and operative duration, is also essential in reducing postoperative complications. In particular, intraoperative blood loss that requires transfusion not only increases the risk of postoperative infection but may also lead to hepatic hypoperfusion. This, in turn, can impair liver functional recovery and its regenerative capacity.

Lactate, a byproduct of anaerobic cellular respiration, is primarily metabolized in the liver. It can rapidly and accurately reflect changes in liver function, and its measurement is relatively simple. Recent studies have shown a significant correlation between lactate levels and PHLF [5]. Postoperative lactate levels are sensitive to intraoperative events and risk factors for PHLF [6]. Additionally, dynamic lactate stratification has strong predictive value for PHLF. However, these studies have not considered lactate in combination with other indicators. The predictive accuracy of lactate alone for liver failure may be affected by factors such as blood pressure, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease [7–9].

This study aimed to develop a lactate-based multivariate prediction model to enhance the interaction between intraoperative dynamic variables and postoperative metabolic indicators. This approach seeks to improve the accuracy of liver failure prediction, enabling early detection and intervention, ultimately leading to better patient outcomes.

Methods

Patient characteristics

We conducted a retrospective analysis of 915 patients who underwent liver resection at Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital from March 2017 to October 2023. Patients meeting the following inclusion criteria were included: (1) Child-Pugh class A; (2) age ≥ 18 years. Exclusion criteria included: (1) presence of biliary obstruction and coagulation disorders; (2) postoperative hospital stay less than 5 days; (3) Severe hypoxia, shock, severe infection or sepsis, or diabetic acidosis leading to elevated lactate levels due to non-hepatic failure causes; (4) Missing data for key variables used in this study.

Clinical characteristics and associated variables

The variables included in this study were retrospectively collected from the medical record system of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital. These variables include age, gender, body mass index (BMI), ASA score, hypertension, diabetes, portal hypertension, liver cirrhosis, and hepatitis B. Surgery-related variables included intraoperative blood loss, intraoperative blood transfusion volume, extent of liver resection, operative time, and length of hospital stay. Additionally, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin (TBil), albumin, and international normalized ratio (INR) were also included.

Definition of comorbidities and other variables

According to the Brisbane 2000 hepatic anatomy and resection classification, minor hepatectomy involves resecting fewer than 3 Couinaud segments, major hepatectomy entails removing 3–4 Couinaud segments, and extended hepatectomy includes resection of 5 or more Couinaud segments [10]. Perioperative indocyanine green (ICG) clearance testing was performed following established protocols, with ICG clearance assessed using the Indocyanine Green Retention Rate at 15 min (ICG-R15). PHLF was defined based on the ISGLS consensus criteria [11]. PHLF was characterized by elevated INR and hyperbilirubinemia occurring on or after postoperative day 5, using laboratory cutoff values from Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital (TBil: 24 µmol/L, INR: 1.2 mg/dL). This definition of PHLF applies to patients with either normal or abnormal preoperative liver function, regardless of the extent of resection or the presence of underlying liver disease.

The cut-off value of postoperative lactate was calculated using the Youden index, while the cut-off values of clinical variables including intraoperative blood loss and length of surgery were derived according to the Youden indexes combined with clinical practical experience. Based on the normothermic intermittent clamping (Pringle maneuver), we defined the cut-off value of inflow occlusion [12].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS version 25. A total of 603 patient records were randomly split into a training set (402 cases) and a validation set (201 cases) in a 2:1 ratio. The optimal lactate cut-off value was determined using the Youden index from ROC analysis. Univariate logistic regression models were used to assess variables associated with PHLF in the training set. Significant variables (P < 0.1) from univariate analysis were included in a multivariate logistic regression model with backward stepwise selection. Odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated to evaluate the strength of association with PHLF. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Significant variables from the logistic model were used to construct a nomogram for PHLF risk prediction. Hosmer-Lemeshow test was performed to evaluate the goodness of fit of the predicting models. Decision curve analysis (DCA) evaluated the model’s net benefit and clinical utility, while calibration curves assessed predictive accuracy. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Prediction models for PHLF

In this study, we identified 11 potential risk factors for PHLF through univariate logistic regression analysis. Variables with unadjusted P -values < 0.05 were included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis, which revealed 6 significant factors in the final model: gender (OR = 0.306, 95% CI 0.163–0.574), ICGR-15 ≥ 10 (OR = 1.901, 95% CI 1.018–3.552), intraoperative blood loss (OR = 2.466, 95% CI 1.378–4.412), intraoperative plasma transfusion (OR = 1.963, 95% CI 1.107–3.483), Extent of hepatectomy (OR = 2.263, 95% CI 1.298–3.945), and postoperative lactate (OR = 2.835, 95% CI 1.698–4.731) (Figs. 1 and 2).

Based on the multivariate logistic regression model, the 6 significant variables were combined to create a nomogram (Fig. 2). The calibration and decision curves for the nomogram demonstrated good consistency, indicating that the model has strong predictive ability for PHLF risk (Figs. 3A and 4A-B). We further performed Hosmer-Lemeshow test to evaluate the goodness of fit of the model. The test yielded a chi-square statistic of 5.192 (P = 0.637) for the models predicting PHLF. ROC curves were also constructed to assess the predictive performance of the new model compared to ALBI, APRI, ICGR-15, and lactate (Fig. 5A-B). The ROC curves of all variables included in the model were also be presented in the Fig. 6 and Table 7.

The discriminative ability of the predictive model was evaluated using the AUC. In the training set, the new model achieved an AUC of 0.781 (95% CI 0.729–0.833), significantly outperforming ALBI (AUC = 0.646, 95% CI 0.597–0.693; P < 0.001), APRI (AUC = 0.618, 95% CI 0.569–0.666; P < 0.001), postoperative lactate (AUC = 0.660, 95% CI 0.612–0.706; P < 0.001), and ICGR-15 (AUC = 0.600, 95% CI 0.551–0.649; P < 0.001) (Table 5). In the validation set, the new model had an AUC of 0.812 (95% CI 0.751–0.864), while the AUC values for ALBI, APRI, postoperative lactate, and ICGR-15 were 0.588 (95% CI 0.517–0.657), 0.605 (95% CI 0.534–0.674), 0.658 (95% CI 0.587–0.723), and 0.546 (95% CI 0.474–0.616), respectively. These results demonstrate that the new model significantly outperforms traditional composite variables in predicting PHLF (Table 6).

Prediction model for CR-PHLF

Univariate logistic regression analysis identified nine potential risk factors for CR-PHLF, including ASA score, ALBI, APRI, ICGR-15, intraoperative blood loss, intraoperative plasma transfusion, Extent of hepatectomy, operative time, and postoperative lactate. Variables with unadjusted P-values < 0.05 were included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis, which ultimately identified four variables for the final model. The results demonstrated that postoperative lactate was significantly associated with CR-PHLF events and was considered a robust independent predictor of CR-PHLF. Additionally, multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that ICGR-15, intraoperative plasma transfusion, and extent of hepatectomy were also independently associated with CR-PHLF occurrence, all showing statistically significant differences (Table 4).

Discussion

The prevention and management of PHLF remain significant challenges in liver resection. Accurate preoperative prediction of PHLF can help guide the selection of appropriate treatment strategies. This study developed a predictive model that incorporates multiple variables reflecting preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative liver function, with lactate serving as a readily accessible and reliable indicator of hepatic functional changes. The model is designed to predict the risk of PHLF, particularly CR-PHLF that requires intervention, in patients undergoing hepatectomy.

Traditional methods for predicting PHLF primarily focus on a single pathophysiological dimension. For example, the ALBI score reflects baseline liver functional reserve but requires complex mathematical formulas or calculators for calculation. Additionally, it does not account for factors related to portal hypertension or liver fibrosis [13]. The ALBI score relies solely on static blood markers (albumin, bilirubin) and cannot dynamically capture intraoperative trauma from blood loss-induced acute anemia or postoperative metabolic changes—both of which are risk factors for PHLF [13]. Non-invasive scores such as FIB-4 and APRI assess liver injury and portal hypertension, showing high sensitivity for liver fibrosis and predicting PHLF by evaluating the severity of fibrosis [14]. However, their predictive performance is limited in non-cirrhotic patients. In this study, APRI lost significance in multivariate analysis (p = 0.434), highlighting its limited value in predicting PHLF. Therefore, combining various blood test-based scores is crucial to enhancing the predictive accuracy of PHLF. Nevertheless, post-hepatectomy PHLF remains a multifactorial condition involving complex mechanisms, including preoperative liver functional reserve, intraoperative trauma stress, and postoperative metabolic disturbances [11].

This study innovatively combines intraoperative blood loss, transfusion volume, postoperative lactate levels, and ICGR-15 to provide a multidimensional risk prediction. It focuses on preoperative liver function reserve, intraoperative hemodynamic fluctuations, and postoperative lactate metabolic imbalance.

Preoperative liver function reserve: Since ICG is almost entirely cleared by the liver, ICG-R15 serves as a non-invasive, dynamic method for quantifying liver function by measuring functional hepatocyte mass. It is widely used to assess preoperative liver function reserve. The clearance of ICG depends on hepatic blood flow, which can be affected by factors such as portal hypertension and low cardiac output. Under normal conditions, portal venous blood makes up the majority of hepatic blood flow. However, in pathological conditions like cirrhosis, portal venous blood flow decreases, leading to impaired hepatic perfusion and reduced liver function, which results in elevated ICGR-15 values. Studies by Søren Møller et al. have shown a direct correlation between ICG clearance and portal hypertension or cirrhosis [15]. Additionally, bilirubin acts as a competitive inhibitor of ICG during hepatocyte transport. Therefore, reduced ICG values in cholestatic patients do not necessarily rule out liver dysfunction and should be interpreted with caution [16, 17]. Consequently, ICG-R15 assessment in patients with preoperative hyperbilirubinemia or altered portal venous flow remains challenging [18]. Furthermore, this study suggests that combining intraoperative variables, such as blood loss, can improve the predictive accuracy of ICGR-15.

However, ICG clearance is time-consuming, costly, and lacks widespread applicability. Several studies have shown a direct correlation between ICG clearance and portal hypertension as well as liver cirrhosis [15]. ICG clearance reflects total liver blood flow, and liver perfusion significantly affects its measurement. This makes ICG clearance particularly challenging to assess in patients with preoperative hyperbilirubinemia or altered portal venous flow [18]. Additionally, this study suggests that combining ICGR-15 with intraoperative variables, such as blood loss, can enhance its predictive accuracy.

Intraoperative hemodynamic fluctuations: Blood loss of ≥ 400 mL and the need for transfusion are directly linked to hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury, potentially worsening hepatocyte apoptosis through the activation of oxidative stress pathways [19]. Schiergens et al. found in a study of 879 hepatectomy cases that intraoperative blood loss greater than 1000 mL was an independent risk factor for postoperative complications [20]. However, effective control of blood loss significantly reduced this risk. Wu et al. suggest that for healthy livers, a future liver remnant (FLR) > 20%, for fibrotic livers > 30%, and for cirrhotic livers > 40% are considered safe resection thresholds [21]. Conversely, resections exceeding 30% of normal liver volume markedly increase the risk of PHLF. Patients with hepatic insufficiency or cirrhosis require careful evaluation of the postoperative remnant liver volume to ensure adequate physiological support for recovery. While portal vein ligation can significantly reduce intraoperative bleeding, it may also impair hepatic perfusion and affect lactate levels [22]. Relevant studies recommend individualized surgical planning to optimize intraoperative techniques. Effective management of these factors can substantially reduce the risk of PHLF and improve postoperative survival rates.

Postoperative lactate metabolism imbalance: Based on the Youden index from ROC analysis, a lactate level > 1.96 mmol/L was identified as the optimal threshold, consistent with values reported by other studies [23]. This threshold not only serves as a marker of tissue hypoxia but also effectively reflects changes in liver function following surgical stress. Since most lactic acid is metabolized in the liver, insufficient residual liver volume and mitochondrial dysfunction after liver resection reduce the lactic acid clearance rate. Patients with PHLF often experience microcirculation disorders, systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), ROS generation, and increased anaerobic lactate production [24, 25]. During PHLF, mitochondrial dysfunction further impairs lactic acid clearance, exacerbating lactic acid accumulation and creating a harmful positive feedback loop [25]. Additionally, lactate can promote liver repair by activating the mTORC1 signaling pathway, which blocks autophagic hepatocyte repair [26] and by involving lactate-induced M2 macrophages in inflammation and liver regeneration [27]. The accumulation of lactic acid in hepatocytes can also increase mitochondrial ROS production, further damaging hepatic sinusoidal endothelial cells [28]. Currently, there is no clear evidence or consensus guideline to define the critical lactate threshold after liver surgery [29]. Due to confounding factors such as infection and shock, lactate’s specificity as an independent indicator remains limited.

In the model predicting all PHLF cases, we included both intraoperative blood loss > 400 mL and intraoperative plasma transfusion as variables, recognizing that these factors may have some clinical overlap. However, we believe that for patients with poor preoperative liver function, intraoperative plasma transfusion can improve coagulation and reduce bleeding, but it does not necessarily lower the risk of PHLF. On the other hand, for patients with normal preoperative liver function who do not require plasma transfusion to maintain coagulation, significant intraoperative blood loss can substantially increase the risk of PHLF. Therefore, both intraoperative plasma transfusion and blood loss > 400 mL were included in the model. In the model predicting CR-PHLF, multivariate regression analysis showed that only intraoperative plasma transfusion was independently associated with CR-PHLF. This may be due to the inclusion of both malignant and benign tumors in our study population, many of whom had underlying liver dysfunction. As a result, patients requiring intraoperative plasma transfusion were more susceptible to CR-PHLF. In contrast, intraoperative blood loss was less significant in predicting CR-PHLF.

In this study, we developed two predictive models, which are clinically important because PHLF grades B and C require treatment modifications, while grade A is only indicated by deviations in laboratory values. In the validation cohort, the model for predicting CR-PHLF showed strong performance, with an AUC of 0.791. Postoperative lactate levels also demonstrated good predictive efficacy, with an AUC of 0.710, showing no significant difference in diagnostic performance compared to the model (P = 0.035). This may be due to the generally elevated postoperative lactate levels in patients who developed CR-PHLF in this dataset, suggesting some degree of randomness. Nevertheless, both the AUC values and the net reclassification improvement (NRI) indicated that the predictive model significantly improved the accuracy of CR-PHLF prediction.

After constructing the model, we compared its performance with traditional predictors, including the ALBI score, APRI score, and ICGR-15. The results showed that the predictive performance of the new model was significantly improved compared to traditional variables (P > 0.05). Additionally, we used net reclassification improvement (NRI) to quantify the net benefit of the model, offering a more intuitive assessment of the improvements it provides. The net benefit of the new model was significantly higher than that of the traditional model, highlighting its applicability in several scenarios.

For preoperative risk assessment, high-risk patients (e.g., those with ICGR-15 > 10% and cirrhosis) should undergo liver perfusion optimization [30]. In terms of intraoperative strategy optimization, for patients with significant blood loss, surgical techniques should be adjusted to minimize bleeding and reduce transfusion needs [31]. For postoperative monitoring, patients with elevated lactate levels should receive intensified lactate monitoring and early intervention [29].

Our study has several limitations. First, the postoperative lactate levels we used were measured immediately after surgery through arterial blood sampling, whereas Ruth Baumgartner et al. conducted multiple postoperative measurements, selecting either the peak value or the lactate level on postoperative day 1. This discrepancy is mainly due to the limitations of retrospective studies. Another limitation is the single-center design, though our findings may still provide valuable insights for clinical decision-making.

Conclusion

The complexity of liver function and its various compensatory mechanisms present challenges in accurately predicting PHLF in clinical practice. Existing unidimensional liver function assessment systems are inadequate for comprehensively predicting postoperative risks. This study develops a predictive model that integrates multidimensional indicators, incorporating preoperative risk stratification, intraoperative dynamic monitoring, and postoperative metabolic assessment. Compared to traditional liver function indicators, this model offers higher sensitivity and accuracy, systematically highlighting the synergistic effects between intraoperative hemodynamic fluctuations and postoperative metabolic imbalances.Although further validation through multicenter clinical studies and continuous optimization of variable weighting algorithms is necessary, this research introduces a new approach for precise PHLF prevention and management. Future advancements may involve integrating artificial intelligence and molecular biology, transforming PHLF management from passive prediction to active prevention, ultimately improving patient outcomes following hepatectomy.
