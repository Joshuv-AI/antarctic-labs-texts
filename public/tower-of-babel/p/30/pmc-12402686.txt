Sleep and Rest-Activity Rhythms are Delayed and Less Robust in People Living With HIV
Authors: Priya V Borker, Stephen F Smagula, Julia Sherman, Alison Morris, Bernard Macatangay, Sanjay R Patel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeople living with HIV (PLWH) commonly have sleep disturbances, but little is known about their habitual sleep patterns and rest-activity rhythms (RARs). We sought to compare sleep and RAR metrics between people living with and without HIV.MethodsAdult participants with (n = 106) and without HIV (n = 105) underwent evaluation with 14 days of wrist actigraphy. PLWH were virally suppressed and on stable antiretroviral therapy for at least 1 year before evaluation. Sleep duration, timing, regularity, and RAR metrics were derived from actigraphy. Differences in sleep and RAR metrics by HIV status were compared using multivariable regression adjusting for age, sex, race, body mass index, education, employment, smoking, alcohol, and sleep apnea severity.ResultsIn adjusted analyses, PLWH had later timing of sleep and activity compared with those without HIV (sleep midpoint 38.9 ± 12.7 minutes later, P = .003, acrophase 44.3 ± 13.1 minutes later, P = .0009) and less consolidated nighttime sleep (sleep efficiency 2.4 ± 0.9% lower, P = .007; daytime napping 10.5 ± 3.9 minutes greater, P = .007). In addition, PLWH had less robust rhythms with more variable nightly sleep (standard deviation of nocturnal sleep duration 18.1 ± 5.3 minutes greater, P = .0007; standard deviation of sleep midpoint 26.0 ± 7.8 minutes greater, P = .001), lower RAR peak (relative amplitude 0.07 ± 0.02 lower, P = .002), and less regular rhythm (pseudo-F statistic 858 ± 426 lower, P = .046; interdaily stability 0.06 ± 0.02 lower, P = .003).ConclusionsPLWH have delayed, less consolidated, and less robust sleep and RARs compared with those without HIV, suggesting intrinsic differences in circadian rhythms. Future research should evaluate the impact of these abnormalities on long-term health outcomes in PLWH.

In the era of antiretroviral therapy (ART), managing cardiovascular disease, mental health disorders, and other non-AIDS comorbidities is paramount in the care of people living with HIV (PLWH), as they are leading causes of morbidity and mortality in this population [1]. Sleep complaints are common in PLWH, and optimizing sleep patterns is likely important to improve quality of life. Furthermore, sleep has been hypothesized to contribute to the occurrence and severity of non-AIDS comorbidities among PLWH [2]. Sleep patterns encompass measures of sleep timing, the time at which sleep takes place in a 24-hour day; sleep consolidation, the extent to which sleep occurs in 1 continuous period each day; and sleep regularity, the consistency of when sleep occurs across days. Emerging research indicates that among people without HIV, individuals with more delayed, unconsolidated, and irregular sleep patterns are at greater risk of cardiovascular disease and depression [3, 4]. As these are common comorbidities in PLWH, improving sleep patterns may be a novel target to reduce the burden of many common comorbidities [5].

PLWH are vulnerable to poor sleep patterns as a result of HIV through both direct and indirect mechanisms. These include HIV-associated inflammation, the presence of comorbid sleep disorders such as obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), and HIV-related stigma leading to social isolation [6–8]. Another possible explanation is that PLWH have a disrupted circadian rhythm. A small study suggests PLWH have delayed circadian timing as measured by dim light melatonin onset testing [9]. If the robustness of circadian rhythms is weakened, this may impact a wide range of health outcomes, given the importance of circadian rhythms in regulating physiologic processes integral to metabolic, cardiovascular, and mental health [10–12]. Because the sleep-wake cycle is the most readily apparent circadian rhythm in humans, analysis of sleep patterns and rest-activity rhythms (RARs) can provide insight into the strength and timing of the circadian system [13]. We sought to determine if there are differences in sleep and RAR between people with and without HIV.

METHODS

Study Population

Adults aged 18 years or older with and without HIV were recruited from the greater Pittsburgh area to study the effects of sleep and immune function between December 2018 and February 2022. PLWH were eligible if they had been consistently taking ART and had a CD4+ T lymphocyte count >200 cells/µL for the past year. Individuals with a sleep disorder, such as narcolepsy or restless legs syndrome, or who were receiving treatment for OSA were excluded, as were those with an autoimmune disorder or on immunosuppressive therapies. The cohort without HIV was recruited, matching for age and sex. The University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board approved the study protocol, and all participants provided written informed consent (IRB#: STUDY19040290).

Actigraphy-measured Sleep Metrics

Participants wore an actigraph (Actiwatch Spectrum Plus, Philips Respironics) on their nondominant wrist for 14 consecutive days, using an event marker and sleep diary to indicate when they were trying to sleep. We utilized a validated method combining event markers, sleep diary, light, and activity to derive the start and end of each rest interval where participants were attempting to sleep [14]. A day was considered valid for analysis if it had no >4 hours of missing data in total and no >2 minutes of missing data during rest intervals, as previously described [14]. Rest intervals were analyzed using Actiware 6.1 software to determine sleep/wake status for each 30-second epoch, and sleep measures were averaged across all valid days of recording.

The following variables were derived: bed time (mean clock time at which participant attempted to go to sleep in main rest interval), wake time (mean clock time at which participant ended attempts at sleep in main rest interval), mean nocturnal sleep duration (mean time in the main rest interval each night scored as sleep), mean sleep efficiency (the percentage of time scored as sleep in the main rest interval), mean sleep latency (mean duration between bedtime and sleep onset across all nights), mean wake after sleep onset (mean time between sleep onset and sleep offset scored as wake across all nights), mean daily nap duration (mean time scored as sleep outside the main rest interval across all days), mean 24-hour sleep duration (mean time scored as sleep across both the main rest interval and any naps), and mean sleep midpoint (mean time of the midpoint between sleep onset and sleep offset across all main rest intervals). Sleep phase was defined as delayed if the midpoint occurred after 4 AM. The standard deviation of nocturnal sleep duration and standard deviation of sleep midpoint were calculated across all days to measure sleep pattern variability.

RAR Measures

RAR measures were derived from raw actigraphy data using both parametric [15] and nonparametric approaches [16]. Extended cosinor-based (parametric) analyses were performed using the software package rar in R to derive measures of up-mesor (clock time when the activity rhythm passes up above the mean of the modeled rhythm or “mesor” and approximates the time the participant “gets going” in the morning), acrophase (clock time of peak activity), down-mesor (clock time when the activity rhythm passes down below the mesor and approximates the time the participant “settles down” for the night), and pseudo-F-statistic, a goodness-of-fit metric of the RAR data to the cosinor model as an idealized circadian pattern, with higher values reflecting better fit. Illustrations demonstrating the modeled parameters have been published previously [17, 18]. Parametric measures could not be obtained in 5 participants (4 with HIV and 1 without HIV) due to failure of model convergence. Nonparametric analyses were performed using custom code in R in order to calculate measures of: maximal physical activity (M10; defined as mean activity count during the most active 10-hour period), minimal physical activity (L5; defined as mean activity count during the least active 5-hour period), rhythm strength (relative amplitude; defined as the difference in mean activity between M10 and L5 normalized to L5); and rhythm cross-daily stability (interdaily stability; defined as the ratio of variance explained by the hour of day to the total variance across all data points in an individual) [19].

Covariates

Demographic and socio-behavioral characteristics, including age, sex at birth, race, ethnicity, height, weight, HIV status, smoking status, alcohol use, and employment status, were obtained by self-report, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated from height and weight. The most recent HIV-1 viral loads and CD4+ T lymphocyte counts within 1 year of enrollment were abstracted from the participant's medical records. Participant medications were reviewed, and ART regimens were classified as integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI)-based, non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI)-based, protease inhibitor (PI)-based, combination therapy (containing a combination of NNRTI and/or INSTI and/or PI), or other (eg, monotherapy, maraviroc-boosted). The severity of OSA was assessed with 1 night of home sleep apnea testing (WatchPAT 200, Zoll® Itamar®, Israel) to measure the peripheral apnea–hypopnea index (pAHI) based on ≥4% desaturations.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were restricted to participants with at least 5 days of valid actigraphy data. Sleep and RAR metrics were compared by HIV status using χ2 or 1-way ANOVA for categorical variables and t-tests for continuous variables. Multivariable linear and logistic regression models included adjustment for age, sex at birth, race (non-Hispanic Black vs. non-Hispanic White vs. other race or ethnicity), BMI, education level, employment status, smoking status, alcohol use, and pAHI. Sensitivity analyses were performed, excluding participants who were taking efavirenz, as this medication has been associated with sleep disturbances [20]. All analyses were conducted in SAS 9.3 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

RESULTS

Sample Characteristics

A total of 219 individuals met eligibility criteria and completed the protocol. Of these, 8 people were excluded due to having <5 days of valid actigraphy, leaving 211 participants. PLWH and people without HIV had a similar number of actigraphy days available for analysis (mean ± standard deviation [SD]: 14.5 ± 3.2 vs. 13.8 ± 2.1 days, respectively). Among PLWH, all were on effective ART with a viral load below 50 copies/µL and a median (interquartile range [IQR]) CD4+ T lymphocyte count of 819 (599, 1028) cells/µL. The ART regimen was INSTI-based in 68 participants, NNRTI-based in 16, PI-based in 7, combination therapy in 10, and other in 3. Only 1 participant was taking efavirenz. The median (IQR) age of the entire cohort was 59.0 (52, 63) years, with 25% female at birth, 34% Black, and a mean BMI of 27.6 ± 5.8 kg/m2. Two participants with HIV identified as transgender. Age, sex at birth, and BMI were similar between people with and without HIV (Table 1). However, fewer Black participants were represented among people without HIV. The mean pAHI among PLWH and people without HIV fell within mild levels of OSA (<15 events/hour).

Sleep Consolidation

PLWH had less consolidated sleep over a 24-hour period (Table 2). Overall, the mean sleep duration at night was similar between groups (388.9 ± 6.8 vs. 404.9 ± 6.0 minutes, P = .08), but PLWH had lower sleep efficiency (82.6 ± 0.7% vs. 86.5 ± 0.5%, P < .0001). This lower sleep efficiency persisted among PLWH after adjusting for age, sex at birth, race/ethnicity, BMI, education level, employment status, smoking status, alcohol use, and pAHI (Table 3). Similarly, L5 was significantly greater in PLWH, suggesting more restless nighttime sleep, and this difference persisted after multivariable adjustment. The amount of daytime sleep differed between groups. PLWH obtained 10.5 ± 3.9 minutes (P = .007) greater sleep each day through napping even after multivariable adjustment.

Timing of Rhythms

PLWH also had delayed timing of sleep and RARs compared with those without HIV. The mean sleep midpoint among PLWH was 43.6 ± 12.7 minutes later compared with those without HIV (4:03 AM ± 4.3 minutes vs. 3:20 AM ± 8.5 minutes, P = .0007) in unadjusted analyses (Table 2) and remained 38.9 ± 12.7 minutes later among PLWH (P = .003) after multivariable adjustment. Similarly, more PLWH had a delayed sleep phase compared with those without HIV (50% vs. 22%, P < .0001), with the odds of delayed sleep phase 4.2-fold greater (95% CI [2.1, 8.7], P < .0001) in PLWH compared with those without HIV in adjusted analyses. Motor activity also occurred later from RAR analyses. In multivariable analyses, PLWH “got going” (up-mesor) 46.5 ± 17.5 minutes later (P = .009), had peak daytime activity (acrophase) that was 44.3 ± 13.1 minutes later (P = .0009) later, and settled down (down-mesor) 42.1 ± 17.6 minutes later (P = .018).

Sensitivity analyses excluding the participant on efavirenz were not substantially different from the primary analyses. Of note, people with and without HIV had similar levels of physical activity as measured by M10 (Table 2), suggesting the differences in sleep patterns were independent of the level of physical activity.

Regularity and Robustness of Rhythms

Sleep patterns were also less consistent among PLWH. The SD of nocturnal sleep duration was 23.4 ± 5.6 minutes greater in PLWH compared with people without HIV (88.8 ± 4.4 vs. 65.4 ± 3.5 minutes, P < .0001), which persisted in adjusted analyses (18.1 ± 5.3 minutes greater variability, P = .007; Table 3). Similarly, the SD of sleep midpoint was nearly half an hour greater in those living with HIV (26.0 ± 7.8 minutes, P = .001) in adjusted analyses.

RAR analysis also demonstrated less robust daily activity patterns in PLWH. In unadjusted analyses, the pseudo-F statistic, relative amplitude, and interdaily stability all suggested significantly worse circadian rhythmicity among PLWH (Table 2). These differences persisted after multivariable adjustment, where the pseudo-F statistic was 858 ± 426 lower (P = .046), indicating poorer fit to an idealized circadian pattern, the relative amplitude, which does not assume the shape of the RAR pattern, was 0.07 ± 0.02 lower (P = .002), and the interdaily stability, which measures the day-to-day regularity in patterns, was 0.06 ± 0.02 lower (P = .003) in PLWH compared with those without HIV (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

We found that even with viral suppression on ART, people with HIV have delayed sleep and rest–activity schedules, less consolidated sleep at night, and more day-to-day variability in both sleep and activity patterns compared with those without HIV. The sleep midpoint and phase markers from RAR analyses occurred ∼45 minutes later in PLWH compared with controls. PLWH had less consolidated sleep with poorer sleep efficiency at night and 11 minutes of greater daytime napping. Across days, PLWH had more irregular patterns as evidenced by greater SDs in nightly sleep duration and sleep midpoint, lower pseudo-F statistic, relative amplitude, and interdaily stability. The differences in sleep and RAR metrics observed suggest PLWH have a delayed and simultaneously less robust circadian rhythm.

Our work adds to the limited literature on sleep patterns and circadian rhythms in PLWH. A small study found that dim light melatonin onset is delayed by an average of 70 minutes in PLWH compared with age- and sex-matched controls [9]. This finding is consistent with our study and suggests that our results are due to a delayed circadian rhythm in PLWH. Interestingly, the prior work, which was performed in predominantly older women in South Africa, found the timing of sleep was not significantly delayed despite the delayed circadian phase, as opposed to the delayed timing of sleep observed in our cohort of the US men. This difference may be due to fewer social restrictions preventing our cohort from adapting their sleep to match their circadian rhythm. In contrast, the South African cohort may have less freedom to alter sleep timing due to familial or work obligations and so may be more at risk for sleep-related symptoms and adverse health consequences of circadian misalignment. Another study using 7 days of actigraphy found PLWH have greater night-to-night variability in sleep patterns compared with HIV-negative controls based on greater SDs in both sleep duration and sleep midpoint [21]. We confirmed these findings using 2 weeks of actigraphy as recommended to obtain an unbiased estimate of regularity measures [22]. We also performed RAR analysis to utilize physical activity patterns in addition to sleep to better estimate circadian rhythms and found confirmatory evidence for weaker and less robust rhythms as a contributor to the less consolidated and less regular sleep in PLWH. Data from women living with HIV have demonstrated low levels of overnight urinary melatonin are common and associated with poor sleep efficiency, supporting the hypothesis that PLWH are at elevated risk for weakened circadian rhythms that then contribute to poor sleep [23].

Disrupted circadian rhythms may contribute to the excess cardiovascular, metabolic, and psychiatric morbidities observed in PLWH. Individuals with highly variable sleep patterns from night-to-night are at elevated risk of developing metabolic syndrome [24]. Similarly, the risk of suffering a cardiovascular event increases with greater sleep irregularity [4]. Moreover, reduced strength of circadian rhythms, as measured by lower RAR relative amplitude, is associated with a greater risk of all-cause mortality [25]. Delayed timing of sleep and activity patterns are also associated with adverse health outcomes, predicting greater risk of anxiety, depression, and substance use [26–28].

Circadian dysfunction may predispose to comorbidities by several mechanisms. The mammalian circadian clock regulates transcriptional circuits that oscillate over the 24-hour cycle, which contributes to diurnal variations in physiology, including hormonal fluctuations and metabolic activity. Dampened and mistimed circadian rhythms may produce suboptimal physiologic reserves to respond to and manage changes in external stimuli effectively [29]. Both circadian factors and sleep deprivation alter gene expression in tissue-specific areas [30, 31]. Apart from intrinsic hormonal fluctuation, circadian rhythms influence gut microbiome composition and function [32]. Chronodisruption alters gut bacterial composition, which causes metabolic imbalances in murine models [33]. Indeed, delayed circadian timing has been associated with enrichment of a proinflammatory genus of microbiota in women with and without HIV [34].

HIV infection directly impairs circadian biology and function. Diurnal oscillations in immune cell counts are impaired in asymptomatic PLWH, suggesting abnormal functioning of peripheral circadian clocks [35]. Evidence from murine models demonstrates that the HIV-associated protein, transactivator of transcription (Tat), induces dysfunction in the suprachiasmatic nucleus, the master circadian pacemaker, reducing the amplitude of circadian output [36, 37]. Simian immunodeficiency virus infection similarly reduces circadian robustness and amplitude [38].

Apart from direct effects on circadian pacemaker function, PLWH may have differing exposure or sensitivity to external or environmental cues that contribute to weakening circadian rhythms. Light exposure is a strong stimulus for circadian entrainment [39]. Studies in individuals without HIV populations have demonstrated greater vulnerability to circadian disruption associated with poor circadian light hygiene and deficient daytime light exposure [40, 41]. To date, light hygiene has not been evaluated among PLWH. Independent of light exposure, PLWH may have reduced sensitivity to light. PLWH are at elevated risk of ocular complications, such as cataracts and HIV retinopathy, which may impact the effectiveness of light in entraining circadian rhythms [42, 43].

Physical activity is another key circadian entrainment factor, but we found the level of physical activity to be similar between those with and without HIV. Another potential explanation is reduced social interactions among PLWH, whether resulting from HIV-related stigma, discrimination, mental health comorbidities, or physical disability, which can reduce regularity in social rhythms that might then contribute to a weakened circadian rhythm [44, 45].

This is the first study, to our knowledge, that uses RAR measures to evaluate circadian rhythmicity in a cohort of PLWH. Additional strengths of this work include the extended period of monitoring (2 weeks) that allowed for a robust assessment of diurnal patterns in sleep and physical activity across many days. We also accounted for differences in socioeconomic factors and sleep apnea severity to more specifically identify intrinsic differences in circadian rhythms. We limited our assessments to PLWH who were virally suppressed on ART, therefore decreasing the potential confounding effect of uncontrolled viral replication.

Despite the strengths of our work, several limitations should be acknowledged. While we found consistent results from analyses of sleep patterns and RAR metrics as surrogate measures of circadian rhythm, we did not directly interrogate a physiologic circadian marker such as dim light melatonin onset or core body temperature. Furthermore, although RAR analysis provides a semi-quantitative measure of physical activity, the monitoring device used in this study does not provide a validated quantitative measure of physical activity, so further work is needed to confirm our findings of no differences in intensity of physical activity between PLWH and those without HIV. Additionally, our cohort consisted primarily of men, and findings may not be generalizable to women. Moreover, although we limited analysis to participants virally suppressed on ART, we cannot exclude the possibility of heterogeneity by duration of HIV infection, nadir CD4+ T-cell count, or the presence of residual viremia that can only be assessed by highly sensitive noncommercial assays (single copy assay). In addition, we cannot exclude the possibility that differences are secondary to ART rather than HIV infection per se. However, our results persisted even after exclusion of the 1 participant taking efavirenz.

Our study suggests that PLWH exhibit abnormalities in the timing of sleep and RARs, the consolidation of sleep at night, and the regularity/robustness of sleep patterns, all consistent with a delayed and weakened circadian rhythm. As abnormal circadian timing and rhythmicity may contribute to a broad range of physiologic derangements, advancing and increasing the robustness of circadian rhythms may be a novel intervention to reduce the risk of non-AIDS comorbidities among PLWH. Established interventions such as regularizing wake time and morning light exposure exist to entrain circadian rhythms [46]. Future research should examine whether interventions focused on regularizing sleep patterns and strengthening circadian rhythms may be a useful therapeutic approach for improving the health of PLWH.
