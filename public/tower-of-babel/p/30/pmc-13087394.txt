The pathogenicity of immigration detention: a systemic conflict between medical ethics and harmful migration policies
Authors: Nicola Cocco, Valentina Marchese, Federico Nicoli, Giulia Russo, Jacopo Testa, Alice Corsaro, Marco Mazzetti
Published: 2026

On 12th February 2026, the Infectious Diseases Department of the Santa Maria delle Croci Hospital in Ravenna, Italy, was searched with regard to the ongoing investigation of eight infectious disease specialists. The doctors were investigated for issuing health certificates of “unfitness for detention” to immigrants to be held in administrative detention in the Pre-repatriation Detention Centres (Centri di Permanenza per il Rimpatrio, CPR).1

Without entering into the details of specific legal cases, the episode calls for careful reflection on the role of health care professionals and the inherent conflict between harmful migration policies, medical practice, and the medical duty to protect health.

The international scientific community and global health agencies are unanimous: administrative immigration detention is inherently pathogenic. The World Health Organization has explicitly stated that these restrictive environments cause severe, and often irreversible, physical and mental health deterioration generated through social isolation, legal uncertainty, and the systematic deprivation of dignity.2 Current literature has increasingly characterized immigration detention centers as “torturing environments”,3 where the manipulation of living conditions—including overcrowding, inadequate hygiene, and barriers to healthcare—produces suffering that meets the functional definition of torture. Evidence reveals that psychopathological elements are inherent in the very nature of administrative detention,4 which justifies the deprivation of liberty based solely on an “irregular” administrative status. In this scenario the medical assessment of “fitness for detention” becomes a clinical paradox: certifying that a patient is “fit” for an environment known to destroy psychophysical integrity intrinsically contradicts the fundamental Hippocratic principle of primum non nocere (first of all, do no harm).

The World Medical Association's Declaration of Tokyo–guidelines for physicians concerning torture and other cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment in relation to detention and imprisonment-mandates that physicians must have complete clinical independence. Article 7 states that “the physician's fundamental role is to alleviate the distress of his or her fellow human beings, and no motive, whether personal, collective or political, shall prevail against this higher purpose”. Within this context, professional deontology and medical independence are the final safeguard for the rights of the most marginalized.

In response to this systemic harm, a significant movement within the Italian medical community, led by the Italian Society of Migration Medicine (SIMM), has called for an end to this detrimental and unethical practice. Doctors are increasingly refusing to act as “notaries” for a system that violates the right to health.5 The certification of unfitness becomes an evidence-based medical intervention to prevent further harm to patients with severe vulnerabilities, such as chronic infectious diseases or psychiatric disorders.

With the entry into operation of the European Pact on Migration and Asylum, immigrants’ detention will play an increasingly important role, and health screening will be carried out prior to entry into detention centres, making it a mandatory gateway to these environments in several European countries.

Institutionalised pathogenic environments should not exist, and a health professional should not be asked to certify fitness for accessing them. Health must remain a fundamental right, regardless of legal and immigration status. We urge European and global health care community and civil society to remain vigilant about the health effects of these restrictive environments and the resulting frictions between current migration policies, medical practice and adherence to fundamental deontological principles.
