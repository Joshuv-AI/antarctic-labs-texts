Factors associated with chronic kidney disease awareness and preventive self-care practices among patients with diabetes: a cross-sectional study at Muhimbili National Hospital, Tanzania
Authors: Upendo Anania Mwansile, Gift Lukumay, Joel Seme Ambikile
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo assess factors associated with chronic kidney disease (CKD) awareness and preventive self-care among people living with diabetes at a national tertiary hospital in Tanzania.DesignHospital-based cross-sectional analytical study.SettingThe study was conducted at the diabetic clinic of Muhimbili National Hospital in Tanzania.ParticipantsA total of 271 patients attending the diabetic clinic were enrolled.Primary outcome measuresPrimary outcomes were awareness and preventive self-care regarding CKD among patients. Median scores were used to categorise awareness as either higher or lower and preventive self-care practices as either better or poorer. Factors associated with awareness and preventive self-care were analysed using logistic regression models.MethodsData were collected using a structured questionnaire capturing sociodemographic characteristics, CKD awareness and CKD preventive self-care practices. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors.ResultsAmong respondents, 114 (42.1%) had higher CKD awareness while 141 (52.0%) demonstrated better CKD preventive self-care practices. Younger age (<40 years; adjusted OR (AOR): 0.40; 95% CI 0.203 to 0.801; p=0.009), lower education level (AOR: 0.48; 95% CI 0.289 to 0.807; p=0.005) and rural residency (AOR: 0.52; 95% CI 0.300 to 0.917; p=0.024) were independently associated with lower CKD awareness. Conversely, respondents with fewer years of living with diabetes (<5 years; AOR: 2.05; 95% CI 1.173 to 3.592; p=0.012) and those with a family history of diabetes (AOR: 1.84; 95% CI 1.082 to 3.127; p=0.024) were more likely to have poorer CKD preventive self-care practices.ConclusionThis study found low awareness and suboptimal CKD self-care among people living with diabetes. Awareness was associated with age, education level and residence, while self-care was associated with diabetes duration and family history. These findings highlight the need for early, structured education addressing CKD risk, misconceptions and culturally appropriate behaviour-change strategies to improve adherence.Keywords: Awareness, Chronic renal failure, Self-Management, DIABETES & ENDOCRINOLOGY, Patients

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is characterised by persistent abnormalities in kidney structure or function, including a sustained reduction in glomerular filtration rate (GFR) below 60 mL/min/1.73 m² for a duration of at least 3 months.1 CKD has emerged as a major global public health challenge, currently affecting an estimated 752.1 million people worldwide and contributing to approximately 2.1 million deaths annually.2 3 The prevalence of CKD varies significantly by geographic region. The highest burden is observed in Asia and sub-Saharan Africa. In Asia, the prevalence is estimated at 33.3%, with China and India accounting for the majority of cases.2 In contrast, the prevalence in Europe ranges from 3.3% to 10.4%, while in the Americas it is approximately 10.4%. In Africa, an overall prevalence of 15.8% has been reported, with marked regional disparities. North Africa exhibits a lower prevalence (6.1%), whereas sub-Saharan African regions report higher rates: South Africa (10.4%), Eastern Africa (14.4%), Central Africa (16.0%) and Western Africa (19.8%).4 In Tanzania, the prevalence of CKD is estimated to range between 7.0% and 13.6%, with variability observed between urban and rural settings.5

Diabetes mellitus is the leading global cause of CKD, contributing significantly to the disease burden across both high-income and low-income settings. Approximately 40% of individuals with diabetes develop CKD during the course of their illness.6 7 Between 2005 and 2019, the proportion of patients with diabetes among those receiving treatment for kidney failure in high-income countries rose from 20% to 59%.8 In Tanzania, a study conducted at Bugando Medical Centre in Mwanza reported a CKD prevalence of 83.7% among adult patients with diabetes, based on the presence of proteinuria.9 However, this estimate was later considered potentially inflated, as the study did not account for schistosomiasis, a known confounding factor in that region.10 Nevertheless, with the growing burden of non-communicable diseases, particularly diabetes, the prevalence of CKD in Tanzania is expected to continue to rise. Moreover, diabetes not only contributes to the onset of CKD but also accelerates its progression from early, asymptomatic stages to end-stage renal disease. This progression is associated with increased healthcare costs, diminished quality of life and a substantial contribution to the global burden of disease.6 11

The management of CKD extends beyond pharmacological treatment to include comprehensive self-care practices. These practices involve equipping patients with the knowledge and skills to adopt and maintain health-promoting behaviours that support disease prevention and control. People living with diabetes are routinely advised to refrain from smoking and excessive alcohol consumption, adhere to prescribed medications, monitor their blood glucose levels, follow recommended dietary and physical activity guidelines, and attend regular medical check-ups.12 Knowledge and lifestyle modifications are pivotal to the prevention of CKD and slowing its progression.13 14 Evidence suggests that the ability to slow the progression of CKD among patients is often constrained by insufficient knowledge about the condition.15 Limited CKD awareness and knowledge of its associated risk factors not only undermine patient engagement in preventive behaviours but also significantly hamper early detection and timely management of the disease.16 17

Although several studies on CKD have been conducted in Tanzania, there remains a notable gap in research focusing on high-risk populations, particularly people living with diabetes, regarding their CKD awareness and engagement in preventive self-care practices. Existing studies have largely focused on different populations or lacked sufficient depth in these areas. For example, one study conducted in Dar es Salaam focused on patients with hypertension rather than diabetes.18 In northern Tanzania, a community-based study in the Kilimanjaro region explored CKD prevalence and general awareness, but not self-care practices among diabetic individuals.5 Another study in the same region developed and validated a survey tool to assess knowledge, attitudes and practices related to CKD19; however, the instrument contained limited items specifically addressing CKD knowledge and preventive behaviours, making it unsuitable for application in the current context. Given this gap, the present study was designed to assess context-specific factors associated with CKD awareness and the adoption of preventive self-care practices among people living with diabetes attending a national tertiary hospital in Tanzania.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study used a quantitative analytical cross-sectional design to assess CKD awareness and preventive self-care practices among patients with diabetes. This was the appropriate approach as it enabled the assessment of CKD awareness, preventive self-care practices and their associated factors.20 21

Study settings

This study was conducted at Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH), located in Dar es Salaam, the largest and fastest-growing commercial city in Tanzania, with a population of approximately 5.4 million.22 23 MNH is the country’s largest national referral hospital, providing advanced and specialised healthcare services to patients from across Tanzania and beyond. Its broad patient base and comprehensive services made it a suitable setting for this study, particularly given its role in managing complex conditions such as diabetes. At the time of data collection, MNH had a bed capacity of 1500, attended to 1000–1200 outpatients daily and admitted up to 1200 inpatients weekly.24

Specifically, the study was carried out at the MNH diabetic clinic, which serves between 90 and 120 patients per day. The clinic operates 5 days a week, Monday through Friday, from 9:00 to 15:00, with patients attending scheduled monthly appointments. The clinic is staffed by qualified personnel, including three endocrinologists, one nurse officer, seven assistant nurse officers and one health attendant. Notably, the clinic provides long-term follow-up care, including screening and treatment for chronic diabetic complications. Routine patient education is provided as part of standard diabetes care, including general information on diabetes management, lifestyle modification, medication adherence and prevention of complications.

Study population and eligibility criteria

The study population consisted of patients with diabetes attending the diabetic clinic at MNH. Inclusion criteria were: being ≥18 years, having a diagnosis of diabetes and attending the clinic for more than 6 months. Patients in critical condition, those with mental disabilities or those unable to participate for other reasons were excluded from the study.

Sample size and sampling procedure

A sample size of 271 was calculated using the standard formula for sample size, n0=z²pq/e²,25 based on the prevalence from a previous study in Ethiopia.26 Simple random sampling using a lottery method was used to recruit respondents from patients attending the diabetic clinic at MNH during the data collection period. On each day of data collection, the total number of eligible patients attending the clinic was first estimated. An equivalent number of small, identical pieces of paper were prepared, some marked with the word 'Yes’ (indicating selection) and the rest were left blank, corresponding to the number of respondents required for that day. All eligible and willing patients were then invited to draw one piece of paper at random from a box. Those who selected a paper marked ‘Yes’ were included in the study. After each round, the box was thoroughly mixed before the next draw to ensure randomness. This process continued daily until the required sample size was reached.

Data collection tools and measurements

A structured questionnaire was used, with items adapted from previous studies conducted in Ethiopia and Jordan.13 26 The questionnaire consisted of three sections. The first section collected respondents’ sociodemographic information, including age, sex, level of education, marital status, place of residence, employment status, duration of living with diabetes, family history of diabetes and recent blood glucose status (categorised as either ‘within control range’ or ‘outside control range’ based on routine clinical evaluation at the clinic). The second section assessed CKD awareness through 13 items. These items were measured using a trichotomous scale (‘Yes’, ‘No’ and ‘Not sure’), requiring respondents to judge whether each statement was correct. Responses of ‘No’ and ‘Not sure’ were considered incorrect. Each awareness item was assigned 1 mark, yielding a maximum possible score of 13 and a minimum of 0. The median score served as the cut-off point for assessing awareness: scores less than or equal to the median were classified as indicating lower awareness, while scores above the median indicated higher awareness. The third section assessed CKD preventive self-care practices using 12 items measured on a 5-point Likert scale, where 1 indicated ‘applies very much’, 2 ‘applies to a considerable degree’, 3 ‘applies to some degree’, 4 ‘applies very rarely’, and 5 ‘does not apply at all’. The total possible score ranged from 12 to 60. Again, the median score was used as the cut-off point for classifying self-care practices. Because higher scores represented poorer practices, respondents with scores less than or equal to the median were classified as having better CKD preventive self-care practices, while scores above the median were classified as poorer. Since no universally accepted thresholds exist for the adapted CKD awareness and preventive self-care measures used in this study, median scores were applied as study-specific cut-off points to classify respondents according to relative levels of awareness and self-care practices. This median-based approach has been used in similar CKD self-care studies conducted in Uganda and Taiwan.27 28

The tool was translated from English into Swahili, the most widely spoken language in Tanzania, and piloted among 32 respondents from the same study population and setting. This pilot group represented approximately 10% of the calculated sample size. Data from the pilot phase were excluded from the final analysis. The purpose of the pilot was to assess the accuracy, consistency and relevance of the tool. Based on feedback obtained during the pretest, the questionnaire was revised and refined accordingly.

Data collection procedure

Data collection was carried out from 13 June 2022 to 1 July 2022, by one of the authors and a trained research assistant. At the start of each clinic day, the nurse in charge introduced the research team to potential respondents. The researcher explained the study’s purpose, its relevance to healthcare improvement, potential benefits and risks, and the inclusion and exclusion criteria to encourage participation. Eligible individuals who provided voluntary consent were selected using the sampling method described earlier. After giving written informed consent, each respondent received a self-administered questionnaire, which took approximately 10–20 min to complete. Respondents filled out the questionnaire at their convenience, some while waiting for their consultation, others afterwards. Completed questionnaires were returned directly to the researcher or research assistant to ensure secure handling and prevent data loss. Each questionnaire was reviewed for completeness and accuracy on submission.

Data analysis

Questionnaire data were initially entered into Microsoft Excel and then imported into IBM SPSS Statistics V.25 for analysis. The dataset used for analysis is included as online supplemental material S1. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the data: continuous variables were presented using median and IQR, while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Bivariate logistic regression was performed to examine associations between independent and dependent variables. Variables with p values ≤0.2 in the bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression model to control for potential confounders. Model fitness was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and multicollinearity among independent variables was evaluated using variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance values. Statistical significance was set at a p value ≤0.05, with a 95% CI.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Preparation of manuscript

We used the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline and checklist to draft and edit this manuscript.29 30 The STROBE checklist is included as online supplemental material S2.

CKD awareness and associated factors

The overall median (IQR) score for CKD awareness was 8 (7–9). Respondents scoring ≤8 out of the total possible score of 13 were classified as having lower awareness, while those scoring >8 were considered to have higher awareness. Based on this classification, 114 respondents (42.1%) demonstrated higher CKD awareness.

Among the 13 individual items assessing CKD awareness, the statement ‘Drinking enough water (at least 2 litres per day) can help prevent CKD’ had the highest proportion of correct responses (n=236; 87.1%), followed by ‘Managing blood sugar levels is important for preventing kidney complications’ (n=225; 83%). Conversely, the item with the lowest proportion of correct responses was ‘Smoking is a risk factor for developing CKD’ (n=84; 31.0%), followed by ‘Consuming too much salt increases the likelihood of getting CKD’ (n=98; 36.2%). Reported CKD preventive self-care practices and associated factors

The median (IQR) for overall CKD preventive self-care practices was 24 (19–27). Of the total possible score of 60, respondents scoring ≤24 were classified as having better self-care practices while those scoring >24 as having poorer self-care practices. Among respondents, 141 (52.0%) demonstrated better CKD preventive self-care practices.

Regarding the 12 individual items assessing CKD preventive self-care practices, the highest-scoring item, based on positive responses (‘applies very much’ and ‘applies to a considerable degree’), was ‘Do you maintain your medication as prescribed when you are ill?’ (n=259; 95.6%), followed by ‘Do you keep all doctors’ appointments recommended for diabetes treatment?’ (n=256; 94.5%). The items with the lowest proportion of positive responses were ‘Do you regularly examine both your feet for diabetic complications?’ (n=89; 32.8%) and ‘Do you go for medical checkups and advice when you experience abnormal signs and symptoms like nausea, vomiting and reduced urine?’ (n=100; 36.9%). Discussion

This study aimed to assess the factors associated with CKD awareness and preventive self-care practices among patients with diabetes, recognising that CKD awareness and lifestyle modifications are critical for its prevention and the management of its progression.14 Our results revealed that only about two-thirds of patients had higher CKD awareness, and approximately half demonstrated better preventive self-care practices. Specifically, younger age and lower education level, and residing outside Dar es Salaam were independently associated with lower CKD awareness. Conversely, a shorter duration of living with diabetes and having a family history of diabetes were independently associated with poorer CKD preventive self-care practices. These findings underscore the need for targeted educational interventions to improve CKD awareness and promote preventive self-care practices, considering the specific factors that influence both awareness and self-care behaviours among patients with diabetes.

The proportion of patients with higher CKD awareness in our study was low (42.1%), indicating that the majority of the patients had lower CKD awareness based on the study-specific median cut-off. This suggests a gap in CKD education and awareness among patients with diabetes, which may contribute to late detection and poor disease outcomes. Our findings are supported by a similar study conducted in Ethiopia and Nigeria, where CKD awareness was even lower, with less than one-third of respondents demonstrating higher knowledge in Ethiopia and none in Nigeria.31 32 Additionally, a study from the USA reinforces this observation, showing that CKD awareness remains low compared with other chronic diseases, regardless of the methods used to assess it.33 In contrast, a study from India reported a much higher level of awareness, with over three-quarters of people living with diabetes showing adequate understanding of CKD prevention strategies.34 These variations in CKD awareness levels across studies may be partly attributed to methodological differences, particularly in the tools used to assess awareness. For instance, the Indian study used a different set of questions, which may have influenced the reported outcomes. Nevertheless, our results highlight the urgent need for targeted educational and awareness programmes, particularly for patients with diabetes who are at increased risk of developing CKD.

In our study, younger age was independently associated with lower CKD awareness. Specifically, patients aged ≥40 years were more than twice as likely to have higher awareness compared with those <40 years. This finding highlights the potential influence of exposure and experience, as older patients may have had more frequent interactions with healthcare systems, a longer duration of illness and greater opportunities to receive health education, all of which could contribute to their higher levels of awareness. Our results are supported by a study among African Americans in the USA where older age among patients was associated with greater CKD awareness.35 However, a study conducted in India found that age was not a significant factor influencing CKD awareness, which may be due to methodological differences that explain the variation in findings.34 Our results underscore the importance of designing health education interventions that not only target older adults but are also tailored to effectively reach and engage younger patients with diabetes, who may have lower levels of awareness despite being at significant risk.

Our study found that a lower level of education was independently associated with lower CKD awareness. Specifically, patients with secondary or higher education were twice as likely to have higher awareness compared with those with primary education or less. This finding highlights a potential health inequity, where individuals with lower educational attainment may be systematically disadvantaged in their ability to prevent or manage CKD due to limited awareness. Our results are supported by a study from the USA, which found that individuals with a college education were significantly more likely to be aware of CKD.36 However, our finding contrasts with a study conducted in the USA, which reported no significant association between education level and CKD awareness.37 The inconsistency in these findings points to a gap in the literature and underscores the need for further research to better understand the relationship between educational attainment and CKD awareness. Nevertheless, our results emphasise the importance of developing tailored health education programmes targeting people living with diabetes with lower levels of education to improve CKD awareness.

Residing in Dar es Salaam was independently associated with higher CKD awareness, with patients living in the city being twice as likely to have such awareness compared with those residing outside the city. This finding suggests that urban patients with diabetes may benefit from greater access to health information, healthcare services and educational opportunities related to CKD prevention. Although studies specifically comparing CKD awareness between urban and rural settings among patients with diabetes are limited, a systematic review reported that urban populations generally have higher health literacy than rural populations, with these differences more pronounced in developing countries, a trend consistent with our findings.38 In contrast, a population-based study conducted in Northern Tanzania among individuals with CKD found no statistically significant difference in awareness between urban and rural residents.39 Given that our study was conducted in a developing country context, the observed urban advantage may be attributed to better access to health services and information in urban settings compared with rural areas.40 Our findings underscore the need for differentiated health strategies that address geographic disparities among patients with diabetes. CKD-specific programmes targeting this high-risk group should incorporate urban–rural sensitivity to ensure equitable access to health education, early screening and preventive care for CKD.

Other sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, including sex, marital status, employment status, duration of living with diabetes, family history of diabetes and most recent blood glucose status, did not show significant associations with CKD awareness in our study. This suggests that these factors did not influence CKD awareness among our study respondents. However, findings from other studies on the association between these variables and CKD awareness have been mixed. For example, while some studies support our finding of no significant association between sex and CKD awareness, others have reported the opposite.32 34 41 42 Similarly, a study conducted in India found no significant association between employment status and CKD awareness, consistent with our results, whereas a study in Nigeria reported a significant association, particularly linking higher education with greater CKD awareness.32 34 Regarding family history of diabetes, findings are also inconsistent: a study in India found a positive association with CKD awareness, while a study in Nigeria did not.32 34 These discrepancies may reflect underlying sociocultural and methodological differences across settings. Therefore, it is important to consider contextual factors when developing strategies to improve CKD awareness among individuals with diabetes.

Regarding CKD self-care practices, our study found that only about half of the patients demonstrated better preventive self-care practices, suggesting suboptimal engagement in CKD prevention behaviours among individuals with diabetes. Similar findings were reported in a study conducted in Ethiopia, where a comparable proportion of patients practiced appropriate CKD self-care.43 In contrast, a study in Malaysia reported a significantly higher adherence rate, with approximately four-fifths of patients demonstrating better CKD self-care practices.44 This disparity may be partly attributed to differences in methodology, particularly the cut-off points used to categorise self-care. The Malaysian study used a 70% score threshold, whereas our study relied on the median score, which may have resulted in different classifications of adherence. Poor adherence to CKD self-care practices places a substantial proportion of individuals with diabetes at increased risk of developing or experiencing progression of CKD. This may also reflect inadequate integration of CKD prevention into routine diabetes care, underscoring the need for more effective patient education and support systems to promote and sustain CKD self-care behaviours. Moreover, enhancing self-efficacy through innovative strategies like self-management programmes and digital tools may improve CKD self-care practices and reduce costs, but their applicability among people living with diabetes in Tanzania requires further evaluation.45

In our study, only clinical characteristics were associated with CKD self-care practices. Notably, patients who had lived with diabetes for ≥5 years were twice as likely to demonstrate better self-care practices compared with those with a shorter disease duration, suggesting that CKD self-care improves with longer experience managing the condition. Similar findings were reported in an Australian study, where a shorter duration of diabetes was associated with lower CKD self-care scores among patients.46 Our results are further supported by a systematic review and meta-analysis indicating that prolonged self-management, reflecting longer exposure to diabetes, leads to sustained improvements in CKD-related health behaviours.47 However, a study in Taiwan found no significant association between diabetes duration and CKD self-care practices.48 This discrepancy may be attributed to methodological differences, particularly in the analytical approaches and how data were categorised. Our findings suggest that patients with longer diabetes duration may have developed greater knowledge, skills, and experience, contributing to better adherence to CKD preventive practices. Conversely, newly diagnosed patients may be more vulnerable to poor adherence, underscoring the need for early, structured education and support to promote CKD self-care from the time of diagnosis.

The other clinical characteristic that showed a significant association with CKD preventive self-care practices was a family history of diabetes. Specifically, having a family history of diabetes was independently associated with poorer self-care practices. This suggests that a family history may not promote, and could even hinder, engagement in CKD prevention. Although studies directly examining this association are limited, one study reported that a family history of diabetes was independently linked to faster estimated GFR decline in individuals with diabetic kidney disease, implying suboptimal management or preventive behaviours.49 This finding may be explained by normalisation, where individuals perceive diabetes as common and manageable, leading to complacency and reduced adherence to self-care when the condition is prevalent within the family.12 Therefore, education and interventions should address family influences, aiming to challenge misconceptions and disrupt shared unhealthy norms.

Finally, none of the sociodemographic characteristics in our study were significantly associated with CKD preventive self-care practices. However, findings from other settings have been mixed. A study in Ethiopia found that formal education was significantly associated with better CKD preventive practices, while age, gender, marital status, residence and occupation were not.43 In contrast, studies in Australia and Taiwan reported that younger age and male gender were associated with lower adherence to certain self-care components.46 48 Additionally, in Malaysia, marital status was found to significantly influence CKD self-care practices.44 These variations highlight the influence of contextual and cultural factors on self-care behaviours, suggesting that sociodemographic factors may play a role in some settings but not others.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the possibility of social desirability bias may have affected the self-reported responses of respondents, particularly regarding CKD awareness and preventive behaviours, potentially resulting in overestimation. Second, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits the ability to infer causal relationships between the independent variables and CKD self-care outcomes. Third, the analysis did not control for potential confounding variables, such as healthcare access, socioeconomic status, comorbid conditions like hypertension and the proportion who had already developed CKD, which may have influenced the observed associations. Fourth, standardised operational definitions or frequency thresholds behaviours such as ‘regular physical activity’ and ‘regular blood sugar monitoring’ were not operationally defined, which may have introduced subjective interpretation and potential overestimation of self-care practices. Fifth, this study was conducted at a single tertiary hospital, which may limit generalisability. Nevertheless, the findings offer important insights into CKD awareness and preventive self-care behaviours among individuals with diabetes in a Tanzanian context, providing a foundation for the development of targeted interventions.

Conclusion

This study highlights suboptimal levels of CKD awareness and preventive self-care practices among patients with diabetes. Older age, higher level of education and urban residency were associated with higher CKD awareness, while longer duration of diabetes was associated with better self-care practices and family history of diabetes with poorer self-care practices. These findings underscore the need for early and structured patient education at the time of diabetes diagnosis, with a focus on CKD risk and self-care. Interventions should address clinical history and family context, challenging misconceptions and unhealthy shared norms. Incorporating behaviour-change strategies such as self-management support, telehealth, mobile applications and culturally relevant education programmes may enhance self-efficacy and adherence to recommended CKD self-care preventive practices. Future research should explore the effectiveness and feasibility of such interventions within the Tanzanian healthcare context, while accounting for potential confounders such as healthcare access, socioeconomic status and comorbidities.
