Retinal optical coherence tomography measures in multiple sclerosis: a systematic review and meta‐analysis
Authors: Nabil K. El Ayoubi, Ali Ismail, Fares Fahd, Lama Younes, Nour A. Chakra, Samia J. Khoury
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpectral domain‐optical coherence tomography plays a crucial role in the early detection and monitoring of multiple sclerosis (MS) pathophysiology. We aimed to quantify differences in retinal layer measures among different groups of MS and explored different variables that correlate with retinal measures. This study was reported according PRISMA guidelines. A comprehensive search was done across PubMed, Embase, and Google Scholar. The mean difference in thickness of retinal layers and macular volume was assessed. Meta‐regression was done to assess the sources of heterogeneity. A total of 100 articles were included in the meta‐analyses. The peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (pRNFL) thickness significantly decreased in the MSON (MD: −16.44, P < 0.001), MSNON (MD: −6.97, P < 0.001), and PMS (MD: −11.35, P < 0.001) versus HC. The macular RNFL was lower among the MSON (MD: −6.24, P = 0.013) and MSNON (MD: −3.84, P <0.001) versus HC. Macular ganglion cell layer and inner plexiform layer (GCIPL) was thinner among MSON (MD: −14.83, P <0.001), MSNON (MD: −6.38, P < 0.001), and PMS (MD: −11.52, P < 0.001) compared with control eyes. Inner nuclear layer (INL) was higher in the MSON (MD: 0.49, P < 0.001) versus HC. Outer nuclear layer (ONL) thickness significantly lower in the MSNON (MD: −1.15, P = 0.019) versus HC. Meta‐regression showed that disease duration, age, EDSS score, and percentage of patients taking DMT are all negatively correlated with pRNFL and GCIPL thickness; however, female gender was correlated with less atrophy. As conclusion, the study highlights substantial thinning in the pRNFL and macular GCIPL between MS versus controls. INL as valuable parameter for capturing inflammatory disease activity.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is one of the most prevalent causes of neurological disability among young to middle‐aged adults.1, 2 Its primary pathophysiological hallmarks include inflammation, demyelination, and neuroaxonal degeneration.3, 4 However, the precise mechanisms underlying MS remain uncertain and complex.5, 6 Given its higher incidence among young adults, MS can lead to considerable disability for affected individuals.7, 8 Consequently, there is an important need to identify clinically useful, noninvasive, and objective biomarkers for the early detection of MS. The retina, being an unmyelinated structure and sharing with the brain the same diencephalic origin and analogous layers, can serve as an indicator for neuroaxonal degeneration induced by MS through modifications to its architecture.9, 10

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) is a cross‐sectional imaging tool that assesses various layers of the retina, including the peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (pRNFL), macular retinal nerve fiber layer (mRNFL), ganglion cell/inner plexiform layer (GCIPL), inner nuclear layer (INL), outer nuclear layer (ONL), and macular volume (MV). 11 The utilization of OCT has proven valuable in the evaluation of multiple sclerosis, offering a noninvasive, accessible, and cost‐effective approach with high reproducibility. 12 This is particularly relevant since axonal loss in the pregeniculate afferent visual pathways are not well assessed on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). 13 Many recent studies have shown that MS is associated with atrophy of GCIPL, pRNFL, and mRNFL and is particularly accurate in measuring neurodegeneration and optic neuritis changes in MS. The progression from relapsing–remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS) to progressive multiple sclerosis (PMS) involves a shift from an inflammatory process driven by the peripheral immune system to a neurodegenerative process. These changes are observable in the neuroretina. Therefore, OCT plays a crucial role in understanding the complex dynamics of this transition and offers insights into the neuro‐retinal manifestations associated with the shift from inflammatory to neurodegenerative processes in the progression of multiple sclerosis.

A previous meta‐analysis conducted by Petzold et al. in 2016 14 examined the differences in retinal layers between individuals with MS and healthy controls. Since then, several additional studies have been reported. Furthermore, confounding factors such as gender, hormonal environment, age, and duration of illness have now been shown to play significant roles in the physiology of retinal and macular layer changes. We aimed to comprehensively summarize and quantify the best evidence on retinal layer measures obtained through SD‐OCT in patients with multiple sclerosis, with or without optic neuritis, compared to healthy controls, and between progressive MS versus control eyes. Additionally, to explore how different factors may affect the physiology of retinal layers during MS by adjusting for age, sex, DMT use, type of MS course (RRMS or PPMS), EDSS score, and disease duration.

Material and Methods

Protocol

A review proposal was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, registration number CRD42023426274). This study was designed, conducted, and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) Statement. 15

Study eligibility criteria

Regarding inclusion criteria, all the studies: (1) were conducted in humans, (2) used SD‐OCT in patients with MS to study the retinal layer thickness, (3) were published in English and full texts are available, (4) were observational prospective or retrospective studies, and (5) included the diagnosis of multiple sclerosis and optic neuritis based on valid criteria (McDonald criteria). Studies with incomplete information, review articles, and studies that did not use SD‐OCT were excluded.

Literature search

In May 2023, a comprehensive electronic search strategy was employed across PubMed, Embase, and Google Scholar, with no restrictions on publication dates. 16 Two independent reviewers conducted the search among the databases, utilizing terms related to multiple sclerosis, retinal layer measurement, and disease progression (see section 1, supplementary material for the complete search strategy). The search terms were adapted for use in other bibliographic databases. Additionally, relevant articles and review articles in the field were screened for citations not indexed in the specified databases. Using the Rayyan platform, 17 which employs artificial intelligence, the titles and abstracts of the identified studies were screened by two independent reviewers. Studies meeting the inclusion criteria for this meta‐analysis were identified, and any disagreements in eligibility were resolved through consensus.

Data extraction

Data were extracted independently using the predetermined structured form. The inter‐rater reliability was high (>95%). Data extraction variables included study design, participant sociodemographic descriptions (age, sex), disease duration, EDSS score, diagnostic criteria of MS, diagnostic criteria of ON, OCT device that was used, number of patients and control, mean and standardized deviation of the thickness of retinal layer, percentage of patient under DMT (disease‐modifying therapy), and mean thickness (SD) of each retinal layer (pRNFL, mRNFL, GCIPL, INL, ONL, OPL, and MV) of eyes of patients with MS (with a history of MS‐related optic neuritis, or MSON, or without history of optic neuritis, or MSNON) and healthy control participants (HC). The baseline OCT values were recorded from longitudinal studies.

Assessment of study quality

Study risk of bias was independently evaluated by two independent reviewers based on the Newcastle‐Ottawa Scale 18 for case–control, cross‐sectional, and cohort studies. Any disagreements were resolved through group discussion. This scale uses a star system with a maximum of nine stars to evaluate a study in three domains: selection of participants (up to 4 stars), comparability of study groups (up to 2 stars), and ascertainment of outcomes of interest (up to 3 stars) (if valid tool and protocol were used to measure the retinal layer thickness). A higher number of stars indicate better methodological quality. Studies that receive a score of nine stars will be judged to be at high risk of bias, studies with seven or eight stars will be considered to be at medium risk, and those scoring six or less at low risk of bias.

Statistical analysis

Data were converted into mean differences (MDs). MD is the mean difference in retinal layer thickness between patients with MS and healthy controls. All analyses were performed using Comprehensive Meta‐Analysis Software (Version 2.2.064 Biostat, Englewood, NJ, USA). The standard meta‐analysis function with a random effects model 19 was used to conduct all meta‐analyses, and effect sizes of difference between case and control groups were reported as the mean difference (MD) with a confidence interval (CI) of 95%. Heterogeneity was examined to determine whether the effect size came from a homogeneous source and assessed by Q statistics, their related P‐value, and the I 2 statistic, which is the percentage of the variability in the estimate of effects that is due to heterogeneity rather than random error. A larger value of the I 2 statistic indicates higher heterogeneity. A rejection of homogeneity suggests that there may be systemic differences existing among the included studies. A random effects model was utilized because initial analysis using a fixed effects model revealed significant heterogeneity between studies. Publication bias was assessed by examining the funnel plot on outcome measures and quantified using Egger's regression intercept. 19 In case of presence of publication bias, Duval and Tweedie's trim and fill analysis 20 was used. Two‐sided P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Meta‐regression analyses were conducted to analyze the effect of mean age, gender distribution (percent female gender in a study), mean duration illness, percentage of DMT used, and OCT instrument that was used (Spectralis vs. Cirrus) for each subgroup in each layer (Table 2).

Results

After a comprehensive systematic literature search, 11,179 articles were identified, and 12 additional articles were found from references in previous meta‐analyses. After removing duplicates, 10,172 articles remained. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two authors, resulting in the exclusion of 9918 articles that were not relevant. After a full‐text examination of the remaining 254 articles, 152 articles were excluded (Fig. 1). Outcome measures

pRNFL Thickness

All pooled estimates of the mean difference are summarized in Table 1. The pRNFL thickness was significantly lower in the MSON group (MD: −16.44, 95% CI: −17.09 to −15.79, P < 0.001) and MSNON group (MD: −6.97, 95% CI: −8.91 to −5.03, P < 0.001) compared with the HC group. Furthermore, the MSON group had a significantly lower pRNFL thickness than the MSNON group (MD: −9.74, 95% CI: −11.79 to −7.7, P < 0.001). Sensitivity analyses that excluded studies one by one confirmed consistent results. Fifteen studies reported data on pRNFL thickness among patients with progressive MS (PMS); the pooled mean difference showed that the thickness of pRNFL was reduced in the PPMS group (MD: −9.39, 95% CI: −11.44 to −7.34, P < 0.001) and the SPMS group (MD: −15.53, 95% CI: −20.71 to −10.34, P < 0.01) compared with the HC group. The overall result showed atrophy among PMS versus HC (MD: −11.35, 95% CI: −13.01 to −9.7, P < 0.001). Funnel plots of MSON vs. HC and MSNON vs. HC did not show evidence of obvious asymmetry, and Egger's test indicated no significant publication bias (Supplementary Material). However, for MSON vs. MSNON the funnel plot showed a right shift and Egger's test showed a significant publication bias with an intercept of 1.91 (95% CI: 0.27–3.55, two‐tailed P = 0.02), indicating potential publication bias. To address this, we employed Duval and Tweedie's trim and fill analysis. 20 This analysis suggested that even in the absence of publication bias, the effect size would still be significant. Under the random effects model, the recalculated mean difference was −12.37 (95% CI: −13.5 to −9.54), indicating a sustained high level of significance postadjustment.

mRNFL thickness

The mRNFL thickness was significantly lower in the MSON group (MD: −6.24, 95% CI: −6.79 to −5.68, P = 0.013) and MSNON group (MD: −3.84, 95% CI: −6.88 to −0.79, P < 0.001) compared with the HC group. Also, the MSON group had a significantly lower mRNFL thickness than the MSNON group (MD: −3.92, 95% CI: −5.98 to −1.87, P < 0.001). Sensitivity analyses and funnel plots indicated consistent results with no significant publication bias (Table 1).

GCIPL thickness

The pooled mean difference showed that both the MSON group (MD: −14.83, 95% CI: −19.43 to −10.23, P < 0.001) and MSNON group (MD: −6.38, 95% CI: −7.88 to −4.87, P < 0.001) have a significant decrease in GCIPL thickness compared with the HC group. Furthermore, the MSON group had a significantly lower GCIPL thickness than the MSNON group (MD: −8.13, 95% CI: −10.29 to −7.39, P < 0.001). Ten studies overall reported data on GCIPL thickness among patients with progressive MS (PMS). The PPMS group (MD: −8.84, 95% CI: −10.29 to −7.39, P < 0.001) and the SPMS group (MD: −10.37, 95% CI: −11.94 to −8.79, P < 0.001) showed decreased thickness compared with the HC group. The overall result indicated atrophy among PMS compared to HC (MD: −11.25, 95% CI: −12.10 to −10.4, P < 0.001). Funnel plots showed asymmetry in the MSON vs. HC and MSNON vs. HC comparisons and a shift to a positive mean difference in the MSON vs. MSNON comparison (Supplementary Material). Egger's test showed no significant publication bias in most comparisons, except for MSON versus MSNON (P = 0.002) (Table 1).

Sensitivity analyses revealed that no study significantly influenced the overall significance of the results. Examination of the funnel plot showed asymmetry in the MSON versus HC and MSNON versus HC comparisons and a shift to a positive mean difference in the MSON versus MSNON comparison (Supplementary Material) indicated asymmetry around the combined effect size. Egger's test showed no significant publication bias in most comparisons, except for MSON versus MSNON intercept was 3.83 (95% CI: 1.22–6.45, two‐tailed P = 0.005), indicating potential publication bias. To address this, we employed Duval and Tweedie's trim and fill analysis. 20 This analysis suggested that even in the absence of publication bias, the effect size would still be significant. Under the random effects model, the recalculated mean difference was −6.5 (95% CI: −9.7 to −3.3), indicating a sustained high level of significance postadjustment.

INL thickness

The meta‐analysis for INL thickness showed a significant increase in the MSON group (MD: 0.49, 95% CI: 0.12–0.86, P = 0.008) compared with the HC group. However, no significant mean differences were detected in the MSNON group compared to the HC group (MD: 0.02, 95% CI: −0.29 to 0.33, P = 0.87), and for the PMS group, no change in INL thickness was detected compared to the HC group (MD: −0.51, 95% CI: −1.92 to 0.89, P = 0.47). The analysis of MSON vs. MSNON revealed significantly increased INL thickness for the MSON group (MD: 0.55, 95% CI: 0.17–0.91, P = 0.004). Sensitivity analyses, funnel plots, and Egger's test indicated consistent results with no significant publication bias (Table 2).

ONPL thickness

ONPL thickness pooled mean difference showed a significant decrease in the MSNON group (MD: −1.15, 95% CI: −2.11 to −0.18, P = 0.019) compared with the HC group, but no significant mean differences were detected in the MSON group compared to the HC group (MD: −0.03, 95% CI: −0.44 to 0.38, P = 0.87). However, the analysis of MSON versus MSNON revealed significantly increased ONL thickness for the MSON group (MD: 1.63, 95% CI: 0.7–2.56, P < 0.01). Sensitivity analyses, funnel plots, and Egger's test indicated consistent results with no significant publication bias in all comparisons (Table 2).

Macular volume

Macular volume was significantly decreased in the MSON group (MD: −0.61, 95% CI: −0.97 to −0.25, P < 0.001) and MSNON group (MD: −0.43, 95% CI: −0.73 to −0.14, P < 0.001) compared with the HC group. However, the MSON versus MSNON comparison showed no significant difference in MV (MD: −0.36, 95% CI: −0.77 to 0.05, P = 0.08) (Table 2). Sensitivity analysis showed that the significant difference was not influenced by any single study.

Assessment of heterogeneity

Moderate to high heterogeneity was detected in the primary meta‐analysis; thus, a meta‐regression was performed to explore the effect of age, disease duration, sex, EDSS score, OCT instrument that was used, and the percentage of DMT use on the retinal OCT measures in patients with MS (Tables 3 and 4). Disease duration was a source of heterogeneity with a negative coefficient in the analysis of pRNFL (MSNON vs. HC and MSON vs. HC groups, P = 0.04, P = <0.001 respectively) and in GCIPL analysis (MSON vs. HC group, P = 0.046) (Table 3). Age influenced positively only the analysis of GCIPL (MSON vs. HC group, P = 0.047). The percentage of females influenced only the GCIPL analysis (Table 4). As for the EDSS score, it was positively correlated with the effect size in the analysis of pRNFL (MSON vs. HC, P < 0.001) (Table 3) and GCIPL (MSON vs. MSNON, P = 0.001, PMS vs. HC, P = 0.043) (Table 4). The percentage of patients taking DMT only influenced the analysis of GCIPL between PMS vs. HC (P < 0.001) (Table 4).

Discussion

Our meta‐analysis revealed a significant reduction in the pRNFL thickness, mRNFL thickness, and GCIPL in both MSON, MSON, and PMS groups compared to the HC group. Moreover, MSON eyes had lower pRNFL thickness, mRNFL thickness, and GCIPL thickness than MSNON eyes. However, heterogeneous associations were observed in the inner nuclear layer (INL) and outer nuclear layer (ONL).

The reductions in pRNFL and mRNFL thickness in MS patients, irrespective of optic neuritis history, align with Petzold et al.'s previous meta‐analysis. 14 Notably, our findings indicate a higher mean difference in mRNFL thickness compared to the earlier study, 14 emphasizing the robustness of pRNFL and mRNFL layer thickness as a neurodegeneration measure in MS and optic neuritis. In MSON patients, the primary cause of RNFL thinning in both the parapapillary and macular areas is caused by the demyelination process that leads to axonal loss, thinning of RNFL, and subsequent visual impairment after optic neuritis, 120 and the inflammatory process during optic neuritis can also cause swelling of the optic nerve head that can contribute to further RNFL thinning.121, 122 Moreover, in patients without a history of ON, RNFL thinning may occur due to chronic inflammation in the central nervous system or subclinical demyelination in the afferent visual pathways, which can lead to axonal damage and loss. Other factors like oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neurodegenerative process may also play a role in RNFL thinning in MS. 123 One limitation of incorporating pRNFL thickness data in meta‐analyses is the potential influence of optic disc swelling observed during the onset of optic neuritis, leading to subsequent rapid atrophy. 124 This concern is highlighted by the longitudinal study conducted by Kupersmith et al 124 that showed the superiority of the ganglion cell‐inner plexiform layer (GCIPL) over pRNFL in detecting early atrophy following optic neuritis. In the progressive MS (PMS) subgroups, pRNFL thickness was reduced compared to HC, with a slightly higher mean difference in SPMS versus HC groups compared to PPMS vs. HC groups. These results could be explained by subclinical optic neuritis attacks suffered by SPMS patients during the relapsing phase. 125

Consistent with the data from the RNFL, atrophy of the CGIPL was noticed in MSON eyes and MSNON eyes compared to HC groups with more severe atrophy among MSON eyes than in MSNON eyes, these findings are consistent with the results of Petzold et al. 14 An important advantage of the GCIPL compared with the pRNFL is that atrophy becomes detectable much earlier.124, 126 It is recommended to wait at least 3 months before measuring GCIPL thinning following optic neuritis caused by multiple sclerosis in comparison with baseline readings.127, 128 Furthermore, the macula has the thickest retinal ganglion cell layer complex. The GCIPL is a useful biomarker for neurodegeneration in the visual pathway since this layer has a wide dynamic range and most of the MS‐related damage in the CNS and the retina involves the macula. In cases with severe atrophy of the pRNFL following optic neuritis, a nadir effect might prevent observation of further atrophy around the optic disc, but analysis of the GCIPL will still be useful. 129 Similarly, patients with PMS showed decreased GCIPL thickness compared to HC groups with worse thinning among patients with SPMS vs. HC groups, which can be explained by recurrent episodes of ON attacks during the remitting–relapsing period. 125

No atrophy was observed in the INL layer. On the contrary, our meta‐analysis showed an increase in the thickness in MSON groups compared to HC groups, and MSON groups compared to MSNON groups, with no significant difference among the MSNON group compared to the HC group, in these results are consistent with results obtained by Petzold et al. 14 In contrast, patients with PMS had a decrease in the thickness of the INL layer compared to HC. The findings of this study would imply that previously suggested mechanisms such as inflammation‐related dynamic fluid shifts and Müller cell dysfunction are more likely than other noninflammatory mechanisms, such as traction and retrograde trans‐synaptic degeneration.130, 131 Another suggested mechanism is a pathology in Müller cells that would impair the absorption of interstitial fluid, also resulting in increased INL volume. This further substantiates the potential of pRNFL/GCIPL as a measure for neurodegeneration, whereas INL volume may be a valuable parameter for reflecting inflammatory activity. 119 Thus, INL volume may be considered as an outcome measure for MSON treatment trials. INL swelling appeared most marked in RRMS especially earlier in the disease when inflammatory activity is higher. Conversely, INL neuronal degeneration and loss may predominate later in the disease course.132, 133, 134 Furthermore, increased INL thickness and microcystic macular edema (MME) occurred in eyes with a history of ON because of the leak of the blood‐retinal barrier during acute ON.135, 136 Thus, INL seems to be a physiological barrier to retrograde trans‐synaptic axonal degeneration.

Minimal thinning of the ONL was noted in MSNON groups compared to HC groups, and a small degree of ONL thickening was apparent in MSON eyes compared with MSNON eyes. Nevertheless, Petzold et al 14 did not find any significant difference between MSNON and MSON groups compared to the HC group. This result can be caused by transient increases in INL and ONL thicknesses following ON, which causes inflammation and edema of the ONL layer.135, 137, 138

A significant macular volume loss was noticed among patients with MS with and without ON attacks compared to the HC group, with no significant difference between MS groups. This is attributable to MME predominantly involving the INL, which was observed in a subset of patients with multiple sclerosis with no other identifiable cause. 139 Effectively, studies showed that MME occurred more frequently in eyes with prior ON and is a well‐established cause of macular volume loss.139, 140, 141

As in many meta‐analyses, clinical heterogeneity was assessed in this study. 142 Through meta‐regression analyses, we found that disease duration, EDSS score, percentage of DMT use, and sex were the main sources of heterogeneity. pRNFL and GCIPL layer thickness were negatively correlated with the disease duration and EDSS score. Our findings are consistent with many studies that reported a significant negative correlation between pRNFL and GCIPL thickness and EDSS,143, 144, 145 suggesting that atrophy of the innermost retinal layers (pRNFL and GCIPL) is indeed a reflection of global neuroaxonal degeneration of the CNS. Low GCIPL measures reflect upper spinal cord atrophy, and spinal cord atrophy is associated with the EDSS score worsening. 146 A robust body of research has demonstrated that with increased disease duration, patients tend to display lower OCT measurements,107, 134, 147, 148 indicating progressive structural damage within the visual system. Specifically, Khalil et al. and Gad et al.,102, 149 reported a statistically significant negative correlation between the average retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness of MS eyes and disease duration, consistent with our findings.

In contrast, GCIPL layers were positively correlated with the percentage of females and possibly less atrophy. This finding is in line with previous studies that have demonstrated an overall worse clinical prognosis of MS in males compared to females, with a faster rate of disease progression and more destructive lesions on MRI scans.150, 151, 152 Gender variations in MS have been attributed to multiple factors including sex hormones, genetic predispositions, immunological biases, and environmental impacts. 152 Estrogen is a sex hormone that has been shown to reduce the severity of experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis in animal models of CNS inflammation.153, 154, 155 In the clinical setting, treatment with estrogen derivatives has been linked to a reduction in MRI‐measured gadolinium‐enhancing lesions in women with RRMS. 156 In animal models and in humans with optic nerve damage, estrogen has also been proven to improve retinal blood flow and safeguard the RNFL. 157

GCIPL measures exhibited a negative correlation with age, aligning with recent studies showing that the ganglion cell layer (GCL) and inner plexiform layer (IPL) are particularly susceptible to age‐related changes. 158 According to histological findings, a gradual loss of neurons occurs each year ranging between 0.3% and 0.6%. 159 Ooto et al., 2011 160 reported a linear decrease of −0.07 and −0.05 μm per year in GCL and IPL thickness, respectively. Similar results were observed by Moreno et al., 2018. 161 These findings support the notion that GCIPL measures are influenced by age.

The percentage of DMT use was associated with a thinner GCIPL in PMS patients, which could be explained by the lack of effect of DMTs (especially therapies targeting the adaptive immune system) on atrophy in PMS. 118

This study has several limitations. First, regardless of the thorough meta‐regression and sensitivity analyses, significant heterogeneity remained in some of the results. This is explained by the cross‐sectional nature of data that was included in the current meta‐analysis. To mitigate this and prevent overestimation, the random‐effect model was exported from the studies. Furthermore, due to the cross‐sectional character, causal inferences cannot be performed. To investigate the changes in retinal microvasculature during the disease process, prospective longitudinal investigations are required.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our meta‐analysis demonstrated that pRNFL, mRNFL, and GCIPL were reduced in MS, and this reduction was more evident in MSON. The INL thickness was increased among MS cases compared to HC. Additionally, EDSS and disease duration were correlated with alterations in the pRNFL and GCIPL thickness. Large prospective studies are required to confirm if retinal OCT measures can be used as predictive biomarkers of clinical outcomes in multiple sclerosis.
