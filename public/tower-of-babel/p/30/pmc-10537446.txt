Predicting PFAS and Hydrophilic Trace Organic Contaminant Transport in Black Carbon-Amended Engineered Media Filters for Improved Stormwater Runoff Treatment
Authors: James
Conrad Pritchard, Yeo-Myoung Cho, Kathleen Mills Hawkins, Stephanie Spahr, Christopher P Higgins, Richard G Luthy
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractImproved stormwater treatment is needed to prevent toxic and mobile contaminant transport into receiving waters and allow beneficial use of stormwater runoff. In particular, safe capture of stormwater runoff to augment drinking water supplies is contingent upon removing dissolved trace organic contaminants (TrOCs) not captured by conventional stormwater control measures. This study builds upon a prior laboratory-based column study investigating biochar and regenerated activated carbon (RAC) amendment for removing hydrophilic trace organic contaminants (HiTrOCs) and poly- and perfluoroalkyl substances (PFASs) from stormwater runoff. A robust contaminant transport model framework incorporating time-dependent flow and influent concentration is developed and validated to predict HiTrOC and PFAS transport in biochar- and RAC-amended stormwater filters. Specifically, parameters fit using a sorption-retarded intraparticle pore diffusion transport model were validated using data further along the depth of the column and compared to equilibrium batch isotherms. The transport model and fitted parameters were then used to estimate the lifetime of a hypothetical stormwater filter in Seal Beach, CA, to be 35 ± 6 years for biochar- and 51 ± 17 years for RAC-amended filters, under ideal conditions with no filter clogging. This work offers insights on the kinetics of HiTrOC and PFAS transport within biochar and RAC filters and on the impact of filter design on contaminant removal performance and longevity.Keywords: stormwater runoff, biochar, regenerated activated carbon, poly- and perfluoroalkyl substances, hydrophilic trace organic contaminants, sorption-retarded intraparticle pore diffusion

Introduction

Improved treatment of urban stormwater runoff is necessary to limit the transport of contaminants into receiving waters while also providing opportunities for beneficial use. Urban stormwater runoff contains nutrients,1,2 pathogens,3,4 metals,2,5 and trace organic contaminants (TrOCs)6,7 and is attributed to impairing waterways, potentially harming human and aquatic health. Opportunities for beneficial use of stormwater runoff include restoring natural ecosystem biodiversity and ecosystem services, enabling human recreation, replenishing drinking water supply aquifers, and protecting against saltwater intrusion through managed aquifer recharge.8 However, beneficial use of urban runoff relies on improved stormwater treatment, as current treatment techniques are largely insufficient for removing trace dissolved contaminants including hydrophilic TrOCs (HiTrOCs) and poly- and perfluoroalkyl substances (PFASs).7 Recent laboratory studies have investigated the use of black carbon (BC) amendments such as biochar and regenerated activated carbon (RAC) to improve dissolved TrOC removal from stormwater through sorption.9−12 However, long-term predictive modeling is necessary to better interpret laboratory study results and understand the suitability and expected lifetime for these filters to operate in the field.

Past studies have evaluated various contaminant transport models to predict BC-amended stormwater filter performance.10,11,13 Specifically, a one-dimensional sorption-retarded intraparticle pore diffusion model, initially developed by Werner et al.,14 was shown to best match observed HiTrOC transport in BC-amended filters when compared with other 1-dimension sorption-retarded solute transport models.10 This model was originally developed to model the fate of hydrophobic organic compound (HOC) fluxes in activated carbon-mixed sediment in benthic environments but has since been applied to predict organic pesticide and pharmaceutical transport in BC-amended stormwater column filters.14 Sorption and tortuosity parameters, critical for describing contaminant sorption and effective intraparticle diffusion, have been determined using batch isotherms10 or by fitting the transport model to observed contaminant migration.11,13 The model, fit to or using experimental data, has then been used to estimate BC filter performance in the field.11,13

However, use of the sorption-retarded intraparticle pore diffusion model for predicting TrOC transport in BC-amended engineered media stormwater filters has been constrained by model limitations and experimental conditions and seldom incorporated validation. While variable influent concentration has recently been incorporated into the model,11 further implementation of variable flow rate is necessary for situations when contaminant removal is kinetically limited and thus flow rates through the filter may impact contaminant removal. A previous study has shown that HiTrOCs and PFAS removal from stormwater using BC-amended filters is kinetically limited.12 As flow rates for urban runoff are variable, if this variability is not accounted for, it can significantly limit the usefulness of such models for predicting real-world conditions. Past efforts using the intraparticle pore diffusion model have investigated BC grain sizes in the range of 53–841 μm with flow rates between 2 and 6 cm h–1.9−11,13,15−17 However, typical design flow rates for stormwater control measures (SCMs) require larger flow rates in the range of 7–22 cm h–1,18,19 which in turn require larger media size, both of which are expected to decrease contaminant removal efficiency. Modeling of higher-flow rate conditions with larger media size is essential to validate the model for more realistic experimental conditions. This is especially important when relatively simple representations of compound-BC interactions such as Langmuir or Freundlich isotherms are used to summarize more complex interactions among a diverse group of compounds. In addition, experimental validation of the modeling approach and understanding of model prediction uncertainty is necessary to increase confidence in the modeled predictions and filter lifetime estimation.

This study seeks to increase the understanding of how a 1-d sorption-retarded intraparticle pore diffusion model may better describe contaminant transport in BC-amended engineered media filters. The forward predictive transport model was validated for HiTrOC and PFAS transport in BC-amended engineered media filters using observations from previously reported column tests conducted at 20 cm h–1 with median particle size of ∼0.85 mm.12 Specifically, sorption and effective tortuosity parameters were optimized by fitting a sorption-retarded intraparticle pore diffusion model to the observed transport data from a quarter of the way through the treatment column length. This model and parameters were then used to predict contaminant transport further down the column. The fitting procedure was then repeated with data observed from various points further along the column to refine the estimated sorption parameters. These refined sorption and effective tortuosity parameters were then compared to the sorption parameters obtained from batch isotherm tests. Finally, the validated sorption parameters were used to estimate filter lifetime and associated uncertainty for a hypothetical case.

Material and Methods

Column and Batch Isotherm Experiments

Procedures for the column experiments and preparation of synthetic stormwater were reported in Pritchard et al.12 Briefly, clear PVC columns (1.22 m length, 7.6 cm diameter) were dry-packed with 60 cm of mixtures of sand, biochar (Environmental Ultra, Biochar Supreme; Everson, WA, USA; 30 v%, 2.4 wt %), and RAC (830R, Cabot Corporation; Alpharetta, GA, USA; 10 v%, 3.3 wt %) with 10 cm barrier sand below and 6 cm above the engineered media. The engineering media materials were sieved to 1.68–0.42 mm, and a particle size analysis was conducted by dry sieving. Columns were saturated with carbon dioxide (2 L min–1) for 1 h and deionized water (2.4 mL min–1) for 8 h and then flushed in a downflow configuration at a flow rate of 20 cm h–1 (15.2 mL min–1). Columns were operated in the downflow configuration at 20 cm h–1 with continuous overflow to ensure constant head for the duration of the experiment. Flow rates were set using globe and needle valves at the bottom of each column, checked gravimetrically, and adjusted periodically.

Tracer tests were conducted using bromide as a conservative tracer to measure the longitudinal dispersivity in the filter beds within the columns. Tracer tests were conducted by first lowering the water level in the column to the top of the barrier sand to ensure no dilution of tracer, pumping a 30 s pulse of bromide solution followed by deionized water, and collecting 5 mL samples from the column effluent. Columns were then conditioned with 100 empty bed volumes (EBVs) of synthetic stormwater with dissolved organic carbon (DOC; time weighted average = 3.7 ± 1.1 mg C L–1) and challenge tested with synthetic stormwater spiked with a suite of metals and TrOCs including herbicides, insecticides, a corrosion inhibitor, polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), polyaromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), and PFASs. The PFASs were introduced from a previously characterized aqueous film-forming foam (AFFF) received from Peterson Space Force Base, CO.20 Challenge tests ran for ∼500 EBV over several months. The synthetic stormwater consisted of a mixture of salts, catch basin material from Naval Weapons Station Seal Beach (NWSSB), CA, and straw. Detailed synthetic stormwater recipe and water quality and flow rate measurements are recorded in Pritchard et al.12

Batch isotherms were conducted to determine Freundlich sorption parameters of the HiTrOCs and PFASs for biochar and RAC. The procedures for the HiTrOCs isotherms are described previously in Pritchard et al.12 Experiments were conducted by adding increasing amounts of biochar (10 to 250 mg) and RAC (10 to 380 mg) to 1 L amber glass jars (for HiTrOCs) and 250 mL polypropylene (PP) tubes (for PFASs). Synthetic stormwater (1 L) with 9.5 ± 0.7 mg C L–1 DOC was added to 1 L amber jars, 200 mL of synthetic stormwater with 10.5 ± 1.8 mg C L–1 was added to 250 mL PP tubes, and each was spiked with all the contaminants together. Biological growth was inhibited in the batch isotherm studies by adding sodium azide (200 mg L–1) to the HiTrOC samples and autoclaving the synthetic stormwater used in the PFAS samples. The samples were placed onto a horizontal shaker for 90 days (for HiTrOCs) and 1 year (for PFASs). Initial contaminant concentrations were increased by a factor of 2.5 from levels used in the column experiment to 100 μg L–1 HiTrOCs, 250 ng L–1 hydrophobic TrOCs, 900 μg L–1 metals, and the PFAS mixture at 8 μg PFOS L–1 to increase the range of detection, as was necessary due to the large range in contaminant sorption properties. Synthetic stormwater was generated with the same recipe as used in the column experiments, with an as similar as possible background level (and source) of DOC.

Water samples were collected from the batch isotherm samples, column experiment influent, sample ports located at 15 and 30 cm from the inlet, and column effluent (60 cm from the inlet) and placed in 20 mL glass vials (HiTrOCs) and 15 mL centrifuge tubes (PFASs) and frozen at −20 °C until analysis. PFAS samples from the batch isotherm PP tubes were directly transferred to 2 mL microcentrifuge tubes and frozen at −20 °C until analysis. HiTrOC samples were filtered using 0.45 μm glass fiber syringe filters (SF15159, Tisch Scientific, OH), transferred to autosampler vials, spiked with internal standards, and analyzed using liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS).12 PFAS samples were prepared according to Murray et al.21 and analyzed via direct injection on a LC-QToF-MS (X500R; SCIEX, Framingham, MA) operated in negative electrospray mode. Only a limited selection of target analysis results is reported here. Due to the high PFOS level of the AFFF used in this study, some of the previously reported PFASs in the AFFF were below the limit of quantification once diluted.

Model Improvements and Parameter Fitting

The transport model, previously modified for variable influent concentration,11 was enhanced to incorporate time dependent flow rate. This modification linearly interpolates flow rate at each timestep that the system of differential equations is solved, from periodically measured flow rates. The time-dependent flow rate is then used to calculate time-dependent mobile phase advection and dispersion fluxes at all interfaces throughout the column. This improves contaminant transport predictions in situations with variations in flow rate, resulting from suspended catch basin material, biofouling, and use of outlet controls to manage the flow rate. This modification allows for the modeling of non-continuous time-dependent flow conditions.

The model was fit to the observed transport data by finding optimal solute Freundlich sorption parameters (Kf, 1/n) and effective tortuosity factors (τe) that minimized the normalized mean squared error of the difference between model prediction and observed values greater than the limit of quantification. The normalized mean squared error (NMSE) is calculated as

where j is the total number of observations, yi, pred is the predicted concentration at the ith observation, and yi, obs is the observed concentration at the ith observation. NMSE is used to avoid bias in the fitting that favors larger absolute residuals relative to smaller absolute residuals that may have a similar percent difference. This particular form of NMSE is normalized by multiplying the yi, pred and yi, obs to avoid bias toward underpredicting breakthrough, as normalizing the difference of yi, pred and yi, obs by yi, obs inflates error resulting from the case in which the yi, obs > yi, pred relative to cases when yi, obs < yi, pred.

The MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA) fminsearch solver was used to perform the multiparameter optimizations, and the fminbnd solver was used to perform the single-parameter optimizations. Fminsearch is a nonlinear programming solver that finds the local minimum of a multivariable function using the Nelder–Mead simplex algorithm.26 The fminsearch solver first makes a simplex computation around the initial guess by adding 5% increments of each component and modifies the simplex until the local minimum of the error function is reached; thus, the starting sorption and effective tortuosity parameters directly influence the resulting fit parameters. Therefore, the fitting procedure was iterated several times using different start parameters to find the start parameters that resulted in the lowest error. Details regarding the start parameters may be found in the Supporting Information. Fminbnd is a single variable minimizer that finds the minimum output for a function for which the input variable is bounded by a minimum and maximum value.

The fitting process was conducted for contaminant transport data observed for each replicate column at two sample ports 15 and 30 cm from the inlet, and for all available transport data, for which all sample locations (15, 30, 60 cm from the inlet) were weighed equally. PFAS fitting was conducted only at the 30 cm sample port and for all available transport data as breakthrough at the 15 cm sample port was rapid. Observations below the minimum quantification limit were not used in the fitting. As the flow rate, material mass, and engineered media length were column-specific, each column was fitted individually. Since the optimized sorption and effective tortuosity parameters resulting from each column fit are dependent combinations, where the value of one parameter influences the values of the other two, the parameters themselves may not be averaged between column replicates. Instead, each column replicate-derived combination of sorption and effective tortuosity parameters was treated as a unique set of parameters, and the breakthrough curves resulting from using the three unique parameter sets were averaged and plotted with the standard deviation.

Long-Term Performance Modeling and Life-Time Prediction

The intraparticle pore diffusion model and fitting parameters were used to estimate long-term performance for a hypothetical unit filter located in Seal Beach, CA. Seal Beach was chosen because it was the source of the catch basin material used to create the synthetic stormwater used in the column and batch experiments. Long-term performance modeling was conducted using the contaminant transport model trained to the observed contaminant transport at the 15 cm sample port and validated using the observed transport at the column 30 cm sample port and effluent (60 cm). The leading breakthrough contaminant (i.e., limiting contaminant) of six HiTrOCs and two PFASs (atrazine, benzotriazole, diuron, fipronil, imidacloprid, mecoprop, PFOS, PFOA) was identified based on typical site parameters, stormwater contaminant concentrations, and available aquatic health benchmarks set by the US EPA27 or the California Drinking Water Notification and Action Levels.28 Conservative design assumptions were made to size the filter. Specifically, the filter was assumed to treat 100% of the precipitation that fell in a unit catchment with the surface area of the filter set to 1% of the catchment area. The flow rate was set to 20 cm h–1 with the filter BC content similar to what was tested in the column experiments and used to fit the model, and the average annual precipitation (29.26 cm) over the last 124 years (1895–2019) was assumed. Tables were constructed to show the estimated filter lifetime in years using both biochar and RAC for filters of various depths.

Modeling Contaminant Transport

Model and material parameters for sand, biochar, and RAC and are shown below in Table 1. Column-specific parameters, including mass and individual column length, and compound-specific parameters are shown in Tables S.2 and S.3 in the Supporting Information. The longitudinal dispersivity, used to calculate the hydrodynamic dispersion (eq S.1), was found by fitting the transport model to the tracer breakthrough observed in the sand columns (Figure S.1). For sand, this is effectively a 1-d advection dispersion transport model, as there was no intraparticle diffusion because the sand grains were assumed to be nonporous. Tracer curves were then fit for the RAC and biochar columns to confirm the longitudinal dispersivity parameter found in the sand columns by introducing an effective tortuosity and apparent linear-sorption coefficient parameter. Including the effective tortuosity factor and apparent bromide linear-sorption coefficient was necessary to reproduce the observed tracer breakthrough using the average longitudinal dispersivity found in the sand columns (Figure S.1). The use of the dispersivity from the sand columns was necessary to reduce the number of parameters optimized for the tracer fitting for the biochar and RAC columns. The necessity of including the apparent bromide linear-sorption coefficient and effective tortuosity factor to model the tracer tests in the BC-amended columns suggest that bromide is weakly interactive when used in BC-amended media. Further, effective tortuosity factor values outside the accepted tortuosity range for black carbon of 1–629,30 suggest an additional pore-diffusion hindrance factor, as postulated by Portmann et al.13 and Ball and Roberts.25 In the case of the tracer tests, the additional pore-diffusion hindrance may be due to electrostatic repulsion between the negatively charged bromide and the negatively charged surfaces of the BC. The larger effective tortuosity factor values observed in the RAC relative to the biochar may be due to the smaller pores of the RAC, which could increase pore diffusion hindrance.12

As was observed for the tracer tests, the fitted effective tortuosity parameters exceed the typical range of material tortuosity and therefore likely also include other pore-diffusion hindrance factors, such as solute steric hindrance, solute electrostatic repulsion and interaction with the BC surface, and BC pore-blocking from background DOC25,31 among others. Mecoprop, the only non-PFAS contaminant studied that exists as an anion under the experimental conditions, has the highest effective tortuosity value among the HiTrOCs (τe, biochar ∼ 67–74; τe, RAC ∼ 31–37). The two perfluorocarboxylates (PFCAs) measured here (PFHxA and PFOA) generally have higher effective tortuosity parameters than the perfluoroalkyl sulfonates (PFPeS, PFHxS, and PFOS), suggesting increased intraparticle pore diffusion hindrance in the BC grains.

The Freundlich sorption coefficients (Kf) are generally lower for the PFASs than the HiTrOCs. Within the HiTrOCs, the order of Freundlich sorption coefficients intensity generally matches the order of compound retention. The Freundlich sorption coefficient is considered to be the capacity parameter while the slope factor () is generally considered to be the intensity parameter and may hint at the complexity of sorption. The nonlinearity of the isotherms, noted by the low slope factors, is considered indicative of heterogeneous interactions with different types of sorption sites and interactions that may be dependent on water chemistry. Thus, the simulation of field conditions for the column experiments when using the empirical Freundlich isotherm to describe sorption is crucial to ensuring the modeling approach’s environmental relevance.

Plots of the predicted breakthrough and observed transport at each sample port are shown in Figure 1 and Figure S.5 (for the parameters fit using all available data at all sample locations) and in Figures S.2–S.4 in the Supporting Information (for the 15 and 30 cm sample ports, respectively). The fluctuations in the prediction are due to variability in both the flow rate and influent concentration during the tests, described in Pritchard et al.12 The short-term fluctuations are likely due to the variable nature of the column flow rate and show the significant impact flow rate has on contaminant transport. The greater fluctuations in the RAC predictions suggest that RAC may be more sensitive to variation in flow rate than biochar, which may be indicative of greater sensitivity of RAC’s contaminant removal kinetics compared to biochar and potentially due to the greater effective tortuosity observed within RAC.

The NMSE of each modeled prediction using the three sets of fitting parameters was computed and is shown in Table S.5 in the Supporting Information. For both the biochar and RAC columns, the sum of NMSE (equally weighted among the three sample locations) was generally highest for the parameters fit at the 15 cm port (HiTrOCs: NMSEbiochar = 81.8 ± 147.6, NMSERAC = 3.1 ± 1.7), lower for the 30 cm sample port(HiTrOCs: NMSEbiochar = 21.6 ± 29.1, NMSERAC = 2.9 ± 1.7), and lowest for the column effluent (HiTrOCs: NMSEbiochar = 28.0 ± 45.9, NMSERAC = 0.9 ± 0.5), indicating that sorption and effective tortuosity parameters fit further down the column and by including the most transport data better predicted contaminant transport than parameters fit to the contaminant transport data at the 15 cm sample port. Indeed, standard deviations for the contaminant transport predictions seen in Figure 1 are largest for the predictions made by parameters fit from the 15 cm sample port. The effective tortuosity and sorption parameters fit using the transport data further down the column appear to be more accurate and are likely better estimates of the sorption and tortuosity values. The greater accuracy of the sorption parameters fit from further down the column may be due to less and/or delayed fouling from microbial activity and DOC that was present within the top 15 cm of the column and may reflect greater sensitivity to the sorption and effective tortuosity parameters at greater depths.

While the parameters found from the 15 cm sample port are likely not as good estimate of the true compound sorption and effective tortuosity parameters, they are still able to predict contaminant transport at each location in the column and may serve as validated parameters to predict contaminant transport. The model with these parameters closely predicts transport at the 30 cm sample port and overestimated breakthrough in the column effluent. Thus, the model with parameters fit to the 15 cm sample port is valid for conservatively predicting contaminant transport in similar BC filters.

The Freundlich sorption parameters observed in the batch tests and those derived from the column-fit data are similar. A notable exception is FHxSA, a perfluoroalkane sulfonamide, for which the isotherm-derived linearized isotherm shows greater sorption capacity than the column-test derived linearized isotherm. FHxSA is known to be a transitory PFHxS precursor32−34 and may have formed from precursors present in the AFFF during the column experiment, as concentrations of FHxSA measured in the sample ports and column effluent exceeded the influent. The slope factors derived from the column data are generally less than the batch isotherms, while the intercepts are considerably higher for the column-fit isotherms. Thus, it appears that the capacity of the carbon in the batch tests was consistently lower than in the column experiments. Since a period of 90 days is considered adequate for assuming equilibrium,13 the difference in estimated isotherm parameters is more likely due to systematic difference between a static, abiotic batch and continuous, biologically active column system, particularly with respect to the effects competitive sorption and DOC fouling.

While all compounds were initially present in the batch systems, TrOCs became separated as they moved through the column, with the better-sorbing compounds being removed earlier in the column and thus providing less competition with poor-sorbing compounds later in the column. Similarly, DOC loading, known to affect TrOC sorption to BC, was different for the batch and column systems. In the batch test, all the BC is subjected to the same initial concentration of DOC (9.5 ± 0.7 mg C L–1) while in the column experiment, the DOC concentration profile in the influent (3.7 ± 1.1 mg C L–1) is reduced as the stormwater travels down the column. Since DOC largely is believed to interact only with the surface of the carbon due to steric limitations and not accumulate inside the carbon, the steady state concentration of DOC is likely to impact TrOC sorption more than the total mass of DOC loading over the course of the experiment. While the DOC was partially removed by the carbon over the course of the batch test, the maximum steady state DOC concentration in the biochar (1.6 mg C L–1) and RAC (1.1 mg C L–1) column effluent was substantially lower than the minimum steady state concentration in the batch test (∼2–10 mg C L–1, Figures S.8 and S.9). Therefore, the BC sorbent at the bottom of the flow-through column saw a lower DOC concentration than the BC in the batch test. As DOC is known to impact TrOC sorption kinetics through slowing pore diffusion by pore-blocking and compete with TrOCs for surface sorption sites,31 the decreased concentration of DOC in the columns likely contributed to increased sorption in the columns relative to the batch tests.

The isotherm-derived parameters were used to fit the observed breakthrough curves from the 15 cm sample port (HiTrOCs) or all available data (PFASs) by conducting a single parameter fit of the effective tortuosity factor. The single-parameter-fit effective tortuosity values are less than those found from fitting the sorption and effective tortuosity parameters together, though the relative order of values is similar. The NMSE of the predicted breakthrough using the isotherm sorption parameters and column fit tortuosity are higher than for the column fit sorption and tortuosity parameter predictions. The model predictions using the isotherm parameters generally over-predicts breakthrough at each sample port, with the over-prediction greater further down the column (Figures S.6 and S.7).

Long-Term Predictive Modeling

Long-term predictive modeling using the column-fit sorption and tortuosity parameters from the 15 cm sample port for the HiTrOCs and 30 cm sample port for the PFASs were used to estimate filter lifetime in a hypothetical case study in Seal Beach, CA. Two situations were modeled: an “acute scenario” in which the stormwater influent was assumed to contain the highest observed levels of contaminants, and a “chronic” scenario in which the stormwater influent was assumed to contain the median levels of observed contaminants. For the “acute scenario”, relevant acute environmental health benchmarks for invertebrates and California Drinking Water Action and Maximum Contaminant Levels were used to assess filter breakthrough, while the chronic environmental health benchmarks and California Drinking Water Maximum Contaminant Goal and Notification Levels were used to assess filter breakthrough for the “chronic scenario”, as shown in Table 4. Some of the contaminants, such as atrazine and mecoprop, have benchmarks that are greater than what has been observed in stormwater runoff, suggesting that these compounds may pose little risk to aquatic or human health at the levels found in stormwater. Benzotriazole does not have US EPA Aquatic Health or US EPA/California Drinking Water Benchmarks.

For biochar filters operated at 20 cm h–1 in the acute scenario, a depth of 0.4 m is necessary to adequately remove PFOS for 16.3 ± 2.8 years, while a depth of 0.6 m is expected to remove PFOS for 34.6 ± 6.3 years. RAC filters with depths less than 0.25 m perform worse than biochar filters for PFOS removal, yet perform better at depths greater than 0.25 m. The difference in performance for biochar and RAC is indicative of the differing material-specific contaminant removal kinetics. RAC filters in the acute scenario operated at a flow rate of 20 cm h–1 must be at least 0.25 m in depth to achieve sufficient PFOS and PFOA removal and are expected to remove PFOS for 25 ± 8.1 years at a depth of 0.4 m and for 51.3 ± 16.7 years at a depth of 0.6 m.

Biochar filters operated at a flow rate of 20 cm h–1 in the chronic scenario perform better than in the acute scenario, requiring a depth of 0.3 m to adequately remove PFOS for 13.9 ± 4.0 years while a depth of 0.6 m may remove PFOS for 57.4 ± 10.2 years, roughly twice as long as in the acute scenario. Interestingly, RAC requires greater filter depths in the chronic scenario to remove PFOS and fipronil to their respective benchmarks (minimum depth of 0.4 m for PFOS), likely due to greater removal needed for the chronic scenario (88% vs 74%) even though the influent concentrations are lower than for the acute scenario (55 ng L–1 vs 156 ng L–1). RAC filters operated at 20 cm h–1 in the chronic scenario with a depth of 0.4 m are expected to remove PFOS for 19.0 ± 18.4 years while filters with a depth of 0.6 m are expected to last 71.6 ± 24.0 years. Similar to the acute scenario, filters of lower depths are expected to perform better with biochar while RAC filters perform better at greater depths.

The standard deviations on the performance estimates indicate the modeling uncertainty and suggest that BC filter depths should be on the order of 0.5 m or more for greater confidence of PFAS removal. Increasing the filter depth disproportionately increases the filter lifetime, demonstrating the impact of contaminant removal kinetics on filter performance. In other words, it is not a linear relationship; doubling the depth (0.25 m to 0.5 m) more than doubles the lifetime (9 ± 3.4 years to 40.6 ± 7.8; Table 5). Interestingly, increasing the filter depth appears to increase the filter lifetime more at greater depths. RAC appears to be more limited by contaminant-removal kinetics than biochar, as the RAC filters exhibit worse contaminant removal than the biochar for shorter filter depths even though the RAC filters contain more carbon by mass (3.3 wt % RAC, 2.4 wt % biochar). The influent concentration and target removal also impact the filter lifetime significantly, as the filter performance is generally superior for the chronic scenario than the acute scenario, especially at greater filter depths. Filter performance and lifetime estimates are sensitive to the concentration of the influent, and higher concentrations would likely result in shorter lifetimes and possibly inadequate treatment.

Practical Implications

The sorption-retarded intraparticle pore diffusion transport model, modified to incorporate variable influent flow rate, adequately predicted contaminant transport within the BC-amended columns with 60 cm depth. The sorption parameters fit using all of the available transport data appeared to best predict breakthrough; however, further experiments are necessary to validate these parameters for future predictive modeling use.

Past studies have attempted to use batch tests to derive sorption and tortuosity parameters to predict contaminant transport in column filters.9,10 While this method is more feasible than conducting long-term column studies for testing many different materials, batch test-derived parameters appear to be limited with respect to how well they predict contaminant transport. Portmann et al.13 similarly reported that sorption parameters from batch equilibrium data, while strong indicators of sorption capacity, were poor estimators of column performance, likely due to kinetics. Batch test-derived sorption parameters have value for comparing different BC materials, but results from this work illustrate that flow-through tests with a suite of contaminants including representative DOC and catch basin material better characterize column performance and kinetic factors. Given the wide range of compound–compound and compound–BC interactions that TrOCs are likely to experience, particularly by hydrophilic and zwitterionic compounds with functional moieties, column experiments are likely to be more useful for fitting contaminant transport models.

This modeling approach does have limitations, however. While the Freundlich isotherm may be convenient for describing sorption, this empirical model of sorption is overly simplistic and does not consider the many known mechanisms of compound–sorbent interactions. Thus, complex chemical interactions are not captured by the model, and so impacts on contaminant transport by changes in water quality, such as DOC, pH, or conductivity, will not be captured by the model. Incorporating more advanced sorption mechanisms into the model would greatly improve the model’s applicability to a wide range of circumstances and would make the modeling approach less dependent on the experimental conditions used to fit the model.

However, this modeling approach is useful for better understanding TrOCs transport in BC-amended stormwater filters to aid improved filter design. Engineered media filters using relatively large grain sizes (1.68–0.42 mm; 12–40 mesh) and operated at fast flow rates (∼20 cm h–1) are sensitive to kinetic considerations, as demonstrated by the filter case study and breakthrough curve shapes. This finding has significant implications for stormwater runoff filter design, as a sufficient contact time must be achieved to remove the contaminants, especially when faster flow rates and larger sized grains of engineered media are used. Past studies investigating smaller grain sizes and slower flow rates appear to be less sensitive to kinetics as filters with similar dimensions and carbon content have predicted longer lifetimes and have steeper breakthrough curves.6,9,10,13 While the filter lifetime decreases with the larger grain size material and faster flow rates, long lifetimes (>50 years in the chronic scenario) are still achievable with proper filter design, though proper maintenance and suspended solids removal to prevent clogging are necessary. Future work is needed to better understand how increased flow rates, contaminant loading, and other parameters affecting kinetics such as background DOC influence engineered media filter performance and contaminant transport modeling predictions.

The modeling approach used here, namely, fitting the three column replicates individually and using the three combinations of sorption and tortuosity parameters to generate mean predicted breakthrough with the associated uncertainty, is novel and greatly informs the degree of confidence in the model predictions. This is especially important with small predicted filter lifetimes, where individual column replicate-derived transport models may disagree about the performance that is achievable, yielding standard deviations similar to or even greater than the mean predicted lifetime. In these cases, caution should be applied to the predicted lifetime. Greater use of transport model predictions for sizing and estimating performance of BC-amended engineered media filters requires quantification of uncertainty, and the approach described here is a significant step toward this goal. While overpredicting modeled breakthrough provides a conservative estimate of filter lifetime, overly conservative estimates risk discounting engineered media filter performance, resulting in the over-design of engineered media filters and decreasing their utility under a cost–benefit analysis. Further work is needed to better understand the accuracy of predictive modeling under field conditions.
