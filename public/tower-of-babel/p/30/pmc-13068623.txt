Correspondence on: “Lateral thoracic myocutaneous flap transfer for post-sternocleidomastoidectomy neck deformity: Anatomy study and case report”
Authors: Kotina Shridevi, Megha Doiphode, Rakhi Mishra, Archana Dhyani
Published: 2026

Dear Sir,

Li et al. present an anatomically grounded reconstruction strategy combining a lateral thoracic myocutaneous flap with medial pectoral nerve transfer for restoration after sternocleidomastoidectomy.1 The integration of cadaveric mapping with clinical application represents an ambitious attempt to address both contour deformity and functional compromise in a single-stage procedure.

The anatomical dataset, although systematically recorded, warrants further contextualization regarding surgical reliability. The lateral thoracic artery was absent on one side and displayed duplication on another, while common trunk configurations were observed in several specimens. Such variability may influence intraoperative decision-making, particularly when flap perfusion must withstand postoperative radiotherapy. Although mean diameters approximated 2.1 mm, the reported standard deviations suggest meaningful dispersion. In microvascular or pedicled flap surgery, even submillimetric differences may alter flow dynamics and resistance, especially in previously irradiated fields.2 Future work may benefit from correlating vessel caliber and pedicle length with perfusion assessment techniques such as indocyanine green angiography to determine whether anatomical consistency translates into reproducible intraoperative vascular stability.

Second, the functional rationale for medial pectoral nerve coaptation to the accessory nerve deserves deeper electrophysiologic validation. The sternocleidomastoid muscle exhibits parallel fiber orientation and specific recruitment patterns during cervical rotation and flexion. The medial pectoral nerve primarily innervates the abdominal head of the pectoralis major, whose firing patterns are linked to shoulder adduction and internal rotation.3 Without objective postoperative electromyographic studies or quantitative strength testing, it remains uncertain whether the reinnervated construct restores coordinated cervical kinetics or merely achieves passive bulk. The reported Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand and M. D. Anderson Dysphagia Inventory scores indicate mild persistent dysfunction, but these instruments are not specific to sternocleidomastoid biomechanics. Incorporation of dynamometric cervical rotation measurements and electromyographic mapping would clarify whether neural integration achieves functional synergy rather than structural substitution.

Third, the oncologic context merits consideration. The presented case involved hypopharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma treated with neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy followed by adjuvant radiotherapy. Vascularized composite tissue transfer into irradiated beds is biologically plausible, yet radiation-induced endothelial injury and fibrosis may evolve beyond 24 months.4 Longitudinal imaging or duplex ultrasonography assessing pedicle patency over extended follow-up would help determine whether the described reconstruction maintains durable perfusion and contour stability in the setting of multimodal cancer therapy.

This report introduces a creative reconstructive option that expands the conceptual repertoire for complex cervicoscapular defects. Further prospective evaluation integrating perfusion analytics, electrophysiologic validation, and long-term vascular surveillance may help define the reproducibility and functional hierarchy of this promising approach within head and neck reconstructive algorithms.
