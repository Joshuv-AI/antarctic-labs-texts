8-azido-3,8-dideoxy-D-manno-octulosonic acid-mediated metabolic oligosaccharide engineering for specific labeling of diverse Enterobacterales
Authors: Eden Laing, Leonardo Boldt, Suwannee Ganguillet, Emma Slack, Lisa Maier, Nicolas Beziere
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCurrent microbiome studies lack real-time or spatial information, often requiring large cohorts for longitudinal investigations. Real-time, in vivo molecular imaging would alleviate these shortcomings but is hampered by the limited labeling performance of current strategies. Here, we use the azido-modified sugar 8-Azido-3,8-dideoxy-D-manno-octulosonic acid (Kdo-N3) to metabolically engineer the core oligosaccharide of Gram negative lipopolysaccharide, enabling conjugation with DBCO-fluorophores. We extend this approach by systematically screening multiple Enterobacteriaceae strains, optimizing labeling conditions, validating core-oligosaccharide-specific labeling using LPS knockout mutants, and demonstrating in vivo species-specific MOE-BCC labeling with Kdo-N3 inside the murine gastrointestinal tract and subsequent in vivo imaging. Beyond Salmonella Typhimurium and Escherichia coli Nissle 1917, a broad range of Enterobacterales species were amenable to Kdo-N3 sugar incorporation, potentially unlocking species-specific studies of bacterial interaction in vitro and in vivo.Subject areas: Molecular biology, Microbiology, Bioengineering

Introduction

The human gut microbiome is a complex ecosystem of microorganisms residing in the gastrointestinal (GI) tract. These microbes interact with each other and with the host, playing essential roles in processes critical to human health.1 Disruptions to an individual’s gut microbiome have been linked to serious diseases, including colorectal cancer, cardiovascular disease, and type 2 diabetes.1,2,3

Dietary habits and drug intake, particularly medication use, can significantly alter the composition of the gut microbiome.4 Such changes have been associated with increased levels of pathobionts and a higher risk of pathogen colonization.5,6,7 As a result, modulating the gut microbiome is increasingly considered a promising complementary approach for preventing and treating various diseases, including infections with multidrug resistant bacteria, which are on the rise.2,8,9,10,11

However, the molecular and ecological principles underlying effective microbiome modulation strategies remain only partially understood. One reason for this is the limited availability of methods to accurately study gut microbiome dynamics and assess the outcome of potential treatments.

The most widely used methods include in vitro culture, fecal and ex vivo sampling, and sequencing.12,13,14,15 However, these methods are time-consuming, provide a restricted view defined by the time of sampling, and require sacrificing large quantities of animals for longitudinal studies.16,17,18 Real-time in vivo imaging as an alternative would remedy this, allowing for a more extensive understanding of what occurs inside the GI tract in real time. Among the different strategies used for in vivo imaging, optical imaging is the most widely used technique due to the stability of fluorescent signals over time, its general affordability, and its scalability from in vitro methods, such as FACS and microscopy, to in vivo imaging.

In vivo optical imaging is a non-invasive technique that enables real-time visualization of biological processes within living organisms using light-based technologies.19 There are three current major strategies for labeling bacteria when performing in vivo optical imaging. The first requires genetic modification of the bacteria, the second uses non-specific optical contrast agents, and the third is achieved through direct microbe labeling with small fluorophores.20,21,22,23

A genetic engineering strategy has several advantages and disadvantages. While species-specific and cost-effective, the clinical applicability of this method is limited by strict governmental regulatory requirements and societal ethical concerns.24 Further, the applicability and success of this strategy are limited to bacterial species genetically tractable.25 When feasible, genetic engineering is time-consuming and labor-intensive, often requiring several days of preparation.26 Additionally, the synthesis of conventional fluorescent probes, such as GFP, can be impaired under anaerobic conditions, limiting its use for the study of anaerobic microbes.26,27,28 Additional limiting factors include the varying transformation efficiency of the bacteria, the promoter compatibility in the target species and the metabolic burden of expressing the fluorescent proteins, which may inhibit bacterial growth in slow growing or fastidious species.28 There is also a risk of horizontal gene transfer to other species in the gut microbiome once the bacteria are integrated into the population.24,29,30

The use of optical contrast agents, such as fluorescent dyes, provides a second strategy for bacterial labeling. This strategy overcomes some of the issues plaguing the genetic labeling strategy—such as requiring little effort or time to implement. However, disadvantages of this technique include a poor signal to noise ratio from the dyes in the body and lack of species-specificity.31

Direct microbe labeling provides an alternative to circumvent some of the issues mentioned above. Different strategies using targeted optical agents have been described.32,33 Recently, metabolic oligosaccharide engineering (MOE) followed by bioorthogonal click chemistry (BCC)-based labeling techniques have emerged as a promising and flexible mechanism for direct microbe labeling.30,34,35,36,37,38 MOE refers to the modification of bacterial lipopolysaccharides (LPSs) through the introduction, and subsequent endogenous metabolism, of synthetic sugar derivatives containing a chemical tag (Figure 1A).30,34,35,36

BCC refers to a set of biologically compatible and highly thermodynamically favorable reactions used to connect molecular building blocks.34,39,40 These reactions are broad in scope, high yielding and result in few or no by-products while being reactive under physiological conditions.34,40 During copper-free strain-promoted alkyne azide cycloaddition (SPAAC), a strained alkyne is capable of reacting spontaneously with an organic azide, resulting in the formation of a covalent bond (Figure 1B).34,40

Recently, MOE targeting the O-antigen of the LPS was used to fluorescently image Bacteroides fragilis (Bf) in vivo.41 This allowed for specific and stable labeling without the need for genetic modification of the bacterial species. However, so far, it has remained unclear whether this strategy can be applied to other bacterial species. Additionally, most MOE-BCC for bacterial labeling have been focusing on the use of natural sugars to introduce the azide function on the bacterial surface, preparing it for click labeling with constrained alkynes. However, these studies show in general limited numbers of bacterial species tested for labeling with this procedure, with results ranging from high labeling ratio to poor efficacy.30

To overcome the limitations in translation and labeling efficacy observed with the O-antigen targeting methodology, we speculated that targeting the more conserved core oligosaccharide for MOE would prove more efficient and broadly applicable. This part of the oligosaccharide is less variable across strains and species of bacteria,42,43 and modified versions of this sugar are likely to be more tolerated by bacterial cells. To target this segment of the LPS, the bacterial species are cultured in the presence of azane; (4R,5R,6R,7R)-8-azido-4,5,6,7-tetrahydroxy-2-oxooctanoic acid (Kdo-N3), an analog of 3-deoxy-D-manno-oct-2-ulosonic acid (Kdo) that presents an azide functional group,30,37,38 which has been shown to incorporate specifically, via the sialic acid transporter NanT, into the LPS core oligosaccharide of the Gram negative bacteria Escherichia coli K12 and Klebsiella pneumoniae.37,38 Kdo is an essential component of the core oligosaccharide structure in all Gram negative bacterial species, excluding the Shewanella genus, which utilizes 8-amino-8-deoxy-Kdo instead.44 It is also a sugar that is supposedly absent in mammals, rare in most other organisms except in a few algae and higher plant species, but abundant across Gram negative bacteria.45,46,47 It has been utilized previously to label Gram negative bacteria for in vivo imaging of the mouse GI tract; however, we aim to adapt this methodology by using BCC rather than copper (1-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition) for a more biocompatible methodology. Additionally, our methodology aims to perform species selective labeling rather than whole-batch labeling of Gram negative bacteria and thus, is modified to do so—while still retaining an in vivo click.48

In this study, we investigate and fully characterize the direct labeling of several Gram negative bacterial species utilizing MOE-BCC targeting the core oligosaccharide of the LPS. We demonstrate Kdo to be a more promising candidate than the O-antigen for MOE-BCC for Enterobacteriaceae, achieving successful labeling in a variety of pathogens, commensals and potential probiotics of interest, without the need for genetic modification. Our approach focuses on carbohydrate metabolism as azide functionalized carbohydrates are readily available, increasing the dissemination potential of our method. Targeting the lipid A would require a radically different approach, not commercially accessible, and potentially limiting the access to the azide by the DBCO-fluorophore. Our results indicate that our simple, translatable procedure enables the monitoring of a wide range of bacterial species, and supports in vivo species-specific BCC labeling and imaging, providing the basis for potential future in vivo studies. This could allow for real-time visualization of bacterial colonization, invasion, and probiotic protection within the gut microbiome or other biological systems for other species of interest.

Results

Fluorescent labeling of the LPS O-antigen shows limited cross-species applicability

To determine whether the MOE-BCC targeting the O-antigen of the LPS (Figure 1) could be applied to Gram negative bacteria other than B. fragilis (Bf), the bacterial outer membranes of Salmonella Typhimurium (STm) and E. coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) were functionalized with an azide moiety, and quantification of the labeling efficiency was performed by “clicking” the constrained alkyne dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO)-linked fluorescent agents to the metabolically incorporated azide moieties, followed by flow cytometry analysis (Figure 2). After performing growth curves that confirmed the chosen simple sugars had no significant impact on the growth of EcN or STm (Figure S1A), MOE was performed within a range of concentrations (10–200 μM) of various acetylated simple sugars (either GalNAz, GlcNAz, or ManNAz) for different reaction times (from 5 min to 4 h) to determine the simple sugar with the highest efficacy for metabolic labeling and its optimal conditions in the main example species EcN and STm (Figure S2). GalNAz turned out to be the most efficient azide modified simple sugar, achieving significant labeling above the negative control samples (n = 3 biological replicates, Tukey two-way ANOVA test, with a p value of 0.0404) when used at a concentration of 20 μM for 4 h in EcN. GalNAz achieved the greatest percentage of fluorescently labeled EcN cells (mean of 21% ± 7) and STm cells (mean of 19% ± 0) when metabolically engineered at a concentration of 150 or 100 μM, respectively, and labeled with a click chemistry incubation period of 4 h. For this reason, we focused on this azidosugar for further testing (Figure 3A and 3B). Using 100 μM GalNAz followed by a 30 min incubation with DBCO-AF488, 47% ± 16 of Bf cells, our reference for the success of labeling in the other species, were successfully labeled with DBCO-AF488 (Figure 3C). However, this labeling was not significantly different from our negative control, where labeling was performed with DBCO-AF647 without prior metabolic engineering with GalNAz.

Fluorescence microscopy analysis confirmed the presence of the bound fluorophore (AF488) in the outer bacterial membrane (Figure 3D). This provided further validation of the flow cytometry results, with the bound fluorophore localized to the outer membrane of the Bf cells, as indicated by the green fluorescent signal circling the exterior of the cells. When overlayed, this signal matched the signal from the membrane stain FM4-64FX. However, when both EcN and STm were metabolically engineered with GalNAz under the same conditions that produced significant covalent labeling for Bf, the percentage of cells fluorescently labeled was not significantly different from those in the negative control groups where MOE was not performed prior to incubation with DBCO-AF488 (n = 3 biological replicates, Tukey two-way ANOVA test). Here, only a mean of 6% ± 2 of EcN and 10% ± 4 of STm cells metabolically engineered with GalNAz produced a fluorescent signal, compared to the mean of 5% ± 4 of EcN and 9% ± 1 of STm negative control cells producing a fluorescent signal. These results were mirrored in the fluorescent microscopy images captured, with neither EcN nor STm having distinct localization of AF488 at the exterior of the cells (Figure 3D).

Thus, targeting the O-antigen of the LPS appeared limited in its translatability to other species beyond Bf, with neither EcN nor STm having a significant percentage of cells fluorescently labeled (Figure 3).

MOE-BCC of the LPS core oligosaccharide enables fluorescent labeling of Escherichia coli Nissle and Salmonella Typhimurium

To overcome these limitations, we targeted a more conserved core oligosaccharide region of the LPS of the outer membrane of Gram negative bacteria. The core oligosaccharide 3-deoxy-d-manno-oct-2-ulsonic acid (Kdo) sugar was selected due to its widespread presence in the LPS of different Gram negative bacterial species (Figure 4A). After performing growth curves that confirmed Kdo-N3 had no significant impact on the growth of EcN or STm (Figure S1B), the MOE-BCC labeling protocol was performed with the azide modified Kdo sugar (Kdo-N3) and the resulting cells analyzed via flow cytometry. A mean of 89% ± 13 of EcN and 92% ± 7 of STm cells were able to achieve fluorescent labeling (Figure 4B, 4C, and 4D). These results were significantly different (n = 3 biological replicates, Tukey two-way ANOVA test, p values ˂ 0.0001) from the negative control samples, which achieved a mean labeling of only 7% ± 2 and 10% ± 4, respectively. Heat-killed controls were compared to the labeled cells after staining with propidium iodide without statistically significant differences, indicating the viability of the cells after labeling (Figure S3). Fluorescence microscopy analysis revealed that performing MOE with Kdo-N3 resulted in AF488 fluorescence in the outer membrane of both EcN and STm cells (Figure 4E).

Blocking assay shows specificity of the click-chemistry reaction

With significant fluorescent labeling achieved by targeting the core oligosaccharide for species previously unable to be labeled, the specificity of the labeling reaction to the intended click chemistry step needed to be confirmed. Validation of the specificity of the labeling reaction was performed through a blocking assay in which the azide reaction site was blocked via a click reaction with a biocompatible but nonreactive moiety conjugated to DBCO. This was performed prior to the click chemistry step with DBCO-AF488 in the original labeling protocol (Figure 5A). In this assay, the blocking step consisted of a 1 h incubation of DBCO-NH2 in excess with azide-labeled bacteria. Blocking led to a significant reduction in labeling efficiency in both EcN and STm (4- and 3-fold respectively, n = 2 biological replicates, Šidák two-way ANOVA test, p < 0.0001) (Figure 5B).

Characterization of the kinetics of the labeling reaction reveals optimal reaction conditions

With the mechanism of labeling confirmed, full characterization of the labeling method was performed through time- and concentration-screening assays for the example species EcN (Figure 5C) and STm (Figure 5D) (n = 3 biological replicates). A range of Kdo-N3 concentrations (from 10 μM to 2 mM), as well as 4 different click chemistry reaction times (5, 15, 30, and 60 min), were tested. While the labeling efficacy differed for each species tested across the time points and concentrations, the trends observed were similar. For both species, the percentage of labeled cells increased with increasing Kdo-N3 concentrations and longer BCC incubation times, but no plateau of percentage of labeled cells could be reached even after 1 h of conjugation. A greater extent of fluorescent labeling was achieved at lower time points and concentrations of Kdo-N3 in STm, which has a more extensively branched LPS structure.

Proliferation leads to diminution of fluorescence signal

The duration of fluorescent labeling was also examined in EcN and STm and a limit of detection, defined as the time after which fluorescent labeling can no longer be detected above the signal obtained from negative control samples, was determined for each species (Figure 5E and 5F). Here the main example species were metabolically engineered with 1 mM of Kdo-N3 before labeling with DBCO-AF488 and cells were monitored every 2 h by flow cytometry, until the fluorescent signal was not detectable over the negative control (n = 3 biological replicates). Under these optimal in vitro conditions, labeling decreased rapidly for EcN, with no significant labeling detectable within 4 h (Figure 5E). For STm, the labeling was maintained for a longer period, with a significant fluorescent signal detected at 6 h after labeling (Figure 5F). This aligns with STm’s slightly longer doubling time and higher labeling efficiency compared to EcN.

The strategy is broadly applicable to bacterial taxa of the Enterobacteriaceae family

To ascertain the broad applicability of the MOE-BCC labeling methodology with Kdo-N3, several other bacterial species, selected for their key roles in the gut microbiome, in several tumor microbiomes, and as pathogens in human health, were screened. This panel of bacterial species included both Gram negative and Gram positive bacterial species, anaerobic and aerobic bacterial species, and commensal and pathogenic bacterial species to allow for a better understanding of how this methodology performs across species from different families (Figure 6). As expected, no Gram positive species were labeled with a significant difference from negative control samples (where no MOE with azidosugar was performed). Several species of the Enterobacteriaceae family (Klebsiella pneumoniae, Shigella sonnei, STm, EcN, and EcED1α) were successfully labeled with significant differences (n = 2 biological replicates, Šidák two-way ANOVA test, all with p values below 0.0001) from the negative controls in the percentage of fluorescently labeled bacterial cells. MOE-BCC of Klebsiella pneumoniae with Kdo-N3 resulted in a mean of 98% ± 1 of cells being fluorescently labeled. Similarly, high labeling ratios could be seen in Shigella sonnei with a mean of 81% ± 9 of cells labeled. However, for several other Gram negative species (including the majority of those in the Bacteroides family), there were large percentages of cells, as high as 93% ± 4 in the case of Porphyromonas gingivalis, producing a fluorescent signal in the negative control samples. This nonspecific intercalation of the fluorophore was in most cases close to a 1 to 1 ratio with the labeling observed in the Kdo-N3 treated samples, indicating that the MOE-BCC strategy does not work for most of the Bacteroides family members tested here. An exception to this was Bacteroides uniformis, with 20% ± 4 of cells being labeled significantly as opposed to only 6% ± 5 when no MOE was performed prior to the click chemistry step.

LPS mutant profiling confirms Kdo-N3 incorporation and reveals O-antigen dependent shielding of Kdo-N3

To further confirm Kdo-N3 incorporation and elucidate the mechanism of labeling, an assay profiling STm SB300, STm ATCC14028, and its Δrfc, ΔrfaB and ΔrfaZ gene deletion mutants was performed (Figure 7) (n = 2 biological replicates, Tukey two-way ANOVA test). STm SB300 and STm ATCC14028 wild types were both significantly labeled, with 64% ± 10 and 62% ± 6 of cells fluorescent respectively. The percentage of cells labeled was not significantly different between the two wildtype strains. The core oligosaccharide mutant ΔrfaZ, a knockout of Kdo (III) transferase, had a significant reduction in the percentage of cells labeled, with only 31% ± 5, compared to its wildtype STm ATCC14028. Furthermore, the ΔrfaB mutant, targeting the outer core D-galactosytransferase, and the Δrfc mutant, targeting the O-antigen polymerase, both exhibited an increased percentage of cells labeled, with 74% ± 3 and 75% ± 4 of cells labeled respectively.

In vivo fluorescent imaging demonstrates feasibility of Kdo-N3 mediated in vivo click chemistry in the mouse gastrointestinal tract

To evaluate whether Kdo-N3 modified EcN could be labeled in vivo, we administered DBCO-fluorophore to C57BL/6N mice colonized with PBS, EcN or EcN-N3 and monitored in vivo fluorescence in the GI tract 3, 12 and 24 h after administration (Figure 8A). Whole-body fluorescence imaging revealed robust signal in the GI tract of mice receiving both Kdo-N3 modified bacteria and DBCO-AF647 at t = 12 h, while mice colonized with unmodified bacteria or PBS showed minimal background fluorescence (Figure 8B). Quantitative analysis of the GI tract regions of interest demonstrated statistically significant average radiant efficiency (7 × 108 ± 4 × 108 [p/s/cm2/sr]/[μW/cm2]) in the mice colonized with Kdo-N3 modified EcN (as compared to the control animals), detectable at t = 12 h post-DBCO administration (n = 5 animals, Tukey two-way ANOVA: p < 0,0008), which is consistent with stable incorporation of Kdo-N3 into the bacterial LPS and successful SPAAC-mediated labeling (Figure 8C). This did not persist to the 24 h imaging period with only 4 × 107 ± 2 × 107 (p/s/cm2/sr)/(μW/cm2) observed for the EcN-N3 colonized mice as compared to 6 × 107 and 1 × 108 (p/s/cm2/sr)/(μW/cm2) for the EcN and PBS control mice respectively. At t = 3 h, the control animals had a significantly greater average radiant efficiency (4 × 108 ± 7 × 107 [p/s/cm2/sr]/[μW/cm2], n = 5 animals, Tukey two-way ANOVA, 0,0073; for the PBS and 2 × 108 ± 4 × 107 [p/s/cm2/sr]/[μW/cm2] for the EcN control mice) than the EcN-N3 colonized mice (with 8 × 107 ± 2 × 107 [p/s/cm2/sr]/[μW/cm2]). Ex vivo imaging of the excised GI tract further confirmed the spatial distribution of labeled bacteria, with strongest fluorescence observed in the cecum and colon, consistent with known colonization patterns of EcN (Figure 8D). However upon quantification, no region of interests had an average radiant efficiency that was statistically significant (Figure 8E).

Discussion

In this study, we aimed at establishing and characterizing a widely applicable method for click labeling of reporters, such as fluorophores, on different Gram negative enterobacterial species without the requirement of genetic engineering. The first aim of the study was to fully characterize and optimize the MOE-BCC toolbox in two enterobacterial species of interest, a well-known probiotic (EcN) and a pathogen (STm), to enable future investigations into the modulation of the gut microbiome in an in vivo STm murine infection model and provide mechanistic insights into the labeling strategy. The second aim was to expand the application of the MOE-BCC labeling protocol to additional bacterial species. We lastly wanted to provide a proof-of-concept in vivo study, demonstrating how the strategy could be used for species-specific, longitudinal, real-time, whole-body imaging of Kdo-N3 metabolically engineered bacteria in colonized mice.

Toward this end, several simple sugars (GlcNAz, ManNAz, and GalNAz), which were modified to include an azide moiety, were metabolically engineered to decorate the LPS of Bf, EcN, and STm. GalNAz proved to be the most successful sugar tested for fluorescent labeling of Bf, in accordance to previously published studies and consistent with its natural incorporation of GalNAc-containing glycans into capsular and surface polysaccharides.41 This sugar was the only one tested that showed significantly different results from the negative control, where neither metabolic engineering nor BCC were performed. However, this was not the case when the click chemistry step with the DBCO conjugated fluorophore was performed without the metabolic engineering step with GalNAz. Here, a non-intended interaction likely occurs between Bf and the DBCO-AF647 that does not originate from the desired MOE-BCC reaction yielding a covalent bond. Irrespective of whether Bf labeling only partially depends on the MOE-BCC reaction, the resulting fluorescent signal is still localized to the cellular membrane of Bf as intended. Overall, in contrast to previously published data,41 targeting the O-antigen of the LPS appeared limited in its applicability to other species beyond Bf. Indeed, the toolbox could not be successfully adapted for use in EcN and STm. This is likely due to the highly variable nature of the targeted O-antigen moiety. This region of the LPS is heavily involved in bacterial virulence and host-microbe interactions, and therefore highly species and even strain-specific.49 We hypothesize that this is the result of the highly specialized metabolism and import capacities of each bacterial species during the biosynthesis of the O-antigen, due to its pivotal role in host immune-system activation.30,49 The GalNAz labeling could partially work in Bf because this species naturally incorporates GalNAc-containing glycans into its capsule and surface polysaccharides, and it has the transport and metabolic pathways to process the azido analog GalNAz.41,50 However, we hypothesize the labeling could additionally be occurring as a result of an as-yet unknown interaction between the LPS of Bf and DBCO-AF647 in the GalNAz depleted negative control. This should be investigated further in future studies to elucidate the origin of this nonspecific labeling. In contrast, EcN and STm appear to lack the necessary metabolic and enzymatic machinery to efficiently incorporate GalNAz (and the other simple sugars tested) into its glycans, and its cell envelope properties further limit GalNAz uptake and incorporation.51

The core oligosaccharide segment of the Gram negative LPS is a more conserved region between bacterial strains and species.42,43 We speculated that the series of enzymes that use sugar moieties/metabolites for construction and decoration of this region of the LPS would be less species-specific, with more promiscuous enzymes that would accept azide modified sugars more readily. Kdo is a highly conserved oligosaccharide in the core oligosaccharide of Gram negative species.42,43 By targeting the Kdo sugars of the bacterial lipopolysaccharide structure, both EcN and STm were successfully and significantly labeled. Not only were these bacterial species able to metabolize and integrate the azide-modified sugar Kdo, we were also able to achieve a 3x higher labeling efficacy than previously observed when testing the O-antigen targeting MOE-BCC strategy in these species. Labeling with Kdo is effective in bacteria like EcN because Kdo is an essential and conserved sugar component of its LPS inner core, and its incorporation occurs naturally in this bacterium and other members of the Enterobacteriaceae family.42,43

Two strategies were used to assess the validity of the labeling strategy and confirm that labeling occurred through the expected click chemistry reaction. The first was fluorescent microscopy of the labeled cells to localize the site of integration of the fluorescent agent in the bacterial cells. In both EcN and STm, the fluorescent agent was concentrated at the exterior of the cell. When overlayed with the FM4-64FX membrane stain, this labeling appears to occur deeper in the LPS structure as the signal is closer to the interior of the cellular outer membrane than when the O-antigen was targeted, matching what would be expected due to the placement of Kdo in the LPS core oligosaccharide. The second method employed to confirm the mechanism of labeling was a blocking assay. We postulated that a reduction in the amount of observed fluorescently labeled cells would signal that the mechanism of labeling was through the desired click chemistry reaction of the AF488 conjugated DBCO moiety with the azide moiety presented as part of the LPS of the bacteria. When the assay was performed, a large reduction in the percentage of labeled cells was indeed observed. These results indicate successful conjugation of DBCO-NH2 to azide-presenting bacteria, preventing binding of DBCO-AlexaFluor 488 in the subsequent step. Both of these experiments taken together served to validate that the fluorescent labeling observed after MOE with Kdo-N3 in EcN and STm is indeed due to a highly specific BCC reaction of the DBCO-linked fluorophore with the azide moiety of Kdo. This results in a highly stable covalent bond between the bacteria of interest and the fluorophore used for detection.

With these two validation experiments completed, the labeling toolbox was characterized thoroughly, firstly to optimize the conditions of the labeling method and define the duration of the fluorescent labeling. The concentration of Kdo-N3 utilized and the conjugation duration for the click chemistry reaction to occur were investigated. Optimal conditions were determined for EcN and STm and resulted in high labeling efficiency with lowest concentrations of Kdo-N3 within a reasonable time frame of less than 1 h. The specific percentages of cells fluorescently labeled for each parameter tested varied from species to species (likely due to structural differences in LPS, as well as distinct sugar uptake and metabolic capabilities); however, the trends were consistent. Labeling efficacy increased for all species with increased concentrations of Kdo-N3 (in a window of 10 μM to 2 mM) as well as with increased intervals of incubation of the click chemistry reactants. This increase is steeper with rising intervals of reactant incubation when the concentration of Kdo-N3 is lower. Additionally, lower labeling efficacy was observed in species such as EcN, where limited LPS structures have been reported.52 This result seems at first counter-intuitive as a more limited LPS structure (with a shorter and less branched O-antigen structure) should allow for a greater ease of contact between the two click chemistry moieties. The more extensively branched O-antigen LPS structure of STm could shield the Kdo-linked azide moiety from the DBCO-linked fluorophore, as Kdo is found in the core oligosaccharide of the LPS. A possible reason for this not being the case could be due to more Kdo-N3 residues being metabolized and included in the LPS structures of these extensively branched LPS-forming species. It is possible that these species have more promiscuous enzymes synthesizing their LPS to account for the various sugars that need to be metabolized as part of these branching oligosaccharides.

The final characterization assay performed was a limit of detection assay, in which the duration of the fluorescent labeling was examined in vitro over 12 h. Under these optimal conditions, where bacterial cells were kept in a logarithmic growth phase, there was a rapid reduction in the percentage of labeled cells over this time period. This reduction was even more drastic for EcN, due to the lower initial efficacy of labeling and the slightly faster doubling times than STm. Both species saw a significant reduction in signal from the initially labeled cells by the 6-h timepoint, leaving the EcN cells no longer detectable. This, however, would not be expected to occur as quickly outside of optimal culture conditions - such as within an in vivo environment. Although slow-growing species have not yet been investigated, they would be expected to retain their fluorescent signal for a longer duration due to a slower dilution of the Kdo linked AF488 into new cells.

Next, this study sought to investigate was the applicability of this toolbox for labeling additional bacterial species. A panel of bacterial species from different families was screened, and the method proved to be most effective for labeling Proteobacteria (now Pseudomonadota), with nearly all species examined in this phylum being labeled with high significance. This was not the case for the other bacterial families screened, where only Bacteroides uniformis was labeled significantly albeit with a lower efficiency than observed with the Enterobacteriales. The phenomenon observed for the Bacteroidia class of bacteria, with high degrees of fluorescence detected when no azide-modified sugar is added, is perplexing and requires more investigation. Members of the Bacteroidiaceae and the Enterobacteriaceae families exhibit different strategies for sugar uptake and metabolism. Bacteroides species are adapted to the degradation of complex polysaccharides, relying on diverse glycoside hydrolases and polysaccharide utilization loci (PULs),53,54,55 while Enterobacteriaceae preferentially utilize simple sugars through high-affinity transport systems such as the phosphotransferase system (PTS), enabling rapid growth in nutrient-rich environments.56,57,58 These distinct strategies, as well as the specific characteristics of each species’ LPS structure, could explain the differential results observed here, but more research is required into this incidence. A systematic investigation of the structural determinants of LPS and their interaction with both the Kdo-N3 and DBCO moieties is warranted, but is beyond the scope of this study. Based on our results, we hypothesize that the DBCO group may interact non-covalently with LPS or membrane components, contributing to the observed signal in certain species. Due to the success of the technique specific members of the Enterobacteriaceae family, many of whose members are pathogens, commensals and also probiotics, the technique could be used to monitor infection kinetics, study differences in the lifestyle of commensals vs. pathogens or follow probiotic engraftment or similar.

To clarify the reason for the varied labeling efficacy observed across bacterial taxa, the core oligosaccharide targeting MOE-BCC strategy was tested in a panel of STm ATCC14028 LPS mutants alongside the wild type. The Δrfc mutant, lacking enzymes required for Kdo incorporation into the inner core, displayed a significant reduction in labeling when compared to the wild type, with approximately half the percentage of fluorescently labeled cells that were labeled in the wild type. This loss in fluorescent labeling correlates with the absence of the inner core region where Kdo is incorporated and provides a strong demonstration that the labeling observed is due to the biosynthetic incorporation of Kdo-N3 into the LPS core and subsequent click chemistry reaction with the DBCO linked fluorophore rather than nonspecific interactions. The lack of necessary cellular machinery to incorporate Kdo in this mutant, resulting in a reduction in labeling, is likely due to less Kdo-N3 being able to be incorporated into the LPS for future targeting, despite the same culture and labeling conditions. Conversely, the mutants lacking components of the outer core or O-antigen of the LPS structure showed an increase in fluorescent labeling relative to the wild type. This could indicate that the O-antigen and outer core oligosaccharide segments of a fully intact LPS shield the Kdo-N3 reactive site from DBCO-fluorophore access. Removal of these segments in the mutants therefore increased the accessibility of the azide reactive site. Together, these findings demonstrate that the LPS architecture of a bacterial species plays an important role in labeling efficiency. Species with long or heavily decorated O-antigens may naturally shield the azide reactive site, providing a possible explanation for the variable labeling efficiency observed across the Gram negative taxa screened here. After establishing the molecular determinants of successful labeling and confirming the mechanistic basis of the fluorescence signal, we next explored the broader applicability of the approach. Specifically, we examined whether Kdo-N3 metabolic labeling could enable non-invasive in vivo visualization of EcN bacterial colonization within the mouse GI tract following an in vivo BCC reaction.

At the 3 h time point, abdominal region of interest (ROI) analysis unexpectedly revealed that the control mice had a significantly greater average radiant efficiency than the mice that were colonized with EcN-N3. We attribute this difference to the distinct anatomical location of fluorophore in each condition. The metabolically engineered bacteria from the EcN-N3 colonized mice could be higher up in the GI tract at this time point and therefore deeper within the peritoneal cavity as compared to the unbound fluorophore in the control mice which could pass through much quicker to the cecum which lies closer to the abdominal surface. This additional depth would increase the amount of tissue scattering and absorption, resulting in a decreased in detectable signal. This interpretation is consistent with known depth-dependent optical properties of near-infrared imaging and highlights the importance of bacterial localization when assessing early in vivo labeling kinetics. Conversely, at the 12 h time point we observed a significant increase in average radiant efficiency in EcN-N3 colonized mice as compared to control mice. At this time point, EcN is expected to have transitioned to the cecum and upper colon, regions that are anatomically closer to the body surface and characterized by reduced tissue attenuation, resulting in a stronger and more readily detectable fluorescent signal. Additionally, enough time has passed at this time point for a significant portion of the unbound fluorophore to be excreted, resulting in the lower average radiant efficiency observed for the control mice. Finally, at the 24 h time point, the differences in average radiant efficiency are no longer significant—suggesting that bacterial proliferation had diluted the incorporated Kdo-N3 to levels below the detection threshold or may have been excreted. This interpretation is supported by the ex vivo imaging results, in which average radiant efficiency values for stomach and cecum ROIs did not differ significantly between EcN-N3-colonized and control mice.

These results demonstrate that the in vivo click chemistry reaction is specific, efficient, and capable of visualizing bacterial colonization within the murine gut. Together, these findings provide a proof-of-concept that Kdo-N3-based metabolic labeling, combined with strain-promoted azide-alkyne cycloaddition, enables non-invasive, real-time imaging of specific gut bacteria in living animals, highlighting its potential for longitudinal studies of microbiome dynamics and probiotic monitoring.

In summary, this study successfully identified and optimized a method for species-specific click chemistry-based labeling of Gram negative bacterial taxa of the Enterobacteriaceae family using customizable reporters more effective and more broadly applicable than previously reported. This was achieved without relying on genetic modification of the organisms of interest, avoiding the main drawbacks of potential interference with the intrinsic behavior of the organism and its behavior within its ecological niche, as well as introduction of potential immunogenicity and cytotoxicity, which is classically the case with fluorescent reporters.59,60

This methodology was demonstrated as a useful toolbox for species-specific in vivo labeling and preclinical in vivo optical imaging of bacteria using EcN as a model organism for colonization in mice. We expect it to be extendable to other specific Gram negative bacteria (pathogens, commensals, and probiotics) for preclinical in vivo imaging without the need for genetic modification.

Targeting the core oligosaccharide of the LPS provides a strategy for efficiently and specifically labeling Gram negative bacterial species, as an alternative to previously published strategies targeting the O-antigen. Additionally, targeting the core oligosaccharide allowed for the labeling of species that previously were not able to be labeled when targeting the O-antigen with the specific simple sugars tested. This methodology has been optimized for in vitro fluorescent labeling of EcN and STm and could be used in future studies to investigate STm infection in vivo as well as the modulation of STm infection with EcN in the GI tract of a murine model. This methodology allows for species-specific labeling of several Gram negative species (predominantly within the Enterobacteriaceae family) without the need for or disadvantages of genetic modification of the bacteria of interest.

Limitations of the study

Limitations include variable performance of the labeling strategy across different families of Gram negative bacteria, with more research needed to understand this phenomenon. Kdo incorporation and subsequent BCC labeling needs to be studied on a case-by-case basis for use in an MOE-BCC strategy for additional bacterial species not screened here to account for differences in metabolism and LPS architecture impacting its efficacy. In future studies, optimization of this toolbox by adapting other imaging modalities could greatly improve the performance of the toolbox, allowing for an extended imaging time (i.e., of several days), with a higher sensitivity and resolution than state of the art methods.

Method details

Growth curves of Escherichia coli Nissle and salmonella typhimurium when treated with azide modified simple sugars

Bacteria were grown overnight at 37°C under aerobic conditions in 12 mL plastic tubes with 5 mL LB medium, shaking at 180 rpm. Bacteria were then diluted to 0.02 OD and 50 μL were plated out with 50 μL of LB media containing azide modified sugars (GalNAz, GlcNAz, ManNAz, or Kdo-N3) at various concentrations (1% DMSO final concentration) in a 96-well flat bottom plate. Plates were sealed with a breathable sealing membrane (Breathe-Easy, Sigma-Aldrich, Z380059). Bacterial growth was tracked by measuring the OD578 every hour for 22 h using a microplate spectrophotometer (Tecan Reader: Infinite 200 PRO). All screening experiments were performed in three biological replicates. For analysis, growth curves were truncated at the transition from the exponential to the stationary phase and the area under the curve (AUC) calculated using the trapezoidal rule. This was normalized to the DMSO controls in the same plate.63

MOE-BCC-labelling of anaerobic bacteria targeting the O-antigen

Media was pre-reduced at least 1 day before use under anoxic conditions in an anaerobic chamber (Coy Laboratory Products Inc.) (2% H2, 12% CO2, rest N2), and experiments were performed under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C unless specified otherwise. Bacteria were grown overnight in 15 mL glass tubes in the anaerobic chamber in 5 mL of mGAM. These samples were then subcultured the following day in 15 mL glass tubes with 5 mL of mGAM, supplemented with either N-azidoacetyl-galactosamine azide (GalNAz) (Sigma-Aldrich, 900915), N-azidoacetyl-glucosamine azide (GlcNAz) (Jena Bioscience GmbH, CLK-1085-5), or N-azidoacetyl-mannosamine azide (ManNAz) (Jena Bioscience GmbH, CLK-1084-5) at a concentration of 10 μM, 20 μM, 50 μM, 100 μM, 150 μM or 200 μM. Next, 1 mL of the bacterial cultures was transferred to 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes and spun down (for 3 min at 11,000 rpm). The cells were then washed three times in 1 mL of 1 x PBS supplemented with 4% FCS. The final pellet was resuspended in 50 μL of 4% FCS in 1X PBS plus 0.5 μL of 2 mM DBCO-AF647 (Jena Bioscience GmbH, CLK-1302A-1) or DBCO-AF488 (Jena Biosciences GmbH, CLK-1278-1). Tubes were incubated (at 37°C while shaking at 700 rpm, protected from light) for 5 min, 30 min, 2h, 5h, or overnight, after which bacteria were pelleted and washed five times with 1 mL of 10% FCS in 1X PBS. After a final wash, cells were resuspended in 1 mL of 1 x PBS and were either used in an experiment or fixed and mounted on a microscope slide for imaging.

MOE-BCC-labelling of aerobic bacteria targeting the O-antigen

Bacteria were grown overnight at 37°C under aerobic conditions in 12 mL plastic tubes with 5 mL LB medium, shaking at 180 rpm, and subcultured the following day in 12 mL plastic tubes with 5 mL LB medium supplemented with either GalNAz, GlcNAz, or ManNAz at a concentration of 10 μM, 20 μM, 50 μM, 100 μM, 150 μM or 200 μM. Next, 1 mL of the bacterial cultures was transferred to 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes and spun down (for 3 min at 11,000 rpm). The cells were then washed three times in 1 mL of 1 x PBS supplemented with 4% FCS. The final pellet was resuspended in 50 μL of 4% FCS in 1X PBS plus 0.5 μL of 2 mM DBCO-AF647 or DBCO-AF488. Tubes were incubated (at 37°C while shaking at 700 rpm, protected from light) for 5 min, 30 min, 2h, 5h, or overnight, after which bacteria were pelleted and washed five times with 1 mL of 10% FCS in 1X PBS. After a final wash, cells were resuspended in 1 mL of 1 x PBS and were either used in an experiment or fixed and mounted on a microscope slide for imaging.

MOE-BCC-labelling of anaerobic bacteria targeting the core oligosaccharide

Media was pre-reduced at least 1 day before use under anoxic conditions in an anaerobic chamber (Coy Laboratory Products Inc.) (2% H2, 12% CO2, rest N2), and experiments were performed under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C unless specified otherwise. Bacteria were grown overnight in 15 mL glass tubes in the anaerobic chamber in 5 mL of mGAM. These samples were then subcultured the following day in 15 mL glass tubes with 5 mL of mGAM supplemented with azane;(4R,5R,6R,7R)-8-azido-4,5,6,7-tetrahydroxy-2-oxooctanoic acid (Kdo-N3) (MedChemExpress, 1380099-68-2) at a final concentration of 2 mM, 1 mM, 500 μM, 100 μM, or 50 μM. Next, 1 mL of the bacterial cultures was transferred to 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes and spun down (for 3 min at 11,000 rpm). The cells were then washed three times in 1 mL of RPMI 1640 medium containing 0.05% bovine serum albumin (RPMI-BSA). The final pellet was resuspended in 50 μL of the RPMI-BSA wash buffer plus 0.5 μL of 2 mM DBCO-AF488. Tubes were incubated (at 37°C while shaking at 700 rpm, protected from light) for 5 min, 15 min, 30 min, or 1h, after which bacteria were pelleted and washed three times with 1 mL of the RPMI-BSA wash buffer. After a final wash, cells were resuspended in 1 mL of 1 X PBS and were either used in an experiment or fixed and mounted on a microscope slide for imaging.

MOE-BCC-labelling of aerobic bacteria targeting the core oligosaccharide

Bacteria were grown overnight at 37°C under aerobic conditions in 12 mL plastic tubes with 5 mL of LB medium, shaking at 180 rpm. These samples were then subcultured the following day in 12 mL plastic tubes with 5 mL LB medium supplemented with azane;(4R,5R,6R,7R)-8-azido-4,5,6,7-tetrahydroxy-2-oxooctanoic acid (Kdo-N3) at a final concentration of 2 mM, 1 mM, 500 μM, 100 μM, or 50 μM. Next, 1 mL of the bacterial cultures was transferred to 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes and spun down (for 3 min at 11,000 rpm). The cells were then washed three times in 1 mL of RPMI-BSA. The final pellet was resuspended in 50 μL of the RPMI-BSA wash buffer plus 0.5 μL of 2 mM DBCO-AF488. In the case of the blocking assay, the cells were incubated at 37°C for 1 h, shaking at 700 rpm in the presence of an excess of DBCO-NH2 (2 μL of a 2 mM stock) suspended in 50 μL of RPMI -BSA and washed thrice with 1 mL of RPMI-BSA wash buffer prior to incubation with DBCO-AF488. Tubes were incubated (at 37°C while shaking at 700 rpm, protected from light) for 5 min, 15 min, 30 min, or 1h, after which bacteria were pelleted and washed three times with 1 mL of the RPMI-BSA wash buffer. In the case of the limit of detection assay, the labeled cells were inoculated back into an excess of LB medium to ensure growth in the logarithmic phase and 1 mL of cells were removed every 2 h for a period of 25 h. These cells were washed three times with the RPMI-BSA wash buffer. For all experiments following a final wash, cells were resuspended in 1 mL of 1 X PBS and were either used in an experiment or fixed and mounted on a microscope slide for imaging. In the case of the viability assay, 5 μL of propidium iodide was added to all the cells after resuspension in 1 mL of 1 x PBS and the heat-killed cell controls were incubated at 80°C for 15 min prior to flow cytometry. All experiments referring to STm are referring to STm SB300 unless otherwise specified as in the LPS knockout assay.

Flow cytometry

Fluorescently labeled bacterial cells were diluted in 1 x PBS to a concentration where less than 1500 events per second were detected and measured as counts of fluorescent events via flow cytometry analysis using a ForTessa Lsr II Flow Cytometer from BD biosciences. Forward Scatter and Side Scatter were used to identify viable single-cell events. Negative controls without added fluorophore were used for gating nonfluorescent cells vs. fluorescent cells. Heat-killed cells were used as a positive control for propidium iodide staining and used for gating nonviable cells vs. viable cells. Fluorescence intensity was measured in specific channels corresponding to the DBCO-conjugated fluorescent agent used for labeling (AF647 channel for AF647 or GFP channel for AF488 or PI channel for Propidium iodide) in each experiment. Acquired data was analyzed using FlowJo software and reported as percentage of fluorescently labeled bacteria for a total of 100,000 events.

Fluorescence microscopy

Following the MOE-BCC labeling protocols, cells were diluted to an OD of 0.2 in 1 x PBS. In a 0.5 mL Eppendorf tube, 8.5 μL of the suspended cells were incubated with 0.5 μL of the membrane stain FM4-64FX (ThermoFischer Scientific, F34653) and 1 μL of the DNA stain DAPI dye (Sigma-Aldrich, D9542-1 MG) for 1 min. Cells were then washed with 20 μL of 1 x PBS before fixing with 10 μL of 4% PFA (ThermoFischer Scientific, J19943.K2) for 10 min followed by an additional washing step with 25 μL of 1 x PBS. Cells were then mounted, viewed and images were captured with an eclipse Ti2 Nikon fluorescence microscope in 4 channels (phase contrast, Texas Red, DAPI, and GFP). Images were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Whole-body in vivo optical imaging

Mice were gavaged with 20 mg streptomycin in 100 μL of sterile 1 x PBS 24 h prior to colonization with EcN-N3.64 Food and water were withheld for 4 h prior to streptomycin treatment and returned immediately following this. For colonization, mice were orally gavaged with 1 × 109 CFU EcN grown overnight in LB medium with Kdo-N3, washed as described above, and resuspended in 100 μL sterile 1 x PBS. Food and water were again removed 4 h prior to colonization, with water immediately returned and food returned 2 h later. At time zero (t = 0), mice received an oral gavage of DBCO-AF647 to enable the in vivo SPAAC reaction. At the indicated time points (t = 3, 12, 24 h) mice were anesthetized with isoflurane in 100% O2 administered by vaporizer and imaged using the In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS) Spectrum (PerkinElmer). Imaging was performed using the built-in narrow-band excitation and emission filter sets: fluorescence was acquired using the Ex 650 nm/Em 680 nm filter pair and a 25 × 25 cm field of view. All images were analyzed using Living Image software (versions 4.3.1 or 4.4), following the manufacturer’s recommended workflow for fluorescence quantification.

Ex vivo optical imaging of the gastrointestinal tract

Following the 24h in vivo imaging time point, mice were euthanized under anesthesia by CO2 inhalation, followed by cervical dislocation to ensure complete euthanasia. The gastrointestinal tract, from stomach through to the end of the colon, of each mouse was excised from the peritoneal cavity and laid flat on a black, non-reflective background for imaging. Ex vivo imaging was performed using the IVIS Spectrum (PerkinElmer) with the emission and excitation filter as used in the in vivo experiments. Images were processed and analyzed with Living Image software (versions 4.3.1/4.4) following the manufacturer’s standard fluorescence quantification workflow.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All quantitative data was graphed with GraphPad Prism 9 software. Additionally, this software was used to perform the statistical analyses in this study (t test or two-way ANOVA tests as indicated in the figure legends and results), considering p-values of 0.05 or less as statistically significant (∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗p < 0.05). Statistical details of the experiments (number of biological replicates, definition of center and dispersion measures) can be found in the figure legends and results section.
