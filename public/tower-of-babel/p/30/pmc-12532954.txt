Respect, equality, and recognition in practice: a qualitative analysis of nurses’ workplace dignity in China
Authors: Yaping Feng, Ting Xu, Yuting Wang, Jingxuan Zhang, Jingxi Xu, Yu Zhang, Hong Luo, Bowen Xue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWorkplace dignity is a fundamental ethical concern in nursing, directly related to professional identity, human rights, and the moral foundations of care. Despite increasing global attention, few qualitative studies have explored how nurses themselves perceive and articulate workplace dignity, particularly within sociocultural contexts marked by rapid healthcare transformation.MethodsThis qualitative study was conducted in January 2025 at a tertiary hospital in Hangzhou, China. Sixteen registered nurses were recruited through purposive sampling and interviewed using semi-structured face-to-face interviews. Data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s method.ResultsNurses described workplace dignity as a subjective experience shaped by respect, equality, and recognition of self-worth. Influencing factors included sociodemographic characteristics, patient-related factors, organizational factors, and social factors.ConclusionWorkplace dignity is dynamic and socially constructed. Enhancing dignity requires supportive management, fair compensation, positive workplace culture, and improved societal recognition of the nursing profession.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-025-01292-4.

Background

Nurses are the backbone of global healthcare systems, playing a crucial role in the delivery of healthcare services worldwide. Ensuring nurses’ workplace safety is essential for maintaining the stability of healthcare systems and improving the quality of patient care. However, past research has shown that nurses, due to their position at the frontline of patient interactions, are the primary victims of violence, harassment, and bullying in hospitals [1]. A review study showed that the proportion of nurses who experienced sexual harassment in the past 12 months was 12.6% and 53.4% [2]. Additionally, workplace bullying has become increasingly severe, causing significant psychological stress for nurses [3]. The frequent occurrence of these issues makes nurses feel that their work environment is unsafe and that they are not respected. This leads to lower job satisfaction, burnout, and turnover, which can also reduce the quality of healthcare services and jeopardize patient safety [4, 5]. Therefore, understanding the issue of dignity in the workplace for nurses is crucial for ensuring a healthy work environment in hospitals and improving the quality of healthcare services.

Dignity, as one of the core values of human civilization, originates from a long historical evolution within philosophy, religion, and ethics. From symbols of social identity in primitive societies to universal rights in modern society, the concept of dignity has always been closely associated with humanity’s exploration of the meaning of existence. In the workplace, excessive workloads, poor management, abuse, infringement on autonomy, and forced participation are identified as violations of dignity [6]. Bolton (2007) described workplace dignity as encompassing subjective factors such as meaningful work, autonomy, and respect, as well as objective factors like job security, economic rewards, equal opportunities, and safe and healthy working conditions [7]. Lucas provided a detailed definition of workplace dignity as “the value derived from gaining (or harmed by losing) self-recognition and recognition from others through engaging in work activities [8]. Previous research has shown that workplace dignity affects individual job performance and intention to leave the profession [9, 10]. Factors such as work-life balance, ethical leadership, and social influence are important determinants of workplace dignity [10].

Bibliometric analysis shows that research on nursing dignity has been increasing [11, 12]. A large focus group study conducted in Italy showed that nurses’ dignity is severely harmed when they are treated with contempt or indifference, or regarded as “mere assistants“ [13]. In Iran, many nurses reported experiencing insulting treatment from doctors [14]. These findings indicate that nurses in multiple countries face violations of workplace dignity in nursing practices. Previous research has extensively discussed the factors that affect nurses’ workplace dignity [15, 16]. A safe and supportive work environment significantly enhances nurses’ dignity and job satisfaction [17]. Threats to dignity, such as disrespect, high workloads, and lack of support, may lead to intentions to leave the profession [18]. A recent cross-sectional study found that nurses’ average ratings of their intrinsic sense of value and overall dignity were relatively high, but their scores for “respectful interactions” in the workplace were lower, and these were correlated with well-being, job satisfaction, and self-esteem [15]. Other studies have found that workplace relationships are an important factor influencing nurses’ workplace dignity [14]. Additionally, studies have found that nurses who endure disrespect or humiliation experience psychological distress and loss of self-worth, which influences nurses’ health and retention [14, 19].Therefore, ensuring workplace dignity for nurses is both a moral obligation and a practical necessity for healthcare institutions.

Currently, research on nurses’ workplace dignity is limited, and there is a lack of qualitative studies on this topic, restricting the academic community’s deeper understanding of this concept [15, 16, 19]. Therefore, this study aims to use qualitative research methods to explore clinical nurses’ understanding of workplace dignity, key factors influencing workplace dignity, and nurses’ suggestions for enhancing workplace dignity. The study seeks to fill the gap in existing research and provide theoretical and practical guidance for creating a dignity-sensitive healthcare environment.

Methods

Design

This study adopted a phenomenological qualitative design to explore nurses’ perceptions of workplace dignity. Colaizzi’s seven-step method was used as the theoretical and methodological framework to guide data analysis. The reporting of this study followed the COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research) guidelines. See Table S1.

Research team

Our research team consists of 7 members: a professor specializing in organizational psychology who provides guidance, a clinical nursing expert who supervises the study, 4 master’s students with interview experience conducting the interviews, and an experienced researcher who provides guidance and training. The translation of the themes and content was carried out collaboratively by the first author and corresponding author.

Sample and settings

The study was conducted in January 2025 at a tertiary hospital in Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province. The hospital employs approximately 400 nurses. The recruitment advertisement was first shared via the hospital’s internal nursing communication group. A purposive sampling method was used to select nurses from key departments, including internal medicine, surgery, and emergency.

Inclusion criteria: Registered nurses; At least one year of work experience (to ensure familiarity with nursing work).

Exclusion criteria: Trainee nurses; Non-clinical frontline nurses.

The sample size was determined based on data saturation, and interviews were stopped when no new information emerged. Ultimately, after interviewing 15 participants, the research team observed that subsequent interviews did not generate additional codes or themes. To ensure saturation had truly been achieved, an additional interview was conducted; this also produced no new findings. The research team then unanimously confirmed that data saturation had been reached. In total, 16 nurses were interviewed. The average age of the nurses was 35.25 ± 6.69 years (range: 23–49). All participants were female, with the majority holding a bachelor’s degree; only one nurse had a diploma-level education. Data collection

Before the formal interviews, the first author provided standardized training to all research members involved in data collection to ensure the process’s consistency. The training covered the study’s purpose, the use of interview guidelines, and key considerations regarding informed consent. To maintain the privacy and comfort of the interview environment, the researcher coordinated with each nurse to schedule a specific time for the interview, which was conducted in a quiet duty room.

During the interview, the researcher recorded the session and simultaneously observed and noted the participant’s facial expressions, body language, and other non-verbal behaviors to capture comprehensive interview data. To ensure the confidentiality of the interview, no one else was present except the participant and the interviewer. The interview was conducted in Chinese. Each interview lasted between 20 and 30 min and consisted of six open-ended questions (see Table 2 for details).

Data analysis

The researcher transcribed the recordings within 24 h after the interview. The researcher de-identified all texts and cross-checked the recordings multiple times, replacing the nurses’ names with identification numbers. The original audio files were securely stored in electronic folders, accessible only to the research team members. The transcribed texts were imported into NVivo 14.0 software for data analysis. This study used Colaizzi’s seven-step method to analyze the data: two researchers repeatedly read the texts to familiarize themselves with the data. Key statements related to workplace dignity were extracted. Recurrent content was coded and transformed into meaningful units for categorization. The coded content was summarized to identify common concepts and form preliminary themes. Each theme was described in detail, with original interview content used to support the findings. Similar themes and descriptions were repeatedly compared and reflected upon to further refine the themes. Finally, the research findings were returned to some participants for feedback and validation [20]. All interviews were conducted in Chinese, and the transcripts confirmed by participants were translated into English by the first and corresponding authors. To ensure accuracy, two independent bilingual experts performed back-translation. Any discrepancies were discussed within the research team until consensus was reached.

Rigour of the study

This study followed Lincoln and Guba’s criteria for trustworthiness: credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability. All interview transcripts were verbatim verified and cross-checked with the nurses, enhancing the study’s credibility. For dependability, detailed records of research decisions, coding processes, and theme development were maintained. Reflexivity was also addressed. All interviewers were registered nurses, which facilitated rapport with participants but also introduced potential bias. To minimize this risk, the research team maintained reflexive diaries throughout the process and held regular discussions to compare interpretations and reach consensus. The researcher preserved all original materials, including interview transcripts and consent forms, thereby enhancing confirmability. In terms of transferability, this study presented all research details in thorough detail.

Results

This study identified four major themes that reflect nurses’ experiences and perceptions of workplace dignity in China: (1) nurses’ understanding of dignity at work, (2) individual-level factors, (3) patient- and societal-level influences, and (4) organizational-level factors.

Perceptions of dignity at work

Nurses perceived workplace dignity as a multidimensional subjective experience, encompassing the comprehensive sense of professional rights nurses derive from equality, respect, and value recognition at work.

Respect for nurses

Nurses emphasized that receiving respect from colleagues, leaders, physicians, and patients was central to their experience of workplace dignity. They believed this respect involved not only politeness and recognition in language and behavior but also acceptance and acknowledgment of their professional identity.

N4: “I think workplace dignity is about whether I am respected at work. This respect could come from colleagues, supervisors, clients, or anyone you interact with in the workplace.”

N4: “I think workplace dignity is about whether I am respected at work. This respect could come from colleagues, supervisors, clients, or anyone you interact with in the workplace.”

N6: “Workplace dignity means being respected by patients, doctors, and colleagues, without any disrespectful words or actions.”

N6: “Workplace dignity means being respected by patients, doctors, and colleagues, without any disrespectful words or actions.”

Equality for nurses

Equality is also a critical component of workplace dignity. Nurses indicated that being treated equally within healthcare teams and during patient interactions signifies appropriate recognition of their professional contributions and skills, contributing to harmonious collaboration.

N3: “I think workplace dignity means having a cooperative and equal relationship with my partners, including doctors, nurses, patients, and their families.”

N3: “I think workplace dignity means having a cooperative and equal relationship with my partners, including doctors, nurses, patients, and their families.”

Expression of self-worth

Nurses also expressed expectations for realizing their self-worth. They believed a dignified workplace should provide safety and support, allowing them to fully utilize their professional skills and achieve a sense of meaning and accomplishment at work.

Factors influencing workplace dignity

Individual level factors

Sociodemographic characteristics

Nurses indicated that factors such as age, job title, and years of experience influence their workplace dignity. Generally, as nurses age, advance in job titles, and accumulate clinical experience, they receive greater trust and respect from patients and colleagues, enhancing their sense of workplace dignity. Additionally, some nurses noted that their perception of similar events varies at different career stages, suggesting that workplace dignity is dynamic and evolves throughout their professional careers.

N3: “Different ages, job titles, and work experience affect workplace dignity. The same situation can feel different to me at different times, depending on when it’s communicated to me.”

N3: “Different ages, job titles, and work experience affect workplace dignity. The same situation can feel different to me at different times, depending on when it’s communicated to me.”

N8: “I think a nurse’s years of experience are really important. Whether it’s patient observation or technical skills, more experienced nurses show a completely different level of professionalism compared to newer nurses. Patients often trust and respect the more experienced nurses, thinking they have stronger skills. On the other hand, younger nurses or those with less experience are often rejected by patients because they’re seen as less experienced.”

Psychological status

Nurses’ emotional states influence their perceptions of workplace dignity. When nurses feel positive and happy, they handle their tasks with greater patience and positivity, effectively managing misunderstandings or challenges, which enhances their overall experience of workplace dignity.

N7 “Emotions also play a role. When I’m in a good mood, I feel energetic and enthusiastic at work, and I handle patients with patience. Even if they misunderstand me, I can explain things calmly. That makes me feel respected at work. On the other hand, when I’m feeling down or unwell, I might just do my job half-heartedly, and that lowers my sense of dignity in the workplace.”

Patient-Level factors

Patients’ educational level

Nurses believed patients’ educational levels influence their understanding and attitudes towards nursing work, consequently impacting workplace dignity. Some nurses noted that patients with higher educational levels typically understand the professionalism and significance of nursing better, leading to more respectful interactions. However, other nurses pointed out that some highly educated patients may still perceive nurses merely as service providers, undermining nurses’ sense of dignity.

Appellation and labels

The manner in which nurses are addressed holds symbolic significance for their experience of workplace dignity. Being called a waiter by patients or their families makes nurses feel their professional status is diminished, resulting in a perceived lack of respect and negatively impacting their dignity at work.

N5: “Doctors are usually called doctor, but sometimes we’re referred to as waiter. In those moments, it can feel like we’re not being respected.”

Workplace violence

Verbal abuse or physical conflicts initiated by patients or their families significantly threaten nurses’ workplace dignity. Dissatisfaction stemming from patients’ pain, emotional instability, or lack of understanding about medical procedures may result in misunderstandings, insults, or even physical attacks on nurses, causing nurses to feel demoralized and deprived of dignity at work.

N4: “When I worked in surgery, I had a drunk patient who asked questions beyond my responsibilities, which required a doctor’s response. Since my answer didn’t satisfy him, he felt we weren’t giving him enough attention, and his emotions escalated, leading to an argument and even physical conflict. I felt there was no dignity in working at the hospital.”

Organizational-level factors

Salary and benefits

Salary and compensation should balance nurses’ efforts and economic rewards to ensure they feel their labor is valued. If salaries are disproportionate to work intensity and professional risks, nurses may feel overlooked and undervalued, weakening their sense of dignity and commitment in the workplace.

Collegial relationships

Trust and cooperation among colleagues not only relieve work pressure but also strengthen team belonging, making them essential to workplace dignity. In high-intensity, high-pressure work environments, nurses develop strong emotional bonds with colleagues through close daily interactions and collaboration. Timely support, attentive listening, and genuine care from colleagues provide nurses with crucial psychological support, enhancing their feelings of being understood and accepted.

Discussion

This study describes nurses’ understanding of workplace dignity and identifies factors perceived to influence their actual work experiences. Results showed that nurses define workplace dignity as a comprehensive subjective experience involving aspects such as respect, equality, and self-worth. Additionally, factors influencing workplace dignity were categorized into sociodemographic factors, patient-related factors, social factors, and organizational factors.

This study found that sociodemographic factors such as age, professional title, and years of experience influence nurses’ experiences of workplace dignity. Nurses with more experience and higher professional titles typically receive greater respect and trust [21], perceiving a higher sense of dignity [21, 22]. These findings align with existing research [16]. From the perspective of professional development, nurses with more experience accumulate substantial professional competencies, such as improved judgment of patient conditions and emergency response skills [23] which in turn enhance patient trust [21, 24]. This suggests that hospital management should adopt differentiated strategies to support nurses at various stages of their careers.

This study revealed that psychological status also plays a significant role in influencing nurses’ perceptions of workplace dignity, which contrasts with the perspectives of previous studies. Prior research has predominantly regarded psychological distress as a consequence of lacking dignity at work [25]. However, this study found that nurses’ momentary emotional states can directly influence their real-time perceptions of dignity when facing adverse situations in specific work contexts. For example, one participant (N7) noted that being in a positive emotional state helped her better withstand minor frustrations and maintain a sense of internal dignity even in adverse situations. Therefore, managers should actively support and promote the use of positive cognitive reappraisal strategies among nurses in the workplace, in order to help them regulate their emotions more effectively and maintain their perception of workplace dignity.

This study found that patients’ educational levels have diverse effects on nurses’ experiences of workplace dignity. While patients with higher education levels are generally more likely to understand and appreciate the professionalism of nursing, some of them may also hold unrealistic expectations, which can paradoxically diminish nurses’ sense of dignity. This nuanced phenomenon has rarely been documented in previous research and warrants further investigation. In addition, the study highlighted the symbolic importance of how patients refer to nurses. Being addressed with non-professional titles such as waiter was found to significantly undermine nurses’ professional pride and their perceived dignity at work [14, 26]. This finding is consistent with prior studies, which have shown that being called a waiter may be perceived as disrespectful and demeaning, damaging the professional identity of nurses [18, 26]. Inappropriate forms of address may disregard the technical and professional nature of nursing work, misclassifying it as merely service labor and thereby weakening nurses’ professional identity [27]. Moreover, workplace violence and the lack of patient trust were also found to negatively affect nurses’ sense of workplace dignity. This finding is consistent with several international studies [28, 29]. Nurses who experience workplace violence may suffer from fear, anxiety, headaches, and other adverse outcomes, which physiologically undermine their sense of dignity at work [30]. In addition, a lack of patient trust disrupts the mutual respect essential in care relationships, increasing nurses’ emotional stress and frustration, and ultimately diminishing their perceived workplace dignity [18, 31].

This study found that attitude of hospital management, salary, and colleagues have significant impact on nurses’ workplace dignity. These findings are consistent with previous research. When managers actively listen to nurses and avoid public criticism, they help create a supportive environment that protects nurses’ dignity and fosters respect [32]. Managerial care and support also help build trust in leadership and a sense of belonging within the hospital [33]. In addition, salary is closely tied to nurses’ sense of economic security, and an effective reward and penalty system not only motivates performance but also helps maintain fairness and justice in the workplace [34]. and fair compensation is a key factor in enhancing their perception of dignity at work [35]. Positive interactions among colleagues’ further support workplace dignity. Research has shown that nurses’ sense of dignity is positively associated with their experience and the quality of interpersonal relationships in the workplace [15], which can improve both job performance and perceived dignity at work [36]. Therefore, hospital administrators can enhance nurses’ sense of workplace dignity and job satisfaction by strengthening trust between managers and staff, optimizing compensation and reward systems, and fostering positive collegial relationships.

In addition, this study identified social support and social attitudes as important factors influencing nurses’ workplace dignity. Nurses consistently reported that support and attitudes from family members, colleagues, and the general public significantly impacted their sense of dignity at work. This is consistent with previous findings. Social support can buffer the effects of effort–reward imbalance and help nurses cope with stress, thereby fostering more positive perceptions of their work environment([37]. Supportive relationships in the workplace also mitigate the psychological effects of violence and stress, fostering a greater sense of safety and respect [38]. Positive societal attitudes toward nurses are essential for maintaining workplace dignity. When nurses feel valued and respected, they are more likely to perceive dignity in their work [15, 39]. Furthermore, public recognition of nurses’ roles during extraordinary periods, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, has enhanced group pride and professional belonging [40, 41], thereby strengthening their perceived workplace dignity. Conversely, persistent stereotypes that reduce nursing to basic tasks such as “giving injections” or “assisting doctors” can diminish nurses’ professional status and dignity [42]. Therefore, maintaining workplace dignity requires not only improving working conditions but also reconstructing societal recognition of the nursing profession through stronger social support systems.

Implication

This study focuses on nurses’ perceptions of workplace dignity and the factors that influence those perceptions, offering significant insights for nursing managers and workplace ethics. First, respecting nurses’ dignity in the workplace is not only a collective responsibility but also a fundamental human right. Managers should foster a culture of respect and fairness within healthcare environments. Second, managers should advocate for legislative measures, such as classifying work-related violence against nurses as an aggravating factor under criminal law [43]. Additionally, initiatives such as the Civility, Respect, and Engagement in the Workplace (CREW) program, an evidence-based organizational intervention developed to improve workplace climate through respectful communication, team dialogue, and relationship-building, can enhance workplace empowerment and reduce incivility from supervisors [44, 45]. Lastly, campaigns such as China’s “Good Nurse of China” initiative can enhance public awareness and foster a workplace culture that upholds respect and dignity for nurses. This initiative is a nationwide selection and publicity program led by the National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China, aiming to promote nursing professionalism, recognize outstanding nurses, and elevate the social status of the profession.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, participants’ accounts may have been influenced by social desirability or selective memory, which could affect the authenticity of the data. Second, all participants were female, which may have influenced the range of perspectives represented in the study. Finally, although rigorous efforts were made to ensure the quality of translation, including back-translation and cross-checking, the researchers’ native language being Chinese may have introduced subtle differences in interpretation or expression that could affect readers’ understanding of the results.

Conclusion

This study explored nurses’ understanding of workplace dignity and the factors that influence it. These results suggest that workplace dignity is a complex and dynamic construct, emerging from the interactions among individual, patient-related, organizational, and societal dimensions. Given the limitations of this study, future research should consider examining workplace dignity in more diverse healthcare environments. Furthermore, the development of culturally sensitive tools to assess workplace dignity, as well as the evaluation of effective dignity-enhancing interventions in nursing practice, will provide valuable insights to advance both the theoretical understanding and practical application of workplace dignity in healthcare settings.
