Assessing fluid responsiveness in mechanically ventilated patients with intra-abdominal hypertension: a two-center, prospective, observational study

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntra-abdominal hypertension (IAH) may reduce the diagnostic accuracy of the passive leg raising (PLR) test for predicting fluid responsiveness, with unclear mechanisms. The reliability of the end-expiratory occlusion (EEO) test and mini-fluid challenge in IAH remains unknown. This study explored the mechanisms underlying PLR impairment and assessed the accuracy of EEO and mini-fluid challenge in detecting fluid responsiveness in patients with and without IAH.MethodsIn this prospective study in two intensive care units (ICUs), we included ventilated patients with IAH (“IAH + ”; intra-abdominal pressure [IAP] ≥ 12 mmHg) and without (“IAH-”), all monitored via transpulmonary thermodilution and receiving a 500-mL fluid challenge. Patients consecutively underwent a 1‑minute PLR, a 15‑second EEO, and a 1‑minute mini-fluid challenge of 100 mL, with cardiac index (CI) changes recorded during each maneuver. Following the mini-fluid challenge, the remaining 400 mL were infused over 14 min, and a ≥ 15% increase in CI was used to define fluid responders. The transmural pressure of the inferior vena cava was estimated by the central venous pressure (CVP) – IAP gradient.ResultsWe included 88 patients, 44 IAH- (25 fluid responders and 19 non-responders) and 44 IAH + (22 responders and 22 non-responders). Baseline IAP was 9 ± 2 mmHg in IAH- and 17 ± 3 mmHg in IAH + (p < 0.001). In IAH- responders, CI increased by 19 ± 11% during PLR and 31 ± 17% after volume expansion, with PLR positive in 24/25 responders. In IAH + responders, CI increased by 6 ± 7% during PLR (p < 0.001 vs. IAH-) and 30 ± 18% after volume expansion (p = 0.907 vs. IAH-). The AUROC of the PLR for detecting fluid responsiveness was 0.96 (0.87–1.00) in IAH- and 0.71 (0.56–0.87) in IAH + (p = 0.009 vs. IAH-). Background

Fluid management remains a cornerstone of hemodynamic optimization in critically ill patients [1]. Accurate prediction of fluid responsiveness is essential for guiding personalized resuscitation and avoiding fluid overload [2]. Dynamic indices such as the passive leg raising (PLR) test, end-expiratory occlusion (EEO) test, and mini-fluid challenge have been increasingly employed to evaluate preload responsiveness [3]. Among these, the PLR test [4] has been demonstrated to reliably mimic a reversible fluid challenge [5] and is recommended as part of the initial hemodynamic assessment [6, 7].

Intra-abdominal hypertension (IAH) is a common condition in critically ill patients, with a reported prevalence of 30–50% [8]. Fluid therapy in this population warrants even greater caution, as inappropriate fluid loading may exacerbate fluid accumulation and further increase intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) [9, 10]. This, in turn, impairs perfusion of the abdominal organs and independently influences the prognosis [11]. Therefore, in patients with IAH, assessing fluid responsiveness should be particularly considered as a prerequisite before fluid administration.

Previous studies have suggested that the predictive accuracy of PLR may be reduced in IAH [12, 13]. One possible explanation is that although IAP is reduced during PLR, it still exerts compressive pressure on the splanchnic circulation, thereby impeding blood flow to the right atrium [14]. However, the mechanisms underlying false-negative PLR results in patients with IAH remain unclear[13]. Alternative preload responsiveness tests, such as the EEO test and mini-fluid challenge, may be less influenced by IAP, but their diagnostic performance in this population has not been well established.

Therefore, the main objective of our study was to explore potential mechanisms underlying the PLR test’s impaired ability to detect preload responsiveness in IAH. The secondary objective was to assess the diagnostic accuracy of the EEO test and mini-fluid challenge in this population.

Methods

We conducted a two-center, prospective observational study across two medical-surgical ICUs (First affiliated hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, Guangzhou, China; and Longgang central hospital, Shenzhen, China) from March 2023 to October 2024. The ethical review board of each participating institutions approved this study (FAH [2020] 296; LGH 2024ECPJ010). Patients or their legally authorized representatives were informed about their participation and agreed to participate. The study was prospectively registered on the China Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR 2000039338).

Patients

Patients were prospectively included if they met all of the following criteria: (i) age ≥ 18 y.o., (ii) presence of circulatory failure, defined as a systolic arterial pressure ≤ 90 mmHg (or a systolic pressure decrease of ≥ 40 mmHg in hypertensive patients), accompanied by at least one of the following signs (mottled skin, oliguria, tachycardia ≥ 100 beats/min, or elevated lactate levels > 2 mmol/L), (iii) infusion of a fluid challenge with 500 mL of crystalloid, as decided by attending physicians, (iv) central venous catheter and hemodynamic monitoring in place, utilizing a transpulmonary thermodilution PICCO2 system (Pulsion Medical Systems, Getinge, Feldkirchen, Germany).

Exclusion criteria included: (i) atrial fibrillation or other significant arrhythmias, (ii) contraindications to the PLR test (hip surgery, lower limb amputation, intracranial hypertension or brain injury, etc.) or use of venous compression stockings; (iii) spontaneous breathing activity preventing 15-s respiratory holds; (iv) pregnancy; (v) extracorporeal membrane oxygenation; (vi) prone positioning; (vii) refusal of patients or their relatives to participate in the study and (viii) change in the dose of catecholamines or ventilatory settings required during the study period.

Data collection

The study was conducted in accordance with the CODEFIRE consensus [15]. Clinical data collected included demographic characteristics, admission shock category, vasoactive drug use, mechanical ventilation parameters, severity scores, and tissue perfusion variables. The 28-day mortality was also recorded.

Hemodynamic variables

A thermistor-tipped femoral artery catheter and a central venous catheter were already in place as part of the patient’s hemodynamic monitoring. Cardiac index (CI) was measured intermittently by transpulmonary thermodilution (TPTD) [16] and continuously through calibrated pulse contour analysis [17]. Thermodilution measurements were obtained by injecting three consecutive 15-mL boluses of cold saline and averaging the obtained CI values [18]. Derived parameters included the global end-diastolic volume index, the extravascular lung water index for ideal body weight, and the pulmonary vascular permeability index.

The pressure sensors were fixed to the patient’s upper arm at the mid-axillary line. Arterial pressure was recorded through the femoral arterial catheter. Central venous pressure (CVP) was measured at the base of the C wave at end-expiration, corresponding to the R wave of the QRS complex on the electrocardiogram, and the average of three successive respiratory cycles was calculated [19].

Ventilation settings

All patients received protective ventilation with a fixed tidal volume of 6–8 mL/kg of predicted body weight in volume-assist-controlled mode. The respiratory rate and the fraction of inspired oxygen were adjusted by the attending physician. Neuromuscular-blocking agents were used if required. Respiratory variables, including positive end-expiratory pressure, plateau pressure, respiratory rate, and tidal volume, were collected. These settings were kept unchanged during the study period.

Intra-abdominal pressure

We measured IAP from bladder pressure by injecting 25 mL of saline into the bladder after clamping the urinary drainage tube at the beginning and end of the protocol [11]. The abdominal pressure transducer was fixed on the lateral side of the pelvis, 2 cm below the anterior superior iliac spine. IAP was continuously monitored during the short-term maneuvers and measured at end-expiration in the absence of abdominal muscle contractions, as confirmed by clinical examination [20]. IAH was defined as an IAP ≥ 12 mmHg [11].

Study design

After patient inclusion, in the semi-recumbent position (Baseline 1), the first TPTD was performed, and IAP and hemodynamic variables, including heart rate, arterial pressure, CVP, and CI measured by TPTD, were collected (Figure S1). The PLR test was then performed as previously described [5] by elevating the legs to 45° while keeping the trunk horizontal. The effects of PLR were assessed by recording the maximum change in pulse-contour-derived CI within 1 min, along with other hemodynamic variables and IAP.

Then, the patient was moved back to the semi-recumbent position (Figure S1). After 1 min, once CI and IAP had stabilized and returned to baseline 1, hemodynamic variables and IAP were recorded again (Baseline 2), except that CI was measured by pulse contour analysis. The EEO test was performed as previously described, by interrupting ventilation at end-expiration for 15 s [21, 22]. Heart rate, arterial pressure, CVP, and pulse-contour-derived CI were averaged over the last 5 s of occlusion, as the maximal hemodynamic effects were observed at this time. IAP was also measured during this period.

One minute after the release of the occlusion, when CI and IAP had stabilized and returned to baseline 1, a second TPTD was performed (Baseline 3), and heart rate, arterial pressure, CVP, CI measured by TPTD, and IAP were recorded. A mini-fluid challenge (100 mL crystalloid over 1 min) was then administered[23], and measurements were repeated, except that CI was measured by pulse contour analysis. The remaining 400 mL of fluid challenge was infused over 14 min at a constant rate [23]. Immediately after volume expansion, a third TPTD was performed, and the final set of measurements was recorded (Figure S1). Catecholamine and sedative drug doses, as well as ventilation settings, were kept unchanged throughout the study.

Definition of abdominal vascular zones

By analogy with the pulmonary vascular West zones [24], abdominal vascular zones were defined based on the relationship between mean systemic filling pressure (Pmsf), IAP, and CVP [25]. In this framework, Pmsf is analogous to pulmonary arterial pressure (forward pressure), IAP to alveolar pressure (closing pressure), and CVP to pulmonary venous pressure (backward pressure). The CVP–IAP gradient was calculated to estimate the transmural pressure of the subdiaphragmatic inferior vena cava (IVC) [13]. As Pmsf is greater than both CVP and IAP, only abdominal vascular zones 2 and 3 are considered (Figure S2).

A condition in which the CVP–IAP gradient was positive was referred to as abdominal vascular zone 3. This condition is typically seen in patients without IAH, in whom venous return is determined primarily by Pmsf and CVP.

A condition in which the CVP–IAP gradient was negative was referred to as abdominal vascular zone 2. This condition is typically observed in patients with hypovolemia and/or IAH, in whom the IVC is compressed by the surrounding IAP, generating a “choke point” (i.e., Starling resistor) and causing flow limitation in venous return.

Statistical analysis

Fluid responsiveness was defined as an increase of CI ≥ 15% during the 500-mL fluid challenge over 15 min, estimated by TPTD, compared with Baseline 3 [18]. Taking into account an α risk at 5% and a β risk at 10%, estimating an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.75 for PLR-induced changes in CI in patients with IAH and 0.90 in those without IAH, and assuming a ratio between responders and non-responders of 1:1, we calculated that 44 patients should be included in each group. Enrollment continued until the target sample size was reached.

The normality of the data distribution was assessed visually. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed data were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs, 25th-75th percentiles). Categorical variables were expressed as counts (n) and percentages (%).

Pairwise comparisons were performed using the paired Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test, depending on data distribution. Independent groups were compared using the two-tailed Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. Comparisons of proportions were assessed by either the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. A generalized linear mixed-effects model (GLMM) was used to calculate p-values for interactions across different study time points, between fluid responders and non-responders, and between patients with and without IAH [26]. A random intercept was included to account for correlations among repeated measurements (as detailed in the Supplementary methods). P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg method [27]. The relationship between changes in variables was tested using Spearman’s or Pearson’s correlation. A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve with 95% confidence interval was constructed to evaluate the ability of changes in CI induced by the PLR test, EEO test, and mini-fluid challenge to detect fluid responsiveness in patients with and without IAH. The DeLong test was used to compare AUROCs between groups. The best threshold value for each test discriminating fluid responders and non-responders was defined by the maximal Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). The precision and least significant change (LSC) of CI measured by TPTD and pulse contour analysis were detailed in the Supplementary methods.

All statistical analyses were performed using MedCalc software (version 20.218; Mariakerke, Belgium) and R (version 4.3.1; R Studio, version 1.0.136). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

During the study period, 116 patients met the inclusion criteria; 88 were ultimately included in the analysis (Figure S3). The characteristics of the 44 patients with IAH (22 fluid responders and 22 non-responders) and the 44 patients without IAH (25 fluid responders and 19 non-responders) are detailed in Table 1. The IAP was higher in the IAH group than in the non-IAH group (17 ± 3 mmHg vs. 9 ± 2 mmHg, p < 0.001), as was the incidence of four-quadrant peritonitis (39% vs. 14%, P = 0.015). At the time of inclusion, all patients received sedation with an intravenous anesthetic (propofol or ciprofol) and/or a benzodiazepine (midazolam or remimazolam). Approximately 30% of patients received both classes of sedatives, but none received neuromuscular blocking agents. All maneuvers were performed outside renal replacement therapy sessions. The LSC of CI was 11.2 (8.3–19.4)% for TPTD and 1.9 (1.1–3.9)% for pulse contour analysis.

Effect of PLR, EEO, and mini-fluid challenge on IAP

During the PLR test, IAP decreased significantly in patients with IAH by 3 (1–4) mmHg, with similar changes in fluid responders and non-responders (Table 2). In these patients, IAP declined below 12 mmHg in 11 patients (25%). Among patients without IAH, IAP decreased by 1 (0–2) mmHg (p = 0.004 vs. IAH patients), with comparable changes in fluid responders and non-responders (Table 2).

The IAP remained unchanged during the EEO test (by 0 [-0.8–0.4] mmHg in the IAH group vs. by 0 [-0.8–0] mmHg in the non-IAH group, p = 0.953), as it was during the mini-fluid challenge (by 0 [0–0.5] mmHg vs. by 0 [0–0.4] mmHg, respectively, p = 0.622). There were no significant differences in IAP changes between fluid responders and non-responders within either group during the above interventions.

Hemodynamic effect of volume expansion in patients with and without IAH

Before volume expansion, compared with patients without IAH, patients with IAH had higher CVP (10 ± 4 mmHg vs. 13 ± 5 mmHg, p = 0.039) and CI (2.8 ± 0.9 L/min/m2 vs. 3.6 ± 1.0 L/min/m2, p < 0.001). No other baseline hemodynamic differences were observed between groups (Table S1).

The proportion of fluid responders was similar in patients with and without IAH (50% vs. 57%, respectively, p = 0.521). In the 25 fluid responders without IAH, volume expansion increased CI by 31 ± 17% (Table 2 and Fig. 1). In the 22 fluid responders with IAH, volume expansion increased CI by 30 ± 18% (p = 0.907 vs. responders without IAH) (Table 2 and Fig. 1).

Comparison of PLR, EEO, and mini-fluid challenge to detect volume responsiveness in patients with and without IAH

In fluid responders without IAH, CI increased by 19 ± 11% during PLR, by 8 ± 3% during EEO, and by 10 ± 6% during the mini-fluid challenge (Table 2 and Fig. 1). In these patients, the best threshold of CI increase for distinguishing responders from non-responders during PLR, EEO, and mini-fluid challenge were 10%, 5%, and 5%, respectively (Table 3 and Fig. 2). The AUROCs for the three test-induced changes in CI as predictors of fluid responsiveness were all significantly greater than 0.5 (all p < 0.05) (Table 3 and Fig. 2). Still in fluid responders without IAH, CI increased by more than 10% during PLR in 24 patients (96%, true positives), and by less than 10% in 1 patient (4%, false negative) (Figure S4).

Exploratory analysis of PLR-induced venous return in the presence of IAH

Considering both patients with and without IAH, IAP was independently associated with ΔCIPLR prediction of fluid responsiveness (Table S3). In patients with IAH, ΔCIPLR did not correlate with volume expansion-induced changes in CI (ΔCIVE) (r = 0.03 [-0.27–0.32], p = 0.868), in contrast to a significant positive correlation in patients without IAH (r = 0.62 [0.48–0.77], p < 0.001) (Fig. 3). The ΔCIPLR/ΔCIVE ratio was significantly lower in fluid responders with IAH compared to those without (0.29 [0.05–0.48] vs. 0.60 [0.42–0.87], respectively, p < 0.001) (Figure S5). Among responders with IAH, no difference in the ΔCIPLR/ΔCIVE ratio was observed between those with IAP of 12–16 mmHg and those with IAP ≥ 16 mmHg (0.29 [0.05–0.47] vs. 0.30 [0–0.54], respectively, p = 0.793) (Figure S5).

During PLR, a reversal of the CVP–IAP gradient occurred in true positives (from -2.4 ± 4.0 to + 2.2 ± 2.7 mmHg, p = 0.014), whereas it remained negative in false negatives (from -6.3 ± 3.7 to -1.4 ± 3.4 mmHg, p < 0.001) (Table S4, Fig. 4). A baseline CVP–IAP gradient ≤ -5 mmHg predicted PLR false negatives with an AUC of 0.80 (0.54–1.0), yielding a sensitivity of 63% (40–82%) and a specificity of 83% (44–99%) (Fig. 4).

Among the six true-positive responders with IAH, two presented with a baseline pattern of CVP > IAP (abdominal vascular Zone 3) and four with IAP > CVP (Zone 2). All but one transitioned to CVP > IAP (Zone 3) during PLR (Fig. 4). In these true-positive cases, the ΔCIVE was 23 (20–38)%, the ΔCIPLR was 15 (12–20)%, and tended to correlate with ΔCIVE (r = 0.83 [0.14–0.98], p = 0.058) (Figure S6). Among the 16 false-negative responders with IAH, all exhibited IAP > CVP (Zone 2) at baseline, with 75% remaining in IAP > CVP during PLR (p = 0.023 vs. true-positives) (Fig. 4). In these false-negative cases, the ΔCIVE was 22 (17–34)% (p = 0.541 vs. true-positives), the ΔCIPLR was 3 (1–6)% (p < 0.001 vs. true-positives), and showed no correlation with ΔCIVE (r = -0.08 [-0.55–0.43], p = 0.759) (Figure S6).

Discussion

In patients with IAH, the diagnostic accuracy of the PLR test for detecting fluid responsiveness is significantly reduced compared with that in patients without IAH; therefore, it should not be used to rule out fluid responsiveness in this population. The exploratory analysis suggested that false negatives on the PLR, when applying the 10% threshold observed in patients without IAH, seem to be associated with a persistently negative CVP–IAP gradient (i.e., transmural IVC pressure) during PLR, which may maintain the abdominal vasculature in Zone 2 and hinder the transition to Zone 3. By contrast, both the EEO test and the mini-fluid challenge demonstrate reliable accuracy for predicting fluid responsiveness in patients with IAH.

Previous studies have shown that the diagnostic accuracy of the PLR test, which has been well validated in multiple studies and meta-analyses [28, 29], may be compromised in the presence of IAH [12, 13]. One study reported a reduced effect of PLR on CI, with a lower diagnostic threshold and an AUROC of 0.60 (not significantly different from 0.5). It found a high false-negative rate (71%) when applying the 10% threshold established for patients without IAH [13]. However, the underlying mechanism for these false negatives has not been fully elucidated.

In the present study, we likewise observed that the PLR maneuver significantly reduced IAP and had a diminished ability to detect preload responsiveness in patients with IAH compared to those without, with a lower threshold of 6%, a reduced AUROC of 0.71 (still larger than 0.5), and a false-negative rate of 73%.

The most interesting observations in our study were changes in the CVP–IAP gradient and abdominal vascular zone transitions during PLR. These results help explain the reduced diagnostic performance of the PLR test in IAH. Although Mahjoub et al. suggested that IAP ≥ 16 mmHg is responsible for false-negative PLR responses [12], from a physiological perspective, IVC compression and flow limitation depend on transmural IVC pressure rather than IAP alone. False-negative PLR responses may also occur when IAP is < 16 mmHg in the presence of severe hypovolemia. Our study showed IAP, per se, may not be responsible for the decreased amplitude of the ΔCIPLR. First, IAP was similar between true positives and false negatives at baseline or during PLR. Second, the ΔCIPLR/ΔCIVE ratio was similar in responders with IAP 12-16mmHg versus those with IAP ≥ 16mmHg. However, the transmural IVC pressure (i.e., the CVP–IAP gradient) differed significantly between true positives and false negatives. At baseline, among patients with IAH, IAP exceeded CVP in most patients across both groups, resulting in negative transmural pressure, which was more pronounced in false negatives. However, during PLR, the maneuver-induced decrease in IAP altered the CVP–IAP gradient, leading to a distinct hemodynamic response.

In true positives, PLR reversed the CVP–IAP gradient, generating a positive transmural pressure, thereby allowing full recruitment of blood from the lower limbs and abdominal compartment into the cardiac chambers [25]. A study in a porcine model of IAH also showed that right atrial pressure exceeding IAP was associated with an increase in intrathoracic blood volume [28]. In preload-responsive patients, this increased CI to a level comparable to that seen in individuals without IAH. Of note, all but one true-positive case in our study were located in the abdominal vascular Zone 3 during PLR. A positive correlation between ΔCIPLR and ΔCIVE was observed exclusively in this group, further supporting this explanation.

In most false negatives, however, PLR failed to reverse the negative transmural pressure, leaving the IVC collapsed and thereby precluding any increase in venous return [25]. In our study, 75% of patients remained in abdominal vascular Zone 2 during PLR. While a decreased CVP–IAP gradient could partially relieve IVC compression and augment venous return relative to baseline, this effect was attenuated by the “vascular waterfall” (i.e., flow limitation) phenomenon [25], as flow remained intercepted by compressed IVC [29], which acted as a Starling resistor [30]. Consequently, only a small PLR-induced increase in CI was observed in preload-responsive patients. These results were consistent with a multicenter study by Vieillard-Baron et al. [31] and a secondary analysis by Aneman et al. [32], showing that the relationship between IVC diameter and preload responsiveness, as well as the correlation between analog Pmsf and PLR-induced stroke volume changes, was lost in patients with IAH.

We also observed that a baseline CVP–IAP gradient ≤ -5 mmHg may be associated with false-negative PLR responses. Similarly, a canine study reported that IVC flow can be augmented by spontaneous breathing or lowering IVC pressure, unless IAP exceeds IVC pressure by ≥ 5 cmH₂O [33]. However, given the limited sensitivity and small sample size of the present study, this threshold should be considered exploratory and warrants further investigation.

Previous studies have reported that changes in intra-abdominal conditions may influence the accuracy of the EEO test [34], particularly in patients undergoing laparotomic surgery [35, 36]. However, no prior study has assessed the performance of the EEO test in ICU patients with closed-abdomen IAH, nor has it assessed the mini-fluid challenge, which represents another novel aspect of our study. Notably, the performance of both tests was largely unaffected by IAH, with comparable AUROC values in patients with and without IAH. The diagnostic threshold of the EEO test was slightly lower in patients with IAH, consistent with our prior reports of a reduced EEO cutoff in prone-positioned patients [37], a condition associated with increased IAP. In contrast, the mini-fluid challenge acts as a quick, transient increase in right ventricular preload via the superior vena cava, which is independent of IAP and therefore less affected by IAH.

Given the methods used in our study, the LSC of CI measured by TPTD was approximately 11%, consistent with a previously reported value of 12% [18], supporting its reliability in detecting fluid responsiveness. In contrast, the LSC of CI, measured by pulse contour analysis, was 1–2%, well below the dynamic test threshold in this study, supporting its applicability. To minimize potential time-dependent drift in pulse contour analysis, TPTD recalibration was performed before the mini-fluid challenge, at a shorter interval than the previously recommended 1-h calibration period [38], thereby making the data more robust.

We acknowledge several limitations to our study. First, IAP was measured using bladder pressure, although gastric pressure may better reflect the IVC’s extramural pressure [39]. Bladder pressure, however, remains the most widely used method in clinical practice. In our protocol, IAP was continuously monitored without repeated bladder filling during short-term maneuvers. Although urine accumulation may influence bladder pressure, this effect is likely limited given the brief duration, particularly as PLR was performed immediately after baseline measurement. Second, the transmission of intrathoracic pressure to the abdominal cavity, which can increase IAP, should be acknowledged, although the plateau and driving pressures were relatively low in the present study. Additionally, esophageal pressure was not measured; therefore, transmural CVP was unavailable. Considering the potential different transmission of thoracic pressure to CVP and abdomen, there is a risk of CVP and IAP overestimation. Third, the number of true-positive PLR cases among IAH responders was small, and no patients included had an IAP exceeding 25 mmHg. Accordingly, the mechanistic exploration is hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory, and the generalizability cannot be extrapolated to the population with severe IAH [40]. Additionally, no cases of localized IAH were identified in our cohort; therefore, these findings may apply only to non-loculated IAH. Fourth, the diagnostic threshold for fluid responsiveness in IAH was relatively small. Pulse contour analysis, as used here, can detect CI changes as low as 1–2% [41], whereas echocardiography requires > 10% change in velocity–time integrals (VTI) to be detectable [42], making it less suitable for this patient population. Fifth, the non-randomized sequential testing design may introduce a risk of bias due to potential carry-over effects. However, a 1-min delay was applied between maneuvers to allow CI and IAP to return to baseline. Sixth, due to measurement variability, dichotomizing fluid responsiveness using a 15% threshold may increase the risk of false-negative results. However, only a small proportion of study patients were close to this threshold, and the diagnostic performance of PLR remained largely unchanged when alternative thresholds of 13% and 17% were applied. Seventh, the sample size may be underpowered for secondary objectives, although the AUROC differences (IAH vs. non-IAH) for either the EEO test or the mini-fluid challenge were within the predefined equivalence margin of 0.15. Eighth, the EEO test cannot be applied to patients who cannot tolerate a 15-s expiratory hold. This limits the external validity of our findings, particularly in the absence of neuromuscular blockade. In this context, the mini-fluid challenge may represent a reliable alternative. Finally, the proportion of obese patients was smaller in our population from Chinese origin than that observed in other parts of the world.

Conclusion

In patients with IAH, the PLR-induced increase in CI among fluid responders is attenuated, which may be related to a persistently negative CVP–IAP gradient (i.e., the transmural pressure of the inferior vena cava) in most false-negative cases, thereby potentially maintaining the abdominal vascular in Zone 2 and impeding transition to Zone 3 during PLR. In contrast, the EEO test and mini-fluid challenge remain reliable, underscoring their value as alternative methods for assessing fluid responsiveness in this population.
