The Effect of Virtual Reality Glasses Application During Urodynamics on Procedural Pain, Anxiety, and Hemodynamic Parameters: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Elife Kettaş Dölek, Gamze Bozkul, Gülay Altun Uğraş, Murat Bozlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the effectiveness of virtual reality glasses (VRG) intervention during urodynamic testing (UT) on procedural pain (PP), anxiety levels, and hemodynamic parameters.MethodsThis prospective, randomized controlled trial enrolled 84 patients undergoing UT, randomized 1:1 to VRG (n=42) or control group (n=42). The VRG group viewed immersive 3-dimensional nature videos via VRG throughout the procedure, while controls received routine care. Primary outcomes included PP (visual analogue scale) and anxiety levels (State Anxiety Inventory) assessed at baseline (pre-UT1), immediately postprocedure (post-UT2), and 15 minutes postprocedure (post-UT3). Secondary outcomes comprised hemodynamic parameters (systolic blood pressure [SBP], diastolic blood pressure [DBP], heart rate [HR], respiratory rate [RR], oxygen saturation [SpO₂]) measured at identical timepoints.ResultsGroups demonstrated comparable baseline characteristics and PP scores (VRG: 2.52±2.76 vs. control: 3.07±2.99, P=0.386). While baseline anxiety levels were similar between groups, the VRG group exhibited significantly lower postprocedural anxiety scores (post-UT2: 34.64±4.98 vs. 39.21±8.11, P=0.003; post-UT3: 34.45±4.68 vs. 39.45±8.27, P=0.001). Hemodynamically, the VRG group demonstrated significantly reduced DBP at post-UT3 (73.4±9.7 mmHg vs. 78.7±12.0 mmHg, P=0.028) and lower RRs at both postprocedural timepoints (P<0.001 and P=0.015, respectively). No significant between-group differences were observed for SBP, HR, or SpO₂.ConclusionsVRG intervention during UT effectively reduced postprocedural anxiety and demonstrated beneficial effects on RR and DBP, without significantly impacting PP. These findings support the use of VRG by nurses as a safe, nonpharmacological adjunct for enhancing patient comfort during invasive urological procedures.

INTRODUCTION

Urodynamic testing (UT) is the gold standard for evaluating lower urinary tract dysfunction, providing objective assessment of bladder storage and voiding function [1]. Invasive urodynamic evaluations frequently cause patient distress, including procedural pain (PP), anxiety, and embarrassment [2, 3]. These adverse responses affect approximately 50%–60% of patients, triggering sympathetic activation with elevated catecholamine and cortisol levels, disrupting hemodynamic homeostasis through alterations in blood pressure, heart rate (HR), and respiratory patterns [4, 5]. Procedure-related anxiety activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, releasing catecholamines and cortisol. This produces 15%–20% increases in blood pressure and 25%–30% elevations in respiratory rate (RR) [6]. Such sympathetic hyperactivation not only compromises procedure tolerability but may also compromise diagnostic accuracy through physiological interference. Contemporary nursing practice increasingly emphasizes evidence-based nonpharmacological interventions as adjuncts to traditional pharmacological approaches for neuroendocrine modulation and patient comfort optimization [7]. Nurses increasingly utilize diverse nonpharmacological interventions (including aromatherapy, music therapy, and mindfulness techniques) as complementary strategies to mitigate procedure-related complications arising from PP and anxiety. Systematic evidence demonstrates the efficacy of these modalities in attenuating both pain [8] and anxiety [9] responses, supporting their integration as adjunctive therapeutic approaches. Among these emerging interventions, virtual reality glasses (VRG) represent a particularly promising nonpharmacological modality for comprehensive management of PP, anxiety, and hemodynamic instability in patients undergoing UT. Virtual reality (VR) technology creates immersive 3-dimensional environments that redirect attention through multisensory engagement, diverting cognitive resources from pain and anxiety perception [10, 11]. VRG suppresses amygdala activity and adrenergic responses, reducing nociceptor activation and pain signal transmission, achieving meaningful reductions in pain and anxiety [11]. Contemporary healthcare delivery increasingly incorporates VRG technology as nursing professionals leverage technological advances to optimize patient care quality and outcomes [12]. VRG demonstrates efficacy in reducing anxiety [10, 13], pain [10, 11, 14], fatigue [10], and hemodynamic instability [13, 14]. VRG demonstrates consistent hemodynamic modulation across diverse clinical contexts. Doğan Yılmaz and Ünlüsoy Dinçer [13] (colonoscopy group) and Genç et al. [14] (transrectal prostate biopsy group) reported that VR interventions positively influenced vital signs, significantly reducing blood pressure, pulse, and RR, while increasing oxygen saturation (SpO₂), thereby providing a stabilizing effect during invasive procedures. Urological studies show VRG effectiveness during cystoscopy [15] and lithotripsy [16]. Although the effectiveness of VRG has been demonstrated in various urological procedures [15, 16], its application in a procedure such as urodynamics—which involves prolonged catheterization, continuous monitoring, and voluntary urination under unnatural conditions, creating unique psychological stressors—has not been previously investigated. This study pioneers the integration of VR technology in functional urology practice, potentially establishing a new standard for patientcentered care. Consequently, this investigation represents the first systematic evaluation of VRG effects on PP, anxiety, and hemodynamic parameters in patients undergoing UT. The study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of VRG on PP and anxiety levels during UT, to examine its effects on hemodynamic parameters (systolic blood pressure [SBP], diastolic blood pressure [DBP], HR, RR, and SpO₂) at 3 different time points, and to assess changes in all outcome measures.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design

This study employed a prospective, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial design. The study protocol adhered to CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) guidelines for randomized controlled trials and was prospectively registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (Registration ID: NCT06528925). The study methods remained consistent with the originally planned protocol throughout the trial.

Participants

The study population comprised patients presenting to the Mersin University Hospital Urology Outpatient Clinic for scheduled urodynamic evaluation. Between August 2024 and February 2025, consecutive eligible patients (n=84) meeting predetermined inclusion criteria were enrolled. Patients were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination of this research. The research protocol was developed in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki principles and received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (approval number: 2024/65).

Sample size determination was based on a previous randomized controlled trial investigating VRG effects on anxiety during emergency surgical procedures under local anesthesia [17]. The referenced study demonstrated substantial between-group differences in anxiety scores (VRG group: 30.00±6.32 vs. control group: 58.77±8.59), yielding a Cohen d effect size of 3.81. Adopting a conservative approach with a large effect size threshold (Cohen d=0.8), 2-tailed α=0.05, and 95% statistical power, G*Power ver. 3.1 analysis determined a minimum sample size of 42 participants per group. Post hoc power analysis was conducted following study completion to confirm adequate statistical power (>0.95) for detecting the observed anxiety differences between groups. Bonferroni correction was applied in multiple comparisons (pre-UT1 vs. post-UT2, pre-UT1 vs. post-UT3, post-UT2 vs. post-UT3) after repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). Supplementary analysis of covariance adjusting for baseline anxiety, age, sex, and procedure duration confirmed the primary findings. The adjusted mean difference in postprocedure anxiety remained significant (F=8.34, P=0.005, partial η²=0.09). Multivariate regression analysis identified baseline anxiety (β=0.42, P<0.001) and group allocation (β=-0.31, P=0.003) as significant predictors of postprocedure anxiety, while age, sex, and procedure duration showed no significant associations. Statistical significance level was set at P<0.05. There were no missing data; all randomized participants completed the study and were included in the final analysis.

Inclusion criteria encompassed patients scheduled for invasive UT, aged 18–65 years, with adequate communication capabilities, absence of physical limitations precluding VRG utilization, no prior VRG exposure, absence of diagnosed anxiety disorders or anxiolytic medication use, and absence of documented mental health conditions.

Of 112 patients undergoing UT during the study period, 28 were excluded from participation. Exclusions comprised: 16 patients failing to meet inclusion criteria (age<18 years [n=6], communication impairment [n=3], concurrent anxiolytic medication use [n=4], prior VRG experience [n=3]), 8 patients declining participation, and 4 patients experiencing procedural discontinuation. Consequently, 84 eligible patients completed the study protocol (Fig. 1).

Randomization and Blinding

Eligibility assessment was conducted by investigator (MB). Participants (n=84) were randomly assigned to VRG and control groups in a 1:1 ratio using computer-generated block randomization based on the order of admission to the clinic. When generating the random sequence, no randomization was performed for stratification variables (age, sex, baseline anxiety level, etc.). Permutation blocks (ABBA, BBAA, ABAB...) were used to ensure balanced group distribution throughout the enrollment period. Following initial randomization, group allocation was determined by random draw, designating group B as the VRG group and group A as the control group (standard care).

Allocation concealment was maintained through sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes containing group assignment information, secured by investigator (GAU). Following informed consent completion, the administering researcher (EKD) opened the corresponding envelope to reveal treatment allocation. Due to the inherent nature of VRG intervention, complete blinding of participants and intervention administrators was not feasible during the treatment phase. However, outcome assessors remained blinded to group allocation throughout data collection.

Poststudy completion, data entry was performed by investigator (GB), who remained blinded to group identity coding. Statistical analysis was conducted by an independent biostatistician with no knowledge of group assignments. In brief, the study was blinded with regard to group assignment, data analysis and reported the results.

Outcome Measures and Data Collection Instruments

Primary outcomes included VRG intervention effects on PP intensity and state anxiety levels. Secondary outcomes encompassed hemodynamic parameter changes, specifically SBP, DBP, HR, RR, and SpO2.

Data collection employed validated instruments including the demographic characteristics form, visual analogue scale (VAS) for pain assessment, State Anxiety Inventory (SAI) for anxiety measurement, and a standardized hemodynamic parameters monitoring form.

Demographic characteristics form

This researcher-developed instrument, based on established literature [8, 9], captured participant characteristics including age, gender, marital status, body mass index (BMI), educational attainment, procedure duration, and UT indications.

Visual analogue scale

Procedural pain intensity was assessed using the validated 10-cm VAS, a unidimensional continuous scale extensively utilized in adult populations for subjective pain measurement. The horizontal scale ranges from 0 cm (no pain) to 10 cm (worst imaginable pain), with established severity classifications: absent (0 cm), mild (1–4 cm), moderate (5–6 cm), and severe (7–10 cm) [18, 19].

State Anxiety Inventory

Anxiety assessment employed the validated Spielberger State Anxiety Inventory (1970), suitable for individuals aged ≥14 years. The Turkish adaptation measures situational anxiety states with established reliability (Cronbach α=0.94) [20]. This 20-item instrument utilizes a 4-point Likert scale (1=not at all, 2=somewhat, 3=moderately, 4=very much), generating scores ranging from 20–80 points. Higher scores indicate elevated anxiety levels, with established cutoffs: no anxiety (0–19), low anxiety (20–39), moderate anxiety (40–59), and high anxiety (60–79) [20, 21]. Current study reliability coefficients were excellent across measurement timepoints: preprocedure (pre-UT1) (α =0.95), immediate postprocedure (post-UT2) (α=0.92), and 15-minute postprocedure (post-UT3) (α=0.91).

Hemodynamic parameters monitoring form

This standardized data collection instrument captured physiological parameters (SBP, DBP, HR, RR, SpO2) at 3 predetermined timepoints: pre-UT1, post-UT2, and post-UT3.

Data Collection

Research data were collected between August 2024 and February 2025. Patients were assigned to VRG and control groups according to randomization. UT is performed by nurses and technicians under the supervision of a urology specialist. Patients in the control group received routine treatment and care interventions during the procedure.

Patients in the VRG group received VRG immediately before the procedure in addition to routine treatment and care and watched 3-dimensional videos throughout the procedure. Patients were asked to wear VRG (VR Shinecon G12 3D Virtual Reality Glasses, China) compatible with an Android operating system mobile phone (Xiaomi Redmi Note 8 Pro 2019 model, China) and minimizing sound loss in supine position. A video containing nature scenes with relaxing music background was adjusted to the patient’s desired sound level and watched through VRG. Permission was obtained from the video owner for the use of the video (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zjxafEiJkSw&t=41s). The video used in the study is a standardized 6-minute 360° nature video, previously proven to have physiological and emotional effects [22]. The same video content was shown to all patients to ensure standardization.

Before starting the VR videos, participants underwent a brief 2-minute familiarization with static (nonmoving) VR content to get accustomed to the virtual environment. During the VRG intervention, patients were continuously monitored by the nurse (EKD), kept in the supine position to minimize discomfort, and observed for side effects. Participants were instructed to report any adverse reactions. No adverse events occurred. All VRG equipment underwent standardized disinfection protocols between patients. The VR headset surfaces were cleaned with 70% isopropyl alcohol wipes and allowed to air dry for 60 seconds. Disposable hygienic face masks were used for each patient to prevent direct contact with the headset. Head straps were either replaced with disposable alternatives or thoroughly disinfected between patients according to hospital infection control guidelines.

The demographic information form, VAS, SAI, and hemodynamic parameters monitoring form were completed by the researcher (EKD) for all patients admitted to the UT unit. SBP, DBP, and HR were measured with a digital blood pressure monitor (Omron Brand, M2 model, Vietnam) in the UT room, and SpO2 values were measured with a portable pulse oximeter device (Life Net Medical, USA). RR was counted by observing chest movements for one minute. Hemodynamic parameters were measured in the waiting room at pre-UT1, post-UT2, and post-UT3. Pain level was evaluated with VAS on a horizontal line only during the procedure. Anxiety status was evaluated with SAI in the waiting room at Pre-UT1, Post-UT2, and Post-UT3. The Post-UT3 assessment was conducted 15 minutes after the intervention, in line with previous VR studies [23, 24], to evaluate both the effects of VR on pain and anxiety and the short-term efficacy of the intervention. All measurements (VAS, SAI, and hemodynamic parameters) were conducted by the same trained researcher (EKD) to ensure consistent and standardized data collection across all participants. Before starting the study, calibration of the digital blood pressure device and pulse oximeter device was ensured.

Data Analysis

The data are presented using descriptive statistics, including frequency with percentage and mean with standard deviation, minimum and maximum. Pearson chi-square or Fisher exact test was applied for categorical variables. Normality assumption was evaluated with Shapiro-Wilk test and Q-Q plot analyses. Normality testing using Shapiro-Wilk test indicated normal distribution for all continuous variables (P>0.05) except baseline RR (P=0.042). Q-Q plots confirmed approximate normality with minimal deviation at distribution tails. Given robustness of parametric tests to minor violations with n>30 per group, we proceeded with planned analyses. Sensitivity analyses using nonparametric tests yielded consistent results. Independent samples t-test was used for comparisons between groups, and repeated measures ANOVA was used for within-group comparisons. Bonferroni correction was applied in multiple comparisons. Analysis was performed for all hemodynamic parameters (SBP, DBP, HR, RR, SpO2).

RESULTS

A total of 84 participants were randomized, and all completed the study protocol, resulting in a retention rate of 100%. The final analysis included 42 participants in the VRG group and 42 in the control group, with complete data available for all outcome measures. Baseline demographic and clinical characteris tics demonstrated excellent between-group comparability, with no statistically significant differences observed across all measured parameters (P>0.05 for all comparisons) (Table 1). Baseline homogeneity tests for all dependent variables confirmed successful randomization, with no significant differences between groups for anxiety, blood pressure, HR, RR, or SpO₂ measures (Table 2). Both cohorts exhibited comparable distributions for age, procedure duration, sex, marital status, BMI categories, educational attainment, and urodynamic indications, confirming successful randomization. The most prevalent urodynamic indications included urinary incontinence (VRG group: 35.7% vs. control group: 50.0%), pollakiuria (VRG group: 21.4% vs. control group: 16.7%), and urinary retention (VRG group: 16.7% vs. control group: 19.0%).

Procedural Pain Outcomes

Both groups experienced mild-to-moderate PP during UT, with mean VAS scores of 2.52±2.76 (VRG group) and 3.07± 2.99 (control group). Between-group analysis revealed no statistically significant difference in pain intensity (P=0.386), although the VRG group demonstrated a numerically lower mean pain score (Table 3).

Anxiety Outcomes

Baseline anxiety levels indicated moderate-to-high stress in both groups (VRG: 52.04±11.46; control: 53.61±12.42), with comparable pre-procedural scores (mean difference=-1.57; 95% confidence interval [CI], -6.72 to 3.58; P=0.549). Postintervention, the VRG group demonstrated significant anxiety re-duction to mild levels, while the control group maintained moderate anxiety levels. Specifically, the VRG group exhibited significantly lower anxiety scores at immediate postprocedure assessment (34.64±4.98 vs. 39.21±8.11; mean difference=-4.57; 95% CI, -7.54 to -1.60; Cohen d=0.69; P=0.003) and 15-minute follow-up (34.45±4.68 vs. 39.45±8.27; mean difference=-5.00; 95% CI, -7.91 to -2.09; Cohen d=0.75; P=0.001) (Table 3; Supplementary Fig. 1). Repeated measures ANOVA confirmed significant within-group temporal changes in both groups, with large effect sizes observed for anxiety reduction (Table 4).

Hemodynamic Outcomes

Comprehensive hemodynamic monitoring revealed differential patterns between treatment groups (Table 5).

Within-Group Temporal Changes

Both groups demonstrated significant SBP reductions at 15-minute postprocedure assessment compared to baseline and immediate postprocedure values (VRG: P<0.001; control: P=0.001). HR exhibited significant decreases in both groups throughout the observation period (VRG: P<0.001; control: P<0.001). RR showed significant reduction in the VRG group at both postprocedural timepoints versus baseline (P=0.007), while the control group demonstrated no significant respiratory changes (P=0.317). DBP and SpO₂ remained stable across timepoints in both groups (P>0.05). Detailed repeated measures ANOVA results with effect sizes for all hemodynamic pa-rameters are presented in Table 4.

Between-Group Comparisons

Critical between-group differences emerged at specific timepoints. The VRG group demonstrated significantly lower DBP at 15-minute postprocedure assessment (73.4±9.7 mmHg vs. 78.7±12.0 mmHg, P=0.028) and reduced RRs at both immediate postprocedure (20.8±1.2 breaths/min vs. 22.3±2.0 breaths/min, P<0.001) and 15-minute assessments (21.0±1.2 breaths/min vs. 21.9±1.9 breaths/min, P=0.015). No significant between-group differences were observed for SBP, HR, or SpO₂ (P>0.05). No adverse events or harms were observed in either group during the trial.

Group×Time Interactions

Significant interaction effects were detected for SBP (F=15.90, P<0.001, partial η²=0.16), DBP (F=4.08, P=0.020, partial η²=0.05), and HR (F=16.43, P<0.001, partial η²=0.17). The VRG group exhibited more pronounced SBP reduction from baseline to 15-minute assessment (-8.14 mmHg; 95% CI, -11.26 to -5.02) compared to controls (-6.93 mmHg; 95% CI, -10.05 to -3.81) (Table 5).

DISCUSSION

Urodynamic testing inherently challenges patient comfort through unfamiliar environments, exposure to healthcare personnel, continuous observation during intimate procedures, and compromised privacy [9, 25, 26]. These psychosocial stressors precipitate heightened pain perception, hemodynamic instability, and psychological distress [2, 4]. Critically, UT validity and diagnostic reliability are fundamentally dependent upon achieving optimal patient comfort, as psychological distress can introduce physiological artifacts that compromise measurement accuracy. Consequently, interventions targeting anxiety reduction, pain mitigation, and hemodynamic stabilization represent essential components of evidence-based urodynamic practice. This study demonstrates that VRG intervention during UT yields significant anxiety reduction and beneficial hemodynamic parameters, particularly affecting DBP and RR parameters, while producing numerically favorable but nonsignificant pain reduction. Although our inclusion criteria specified an age range of 18–65 years, the mean age of participants was 54 years, representing the typical demographic presenting for urodynamic evaluation. This age distribution is consistent with epidemiological data showing an increased prevalence of lower urinary tract symptoms in the 50–65 age group [27]. Additionally, the upper age limit of 65 was chosen to control for age-related factors affecting VR tolerance, and future research should consider extending the age range to enhance generalizability to geriatric populations.

The Impact of VRG Use on PP

Both groups experienced mild-to-moderate PP, with the VRG group demonstrating numerically lower but statistically nonsignificant pain scores. This contrasts with existing literature demonstrating VRG efficacy in urological pain management [16, 26]. Weynants et al. [16] documented significant pain reduction during shock wave lithotripsy (P=0.011), while Toraman and Eskici Ilgin [28] reported benefits during transrectal prostate biopsy (P<0.05). Several methodological and procedural factors may explain these divergent findings. Primarily, UT may inherently generate lower pain level compared to lithotripsy or prostate biopsy procedures, creating a potential floor effect that limits detectable intervention benefits. This study, observed baseline pain scores (mean <3/10) indicate relatively mild procedural discomfort, potentially insufficient to demonstrate statistical significance despite numerical improvements. Interactive VRG environments requiring active cognitive engagement may provide superior analgesic effects through enhanced attentional diversion mechanisms. Despite statistical nonsignificance, the observed 0.55-point VAS reduction in the VRG group warrants clinical consideration, as this magnitude approaches the minimal clinically important differences for pain assessment.

The Effect of VRG Use on Anxiety

This study demonstrated substantial baseline anxiety levels (moderate-to-high range) in both groups, consistent with literature documenting the anxiogenic nature of invasive urodynamic procedures [2, 3, 8, 9, 25]. In the group receiving VRG patients’ anxiety decreased to mild levels after UT, while controls maintained moderate levels. The sustained reduction 15 minutes postprocedure highlights intervention durability. The baseline anxiety may be influenced by conscious awareness during invasive procedures, compromised privacy, embarrassment, anticipatory pain, unfamiliar environments with medical devices, and fear of unknown outcomes.

Previous studies examining VRG effects on anxiety during invasive procedures confirm VR’s effectiveness in anxiety reduction [13-15, 28]. Ketsuwan et al. [15] found VRG significantly effective during flexible cystoscopy, while another study showed VRG reduced anxiety and increased satisfaction during transrectal prostate biopsy [28]. This effect is attributed to VRG’s immersive experience creating alternative focus by diverting attention and creating relaxation responses [10]. Neuroimaging studies reveal VRG’s anxiolytic effects by reducing amygdala activity [29]. VRG controls anxiety by suppressing sympathetic and activating parasympathetic nervous system branches [30]. The current study demonstrated clinically significant anxiety reduction in the VRG group, with a within-group decrease of 17.59 points (from 52.04±11.46 to 34.45±4.68), substantially exceeding the 10-point minimal clinically meaningful difference. The between-group differences (4–5 points) were supported by medium-to-large effect sizes (Cohen d=0.69–0.75). Additionally, the effect of VRG application on anxiety paralleling improvement in hemodynamic parameters suggests that the intervention also positively affects the physiological stress response. Similar to the current study, previous studies [13, 15, 28] have found that VRG use significantly reduces anxiety.

The Effect of VRG use on Hemodynamic Parameters

In this study, it was found that SBP, HR, and RR in VRG group patients decreased over time compared to preprocedure and were statistically significant. These findings show that changes in hemodynamic parameters over time demonstrate the effect of VRG on physiological stress response and support the sympathetic activity suppressive effect of VRG [31]. These findings align with literature results. Genç et al. [14] found significant HR and RR decreases in VRG prostate biopsy patients, while colonoscopy studies showed significant SBP and RR decreases in VRG groups.

In the current study, it was determined that in control group patients, SBP and HR also decreased significantly compared to preprocedure, while there were fluctuations in DBP, RR, and SpO2 values, but these fluctuations were not significant. This finding suggests that the significant decrease in SBP and HR values in individuals in the control group may be partially reduced due to increasing adaptation to the procedure and environment as the procedure progresses, related to autonomic nervous system responses due to high preprocedure anxiety levels.

The VRG group showed lower DBP at 15 minutes postprocedure and lower RR at both postprocedure measurements. Lower DBP suggests visual-auditory relaxation stimuli may regulate blood pressure through increased parasympathetic cardiovascular activity. Lower RR values indicate regulatory and stabilizing respiratory effects. As this is the first study evaluating VRG effects on UT hemodynamic parameters, replication is needed for generalization.

These hemodynamic changes are clinically important. SBP, HR, and RR decreases in the VRG group remained within normal physiological limits, demonstrating intervention safety. These findings are consistent with studies in the literature emphasizing the importance of various nonpharmacological interventions such as, music therapy [4, 8, 32], aromatherapy [9], patient education [2, 32], heating pad use [33], breathing exercises and relaxation, meditation, and attention diversion techniques in anxiety and stress management during UT [3]. Continued SBP and HR improvement 15 minutes postprocedure demonstrates VRG’s sustainable effect. These results support VRG as an effective nonpharmacological method for reducing physiological stress response and managing hemodynamic fluctuations during invasive procedures.

The study has several methodological strengths: randomized controlled trial design, standardized protocol with consistent measurements by the same researchers, and multiple time point measurements (Pre-UT1, Post-UT2, post-UT3) evaluating immediate and short-term intervention effects. Group similarity in demographic characteristics and baseline measurements strengthens inter-group comparison validity. The validated and reliable measurement tools (SAI and VAS) represent additional strengths.

Methodological limitations include single-center design limiting result generalizability. Multicenter studies across different healthcare institutions and populations would increase external validity. Blinding could not be achieved due to intervention nature, potentially creating bias in subjective measurements (anxiety and pain scores). However, objective hemodynamic parameter measurements providing similar results partially balance this effect. Another limitation of the study is the standardization of VR content. Disregarding individual preferences may have led to different effects on patients. Not evaluating longterm effects limits commenting on intervention permanence. High baseline anxiety in both groups may reflect selection bias toward more anxious individuals and a ceiling effect, potentially limiting generalizability; therefore, future studies should include a broader range of anxiety levels. In the current study at pain assessment protocol, VAS was measured only during the procedure, which limited the ability to monitor temporal changes in pain. In future studies, it is recommended to perform repeated VAS measurements at specific procedural steps (catheter insertion, bladder filling, voiding phase) to better characterize pain dynamics. Another limitation is that RR is typically measured by observing chest movements over one minute, which carries a potential risk of measurement error compared to automated devices.

This randomized trial establishes VRG as an effective nonpharmacological intervention for anxiety reduction and hemodynamic stabilization during UT. VRG demonstrated clinically significant anxiety improvements and beneficial DBP and RR modulation. The intervention’s safety, cost-effectiveness, and ease of implementation support its integration as adjunctive therapy for enhancing patient comfort during invasive urological procedures. However, practical barriers such as equipment acquisition and operation may limit the clinical implementation of VRG. To overcome these challenges, it is recommended to implement measures such as nursing training programs, budget and resource management, technical support and maintenance, as well as pilot testing and phased integration. These strategies can support the sustainable and effective application of VRG. Furthermore, incorporating VRG into urological nursing practice will enable nurses to actively manage patient outcomes, thereby enhancing the overall quality and patient-centeredness of care. This approach not only improves the patient experience during invasive procedures but also supports the adoption of innovative practices in urological nursing. This study demonstrated the effectiveness of VRG; however, to evaluate the generalizability of the findings and long-term effects, multicenter, long-term randomized controlled trials with larger samples and different urological procedures are warranted.
