Fecal Microbiota Transplantation by Rectal Enema Improves Short-Term Insulin Resistance in Metabolic Syndrome: A Pilot Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Setthachai Piwchan, Natsuda Aumpan, Soonthorn Chonprasertsuk, Bubpha Pornthisarn, Sith Siramolpiwat, Patommatat Bhanthumkomol, Pongjarat Nunanan, Navapan Issariyakulkarn, Arti Wongcha-Um, Muhammad Miftahussurur, Varocha Mahachai, Yoshio Yamaoka, Ratha-korn Vilaichone
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe gut microbiota plays a vital role in various physiological processes, including metabolism. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) involves transferring fecal matter from a healthy donor to rebalance a patient’s intestinal dysbiosis. The impact of FMT on metabolic syndrome (MetS) is subject to debate. This study assesses the effects of FMT on MetS when administered by rectal enema.MethodsIn a double-blind, randomized controlled trial, subjects with MetS were assigned to receive either FMT (n=8) or a sham intervention (n=10) via rectal enema. Participants were followed at 6 and 12 weeks. The primary outcome was changes in the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR). Secondary outcomes included fasting blood glucose (FBG), body mass index (BMI), inflammatory markers, and hepatic steatosis. The mean adjusted difference (MAD) and 95% confidence interval (CI) between groups were reported as treatment effects using a linear marginal model for repeated measures.ResultsThe study included patients with a mean age of 50.4±10.7 years. Baseline BMI and HOMA-IR were similar between groups. Over 6 weeks, FMT significantly improved HOMA-IR (MAD, −1.63; 95% CI, −2.63 to −0.64; P=0.001). The FMT group also showed improvements in serum FBG and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein compared with levels in the sham group (P=0.044 and P=0.025, respectively). However, no significant changes in MetS-associated variables or liver steatosis were evident at 12 weeks. Stool microbiota analysis revealed a reduced relative abundance of Desulfovibrio, Bacteroides, and Parabacteroides after FMT.ConclusionFMT by rectal enema produced favorable changes in IR in patients with MetS. FMT may be an effective treatment for patients with metabolism-related diseases. Further research into the long-term benefits of the procedure is warranted.Keywords: Dysbiosis, Fecal microbiota transplantation, Insulin resistance, Metabolic syndrome

INTRODUCTION

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a widespread condition marked by insulin resistance (IR) and central obesity, with significant public health implications due to its strong association with increased risks of cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), stroke, myocardial infarction, and mortality.1-3 It has also been linked to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis and various cancers.4,5 The pathogenesis of MetS involves both genetic and environmental factors, including poor diet and sedentary lifestyles. Recent studies have highlighted the role of gut health in MetS, specifically mechanisms such as the translocation of lipopolysaccharides that can cause metabolic endotoxemia, and systemic inflammation, which negatively affects insulin regulation. Reduced short-chain fatty acids and altered bile-acid metabolism can also contribute to IR, underscoring the importance of gut dysbiosis in MetS pathology.6

Research on fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) for improving insulin sensitivity (IS) in individuals with MetS has yielded inconsistent results, possibly due to variation in administration methods, treatment duration, and participant characteristics.7 Although some studies on duodenal FMT have shown positive effects on IS,8-10 others have found no significant improvement in IR measures, such as the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR). Repeated duodenal FMT that increased donor microbiota engraftment reportedly produced no meaningful changes in levels of glucose or glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels,11 and oral-capsule FMT is associated with mixed results.12,13 Similarly, colonoscopy-administered FMT in severely obese patients did not improve metabolic markers.14 FMT by rectal enema, which is widely available and less invasive, has shown promise when conducted in combination with colonoscopy for improving hepatic steatosis.15,16 This pilot study was designed to evaluate the effects of rectal enema FMT on IR in MetS patients, focusing on changes in HOMA-IR values, fasting blood glucose (FBG), HbA1c, body mass index (BMI), inflammatory markers, lipid profiles, and liver steatosis over 6 and 12 weeks.

METHODS

Study design

This study was a parallel, double-blind, randomized controlled trial conducted between June 2023 and February 2024 at Thammasat University Hospital, Pathum Thani, Thailand. It involved participants with MetS and moderate-to-severe IR (HOMA-IR values >2.5). The trial was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Thammasat University (Medicine; COA number: 130/2023) and registered with the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR20240805004). The study protocol adhered to good clinical practice guidelines and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The authors had complete access to the study data and jointly reviewed and approved the final manuscript, ensuring accuracy and integrity in reporting.

Study population

Outpatient adults aged 20 to 70 years and diagnosed with MetS as defined by the International Diabetes Federation, American Heart Association, and National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute were considered for enrollment.17 Qualifying individuals had to exhibit at least three of the following: abdominal obesity (waist circumference >80 cm for females and >90 cm for males), hypertension (blood pressure ≥130/80 mmHg or above, or use of hypertension medications), impaired FBG (≥100 mg/dL or higher), high triglyceride (TG) levels (>150 mg/dL), or low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) <40 mg/dL for males and <50 mg/dL for females. Exclusion criteria included severe chronic conditions such as end-stage kidney disease (with or without renal therapy); hepatic cirrhosis (a Child-Pugh score of class B or C); congestive heart failure; type 1 diabetes mellitus or T2DM; immunodeficiency disorders; and use of immunosuppressants, chemotherapy, antibiotics, pre- or probiotics, or experimental drugs within the last 12 weeks. Other exclusions were prior gastrointestinal surgery affecting the anatomy, smoking, pregnancy, and breastfeeding. Participants were advised to maintain their usual dietary habits and caloric intake while engaging in light-to-moderate physical activities without vigorous exertion. The use of antibiotics, prebiotics, or probiotics was not allowed during the study period.

Randomization and allocation

Participants were initially screened and interviewed at the medical outpatient department to determine eligibility. Upon providing informed consent, basic clinical information was collected, and participants were randomly assigned to either a FMT enema group or a sham intervention group at a 1:1 ratio. Randomization was achieved through computer-generated codes with a block size of 4 and managed by a research assistant blinded to the study protocol. Allocation sequences were maintained using sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes. Allocation information was revealed only to the technician responsible for preparing the filtrate for each FMT procedure. Both the study investigators and participants remained blinded to the intervention allocation. Follow-up visits were conducted exclusively by a registered nurse who was also blinded to the assigned intervention, ensuring unbiased assessment of outcomes.

Intervention

Potential stool donors were screened for a BMI of more than 23 kg/m2 and evaluated for both physical and mental health, following stringent criteria.18,19 After comprehensive screening for pathogens in stool, urine, and blood, only one donor out of 11 met the eligibility criteria. Stool samples provided within 4 hours of defecation were inspected visually to ensure they were well formed and free of blood or mucus. Donor stool was homogenized with isotonic saline and glycerol, filtered, and stored at −80 °C. FMT solutions were prepared using the stool sample from this single donor, while sham transplantation was performed using a saline infusion. To ensure blinding, all solutions were contained in opaque syringes. During the procedure, 200 mL of either the FMT solution, containing 50 g of stool, or the sham solution was administered through the rectum and distal colon over 2–3 minutes. Following administration, participants were instructed to lie on their left side for 15 minutes, and then on their back for another 15 minutes, to minimize the urge to defecate.16 No bowel preparation was performed prior to the procedure.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the mean difference in HOMA-IR values between baseline and follow-up intervals because a high HOMA-IR value is reportedly associated with the development of diabetes and MetS outcomes, including non-fatal cardiac events.20 Secondary outcomes included changes in metabolic parameters (i.e., BMI, FBG, HbA1c, and lipid profiles), IR indicators (serum fasting insulin level, and the triglyceride glucose [TyG] index21), hepatic steatosis as measured by the liver-controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) using transient elastography, and inflammatory markers (high-sensitivity C-reactive protein [hs-CRP], erythrocyte sedimentation rate [ESR], and ferritin). Adverse events related to FMT were also evaluated.

Follow-up assessment

At baseline, clinical data and baseline blood chemistries of interest were collected from participants. We followed up with participants at 6 and 12 weeks after the interventions to assess primary and secondary outcomes. Adverse events were evaluated by telephone at 1 and 4 weeks after the interventions, and in person at 6 weeks. Both the laboratory interpreter and the assigned outpatient nurse assessing adverse events were blinded to the interventions. The composition of the fecal microbiota between baseline and 12 weeks was also analyzed.

Stool microbiota composition analysis

Full-length 16S rRNA sequencing

Full-length 16S rRNA sequences of microorganisms were obtained using Oxford Nanopore Sequencing and recorded in the FASTQ file format. All sequences were classified against the SILVA database v138 (Max Planck Institute for Marine Microbiology) for taxonomy identification and quantified into taxonomic abundance using Emu v3.4.5 (Rice University).22

Microbiome data analysis

Taxonomic data were categorized by abundance for each taxonomic level, including phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species, using R v4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Alpha diversity, including Shannon diversity, Simpson diversity, observed features, and the Pielou index were calculated in R using the vegan v2.6-6.1 package. Alpha diversity metrics were statistically compared before and after FMT using a paired Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Beta diversity, including Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, Jaccard similarity, unique fraction metric (UniFrac) phylogenetic distance, and weighted-Unifrac phylogenetic distance, was analyzed in R using the phyloseq v1.46.023 package. Beta diversity metrics were compared statistically before and after FMT using a paired permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) test. Differentially abundant taxa, including phylum, genus, and species, were identified using the paired Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The gut dysbiosis score (GDS), which ranges from 1 to 10, with higher scores indicating worse dysbiosis, was used to evaluate the level of gut dysbiosis. Each GDS was compared before and after FMT using a paired Wilcoxon rank-sum test. All statistical tests were conducted in R v4.3.1 and visualized in R using the ggplot2 v3.4.4 package.

Statistical analysis

We adopted the efficacy of FMT from a previous study14 that reported mean differences on the HOMA-IR of −0.24±0.12 between groups to calculate the sample size. We determined the number of patients to be enrolled in this trial using an alpha error of 0.05 and a beta error of 0.10. Data from seven participants in each arm were used for the calculations. Factoring in an estimated dropout rate of 20% among the included patients, a minimum of 18 patients were required to achieve the primary outcome.

Continuous data with normal distributions were described using means and standard deviations, while non-normal data used medians and interquartile ranges. Categorical data were shown as frequency and percentage. Independent t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests compared continuous variables; Fisher’s exact tests compared categorical variables. The primary outcome focused on the mean change in HOMA-IR values at baseline, week 6, and week 12 across the intervention groups. We used a linear marginal model with mixed effects and restricted maximum likelihood regression to analyze repeated measures. We accounted for within-patient correlations using an unstructured covariance pattern. The final model also included the following covariables: the baseline HOMA-IR value, intervention group, time point (visit), and the interaction term of time point by interventions. The treatment effect was quantified as the mean adjusted difference (MAD) between groups and 95% confidence interval (CI). For secondary outcomes, statistical analysis followed the same approach and were conducted on an intention-to-treat basis. A 2-sided P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The data were analyzed in STATA version 16.0 (Stata Corp.).

RESULTS

From June 2023 to February 2024, we recruited 52 individuals diagnosed with MetS at least 3 months prior to the trial’s commencement. Thirty-four individuals were excluded from the study. Consequently, 18 individuals were enrolled and randomly assigned to either the FMT enema group (n=8) or the sham enema group (n=10). There was no loss to follow-up by the end of the study; all participants received the interventions and completed the follow-up assessments at 6 and 12 weeks (Fig. 1). Demographic characteristics of the two study groups were comparable (Table 1). Participant distribution exhibited near equivalence between males (44%) and females (56%), with mean BMIs of 29.6±4.7 and 29.8±6.2 kg/m2 in the FMT and placebo arms, respectively. All participants were classified as having moderate-to-severe IR, with a baseline HOMA-IR value ≥2.5. However, the HOMA-IR values of the FMT group tended to be randomly higher than those of the other group, with means of 6.53±3.95 and 4.57±1.48, respectively.

Effect of FMT on HOMA-IR values

After 6 weeks of intervention, notable enhancements were evident in HOMA-IR values within the FMT group in contrast to the sham group (MAD, −1.63; 95% CI, −2.62 to −0.64; P=0.001), as shown in Fig. 2. These improvements corresponded with reductions in FBG (MAD, −7.5; 95% CI, −14.7 to −0.2; P=0.044). However, by week 12 of the interventions, there were no significant advancements in HOMA-IR values compared with the findings at the week 6 follow-up (MAD, −1.11; 95% CI, −3.66 to 1.42; P=0.388). Similar patterns and no statistically significant differences emerged in the discrepancies observed in FBG (Table 2).

Effect of FMT on cardiometabolic parameters, hepatic steatosis, and inflammatory markers

For cardiometabolic parameters, no differences were noted between the groups in terms of BMI, HbA1c, TyG index, TG, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, or HDL-C from baseline to the week 12 follow-up. Additionally, hepatic steatosis, which was assessed through CAP values, did not vary between the interventions. Regarding inflammatory markers, there were no significant discrepancies in ESR and ferritin levels between the groups were evident. A notable contrast emerged in hs-CRP levels at the week 6 follow-up (MAD, −1.37; 95% CI, −2.57 to −0.18; P=0.025), but these differences were not sustained at the end of the 12-week period (Table 2).

Effect of FMT on intestinal microbiota

Stool microbiota analysis was completed for six of eight participants (A1–A3, A5, A6, and A8) in the FMT group at both baseline and the 12-week follow-up, while half of the participants (B1–B5) in the sham group were sampled. Alpha diversity, which reflects microbial richness and evenness, was assessed using Shannon and Simpson diversity indices. No significant changes in these indices were observed across groups from baseline to follow-up. However, there was a nonsignificant trend toward reduced alpha diversity in the sham group compared with the intervention group (P>0.05) (Fig. 3).

Differences in alpha diversity indexes between pre- and post-intervention. (A) Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) group and (B) sham group (P<0.05 is considered significant).

Beta diversity, which evaluates species presence, abundance, and phylogenetic relationships, was analyzed using Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, Jaccard similarity, UniFrac phylogenetic distance, and weighted-UniFrac phylogenetic distance. The overall gut microbiota profiles of individuals showed no significant changes before and after the interventions (P for PERMANOVA test >0.05) as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Similarly, individual-level analyses using Euclidean distances yielded comparable findings, indicating that the gut microbiota structure remained relatively stable.

As anticipated, the dominant taxa across all samples were Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, and Proteobacteria. Although alpha and beta diversity measures did not shift significantly, differences in the overall microbiota composition were observed between the pre- and post-FMT states. Specifically, a relative abundance analysis revealed that FMT participants exhibited reduced levels of Desulfovibrio (DSV), Bacteroides, and Parabacteroides (Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. 2). Furthermore, the GDS decreased in the FMT group, indicating potential improvements in gut microbial balance, while the sham group showed minimal changes.

Tolerance and safety

No serious adverse events were reported in either group during the 12-week study (Supplementary Table 1). Nausea and abdominal bloating occurred more frequently in the sham group than in the FMT group, but all adverse symptoms were classified as mild. Both participants who experienced diarrhea reported improvement within 2 days, with spontaneous resolution of symptoms that did not require antibiotic intervention. This suggests that the adverse effects of FMT were minimal and self-limiting.

DISCUSSION

This double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial was designed to test the efficacy of FMT by rectal enema in healthy lean donors and its ability to alter the gut microbiota in adults with MetS and moderate-to-severe IR. We found that, after 6 weeks of FMT enemas, significant improvements were evident in HOMA-IR values and FBG and hs-CRP levels, all without serious adverse effects, even in the absence of antibiotic pretreatment or bowel lavage. Despite these initial promising results, the benefits of short-term (6-week) rectal FMT in MetS patients were not sustained at the 12-week interval. This raises important questions about how to maintain the positive effects of the treatment of MetS. Although repeated FMT administrations have shown notable benefits in terms of engraftment,11,12 which may help ameliorate chronic inflammation and improve the intestinal environment in patients with non-communicable diseases, and MetS in particular, it is crucial to enhance our understanding of how to maintain a balanced microbial ecosystem. A recent review suggested incorporating prebiotics and probiotics can enhance the efficacy of FMT when treating metabolic disorders.24 The validity of this concept was confirmed by a study that used oral FMT capsules alongside dietary fibers such as dextrin, inulin, galactooligosaccharide, fructo-oligosaccharide, and probiotics.25 However, further studies are needed to determine whether repeated FMT or incorporating these methods can help extend the observed clinical benefits.

Our study was an attempt to evaluate the impact of FMT via rectal enema on MetS-related variables. The primary metric, HOMA-IR value, exhibited a significant improvement 6 weeks after FMT. HOMA-IR values are considered a validated surrogate measure for IR,26 reflecting both hepatic insulin and peripheral insulin sensitivities. They are strongly correlated with hyperinsulinemic euglycemic clamps, which are the gold standard for IR measurement.27 Further investigation revealed the HOMA-IR value is significantly associated with outcomes related to MetS, and it has been used in other studies involving FMT, as well. FMT via rectal enema may be preferable to duodenal FMT due to potential risks such as malodorousness and aspiration.28 Another factor in favor of enema delivery is the high diversity and abundance of gut microbiota inhabiting the large intestine.29 Targeting these microbes with an enema could yield benefits comparable to those provided through other routes, as indicated by positive outcomes observed in refractory infections of Clostridioides difficile and in critically ill patients when colonoscopy was not feasible.30

Our study also aimed to assess the impact of FMT enemas on lipid profiles and hepatic steatosis due to the known relationship between gut dysbiosis and liver inflammation that leads to fat accumulation and fibrosis.31 However, we did not observe any improvement in lipid metabolism or liver fat content, which is consistent with previously reported findings on non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. However, some studies have shown a slight reduction in liver fat content and markers of steatohepatitis after repeated FMT dosing, which contrasts with the results of our study, which used a single dose of rectal FMT. This underscores the potential importance of repeated dosing intervals to ensure successful engraftment of intestinal microbiota. Our study also assessed changes in liver fat content at 12 weeks, which may have been a relatively short follow-up period compared with previous studies and could have contributed to less-pronounced findings.32,33 Moreover, elevated inflammatory marker levels, particularly hs-CRP, are associated with metabolic disorders and increased cardiovascular risks.34 Higher ferritin levels have also been linked to a doubled risk of MetS.35 However, our study suggests there is a favorable short-term effect of FMT enemas on hs-CRP compared with the sham group, indicating potential benefits in addressing MetS outcomes.

The adverse effects commonly associated with rectal enema FMT include mild and temporary symptoms, such as abdominal discomfort, cramping, bloating, diarrhea, and constipation. In this trial, participants primarily experienced mild and self-limiting diarrhea. Despite the generally low incidence of side effects, there was no clear explanation for the observed variation in bowel-movement frequency among participants. This inconsistency in bowel patterns could not be directly correlated with the reported side effects, and further study is warranted.

Through the analysis of fecal samples collected using metagenomic sequencing, we found IR was associated with alterations in the gut microbiota. Specifically, enteric dysbiosis was characterized by a shift in the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio, with a decrease in butyrate-producing Firmicutes and an increase in Bacteroidetes. These microbial changes have been linked to increased intestinal permeability and bacterial translocation, contributing to IR and, ultimately, the development of T2DM. In stool samples from patients with diabetic nephropathy, a significant reduction in the abundance of Roseburia and a marked increase in Bacteroides was observed.36,37 Although our study did not detect significant changes in alpha and beta diversity, this may be due to the loss of a sustained response at 12 weeks and the small sample size. However, short-term monitoring revealed a decrease in the levels of the phylum Bacteroidota, a particularly in species of Bacteroides and Parabacteroides. These findings emphasize the potential of FMT to restore microbial balance and alter the progression of metabolic diseases. Our study also revealed lower levels of DSV, a sulfate-reducing bacterium and resident commensal within the human gastrointestinal tract, which belongs to the phylum Deltaproteobacteria. Hydrogen sulfide produced by DSV has been shown to cause inflammation in the human intestine, degrade the mucin layer, and reduce gut microbial diversity. Recent reviews have demonstrated a positive correlation between DSV overgrowth and various diseases, particularly MetS and T2DM. In vivo studies using animal models of atherosclerosis have linked elevated levels of DSV to increased formation of atherosclerotic lesions, heightened inflammation, and increased intestinal permeability. In models of obesity, DSV has been associated with increased expression of CD63, a marker of inflammatory processes.38 A reduction in the number of this species following rectal enema FMT is expected to result in improved metabolic profiles.

Although our sample size was small, it was consistent with those of similar studies and supported by appropriate sample-size calculations. The study’s relatively brief duration aligned with that used in prior research, yet only a limited response was observed at 12 weeks. Despite these limitations, the trial’s design and analytical rigor contribute valuable insights into the potential therapeutic role of FMT in improving metabolic outcomes in patients with MetS and IR. Another limitation lies in the stool microbiota analysis, as we were only able to demonstrate a minimal trend in the decreased relative abundance IR-related microbial strains. We did not perform a functional pathway analysis of serum and fecal metabolites, particularly short-chain fatty acids and bile-acid components, and did not measure appetite or gut hormones.14 The possible direct mechanisms linking microbiota shifts to biological responses therefore could not be fully explained.

In conclusion, FMT by rectal enema demonstrated early improvements in metabolic markers, such as HOMA-IR values, FBG, and hs-CRP within 6 weeks. However, other variables related to MetS showed no significant changes compared with the sham group. These results suggest that repeated FMTs or the incorporation of complementary strategies may be necessary to achieve a more durable and effective outcomes.
