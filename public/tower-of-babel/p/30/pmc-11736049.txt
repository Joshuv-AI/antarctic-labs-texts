Double trouble: The intersection of neurobrucellosis and systemic lupus erythematosus in a 24-year-old female
Authors: Hashim Talib Hashim, Aws Murad Frhood, Ahmed Abdulhussain Shahatta, Ahmed Dheyaa Al-Obaidi, Arshed Shakir Kadim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease affecting multiple organs, while brucellosis is a zoonotic infection prevalent in endemic areas. Neurobrucellosis, a severe complication of brucellosis, can mimic or coexist with autoimmune conditions like SLE, complicating diagnosis and treatment. Introduction

Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) and brucellosis are two distinct conditions with unique pathophysiological mechanisms and clinical presentations. SLE is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by widespread inflammation and tissue damage, affecting multiple organ systems. It predominantly affects women of childbearing age and presents with a variety of symptoms, including malar rash, polyarthralgia, and systemic manifestations such as fever and weight loss. The etiology of SLE is multifactorial, involving genetic, environmental, and hormonal factors [[1], [2], [3]].

Brucellosis, on the other hand, is a zoonotic infection caused by bacteria of the genus Brucella, typically acquired through direct contact with infected animals or consumption of contaminated animal products. The disease is endemic in many parts of the world, including the Mediterranean basin, Middle East, and Latin America. Brucellosis can present with a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations, ranging from acute febrile illness to chronic debilitating conditions. One of the serious complications of brucellosis is neurobrucellosis, which involves the central nervous system and can lead to meningitis, encephalitis, or myelitis [4,5].

The coexistence of SLE and brucellosis, particularly neurobrucellosis, is exceedingly rare and presents a diagnostic challenge. The overlapping symptoms, such as fever, arthralgia, and neurological manifestations, can complicate the clinical picture and delay accurate diagnosis. Moreover, the immunosuppressive treatment often required for SLE can predispose patients to infections, including brucellosis, adding another layer of complexity to the management of these patients [6,7].

Case presentation

A 24-year-old female initially presented with vaginal bleeding that was diagnosed as a “vaginal ulcer.” Subsequently, she experienced migratory polyarthralgia, nocturnal fevers, and significant weight loss over a two-month period. After a thorough assessment, which included normal abdominal and pelvic ultrasounds that ruled out any structural problems, her condition worsened with the sudden occurrence of severe headaches and dizziness. As a result, she was diagnosed with meningitis. The clinical examination revealed a malar rash, mild hepatomegaly, and positive anti-dsDNA antibodies (3.24), along with elevated CRP (126.32) and ESR levels, indicative of systemic inflammation. Normal serum electrolytes and negative virology results excluded alternative infectious etiologies. The study of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) demonstrated features that are consistent with a bacterial infection. This is aligned with the patient's history of animal contact and living in an endemic area. These findings confirm the diagnosis of neurobrucellosis (Fig. 1). The prompt and significant response to empirical treatment with doxycycline and rifampicin provided additional evidence supporting the infectious cause. These findings collectively underscore a complex presentation likely involving neurobrucellosis superimposed on an underlying autoimmune process, possibly systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

Discussion

The coexistence of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and neurobrucellosis in a single patient is rare and poses significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. This case underscores the complexity of diagnosing overlapping infectious and autoimmune conditions, particularly in regions where brucellosis is endemic [7].

The patient's initial presentation with a vaginal ulcer, migratory polyarthralgia, nocturnal fevers, and significant weight loss could be attributed to either SLE or brucellosis, complicating the differential diagnosis. The presence of a malar rash and positive anti-dsDNA antibodies strongly suggested SLE, a diagnosis further supported by elevated inflammatory markers (CRP and ESR). However, the development of severe headaches and dizziness, combined with the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) findings indicative of bacterial infection, directed attention towards an infectious etiology, specifically neurobrucellosis [2,3,7].

Neurobrucellosis, although uncommon, is a recognized complication of brucellosis and can present with a variety of neurological symptoms, including meningitis. The patient's history of animal contact and residence in an endemic area further supported this diagnosis [8]. The rapid and significant response to empirical treatment with doxycycline and rifampicin not only confirmed neurobrucellosis but also highlighted the importance of prompt intervention in such cases.

This case illustrates the need for heightened clinical suspicion and comprehensive diagnostic evaluation in patients presenting with complex, multifaceted symptoms. The overlapping clinical features of SLE and brucellosis necessitate a thorough approach to differentiate and manage these conditions effectively. Furthermore, it emphasizes the critical role of considering endemic infectious diseases in the differential diagnosis, particularly in patients with autoimmune conditions that may predispose them to infections [9,10].

Conclusion

The intersection of SLE and neurobrucellosis in this patient underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach to diagnosis and treatment, ensuring that both the autoimmune and infectious aspects of the patient's condition are adequately addressed. This case contributes valuable insights into the management of such rare and complex presentations, emphasizing the need for vigilance and adaptability in clinical practice. This case underscores the importance of considering dual diagnoses in patients with multifaceted clinical presentations and emphasizes the need for a thorough and multidisciplinary approach to diagnosis and treatment.
