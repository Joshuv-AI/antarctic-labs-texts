A prospective audit of thoracic reirradiation practice and peer-review in a high-volume academic center☆
Authors: Geraldine Murphy, Daniel Tong, Grace Wu, B.C. John Cho, Meredith Giuliani, Andrew Hope, Benjamin H. Lok, Alexander Sun, Jean-Pierre Bissonnette, Andrea Bezjak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Prospective audit of thoracic reirradiation cases presented at quality assurance rounds in a high-volume academic center.•Challenges encountered in reconstructing and evaluating cumulative dose.•Variable decision-making regarding optimal plan modifications when required and normal tissue recovery.•Prospective evidence and guidelines are required to support practice in reirradiation.

Introduction

Reirradiation is an increasingly common challenge faced by the radiation oncology multidisciplinary team [1], defined as a new course of radiotherapy to a previously irradiated volume or in which the cumulative dose raises concern of toxicity [2]. Due to improvements in radiotherapy technology, reirradiation is an increasingly viable option to offer patients. With advancements in systemic treatment options leading to longer survival [3] and increased recognition of the oligometastatic disease paradigm [4], patients are receiving more local therapy including multiple courses of radiation. Despite the increased utilization of reirradiation, high-quality prospective evidence to guide practice is lacking. The current evidence guiding thoracic reirradiation practice consists predominantly of retrospective data and expert consensus guidelines [5,6]. Radiation oncologists internationally report a scarcity of institutional reirradiation guidelines [7]. Furthermore, cumulative dose constraints for organs-at-risk (OARs), normal tissue recovery and the minimum interval between radiotherapy courses varies widely [7].

Inconsistency in practice is also recognized in the treatment planning of lung cancer, where inter- and intra-clinician variability is well-documented [8]. Quality assurance (QA) rounds aim to address this issue by identifying deficiencies in radiotherapy plans that could compromise patient outcomes, ultimately aiming to improve the therapeutic index and, thus, the quality of care for patients [9]. One study reported that 7 % of cases discussed received suggestions for improvement during peer-review, with thoracic radiotherapy plans amongst the most frequently critiqued [10]. Considering the increasing frequency of reirradiation, the lack of prospective evidence and the heterogeneity in practice, peer-review is a crucial step to improve quality in reirradiation treatment.

The primary aims of this study are to examine the patterns of practice in relation to thoracic reirradiation in our center, to determine the level of consistency with current best evidence in the area and to identify the challenges and specific patient safety concerns encountered in planning reirradiation.

Materials and Methods

Data collection

This prospective study included all patients undergoing thoracic radiotherapy discussed at weekly thoracic radiotherapy QA rounds over a twelve-month period between April 2024 and March 2025. All thoracic radiotherapy target volumes and treatment plans are reviewed by a multidisciplinary team including radiation therapists involved in radiotherapy planning, thoracic radiation oncologists, radiation oncology trainees, and medical physicists. The treating radiation oncologist presents the patients’ clinical history including previous radiotherapy and current treatment rationale while the radiation therapist presents the radiotherapy plan. Additional information reviewed includes diagnostic imaging, previous radiotherapy plans if available, the intended radiotherapy prescription, target volumes and OAR constraints.

Patients undergoing thoracic reirradiation were identified at the QA meeting. The data was prospectively collected by a radiation oncology fellow and recorded in a database with predefined categorical variables. Data collected included: patient demographics, primary tumor site, prior radiotherapy details (site, dose/fractionation, previous treatment year) current treatment intent (radical vs palliative) and planned dose/fractionation. Radical intent was defined as reirradiation dose ≥ 50 Gray biologically equivalent dose (BED) in 2 Gray per fraction (utilizing α/β = 10 Gy). Reirradiation was classified as per Andratschke et al [2].

At our center, prior planning datasets are reproduced to evaluate cumulative dose overlap by importing DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) files containing CT images, previous target contours, and dose distributions. We generate relevant isodose lines from the previous plan, followed by rigid registration of the prior dataset to the current imaging dataset. The corresponding isodose lines are then transferred rigidly onto the new CT dataset. To enhance the accuracy of cumulative dose estimation, we optimize the alignment of the previously treated area to current anatomy. Deformable registration is not routinely used. All reproduced datasets undergo formal medical physics evaluation to assess reliability. The extent of overlap of the current and previous radiotherapy treatment plan, what OARs were involved and if any critical structure was identified within the overlap was recorded including any dose discount applied to OARs for previous treatment. Tissue-dependent recovery or dose discount is defined as the amount of previously administered radiotherapy dose that is assumed to be recovered and can be subtracted from cumulative dose calculations [2]. Cumulative OAR dose constraints followed international expert guidance where available [5]. We documented any compromise that was made to the current radiotherapy plan, defined as a reduction of prescription dose coverage of the target volumes from our institutional standard dose coverage goals or a reduction in the prescription dose from standard dose prescription protocols. We also captured details of the discussion between the multidisciplinary team about each case; any challenges encountered in planning and special recommendations made with regards to the radiotherapy treatment.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to determine the distribution of the current treatment intent, extent of overlap, dose-limiting OARs identified, the frequency that a compromise was made in the plan, and the proportion of plans that received a recommendation for change.

This project was undertaken as a clinical audit to assess and improve current practices and received formal exemption from institutional research ethics board review.

Results

General thoracic radiotherapy QA rounds and patient characteristics

466 unique cases were reviewed in 26 thoracic radiotherapy QA meetings recorded over six non-consecutive months between April 2024 and March 2025, averaging 24 cases (range: 16–33) per QA round. 85 (18.2 %) cases of reirradiation were identified. 58 (68.2 %) of the reirradiation plans were of radical intent. Cumulative dose overlap occurred in 76 (89.4 %) reirradiation plans. The most common primary cancer histology that was being treated included cancers of the lung (56.5 %, n = 48) and intrathoracic metastatic disease from head and neck cancer (17.6 %, n = 15). The most frequent anatomical locations of previous radiotherapy treatment comprised of the lung and/or mediastinum (69.4 %, n = 59), the head and neck including supraclavicular fossa (21.2 %, n = 18) and breast or chest wall (9.4 %, n = 8). Challenges encountered while planning reirradiation, as discussed at QA rounds

Reproduction of the previous planning dataset

In 14 (16.5 %) cases, the peer-review group deemed the registration of the previous radiotherapy planning dataset unreliable. This was due to anatomical changes, most commonly fibrosis of previously irradiated lung or interval surgery (e.g. pneumonectomy), in seven cases (8.2 %). The patient was simulated in a different position in 3 of 85 (3.5 %) cases (e.g. differences in arm position). It was deemed that the registration had been registered inaccurately in 3 of 85 (3.5 %) cases, and an adjustment was recommended to improve it, including registration to a stable anatomical structure within the vicinity of the treatment target volume (e.g. vertebra).

Considerations for OARs

Critical OARs in reirradiation plans

The most frequently identified dose-limiting OARs, within the reirradiation plans included the proximal bronchial tree (20 %, n = 17), esophagus (18.8 %, n = 16) and spinal canal (15.3 %, n = 13). Less commonly, the great vessels (10.6 %, n = 9) and brachial plexus (9.4 %, n = 8) were considered at high risk of toxicity during peer-review discussion. The lungs (7.1 %, n = 6), ribs or chest wall (4.7 %, n = 4), skin (1.2 %, n = 1) and heart (1.2 %, n = 1) were infrequently highlighted as at risk of toxicity from reirradiation.

Recovery of OARs from previous radiotherapy

In six cases (7.1 %), concerns were raised about normal tissue recovery following previous radiotherapy, prompted by visible changes in the patient’s normal tissue and uncertainty about the implications of these findings in the context of reirradiation. In four cases, there was significant lung fibrosis from prior radiotherapy. There was significant skin fibrosis in one case and osteoradionecrosis of a rib in another case. A discount for the previous radiotherapy dose to an OAR was specifically discussed at QA rounds in nine (10.6 %) cases. The median time interval since prior radiotherapy in this cohort was 12 years (IQR 14.5 years). In three cases, a dose discount of 50 % was applied to the spinal cord or esophagus [11,12]. The interval between radiotherapy courses was 10 years or more in these patients. In the remaining six cases, the dose discount did not follow a specific percentage and involved clinical judgement from the treating radiation oncologist.

Compromises made to reirradiation plans to improve safety

Compromise target volume coverage

In 13 (15.3 %) cases, target volume radiotherapy dose coverage was intentionally compromised from our institutional standard dose coverage goals. In five cases, the target volume contoured was reduced, limiting the target volume to the center of the tumor or to “Positron Emission Tomography (PET)-avid” disease only. In ten such cases, the planning target volume (PTV) was under-covered to reduce high-dose overlap, including prescribing different dose levels to the PTV and clinical target volume (CTV).

Dose distribution optimized to respect the dose-volume constraints of a critical OAR

The dose distribution of the radiotherapy plan was optimized to meet a specified lower dose-volume constraint for an OAR in 21 (24.7 %) cases. In 13 cases, this included sparing central mediastinal structures such as the proximal bronchial tree, esophagus and great vessels (Sample Case 1). The dose distribution was optimized to reduce the reirradiation dose to the spinal cord in six cases, the brachial plexus in three cases and a rib in two cases.

Sample case 1

An elderly patient had a diagnosis of biopsy-proven non-small cell lung cancer. They had a history of two prior courses of stereotactic ablative radiotherapy (SBRT) to three nodules in total, 18 months and 10 months prior. They were planned for SBRT to a central apical right upper lobe nodule which was abutting the mediastinum and immediately adjacent to a previous nodule treated with 60 Gy in 8 fractions 18 months previously. The most concerning OARs within the overlap were the spinal canal, esophagus, brachial plexus and trachea.

Figure 1Dose reduction from standard prescription

A modified radiotherapy dose was prescribed relative to standard dose prescription protocols in 20 (23.5 %) cases. In many cases (n = 17), the prescription dose was reduced while the same number of fractions was maintained. In two cases, the number of fractions was increased, while maintaining the same prescription dose (e.g. change from 20 Gy in 5 fractions to 20 Gy in 8 fractions), to reduce the BED. It was recommended to avoid hypofractionation and treat with maximum 2 Gray per fraction in the remaining case.

The impact of peer-review at thoracic radiotherapy QA rounds

Peer-review prompted revision of the reirradiation plans in ten (11.8 %) cases. In four cases, a replan was recommended to reduce the cumulative dose to an OAR. In two cases, reducing the size of the treatment target volumes was recommended to decrease the dose to the surrounding normal tissue. To ensure the accuracy of cumulative dose estimations, OAR structures were not altered beyond routine contour corrections. In four cases, it was recommended to abandon radiotherapy and consider alternative treatment options, including systemic treatment and surgical resection in the case of an oligoprogressive metastasis.

Discussion

This prospective review of reirradiation cases at a high-volume thoracic radiotherapy department demonstrates the challenges of reirradiation planning and the role of peer-review. With 18.2 % of cases discussed involving reirradiation, most being of radical intent with direct overlap of dose distributions, our findings highlight the growing prevalence and complexity of thoracic reirradiation.

The challenges encountered in reproducing prior radiotherapy dose distributions highlight a need for improvements in this area. Recent consensus states the overlay of previous dose distributions in 3D should be mandatory when available [2], which is a frequently used dose summation method internationally [7]. However, in our experience, image registration and reconstructed dose distributions were unreliable in 16 % of cases due to reasons including anatomical changes and the inherent limitations of rigid registration, the impact of which has been previously described [13,14]. Inaccuracies in cumulative dose estimations risk unintentional over or under-dosing of both the tumor and normal tissues, posing a significant patient safety concern.

A prior dose discount was applied for OARs in a minority of cases, with a minimum interval of 10 years since previous treatment. However, the majority were based on clinicians’ experience, rather than evidence-based rationale. Tissue-dependent recovery is the subject of ongoing research including the prospective observational ReCare cohort (EORTC RP-2011) with limited prospective evidence to guide practice outside of the central nervous system [2]. While consensus guidelines suggest cumulative thoracic OAR dose constraints [5,6], recommendations for a prescribed dose discount for OARs are lacking [6]. Nonetheless, partial tissue recovery for most thoracic OARs is commonly assumed internationally [7]. This reinforces the need for ongoing research into normal tissue recovery and validated cumulative dose constraints for thoracic OARs.

Despite increasing experience in reirradiation amongst radiation oncologists, there was considerable variability in the approach to reirradiation plans requiring modification to ensure plan safety. Modest target under-coverage to achieve acceptable normal tissue doses appears to be an appropriate modification, supported by consensus guidelines [5] and international practice patterns [7]. Altered dose/fractionation or patient-specific priorities, such as optimizing the plan to a particular OAR dose constraint are rarely reported in the literature. These approaches, while logical, may reduce the therapeutic benefit of an already complex treatment, emphasizing the need for clear guidance outlining appropriate modifications and their recommended prioritization.

Peer-review proved critical, prompting significant plan modifications in 11.8 % of cases, consistent with rates in routine radiotherapy QA rounds [15,16]. However, crucially in this cohort, 40 % of cases flagged for reassessment, were advised against reirradiation. Peer-review serves as a valuable mechanism for external validation and consensus building for clinicians [17] and promotes shared clinical decision-making [18], supporting clinicians in reirradiation, where treatment recommendations may carry significant risk. This demonstrates peer-reviews role in improving plan quality and reducing practice variation, particularly in complex treatment scenarios when robust guidance is lacking.

Limitations of this audit include its single-center design, reflecting institutional-specific practices which may not be adopted widely, affecting its generalizability. The clinical cases are heterogenous, making it difficult to infer universally applicable recommendations. Additionally, data collected was reliant on the depth of discussion at the QA meeting, which is practitioner-dependent and influenced by time constraints, a well-documented barrier at peer-review [19], potentially resulting in incomplete data collection. Nevertheless, this prospective analysis offers meaningful real-world insights into the nuances of thoracic reirradiation and current practices.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate the increased frequency, complexity and variability in thoracic reirradiation, highlighting the need for consensus guidelines and prospective data to support safe, effective practice. Until such time, multidisciplinary QA rounds play an essential role supporting decision-making in a challenging clinical scenario.
