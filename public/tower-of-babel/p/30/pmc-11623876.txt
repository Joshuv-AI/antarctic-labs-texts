Prevalence of bacteriuria and treatment outcomes in patients with symptomatic benign prostatic hyperplasia: a prospective observational study
Authors: Niroj Ramdam, Pawan Sapkota Upadhya, Prajjwol Luitel, Ishwor Thapaliya, Sujan Paudel, Shiwangi Kashyap, Rikesh Jung Karkee, Rakesh Kumar Gupta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Introduction:The global incidence of benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is rising, with bacterial causes of lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) being a common factor contributing to complications in these patients.Aim:To identify the prevalence of bacteriuria and treatment response in patients with symptomatic BPH.Materials and methods:This 1-year prospective study included all patients presenting with LUTS due to BPH who met the inclusion criteria. Urine samples were collected and analyzed for microscopy, culture, and sensitivity following standard protocols. Bacterial growth in urine specimens and International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) of LUTS were recorded before and after antibiotic therapy.Results:A total of 131 patients with symptomatic BPH were studied. Escherichia coli (47.6%) was the most commonly isolated bacteria, followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae and Pseudomonas species. E. coli and Klebsiella showed the highest sensitivity to nitrofurantoin. After 1 week of antibiotic therapy, 69% of patients had sterile urine cultures, while 31% remained E. coli positive. Significant improvement in IPSS scores was observed after 1 week of therapy (P<0.05), and there was no significant change in urine culture (P=0.854).Conclusion:Bacteriuria is a common finding in patients with symptomatic BPH in our setting. Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae were the most frequently isolated organisms, with sensitivity to nitrofurantoin and cefixime. A week of antibiotic therapy significantly reduced IPSS scores but did not reduce bacteriuria.

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a prevalent condition in aging males1. Symptomatic BPH is characterized by both irritative (urgency, frequency, nocturia) and obstructive symptoms (hesitancy, weak and interrupted urinary stream, straining to initiate urination, sensation of incomplete bladder emptying), leading to considerable impairment in quality of life1,2. The clinical severity of lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) is assessed by the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS), which uses seven questions about urinary symptoms, scoring from 0 to 35. Symptoms are categorized as mild (≤7), moderate (8–19), and severe (20–35)3. IPSS is recommended for baseline assessment of symptom severity in men with LUTS4. BPH can result in complications such as urinary tract infection (UTI), hematuria, urinary retention, obstructive uropathy, bladder stones, bladder diverticula, or necessitate prostate surgery5.

Bacteriuria is commonly observed in men with BPH, with a prevalence ranging from 4.4 to 44.7%6. Escherichia coli is the leading cause of LUTS among bacterial isolates, followed by Klebsiella species, Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Proteus species, and Providencia species1,7. Antibiotic sensitivity patterns reveal that ampicillin is the most effective first-line antibiotic for Gram-negative isolates, followed by amikacin. For Gram-positive isolates, Cefoxitin is most effective (53.8%), though there is evidence of increasing resistance to oxacillin and vancomycin7.

To the best of our knowledge, there has been no study regarding the bacteriology of urine and severity assessment of LUTS among patients with BPH in Nepal. This study aims to evaluate LUTS severity in symptomatic BPH patients using IPSS both before and after antibiotic therapy. It also seeks to identify common pathogens and determine the antibiotic sensitivity patterns of bacterial isolates from cultured urine samples of men with symptomatic BPH.

Material and methods

Study design

This was a hospital-based prospective observational study. The study protocol conforms to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki 2013 and has been reported in line with the STROCSS criteria8.

Setting and population

This study was performed at a tertiary academic center from September 2020 to August 2021.

Sample size

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients who presented with symptomatic BPH were included. We excluded patients with indwelling catheter, recent antibiotic use, immunocompromised state (for e.g.: HIV, HCV), uncontrolled DM (Hemoglobin A1c or Glycated hemoglobin>7%), use of antibiotics in preceding 1 month, steroid therapy and those who declined to provide consent.

Methodology

All patients with symptomatic BPH attending the Urology Out-Patient Department (OPD) of the study site were enrolled. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants. Demographic data (age, race, ethnicity) were recorded, and detailed histories were taken regarding the duration and symptoms of BPH, including incomplete bladder emptying, frequency, intermittency, urgency, weak stream, straining, nocturia, and relevant past history. IPSS scoring was done. A thorough clinical examination, including general physical, systemic, and digital rectal examination, was performed. During the rectal examination, the prostate was checked for nodules, softness, induration, or tenderness. Through ultrasonography, the size of the prostate in three dimensions was determined in a full bladder. Then after voiding urine, three-dimensional post-void residual volume was also assessed. Diagnosis of symptomatic BPH was made based on history, clinical examination, and radiological examination. Urine culture and antibiotic sensitivity tests were conducted in all patients with symptomatic BPH to assess the bacteriological profile.

For urine culture, 5 ml of midstream urine of the early morning sample was collected in a sterile vial under strict aseptic conditions. The sample was incubated aerobically at 35°C for 18–24 hours. If no growth was observed, the culture was reported as “sterile.” Individual colony types were examined microscopically. Pure cultures were obtained and subjected to Gram staining for identification. All procedures were conducted in accordance with Clinical Microbiology Laboratory Standard Operating Procedures. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the Kirby–Bauer disc diffusion method on Mueller–Hinton agar, following Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines. Commercial antimicrobial disks (penicillin, cephalosporins, carbapenem, fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, macrolides, sulfonamides, tetracyclines) were used to determine susceptibility patterns. Bacterial strains were classified as susceptible (S), intermediate (I), or resistant (R) based on the diameter of the zones of inhibition. After a week of antibiotic therapy, these procedures were repeated.

Outcomes

The primary objective of this study was to find the prevalence of bacteriuria and treatment response in patients with symptomatic BPH. Secondary objectives were to identify the common pathogens and antibiotic sensitivity patterns of bacterial isolates in cultured urine samples.

Data collection

Data were collected and entered in a predesigned proforma. The surgery resident recorded all data in a patient proforma, which was subsequently compiled into a Microsoft Excel sheet (Microsoft Corporation). The study measured several parameters and variables, including bacterial growth in urine specimens and the IPSS before and after antibiotic therapy.

Data analysis

The collected data were edited and then entered in Microsoft Excel 2010 and entered into SPSS 21.5 version for statistical analysis. Continuous variables were summarized using mean±standard deviation and examined using either the independent t-test or the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were described using frequencies or percentages and subjected to analysis using either the χ2-test or Fisher exact test. All P values were considered from a two-tailed perspective, and significance was defined by a P value of less than 0.05.

Ethical consideration

The study protocol was performed in accordance with the principle of the Declaration of Helsinki and after ethical approval by the Institutional Review Committee and Protocol committee (IRC reference no. IRC/1694/019) of our institution. To maintain confidentiality, patient information was safeguarded.

Results

A total of 131 patients were enrolled in our study.

Demographic and clinical characteristics

The mean age was 67.4±13.4 years (range 41–90 years) with the most common age at presentation being 61–70 years (28.2%). The majority (55%) of patients had symptoms of LUTS for less than 6 months. The majority (52.6%) of patients had no past history of recurrent UTI, while 34.4% had more than or equal to 2 episodes/6 months and 13% had more than or equal to 3 episodes/1 year. The majority of patients (67.2%) had grade 1 prostatomegaly. The majority of patients (55%) had PVRU less than or equal to 0 ml out of which 15 patients had insignificant PVRU (Table 1).

Forty-eight out of 59 culture-positive patients had a decrease in their IPSS scoring, irrespective of whether they had a change in their grading of severity or not. Discussion

BPH-induced bladder outlet obstruction is a significant contributor to bacteriuria and LUTS in men over 50 years old1,9. In our study, the most common age group affected by BPH was 61–70 years, which aligns with previous studies1,9.This occurs due to increased bacterial colonization in the urinary tract, caused by urinary stasis and incomplete bladder evacuation resulting from impaired bladder contractility10.

Our study observed bacteriuria in 45% of patients, consistent with findings reported by Agbugui et al. 1. The rate of UTI in this study falls within the range of 28–54% as reported by systematic review of bacteriuria in patients with underlying urological abnormalities11, but is lower than the rate observed by Asafo-Adjei et al. 12. This discrepancy may be due to the exclusion of BEP patients with indwelling catheters in our study, which were included in previous studies12,13. Procedures such as urethral catheterization and cystoscopy examinations may further elevate the risk of bacteriuria in these individuals14.

E. coli was the most commonly isolated organism, found in 22.1% of culture-positive specimens. This finding is consistent with previous studies conducted both within and outside the region1,7,13. The predominance of coliform organisms in urine samples suggests that contamination by endogenous bowel flora is a major factor responsible for bacteriuria among aging male population1. Other organisms isolated from culture include Klebsiella, Pseudomonas, yeast cells, staphylococcus aureus, and Enterococcus.

Recurrent UTI was observed in 34.4% of individuals within 6 months and 13% within a year. This recurrence could be due to the persistence of micro-organisms even after treatment or the occurrence of new infection13. Most patients (55%) had LUTS symptoms for less than 6 months, which is lower than reported by Oruma et al. 15, but higher than the 25.3% observed in population-based studies16. It may be because population-based studies might encompass a broader range of individuals, including those who delay seeking care or have milder symptoms that resolve without medical intervention.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing showed that E. coli and Klebsiella were most sensitive to nitrofurantoin, followed by cefixime. Their affordability, availability in oral formulations, and effectiveness against most Gram-negative organisms suggest these antibiotics could play a significant role in UTI prophylaxis and treatment in our region1 Despite a week-long course of antibiotics, organisms were isolated in the majority (66.10%) of culture-positive patients. Although Pourmand et al. 17 reported a reduction in bacteriuria from 15 to 3.3% with antibiotic administration, our study did not observe a significant difference.

The study identified an association between IPSS scores and UTIs as complications of BPH. Among culture-positive patients with BPH, 44.27% were in the moderate-to-severe IPSS category. Likewise prior studies reported higher IPSS scores were significantly correlated with an increased incidence of UTI complications5. In our study most patients from moderate and severe categories showed only slight reductions in IPSS score and remained in their respective categories, symptomatic relief was achieved across all groups after a week of antibiotic treatment. Our study reported a higher incidence of moderate-to-severe LUTS in our study compared to the 23.3% reported in a community-based epidemiological study of Korean population aged 50 and older18. Conventional treatment for UTI typically involves 7–14 days of antibiotic administration, chosen based on in-vitro sensitivity testing19. A short course of antibiotics resulted in a reduction of IPSS by decreasing inflammation and bacterial load, which alleviated urinary symptoms like bladder irritation and obstruction. However, it did not significantly change urine culture results, as shown in previous studies where a 1–3% failure rate for bacteriuria clearance was observed with short-term use of antibiotics19. Thus, longer antibiotic therapy may be necessary for complete bacteriuria eradication.

Our study has several limitations, including its single-center design, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings and populations. Future research to evaluate the effectiveness of longer antibiotic therapy. Additionally, larger, multi-center studies are necessary to confirm these findings and to optimize treatment protocols for managing bacteriuria in patients with BPH. The patient’s compliance to medication was not certain.

Conclusion

Bacteriuria is prevalent among patients with symptomatic BPH in our setting. Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae are the most frequently isolated organisms, with sensitivity to nitrofurantoin and cefixime, which are both affordable and available in oral formulations. A week of antibiotic therapy significantly reduced IPSS scores, providing significant symptom relief for patients. However, this short-term treatment did not significantly alter urine culture results, indicating that while antibiotics can effectively alleviate symptoms, they may not completely eradicate bacteriuria.
