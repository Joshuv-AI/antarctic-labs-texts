Contribution of albumin-corrected anion gap and associated biomarkers to the mortality of patients with congestive heart failure: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Mingyou Gao, Qi Li, Jin Bian, Xuan Wang, Chuyun Wang, Run Yuan, Xiang Li, Sheng Liu, Zhongkai Liao, Jichun Chen, Jie Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlbumin-corrected anion gap (ACAG) is a prognostic biomarker for various diseases. As a derived metric, the relationship between ACAG and potential related biomarkers, along with their combined effect on death, has yet to be fully elucidated. This study aims to investigate the association between ACAG and mortality of congestive heart failure (CHF), and to identify the specific biomarkers related to increased ACAG and the risk of mortality in CHF.MethodsThis study selected patients with CHF in intensive care units (ICU) from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database. Patients were stratified based on their ACAG levels into four groups. The outcomes were 28-day and 1-year mortality of CHF. Cox proportional hazard analysis and subgroup analysis were performed to explore the predictive value of ACAG. The mediation analysis and Pearson correlation analysis were used to explore potential associated biomarkers.ResultsA total of 2,689 patients with CHF were included in this study. Of these, 697 and 1,207 patients died within 28 days and 1 year, respectively. Cox proportional hazard analysis showed that increased ACAG level was significantly associated with both 28-day and 1-year mortality (hazard ratio [HR]: 2.18, 95%CI: [1.67, 2.84] and HR: 1.44, 95%CI: [1.18, 1.74], respectively) after adjusting for confounding factors. The subgroup analysis demonstrated that ACAG exhibited a pronounced predictive value among patients admitted to the coronary care unit (CCU), or with myocardial infarction in both 28-day and 1-year mortality. The mediating effect of ACAG was found to be significant between lactate, phosphate and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) concerning 28-day mortality (mediated proportion: 44.0%, 37.4% and 16.5%, respectively), but not 1-year mortality. All three biomarkers showed significant correlations with ACAG.ConclusionsElevated ACAG was a significant risk factor for 28-day and 1-year mortality in critically ill patients with CHF. ACAG plays an important role in mediating the association between lactate, phosphate and BUN and 28-day death in CHF patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05266-7.Keywords: ACAG, Congestive heart failure, Lactate, Phosphate, Blood urea nitrogen, Mediation analysis

Introduction

Congestive heart failure (CHF) is a serious medical condition characterized by the heart’s inability to pump sufficient blood to meet the body’s needs, this condition usually occurs in the advanced stages of various diseases, including hypertension, valvular, or other heart diseases. According to the American Heart Association, CHF accounted for 9.1% of cardiovascular deaths in its 2024 report [1], and 9.2% in 2023 [2], ranking as the fifth leading cause of death from cardiovascular diseases in the United States. Globally, CHF affects over 64 million people, and although the incidence of CHF has declined steadily in recent years, its prevalence is increasing and mortality and hospitalization rates remain high [3].

The albumin-corrected anion gap (ACAG), which is derived from the anion gap (AG), is a widely used, inexpensive and accessible biomarker. AG, calculated as ([Na] + [K] - [HCO3] - [Cl]), quantifies the difference between the amounts of negatively and positively charged electrolytes in serum. It is often associated with metabolic acidosis, a condition characterized by an increased level of acid in the blood, indicating the presence of acid-base disorders. Elevated AG may suggest several conditions, including tissue ischemia and hypoxia due to hemodynamic disorders [4], impaired lactate clearance [5], chronic kidney diseases [6], or impaired fasting glucose and diabetes [7]. However, AG calculations ignore changes in serum albumin [8, 9], which is the main negatively charged plasma protein and can decrease in certain conditions, obscuring the presence of unmeasured anions and underestimating the severity of acidosis. Patients with CHF often have low serum albumin levels [10], which may cause a false normal AG despite the presence of increased unmeasured anions. To correct this bias, AG can be adjusted for these anionic electrolytes [11]. Previous authors have used ACAG as a more accurate measure of AG that accounts for the effect of unmeasured anion corrected for albumin, which is the main contributor to the normal anion gap [12–15].

Previous studies have demonstrated that ACAG is a valuable prognostic indicator in critically ill patients with various conditions, such as acute myocardial infarction (MI) [12], sepsis [13] and cardiac arrest [14]. A recent study involving 205 patients with CHF reported a significant correlation between elevated ACAG levels and increased mortality [15]. Nevertheless, the available literature is very limited. The present study therefore aims to explore the association between ACAG and mortality in a larger number of critically ill patients with CHF. Since ACAG is a derived variable calculated through a formula, its changes may originate from variations in other biomarkers. The study also aims to further analyze the relationship between ACAG and related biomarkers that may contribute to an increased risk of CHF. The Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database [16], which provides a large-scale historical cohort of ICU patients based on real-world data, is employed to examine this issue.

Methods

Study design and data source

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using health data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV version 2.2 (MIMIC-IV v2.2), which is a free, public, large, single-center database obtained from the electronic health record of ICU at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) between 2008 and 2019. Our team was authorized to use the database after one of the authors Qi Li completed the National Institute of Health (NIH) web-based course and passed the Protecting Human Research Participants exam (certification number: 49268863).

The MIMIC-IV database received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Boards of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT IRB No. 0403000206) and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC IRB No. 2001-P-001699/14). All patient data were de-identified and anonymized [16].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria (study population)

The MIMIC-IV database includes data from 299,712 patients, 431,231 admission records, and 73,181 ICU admission records collected between 2008 and 2019 [16]. Our study population focuses on adult patients (age ≥ 18 years at the time of first admission) who were admitted to the ICU with a diagnosis of congestive heart failure, as defined by the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) code. Patients who met the ICD-9 codes for CHF of 4280-4289 or the ICD-10 codes of I50-I509 were enrolled in the study. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients who were not first-time admitted to the ICU for CHF (8,924 records); (2) Patients with a length of stay in the ICU of less than 24h (1,935 records); (3) Patients who were missing important laboratory tests on the first day of ICU admissions, such as lactate, albumin, or anion gap (7,330 records). Finally, a total of 2,689 patients were enrolled. The workflow of data extraction is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Workflow showing patient selection for this study

Data collection

PostgreSQL (version 15) and Navicat Premium (version 16.2.5) software were used to obtain raw data from the MIMIC-IV database. The extracted data includes demographics such as age, gender, and race, as well as weight, initial care unit, comorbidities, the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), the Acute Physiology Score III (APS III), the Glasgow Coma scale (GCS) score, systolic blood pressure (SBP), oxygen saturation (SpO2), red blood cells (RBC), white blood cells (WBC), platelet, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and other disease-related information. All patient characteristics were collected within the first 24 h of ICU admission.

Indicators with more than 20% missing values, such as Troponin T (21.8%), CK-MB (23.4%), height (35.6%), and NT-proBNP (51.1%), were excluded. For the indicators with less than 20% missing values, such as PT (2.1%), SBP (0.3%), and SpO2(0.3%), we performed the multiple imputation and included them in the analysis using the “mice” package of R software [17].

Definition and criteria of grouping and outcome measurement

Participants were stratified based on their ACAG levels, which were divided into four quartiles: Q1: <15.5 mmol/L, Q2: 15.5–18.0 mmol/L, Q3: 18.0–21.0 mmol/L, Q4: ≥21.0 mmol/L at their first-ICU-admission. The ACAG is defined as follows: ACAG (mmol/L) = AG (mmol/L) + [40 - Albumin (g/L)] * 0.25, using the Figge equation [18], where AG (mmol/L) = plasma sodium (mmol/L) + plasma potassium (mmol/L) - plasma chloride (mmol/L) - plasma bicarbonate (mmol/L).

The primary outcome of this study was the 28-day all-cause mortality, and the secondary outcome was the 1-year all-cause mortality after admission to the ICU. The number of days was calculated using the following equation: the date of death - the date of ICU admission + 1.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were described as median (Interquartile range [IQR]), the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for comparing groups with non-normal distribution. Categorical variables were presented as rates or proportions, and the Chi-square test was employed for group comparisons. Cox regression models were used to identify predictors of mortality, with hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) calculated for various group outcomes. The multivariate model included: Model 1: unadjusted; Model 2: adjusted for age, gender, weight, and race; Model 3: adjusted for age, gender, weight, race, initial care unit, APSIII, CCI, SBP, RBC, WBC, platelets, and creatinine. P values for trends were calculated using linear trend test by entering the median value of each ACAG group as a continuous variable in the models. To compare the predictive performance of ACAG versus conventional AG for mortality, we assessed the areas under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUCs) using DeLong’s test and calculated continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). As a sensitivity analysis, we performed 1:1 nearest-neighbor propensity score matching to balance baseline covariates. A restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression model with five knots was used to analyze the nonlinear association between ACAG and mortality. Post-hoc subgroup analysis was used to explore the effect of ACAG on 28-day mortality in different subgroups of CHF patients. Causal mediation analysis was performed using the “Mediation” package in R [19] to explore the potential role of ACAG as a mediator between baseline biomarkers and CHF mortality. Pearson correlation analysis was used to analyze the correlation between associated biomarkers and ACAG. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided P-value of < 0.05. The statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 4.3.1).

Results

In this study, a total of 2,689 patients with congestive heart failure were enrolled. Baseline characteristics of included and excluded patients were presented in Supplementary Material 3: Table 1. Among the included patients, 1,555 (57.83%) of them were men. The median age of included patients was 73.11 (IQR: 62.17–82.50) years. The median ACAG level was 17.88 (IQR: 15.50–20.75) mmol/L. Of the patients with CHF, 697 (25.9%) and 1,207 (44.9%) died within 28 days and 1 year, respectively (Table 1).

Clinical characteristics of patients

The baseline characteristics of enrolled patients were summarized in Table 1: we stratified the participants according to their ACAG at their first ICU admission. Compared to those in lowest quartile of ACAG, patients in the highest quartile of ACAG generally had higher severity of illness scores on admission, such as Acute Physiology Score III (APSIII). They also had a higher prevalence of comorbidities as the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) scores are higher. These patients also had higher levels of heart rate, respiratory rate, WBC, creatinine, glucose, BUN, lactate, phosphate, PT, as well as higher 28-day and 1-year mortality rates.

Association between ACAG and mortality

When treated as a continuous variable, ACAG was a significant risk factor for 28-day and 1-year mortality in the Cox proportional hazards model (Fully adjusted HR = 1.06, 95% CI: [1.04, 1.08], p < 0.001 and HR = 1.03, 95% CI: [1.01, 1.05], p < 0.001, respectively). When categorizing ACAG into four quartiles, the upper quartile of ACAG was significantly associated with an increased risk of 28-day mortality compared to the lowest quartile in the unadjusted models and remained significant after adjustment for potential confounders (fully adjusted model: HR = 2.18, 95% CI: [1.67, 2.84], p < 0.001). The results for 1-year mortality were similar, with patients in the highest quartile of ACAG having a 44% higher one-year risk of death than those in the lowest quartile of ACAG (Fully adjusted model HR = 1.44, 95% CI: [1.18, 1.74], p < 0.001). Moreover, there was a significant upward trend in both 28-day and 1-year mortality with increasing ACAG levels (both p for trend < 0.001) (Table 2). The restricted cubic spline curves demonstrated a linear increase in both 28-day and 1-year mortality with rising ACAG levels (nonlinear p = 0.550 and nonlinear p = 0.429, respectively) (Fig. 2). ACAG also outperformed AG in predicting both 28-day and 1-year mortality, as demonstrated by significantly better AUC, continuous NRI and IDI. (Additional File Fig. 1 and Additional File Table 2). Additional propensity-score matched analysis showed results consistent with Cox model: Q4 versus Q1 was associated with increased 28-day and 365-day mortality (HR = 2.22, 95% CI: [1.50, 3.29] and HR = 1.49, 95% CI: [1.10, 2.03], respectively) (Additional File Fig. 2 and Additional File Table 3).

Subgroup analysis

Further exploratory subgroup analysis indicated that 28-day and 1-year mortality increased in conjunction with elevated ACAG across various subgroups of CHF patients, regardless of age, gender, initial care unit, comorbidities of MI, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and diabetes. The adjusted HRs for each subgroup were shown in Fig. 3. No significant interactions were observed in either 28-day or 1-year mortality in most stratifications, with the exception of initial care unit, and MI (p for interaction < 0.001). Patients with CHF and elevated ACAG levels in the CCU or MI subgroups exhibited a significantly worse 28-day and 1-year mortality prognosis than those in the other subgroups. In addition, elevated ACAG levels were found to be significantly associated with increased 30-day and 1-year mortality in patients with CHF without atrial fibrillation (AF) (HR = 2.57, 95% CI: [1.85, 3.58], and HR = 1.48, 95% CI: [1.16, 1.88]) or receiving vasoactive drug treatment (HR = 2.46, 95% CI: [1.79, 3.39], and HR = 1.62, 95% CI: [1.27, 2.07]), both p < 0.001), but not in patients with concomitant AF or vasoactive drug administration.

ACAG in relation to associated biomarkers

A mediation analysis was conducted to explore whether ACAG mediates the relationship between baseline biomarkers showing significant intergroup differences and mortality among CHF patients. Specifically, the mediating effect of ACAG on 28-day and 1-year mortality in relation to WBC, RBC, BUN, creatinine, hemoglobin, platelets, glucose, lactate, and phosphate was examined (Fig. 4A). Among these potential biomarkers, ACAG was found to significantly mediated the positive associations between lactate, phosphate, and BUN with the risk of 28-day mortality in CHF patients. The mediation proportions were 44.0% (95% CI: [0.142, 1.005], p = 0.004) for lactate, 37.4% (95% CI: [0.165, 1.004], p = 0.008) for phosphate, and 16.5% (95% CI: [0.060, 0.717], p = 0.012) for BUN, after adjusting for confounding variables. However, no mediation effect of ACAG was observed in the association between all the aforementioned potential biomarkers and 1-year mortality (Table 3).

Further analysis was conducted to examine the impact of lactate, phosphate, and BUN on mortality in patients with CHF, as well as their correlation with ACAG. After adjusting for potential confounders, we found that elevated levels of lactate, phosphate, and BUN were significantly associated with increased risks of 28-day mortality, with HR of 1.35 for lactate, 1.25 for phosphate, and 1.52 for BUN (Table 3). Violin plots of these biomarkers across different quartiles of ACAG demonstrated statistically significant disparities in their distribution among different levels of ACAG (p < 0.001 for all comparisons) (Fig. 4B-D). Pearson correlation analysis demonstrated statistically significant positive correlations between all three biomarkers, with the highest Pearson correlation coefficient observed for lactate (r = 0.56, 95% CI: [0.53, 0.58], p < 0.001), followed by phosphate (r = 0.48, 95% CI: [0.45, 0.51], p < 0.001), and BUN (r = 0.41, 95% CI: [0.38, 0.44], p < 0.001) (Fig. 4E-G).

Discussion

In this retrospective study, elevated levels of ACAG were found to be associated with an increased risk of both 28-day and 1-year mortality among critically ill patients with CHF. Moreover, our findings indicate that ACAG significantly mediated the associations between several biomarkers, including lactate, phosphate and blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and 28-day mortality in CHF patients, but not 1-year mortality. This result not only confirms the potential of ACAG as an independent biomarker for predicting mortality risk in patients with CHF, but also reveals for the first time a significant mediating role of ACAG in relation to classical biomarkers. The findings underscore the importance of ACAG in the comprehensive assessment of short- and medium-term prognosis in CHF patients and reflect their metabolic status, providing clinicians with a convenient and integrative strategy to assess the acid-load state associated with CHF prognosis.

The ACAG is calculated using serum sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate, and albumin. Its clinical value derives from its ability to represent the total burden of unmeasured anions that cannot be directly assessed in routinely clinical practice, and these unmeasured anions are closely linked to the pathophysiology of CHF. As previously reported by Tang and colleagues, elevated SAG (serum AG) levels were significantly linked to higher 30- and 90-day all-cause mortality in patients with severe CHF. Our results were consistent with this finding [20]. They also found a U-shaped relationship between SAG and 30-day mortality, meaning that both lower and higher SAG were associated with increased mortality, and suggested that this may be influenced by undetected anions such as albumin. In our study, the result did not present a similar U-shaped association between ACAG and 28-day or 1-year mortality in CHF patients after correcting for AG using albumin (nonlinear p >0.05). Aydın et al. showed that elevated ACAG was associated with high mortality in 205 hospitalized CHF patients [15]. The study had a longer follow-up of about 6 years, but relatively small sample size. Our study utilized a larger sample size and obtained valuable short-term prognostic outcomes in the ICU, providing more immediate clinical indicator for critically ill patients.

Subgroup analysis in this study showed that patients admitted to the coronary care unit (CCU) with elevated ACAG levels had a higher mortality risk than those admitted to the neurointensive care unit (NICU) and trauma surgical intensive care unit (TSICU). Similarly, MI patients with higher ACAG levels also faced a higher risk of death compare to those without MI. This may be attributed to the fact that CCU patients and MI patients typically present with more severe cardiac conditions than those in other care units. Prior research reported a correlation between AG and unfavorable clinical outcomes of CCU patients [21]. Furthermore, elevated ACAG levels have been identified as a significant risk factor for 30-day mortality in patients with acute myocardial infarction [12]. Given that patients in CCU or after a heart attack often have more severe CHF, the results of our study suggested that ACAG may be a stronger predictor of death when heart function is severely impaired. Additionally, we also found that CHF patients without atrial fibrillation or not taking vasoactive agents had a significant risk of mortality associated with high ACAG levels. Previous studies have demonstrated that catheter ablation therapy, a common treatment for atrial fibrillation patients, may be beneficial to patients with CHF [22–24]. A high ACAG may reflect metabolic acidosis, which impairs vasopressor efficacy and promotes lactate production during vasoactive therapy [25, 26]. Clinicians often correct metabolic acidosis before initiating vasoactive therapy. So, absence of vasoactive agents may indicate more severe conditions, which likely explains the lower use of vasoactive agents in patients with high baseline ACAG, but further research is needed.

The results of our mediation analysis demonstrated that ACAG had the greatest mediating effect of lactate on 28-day mortality in CHF patients, followed by phosphate, and BUN. This suggests that ACAG may serve as an integrative marker reflecting the overall severity of metabolic disturbances and organ dysfunctions. However, these mediating effects were not observed in the 1-year mortality outcomes. One possible explanation was that 1-year mortality may be more influenced by chronic factors, whereas ACAG reflects acute metabolic acidosis, potentially resulting from lactate accumulation [27]. Lactate levels may vary with therapy, and the correlation between ACAG and lactate may also change accordingly. Further exploration of the underlying mechanisms is necessary.

Lactate may accumulate when there is insufficient oxygen available for cellular energy production, particularly in the heart [28]. Elevated lactate levels can indicate cardiac ischemia, hypoxia, or dysfunction, and are associated with poor prognosis in patients with cardiovascular diseases including MI and atherosclerosis [29]. Consistent with our analysis, the relationship between ACAG and lactate has been previously reported. Weapon et al. reported a strong correlation between lactate and AG in patients with septic shock, suggesting that they could be used to diagnose septic shock earlier [30]. In a chart review of ICU patients, Lakhmir et al. observed that ACAG can identify hyperlactatemia [31]. Our study revealed that ACAG mediates the relationship between lactate and mortality in CHF patients, and that CHF patients with hyperlactatemia (lactate levels >2 mmol/L) had a higher risk of experiencing 28-day mortality (HR = 1.35, 95% CI: [1.15, 1.58], p < 0.001). Similarly, Zymlinski et al. found that patients with CHF, but without peripheral hypoperfusion, and higher lactate levels on admission had a worse prognosis, with a 36% mortality rate at 1 year [32]. Uyar et al. discovered that patients with CHF and reduced ejection fraction had a 5.35 times higher risk of cardiovascular death and hospitalization for HF when their lactate levels were elevated [33]. Additionally, Gjesdal et al. reported that patients with MI complicated by CHF, but not cardiogenic shock, had a 28% higher short-term mortality rate with lactate levels above 2.5 mmol/L [34]. The role of lactate in the pathophysiology and prognosis of CHF patients is complex and not fully understood. Elevated lactate levels indicate cellular hypoxia and impaired metabolic efficiency, which may exacerbate cardiac dysfunction and lead to a poor prognosis in CHF patients [35].

Phosphate plays a crucial role in cellular function and energy metabolism, while BUN is a byproduct of protein metabolism, cleared by the kidneys. The levels of these biomarkers reflect renal filtration and excretory functions [36, 37]. Michael Ess et al. demonstrated that phosphate is independently associated with a 26% higher mortality rate in CHF, regardless of the presence of chronic kidney disease [38]. Duan et al. reported that the risk of death in CHF is 2.29 times higher in patients with elevated BUN levels compared to those with lower levels [39]. Our study found similar results in 28-day mortality of CHF patients, with hazard ratios of 1.25 (95% CI: [1.06, 1.48]) for phosphate and 1.52 (95% CI: [1.25, 1.86]) for BUN. Renal insufficiency is a common complication in CHF patients, contributing to higher mortality and hospitalization rates. It can lead to fluid retention, increasing the volume load on the heart, resulting in hyperphosphatemia and BUN accumulation. This condition is associated with cardiac hypertrophy, which exacerbates cardiac burden and impair cardiac contractility, thereby worsening CHF [40]. In cases of renal insufficiency, phosphate and BUN occupy a portion of the anion space, leading to an increase in ACAG. This study is the first to suggest that the effects of phosphate and BUN on CHF mortality were mediated by ACAG. Nevertheless, more research is required to confirm this finding.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design and single-center MIMIC-IV cohort may limit generalizability to other regions or countries. Second, our study lacked echocardiographic parameters (e.g., LVEF, diastolic function) reflecting ventricular structure and function. Relying on clinician-reported death as the primary outcome prevented the evaluation of dynamic interactions between ACAG and ventricular changes. Third, excluding patients without essential tests (anion gap, albumin, lactate) may introduce selection bias toward more severely ill individuals (higher APS III scores and higher mortality rates), as patients with more severe conditions are more likely to undergo comprehensive laboratory examinations, thereby increasing their likelihood of being included in the study. Furthermore, higher percentage of patients admitted to the MICU/SICU may introduce additional bias, since management protocols in these units can differ from those in the CCU. Finally, our cohort predates currently heart failure therapies (SGLT2-inhibitors, GLP-1 agonists), limiting the applicability of our findings. Prospective studies are needed to improve the generalizability of results across different regions, clinical outcomes, and therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

ACAG was a significant predictor for both 28-day and 1-year mortality in critically ill patients with CHF. The mediation effects of ACAG were observed in the associations between lactate, phosphate, and BUN with 28-day mortality in CHF patients. These findings are expected to assist clinicians in rapidly predicting outcomes and implementing more rational clinical treatment measures.
