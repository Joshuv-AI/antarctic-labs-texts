Culture breakthrough enables goose circovirus (GoCV) isolation and pathogenic gosling modeling to uncover the association between viral infection and vaccine failure
Authors: Zhanxin Yao, Wenchang Xue, Jialong Chen, Chao Wang, Yinsheng Lin, Jiehuang Wang, He Wang, Wanjun Zhu, Jipei Zhang, Jidang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGoose circovirus (GoCV), recognized as the most economically significant immunosuppressive pathogen in global waterfowl production, causes considerable economic losses annually, and multiprovincial surveillance studies have documented high seroprevalence rates. The critical barrier to combating GoCV has persisted in the complete absence of viable in vitro culture systems, fundamentally limiting pathogenesis studies and vaccine development. In a breakthrough advancement, this study pioneers the first successful establishment of a stable primary goose embryo kidney (GEK) cell culture system for GoCV propagation, achieving sustained viral replication through innovative culture protocol optimization. Comprehensive virological characterization via synchronized growth curve analysis, transmission electron microscopy, and IFA quantification not only confirms efficient viral proliferation but also resolves longstanding debates regarding the intracellular replication mechanisms of GoCV, termed GoCV/369/2020. Pathogenesis trials involving gosling with the novel isolated GoCV revealed unprecedented clinicopathological correlations, revealing severe growth retardation, feather disorders, persistent fecal shedding, and biphasic organ damage progression from acute hypertrophy to chronic atrophy. Most critically, we provide the first experimental evidence linking GoCV infection to catastrophic vaccine failure, showing reductions in the antibody titers of avian influenza virus and Newcastle disease virus. This breakthrough has led to the establishment of poultry industry solutions, such as standardized viral isolation for diagnostics, optimized vaccination schedules, and targeted biosecurity against fecal-oral transmission. This study established a stable in vitro culture system and a pathogenic gosling model for GoCV, elucidating its replication, pathogenesis, and vaccine interference, providing crucial insights for future research and control strategies.

Introduction

Goose Circovirus (GoCV), a member of the genus circovirus in the Circoviridae family, is a nonenveloped, spherical virus with a circular single-stranded DNA genome that is 1.8 kb in size [1]. GoCV has been identified as the pathogen responsible for goose circovirus disease (GoCVD), which was initially discovered in a large commercial goose flock in Germany [2]. The viral genome of GoCV was subsequently reported by Todd et al. in 2001 [3]. Since then, GoCVDs have spread widely and are frequently found in Europe [4–6]. In China, GoCVD was first noted by Chen et al. in Taiwan in 2003 [7]. The prevalence rates of GoCV in Zhejiang, Chongqing, Fujian, Shandong, and Guangxi reportedly range between 20.2% and 55.49% [8–10]. In our previous study, from December 2019 to August 2020, a total of 310 tissue samples from geese exhibiting growth retardation and feather disorder syndrome in 13 major goose breeding cities in Guangdong were collected. Among these, 52.9% of the samples subjected to qPCR tested positive for GoCV. Furthermore, 110 goose farms tested positive for GoCV, indicating a positive rate of 60.8% [11]. The above information indicates that GoCV has been widely spread within the goose population, with a relatively high positivity rate.

Commonly, goose circovirus (GoCV) infection may cause mild clinical signs, such as dysplasia, weight loss, feather growth disorders and slight histopathological changes, including mildly cloudy air sacs, liver and kidney hemorrhage, yellow staining, and osteoporosis [12]. According to the results of histopathological examination of GoCV-infected geese conducted by Smyth et al., a decrease in lymphocytes in the bursa was observed along with basophilic inclusions in the medullary and cortical follicular cells as well as in the cytoplasm of cystic epithelial cells. Previous studies reported that vacuolar lesions are present in the liver and that there is infiltration of inflammatory cells around the central vein of GoCV-infected geese [13]. In our previous study, statistics on the clinical symptoms of GoCVD in Guangdong revealed that 71.4% of GoCVD-infected geese experienced slow growth, weight loss and feather disorders. Infected geese are often accompanied by symptoms such as decreased egg production, panting and slow growth [11]. Moreover, compared with subtle clinical signs, immunosuppression followed by coinfection with multiple pathogens induced by GoCV infection is considered to be more detrimental to goose breeding. It has been reported that GoCVD is significantly correlated with various common infectious diseases caused by avian influenza virus, Tembusu virus, waterfowl reovirus, paramyxovirus, goose parvovirus, West Nile fever virus, goose hemorrhagic polyomavirus, Salmonella and Aspergillus in geese [14–19]. We reported that 31.1% of GoCV-positive samples were found to have two, three or four coinfections during an epidemiological survey conducted from 2019–2020 in Guangdong Province [11]. Accordingly, coinfection may increase the pathogenicity and complexity of goose epidemic diseases, thereby increasing the difficulty of preventing and controlling goose infectious diseases. However, it is important to note that despite these symptoms, GoCVD is associated with low morbidity and mortality, which can lead to challenges in terms of diagnosis during breeding processes due to a lack of methods for GoCV isolation and cultivation, resulting in an unclear understanding of the mechanisms of infection pathogenesis associated with GoCV infection.

While GoCV has been the subject of study for more than 20 years, effective vaccines and therapeutic measures against it are still lacking because of the absence of a cell culture system for proliferating the isolated virus in vitro and a reliable animal infection model. In this study, we address these challenges through two groundbreaking advancements: the first successful establishment of a stable primary goose embryo kidney (GEK) cell culture system for GoCV propagation, which enables sustained viral replication, and the development of a standardized gosling infection model using the GoCV/369/2020 strain. By integrating virological characterization, controlled pathogenesis trials, and vaccine immunization effective assessments, our research aims to systematically investigate the tropism of GoCV for immune organs (e.g. bursa of Fabricius, spleen, and thymus), its biphasic pathological progression from acute hypertrophy to chronic atrophy, and its role in dysregulating host immunity. Furthermore, we explored the direct correlation between GoCV infection and catastrophic reductions in avian influenza/Newcastle disease vaccine efficacy, a phenomenon previously hypothesized but never experimentally validated. This work provides valuable reference data for further understanding the pathogenic and immunosuppressive mechanisms of goose circovirus.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

The GoCV strain was isolated from pooled liver, kidney, spleen, thymus and bursa of Fabricius tissues of a 40-d-old Magang goose obtained from a commercial farm in Guangdong Province during our previously reported epidemiological survey in 2020 [11]. The suspected GoCV-positive Magang geese presented typical clinical symptoms, including dysplasia, weight loss and feather growth disorders. A gram of pooled tissue sample was collected from PCR-identified GoCV-positive Magang geese harboring multiple common goose viral pathogens, such as avian influenza virus (AIV), Newcastle disease virus (NDV), Fowl Adenovirus (Fadv), Tembusu virus (TMUV), goose plaque virus (GPV), duck plague virus (DPV) and mycoplasma (MG), and the primers used were identified (Supplementary Table S1). It was frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground into a powder. The ground tissue powder was subsequently mixed with Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing penicillin (200 U/ml) and streptomycin (200 μg/ml) at a ratio of 1:5. The mixture was then filtered through a .45-micron filter membrane manufactured by Sorfa Life Science Research Co., Ltd. China. Prior to storage at −80°C in aliquots of 500 μL/tube, the filtrate was confirmed for the absence of bacteria and mycoplasma contamination.

Preparation of cells and goose embryos

To select a platform suite for GoCV isolation and amplification in vitro, six passage cell lines, namely, DF-1 (chicken embryo fibroblasts), LMH (chicken liver cancer cells), BHK-21 (Baby Hamster Syrian kidney cells), PK-15 (porcine kidney cells), MDCK (dog kidney-transformed cells), and Vero (African green monkey kidney cells), and three primary cell lines, namely, GEF (goose embryo fibroblasts), DEF (duck embryo fibroblasts), and GEK (goose embryo kidney cells), along with embryonized goose eggs, were utilized. All of the cells mentioned above were cultured in a 37°C incubator with 5% CO2 for future use.

All of the aforementioned passage cell lines were previously stored in the laboratory, while all primary cells were predominantly prepared according to the methods briefly introduced below. Magang goose embryos aged 18 to 20 d or Muscovy duck embryos aged 14 d, obtained from two commercial farms in Zhaoqing and Foshan of Guandong Province, served as donors of GEFs, GEKs and DEFs, respectively. The breeders providing the embryos had been immunized with H5 and H7 subtypes of avian influenza inactivated vaccines one month prior to egg laying. The eggs were delivered to the laboratory and incubated at 37°C for two days before cell generation, with daily monitoring of embryo activity. To generate GEFs and DEFs, active embryo bodies were aseptically removed from the eggs and dissected by removing the heads, limbs, bones and all visceral organs before being cut into 1 mm3 cubes. The tissue was digested with 0.05% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, USA) at 37°C for 10–15 min. After digestion, the mixture was rinsed and centrifuged in PBS three times before the cells were seeded onto a 10 cm diameter plate for further growth into monolayer GEFs or DEFs. To generate GEKs, kidneys obtained from goose embryos were rinsed in PBS until transparent. The kidneys were subsequently trypsin-digested (0.05% trypsin-EDTA, Gibco, dispersed through a 70 µm sieve), repeatedly rinsed in PBS, and centrifuged at 1500 × g for 8 min before the seeding of 106 cells/mL density onto 10 cm diameter plates for incubation at 37°C and 5% CO2 until a monolayer formed. Under an inverted microscope, the GEK cells were predominantly fusiform (Figure 1(A)). All newly generated primary cells were confirmed to be negative for goose circovirus (GoCV), avian influenza virus (AIV), Newcastle disease virus (NDV), poultry adenovirus (Fadv), Tembusu virus (TMUV), goose plaque virus (GPV), duck plague virus (DPV) and mycoplasma (MG) by PCR (Supplementary Table S1). The cells were monitored every 12 h to assess their growth conditions and subcultured as needed until stable growth was achieved. Virus isolation, identification and serial passage

Before infection, the prepared cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) for 20 hours. Two hours prior to infection, the cells were starved by changing the medium without FBS. The previously prepared tissue sample filtrates stored at −80°C mentioned above were diluted five times and incubated with the cells at 500 µl/well for 2 h at 37°C and 5% CO2. The cells were subsequently rinsed with PBS three times and then filled with 2 ml/well of DMEM containing 2% FBS before being cultured in an incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2. After culturing for 96 h, the cell cultures were subjected to three rapid freeze‒thaw cycles and then centrifuged at 8000 × g at 4°C for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was collected and aliquoted into tubes at 200 µl/tube. The total DNA was extracted from the supernatant via a DNA extraction kit (Guangzhou Magen) and the extracted nucleic acid was subsequently used as a template for qPCR amplification with the SYBR Green method and virus-specific primers (Supplementary Table S1). The viral genome copy number was calculated on the basis of a standard curve generated on the (qTOWER3/G, Analytik Jena) real-time qPCR system using a serial dilution of a known-quantity plasmid standard. The standard curve formula was y = −3.4663x + 39.21 (R2 = 0.9996). Other common goose viral pathogens mentioned above, were also detected via PCR to exclude their presence (Supplementary Table S1). Following these methods, the PCR-identified positive GoCV nucleotide supernatant samples were serially undiluted and passed through 3 generations. The amount of GoCV nucleic acid in the supernatants of each passage was quantified via the same SYBR Green qPCR method and a standard curve as described above (Supplementary Table S1). qPCR results from the first three passages demonstrated that GoCV replicated more efficiently in goose embryo kidney (GEK) cells than in other cell types or embryos. No significant cytopathic effect (CPE) was detected in any of the cell cultures, and the inoculated goose embryos remained viable. To further confirm the effectiveness of serial propagation, the nucleotide-positive supernatant was passaged undiluted for two additional generations (up to passage 5) in GEK cells, and the viral nucleic acid levels were quantified via qPCR. Whole-genome sequencing confirmed that there were no mutations across all passages. The isolated goose circovirus strain was designated GoCV/369/GD/2020 (abbreviated as GD/369 for simplicity hereafter), with the complete viral genome deposited under GenBank accession number MT831925.

The viability of using embryonated goose eggs as a platform for culturing GoCV was also evaluated. Five 11-d-old Magang goose embryos with normal activity obtained from Hongteng Breeding Geese Specialized Cooperative, a commercial farm in Zhaoqing City, Guangdong Province, and incubated at 37°C in a laboratory for two days were randomly allocated, with three assigned to the infection group and two to the control group. The activity of the embryo bodies was observed prior to infection. For actual infection, 200 µl of the tissue sample suspension (quantified by qPCR) mentioned above was inoculated into the chorioallantoic cavity and then placed in a 37°C incubator for 7 d. The survival of the goose embryos was monitored daily, and those that died within 24 h were discarded. After 7 d of incubation, the allantoic fluid was harvested and stored at −80°C. Subsequently, 200 µl of the harvested allantoic fluid was subjected to total DNA extraction, and the amount of GD/369 in this mixture was quantified via the same qPCR method and standard curve used for virus identification.

Growth curves of GoCV in GEK cells

To evaluate the growth patterns of GD/369, 2 × 105 GEK cells/well were seeded in five 6-well plates, with 3 wells in each (for collecting the cells at five-time points) approximately 20 h prior to infection. After 2 h of starvation, the cells were washed twice with 1 ml/well PBS and then incubated with viral stock collected from the 5th passage of GD/369 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1 for 2 h. The wells were subsequently replenished with 2 ml/well of DMEM containing 2% FBS. The cells were cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2, and the GoCV was collected by rapid freeze‒thaw cycles three times at 0, 24, 48, 96, and 120 h post-infection (hpi). The supernatants of the cell cultures were collected after centrifugation at 8000 × g at 4°C for 10 min, followed by total DNA extraction. The amount of GD/369 viral DNA at each time point was quantified via the aforementioned qPCR assay and the same standard curve (Supplementary Table S1). The growth curve of the virus in GEK cells was plotted via Origin software.

Viral genome sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

The whole viral genome sequence of GD/369 was obtained from tissue samples and supernatants of the 1st and 5th passages through DNA extraction and segmented via PCR amplification via the primers “GoCV-F1/-R1” and “GoCV-F2/-R2.” The PCR-positive products, verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, were linked to the pMD18-T vector (Takara Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing) and transformed into DH5ɑ cells (Takara Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing). Five colonies of each virus gene segment were sent to Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., for complete genome sequencing on an ABI 3730XL automatic DNA analyzer (Applied Biosystems) with an ABI BigDye Terminator v3.1 cycle sequencing kit (Applied Biosystems). Comparisons of the nucleotide sequences of the newly obtained GoCV isolates were performed via the ClustalW method via MegAlign 7.0 software (DNASTAR Inc., USA). Initial phylogenetic analysis involved comparing sequences with the genomes of previously reported GoCV strains available in GenBank (NCBI). Phylogenetic trees were generated via the maximum likelihood method in MEGA 7.0 software (Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis, USA), with bootstrap values calculated on the basis of 1000 alignment replicates.

Indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA)

After the GEK cells seeded in the 6-well plates were infected with GD/369 at an MOI of 0.5 for 96 h, the culture medium was discarded, and the cells were washed three times with PBS for 5 min each. The cells treated with an equivalent volume of serum-free DMEM under identical conditions served as the negative control group. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 30 min and washed three times with PBS for 5 min each. The cells were subsequently incubated with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 min and then washed three more times with PBS. Next, the cells were blocked with 5% BSA at room temperature for 1 h before being incubated with a rabbit anti-GoCV Cap polyclonal antibody (generated by our laboratory) at a dilution of 1:100 for 1 h. Afterward, the cells were washed five times with 1% BSA for 5 min each. The cells were incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (AlexaFluor®488) at a 1000-fold dilution for 1 h in the dark. Afterward, the cells were washed with 1% BSA five times for 5 min each. Prior to observation and imaging via a fluorescence microscope (RVL-100-G, ECHO, USA), the cells were treated with DAPI dihydrochloride (DAPI) at a final concentration of 100 ng/ml for 3 min, followed by three washes with PBS buffer for 5 min each. Any remaining liquid was discarded, and the stained cells were then stored in 50% glycerol for future use.

Electron microscope observation

To prepare infected cell samples for electron microscopy, GEK cells infected with GD/369 were gently detached from a 10 cm diameter dish via a cell scraper. The dislodged cells were then collected into a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube containing 500 µl of PBS at 96 hpi. The tube was then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min, resulting in clumps of approximately 1 mm3 in size. The supernatant was discarded, and 1 mL of 2.5% glutaraldehyde fixative with a pH of 7.3 was added to the tube to fix the cells for at least 2 h at 4°C. Following fixation, the samples were rinsed in triplicate in PBS for 15 min each at 4°C, and the supernatant was discarded after each rinse. The samples were subsequently dehydrated by adding acetone at increasing concentrations (50%, 70% and 90%), and the supernatant was discarded before being fully dehydrated with 100% acetone at room temperature for 10 min twice. The resin was macerated overnight, followed by polymerization at high temperatures. Afterward, the resin was sliced to a thickness of 70 nm via a Lycra slicer. The resin was subsequently stained with dioxin acetate for 15 min, followed by lead citrate for 10 min. Finally, the resin was dried and examined via transmission electron microscopy (Hitachi H-7650). Additionally, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was conducted at the Harbin Veterinary Research Institute.

To prepare virions of GD/369 for electron microscopy, suspensions were obtained from three rounds of freezing‒thawing and centrifugation of five 10 cm diameter dishes of GD/369-infected GEK cell cultures at 8000 × g and 4°C for 10 min. Next, the suspensions were further ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g and 4°C for 4 h to concentrate the GoCV virions, which were then resuspended in 100 μl of PBS. The prepared sample was diluted to 10× and 1000× before being subjected to TEM. A droplet of the concentrated viral suspension was placed on a coated copper network, and the excess sample suspension was removed from the side of the copper mesh via filter paper. A negative staining solution was then added to the sample before it was dried. After the samples were dyed for 1–2 min, any excess dye solution was absorbed with filter paper, and the samples were washed with distilled water 1–2 times. Finally, moisture absorption by filter paper preceded the observation of the viral particles under electron microscopy after drying. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis was conducted at the Guangdong Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Hitachi HT7700) and Harbin Veterinary Research Institute (Hitachi H-7650).

Pathogenicity assessment of the isolated GoCV strains GoCV/369/GD/2020 in geese

Forty 7-d-old male white goslings without significant clinical signs and similar weights were obtained from a commercial farm in Zhaoqing, Guangdong. They were maintained in the laboratory for one week prior to infection. Before infection was conducted, PCR and ELISA confirmed that the geese were negative for GoCV, GPV, TMUV, REV, MG, Fadv, AIV and NDV nucleic acids, as well as serum antibodies. Afterward, the goslings were randomly divided into an infection group and a negative control group, with 20 goslings in each group. The infected and control groups were housed in separate rooms to prevent cross-infection of the control goslings, and all subsequent procedures were conducted in a randomized manner. Each gosling in the infection group was inoculated with GD/369 (the 5th generation GEK cell culture) via intramuscular injection, with 107 copies previously quantified via qPCR. The negative control group was inoculated with an equal volume of PBS instead. Daily photographs were taken to document feather growth and development in the goslings, and body weight changes were monitored and recorded from 0 to 49 dpi. At 0, 7, 14, 21, 28, 35 and 49 dpi, three goslings were randomly selected from each of the infection and negative control groups and euthanized. Tissue samples, including the brain, thymus, heart, lung, liver, spleen, pancreas, duodenum, kidney and bursa of Fabricius, were collected and photographed. The tissues from the infection group and control group were ground and mixed with DMEM containing penicillin (200 U/ml) and streptomycin (200 µg/ml) at a 1:2 ratio. The mixture was then thawed three times before being centrifuged at 8,000 × g at 4°C for 8 min. The supernatants were filtered through 0.45 nm filter membranes, and total DNA was extracted from the filtrates. The quantity of the viral genome was measured via qPCR with the primers “qGoCV-F/-R” (Supplementary Table S1) to assess the variation in virus distribution among 11 different organs, including the bursa of Fabricius, lung, liver, brain, skin, spleen, kidney, duodenum, heart, thymus, and pancreas.

Prior to each culling time point, throat and cloacal swabs were collected via sterilized cotton swabs soaked in PBS. The swabs were then placed into a tube containing 1 mL of PBS and thoroughly vibrated on a shaker for 5 min. Total nucleic acid was extracted from the vibrated swab suspension via a previously described method. Owing to the use of a separate qPCR instrument (QuantStudio™ 5, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for these samples, a distinct standard curve was established and applied for quantification. The extracted nucleic acid was used as a template for qPCR amplification via the SYBR dye method. The viral genome copy number was calculated on the basis of this instrument-specific standard curve, which was defined by the formula y = −3.1786x + 36.434 (R2 = 0.9954). Tissue samples collected from sacrificed geese at 0, 7, 14, 21, 28 and 35 dpi were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histopathological examination via hematoxylin‒eosin (HE) staining. Specifically, the bursa of Fabricius, the thymus, and the spleen obtained from geese at 35 dpi were also processed for in situ hybridization analysis. Briefly, the tissues were fixed in DEPC fixative for 8 h,dehydrated through an ethanol series, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned. The sections were incubated at 62°C for 2 h, dewaxed in xylene and absolute ethanol, and then rehydrated in DEPC watervia a graded alcohol series. For fixation, the sections were subjected to antigen retrieval, followed by proteinase K digestion (20 μg/ml, 37°C, 20–30 min) within hydrophobic pen circles and washingwith PBS. Endogenous peroxidases were blocked at room temperature (RT) for 15 min (3% methanol-H2O2, dark) and then washed. Prehybridization was performed at 37°C for an hour, and probe hybridization was performed at 37°C overnight. Probes (Supplementary Table S1) conjugated with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB) were designed and synthesized by Wuhan Xavier Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Posthybridization washes were performed with 2×Saline Sodium Citrate (SSC) once at 37°C for 10 min, 1×SSC twice at 37°C for 5 min each, and 0.5×SSC once at RT for 10 min. Sections were blocked with rabbit serum for 30 min at RT, followed by incubation with anti-DIG-HRP. DAB development was visually monitored and stopped. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin, differentiated with 1% acid alcohol, blued with ammonia water, dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted. Positive signals were brownish-yellow, and the nuclei were blue. The remaining tissue samples were stored at −80°C for future use. Notably, the sample size per group (n = 3), although common in longitudinal challenge studies due to practical constraints, is a limitation of this study, and the results should be interpreted as indicating a strong effect warranting further investigationwith larger cohorts.

Titration of antibodies against the GoCV capsid protein

An enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was previously established to evaluate the antibody titer against the GoCV capsid protein (data published) [20]. Serum samples collected from both the infection and negative control groups at 0, 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, and 49 dpi were diluted 400 times for ELISA evaluation. Subsequently, 1 µl of each serum sample was added to wells containing base solution and incubated at room temperature for 15 min before being washed three times with PBS. Following this step, HRP-labeled goat anti-Duck IgG (H+L) antibodies from KPL USA were added at a dilution of 1000-fold and incubated at room temperature for another 15 min before being washed three times with PBS. The color development was then initiated via TMB from Solarbio China, and the reaction was stopped via an ELISA stop solution from Solarbio China. Finally, the absorbance of each well at OD450 nm was measured via a microplate reader. The cutoff value of the ELISA test was determined by testing 13 GoCV-negative serum samples (verified by western blotting and PCR) under optimized iELISA conditions according to the following formula: cutoff value = mean OD450 value +3 standard deviations.

Impact of goose circovirus infection on the immunization efficacy of avian influenza and Newcastle disease vaccines

Nine 14-day-old, GoCV-negative male white goslings were randomly assigned to three equal groups. Group A received 0.6 ml bivalent inactivated vaccine (H9 AIV and La Sota NDV) subcutaneously in the neck and 1 ml sterile PBS intramuscularly simultaneously; Group B received the vaccine dose and route identical to those of Group A and 1 ml (107 copies/ml) GD/369 (4th passage) intramuscularly; Group C received 1 ml sterile PBS intramuscularly alone. Serum was collected from each gosling before vaccination and every 7 days for 70 days to measure hemagglutination inhibition (HI) titers against AIV (H9N2 A/Goose/Guangdong/A11/2016, KY794639) and NDV (La Sota), and geometric mean titers (GMTs) were calculated. Infected and control birds were housed separately to prevent cross-contamination, and all procedures were randomized.

Statistical analysis

Gene expression was analyzed via the 2−∆∆Ct method and normalized to the endogenous reference gene GAPDH. The results are presented as the fold change relative to that of GAPDH and were log-transformed for analysis. Statistical significance was determined via Student’ t-tests in SPSS (SPSS, Armonk, NY, USA), with significance thresholds set at p < 0.05 (*) and p < 0.01 (**). Owing to the exploratory nature of this study and practical constraints inherent in animal challenge trials, no formal power analysis was performed. For multitime point comparisons, Student’s t-test was applied at each time point without adjustment for multiple comparisons.

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the Committee on Animal Ethics of Foshan University (Permission number: FOSU2021194501), and all experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health of the USA. Specifically, goslings were euthanized after anesthesia via the intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital at a dosage of 150 mg/kg for the animal experiments. In cases where goslings displayed symptoms, they were anesthetized with a low dose of sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg) before dissection was performed.

Results

Sample information and viral genetic evolution analysis

The organs used for virus isolation were obtained from a 40-d-old female Magang goose raised on a farm, which reported slow growth, weight loss, and feather disorders, with some cases of egg loss, coughing, panting, and depression. After euthanasia under anesthesia, the sampled geese were dissected and showed slight liver, kidney or spleen enlargement; liver and kidney atrophy; yellow-pale liver and hemorrhage; and urate deposition, whereas some other breeding birds at the farm presented with ovarian hemorrhage and follicular malformation. The clinical signs and organ lesions at necropsy mentioned above were previously published [11]. Necropsies were performed on the affected geese, and kidney, liver, spleen, and bursa of Fabricius samples were collected and homogenized. Total DNA was extracted from the tissue samples and subjected to PCR with GoCV-specific primers, confirming the presence of the GoCV viral genome. Subsequent segmental amplification and sequencing allowed the determination of the complete viral genome sequence of GoCV, which was identified via BLAST analysis as GoCV. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis revealed that the viral nucleic acid content in the tissue samples was 108 copies/ml, indicating that the sample had a high viral concentration and that it was an optimal sample for further virus isolation.

The complete GoCV viral genome obtained from this tissue sample mixture was submitted to GenBank under accession number MT831925. In our previous phylogenetic analysis, the known GoCV genomes were categorized into three clades, I, II, and III, with clade I further divided into subtypes Ia and Ib. The GoCV genome obtained in this study was classified as subtype Ia (Figure 1(B)), similar to the predominant strains currently circulating in China. These findings suggest that the virus present in these tissue samples is representative of the main circulating strain in the region.

Characteristic estimation of novel GoCV isolation

After the GoCV strain GoCV/369/GD/2020 was obtained, we constructed a one-step growth curve of GD/369 by quantifying the viral nucleic acid levels at multiple time points during the proliferation of the P5 strain in GEK cells to understand its growth characteristics. The results revealed that the viral nucleic acid level of GD/369 reached 105.7 copies/ml at 24 hpi in GEK cells, peaked at 106.5 copies/ml at 96 hpi, and decreased to 106.1 copies/ml at 120 hpi. This finding indicates that the viral load of GD/369 reaches its maximum between 72 and 96 hpi in GEK cells (Figure 1(C)).

To study the morphology of GoCV, we collected the supernatants of GD/369-infected GEK cells that had been rethawed and lysed them at 96 hpi. We then concentrated the particles through ultracentrifugation and observed them via transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Figure 1(D)). Under TEM, the GoCV/369/GD/2020 (1000x dilution of the ultracentrifuged sample) viral particles appeared as small, spherical bodies with dense edges measuring approximately 20 nm in diameter (Figure 1(D), middle). The virus particles were observed to be arranged in a dense lattice-like pattern when we observed the sample as having a greater density of GoCV particles (Figure 1(D), left). Furthermore, fixed and stained sections of GEK cell clusters collected at 96 hpi revealed inclusion bodies containing viral particles within the cytoplasm. The virus particles within the inclusion bodies also exhibited a dense lattice-like arrangement (Figure 1(D), right).

An immunofluorescence assay (IFA) was employed to confirm further the proliferation of GoCV in GEK cells at 96 hpi (Figure 1(E)). Intense green fluorescence was observed within the cytoplasm of the infected cell population, whereas no green fluorescence was detected in the negative control group. These findings suggest that virus particles predominantly localize within the cytoplasm of GEK cells.

Pathogenicity assessment of the isolated GoCV strains GoCV/369/GD/2020 in geese

To assess the pathogenicity of GoCV infection in goslings, 14-d-old white geese were inoculated i.m. with the GD/369 strain (5th-generation GEK cell culture). Clinical symptoms were continuously monitored and recorded until 49 dpi. The results revealed that the body weight of the infected group was significantly lower than that of the control group at 7 dpi (p < 0.05) (Figure 1(F)). Furthermore, the weight difference between the two groups continued to increase over time. Between 10 dpi and 28 dpi, the average body weight of the infected group remained significantly lower than that of the control group (p < 0.01), indicating a significant impact of GoCV on the growth of goslings.

Research has indicated that GoCV infection is associated with feather development disorders. In this study, feather development in the wings, head and neck, abdomen, and back was compared between the infected and control groups at 1, 7, 14, 21, 28, 35 and 49 dpi. The findings revealed no significant disparity in wing feather development between the infected and control groups prior to 7 dpi (Figure 2). However, at 14 dpi, there was a noticeable delay in feather growth within the infected group as opposed to the control group. Specifically, at this time point, the control group had already begun developing primary, secondary and tertiary flight feathers with relatively robust and lengthy primary shafts, whereas the infected group had only managed to produce primary flight feathers characterized by thinner and softer shafts that were notably shorter in length and prone to detachment. The control group presented well-developed feather barbs at the ends of the flight feather shafts, resulting in small feather blades at 21 dpi. Tissue lesions

Tissue samples, including brain, thymus, heart, lung, liver, spleen, pancreas, duodenum, kidney and bursa of Fabricius samples, were collected at 0, 7, 14, 21, 28, 35 and 49 dpi for lesion observation, viral distribution detection and in situ hybridization analysis. No animals died from infection during the entire duration of the animal study.

For pathological comparison, compared with the control group, the infected group presented organ lesions characterized primarily by swelling and hemorrhage (Figure 4). From 0 to 21 dpi, the organs in the infected group were enlarged and larger than those in the control group. However, from 28 to 49 dpi, the organs in the infected group presented signs of poor development and atrophy and were smaller than those in the control group. The bursa of Fabricius in the infected group at 7–21 dpi was significantly swollen. Punctate hemorrhage was observed in the infected group at 7 dpi, whereas severe hemorrhage was observed in the infected group at 21 dpi. Atrophy was noted on the bursae of Fabricius in the infected group at 28 dpi, with a significantly smaller volume than that in the control group. Partial atrophy was also observed in the infected group at 49 dpi (Figure 4). Throughout the period from 7 to 49 dpi, the thymus of the infected group exhibited significant swelling and hemorrhage, with some lobes showing atrophy at both 14 dpi and 21 dpi (Figure 4). The spleens of the infected group were enlarged at 7 and 14 dpi and were larger than those of the control group. However, it showed atrophy from 21 to 49 dpi and was smaller than that in the control group (Figure 4). The kidneys were noticeably swollen from 7 to 21 dpi, and from 28 to 49 dpi, the kidneys of the infected group were atrophied and smaller than those of the control group (Figure 4). Additionally, at 7 to 21 dpi, the liver edges of the infected group were blunt and rounded, indicating liver enlargement compared with those of the control group. Yellowing and hemorrhage were observed at 14 dpi. At 28 dpi, the liver of the infected group was found to be atrophied and smaller than that of the control group (Figure 4). Virus distributions

qPCR was conducted to measure the GoCV viral load in various tissues, including the bursa of Fabricius, lung, liver, brain, skin, spleen, kidney, duodenum, heart, thymus, and pancreas (Figure 7). The results revealed that all the organs in the control group tested negative for GoCV nucleic acid throughout the experiment. Conversely, positive samples of GoCV nucleic acid were detected in all the organs of the infected geese, indicating the broad tissue tropism of GoCV. The viral load at 7 dpi was relatively low and remained similar to that at 14 dpi before significantly increasing by 21 dpi. A decrease in viral load was observed across all the tissues by 28 dpi; however, by 35 dpi, there was a significant increase again, with peak levels reached at 49 dpi. Among the various organs studied post-infection, the bursae of Fabricius presented the highest viral load, followed by the thymus and spleen, whereas other organs presented relatively lower viral loads. These findings suggest a pattern of changes in the viral load at different time points post-infection: initial low levels during early infection stages, fluctuating virus proliferation during mid-stage infection, and peaking at 49 dpi. Notably, immune organs such as the bursa of Fabricius, the thymus and the spleen presented the highest virus loads following infection, indicating preferential invasion of these immune organs by goose circovirus. Detection of Cap antibody levels after GoCV infection

Serum samples were collected at 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, and 49 dpi, and the levels of GoCV Cap antibodies were detected via ELISA (Figure 8(C)). The results indicated that all geese in the negative control group tested negative for GoCV Cap protein antibodies throughout the experiment. The antibody levels subsequently continued to rise, reaching their peak at 35 dpi before plateauing at this level.

Study of the interference of vaccine immunity during GoCV infection

Goslings received the GD/369 together with the AIV+NDV bivalent inactivated vaccine at 14 days of age, and the effect of GoCV on vaccine-induced immunity was evaluated via HI assays against AIV (H9N2 A/Goose/Guangdong/A11/2016, KY794639) and NDV (La Sota) (Figure 8(D,E)). No HI antibodies against either virus were detected in group C (neither vaccinated nor GoCV-infected). From 14 dpim onward, group B (concurrently vaccinated and GoCV-infected) presented lower HI titer for both AIV and NDV than did group A (vaccinated but not infected). By 35 dpim, the AIV HI titer in group B was significantly lower than that in group A, and at 42 dpim the NDV HI titer in group B was likewise significantly lower than that in group A. Throughout the follow-up, all the GoCV-infected birds in group B maintained consistently lower antibody levels than their uninfected counterparts in group A did, demonstrating that GoCV infection suppressed the immune response to both vaccines and prevented the attainment of peak immunity.

Discussions

The successful isolation and in vitro culture of GoCV from primary goose embryonic kidney (GEK) cells represent critical advances in the study of its pathogenesis, host interactions, and impact on the goose industry. On the basis of host specificity and genetic evolutionary similarity (33–40%) to PCV, multiple culture systems – including poultry cell lines (DF-1, LMH), primary waterfowl embryo cells (GEFs, DEFs), and kidney cells from various species (PK-15, BHK21, MDCK, Vero, and GEK) – were evaluated (Figure 1(A)), and propagation in goose embryos was also examined. Electron microscopy revealed thatviral particles in GEK cells resembled porcine circovirus, and replication was confirmed via growth kinetics and IFA assays, demonstrating successful propagation through five consecutive passages. While currently limited to primary cultures requiring fresh embryos, the GEK system offers a reproducible platform for isolation, propagation, and diagnostics via standard techniques and accessible SPF or healthy embryos. Future work will focus on developing an immortalized GEK cell line to support sustainable, standardized research in vaccine development and antiviral testing.

Since the discovery of GoCV by Soike in 1999 in a group of stunted geese in Germany, this virus has become widespread in geese around the world [2]. However, research on GoCV infection has focused primarily on natural cases, and there are relatively few reports on the pathogenicity of GoCV following experimental infection. Owing to the challenges associated with isolating and culturing GoCV, information about the pathogenicity of specific isolates is limited. In this study, we tested the pathogenicity of the laboratory-isolated strain GoCV/369/2020 on goslings. We recorded changes in body weight, mental state, and feather development at different time points after infection, as well as observed tissue and organ lesions. Histopathological examination and virus distribution analysis were performed on relevant tissues.

Additionally, viral shedding, cap antibody level detection, and evaluation of the immune effects of avian influenza and Newcastle disease vaccines following GoCV infection were conducted to systematically understand the pathogenicity and influence of GoCV on its host. The experimental results indicated that geese infected with GoCV presented clinical symptoms, including growth retardation and feather dysplasia. In contrast to Guo et al., who reported feather lesions in individual geese at 28 dpi [12], we found that the feather development of infected geese significantly lagged behind that of control geese at 14 dpi, persisting until the conclusion of the 49 dpi experiment. The changes in body weight mirrored the natural infection cases reported by Soike et al. [2]. Starting at 7 dpi, the average body weight of the infected geese was notably lower than that of the control geese. As the infection time increased, the disparity in body weight gradually increased to 49 dpi, resulting in an average weight loss of approximately 0.41 kg for infected geese, potentially leading to significant economic losses in the goose breeding industry.

The necropsy of geese infected with GoCV revealed that, compared with those of the control geese, the organs of the 0 to 21 dpi infected geese were enlarged, whereas those of the 28 to 49 dpi infected geese were atrophic. Histopathological analyses revealed biphasic organ damage: acute lymphoid hyperplasia and hemorrhage (7 to 21 dpi) followed by chronic atrophy (28 to 49 dpi), accompanied by lymphocyte necrosis, interstitial edema, and corticomedullary disorganization. Additionally, histopathological examination after HE staining and in situ hybridization analyses demonstrated that GoCV predominantly infects the lymphatic tissue of geese. In geese infected with GoCV from 7 to 35 dpi, significant pathological damage was observed in the bursa of Fabricius, thymus, spleen, and skin. Lesions in lymphoid tissues are characterized by lymphocytopenia, necrosis, and widening of the tissue space [21]. In the skin, hair follicle cell necrosis and inflammatory cell infiltration were observed. These structural disruptions are directly correlated with functional immunosuppression [22]. It is speculated that in the early stage of virus infection, the host immune system quickly responds, and immune cells proliferate and activate to combat the invading virus,which may result in the enlargement of immune organs, such as the lymph nodes and spleen, as well as the accumulation of inflammatory cells in infected tissues, leading to local tissue enlargement and inflammation. In the later stages of viral infection, persistent infection and tissue damage may lead to the destruction of tissue cells without regeneration, resulting in tissue and organ atrophy [23–25]. However, the detailed reasons for this phenomenon require further study and discussion.

These findings aligned with the lesion observations, histopathology, and in situ hybridization results, revealing that GoCV infected all the tested tissues but presented the highest viral load in the bursa of Fabricius, thymus, and spleen, indicating that immune organs are primary targets for viral invasion. By infecting these organs – which are critical for producing and regulating immune cells – GoCV likely disrupts host immune function to facilitate its transmission and survival [26,27], suggesting a strategy to evade or suppress immune responses. Moreover, the specific environment of the immune system may support persistent viral survival and replication [28–30], enabling GoCV to incubate long-term in immune organs and establish chronic infection. The observed suppression of vaccine-induced HI antibody titers against NDV and AIV further indicates that GoCV-mediated immunosuppression compromises humoral immunity. While the precise mechanisms require further elucidation, profound lymphocytolysis, necrosis, and architectural disruption in primary (bursa, thymus) and secondary (spleen) lymphoid organs – as evidenced by histopathology and ISH – likely play a central role. This damage may impair antigen presentation by dendritic cells or macrophages and disrupt lymphocyte maturation and function in the bursa and thymus, particularly in the context of follicular atrophy, which is indicative of B-cell dysfunction. Like porcine circovirus type 2 (PCV2) infection, where lymphoid depletion leads to impaired immunity and coinfections [31,32], the systemic detection of GoCV DNA in multiple tissues may induce immune modulation or exhaustion. Future studies should focus on lymphocyte subpopulations, cytokine profiles, and functional assays postvaccination under GoCV infection to dissect these mechanisms in detail.

GoCV-induced immunosuppression enables viral evasion of host immune clearance, facilitating viral replication and the proliferation of coinfecting pathogens while concurrently impairing the host immune response to vaccination. To investigate this phenomenon, geese were coadministered a dual inactivated vaccine (Newcastle disease virus and H9 avian influenza virus) and artificially infected with GoCV. HI testing revealed that geese infected with GoCV presented consistently lower HI antibody levels against both vaccines than uninfected controls did by 14 dpi, which aligns with prior reports of circovirus-mediated immunosuppression. Notably, antibody titers against avian influenza and Newcastle disease were significantly lower (p < 0.01) at 35 and 42 dpi, respectively, and failed to reach the peak levels observed in the controls. These findings demonstrate that GoCV infection suppresses both humoral and cellular immunity, critically undermining vaccine efficacy during key developmental stages. These findings provide the first experimental evidence linking GoCV to vaccine failure, highlighting its role in rendering routine vaccination protocols ineffective while increasing the risk of secondary infection and reducing breeding efficiency.

Although the high prevalence of GoCV in goose farms is well documented, its transmission mechanisms remain incompletely understood; viral detection in cloacal swabs specifically at 35 and 49 dpi suggests a fecal‒oral transmission route, informing improved biosecurity protocols. The delayed anal shedding – despite early high viral loads in organs – may be linked to biphasic immune organ pathology, where initial intracellular replication limits luminal release, whereas later atrophy and cell disruption facilitate viral entry into the intestinal lumen. Additionally, rising antibodies after 14 dpi may form complexes that initially suppress free virus but later promote fecal shedding via biliary excretion. All infected geese seroconverted by 14 dpi, with antibodies rising until 35 dpi and persisting throughout the study, coinciding with progressive lymphoid depletion and indicating incomplete viral clearance. A previous serosurvey in Guangdong revealed age-dependent seropositivity, reaching 96.24% in geese over 100 days old; however, further studies are needed to clarify antibody persistence and whether high seroprevalence stems from single or recurrent infections.

In summary, this study redefines the research paradigm of GoCV by establishing innovative in vitro and in vivo models. Comprehensive analyses demonstrated that GoCV exhibits distinct tropism for immune organs, disrupting immune function through biphasic pathological damage, ultimately leading to vaccine failure and persistent viral transmission. Research should further elucidate the molecular mechanisms of pathogenesis and develop effective vaccines and preventive control measures. Goose farms must enhance monitoring and management to curb the spread of GoCV promptly. These findings not only unravel the immunosuppressive axis of the GoCV but also provide a framework for evidence-based biosecurity measures, thereby enhancing the resilience of global waterfowl industries against the GoCV.
