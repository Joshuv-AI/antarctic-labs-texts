Eosinophilic pleural effusion in an individual in a high tuberculosis-burden country
Authors: Amitesh Krishna Srinivasan, Akshay Kawedia, Nipun Malhotra, Saurabh Mittal, Anant Mohan, Karan Madan, Vijay Hadda, Pawan Tiwari, Tejas Suri, Irfan Shekh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Shareable abstractAn interactive case report discussing a case of eosinophilic pleural effusion https://bit.ly/4eTX8QP

A 29-year-old previously healthy Indian man, without any comorbid illnesses, presented with a 2-month history of a left-sided chest pain that worsened with deep inhalation. He also had dry cough and a low grade intermittent fever. He denied having breathlessness, haemoptysis, joint pains or skin rashes. He had weight loss of 3 kg over the duration of his illness. There was no past or close contact history for tuberculosis. He had travelled to a Southeast Asian country about 2 months before the onset of symptoms. However, he followed a vegetarian diet and denied having had seafood or other non-vegetarian foods. He had snorkelled for 20 min while on the trip.

The patient had a weight of 65 kg and a body mass index of 23.9 kg·m−2. A physical examination revealed normal temperature (36.8°C), pulse rate (80 per min), respiratory rate (16 breaths per min), blood pressure (116/80 mmHg) and oxygen saturation (98% on room air). He had an immunisation scar, likely to be from bacille Calmette–Guérin vaccination, on his left arm. No pallor, cyanosis, clubbing, peripheral lymphadenopathy or limb swelling were noted. His cardiac, neurological, abdominal, skin and orthopaedic examination were normal. There was no tracheal shift and the apex beat was in the fifth intercostal space, medial to the midclavicular line. The respiratory movements were reduced in the left inframammary, infraaxillary and infrascapular areas, and a stony dull note was present on percussion. The breath sounds were vesicular but their intensity was reduced in these areas.

Blood investigations revealed a haemoglobin level of 119 g·L−1 (table 1). The total leukocyte count was 8.3×109 cells·L−1, lymphocyte count 3.2×109 cells·L−1, neutrophil count 4×109 cells·L−1 and eosinophil count 1.2×109 cells·L−1. His liver and kidney function tests were normal. His stool examination was negative for parasites.

Task 1

What thoracic pathology is suggested by the clinical examination findings and which imaging studies would be most useful to evaluate for it?

Answer 1

Left-sided moderate quantity pleural effusion without a contralateral mediastinal shift. A dull percussion note and reduced intensity breath sounds may be seen in either pleural effusion or atelectasis. A small–moderate sized effusion, such as that seen in this individual, is often not associated with a contralateral mediastinal shift. To confirm the pathology, a chest radiograph and/or sonography of the chest would be the next step. The minimum quantity of pleural fluid that can be noted on a chest radiograph is 200 mL on a posteroanterior view, 50 mL on a lateral view and 10–20 mL on a lateral decubitus view (with the ipsilateral side down). A chest radiograph can also clearly delineate pulmonary parenchymal involvement, if present, and the extent of it. On chest sonography, as little as 3–5 mL of fluid can be detected, with the additional advantage of a simultaneous pleurocentesis and avoidance of ionising radiation.

Task 2

What are the causes of cough with peripheral eosinophilia in tropical countries?

Answer 2

The potential aetiology includes:

1) Löffler syndrome or simple pulmonary eosinophilia. The commonly implicated parasites are Ascaris, Ancylostoma, Necator, Strongyloides and Toxocara. Parasitic pleural effusions or consolidations in hydatid disease or paragonimiasis can also lead to this symptomatology.

2) Tropical pulmonary eosinophilia is frequently encountered in the tropics.

3) Eosinophilic illnesses that do not have a tropical predilection, which include:

• allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis, bronchial asthma with fungal sensitisation and allergic fungal sinusitis;

• eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA);

• lymphomas and acute myeloid leukaemia (AML); and

• acute eosinophilic pneumonia (AEP).

A chest radiograph revealed a radiopaque density with a meniscus in the left lower zone (figure 1a). Ultrasonography showed an anechoic fluid collection in the left pleural space with a maximum separation of 5 cm between the chest wall and the lung, which confirmed the pleural effusion (figure 1b) and a thoracocentesis was performed. Around 25 mL of straw-coloured fluid was drawn. The fluid analysis revealed: protein 48 g·L−1, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) 259 U·L−1, glucose 5.4 mmol·L−1 and adenosine deaminase (ADA) 11 U·L−1. Cytological examination revealed a total leukocyte count of 0.16×109 cells·L−1 (60% lymphocytes, 20% eosinophils and 20% neutrophils) (table 2). There were reactive mesothelial cells without atypical cells, ova or cysts. The fluid Gram stain and culture, fungal culture, Ziehl–Neelsen stain, and nucleic acid amplification for Mycobacterium tuberculosis were negative.

Task 3

What are the potential causes of this type of pleural effusion in the presented case?

Answer 3

This effusion is an exudate with eosinophilia, normal sugar, slightly elevated LDH, normal range ADA and negative results from routine microbial screening. This type of effusion is commonly found with hydatid disease, paragonimiasis, strongyloidiasis, toxocariasis, EGPA, and AEP. Lymphomas and, rarely, AML, can also lead to eosinophilic effusions; however, in these cases the LDH levels are typically high. Tubercular effusions may less frequently be eosinophilic, but have a high ADA level. Pulmonary embolism has an acute presentation and is unlikely in this patient. A traumatic tap can also be associated with eosinophils in the pleural fluid, as can mixing of air with the pleural fluid.

HIV testing was negative. Peripheral blood antibody workup was negative for antineutrophil cytoplasmic (ANCA), antinuclear, antifilarial and Aspergillus-specific antibodies. However, Paragonimus westermani IgG was detected by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Subsequently, the pleural fluid was also evaluated for parasitic serology and found to be positive for Paragonimus-IgG (by ELISA).

Task 4

What is the clinical diagnosis in this patient and its mode of transmission?

Answer 4

This patient has a Paragonimus-related left-sided pleural effusion. The individual has a history of chronic unilateral chest pain, cough and fever. An eosinophilic exudative pleural effusion with a low ADA, along with the history of travel to a Southeast Asian country, points to a potential tropical parasitic as cause. Elevated Paragonimus-IgG in blood and pleural fluid on a parasitic serology panel confirms the diagnosis. The absence of elevated blood ANCA and atypical cells in the pleural fluid also rule out important differential diagnoses. Paragonimiasis is acquired from consuming raw or undercooked fresh-water crabs or crayfish. In an individual without a history of consuming these foods, eating food from contaminated surfaces (e.g. utensils) or due to contamination of the hands of the food-handler are thought to be the modes of transmission. Swimming or snorkelling in the sea or fresh-water does not transmit the disease.

Treatment

The individual was treated with oral praziquantel (1800 mg, three times a day, for 3 days) and ibuprofen was given for analgesia. However, the chest pain persisted through the first week. Another course of praziquantel was given at 4 weeks but the chest pain, cough and fever persisted. A subsequent radiograph revealed persistent left-sided pleural effusion. A computed tomography was performed and the presence of a pleural effusion was confirmed (figure 2a, b).

Task 5

What would be the next steps in the management of the praziquantel-refractory pleural effusion in this case?

Answer 5

Oral triclabendazole is an alternative medication that can be used in praziquantel-refractory cases of paragonimiasis. In nonresolving or significantly symptomatic pleural effusions, intercostal drainage is required. Additionally, a pleural biopsy should be considered for ruling out differential diagnoses that are relevant to an individual's case. In the present case, a chronic pleural effusion with fever in an individual living in a high tuberculosis-burden country, led to a coexisting tubercular effusion as a key differential.

A medical thoracoscopy was performed in view of a nonresolving pleural effusion. Multiple white plaque-like lesions were noted over the parietal pleura with nodularity of the visceral pleura and thin adhesions (figure 2c). Histopathological examination of the pleural biopsy tissue demonstrated chronic fibrinous pleuritis without any parasite. Around 300 mL of straw-coloured fluid was drained during the procedure. Subsequently, an intercostal drain was kept in place and removed on the second day. Oral triclabendazole was given (one dose of 10 mg·kg−1).

Follow-up and outcome

There was symptomatic improvement within the first week of drainage and triclabendazole treatment, with complete resolution within 3 weeks. A 6-month follow-up radiograph revealed minimal pleural thickening and no pleural effusion (figure 3). Ultrasonography confirmed the finding.

Discussion

Paragonimus is a parasitic lung fluke (trematode). Paragonimiasis is acquired after ingesting raw or undercooked crabs or crayfish containing live metacercarial larvae of Paragonimus species [1]. It may also be transmitted by contaminated surfaces of utensils. Paragonimiasis occurs mainly in tropical countries like Thailand, Vietnam, Laos, the Philippines, Indonesia, Malaysia and Myanmar [2]. It is also found in China, Japan, South Korea and Taiwan [2]. Cases have also been reported from India, Sri Lanka, USA, Canada, Latin America, and West and Central Africa [2]. After ingestion, the larvae traverse the duodenal wall and enter the peritoneal cavity. From here, they traverse through the diaphragm into the pleural cavity. Hereafter, they enter the lung parenchyma and mature into adults. The time interval between ingestion and maturation is 60–90 days [3].

Pulmonary parenchymal manifestations include consolidation(s) (up to 80%) and nodular opacities (up to 60%) [4, 5]. These are often multiple in number. The distribution is often reported in a subpleural pattern. The characteristic track, burrow or tunnel sign represents the migration path taken by the worm from the visceral pleura to the pulmonary nodule but is not universally present [6]. The symptomatic presentation in lung parenchymal paragonimiasis includes a variable combination of cough, chest pain, haemoptysis, loss of appetite or dyspnoea [3, 5]. The presentation mimics other subacute or chronic tropical infections like tuberculosis, melioidosis, leptospirosis, hydatidosis and amebiasis [7–9]. Caution should be maintained while using corticosteroids in an individual where paragonimiasis is a possible aetiology. Empirical use of corticosteroids in eosinophilic diseases, like chronic eosinophilic pneumonia, can lead to severe manifestations of paragonimiasis [10].

Effusion is the most common pleural manifestation in paragonimiasis (up to 60–70%) [4, 5]. The fluid is characteristically an exudate, with the presence of eosinophils [11]. Paragonimus larvae may not be detected in all individuals. If tested, Paragonimus-specific antibodies are detected in the pleural fluid [11]. Occasionally, a pneumothorax may be present, with or without a pleural effusion [4]. On thoracoscopic examination, plaques or nodules are noted on the parietal pleura [12]. In Paragonimus-related pleural effusion, cough, sputum or haemoptysis, and chest pain are the usual presenting symptoms [1, 3]. Pleural fluid glucose has been described to be very low (<100 g·L−1) and LDH to be greatly elevated (<1000 U·L−1) [11]. However, these are markers of the intensity of pleural fluid inflammation or cellular metabolism, rather than specifically for Paragonimus. A relatively less severe pleural inflammation at the time of pleurocentesis in the present case may explain why the pleural fluid glucose was not markedly reduced and LDH was not markedly elevated. Differentials include other parasitic diseases like hydatid disease, strongyloidiasis and toxocariasis, which are associated with elevated levels of parasite-specific antibodies. A tuberculous pleural effusion can also rarely be associated with an eosinophilic pleural effusion [13]; although not characteristic a very low pleural fluid glucose and an elevated LDH may also be present, signifying severe pleural infection. However, ADA levels are elevated due to activation of macrophages in a tuberculous pleural effusion. EGPA presents with features of small vessel vasculitis with a history of breathlessness, wheezing or recurrent sinusitis. Elevated ANCA levels are noted in up to 60% of EGPA cases. AEP presents with rapidly progressive breathlessness, fever and cough, mimicking acute respiratory distress syndrome [14]. Radiological findings include bilateral lung infiltrates [14]. A history of recent exposure to vaping, cigarette smoking or significant environmental exposure to dust may be present. It is highly responsive to corticosteroids [14]. Chronic eosinophilic pneumonia (CEP) can also rarely be associated with a pleural effusion, which is eosinophilic on analysis [14]. Chronic cough, weight loss, fever and malaise are the typical presenting features of CEP [14]. Radiology shows bilateral peripheral pulmonary infiltrates in the classical “photographic negative of cardiogenic pulmonary oedema” [14]. The diagnosis is one of exclusion. Malignant pleural effusions are the most common cause of eosinophilic effusions in the developed world [13, 15]. Lymphomas (Hodgkin and T-cell associated) and AML may also present with eosinophilia [13, 15, 16]. Additionally, rheumatoid arthritis, pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, traumatic pleural tap and chronic drug exposure can also cause eosinophilic pleural effusion. A history of small joint pains and/or stiffness and of chronic drug use should be elicited from all patients with eosinophilic effusion. A peer-reviewed global database of specific pulmonary toxicities can be found on https://www.pneumotox.com.

Diagnosis requires demonstration of the parasite or ova. This is often achieved on sputum samples, and less commonly on lung or pleural biopsy [1]. The ova are microscopic (around 100×55 µm), yellow-to-brown in colour, and have an operculum [1]. There are no large scale studies evaluating the yield of samples for paragonimiasis. Parasitic ova are detected in sputum in 20–70% and in pleural fluid in 10% individuals with pulmonary disease [1, 3, 4]. Stool examination has a yield of 25–60% [1] The yields are severely affected by the degree of infection [1]. Serological demonstration of IgG-specific against Paragonimus can also clinch the diagnosis [5]. The sensitivity and specificity of ELISA has been found to be high (100% and 97%, respectively) in case series [1, 3, 17]. Whole genomic sequencing on blood samples are also being used to detect Paragonimus [18]. Immunoblot assays have also been evaluated and found to have high sensitivity and specificity, but their availability is scarce [19]. Histopathological examination of pleural or lung biopsy shows chronic inflammation with or without eosinophils. Demonstration of the parasite is less common. Classic lesions are 1–3 cm sized cysts containing the worm along with dark red or rusty fluid, erythrocytes, eosinophils, Charcot–Leyden crystals and necrotic material [1, 20]. Lung cysts eventually rupture into the bronchioles, releasing the parasite into the sputum. This process may also lead to haemoptysis.

A 2–3 day course of oral praziquantel (25 mg·kg−1 dose, three times a day) is the drug of choice for treatment [1]. In continued infection, repeated courses are required. However, ova may persist despite adequately dosed praziquantel. For refractory cases, oral triclabendazole (10 mg·kg−1, twice a day) for 1 day is advised for patients aged 6 years and above [21]. Praziquantel and triclabendazole are associated with parasitological cure rates of 96% and 75% at 90 days, respectively [22]. The common adverse events associated with these drugs are headaches, dizziness and nausea [22]. Pleural effusion may require intercostal drainage in the presence of dyspnoea or in cases refractory to praziquantel. In the presence of empyema or extensively loculated pleural effusion, intrapleural enzyme therapy with fibrinolytics may be required. Corticosteroids are not routinely recommended in Paragonimus-associated pleural effusion. However, cerebral paragonimiasis requires additional treatment with corticosteroids.

Conclusion

Paragonimiasis should be considered in exudative pleural effusions, especially in patients with travel to tropical countries and eosinophilia. The absence of dietary exposure history or lack of ova detection does not exclude the diagnosis. Immunoglobulin positivity is valuable when microscopic confirmation on sputum or stool is non-contributory. Triclabendazole is an effective alternative in cases that lack response to praziquantel.
