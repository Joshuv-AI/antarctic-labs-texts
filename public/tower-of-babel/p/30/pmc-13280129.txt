Computed tomography evaluation of periportal halo in healthy dogs under medetomidine-based sedation and alfaxalone-sevoflurane anesthesia
Authors: Miju KANG, Daji NOH, Sooyoung CHOI, Hojung CHOI, Youngwon LEE, Sang-Kwon LEE, Kija LEE
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeriportal halo (PPH) sign on contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) is considered a pathological finding; however, recent studies indicate that it may also appear in healthy individuals. We hypothesized that PPH can be observed in healthy dogs and can vary depending on the anesthetic agents. This study assessed the PPH sign in healthy dogs under different anesthetic agents and across different postcontrast phases. In this cross-over study, 10 healthy Beagle dogs underwent non-contrast and postcontrast CT examinations (arterial, venous, delayed, 5-min delayed, and 10-min delayed phases) under two anesthetic agents, medetomidine and alfaxalone, with a 1-week interval. The presence and severity of PPH were assessed on a scale of 0–3 and compared across different postcontrast phases. Significant differences in PPH were noted between the two anesthetic groups. In the medetomidine group, PPH was absent in the arterial phase, peaked at grade 3 in the delayed phase, and gradually decreased thereafter in the 5- and 10-min delayed phases. In contrast, PPH was minimal or absent in the alfaxalone group across all phases. In conclusion, PPH can be detected in healthy dogs depending on the sedative or anesthetic agent. It can particularly be detected with medetomidine in the delayed phase and should not be misinterpreted as a pathological change on postcontrast CT.Keywords: alfaxalone, computed tomography, dog, medetomidine, periportal halo

INTRODUCTION

The periportal space is the area surrounding the portal vein (PV) and its branches and is embedded in a loose connective tissue that also contains the hepatic artery, bile duct, nerves, and lymphatic vessels [6]. At the level of the porta hepatis, perivascular fat is commonly observed around the PV as a normal finding [6]. The periportal halo (PPH) sign, also known as the periportal collar sign, is a circumferential region of low attenuation around the intrahepatic PV on contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) [8, 18]. This sign represents fluid accumulation or lymphatic dilatation within the loose connective tissue around the portal triads and is generally interpreted as periportal edema, which is often used synonymously with PPH [8].

PPH is nonspecific and can be detected in various conditions [6, 17]. In humans, PPH caused by periportal edema can be observed in patients with congestive cardiac failure, secondary cardiac congestion, acute hepatitis, lymphadenopathy at the porta hepatis that causes lymphatic obstruction, liver transplant, liver transplant rejection, cholangitis, and acute pyelonephritis [4, 8, 10, 14, 18]. In veterinary medicine, PPH has been reported in both hepatic and extrahepatic diseases of neoplastic and non-neoplastic origin in dogs and cats, with neoplasia identified as the most common cause [6]. However, in small animal practice, PPH is occasionally observed without any identifiable cause, consistent with the finding of a previous study, in which no underlying disease was detected in 10 out of 143 dogs and cats with PPH [6]. However, based on our review of the literature, no study has investigated this finding in healthy dogs.

In veterinary practice, patients require sedation or anesthesia for CT examinations. As anesthetic agents may affect the cardiovascular system and lymphatic circulation, it can be challenging to determine whether PPH represents hepatic disease or is associated with another issue, such as drug-induced sedation [2, 3, 11, 13]. Medetomidine, an α2-agnoist, is commonly used for sedation in dogs and cats because of its reversible sedative, anxiolytic, and analgesic properties [16, 19]. Alfaxalone is a neuroactive steroid used as a general anesthetic in several species, including dogs, cats, rabbits, rats, and ferrets [1].

We hypothesized that PPH could also be observed in healthy dogs and the type of sedative or anesthetic drug could influence its appearance. Moreover, we hypothesized that the thickness of PPH would vary over time across different postcontrast phases. This study aimed to (1) assess the relationship between the sedative or anesthetic drug and the presence of PPH on postcontrast CT in clinically healthy dogs and (2) evaluate the changes in the severity of PPH, if present.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

This study had a prospective crossover design. In total, 10 purpose-bred Beagle dogs (five intact males and five intact females) were included in the study. The ages of the dogs varied from 1 to 6.2 years. Their median body weight was 10.5 kg (range 8.3–13.5 kg). All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kyungpook National University, and the protocol for the care of the dogs adhered to the Guidelines for Animal Experiments of Kyungpook National University (No. KNU 2024-0125). All dogs were clinically healthy based on physical examinations, complete blood counts, serum biochemical data, and thoracic radiographs. Moreover, all dogs included in the study had no evidence of hepatobiliary abnormalities on ultrasound.

The dogs were divided into two groups: the medetomidine group, which was sedated with medetomidine, and the alfaxalone group, which was maintained with sevoflurane after the induction of anesthesia with alfaxalone. All dogs were maintained in a fasting state for 12 hr for the CT scan.

CT scanning protocol

CT scans were performed twice for each dog, with an interval of a week. Before the CT examination, blood pressure and heart rate were recorded. The dogs in the Medetomidine group were sedated and those in the Alfaxalone group were anesthetized for CT examination of the cranial abdomen. The dogs in the Medetomidine group were intravenously sedated with medetomidine (10 µg/kg, Domitor®; Zoetis, Seoul, Korea). Additional sedation with 5 µg/kg of medetomidine was administered if the dogs were not sedated enough to perform a CT examination. In the alfaxalone group, general anesthesia was induced with alfaxalone (3 mg/kg, intravenously, Alfaxan®; Careside, Seongnam, Korea). The dogs were then intubated, and general anesthesia was maintained with 2% sevoflurane (Sevofran; Hana Pharm, Seoul, Korea) in oxygen delivered at a flow rate of 2.0 L/min. All dogs were positioned in ventral recumbency on the CT table. All dogs breathed spontaneously throughout the CT examination without mechanical ventilation, under continuous respiratory monitoring. Each dog was equipped with monitoring instruments to measure physiological variables. A multiparameter monitor (CARESCAPE Monitor B650; GE Healthcare, Helsinki, Finland) was used for tracking the heart rate (via lead II electrocardiography), oxygen saturation (SpO2, via pulse oximetry), and systolic, diastolic, and mean arterial blood pressures.

CT was performed using a 32-multislice CT (AlexionTM; Cannon Medical System, Tokyo, Japan). The scanning parameters were as follows: field of view, 320 mm; slice thickness, 1 mm; pitch, 0.8; rotation time, 0.75 sec; tube voltage, 120 kV; and tube current, 112 mA. CT was performed in non-contrast, and five time points of after the injection of iohexol (600 mgI/kg, Bonorex; Dai Han pharm, Seoul, Korea): arterial, venous, delayed, 5-min delayed, and 10-min delayed phases. The contrast material was injected via an automatic injector (Nemoto Single Head Pressure Contrast Media Injector; Nemoto, Tokyo, Japan) at an injection speed of 1.5 mL/sec. CT scans were started 10–15 min after sedation with medetomidine in the medetomidine group. Pre-experiments with two dogs were conducted to set appropriate times for the arterial, venous, and delayed phases in Medetomidine and Alfaxalone group, respectively. A postcontrast multi-phase CT scan was performed using a bolus-tracking technique, with an ROI set in the descending aorta. In the medetomidine group, the arterial, venous, and delayed phases were scanned 4, 70, and 150 sec after reaching 60 Hounsfield units (HU), respectively. In the alfaxalone group, the arterial, venous, and delayed phases were scanned 4, 30, and 90 sec after reaching 60 HU, respectively.

Image analysis

All CT data were reconstructed using a picture archiving and communication system (INFINITT PACS; INFINITT Healthcare, Seoul, Korea) for analysis. The data were reconstructed in the transverse plane with 1 mm slice thickness using a soft tissue kernel and soft tissue window (window width, 400 HU; window level, 40 HU). The CT images were randomly presented and independently evaluated without knowledge of the drugs used. The distribution of the PPH sign was assessed based on the involvement of the complete first, second, or third generation of portal branches, according to the ramifications of the intrahepatic PV. The severity of PPH was assessed at the proximal portion of the second-generation portal branches and the third-generation branches. It was graded from 0 to 3 based on the thickness of the PPH sign on transverse CT images. The measurement was performed at a 90-degree angle to the vessel in the low-attenuation area parallel to the PV. Grade 0 was defined as the absence of any PPH signs. Grade 1 was defined as the appearance of a halo that was unmeasurably faint or was as thin as the hairline. Grade 2 was defined as the presence of a clear PPH sign with a thickness of <1.4 mm, while grade 3 was defined as the presence of a clear PPH sign with a thickness of ≥1.4 mm (Fig. 1). The threshold was set at 1.4 mm, which was the average value derived from the results.

Representative images of periportal halo (PPH, arrowheads) of four grades: Grade 0 (A) refers to no halo sign, and Grade 1 (B) refers to a faint halo sign. Grade 2 (C) is defined as a clear PPH with a thickness of <1.4 mm. Grade 3 (D) is defined as a clear PPH with a thickness of ≥1.4 mm.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using commercial software (SPSS 25.0; IBM SPSS Statistics, New York, NY, USA). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov normality test was used to assess the normality of data. The thickness of the PPH sign was compared between the alfaxalone and medetomidine groups using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Numerical data were reported as medians with interquartile ranges. The thickness of the PPH sign in each phase (arterial, portal, delayed, 5-min delayed, and 10-min delayed) within the same group was compared using the Friedman test; the post-hoc Wilcoxon signed-rank test was employed for multiple comparisons. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses.

In both groups, PPH in the second-generation branch of the PV was not observed during the arterial phase (grade 0 for all). In the medetomidine group, the median PPH score for the second-generation branch of the PV peaked at grade 3 in the delayed phase and then decreased to grade 1 in the 10-min delayed phase. In the alfaxalone group, the median PPH score was grade 0 in all phases. However, in the alfaxalone group, a faint PPH sign was observed in two dogs in the venous phase, three dogs in the delayed phase, and two dogs in the 5-min delayed phase. The median PPH scores within each group significantly differed across the arterial, venous, delayed, 5-min delayed, and 10-min delayed phases (P<0.05). The median PPH scores in the venous, delayed, 5-min delayed, and 10-min delayed phases also differed significantly between the two groups (P<0.05, Fig. 2).

The PPH sign was localized along the first, second, and third generations of the right and left intrahepatic portal branches in the medetomidine group. In all venous and delayed phases, PPH was observed up to the third-generation branches, with the median PPH scores for the third-generation branches being grade 1 in the venous phase and grade 2 in the delayed phase. In the 5-min delayed phase, PPH in the third-generation branches was observed in nine out of 10 dogs, with the PPH score being grade 1 in all dogs in the medetomidine group. In the 10-min delayed phase, PPH was seen in the first-generation branches in only one out of 10 dogs, second-generation branches in four out of 10 dogs, and third-generation branches in three out of 10 dogs; PPH was not detected in two out of 10 dogs. PPH seen in the third-generation branches was graded as either grade 1 or grade 0. The generations and PPH scores for branches in the right and left PVs were found to be identical.

DISCUSSION

In this study, PPH was more prominent in the medetomidine group than in the alfaxalone group, emphasizing the effect of the sedative agent on PPH findings. PPH was observed in all medetomidine-sedated dogs; however, PPH was not observed in most dogs anesthetized with alfaxalone. Moreover, the severity of PPH varied over time, peaking during the delayed phase. These results support the hypothesis that the type of sedative or anesthetic agent can affect the PPH sign in dogs and that PPH can occur even in clinically healthy dogs under certain sedative or anesthetic conditions.

In recent studies, PPH appeared on CT scans in a small subset of cases (n=10/143) without any other detectable abnormalities [6], suggesting that PPH can represent an incidental finding without clinical significance. However, validating this hypothesis is challenging because of the lack of control groups consisting of healthy animals undergoing CT examinations. Based on our review of the literature, this is the first study to specifically assess the impact of sedation protocols on PPH findings in CT imaging. Previous studies used various protocols, making it difficult to determine whether sedative or anesthetic agents contribute to the occurrence of PPH [6, 8]. The present study focused on two sedative or anesthetic agents—medetomidine and alfaxalone—which are widely used in small animal practice and have distinct mechanisms of action that exhibit different effects on the cardiovascular and lymphatic systems.

Medetomidine, an α2-adrenergic receptor agonist, primarily acts on the central nervous system, inducing sedation, analgesia, and muscle relaxation. By suppressing sympathetic tone, it causes peripheral vasoconstriction and lowers blood pressure and heart rate [5, 15, 19]. These hemodynamic changes may reduce blood flow and potentially inhibit lymphatic drainage, enhancing the visibility of PPH in medetomidine-sedated dogs. The attenuation extending to the third-generation branches of the PV can be attributed to the uniform distribution of contrast media coupled with these hemodynamic effects [5]. Vasoconstriction slows blood flow and causes transient lymphatic stasis, thereby promoting fluid accumulation around the portal triads, which manifests as a uniform halo sign across the intrahepatic PV system. Furthermore, the anatomical symmetry of the PVs and lymphatic pathways facilitate consistent contrast distribution between the left and right branches. Further research is warranted to determine whether these observations are unique to medetomidine or are also applicable to other sedative agents.

In our study, PPH was more pronounced in the medetomidine group, with higher median grades noted in the venous, delayed, and 5-min delayed phases. This may reflect the vasoconstrictive properties of medetomidine, which could lead to temporary lymphatic stasis or fluid retention around the portal triads [10, 13, 14, 16]. Moreover, delayed clearance of contrast material may accentuate the PPH sign during later imaging phases. The presence of PPH in medetomidine-sedated dogs raises questions about its clinical relevance. Although the PPH sign has been associated with various conditions, such as periportal edema and lymphatic dilation, all dogs in this study were clinically healthy. This suggests that the observed PPH sign was a physiological response to the sedative rather than a pathological one. Therefore, veterinary practitioners should interpret PPH on CT scans of medetomidine-sedated dogs with caution, as it may mimic underlying hepatic or systemic disease but may actually reflect the pharmacological effects of the drug [11, 12]. This emphasizes the importance of correlating imaging findings with clinical and laboratory data.

In contrast, PPH was rarely observed in the alfaxalone group. Alfaxalone, a neuroactive steroid anesthetic, induces general anesthesia without significant vasoconstriction [1, 9]. Its effect on arterial blood pressure is dose-dependent, and it promotes smooth muscle relaxation, which may reduce the possibility of fluid accumulation [1, 7]. The minimal or absent PPH in this group underscores the importance of sedative choice in influencing diagnostic outcomes. These findings support the need for standardized sedation protocols in veterinary imaging to reduce variability.

Another significant finding of our study is the inconsistent appearance of PPH in terms of thickness and grade across all imaging phases. The delayed phase exhibited the highest degree of PPH, which remained detectable up to the 10-min delayed phase. Contrast agent accumulation predominantly occurs around lesions or adjacent to vascular and lymphatic structures [4]. Although this phenomenon is rarely observed in healthy dogs, the contrast agent may transiently accumulate within vessels or lymphatic tissues, potentially impacting imaging. Moreover, in our study, the PPH grade decreased from the second- to the third-generation portal branches. This may be due to the progressive reduction in vessel diameter and contrast medium volume. Therefore, CT scans performed within 20 min after sedation in dogs with extrahepatic disease or in healthy dogs should take into account the type of anesthetic or sedative used, as it may affect diagnostic interpretation. Specifically, short-term CT imaging with alfaxalone appears to provide better diagnostic clarity than that with medetomidine. Further assessment of the duration and temporal dynamics of PPH would enhance the understanding of this phenomenon.

This study has several limitations. First, the study was conducted on a small scale. Even under the same conditions, differences were noted in the severity of PPH among individuals. Second, the diversity in dog breeds and ages was restricted, and the study was conducted only on healthy dogs. Therefore, the findings may not be directly applicable to dogs with specific diseases. Third, although a few dogs received an additional dose of medetomidine to achieve adequate sedation, the potential dose-dependent effect on PPH severity was not considered in this study. However, based on previous findings that the cardiovascular effects of medetomidine are not strongly dose-dependent [15], this influence is presumed to be minimal. Fourth, the venous and delayed phase timings differed between the groups, which may limit direct comparability. Although this adjustment was necessary to reflect each protocol’s hemodynamic response and to ensure appropriate phase capture, it remains a methodological limitation.

In conclusion, PPH was observed in healthy dogs sedated with medetomidine but not in those anesthetized with alfaxalone. It was most prominent in the delayed phase and tended to diminish or fade during the 10-min delayed phase. Third-generation branches of the PVs exhibited either a faint halo or no halo, with consistent patterns noted on both right and left sides. Our findings demonstrate that the occurrence of PPH depends on the type of anesthetic or sedative drug and highlight the phases where it is most evident. Importantly, PPH observed in venous, delayed, and 10-min delayed phases should be interpreted as a normal physiological phenomenon influenced by sedation rather than a pathological change.
