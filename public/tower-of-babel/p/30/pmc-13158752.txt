Who Gets Panretinal Photocoagulation? National Patterns in Proliferative Diabetic Retinopathy Treatment
Authors: Serina S Applebaum, Julia Fu, Mathieu Bakhoum, Yanhong Deng, Kristen H Nwanyanwu, Sight Outcomes Research Collaborative (SOURCE) Consortium
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo identify factors associated with receiving panretinal photocoagulation (PRP) among individuals treated in the first year after proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) diagnosis.DesignA retrospective cohort analysis from 2015 to 2022 using electronic health record data from 13 nationwide sites in the Sight Outcomes Research Collaborative (SOURCE) registry.ParticipantsAdults aged ≥18 years newly diagnosed with PDR, with ≥2 visits and treatment with PRP or anti-VEGF intravitreal injection (IVI) in the first year after diagnosis.MethodsWe analyzed adjusted annual probability of PRP and anti-VEGF IVI treatment. We used multivariable logistic regression to determine associations of sociodemographic and clinical factors with receiving PRP.Main Outcome MeasuresTreatment with PRP in the first year after PDR diagnosis.ResultsOf 2442 individuals with incident PDR, 1912 (78.3%) received treatment within a year of PDR diagnosis, of which 1147 (60.0%) received PRP. From 2015 to 2022, adjusted probability of PRP decreased from 78.6% to 59.0%, while probability of receiving anti-VEGF IVIs increased from 68.2% to 85.6%. Odds of treatment with PRP were greater for younger (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 0.86 per 5-year increase in age; P < 0.001), non-Hispanic Black (aOR, 1.44; P = 0.02), Hispanic (aOR, 1.72; P = 0.004), unemployed (aOR, 1.42; P = 0.07), and better-seeing individuals (aOR, 1.08 per 5-letter increase in visual acuity; P < 0.001), and those with a prior ≥1-year lapse in care (aOR, 1.43; P = 0.01). Prior anti-VEGF IVI (aOR, 0.44; P < 0.001) and diabetic macular edema (aOR, 0.52; P < 0.001) decreased odds of PRP.ConclusionsPanretinal photocoagulation treatment declined over time. Black, Hispanic, and younger individuals, and those with a prior lapse in care, were more likely to receive PRP. Diabetic macular edema and history of anti-VEGF IVI during nonproliferative stages decreased odds of PRP.Financial DisclosuresProprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.Keywords: Proliferative diabetic retinopathy, Panretinal photocoagulation, Anti-VEGF therapy, Treatment disparities, SOURCE registry

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a vision-threatening complication of diabetes, which causes 12 000 to 24 000 new cases of blindness each year in the United States.1 For decades, panretinal photocoagulation (PRP) has been the standard treatment for proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR), which reduces the risk of severe vision loss by 50%.2 However, PRP is associated with adverse consequences including pain, loss of peripheral and night vision, and worsening diabetic macular edema (DME).3, 4, 5 The therapeutic landscape shifted in 2015, when the Diabetic Retinopathy Clinical Research Network (DRCR) Protocol S and the UK-based CLARITY trial demonstrated that anti-VEGF intravitreal injections (IVIs) can yield visual outcomes that are statistically noninferior to PRP in selected patients with PDR over several years.6, 7, 8 Anti-VEGF therapy avoids the destructive nature of PRP and concurrently treats DME, but imposes an increased visit and treatment frequency burden due to its transient biological effect.

Patient adherence to follow-up remains a major challenge in PDR management; in the DRCR Protocol S study, only 61% of participants completed 5 years of follow-up in both treatment arms.7 Anti-VEGF therapy for PDR requires close monitoring and consistent treatment schedules. In contrast, PRP, once complete, provides durable protection. Therefore, treatment decisions may be influenced not only by clinical presentation but also by the treating physician's assessment of a patient’s likelihood of adherence to follow-up. There is significant variability in practice patterns for PDR treatment.9 In current practice, patients with PDR may be treated with anti-VEGF IVI, PRP, or a combination of both. Prior studies of PDR treatment trends have not assessed patient-level characteristics, but rather focus on temporal shifts in how the use of each treatment has changed over time since the emergence of anti-VEGF monotherapy.10,11 Landmark clinical trial results and regulatory approvals have influenced practice patterns over time, but it is unknown, in clinical settings, what other factors are associated with treatment modality. In this study, we address this gap in knowledge by evaluating a broad set of clinical and demographic factors for associations with PRP treatment in the first year following a PDR diagnosis.

Methods

Data Source

We analyzed data from patients seen at any of 13 active sites in the Sight Outcomes Research Collaborative (SOURCE) for this multicenter retrospective cohort study. The SOURCE repository aggregates longitudinal electronic health record data for all patients receiving eye care at participating academic health systems in the United States. The SOURCE includes deidentified demographic information, diagnoses identified by International Classification of Diseases billing codes, and Current Procedural Terminology codes for services rendered from all clinical encounters. The data are completely deidentified using software from Datavant, Inc. More information about the database is available at https://www.sourcecollaborative.org. This study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and received exemption from the Yale University Institutional Review Board.

Population

We identified adults aged ≥18 years who were newly diagnosed with PDR (International Classification of Diseases 10 codes: E08.35, E09.35, E10.35, E11.35, and E13.35) between 2015 (the year of DRCR Protocol S publication) and 2022 and were treated with PRP and/or anti-VEGF IVI in the year after PDR diagnosis.6 Our inclusion criteria required individuals to have at least two ophthalmology office visits within the first year after PDR diagnosis, allowing for an initial evaluation visit and subsequently scheduled treatment visit. To exclude nonincident cases, we required patients to have at least 1 prior visit with a diagnosis of non-PDR before their first PDR diagnosis.

Outcomes and Covariates

We identified each instance of treatment with anti-VEGF IVI and PRP in the year following PDR diagnosis. The primary outcome was treatment with PRP (with or without anti-VEGF IVI) compared with IVI alone within the first year after PDR diagnosis.

We evaluated demographic variables including age at PDR diagnosis, sex, race and ethnicity, health insurance status, Distressed Communities Index score, rural–urban commuting area code, and employment status. The Distressed Communities Index is a composite score for a geographic area measured as a percentile from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater distress.12 We also examined calendar year of PDR diagnosis and prior ophthalmic history, including history of follow-up nonadherence (defined as any ≥1-year lapse in follow-up care between non-PDR and PDR diagnosis dates) and prior treatment with PRP or anti-VEGF IVI. We determined clinical factors including diabetes type and systemic comorbidities (Charlson Comorbidity Index, composite diabetic complication indicator [diabetic nephropathy, diabetic neuropathy, or nonhealing ulcer], hypertension, dyslipidemia, and limb amputation). We modified the Charlson Comorbidity Index to exclude points accrued from the age and diabetes categories because age was independently included in our models, and all included participants had end-organ damage due to diabetes mellitus (i.e., PDR).13 Possible values for our modified Charlson Comorbidity Index ranged from 0 to 31 points. Lastly, we assessed ophthalmic variables including visual acuity at PDR diagnosis (in the better-seeing eye, converted to ETDRS letter score), history of open-angle glaucoma or cataracts, and presence of DME, tractional retinal detachment, and vitreous hemorrhage. We classified missing values of categorical variables as "Unknown" to avoid bias and sample size reduction from list-wise deletion in multivariable analysis. We performed a sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation as an alternative method for handling missingness.

Statistical Analysis

We reported continuous variables as means and standard deviations and categorical variables as proportions, unless otherwise specified. Among the treated cohort, we compared continuous variables using t-tests and categorical variables with the chi-squared test between treatment types. We interrogated this study population by comparing the cohorts before versus during or after the COVID-19 pandemic (March 2020). We also used logistic regression to determine factors associated with receiving treatment in the first year after PDR diagnosis, compared with those who were not treated and excluded from the primary analysis. Among the treated population, we then used univariate logistic regression to determine the association of each covariate with receiving PRP treatment. We also calculated overall P values for categorical variables in the multivariable model using Wald chi-square tests. We built multivariable logistic regression models including site number, as well as age, sex, race/ethnicity, and covariates that were associated with the outcome at a P value < 0.20 in univariable analysis. We analyzed annual trends in the probability of receiving PRP and of receiving anti-VEGF IVI treatment from 2015 to 2022, using calendar year as a categorical variable. We analyzed the presence or absence of each treatment independently, such that treatment probabilities did not add to 100%, given the overlap of patients receiving both. We estimated probabilities of treatment at each year, assuming all continuous variables to be at mean levels and categorical variables at the reference level. We used RStudio (version 2024.04.2) for all analyses, and considered P < 0.05 statistically significant.14

Results

Of 2442 individuals with newly diagnosed PDR, we included 1912 individuals (78.3%) who received treatment within the first year (Fig 1). The mean age at diagnosis was 57.5 (standard deviation [SD] 12.6) years, and 939 (49.1%) were female. Non-Hispanic Black individuals comprised 26.7% of the population, and 17.3% were Hispanic or Latine. Included individuals had a mean of 7.68 (SD, 4.60; median, 7.00) visits during the first year. Among treated individuals, 1147 (60.0%) received PRP, including 462 (24.2%) with PRP alone, and 685 (35.8%) receiving both PRP and anti-VEGF IVI. Individuals who received PRP had a mean of 2.42 (SD, 1.51; median, 2.00) sessions of PRP and 2.51 (SD, 3.26; median, 1.00) anti-VEGF IVIs in the first year. Individuals who did not receive PRP had a mean of 4.81 (SD, 3.38; median, 4.00) anti-VEGF IVIs in the first year. Only 107 (5.6%) individuals had received PRP prior to PDR diagnosis. Nearly a quarter (443; 23.2%) received anti-VEGF IVI prior to PDR diagnosis, including 187 individuals who ultimately received PRP in the first year after PDR diagnosis, and 256 individuals who received anti-VEGF IVI only in the first year after PDR diagnosis. Only 43.2% of patients who received PRP had PDR with DME, whereas 63.3% of individuals treated with anti-VEGF IVI only had DME. Treatment distributions, grouped by DME status, are shown in Figure 2. In a subgroup analysis of the cohort stratified by relationship to the COVID-19 pandemic, the group diagnosed with PDR during or after March 2020 tended to be younger with lower comorbidity burden (Supplemental Table 1). Trends in the Use of PRP and Anti-VEGF IVI from 2015 to 2022

After adjusting for confounders, we observed a decrease in the adjusted probability of treatment with PRP from 78.6% in 2015 to 59.0% in 2022. In contrast, the adjusted probability of receiving anti-VEGF IVI in the first year after PDR diagnosis increased from 68.2% in 2015 to 85.6% in 2022. A substantial difference from 2015 to 2016 primarily drove both trends, after which annual probability of each treatment remained relatively stable (Fig 3). Factors Associated with PRP Treatment in the First Year since PDR Diagnosis

Individuals with a prior ≥1-year lapse in ophthalmic care were 1.43 times more likely to receive PRP than IVI monotherapy (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.43; confidence interval [CI], 1.08 – 1.90; P = 0.01). For patients treated for PDR, PRP was also more likely in younger individuals (per 5-year increase in age: aOR, 0.86; CI, 0.82 – 0.89; P < 0.001). Non-Hispanic Black (aOR, 1.44; CI, 1.05 – 1.97; P = 0.02) and Hispanic or Latine individuals (aOR, 1.72; CI, 1.19 – 2.50; P = 0.004) had higher odds of receiving PRP compared to non-Hispanic White individuals. For patients treated for PDR, odds of receiving PRP were higher for better-seeing individuals (per 5-letter increase in visual acuity: aOR, 1.08; CI, 1.05 – 1.12; P < 0.001). In contrast, PRP was less likely among individuals with DME at PDR diagnosis (aOR, 0.52; CI, 0.42 – 0.65; P < 0.001) or previous anti-VEGF IVI prior to PDR diagnosis (aOR, 0.44; CI, 0.34 – 0.57; P < 0.001) (Fig 4, Table 2). Unemployed individuals also trended toward higher odds of PRP (aOR, 1.42; CI 0.98 – 2.07; P = 0.07) compared to individuals employed full-time. In the univariable analysis, presence of a diabetic complication was associated with higher odds of receiving PRP (odds ratio, 1.26; CI, 1.05 – 1.52; P = 0.01), though this trend did not persist in the multivariable model (P = 0.33). Use of PRP also varied significantly by clinical site (P < 0.001). Within the multivariable model, we tested linear contrasts of marginal means comparing probability of PRP before versus during or after the COVID-19 pandemic and found no statistically significant difference (aOR, 1.10;95% CI, 0.89 – 1.39; P = 0.38).

Discussion

Our multicenter analysis from academic centers in the United States found that over 8 years, 60% of patients treated in the first year after PDR diagnosis received PRP. Factors associated with treatment with PRP, compared to anti-VEGF IVI monotherapy, included history of a >1-year lapse in ophthalmic care prior to PDR diagnosis, younger age, non-Hispanic Black race, Hispanic or Latine ethnicity, and better visual acuity at the time of diagnosis. Individuals with prior anti-VEGF IVI and those with DME were less likely to receive PRP.

We demonstrated a shift in PDR treatment strategies over time. Consistent with this, prior work using the national IRIS® Registry (Intelligent Research in Sight) showed that anti-VEGF therapy overtook PRP as the most common treatment for PDR between 2015 and 2016.10 The authors examined temporal trends through 2017; our study extends this timeline through 2022. From 2015 to 2022, the adjusted probability of receiving PRP within the first year of diagnosis decreased from 79% to 59%, while the probability of receiving anti-VEGF IVIs increased from 68% to 73%. This trend reflects the paradigm shift initiated by the DRCR Protocol S study, published in 2015, which demonstrated noninferiority of anti-VEGF therapy compared to PRP for PDR.6 We found that treatment patterns have largely stabilized in more recent years. This plateau may indicate a new equilibrium following a rapid initial adoption of anti-VEGF treatment. Interestingly, while the COVID-19 pandemic caused significant health care disruptions, disproportionately affecting socially disadvantaged groups, we did not identify any clear impact of this period on treatment choices.15

Patients with a lapse in ophthalmic care before PDR diagnosis had 1.4-fold higher adjusted odds of receiving PRP after PDR diagnosis. Panretinal photocoagulation can often be completed in one or a few sessions and can provide lasting protection against vision-threatening neovascular complications of PDR.16 In contrast, anti-VEGF therapy requires monthly injections to achieve disease control.6,17,18 A large proportion of individuals require additional injections during follow-up, and missed appointments can result in progression of neovascularization and vision-threatening sequelae.6,19, 20, 21 Individuals with a prior lapse in care may be deemed more likely to miss follow-up in the future. Indeed, most patients with 1 significant lapse go on to experience more than one.22,23 Similarly, unemployed individuals had a nonsignificant trend (P = 0.07) toward higher adjusted odds of PRP. Unemployment is often associated with socioeconomic instability and inadequate insurance coverage, which can pose barriers to consistent health care access.24,25 Taken together, this suggests that physicians may appropriately favor a more durable therapy like PRP for patients at higher risk of loss to follow-up.

Non-Hispanic Black individuals had 1.4 times the adjusted odds, and Hispanic or Latine individuals 1.7 times the adjusted odds, of receiving PRP compared to non-Hispanic White individuals in the multivariable model. These differences likely reflect intersecting individual and structural factors, including disease severity and barriers to accessing care. Clinical trial data are limited in guiding treatment decisions for patients with advanced PDR. Only 1% of eyes in DRCR Protocol S had level 81 or level 85 disease.2,6 This represents a lack of evidence for treating individuals with very severe disease using anti-VEGF IVI alone. In the absence of robust clinical trial data, physicians may be more likely to use PRP, which is well-established and durable, for patients with more advanced pathology. Although we lacked precise documentation of disease severity, well-documented disparities in diabetic eye care show that racially and ethnically minoritized individuals are likely to be diagnosed with DR at more advanced stages and experience higher rates of vision loss and blindness from DR.26,27 If minoritized patients in our study had more advanced PDR at diagnosis, PRP may have been more clinically appropriate. These populations are also more likely to experience lapses in DR care.28, 29, 30 Economic hardship and structural inequities disproportionately affecting minoritized populations may limit access to or feasibility of frequent anti-VEGF injections.

We found that younger age and better visual acuity at PDR diagnosis had higher adjusted odds of PRP. Younger individuals are more likely to experience lapses in care, which may be due to competing work and social demands, or deprioritization of routine care due to better overall health.28,29,31 A further possible explanation is that physicians may prioritize a long-lasting intervention for younger patients, who have more years of potential visual function to preserve. Better visual acuity at diagnosis is similarly associated with lapses in care.28,32 Patients with good vision may not perceive the need for immediate or intensive treatment, especially if they are asymptomatic. Conversely, worse visual acuity at diagnosis may more likely coincide with pathology, such as center-involving DME, which is more appropriately managed with anti-VEGF therapy. In such cases, anti-VEGF IVIs may be favored initially for DME management, with PRP deferred or omitted depending on the patient's course and response to injections.33

Indeed, two of the strongest negative predictors of PRP in the multivariable model were the presence of DME and prior anti-VEGF therapy before PDR diagnosis. Panretinal photocoagulation is known to exacerbate DME.4 Furthermore, those already receiving anti-VEGF therapy for DME during nonproliferative stages may have had less severe disease since anti-VEGF improves DR and may be continued on an anti-VEGF injection regimen after progression to PDR. Studies have shown that patients receiving anti-VEGF injections are more adherent to follow-up, and the increased treatment burden of PRP may not be justified if anti-VEGF IVIs are already controlling neovascularization and macular edema.28 It may be reasonably hypothesized that payer-specific requirements, such as prior authorizations for anti-VEGF therapy, may also influence real-world decision-making. Despite this, insurance type was not significantly associated with treatment modality in our analysis. Future directions may include a more granular exploration of insurance plans to examine this effect.

There are several limitations to this study. First, the SOURCE registry includes only data from academic medical centers, and findings may not be fully generalizable to community-based or more resource-limited settings. Second, we were unable to evaluate several unmeasured variables. For example, we were not able to assess patient preference, which may also play a significant role in management decisions.34 In addition, International Classification of Diseases coding does not distinguish between high-risk and low-risk PDR, though this factor is likely to influence treatment decisions. However, we included variables representing neovascular complications of PDR, which indicate more severe disease. While we adjusted for site, we were unable to evaluate regional patterns in treatment preference because our dataset did not contain geographic identifiers or practice characteristics. Lastly, a recognized limitation of large registry-based studies is the potential for inaccurate or delayed coding, though there is generally strong agreement between billing data and the actual medical record.35 For example, about 5% of individuals in our cohort of incident PDR had a prior history of PRP. While some clinicians may incorporate PRP into their practice for severe non-PDR, this may reflect some delayed coding of PDR diagnosis. Regardless, eyes receiving additional PRP may represent more complex cases. Assumed trainee participation in patient care, given the academic nature of included sites, may also contribute to coding inaccuracies.36 Ultimately, without access to full clinical charts, coding inaccuracies could introduce unmeasured confounding, and these findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Strengths of the study include the large sample size from across the United States, which allows us to identify factors independent from institution-specific practice preferences.

This study adds insights into the evolving landscape of PDR treatment in the United States. Panretinal photocoagulation is a permanent treatment but comes with tradeoffs in peripheral vision, nocturnal vision, and contrast sensitivity. Anti-VEGF IVIs are a newer but biologically transient therapy with a different side effect profile, which necessitates a higher visit and treatment burden. We found that individuals more likely to receive PRP within the first year of PDR diagnosis were non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic or Latine, younger, unemployed, and better-seeing, as well as those with a prior lapse in care. These same populations are at higher risk for future lapses in care.22,28, 29, 30,37 An individualized assessment of each patient’s clinical and social circumstances is important for good PDR management. In addition, some combination of explicit and implicit understanding of these risk factors may cause ophthalmologists to prioritize PRP in populations for whom they anticipate lower ability for consistent follow-up. To manage these patients, some evidence suggests a positive effect of combination therapy, as in the 36% of our population who received both PRP and anti-VEGF IVI.38 This combination therapy has become a common and practical approach, which may particularly benefit patients at high risk for lapses in care. Long-term effectiveness and patient-related outcomes in real-world settings should be studied, with attention to the social determinants of health. Reducing disparities to optimize outcomes in diabetic eye disease will require equitable access to effective PDR treatment through community-partnered approaches, tailored to individual clinical and social context.
