Antimicrobial activity and material compatibility of Anatolian essential oil–based oral care formulations
Authors: Betül İşiner, Elif Nihan Küçükyıldız, Eray Tatlıcı, Ozfer Yesilada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedicinal plants have long been used in Anatolia for the management of infections and oral health–related conditions. Essential oils obtained from these plants are traditionally valued for their antimicrobial properties; however, their applicability in modern oral care formulations (mouthwashes) and their compatibility with oral biomaterials remain insufficiently explored. This study aimed to evaluate the antimicrobial activity of region-specific essential oil–based mouthwash formulations prepared using Salvia triloba, Origanum vulgare, Lavandula angustifolia, and Myrtus communis, and to assess their compatibility with commonly used restorative materials.MethodsGlass ionomer and resin-based restorative materials were immersed in herbal essential oil–based mouthwash formulations to simulate 1- and 5-year clinical exposure periods. Weight change was recorded, while color stability was assessed using the CIEDE2000 (ΔE₀₀) formula, and antimicrobial activity was determined by the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) method.ResultsThe physical effects of herbal mouthwash formulations were material-dependent; in glass ionomer cement, weight change was affected by mouthwash type and duration (p < 0.05), while in resin-based materials color change remained within clinically acceptable limits (ΔE₀₀ < 1.8). All herbal formulations exhibited antimicrobial activity, as indicated by MIC values, with the lowest MIC values observed for the O. vulgare mouthwash against S. mutans and C. albicans (0.02%), while the strongest antifungal activity against C. albicans was noted for the M. communis formulation (0.05%).ConclusionsThe demonstrated antimicrobial efficacy together with acceptable effects on restorative materials suggests that essential oil–based mouthwash formulations derived from Anatolian medicinal plants provide a favorable balance between antimicrobial activity and material compatibility.Keywords: Essential oils, Herbal formulations, Antimicrobial activity, Material compatibility, Color stability

Introduction

Composites are a cornerstone of contemporary restorative dentistry, and preserving their physical and aesthetic stability is essential for long-term clinical performance. Parallel to advances in restorative materials, interest in herbal products and essential oil-based formulations has increased substantially, largely because these extracts contain phenolic and terpenoid compounds with well-documented antimicrobial activity [1, 2]. Their inhibitory effects against pathogens such as Streptococcus mutans and Candida albicans have been demonstrated in multiple studies [3–5] and oils rich in carvacrol or thymol show particularly strong antibacterial potential [6, 7]. In addition, certain medicinal plants have attracted scientific interest as potential sources of bioactive compounds relevant to oral care [8]. In this context, essential oils obtained from region-specific medicinal plants, including those sourced from Anatolia, have attracted increasing interest for their potential use in oral care formulations [9]. Accordingly, this study provides a comprehensive evaluation of essential oil–based mouthwash formulations, focusing on their antimicrobial activity and compatibility with commonly used restorative materials.

In addition to their antimicrobial effects, the potential for essential oils to be incorporated into dental materials and to affect composites is also important. Some studies suggest that certain oils can enhance the antimicrobial profile of composite materials without disrupting their structure [10, 11]. However, despite this biological promise, the interaction between essential oils and restorative materials has not yet been sufficiently elucidated. Mouthwashes containing natural essential oil components are widely used to support mechanical plaque control and are particularly recommended for patients with high caries risk, limited oral hygiene capacity, or postoperative needs [12, 13]. However, some studies indicate that certain ingredients found in mouthwashes may negatively affect the color stability or surface properties of composite restorations [14–17]. It is clinically important to understand how new herbal formulations interact with composites, as color change can be affected by both intrinsic material factors and extrinsic exposures [15, 18–20].

Despite this increasing interest in essential oils, much of the existing literature focuses on only one or two essential oils under different conditions, limiting direct comparisons. Furthermore, essential oils from regionally specific plant species with distinct phytochemical profiles, such as sage (Salvia triloba), oregano (Origanum vulgare), lavender (Lavandula angustifolia), and myrtle (Myrtus communis), are underrepresented in studies evaluating their effects on dental materials. Considering the expanding use of essential-oil–containing mouthwashes in daily oral hygiene, it is important to determine whether such agents pose any risk to the structural or aesthetic stability of composite restorations while also assessing their antimicrobial effectiveness.

In this study, a plant-based mouthwash formulation was prepared using four different essential oils (S. triloba, O. vulgare, L. angustifolia, and M. communis) obtained from plant sources native to a specific Anatolian ecological region. The short-term and long-term physical effects of these formulations on a high-viscosity glass ionomer-based material and nanohybrid composite restorative materials were evaluated comparatively. In addition, the antimicrobial efficacy of the developed mouthwash formulations was analyzed, and biological efficacy and material compatibility were considered together. It was designed to investigate whether essential oil–based mouthwash formulations would exhibit significant antimicrobial activity without causing clinically unacceptable changes in the physical properties of oral biomaterials.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and plant essential oils

Tween 80, PEG 400, sodium saccharin and sodium benzoate were bought from Sigma-Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany). All essential oils were supplied by Goya Botanicals (Muğla, Turkey) and produced by pressure-free steam distillation from aerial plant parts. Botanical nomenclature, authorities, and accepted plant names were verified according to the World Flora Online (WFO) Plant List.

According to supplier documentation, S. triloba (Lamiaceae) plant material was collected from natural populations in the Bozburun region (100–200 m altitude) during August, O. vulgare (Lamiaceae) from conventionally cultivated plants in Fethiye, Muğla (1130 m altitude) during July, L. angustifolia (Lamiaceae) from traditionally cultivated plants in Burdur (1200 m altitude) during July, and M. communis (Myrtaceae) from naturally growing populations in the Gökova region, Muğla (300–600 m altitude) during August–September. According to supplier documentation, GC–MS analyses of the essential oils were performed at the Food Analysis Application and Research Center, Muğla Sıtkı Koçman University. The major constituents and their relative percentages are reported, and no contamination or adulteration was detected.

Herbal mouthwash preparation

Mouthwash solutions were prepared based on a previously described formulation [21]. Mouthwash solution was prepared by mixing 3 mL of Tween 80, 2 mL of PEG 400, 0.5 g of Na-saccharin and 0.1 g of Na-benzoate in sterile distilled water for a total volume of 100 mL (mouthwash without essential oil) at room temperature. For mouthwashes containing essential oils, the solutions were prepared by incorporating 1 mL of each essential oil (O. vulgare, L. angustifolia, M. communis, and S. triloba) into the base formulation and adjusting the total volume to 100 mL. The pH values of the prepared mouthwash formulations were measured using a calibrated digital pH meter at room temperature. The recorded pH values were as follows: M. communis (8.30), L. angustifolia (7.55), S. triloba (7.65), and O. vulgare (7.88).

In accordance with previously described in vitro immersion protocols indicating that 12 h of immersion corresponds to approximately one year of daily 2-minute mouthwash exposure, based on cumulative time calculation [22], the composite specimens in the present study were stored in each solution for 12 and 60 h to simulate 1- and 5-year clinical exposure, respectively. Two different immersion durations were therefore used to comparatively evaluate the short- and long-term effects of mouthwash exposure on restorative materials. Accordingly, a 12-hour immersion period was selected to represent short-term exposure, whereas a 60-hour immersion period was chosen to represent prolonged exposure. During the immersion period, the mouthwash solutions were renewed every 12 h.

Composite materials

This study included three restorative materials: Equia Forte, G-ænial™A’CHORD, and Omnichroma. Equia Forte (Shade: A2; Lot: 294E14, GC Europe, Leuven, Belgium) is a high-viscosity glass ionomer material frequently used in routine clinical applications. G-ænial™ A’CHORD (Shade: A2; Lot: 221020 A, GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) is a nanohybrid composite designed to offer balanced strength and polishability. Omnichroma (Lot: 092E82, Tokuyama Dental, Tokyo, Japan) is a single-shade nanohybrid composite that matches tooth color through structural color technology.

Disk-shaped specimens were prepared to evaluate color stability, and weight change [23]. For standardization, Teflon molds (8 mm in diameter and 2 mm thick) were used. Equia Forte capsules were mixed for 10 s in an amalgamator (YSDENT, Model: DB 338) and dispensed into the molds using a capsule applicator, then covered with a Mylar strip and a glass slide to obtain a flat surface. After an initial setting time of 2 min and 30 s, minimal surface finishing was performed, followed by application of Equia Forte Coat and light polymerization. The composites (G-ænial™ A’CHORD, and Omnichroma) were placed into the molds with a spatula, covered with a Mylar strip and a 1-mm glass slide, and then light-cured with an LED device (O-Star, Guilin Woodpecker Medical Instrument Co., Guangxi, China) at 1000 mW/cm² for 20 s.

To standardize the surface, all specimens were polished using the four-step OptiDisc system (Kerr Corp., Orange, CA, USA), progressing from coarse to fine discs. A new polishing disc was used for every five specimens. The composites were stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h before being used in the experiment. Following storage, weight was recorded using an analytical balance with a sensitivity of 0.0001 g. Color, and weight measurements were taken at baseline (year 0), year 1, and year 5.

Measurement procedures

All physical analyses were performed using n = 10 specimens for each experimental group.

Weight

Weight measurements were performed at three time points: baseline, year 1, and year 5 using a precision analytical scale. Weight measurements were performed using an electronic analytical balance (ISOLAB, Model 1.602.31.002; sensitivity: 0.001 g, Turkey) calibrated prior to measurements [24]. At each interval, samples were removed from their respective solutions, gently blotted, and allowed to dried for 10 min before recording the measurements.

Color

The mouthwash solutions used in the study were prepared immediately before the experiment. Each disc was placed in 20 mL of solution, and the solutions were replaced every 12 h. To determine color change, samples were removed from the solutions at the beginning, the end of year 1, and year 5 and dried for 10 min. Then, color measurements were performed using a spectrophotometer (VITA Easyshade V; VITA Zahnfabrik, Germany), and the device was calibrated regularly before and during each measurement. Color measurements were taken on a white background with the probe tip positioned at a 90° angle to the sample surface; three measurements were taken from each sample.

The measured color difference was calculated using the CIEDE2000 (ΔE₀₀) formula, and the clinically acceptable color change threshold was considered to be ΔE₀₀ = 1.8 [25]. Three separate measurements were taken from each sample and the averages of the L*, a* and b* values were calculated.

Antimicrobial activity

Mouthwash formulations supplemented with essential oils (O. vulgare, L. angustifolia, M. communis, and S. triloba) were used for the antimicrobial analysis.

Microorganisms

The following microorganisms were used as test organisms to evaluate the antimicrobial activity of the prepared mouthwash solutions: Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922, Enterococcus faecium NJ-1, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29,213, Streptococcus mutans ATCC 25,175, and Candida albicans ATCC 90,028. These microorganisms are maintained as stock cultures in the Biotechnology Laboratory, Inonu University (Malatya, Turkey).

Determination of the antimicrobial activity of the mouthwash containing plant extract

The antimicrobial activity of mouthwash solutions were determined by Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) method. Firstly, E. coli, E. faecium and S. aureus were grown on Mueller Hinton agar, S. mutans was grown on Brain Heart Infusion agar and C. albicans was grown on Sabouraud Dextrose agar for 24 h. Then, these cultures were used to prepare the microbial suspensions, whose turbidity was standardized to 0.5 McFarland Standard and after that, the bacteria and yeast suspensions were diluted 100 and 1000 times, respectively. 100 µL from these broth cultures was used to inoculate 96-well microplates containing 100 µL mouthwash solutions serially diluted with liquid media and they were incubated at 37 °C [26]. Wells of sterility control and growth control were also used. Gentamicin and fluconazole antibiotics were also used for testing and confirming the antibiotic susceptibility of these microorganisms. MIC is the lowest concentration of mouthwash or antibiotic that inhibits the microbial growth. MIC values were expressed as percentage concentrations (%) based on serial dilution of the mouthwash formulations, in accordance with previously reported studies [27].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (SPSS 20.0, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics (minimum, maximum, mean, and standard deviation) were calculated for all variables prior to inferential testing. The normal distribution of data was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was verified using Levene’s test. Weight change, and color difference (ΔE₀₀) values were analyzed separately for each restorative material (Equia Forte, G-ænial™ A’CHORD, and Omnichroma).

Comparisons among the essential-oil groups and across the evaluation periods were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When statistically significant differences were detected, Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test was applied to identify pairwise differences. To examine the effects of mouthwash type, time, and their interaction on the physical properties of the three restorative materials (Equia Forte, G-ænial™ A’CHORD, and Omnichroma), a two-way ANOVA was performed for each composite.A significance level of p < 0.05 was adopted for all analyses.

In addition, the adequacy of the sample size used in the study was evaluated by performing a power analysis. To assess the adequacy of the sample size in this study, a power analysis was performed using G*Power software (3.1.9.7, Düsseldorf, Germany). The effect size was considered moderate (f = 0.25), the significance level was set at α = 0.05, and the targeted statistical power was 80% (1 − β = 0.80). Calculations considering six different mouthwash groups and three measurement times showed that the statistical power obtained with 10 samples in each group was 0.82, exceeding the 80% power threshold accepted in the literature [28].

To provide a chemical basis for the observed biological effects of the herbal mouthwash formulations, the chemical composition of the essential oils used in the study was considered. The essential oil of L. angustifolia was dominated by linalyl acetate (35.94%) and linalool (22.10%), followed by β-trans-ocimene (7.25%), terpinen-4-ol (6.98%), and β-caryophyllene (4.63%). The essential oil of M. communis was characterized primarily by 1.8-Cineole (39.17%) and α-pinene (17.97%), with lower proportions of α-limonene (10.35%), linalool (8.20%), and α-terpineol (6.46%). In O. vulgare essential oil, carvacrol (45.79%) and thymol (19.22%) were the predominant components, followed by p-cymene (13.85%), γ-terpinene (7.79%), and β-bisabolene (1.87%). The essential oil of S. triloba was rich in 1.8-Cineole (47.65%), with notable amounts of β-caryophyllene (12.53%), β-pinene (8.10%), α-pinene (4.90%), and camphor (4.10%).

In addition to their effects on the physical properties of the restorative materials, the antimicrobial activity of the prepared herbal mouthwash formulations was evaluated using the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) method. All the mouthwash solutions containing plant extracts exhibited high antimicrobial activity on microorganisms, with MIC values between 0.02% and 0.78% (Table 1). It was reported that MIC values of commercially available mouthwashes are ranging from 0.02% to 50% [27]. While mouthwash solution without plant extract showed no antimicrobial activity (MIC value above 50%), the mouthwash solutions containing plant extract exhibited high antimicrobial activity against the microorganisms. Among the mouthwash solutions containing plant extract, the mouthwash containing O. vulgare had the highest antimicrobial activity against these pathogenic microorganisms. All of these prepared mouthwashes containing plant extract were effective antibacterial and antifungal solutions. Therefore, these plant extracts could be used as antimicrobial additives for mouthwash preparation.

Discussion

In recent years, people have been demanding naturally occurring antimicrobial and preservative products to replace chemicals in the products they use. In this in vitro study, the effects of four herbal essential-oil–containing mouthwashes (S. triloba, O. vulgare, L. angustifolia, M. communis) on the physical properties and color stability of three restorative materials (Equia Forte, G-ænial™ A’CHORD, Omnichroma) were evaluated in parallel with their antimicrobial activity. These findings are particularly relevant in the context of oral applications of plant-derived products, where biological efficacy and material compatibility must be considered together.

The effects of adding plant oils or extracts on the water absorption and weight change of restorative materials have been investigated in various studies. While no significant change was observed at low concentrations in composites, an increase in solubility was reported at high concentrations [29]. Plant additives added in powder form to glass ionomer cement materials did not alter water absorption [30]. In the present study, the statistically significant differences observed in the water group are thought to be due to the fact that glass ionomer cements absorb water faster and more than essential oil solutions due to their hydrophilic structure [31–33]. During the five-year evaluation period, no statistically significant weight variation was observed in any of the materials (Equia Forte, G-ænial™ A’CHORD, Omnichroma) or among the essential oils (O. vulgare, L. angustifolia, S. triloba, M. communis) (p > 0.05). Although small fluctuations were recorded in the mean values, these changes were not clinically or statistically significant. This finding demonstrates that essential oils used at low concentrations do not lead to a mass change sufficient to increase water absorption in either glass ionomers or resin-based composites. Furthermore, the weight stability recorded at all time points suggests that water absorption is primarily determined by the matrix composition and hydrophilic properties of the material, while plant additives have a limited effect on this parameter. The pH values of the formulations were close to neutral to slightly alkaline, which may have contributed to the limited effects observed on material properties.

Color stability was also evaluated as a separate parameter. In color difference assessments, the CIELAB (ΔE*ab) or CIEDE2000 (ΔE₀₀) formulas are frequently used in the literature. Paravina et al. (2015) reported that the CIEDE2000 system gives results closer to the color perception of the human eye and that the clinically acceptable threshold value is approximately ΔE₀₀ = 1.8 [25]. Therefore, in the present study, color change assessments were performed using the CIEDE2000 formula, and the results were compared with this acceptability limit stated in the literature. The literature shows that the color stability of composite restorations is affected by various factors such as the organic matrix structure of the material, the filler content, water absorption, and the chemical properties of external solutions. In vitro studies have reported that solutions containing plant or essential oils can increase color change in composites depending on their pH, viscosity, and phenolic structure [14, 15, 34].

Studies report that glass ionomer-based materials exhibit higher color change due to their hydrophilic structure, while nanohybrid composites demonstrate more stable optical performance with lower ΔE₀₀ values [23, 25, 35]. In our study, it was also found that Equia Forte material showed a more pronounced color change towards plant-based solutions. In the S. triloba group, the measured ΔE₀₀ values increased from 1.66 to 2.60, exceeding the clinically acceptable limit of 1.8 in some samples. Similarly, in the O. vulgare group, the relatively low average values (0.97) in year 1 increased to 1.74 in year 5, indicating that this oil may have an effect on the glass ionomer structure over time. In contrast, ΔE₀₀ values in the M. communis and water groups ranged from 1.13 to 1.58, remaining close to or below the clinical threshold. In G-ænial™ A’CHORD and Omnichroma materials, ΔE₀₀ values mostly remained below the acceptable limit of 1.8 in all solutions, with only slight increases observed in some plant-based solutions. This finding is consistent with previous in vitro studies indicating that immersion in plant-based solutions, such as herbal teas, can influence the color stability of resin composites, and that certain solutions, including rosehip tea, may result in clinically perceptible color changes [36]. Overall, it appears that some plant oils, such as S. triloba and O. vulgare, can cause more color change on glass ionomer-based materials; however, this effect remains limited in nanohybrid composites. These findings are also consistent with the literature reporting that glass ionomers are more sensitive to external solutions due to their hydrophilic structure.

Overall evaluation of the physical properties revealed material-dependent differences in response to the tested mouthwashes. Two-way analysis results show that each restorative material responds differently to herbal mouthwashes. For Equia Forte, while mouthwash type or time had no significant effect on color, both mouthwash type (p < 0.001) and time (p = 0.033) had a significant effect on weight change. In G-ænial™ A’CHORD no significant changes were detected in terms of color and weight. For Omnichroma, color change was significantly affected by the time factor (p = 0.025), while only mouthwash type was found to be significant in weight change (p = 0.010). These findings reveal that the responses of restorative materials to herbal mouthwash ingredients are material-specific, and that certain physical properties, particularly in resin-based materials, can be sensitive to the type of solution.

Although some variations in color and weight were observed across groups, not all differences reached statistical significance. This apparent divergence between descriptive findings and statistical outcomes may be explained by the distinction between statistical and clinical relevance. In particular, color changes were evaluated using the CIEDE2000 threshold (ΔE₀₀ = 1.8), which reflects perceptibility and clinical acceptability. Therefore, even in the absence of statistical significance, changes approaching or exceeding this threshold may still be considered clinically meaningful. This highlights the importance of interpreting the results not only based on p-values but also in the context of clinical thresholds and material behavior.

In addition to their effects on material properties, the antimicrobial activity of the prepared mouthwashes was also evaluated. While S. mutans is recognized as the primary causative agent of dental caries [37], other microorganisms have also been reported as potential contributors to oral diseases. For example, elevated counts of E. coli and S. aureus have been detected in diseased periodontal sites [38], and E. coli has also been identified in infected root canals [39]. In addition, C. albicans is a well-recognized opportunistic pathogen in the oral cavity [27], and E. faecium has been associated with periodontal conditions [40]. The antibacterial activity of herbal-based mouthwashes has also been reported in previous studies [41]. Therefore, in addition to S. mutans, these microorganisms were included to provide a broader evaluation of antimicrobial activity. In our study, the mouthwash containing S. triloba (sage) demonstrated a notable antimicrobial effect, particularly against S. mutans and C. albicans. These results are consistent with previous reports indicating that sage extract at a concentration of 50 mg/mL is effective against S. mutans, S. aureus, and Candida species [42], as well as with studies showing anticariogenic activity when sage powder is incorporated into glass-ionomer cement at concentrations of 0.75–1.25% [43]. The antimicrobial activity of sage is primarily attributed to its bioactive constituents such as camphor and 1,8-cineole, which are known to exert strong antibacterial and antifungal effects [44]. This attribution is consistent with the chemical profile of the sage essential oil used in the present formulation.Therefore, the sage-based mouthwash formulation in our study emerges as a potent and low-dose alternative against key oral pathogens such as S. mutans and C. albicans.

Similarly, O. vulgare essential oil exhibits pronounced anticariogenic and broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties. In the study by Yuan et al. (2023), the minimum inhibitory concentration of oregano essential oil against S. mutans was reported to be as low as 0.625 µL/mL [45]. The strong antimicrobial activity of oregano oil is attributed to its major constituents, carvacrol and thymol, which are known to disrupt bacterial cell membranes and inhibit membrane pump mechanisms [46]. The predominance of these compounds in the oregano essential oil used in the present formulation may explain the particularly strong antimicrobial activity observed. Consistent with these findings, in our study the oregano-containing mouthwash demonstrated remarkably low MIC values against S. mutans and C. albicans (0.02% for both), indicating a high level of efficacy even at minimal concentrations. Taken together, these results support that oregano essential oil is a potent natural agent with substantial antimicrobial potential in oral health applications.

L. angustifolia has also been evaluated for its antimicrobial effects. A study published in 2024 reported that an extract obtained from lavender flowers exhibited significant inhibitory activity against microorganisms such as C. albicans, S. mutans, and S. aureus; the minimum effective concentration for the pure extract was approximately 0.02 mg, and even at this level, it exhibited low cytotoxicity [47]. Consistent with these literature findings, in our study, a lavender-containing mouthwash showed low MIC value for C. albicans, suggesting that lavender may be particularly effective against yeast species at lower concentrations. This antifungal effect may be related to the characteristic constituents of lavender essential oil, particularly those commonly associated with antifungal activity. Thus, it appears that lavender essential oil has a remarkable antifungal potential, especially against Candida species, although it does not exhibit high-level bacterial activity.

Another plant evaluated in the present study was M. communis, which has been reported to exhibit antimicrobial activity against oral pathogens. A recent in vitro study reported that an ethanolic extract prepared from myrtle leaves exhibited a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 5 mg/mL and a minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) of 10 mg/mL against S. mutans, demonstrating an efficacy comparable to that of 0.2% chlorhexidine used as a positive control [48]. Another investigation found that myrtle essential oil had a MIC of approximately 1.56% (v/v) against S. mutans and about 3.13% against other oral streptococci [49]. The antimicrobial activity of myrtle oil has been attributed to its monoterpene constituents, particularly α-pinene, limonene, and 1,8-cineole [50]. This observation may be explained by the characteristic monoterpene-rich composition of the myrtle essential oil used in the formulation. Additionally, the inhibitory effects of Myrtus extracts on S. mutans have been linked to their phenolic and flavonoid components, suggesting that they may serve as potential natural alternatives for caries prevention when incorporated into suitable formulations [51]. In alignment with these findings, in our study this oil containing mouthwash exhibited MIC values of 0.20% against S. mutans, S. aureus, and E. faecium, and 0.05% against C. albicans, indicating substantial antimicrobial efficacy even at low concentrations. These results support the view that M. communis extract is a potent natural antimicrobial agent with strong activity against key oral pathogens.

Overall, while all four herbal ingredients used in our study exhibited varying levels of antimicrobial potential, O. vulgare formulation stood out with its low MIC value. L. angustifolia demonstrated significant antifungal activity, particularly against Candida species, exhibiting a more selective but effective microbial profile. The formulation containing M. communis provided significant inhibition against three different bacterial species and Candida at low concentrations, confirming its broad-spectrum effect reported in the literature. The fact that all herbal formulations showed activity at much lower concentrations compared to control mouthwashes suggests that these natural components exhibit notable antimicrobial activity at low concentrations. These findings indicate that herbal ingredients can offer powerful and multifaceted biological effects against the oral microbiota, providing a basis for further research and new product development.

In summary, this study demonstrates that low-concentration essential oil formulations can provide relevant antimicrobial activity without negatively impacting the structural and optical properties of restorative materials. The evaluated essential oils are region-specific botanical extracts obtained from a single ecological region in Anatolia and are evaluated for the first time in this context, providing a meaningful contribution to the existing literature. The low inhibitory concentrations observed for S. triloba, O. vulgare, L. angustifolia, and M. communis suggest that these plant-derived agents exhibit notable antimicrobial activity. However, the in vitro design of the study cannot fully reflect the complex conditions of the oral environment. One limitation of this study is that mechanical properties such as compressive strength, tensile strength, and water aging were not evaluated. Although color stability is a primary factor in the clinical acceptability of restorative materials, further studies including mechanical performance analyses would provide a more comprehensive assessment. Within these limitations, the results obtained demonstrate that the integration of selected essential oils into mouthwash formulations can allow for the simultaneous preservation of material stability, aesthetic performance, and antimicrobial efficacy, and can provide a basis for further experimental and clinical research.

Conclusion

The present findings indicate that essential oil–based herbal formulations may provide a balanced combination of antimicrobial efficacy and material compatibility under the conditions of the present study. At low concentrations, the essential oils were effective against oral pathogens and did not induce changes exceeding clinically acceptable limits in material stability. The observed variability in the responses of different restorative materials to herbal ingredients highlights the need for further investigation within the context of oral applications of plant-derived products.
