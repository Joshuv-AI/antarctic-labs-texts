Systems serology in cystic fibrosis: Anti-Pseudomonas IgG1 responses and reduced lung function
Authors: Shiping Lu, Kong Chen, Kejing Song, Joseph M. Pilewski, Bronwyn M. Gunn, Katie R. Poch, Noel M. Rysavy, Brian E. Vestal, Milene T. Saavedra, Jay K. Kolls
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryNearly one-half of patients with cystic fibrosis (CF) carry the homozygous F508del mutation in the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) gene but exhibit variable lung function phenotypes. How adaptive immunity influences their lung function remains unclear, particularly the serological antibody responses to antigens from mucoid Pseudomonas in sera from patients with CF with varying lung function. Sera from patients with CF with reduced lung function show higher anti-outer membrane protein I (OprI) immunoglobulin G1 (IgG1) titers and greater antibody-mediated complement deposition. Induction of anti-OprI antibody isotypes with complement activity enhances lung inflammation in preclinical mouse models. This enhanced inflammation is absent in immunized Rag2−/− mice and is transferrable to unimmunized mice through sera. In a CF cohort undergoing treatment with elexacaftor-tezacaftor-ivacaftor, the declination in anti-OprI IgG1 titers is associated with lung function improvement and reduced hospitalizations. These findings suggest that antibody responses to specific Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA) antigens worsen lung function in patients with CF.

Introduction

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is the most common life-threatening genetic disease in people of Northern European ancestry, caused by mutations in the CF transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) gene. Today, over 70,000 individuals are diagnosed with CF worldwide.1 The homozygous F508del mutation (deletion of phenylalanine at residue 508, ΔF508del) accounts for approximately 60% of CF cases and is associated with a more severe disease phenotype.2 Exocrine pancreatic insufficiency is present in nearly 99% of ΔF508del patients. In contrast, the lung function within this patient population displays considerable variability.3 Based on lung function, patients can be categorized into “mild” and “severe” groups at the age of 20.4 These data suggest that environmental stimuli or modifier genes may influence lung disease phenotype.

To better understand this heterogeneity in the pulmonary phenotype, multiple gene modifier studies have been conducted in CF subjects with ΔF508del mutation, and it has been found that several gene polymorphisms in several immune-related genes are associated with lung function as measured by the forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) or the age of first detectable pulmonary Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Pseudomonas, PA) infection.5,6,7,8 Among those, the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II (or human leukocyte antigen class II [HLAII]) is a hub gene. HLAII genes were highly enriched in pathways related to age of onset of persistent Pseudomonas infection.7 Corvol et al. also identified the HLAII locus that was significantly associated with lung function variability in a meta-analysis of 6,365 patients with CF, suggesting that adaptive immunity to infection may be a critical co-factor in CF lung disease.9 Consistent with humoral immune responses10 and T helper cell signatures in the CF airway,11 anti-Pseudomonas antibodies (to lipopolysaccharides, alginate, and crude total PA antigens) are associated with CF lung disease,12,13,14 but these studies did not identify specific PA antigens. Moreover, they were conducted prior to the widespread use of oral quinolones or inhaled anti-PA directed antibiotics that are in use today.

In the CF lung, Pseudomonas often converts to a mucoid alginate hyperproducing phenotype during chronic infection, which could induce dramatically different immune responses from planktonic acute Pseudomonas infection.15 Systems serology assays are designed to capture a comprehensive characterization of the host humoral immune response, and thus provide an in-depth understanding of the profiled antibodies, in part to discriminate between protective and non-protective serological responses.16 In this study, we assessed anti-Pseudomonas antibodies in recent (but premodulator) serum samples from chronically infected ΔF508del patients with the prespecified criteria of normal lung function (defined by percent predicted FEV1 or ppFEV1 >90%) or reduced lung function (defined by ppFEV1 <70%) to determine whether there are serological differences against mucoid PA or specific PA antigens between these two groups. Using flow cytometry, we found increased mucoid PA surface binding of serum immunoglobulin G (IgG) as well as higher IgG titers (particularly the complement activating IgG1 isotype) in people with CF (PwCF) with reduced lung function. Using immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS), we identified outer membrane protein I (OprI), which is upregulated in biofilm PA, as a target of these antibodies. Patients with reduced lung function had substantially higher anti-OprI IgG1 titers. This was also associated with a greater complement deposition of IgG-OprI immune complexes ex vivo. These findings were validated in an independent CF cohort. As age was a co-variate of reduced lung function in both CF cohorts, we first studied anti-PA and OprI antibodies in a mouse model. We immunized age-matched mice with OprI or inactivated whole PA and used adjuvants to manipulate the anti-OprI IgG isotypes. OprI-immunized mice that generated complement fixing IgG2c responses (analogous to human IgG1 in complement activation) showed greater pulmonary inflammatory responses when they were challenged with mucoid PA compared with unimmunized controls. This pathology was abrogated in immunized Rag2−/− mice, strongly implicating adaptive immunity in the model. Moreover, enhanced lung pathology could be transferred to unimmunized mice using sera from immunized mice with a dominant anti-OprI IgG2c response. We next studied a CF cohort before and after initiation of elexacaftor-tezacaftor-ivacaftor (ETI) and found a significant decline in anti-OprI IgG1 following treatment. Higher anti-OprI IgG1 titers were positively correlated with reduced ppFEV1 and increased hospitalizations after starting ETI treatment.

Together, our findings support that adaptive immune responses to specific PA antigens influence lung function in CF subjects with chronic infection. Moreover, our data suggest that assaying anti-OprI antibodies may be a useful assay to monitor PA serology after modulator therapy.

Results

Upregulated B cell signatures in CF lung epithelium during exacerbation

To understand adaptive immune responses in the airway of patients with CF, we previously performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analyses on bronchial brushing samples of pediatric patients with CF during clinical exacerbation to assess T cell immunity.11,17 Here, we reanalyzed these data for B cell signatures. Compared with non-CF control subjects, we found evidence of strongly upregulated B cell receptor signaling pathways in the CF specimens (Figure 1A). We also observed increased expression of class-switched Ig genes in the mucosa (Figure 1B), which included abundant constant regions of IgG1 isotype (e.g., Mcg marker, G1m marker). To determine the antigen specificity of B cells in the CF airways, we cloned B cells from lung explants from patients with end-stage CF who underwent lung transplantation through limiting dilution, which allowed them to proliferate and secret antigen-specific IgG.18 Compared with naive antigens, which only occupy a frequency of 0.005%–0.05% reactive B cells in the normal repertoire,19 we found that up to 7% of these B cells were producing Pseudomonas-specific IgG (Figure 1C). Enhanced antibody responses against Pseudomonas in sera from patients with reduced lung function

Although the above data implicated the presence of robust adaptive immune responses during CF exacerbation, it did not address whether some antibody responses are damaging versus protective, as patients that may have protective responses would not undergo clinical bronchoscopy. Therefore, we adopted a systems serology approach in archived samples with prespecified inclusion and exclusion criteria (STAR Methods; Table 1) to overcome the bias in clinical bronchial brushing samples. To examine the overall interaction between serum IgG from patients with CF and PA, we conducted assays with a CF mucoid isolate PA46, which represents the chronic infection stage. We first performed whole bacterial flow cytometry using sera from CF cohorts with normal lung function or reduced lung function (defined as ppFEV1 >90% or ppFEV1 <70%) and non-CF control subjects. We examined the four IgG isotypes to clarify which, if any, associates with lung function differences between the CF cohorts. Here, we found significantly greater IgG1 surface binding in the CF sera with reduced lung function compared with those with normal lung function (Figure 2A). Even though all subjects with CF exhibited significantly higher total IgG and IgG3 surface binding against mucoid PA46 compared with non-CF control subjects, there were no differences between CF cohorts other than the IgG1 isotype (Figures S1A and S1B). Further, we tested anti-PA46 antibody titer using purified whole outer membrane proteins from PA46 (OMP46) as antigens. To determine which antigen(s) in the OMP46 fraction contribute the most to serum IgG responses in CF, we conducted a protein IP-MS assay using CF sera and OMP46. We identified Pseudomonas outer membrane lipoprotein I (OprI) (Figure 2C), which is an 8.83 kDa protein anchored in the bacterial outer membrane regulating cell shape and outer membrane stability.20 Using recombinant OprI as the antigen, we observed that sera from the CF cohort with reduced lung function had substantially higher total anti-OprI IgG and IgG1 levels compared with those with normal lung function and non-CF control subjects (Figure 2D). Notably, we also observed a higher anti-OprI IgG4 level in the subjects with CF with normal lung function in contrast to subjects with CF with reduced lung function (Figure 2D). We next tested anti-OprI antibody titers using serial dilution. The titer of anti-OprI total IgG was higher in the two CF groups compared with non-CF control subjects, while it was significantly higher in individuals with CF with reduced lung function inside the CF population (Figure 2E). Further, we identified that patients with CF with reduced lung function have considerably higher anti-OprI IgG1 titers but lower anti-OprI IgG4 titers (Figure 2F). These differences in Pseudomonas-specific antibodies were not due to different total amounts of IgG in serum (Figure S1D). In addition, we assayed antibody responses against other important Pseudomonas membrane antigens to clarify the specificity of the OprI results such as OprF and OprG.21 No statistical differences in anti-OprF or anti-OprG IgG levels were observed between the two CF cohorts (Figure S1E).

Characterization of Fc-mediated effector function between subjects with CF and non-CF control subjects

One of the most crucial antibody functions is forming antigen-antibody complexes, which facilitate or inhibit phagocytosis via interacting with different Fc-domain receptors on phagocytes.22,23 Therefore, we conducted antibody-mediated phagocytosis assays using recombinant OprI and human monocytic THP1 cell lines, as well as primary human neutrophils.24 THP1 cells were incubated with preformed antibody-OprI-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) bead complexes. The phagocytic score was calculated using the mean fluorescent intensity (MFI) and the percentage of intracellular FITC+ beads by flow cytometry (Figure 3A). The intracellular nature of the phagocytized beads was confirmed using fluorescent microscopy (Figure S2A). Results supported significantly enhanced phagocytosis mediated by CF sera compared with non-CF sera. However, we observed no differences between the two CF cohorts (Figures 3B and S2B). Neutrophils express FcγRIIIB/CD16B Fc receptors, which are absent on macrophages/monocytes.22,25 Thus, we additionally examined antibody-mediated neutrophil phagocytosis in these cells by incubating primary non-CF neutrophils from three healthy donors with antibody-OprI-FITC beads (Figure 3C). Similarly, in this assay, no statistical differences were observed between two CF cohorts (Figures 3D and S2C). Furthermore, antibody-antigen complexes can activate the classical complement cascade, which could mediate protective or disease-enhancing effects.26 Therefore, we next conducted an antibody-dependent complement deposition (ADCD) assay in which OprI-conjugated fluorescent beads formed immune complexes with antibodies from the above three cohorts, respectively, and the amount of complement deposition was analyzed through anti-C3 staining (Figure 3E). Results showed significantly higher C3 deposition on beads in the two CF cohorts compared with non-CF control subjects. More importantly, the CF sera with reduced lung function had significantly higher complement deposition than that with healthy lung function (Figures 3F and S2D), consistent with its greater amount of anti-OprI IgG1 (Figure 2F). This assay indicated a considerably higher antibody-complement complex formation/deposition in the CF group with reduced lung function, which may explain their lower lung function.

Key antibody biophysical and functional profiling replication using an independent validation cohort

To verify previous key findings, for example significantly higher anti-OprI IgG1 titers and higher ADCD in CF sera with reduced lung function, we performed these analyses using an independent set of CF sera with the same inclusion and exclusion criteria (Table 1). Again, we found significantly higher anti-OprI IgG titers in the CF with reduced lung function group compared with the CF with normal lung function group (Figure 4A). Further isotype characterization revealed a considerably greater anti-OprI IgG1 titer in the CF with reduced lung function group, even though the two CF groups displayed a similar anti-OprI IgG4 titer in this cohort (Figure 4B). We then conducted similar ADCD assays in this cohort using OprI-conjugated beads. Consistently, CF sera from subjects with reduced lung function had significantly increased complement deposition (Figure 4C). Anti-OprI responses and lung pathology in murine PA infection models

In both cohorts, age was a confounder, and thus in order to match for age and understand whether there was a direct relationship between high serum antibodies against Pseudomonas (OMPs and/or OprI) and reduced lung function in patients with CF, we established age-matched murine PA immunization and infection models. Initially, we immunized Th1-biased B6 mice, Th2-prone BALB/c mice, and Rag2−/− mice (which lack B and T cell responses) with formalin-inactivated PA46 (FI-PA46) and heat-killed PA46 (HK-PA46) (Figure 5A). HK-PA46 immunization induced significantly higher levels of serum anti-OprI IgG1 and IgG2 subclasses (IgG2c in B6 mice and IgG2a in BALB/c mice27) compared with the unimmunized mice (Figures 5B and 5C). Both mouse strains demonstrated high levels of anti-OprI IgG2 antibodies after immunization (Figures 5B and 5C). Three days after PA46 infection, the FI-PA46-immunized B6 mice lost the greatest weight, whereas immunized BALB/c mice had a similar weight loss to the unimmunized controls. Rag2−/− mice lacking B and T cells had transient weight loss associated with PA46 inoculation but nearly fully recovered at day 3 (Figure 5D). To further clarify the relationship between immunization-induced pathology and anti-OprI antibody isotypes, we carefully modulated antibody isotype production using recombinant OprI with different adjuvants. B6 and BALB/c mice were vaccinated with OprI/LTA1 (enzymatic A1 domain of heat-labile toxin, a Th17 adjuvant)28 or OprI/Poly(I:C) (a Th1 adjuvant)29 twice to modulate antibody responses toward Th17 and Th1 types, respectively (Figure 5E). Even though a high level of anti-OprI IgG was induced in both mouse strains (Figure 5F), immunized B6 mice displayed a significantly greater ratio of anti-OprI IgG2 versus IgG1 titers compared with immunized BALB/c mice (Figure 5G). Moreover, immunized B6 mice lost significantly more body weight and exhibited greater lung inflammation than BALB/c mice who received the same immunization compounds (Figures 5H, S3B, and S3C). These results suggested a relationship between anti-OprI IgG2 dominant sera and immunization-induced pathology.

To investigate if antigen-specific antibodies directly mediated the above immunization-exacerbated pathology, we transferred pooled sera from OprI/LTA1- and FI-PA46-immunized mice to naive B6 mice 2 h before PA46 inoculation (Figure 5I). We confirmed the presence of anti-OprI IgG in both donor sera as well as the dominant IgG isotypes (Figure 5J). FI-PA46-immunized sera possessed a higher ratio of IgG2/IgG1 compared with OprI/LTA1-immunized sera (Figure 5K). Furthermore, we observed greater histological inflammation in FI/PA46-recipient mice, while OprI/LTA1-recipient mice showed restricted peribronchial inflammatory infiltration 3 days post-challenge (Figures 5L, S3D, and S3E). These results suggest that antibody isotypes are critical drivers of immunization-induced pathology and that anti-OprI IgG isotypes with strong complement activation are associated with greater pathology upon PA infection.

Effect of ETI on anti-OprI IgG1 antibody

Plasma samples were taken from a cohort of subjects with CF immediately pre- and on average 5 months post-initiation of ETI therapy (n = 20). Subjects had a mean ppFEV1 of 58% and a mean absolute improvement of 11% following ETI based upon spirometry, which was performed at the same time as plasma samples were taken (Table 2). Plasma concentrations of anti-Oprl IgG1 declined significantly after ETI treatment (Figures 6A and 6B; p = 0.002). The relationship between change in anti-OprI antibodies and change in FEV1 following ETI was analyzed. As a comparison, clinical microbiology data were analyzed from the same subjects in which the last quantitative cultures prior to starting ETI and the first quantitative cultures >1 month following ETI initiation were analyzed. Change in Pseudomonas counts (CFU/mL) were not significant (Figure 6C; p = 0.46). Figure 6D demonstrated the regression results for between- and within-subject associations for anti-Oprl IgG1 antibody. A significant association was identified for the within-subject changes in anti-OprI IgG1 antibody and FEV1 where a unit increase in optical density (O.D.) within an individual was associated with a drop of 17 percentage points of FEV1 (p = 0.01). A similar strategy was used for the number of hospitalizations, as seen in Figure 6D. Hospitalization counts were tallied based upon the number of hospitalizations in the year prior and in the year subsequent to starting ETI. A significant association existed between hospitalizations and within-subject changes in anti-OprI IgG1 where a unit increase in OprI antibody was associated with a 700% increase in the number of hospitalizations (p = 0.007). Bacterial counts pre- and post-ETI were then analyzed in identical models with pre- and post-ETI anti-Oprl IgG1, ppFEV1, and hospitalization counts, utilizing Pseudomonas counts on the log10 scale as predictors of clinical outcomes. In this analysis, within-subject associations were non-significant to predict FEV1 (p = 0.71) and hospitalization outcomes (p = 0.55) from total Pseudomonas burden (CFU/mL). However, there were significant positive correlations between Pseudomonas abundance and anti-Oprl IgG1. Discussion

The premise of this study was that there are strong genotype:phenotype relationships in CF regarding pancreatic sufficiency/insufficiency; however, the lung phenotype is quite variable. This could be due to either genetic or environmental factors. Prior gene modifier studies have been conducted in homozygous F508del individuals to understand the variability of lung function, and a large percentage of these genes are involved in immune function.9,30,31 Given the evidence of strong adaptive immunity in the CF lung,11,32,33 we hypothesized that the serological responses to PA might influence lung function in CF. To accomplish this, we used a systems serology approach in homozygous F508del individuals with documented prior PA exposure. We thoroughly profiled the antibody components and antibody-mediated effector functions in sera/plasma samples from the three clinical cohorts focusing on Pseudomonas (especially OprI)-specific activities. OprI is one of the major proteins on the Pseudomonas outer membrane and is expressed across all serotype strains.34 It serves as an essential structural protein on the surface, and the oprI mutant has been shown to produce more outer membrane vehicles (OMVs) than wild type due to the loss of peptidoglycan tethering.35 Consistent with a role in CF, Damron et al. demonstrated that OprI is upregulated in mucoid strains compared with non-mucoid strains.36 OprI has also been predicted to be highly antigenic,37 and we confirmed this with the presence of a high level of anti-OprI IgG antibodies in patients with CF. OprI has been considered a vaccine candidate, but our data suggest that the isotype generated could exacerbate disease in the context of mucoid biofilm Pseudomonas infection.

We found that both anti-OMP46 and anti-OprI IgG1 isotypes were significantly higher in subjects with CF with reduced lung function compared with those with normal lung function. However, we observed similar antibody-mediated phagocytosis activities using OprI-conjugated beads in THP1 monocytes and primary human neutrophils. Interestingly, we found a higher amount of anti-OprI IgG4 isotypes in the CF sera with normal lung function in our initial cohort (Figure 2D), even though this was not observed in our validation cohort. IgG4 lacks opsonophagocytic activity as well as the ability to activate the classical complement system, perhaps inhibiting the formation of the antigen-antibody immune complex.38 Another study has described higher anti-LPS IgG4 antibodies in patients with Pseudomonas-uncolonized CF than in patients with no evidence of colonization.39 Since IgG1 responses were elevated in subjects with CF with reduced lung function in both our discovery and validation cohorts, we assessed complement activation in these samples. Bead-based exogenous complement deposition assays reiterated the existence of a significantly higher antibody-complement complex in patients with reduced lung function than those with healthy lung function. These results are consistent with other studies in which high levels of circulating immune complexes (CICs) and complement activation were highly correlated to mortality in Pseudomonas-colonized patients.40,41 Our data are consistent with older data showing that anti-PA antibodies are associated with lung disease14 and more recent data demonstrating a role of complement proteins.42

It is interesting that age is a co-factor in both CF cohorts, which may reflect the duration of PA colonization or also the fact that older patients are more likely to have mucoid strains of PA as opposed to younger subjects. Due to this, we took two approaches to address this confounder. First, we assessed these antibodies after immunization using age-matched murine models, which have been used to study antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE) in prior studies.43,44,45 One model is the respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) ADE model, which was associated with the use of an FI vaccine against the RSV (FI-RSV) that was associated with enhanced disease severity when vaccinees subsequently contracted RSV. The RSV vaccine elicited low-avidity non-protective antibody responses with poor neutralization activity as well as a Th2-biased response that is characterized by immune complex formation, C3 activation in the bronchiolar epithelium, and pulmonary neutrophilia.43 We identified OprI as a potential antigen whose antibody response, instead of protection, exacerbated the pathology when mice were later infected with the mucoid PA46 strain. We also observed greater airway inflammation in mice after FI-PA46 immunization (to model FI-RSV) compared with unimmunized controls. Predominant anti-OprI IgG2 responses can be induced by immunization with recombinant OprI with either LTA1 or Poly(I:C) adjuvants in C57BL/6J, but not in BALB/c, mice, consistent with the disease severity in two mouse models. More importantly, immunized sera, containing an anti-OprI IgG2c dominant antibody response, could transfer this phenotype to unimmunized mice. The murine IgG2 isotype is functionally equivalent to human IgG1, mediating complement activation, phagocytosis, and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity.46 These results further demonstrated that anti-OprI antibody-related pathology is possibly induced by immune complex deposition and complement activation.

The second approach to control for the age cofounder was to study an adult CF cohort undergoing ETI treatment. In this cohort, while the changes in Pseudomonas quantities pre- and post-ETI were not significant, there were significant associations between pre-/post-ETI anti-Oprl IgG1 and corresponding changes in percent predicted FEV1 and hospitalization counts. Thus, changes in virulence as measured by host response to Pseudomonas Oprl were more informative than changes in sputum bacterial culture abundance. It is unclear why Pseudomonas quantities did not change in this cohort but might reflect the limitation of sputum to represent total lung bacterial biomass. Alternatively, ETI could have effects on the immune response independent of sputum bacterial load.

Overall, to state the relationship between anti-Pseudomonas antibodies (more precisely, anti-OprI antibody) and lung inflammation exacerbation in patients with CF, we fulfilled the four criteria of Koch’s postulates as follows: (1) we identified higher anti-OprI IgG/IgG1 titers, higher Pseudomonas surface binding, and higher antibody-mediated complement deposition in patients with CF with reduced lung function than in those with normal lung function. (2) We immunized C57BL/6J mice with recombinant OprI or FI-PA46. Those mice developed higher anti-OprI IgG2c (the equivalent of human IgG1) and more severe inflammation after PA46 challenge than the unimmunized ones. (3) This exacerbation phenotype can be transferred to infected unimmunized mice via injecting pooled sera from FI-PA46-immunized mice 2 h before infection. (4) We identified an anti-OprI IgG2c dominant isotype in the pooled sera from FI-PA46. Therefore, we predict a model that in homozygous F508del patients with CF, excessive anti-OprI IgG1 production is associated with reduced lung function, likely through antibody-mediated complement deposition in the lung tissue. Altogether, our results clearly stated that adaptive immunity is critically involved in the CF pathogenesis.

Limitations of the study

One limitation of this study is the lack of a C3−/− animal model to show the decreased immunization-induced pathology, as C3 is indispensable for immune protection against PA pneumonia that C3−/− mice have significantly higher mortality rate after PA infection.47,48 Another limitation is that the co-infection, length of PA infection, and medication history of the preliminary cohort and the validation cohorts are unknown. In two of the cohorts, the age of patients with ppFEV1 <70% was older than those with ppFEV1 >90%. Future studies should minimize these limitations by using age-matched individuals with a known medical history. We have attempted to strengthen clinical correlations in the third cohort by pairing samples, demonstrating the correlation between anti-OprI antibody and FEV1 and hospitalization changes using paired plasma before and after modulator treatment. However, this current dataset is limited to a single center cohort at present. Moreover, at present, our data are limited to CF. Whether similar immune responses play a role in other lung diseases associated with chronic airway infection such as primary ciliary dyskinesia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, non-CF bronchiectasis, or diffuse pan-bronchiolitis is unclear but is the subject of future research.

Experimental model and subject details

Human serum samples

For systems serology assays, ten healthy sera were purchased from Innovative Research as non-CF controls. All CF sera were obtained from Cystic Fibrosis Foundation (CFF) irrelevant to gender or race but with the following inclusion criteria: 1) Confirmed homozygous F508del mutation in CFTR gene; 2) Positive P. aeruginosa detection in ≥ 2 sputum culture; 3) Age>14-year-old; 4) Do not have uncontrolled cystic fibrosis-related diabetes or prednisone treatment > 5mg/day. 5) Percent predicted forced expired volume in 1 s (ppFEV1) >90% (referred to as “normal lung function”) or ppFEV1<70% (referred to as “reduced lung function”). CFFT is a nationwide organization which archives samples from all CF related projects and in compliance with all applicable federal regulations of the protection of human participants. We chose a more conservative FEV1 value (90% vs. 70%) to better separate two groups as an FEV1 of 80% indicates a normal lung function in the clinic standard.54 Demographic details are provided in Table 1.

For the ETI Cohort, plasma samples were collected from 20 CF subjects immediately before and on average 5 months of post ETI therapy initiation in the National Jewish Hospital (NJH). All participants provided written informed consent and this study was approved by NJH research ethics committee. Demographic details are provided in Table 2.

Bacteria strain

Mucoid P. aeruginosa strain 46 (PA46) were initially isolated from a chronically infected CF patient and generously provided by Dr. Jennifer Bomberger (University of Pittsburgh).55 Only 2nd - 5th generations of bacteria were used for experiments to maintain a stable mucoid phenotype.

Animals

Wild-type C57BL/6J (B6) and BALB/c mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor). C.129S6(B6)-Rag2tm1FwaN12 (constitutive Rag2−/−) mice on BALB/c background were purchased from Taconic (Hudson), No. 601. All animals were female and at 6–10 weeks of age when the experiments started. They were housed in a pathogen-free environment and kept food and water ad libitum. All procedures were previously approved by the Tulane University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Cell lines

The THP1 cell line (ATCC #TIB-202) is a human monocytic cell line derived from an acute monocytic leukemia infant. Cells were maintained at 2-8x105 cells/mL in RPMI 1640 medium, supplemented with streptomycin/penicillin antibiotics and 0.05mM β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco #31350010) in a 37°C humidified cell incubator and 5% CO2.

Primary cell culture

For the study of Pseudomonas-specific B cell detection by limiting dilution, B cells were purified from CF lung explant tissue obtained from the Human Airway Core at the University of Pittsburgh.

For the antibody-dependent neutrophil phagocytosis (ADNP) assay, fresh human peripheral blood was drawn into BD Vacutainer Plastic Blood Collection Tubes with Lithium Heparin during incubation. The erythrocytes were lysed by 10 volume times ACK lysing buffer (ThermoFisher #A1049201) at room temperature (RT) for 5min. 1mL of whole blood has an approximate yield of 3.5 million white blood cells (WBCs). WBCs were collected by centrifugation at 500g for 5 min at RT and washed by cold PBS twice. Cell pellets were adjusted to 5x105 cells/mL in R10 medium and ready to use.

Method details

Pseudomonas-specific B cell detection

Briefly, mononuclear cells from explant lung tissue were stained in dark for 30 min using PE-Cy7 mouse anti-human CD19 (Clone HIB19, BD#560728), APC goat anti-human serum IgA (Jackson ImmunoResearch #109-135-011), FITC mouse anti-human IgD (Clone IA6-2, BD# 555778) and PE donkey anti-human IgM (Jackson ImmunoResearch #709-116-073). Then the memory B cells, CD19+IgA−IgD-IgM- cells were sorted using FACS Aria III cell sorter and resuspended with 10% FBS containing 100 U/mL IL-2, 50 ng/mL IL-21 and 1 × 105/mL irradiated 3T3-hCD40L feeder cells, which allow them to proliferate and determine the antigen specificity of the secreted IgG.18 Wells were monitored for B cell proliferation and cell supernatants were harvested between days 12–14 and assayed for anti-PA IgG using ELISA with sonicated PAO1 as coat antigen.

Outer membrane protein (OMPs) extraction

OMP46 were isolated from PA46 as previously described.56 In brief, fresh PA46 were pelleted and lysed by a microfluidizer. OMPs in the supernatant were extracted by a sucrose gradient centrifugation and resuspended in sterile water. The protein concentration was measuring using a Pierce BCA protein assay kit (TheromoFisher) and qualified through Coomassie Blue to visualize the protein distribution.

Antigen identification and verification

OMP46 protein preparation was incubated with CF serum (two samples in each CF cohort), followed by SureBeads Protein A Magnetic Beads (Bio-Rad) following the manufacturer’s instruction. Briefly, the beads were incubated with serum samples and OMP46 sequentially, then the unbound protein fractions were washed out only the bound antigen were eluted and analyzed at the Proteomics Core Facility at Louisiana State University Health Science Center using Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS). Recombinant OprI protein was synthesized by GenScript USA, Inc. Recombinant OprF and OprG were provided by Dr.Lisa Morici (Tulane University).

Bacterial surface binding

Overnight cultured bacteria (OD600: 0.7–1.3) were incubated with diluted human sera for 1 hour at RT, followed by incubation with biotin-conjugated secondary antibody and streptavidin-PE directly for 30min under dark at RT. Fixed bacteria were measured using Cytek Aurora as positive percentage (%), normalized by OneComp compensation beads. All used antibodies were listed: Goat anti-human IgG BIOT (1:50 dilution, SouthernBiotech#2040-08); Mouse anti-human IgG1 Hinge BIOT (1:50 dilution, SouthernBiotech#9052-08); Mouse anti-human IgG2 Fc BIOT (1:50 dilution, SouthernBiotech#9070-08); Mouse anti-human IgG3 Hinge BIOT (1:50 dilution, SouthernBiotech#9210-08); Mouse anti-human IgG4 Fc BIOT (1:50 dilution, SouthernBiotech#9052-08); Streptavidin-PE (1:250 dilution, eBioscience #12-4317-87)

ELISA

Antibody titers in human and mouse sera were determined by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA). 100ng/well of each antigen in carbonate buffer was coated in 96-wells flat bottom microplates overnight at 4°C and blocked for 1hour at RT. Diluted serum samples were incubated for 2hours at RT, followed by incubation of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (See key resources table). The plates were washed twice by 200μL/well of PBS with 0.05% tween 20 between each incubation step. Eventually, the color reaction was developed using 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate and read using a spectrophotometer at an OD of 450nm. Absolute total amount of IgG was measured using a commercial human IgG ELISA kit (Invitrogen#88–50550).

Antibody-dependent neutrophil phagocytosis (ADNP) and antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP)

ADNP and ADCP assays were performed as previously described.24 Briefly, recombinant OprI was biotinylated and coupled to 1μm size yellow-green FluoSphere NeutrAvidin-labeled Microsphere (FITC channel, Invitrogen#F8776). Diluted serum samples were incubated with antigen-coupled beads for 2hours and then added 50,000 cells/well of fresh isolated white blood cells (WBCs, donated by healthy labmates) for 1hour at 37°C. Neutrophils were stained with Pacific Blue mouse anti-human CD66b (Clone G10F5, BioLegend#305112), Alexa Fluor 700 conjugated Mouse anti-human CD3 (Clone UCHT1, BD#557943) and APC-Cy7 conjugated Mouse anti-human CD14 (Clone MΦP-9, BD#557831) antibodies at 1:100 dilution and analyze by Cytek Aurora flow cytometry. A total of 15,000 cells were acquired, in which SSChigh, CD3−, CD14−, CD66+ cells represented as neutrophils. The phagocytic score was calculated as [(percentage of FITC+ neutrophils) × (MFI of the FITC+ neutrophils)]/10,000.

ADCP were measured using human monocyte THP1 cell line.57 2.5x104/well of THP1 cells were incubated with antibody-antigen-microsphere complex at 37°C for 4 and 18 hours. The phagocytized beads were confirmed by fluorescent microscopy and quantified using Cytek Aurora flow cytometry. The phagocytic score was calculated as [(percentage of FITC+ cells) × (MFI of the FITC+ cells)]/10,000.

Antibody-dependent complement deposition (ADCD)

Freshly biotinylated OprI was coupled to 1μm red fluorescent NeutrAvidin beads (PerCP-Cy5.5, Invitrogen#F8775). Human serum complements were inactivated at 56°C for 30min. Diluted serum was incubated with OprI-coupled beads for 2 hours at 37°C, followed by the addition of freshly reconstituted guinea pig complement (Cedarlane#CL4051). C3 deposition onto beads was detected by FITC conjugated anti-guinea pig complement (MP Biomedicals #0855385) at RT for 15min and read immediately on Cytek Aurora flow cytometry.58 The deposition score was calculated as (geometric MFI of FITC+ beads) x (%FITC beads in red beads).

Immunization experiments

For whole bacteria immunization experiments, we used heat-killed PA46 (HK-PA46) followed by washing twice using PBS and incubated at 65°C for 15min. To mimic prior models of antibody enhanced disease,43 we generated formalin-inactivated PA46 (FI-PA46) by fixing alive PA46 in 0.4% formalin for 72hours followed by thoroughly PBS washing. Heat-killed/formalin-inactivated immunogens are known to elicit relatively weak IgG responses,59 so we primed and boosted twice three weeks apart using HK-PA46 or FI-PA46 along with adjuvant Imject Alum (v/v = 2:1, Thermo Scientific). For OprI immunizations, 10μg of recombinant OprI was admixed with 10 μg of LTA128,49 or 50μg/mouse of Poly(I:C) HMW (InvivoGen).29 All mice were immunized two times (three weeks interval) using a volume of 100μL antigen/adjuvant mixture subcutaneously (s.c.). For serological responses, submandibular blood of above immunized mice was collected one week after the final booster. Then mice were challenged one week after bleeding with either 106 or 107 fresh PA46 inoculum intratracheally (i.t.)28 and sacrificed three days post-challenge for lung pathology examination. We lowered the inoculum from 107 to 106 in OprI immunization and adoptive transfer experiments to limit infection-induced inflammation and emphasize on antibody-mediated pathology.

Serum adoptive transfer

Blood was collected using terminal cardiac puncture immediately after carbon dioxide euthanasia to maximize the collection volume. 300μL of immunized sera were injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) 2 hours before PA46 challenge to unimmunized recipient mice. The same amount of naive mouse serum (Invitrogen#10410) was used as a control. The recipient mice were sacrificed three days after challenge.

Histology

Collected tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and sliced into 4μm. All histological slides were stained by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E staining) by GNO histology (New Orleans) and examined by a professional pathologist. Airway scoring system: score 0, no inflammation; score 1, mild and unconsolidated inflammation; score 2, mild inflammation with obvious cuffing around airway but under 25% of lung; score 3, severe inflammation with obvious bloody edema and debris in the airway; score 4, no discernable unaffected lung structure. Parenchymal scoring: 0, no inflammation; 0.5 a few local inflammatory cells; 1 some degree of inflammation; 2, obvious cuffing around airway and vasculature; 3, inflammation and debris in the airways; 4 no discernable unaffected lung structure. The final histology score was graphed by the sum of airway and parenchymal scoring.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism, version 9.2.0. Flower plots were performed by reshape2 and ggplot2 packages in R programming, version 1.3.

Specific analysis methods for other datasets were specified in the figure legends. Mann-Whitney test and Kruskal-Wallis test were used to compare two human groups and three human groups, respectively. Mouse results were analyzed by unpaired t test. Data were presented as Mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) in bar charts. ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.

In the analysis of CF clinical outcomes following ETI and anti-Oprl IgG1 values, the paired pre- and post-ETI OprI measurements were analyzed using a linear mixed model (via the lmerTest R package)52 with study visit as the only fixed effect and a random intercept for each subject. In order to analyze the relationship of anti-OprI IgG1 to ETI-specific clinical outcomes, the association of anti-OprI values with FEV1 and total number of hospitalizations in the year preceding and the year following ETI initiation was tested, with a generalized linear mixed model framework. Between- and within-subject effects were partitioned by transforming the anti-OprI O.D. values into two new variables: the mean value for each individual across their paired samples and the deviation from this mean at each visit,.60 For ppFEV1 a normal linear mixed model was fit using the two transformed OprI covariates as the only fixed effects while again using a random intercept for each subject. For the analysis of hospitalizations before and after ETI, a Poisson generalized linear mixed model was fit using the glmmADMB R package,53 to accommodate the count nature of this measure.
