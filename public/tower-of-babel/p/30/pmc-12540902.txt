Electric impedance tomography to monitor body positioning and chest physiotherapy in mechanically ventilated pediatric intensive care unit patients
Authors: Javier Trastoy-Quintela, Maria Ciutad-Celdran, Lluïsa Hernandez-Platero, Miquel Rausa-Garcia, Ines Mata, Maria Isabel Castillejo, Marti Pons-Odena
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo evaluate whether electrical impedance tomography (EIT), a non-invasive, bedside, radiation-free imaging tool that allows real-time ventilation monitoring, may help evaluate if children under mechanical ventilation exhibit a gravity-dependent ventilation phenomenon and positively respond to chest physiotherapy. Prospective observational study conducted from January 1st, 2023, to April 30th, 2024, in a 24-bed Pediatric Intensive Care Unit at a quaternary center. Children eligible were patients between one week and six years old who required mechanical ventilation for at least 24 h. One-hour episodes, involving postural changes or chest physiotherapy, were recorded and evaluated through the primary study outcome obtained by EIT, Delta Z, which surrogates mechanical ventilation tidal volume. The study included 25 patients and recorded 94 episodes. 69% of the patients were male. The median age and weight were 20 weeks and 5.5 kg. Acute bronchiolitis was the most common diagnosis upon admission (64%). 80% of the patients required invasive MV. 58% of the events involved PC, and 42% CPT. EIT showed an absence of a consistent gravity-dependent ventilation phenomenon, with high variability between patients. EIT-monitored CPT achieved a median 4% increase of Delta Z (p = 0.018). Children under mechanical ventilation may not follow a gravity-dependent ventilation phenomenon. EIT may provide a more precision-based approach to the delivery of effective CPT in pediatric patients.Keywords: Pulmonary ventilation, Physical therapy, Electric impedance, Intensive care units, Child, Diagnostic imagingSubject terms: Respiratory tract diseases, Paediatric research, Medical imaging

Introduction

Over 20% of children admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) require mechanical ventilation (MV)1. Prolonged admissions may be associated with an increased risk of infections or secondary lung injury. Thus, understanding how air distribution happens during ventilation and optimizing respiratory strategies through advanced monitoring tools is crucial to improve patients’ outcomes.

Electrical impedance tomography (EIT) is a non-invasive, radiation-free imaging tool that allows real-time regional lung aeration monitoring at the patient’s bedside2,3. EIT delivers small electrical currents through an electrode thoracic belt. Electrical impedance changes are converted into a 2D image and plethysmogram registry2–5 (Fig. 1).

EIT has been primarily implemented in adult and neonatal settings, focused on characterizing ventilation in MV patients3,6,7. On the other hand, despite pediatric EIT-based research running for the last three decades, evidence is still scarce, especially in critical care settings. Since the first reports of EIT monitoring in the PICUs8, some studies have explored the effect of body positioning4, mode of ventilation9,10 or periodic suctioning11 on ventilation distribution or studied the pendelluft phenomenon in children12.

Evidence obtained in adults and neonates suggests a predominant ventilation directed towards the right lung and dorsal areas4,5,13, the presence of a gravity-dependent ventilation phenomenon4,14, and a significant improvement of ventilation distribution after the implementation of EIT-guided CPT.

Considering that it remains unclear whether children under MV follow the same patterns, we have conducted this EIT-based prospective observational study that includes invasive and non-invasive MV patients.

Materials and methods

A single-center prospective observational study was conducted at the 28-bed Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) of Sant Joan de Déu Children´s Hospital in Barcelona, Spain, from January 1 st, 2023, to April 30th, 2024. The study PICU admits medical, surgical, trauma, and cardiac patients. The Hospital’s Institutional Review Board (CEIm Fundació Sant Joan de Déu with code PIC-91-25) approved the study and aligned with the Helsinki Consensus ethics. The study was designed following STROBE guidelines. Convenience recruitment was used.

Patients eligible for recruitment had to meet the following criteria: be between one week and six years old, be admitted due to respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation for an expected duration of at least 24 h, and have a mandatory informed consent signed by the patient’s parents or legal guardians. Children older than 6 years old were not considered recruitable due to a lack of proper electrode belts in our unit.

Exclusion criteria included significant thoracic deformities, skin lesions, the presence of chest tubes or surgical wounds that could interfere with the proper positioning of the electrodes-belt.

Objectives

Primary objective: To evaluate the effect of EIT-monitored PC on the regional ventilation of patients, to determine if there is a gravity-dependent ventilation phenomenon in children under mechanical ventilation.

Secondary objectives: To investigate how air is distributed globally in mechanically ventilated children in the PICU, considering the percentage of total air corresponding to specific lung regions. To evaluate the effectiveness of EIT-monitored CPT in mechanically ventilated children in the PICU.

Procedure

The patients in the study were continuously monitored using the TIMPEL® (São Paulo, Brazil) EIT system, which includes a thoracic electrodes belt, a flow sensor, and a monitor. The size of the electrode belt was determined based on the patient’s thoracic perimeter and was placed around the patient’s chest at the level of the T4-T5 vertebrae. Two models of EIT flow sensors were used: neonatal and pediatric, depending on the patient’s weight as it could cause rebreathing and/or significant dead space in small children (Neonatal flow sensor SERIES 3, Respironics®, and Pediatric/Adult flow sensor SERIES 3, Respironics®). Neonatal flow sensor was used, as reported by the manufacturer, in patients with an expected tidal volume (7 ml/kg ideal weight) below 100 ml. The EIT flow sensor was positioned on the Y-piece, near the patient’s endotracheal tube during invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV), or on the total face mask during non-invasive ventilation (NIV). The EIT monitor was placed next to the patient’s bed.

According to previous routine management of the unit, patients on IMV were ventilated with Servo U/I ventilators (Getinge ®, Gothenburg, Sweden) with Assisted/Controlled by Volume or Volume Support modes. Patients on NIV were ventilated with Pressure Control, Spontaneous/Timed, or Pressure Support modes, with Servo U/I ventilators or V60 (Philips, Respironics), depending on whether the weight was below or above 5 kg. Cuffed endotracheal tubes for IMV, Total-Face masks for NIV, and a head of bed raised 30 degrees were the standard of care. Transcutaneous CO2 or End Tidal CO2 were continuously monitored depending on the material availability.

One-hour episodes involving two respiratory interventions were recorded (PC, CPT). All the episodes were part of the unit’s routine management and were not performed exclusively for study purposes. Only one intervention was allowed in every episode recorded.

PC included four possible final positions: supine, prone, right lateral decubitus (RLD) and left lateral decubitus (LLD). The starting position was supine in all cases except when the goal position was supine, in which case the prone position was chosen.

CPT was conducted by respiratory physiotherapists in 30-minute sessions. The therapy was based on supine autogenic drainage combined with real-time 2D Image EIT feedback.

Main study outcomes

EIT monitoring provided the main study outcome, Delta Z. Delta Z consists of a tidal oscillation registered on the EIT plethysmogram caused by each respiratory cycle (Fig. 2), measured in Arbitrary Units. Delta Z was calculated as the difference between the maximum impedance and the minimum impedance from the plethysmogram. Delta Z is used as a surrogate of tidal volume. The variation of Delta Z was the outcome measure selected for EIT-guided CPT evaluation.

DZ: Delta Z, corresponding to each tidal oscillation of the plethysmogram over time, and that surrogates tidal volume. EELV: End Expiratory Lung Volume. PEEP; Positive End Expiratory Pressure.

On the other hand, to assess a possible gravity-dependent ventilation phenomenon in MV children, EIT ventilation maps were segmented into regions of interest (ROIs)(Fig. 2). Two equally sized ROIs were compared, in terms of percentage of total Delta Z as follows: on supine and prone positions, %ventral versus %dorsal; for lateral positions, % left versus % right. In addition, to help understand regional ventilation distribution, four layers of ROIs were compared in terms of percentage of total DeltaZ in an axial axis (%ventral, % mid ventral, % mid dorsal and % dorsal).

Data recorded

In addition to the main study outcomes obtained by EIT monitoring, data recorded included:

Patient’s demographics: age (weeks), weight (kg), gender (male/female), thoracic perimeter (cm), previous prematurity or respiratory chronic condition, previous home respiratory support (including oxygen therapy or mechanical ventilation) and previous admission to hospital due to a respiratory condition.

Clinical data: Heart rate (HR, bpm), respiratory rate (RR, brpm), SatO2 (%), CO2 (EtCO2 or transcutaneous CO2, which was the preferred CO2 monitoring system when available, as it avoids causing dead space).

Relevant chest radiological findings were obtained before every episode. Images were acquired from Point-of-Care Lung Ultrasound, Chest X-ray or Chest CT Scan. Chest X-ray, and CT Scan images were interpreted by Pediatric Radiologists. Ultrasound was assessed by pediatric intensivists with high-level training in POCUS (more than 5 years of experience in POCUS).

MV data: Type (NIV, IMV), Plateau Pressure (cmH2O), Tidal Volume (TV, expiratory, ml), FiO2 (%), PEEP/EPAP (cmH2O), P.Support/PIP/IPAP (cmH2O).

Since acute bronchiolitis represents the most frequent cause of MV initiation in the PICUs, data regarding this specific subgroup was obtained for analysis, apart from the data collected for the global sample.

Statistical analysis

The “SPSS® 24.0” program was used to analyze the variables statistically. Categorical variables were analyzed using absolute numbers, frequencies, and percentages. The mean and standard deviation were used for the description of quantitative variables, if they followed a normal distribution (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test p > 0.05). If they did not follow a normal distribution, the median and the interquartile range [25th percentile; 75th percentile] were used instead. The categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s test. Quantitative variables that did not follow a normal distribution (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test p > 0.05) were compared using non-parametric tests such as the U-Mann-Whitney test. All analyses were considered statistically significant at a 5% level of significance (p = 0.05), with a 95% confidence interval.

Results

Ninety patients met eligibility criteria during the study period. Ninety-four episodes were recorded from twenty-five patients enrolled. Subjects had a median age of 20 weeks (5–48) and a weight of 5.5 kg (3.9–10). 69% were male and 31% female. Acute bronchiolitis (64%), ARDS (12%) and pneumonia (8%) were the most frequent diagnoses at admission. 41% were preterm at birth, 25% had previous respiratory conditions and 25% had previous home respiratory support. 84% of patients received IMV, with a median Plateau pressure of 27 cmH20, FiO2 of 0.3, and PEEP of 8 cmH2O. PC was the most frequent type of intervention (58%), followed by CPT (42%). Basal EIT monitoring obtained from all the episodes showed a median ΔZ of 24 AU (IQR 21–39), with predominant ventilation towards the right lung (56% vs. 44% left lung) and dorsal areas (53% vs. 47% ventral areas). In addition, ventilation was seen to be centered-like (88% corresponding to midventral plus middorsal layers). Despite not meeting statistical significance (p = 0.433), dorsal contribution to ΔZ was lower in patients under IMV than those on NIV (50% vs. 57%). Sub-analysis of the acute bronchiolitis group also revealed predominant right lung (58%) and dorsal areas (54%) aeration, and a median ΔZ of 20 AU (16–26).

PC episodes revealed an absence of a consistent gravity-dependence ventilation phenomenon, with high variability interpatient: prone increased the percentage of dorsal contribution to global Z by a median of 16% (but with an IQR of 33); supine decreased dorsal contribution by a median of 6.5% (IQR 20); right and left lateral decubitus produced a median increase of 2% in the left lung (IQR 13) and right lung (IQR 15) ventilation respectively. No differences were observed between the bronchiolitis and non-bronchiolitis groups. No hemodynamic instability was reported in any of the PC episodes. A median increase in DeltaZ of 1 AU (IQR 3, p = 0.018) was observed at the end of CPT episodes, representing a 4% increase from the basal value. If considering only bronchiolitis patients, median Delta Z gain was 1.5 AU (7.5% increase from basal). However, clinical and MV data did not improve. No additional bolus of analgesics was required during CPT episodes.

As a safety incident, one patient enrolled did not complete the data recording process because a phenomenon of CO2 rebreathing happened with secondary severe hypercapnia. The patient weighed within the limit of the manufacturer’s indication of neonatal and pediatric flow sensors, and a pediatric one was placed. We consider it necessary to report, and it may be recommended that if a patient’s weight is near the limit, the neonatal EIT flow sensor should be selected. On the other hand, no significant CO2 increase due to dead space was observed in any of the other patients, with above 80% of the sample continuously monitored by transcutaneous CO2 or EtCO2. In addition, no skin lesions were detected during the study period secondary to EIT belt placement.

Discussion

In this study EIT monitoring enhances characterization of ventilation in mechanically ventilated children admitted to the PICU. A predominant right lung and dorsal regions aeration, an absence of a consistent gravity-dependence phenomenon and an improvement in Delta Z with EIT guided CPT were observed.

MV is a frequent therapy in critical care settings, and an optimal lung protective ventilation strategy is crucial for improving patient outcomes and minimizing morbidity and mortality5,14. Considering the vulnerability of children’s lungs and the potential benefits of EIT on MV optimization, our study has addressed some of the primary targets that could impact PICU patient’s outcomes through EIT monitoring.

Our first objective was to assess how regional aeration happens during MV in children. In the global sample, our results suggest that the pediatric population mirrors previous evidence from neonates and adults4,13,14, as air distribution was seen to be predominantly towards the right lung (56% vs. 44% left lung) and dorsal areas (53% vs. 47% ventral areas). In addition, ventilation was significantly centered-like, with 88% of ventilation corresponding to the center layers of the chest. Similar findings were obtained when analyzing exclusively acute bronchiolitis patients. Lastly, considering that, as Inany et al.10 and Nascimiento et al.9 reported in their studies, dorsal contribution to global aeration decreases when patients are under controlled modes of ventilation, we compared controlled IMV vs. NIV patients to assess this matter. Despite not meeting statistical significance, we also observed a tendency to decreased dorsal aeration in patients when on controlled modes, which may be related to loss of diaphragm contribution. The small NIV population in the study is probably the explanation for the lack of statistical significance.

Our second objective was to study how body positioning impacts ventilation in children under MV. Previous evidence suggests that both preterm neonates and adults follow a gravity-dependent ventilation phenomenon4,14–18, with increased ventilation towards the gravity-dependent lung4,14. On the other hand, evidence on children remains scarce. In non-ventilated healthy children between 6 months and 9 years, Lupton Smith19 et al. found an absence of a consistent gravity-dependent phenomenon, with high variability interpatient in the predominant lung aeration (dependent versus nondependent lung). Similar findings were reported by Van Dijk et al.20 in children admitted to the PICU undergoing an IMV liberation process. On the other hand, in a cohort of more than 200 ventilated neonates and infants monitored with EIT, Becher et al.4 reported that preterm neonates followed the classical adult pattern. Still, a challenging finding was observed in non-preterm neonates and infants, with predominant ventilation towards the gravity non-dependent lung and regions. Our findings suggest that globally, ventilated children in the PICU may show, similarly to non-ventilated healthy children, an absence of consistent gravity-dependent phenomenon. Despite our findings, an interesting fact to explore in further studies would be whether, considering the location of pleuropulmonary disease of ventilated children in the PICU, a gravity effect may be observed.

CPT represents a widely established treatment in intensive care units for airway secretions clearance or treatment of atelectasis21. Despite its regular application in critical care patients, evidence is still scarce on whether CPT in ventilated children improves lung volume or mechanics, patients’ length of stay or mortality. In this line, some authors have tried to answer this matter with new tools like EIT. Davies et al.22 first proposed EIT as a possible method to monitor CPT. Furthermore, McAlinder23 compared in an EIT-based study, in mechanically ventilated patients admitted to the PICU, CPT plus periodic suctioning versus suctioning alone, finding the CPT-suctioning combination to be more effective. In our study, CPT has shown a sustained modest increase in tidal volume (especially in the acute bronchiolitis subgroup of patients)measured by delta Z, but neither clinical nor MV mechanics improvement was observed, so the need for more studies to confirm the clinical value of CPT in mechanically ventilated children in the PICU.

Our study has several limitations. It is observational and single-center, with a relatively low number of patients and episodes included, which limits the strength of the results. In addition, the study sample was not homogeneous, as different ages, conditions at admission and lung disease locations were included amongst the study group. Despite these biases, since the majority of the sample were patients with acute bronchiolitis under IMV, and most of the study group had an age between 1 and 12 months, the effect of this bias may be limited.

The recruitment in our study was hindered by the availability of only one EIT monitor and one belt of each size in the unit. Monitoring was usually performed for a 48-hour period and as many episodes as possible were collected during the monitoring period. Thus, patients eligible during the study period but not recruited were mostly related to this limitation. During the second winter season, due to the implementation of Nirsevimab therapy in our region, the incidence of RSV infections in infants younger than one year reduced dramatically. In addition, we could not include patients older than six years old due to the lack of appropriate belt sizes during recruitment. While this bias may be limited since most of the patients requiring mechanical ventilation in the PICU have an age in the range of the included in our study, further studies are necessary to assess ventilation with EIT in children older than 6 years old.

In conclusion, EIT monitoring reveals that ventilation in children under MV admitted to the PICU, seems predominantly to the right lung and dorsal areas and lacks a consistent gravity- dependent ventilation phenomenon. On the other hand, further studies are required to assess the effectiveness of EIT-guided CPT. Finally, the impact of the flow sensor’s dead space should be monitored in children whose weight is close to the limits recommended by the manufacturer.
