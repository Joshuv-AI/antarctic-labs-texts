Identification of key genes associated with T cell exhaustion in HIV infection based on transcriptomic data
Authors: Yan Zhang, Yutao Yong, Wenqing Liu, Die Hu, Chengjie Ji, Chunyu Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHuman immunodeficiency virus (HIV) is a lentivirus belonging to the retrovirus subclass. HIV specifically targets and infects human CD4 + T lymphocytes, and T cell exhaustion (TEX) plays an important role in disease progression. However, the specific molecular mechanisms have not been fully elucidated. In this study, we explored the potential mechanisms by analyzing transcriptomic data and TEX-related genes, and screened novel markers closely associated with HIV neutralization and T cell function to assess TEX severity and guide precise immunotherapy.MethodsTranscriptomic data related to HIV were screened from public databases. Key genes associated with TEX were identified through machine learning, receiver operating characteristic curve analyses, and gene expression analyses, and a molecular regulatory network was constructed. Subsequently, methods such as localization analysis, gene set enrichment analysis, and immune infiltration analysis were employed to explore the underlying mechanisms. Finally, the mRNA expression of diagnostic genes in clinical samples was verified by RT-qPCR.ResultsEPSTI1 and MSC were identified as key genes. EPSTI1 was enriched in the Epstein–Barr virus infection pathway, whereas MSC was associated with base excision repair. MSC protein was mainly localized in the nucleus, while the subcellular localization of EPSTI1 remained unclear. Immune infiltration analysis revealed the significant differential infiltration of 12 cell types, among which MSC was associated with three cell types and EPSTI1 was associated with one cell type. RT-qPCR results further suggested that EPSTI1 expression was significantly elevated in HIV-infected individuals, and its upregulation was likely positively correlated with the severity of TEX. No statistically significant difference was observed in MSC expression between the two groups.ConclusionThis study indicated that EPSTI1 may serve as a potential functional gene during HIV infection. EPSTI1 may be a promising candidate biomarker for TEX in HIV infection and could act as a tentative therapeutic target for follow-up research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12985-026-03243-w.

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) is a retrovirus that attacks the human immune system and spreads widely among people. Its main transmission routes include blood-borne transmission, sexual transmission, and mother-to-child transmission. Once HIV invades the human body, it attacks the immune system, enters the thymus, and interferes with the normal differentiation, development, and maturation of immature T precursor cells. As HIV replicates, the count of CD4 + T cells decreases, while that of CD8 + T cells increases, resulting in an inverted CD4+/CD8 + ratio. This leads to a decline in immune function, disruption of immune homeostasis, and the development of acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) with immunodeficiency [1]. HIV infection remains a serious problem globally, including in China. As of June 30, 2024, the total number of existing HIV/AIDS cases in China totaled 1,329,127, including 740,787 HIV-infected individuals and 588,340 patients with AIDS, with most transmissions being sexual. Moreover, there are obvious trends of younger age and increasing aging in the reported population, and significant regional differences have been identified. Sichuan Province has the highest number of reported HIV cases [2]. Currently, HIV treatment mainly relies on antiretroviral drugs to reduce the viral load. After most infected individuals receive antiretroviral therapy (ART), their peripheral blood CD4 + T cell count rises, and the CD4/CD8 ratio gradually returns to normal. Meanwhile, the viral load can be reduced to below the detection limit, effectively suppressing viral replication and decreasing the risk of transmission. However, antiretroviral drugs cannot completely eliminate HIV from the body, and some patients continue to experience chronic inflammation, persistent immune activation, and incomplete immune reconstitution. One possible key factor contributing to this phenomenon is T cell exhaustion (TEX), which may hinder the body from mounting an effective virus-specific immune response and limit the control of viral latency and reactivation [3, 4]. TEX is the core mechanism of immune function failure induced by HIV infection, being characterized by the gradual loss of effector function and the persistent high expression of inhibitory receptors. Currently, immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-1, LAG3, TIGIT, and Tim-3 have become classic markers for evaluating TEX [5], but they carry limitations. First, inhibitory signal receptors such as PD-1 can also be transiently expressed on activated T cells, leading to relatively poor specificity between the “activated” and “exhausted” states of T cells [6]. Second, these markers only reflect the surface signals of T cells being “inhibited,” and they do not reflect the changes in the internal and metabolic functions of T cell proliferation. Meanwhile, these markers reflect the strength of inhibitory signals, and they have limited ability to distinguish the degree of exhaustion (reversible exhaustion in the early stage and irreversible exhaustion in the late stage) [7]. Therefore, it is of great significance to explore novel markers associated with T cell function, so as to assist in assessing TEX severity and optimizing immunotherapy strategies. Bioinformatics analysis can be applied to the diagnosis and treatment of various diseases. It can quickly process large samples using existing biological information, provide important information about diseases, and identify gene regulatory networks, key genes, and their related pathways. This approach can also be applied to research on TEX in HIV infection.

Using data from public databases, this study performed differential expression analysis, machine learning, receiver operating characteristic curve analysis and expression profile analysis to identify key genes associated with TEX in HIV infection. Additionally, we constructed a nomogram and a molecular regulatory network based on these key genes and then conducted gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), GeneMANIA network analysis, localization analysis, immune infiltration analysis, drug prediction, and disease association analysis to further explore the potential molecular mechanisms in patients with HIV.

Materials and methods

Data collection

HIV transcriptome datasets were acquired from the Gene Expression Omnibus database. The GSE37250 (GPL10558) dataset included expression data for 274 HIV-infected and 263 control whole-blood samples. Additionally, the GSE160184 (GPL18573) dataset included expression data for eight HIV-infected and four control peripheral blood monocyte samples. The background information of the two HIV-infected cohorts included in this study is described as follows: GSE160184: The study was explicitly titled “Transcriptional and functional reprogramming of innate immune cells during chronic HIV infection” and included adult HIV-positive individuals, confirming it as a chronic infection cohort. GSE37250: The infection stage was not directly annotated, but the study enrolled African adult HIV-positive individuals with co-infected tuberculosis. Based on the study background, these subjects were reasonably inferred to be chronic HIV infection cases. Furthermore, 683 TEX-related genes (TEX-RGs) were obtained from the literature.

Differential expression analysis

We combined the GSE37250 and GSE160184 datasets, and corrected batch effects using the removeBatch function in the limma package (v3.56.2). Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed before and after correction to ensure that sample clustering was driven by biological differences. The limma package (v3.56.2) was used to perform differential expression analysis on samples from the HIV-infected and control cohorts in the GSE37250 dataset (|log2FC| > 0.5, p < 0.05). Volcano plots were created via the ggplot2 package (v3.5.1), and the top 10 most markedly upregulated and downregulated genes were labeled. Subsequently, the expression trends of these genes were visualized using the ComplexHeatmap package (v2.16.0).

Identification and function analyses of candidate genes (CGs)

CGs were identified by intersecting differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with TEX-RGs, and the results were visualized using the ggvenn package. Then, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses (p < 0.01, q-value < 0.05) were performed using the clusterProfiler (v4.2.2) package. Additionally, a network of genes with an interaction score greater than 0.4 was built using the STRING website. The Cytoscape package (v3.10.2) was utilized to visualize this network.

Identification of feature genes

Feature genes were identified from CGs in GSE37250 via two machine learning algorithms. Specifically, CGs were analyzed by least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis using the glmnet package (v4.1-8) and by support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) analysis using the e1071 package (v1.7-16). Ultimately, the intersecting genes from these analyses were designated as feature genes via the ggvenn package.

Identification of key genes

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis in the GSE37250 dataset was executed via the pROC package (v1.18.5), and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for each key gene (1 > AUC > 0.75). Then, gene expression was compared between HIV and control specimens in both the GSE37250 and GSE160184 datasets. Genes with significant intergroup differences (p < 0.05, Wilcoxon test) and consistent expression trends across both datasets were identified as key genes. To evaluate the clinical diagnostic potential of key genes, a nomogram was developed using the rms package (v6.8-1). Additionally, calibration curves and ROC curves (1 > AUC > 0.75) were generated using the calibrate (v1.7.7) and pROC packages, respectively, to validate the accuracy of the nomogram. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test was utilized as an indicator of model fit in calibration curves to assess the dispersion between the predicted and true values (p > 0.05). To further verify whether the key genes were specifically involved in T cell exhaustion (TEX) rather than generalized immune activation, correlation analyses were performed between the key genes and T cell activation markers (CD69, HLA-DR) as well as terminal exhaustion markers (PDCD1, LAG3, TIGIT, HAVCR2) based on the GSE37250 dataset. In addition, to evaluate whether the key genes showed superior predictive efficacy compared with classic TEX markers, we compared the ROC curves, AUC values and decision curve analysis (DCA) results of the key genes with those of the classic exhaustion marker combination (PDCD1 + LAG3+TIGIT) in the GSE37250 dataset.

GSEA

To probe biological pathways linked to key genes, GSEA was conducted across all samples in the GSE37250 dataset, with the c2.cp.kegg.v7.5.1symbols.gmt genome, which was sourced from MSigDB, utilized as a reference. The psych package (v2.4.6.26) was adopted to perform Spearman correlation analysis between key genes and all other genes. Subsequently, the remaining genes were ranked in descending order by correlation coefficient. Subsequently, GSEA was performed to characterize the function of each key gene via the clusterProfiler package (|NES| > 1, p.adjust < 0.05). Finally, the outcomes were graphically presented using the ggplot2 package.

Gene set variation analysis (GSVA)

GSVA was performed on HIV-positive samples in the training set GSE37250. Initially, samples were divided into high and low expression groups according to the median expression level of each key gene. Then, GSVA was subsequently conducted via the GSVA package (v0.99.0), with the c2.cp.kegg.v7.5.1.symbols.gmt genome, which was retrieved from MSigDB, utilized as a reference. GSVA scoring was performed for each of the samples from the high and low expression cohorts of key genes. Finally, discrepancies in GSVA scores between the high and low expression groups were compared for each key gene via the limma package (|t| > 2, p < 0.05).

Function analyses of key genes

This study adopted the GeneMANIA database to predict the functions and pathways involving key genes, subsequently constructing a co-expression network. To elucidate the subcellular localization of key gene-encoded proteins, predictions were conducted using the UniProt online database. Subsequently, the results were graphically visualized to illustrate the specific cellular locations of these proteins. Additionally, to determine the chromosomal locations of key genes, the OmicCircos package (v1.38.0) was employed to analyze the key genes, and the genomic distribution of these genes was subsequently visualized.

Immune infiltration analysis

The infiltration levels of 22 immune cell types (excluding samples with p > 0.05) were analyzed using the CIBERSORT algorithm from the IOBR package (v0.99.0) and the pheatmap package (v1.0.12). Subsequently, immune cells exhibiting marked differences (p < 0.05) between the two cohorts were identified via the Wilcoxon test. Spearman correlation analysis was executed by the psych package to evaluate correlations among the differentially abundant immune cells and between these cells and key genes (|cor| > 0.3 and p < 0.05).

Regulation network analysis

Non-coding regulatory networks of key genes were developed to illustrate regulatory interactions among the key genes. Initially, the potential miRNAs of key genes were predicted using the miRWalk database. Subsequently, the potential transcription factors (TFs) of key genes were forecasted via the TRRUST database. Finally, the potential TF–key gene interaction network and key gene–potential miRNA network were constructed and visualized using the Cytoscape package.

Drug prediction and disease association analyses

To predict potential therapeutic potential small-molecule drugs for HIV, the DSigDB database was first adopted to identify drugs targeting key genes. Next, the drug–key gene interaction network was then constructed and depicted via the Cytoscape package. Additionally, to investigate the relationship between key genes and various diseases, the DisGeNET database (http://www.disgenet.org/) was utilized, and the association between key genes and other diseases was analyzed. Then, the disease–key gene interaction network was then constructed and visualized using the Cytoscape package.

Clinical sample collection, RNA extraction, and RT-qPCR

In total, 20 HIV-1 infected individuals (regardless of whether they were newly infected or previously infected) were included in this study, all from the Department of STD and AIDS of The People’s Hospital of Jianyang. All patients were diagnosed with HIV-1 infection by Western Blot or HIV-RNA nucleic acid testing. There were no active opportunistic infection, nor were there co-infections of hepatitis B (HBV) and hepatitis C (HCV). Another 20 healthy individuals who underwent physical examinations during the same period were selected as the healthy control group. All the subjects were over 18 years old, excluding those with co-infection of HBV and HCV and pregnant women.Peripheral venous blood was collected from all subjects for the isolation of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and the extraction of total RNA. In the RNA extraction and subsequent RT-qPCR experiments, one sample from the healthy control group was excluded due to unqualified RNA quality. Eventually, the samples included in the statistical analysis were 20 cases from the HIV infection group and 19 cases from the healthy control group.

To effectively distinguish severe immune exhaustion from non-exhaustion in clinical validation, all samples from the HIV-infected group were subjected to viral load testing and flow cytometric analysis. Patients with detectable viral loads were divided into an exhaustion group (CD4/CD8 ratio < 0.2 AND CD4 + T cell absolute count < 200 cells/µL) and a non-exhaustion group, with a healthy control group included for subgroup analysis. The CD4/CD8 ratio is an important indicator for evaluating incomplete immune reconstitution and T cell exhaustion in HIV-infected individuals. Previous studies have confirmed that when this ratio falls below 0.2, patients exhibit extremely severe immune dysregulation and markedly elevated risk of non-AIDS events (PMID:37639938).

The main instruments used in the experiment included the ABI QuantStudioTM 1 Plus real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR instrument, Eppendorf centrifuge 5428 R, Beijing Junyi electrophoresis apparatus and gel imaging system, and BioTek microvolume spectrophotometer. Key reagents included Qingzuo Total RNA extraction reagent from Qinke, Dalin Biosynthesis® III, RT SuperMix for qPCR reverse transcription kit, and ArtiCan CEO SYBR qPCR mixed amplification reagent. Operations were performed strictly following the relevant steps. For example, the obtained total RNA was purified using the chloroform–isopropanol method, reverse-transcribed into cDNA by the two-step method, and then amplified by the SYBR Green qPCR method. The reaction volume was 20 µl. The PCR program was set as follows: pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and extension at 60 °C for 20 s; and final extension at 72 °C for 20 s. The fluorescence values were detected during extension at 72 °C. The PCR primer sequences are listed in Table 1. The experimental results were normalized to GAPDH as the internal control gene. R1 was used as the control, and the relative expression was determined by the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

R software (v4.4.2, v4.5.1) and the ggplot2, ggpubr, and rstatix packages were used to create plots and analyze the data. Two-sided independent-samples t-tests were performed to assess the relative expression of EPSTI1 and MSC in the HIV-infected and healthy control groups. Both groups of data were subjected to the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene’s test, and they were found to conform to a normal distribution and display homogeneous variance. p < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference. HIV-infected individuals with positive viral loads were divided into an exhaustion group (< 0.2, n = 5) and a non-exhaustion group (≥ 0.2, n = 8) based on their CD4/CD8 ratios. A healthy control group (n = 19) was also included. Data were presented as the mean ± standard error. After normality and homogeneity of variance tests, the Kruskal–Wallis test and Dunn test (corrected Dunn method) were used for EPSTI1, and one-way analysis of variance and Tukey’s HSD test were used for post hoc comparisons of MSC among the three groups. p < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference.

Results

Identification and functional analysis of CGs

Before correction, PCA results showed severe batch separation between GSE37250 and GSE16018 samples. The two datasets formed completely independent clusters with no overlap, indicating significant batch effects. After correction using limma::removeBatchEffect, the samples from the two datasets were fully integrated and no longer formed separate clusters, effectively eliminating batch-related technical variations (Figs. 1A). The results demonstrated that the variation in the corrected data was mainly attributed to biological differences rather than technical batch artifacts, which helps improve the reliability of follow-up analyses to a certain extent. This study identified 224 DEGs in the GSE37250 dataset, including 152 upregulated and 72 downregulated genes (Figs. 1B-C, Supplementary Table 1). The intersection of these DEGs with 683 TEX-RGs yielded 54 CGs, accounting for 6.3% of all DEGs (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Table 2). GO enrichment analysis linked these genes to 122 biological process (BP) terms and 1 molecular function (MF) term (Fig. 1E). The enriched results were sorted by significance, and the remaining pathway enrichment results were presented in Supplementary Table 3. Regarding BP terms, CGs were significantly enriched for response to virus, defense response to virus. Concerning MF terms, CGs were enriched for double-stranded RNA binding. Finally, KEGG pathway analysis was performed, which revealed significant enrichment in 18 key pathways, including influenza A, hepatitis C, and coronavirus disease–COVID-19 (Fig. 1F, Supplementary Table 4). A protein–protein interaction network comprising the 54 CGs was constructed, and the resulting network graph, which contained interactions, was generated with these genes and 664 interactions (Fig. 1G).

Identification of key genes

LASSO regression analysis in GSE37250 recognized 22 genes at log(lambda.min) = − 5.2826 (Fig. 2A–B, Supplementary Table 5). SVM-RFE analysis was performed, achieving the highest accuracy and lowest error rate with 36 variables, leading to the identification of 36 SVM-RFE genes (Fig. 2C–D, Supplementary Table 6). Subsequently, an overlap analysis of the LASSO and SVM-RFE genes was performed, yielding 13 feature genes (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Table 7).

ROC analysis of these 13 genes revealed that nine genes (CCL5, MX1, IFI27, EPSTI1, LAG3, RTP4, TIGIT, MSC, and PDCD1) exhibited strong discriminatory ability (AUC > 0.75), and they were subsequently selected as candidate key genes (Fig. 3A). Among these, eight genes identified in the training set were also detected in the validation set, with EPSTI1 and MSC exhibiting significantly higher expression in HIV samples than in control specimens across both datasets (p < 0.05). These two genes exhibited consistent and significant expression differences, confirming them as key genes in HIV (Fig. 3B–C).

A predictive nomogram was developed to estimate the likelihood of HIV occurrence using gene scores, with higher total scores correlating with an increased probability of HIV (Fig. 4A). The nomogram’s predictive performance was further evaluated using calibration and ROC curves. The p-value of the calibration curve exceeded 0.05 (p = 0.104), and the AUC of 0.867 suggested relatively favorable predictive efficiency and potential diagnostic value (Fig. 4B–C). The DCA curve of the nomogram was located above that of the individual biomarkers, which may indicate that the nomogram could deliver relatively acceptable diagnostic performance (Fig. 4D). Results showed that EPSTI1 was significantly positively correlated with the terminal exhaustion markers HAVCR2 and LAG3, while no significant correlation was found with activation markers such as CD69 and HLA-DR. These findings indicated that EPSTI1 was specifically associated with T cell terminal exhaustion rather than a mere marker of non-specific immune activation (Fig. 4E). In addition, the EPSTI1 + MSC combination yielded an AUC value comparable to that of the classic marker panel and demonstrated favorable net clinical benefit in DCA analysis. Among all markers, LAG3 exhibited the highest predictive accuracy, followed by PDCD1 and MSC; EPSTI1 and TIGIT also showed stable predictive performance (Fig. 4F-G). These findings raise the possibility that the EPSTI1 + MSC signature may help to supplement the predictive capacity of classic TEX markers and might serve as a novel TEX-related signature distinct from conventional markers.

Enrichment and function analysis of key genes

The GSEA results demonstrated that EPSTI1 was predominantly enriched in 70 pathways, with Epstein–Barr virus infection among the most significantly enriched pathways (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Table 8). Similarly, MSC was primarily enriched in 66 pathways, including base excision repair, DNA replication, cell cycle, and neutrophil extracellular trap formation pathways (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Table 9). Furthermore, GSVA revealed 42 upregulated and 42 downregulated pathways, with the cytosolic DNA sensing pathway, steroid biosynthesis pathways being the top upregulated pathways and arachidonic acid metabolism, ribosome, and circadian rhythm mammal pathways being the top downregulated pathways (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Table 10). In addition, GeneMANIA analysis indicated that the key genes were involved in a co-expression network with 20 other genes, primarily enriched in processes such as response to virus, cellular response to type I interferon, and response to type I interferon (Fig. 5D). Moreover, subcellular localization analysis indicated that MSC predominantly localized to the nucleus, whereas the localization of EPSTI1 was not confirmed (Fig. 5E). Finally, genomic location analysis revealed that EPSTI1 was located on chromosome 13 and MSC was located on chromosome 8 (Fig. 5F). These results indicated that EPSTI1 and MSC may participate in antiviral responses and cellular activities, and provided preliminary clues for exploring their regulatory roles in HIV-related diseases.

Immune infiltration analysis

Analysis of immune cell infiltration revealed that memory B cells, M2 macrophages, and activated memory CD4 T cells were the most common immune cell types in both HIV and control samples (Fig. 6A). In addition, 12 types of immune cells exhibited marked disparities between the two cohorts (p < 0.05). These populations included memory B cells, plasma cells, and CD8 + T cells (Fig. 6B). Subsequently, correlation analyses were performed between the key genes and the differentially infiltrated immune cells, as well as among the differentially infiltrated immune cells. Significant correlations were found between resting NK cells and CD8 + T cells (r = 0.5117589, p < 0.05), monocytes and CD8 + T cells (r = − 0.5303441, p < 0.05), activated NK cells and resting NK cells (r = − 0.3999291, p < 0.05), and monocytes and resting NK cells (r = − 0.4300663, p < 0.05; Fig. 6C, Supplementary Table 11). Furthermore, MSC displayed significant correlations with CD8 + T cells (r = 0.5520924, p < 0.05), resting NK cells (r = 0.5572451, p < 0.05), and monocytes (r = − 0.4391858, p < 0.05). A significant correlation was also observed between EPSTI1 and naïve CD4 T cells (r = − 0.4512112, p < 0.05; Fig. 6D, Supplementary Table 12). These results reflected the complex correlation between the two candidate genes and immune cells during HIV infection, and offered references for exploring potential intervention targets for immune disorders.

Networks construction of key genes

To uncover the molecular regulatory mechanisms, the non-coding regulatory networks associated with key genes were systematically examined. First, the potential TFs of the key genes were predicted using the TRRUST database. In the key gene–potential TF regulatory network, MSC interacted with 11 potential TFs, while an interaction between EPSTI1 and KLF8 was observed (Fig. 7A). Subsequently, the potential miRNAs of the key genes were forecasted using the miRWalk database. In the key gene–potential miRNA pairs, EPSTI1 had interactions with 14 potential miRNAs, whereas MSC was found to interact with only one potential miRNA (miR-4428; Fig. 7B). Furthermore, drug predictive analysis was conducted, which predicted 29 MSC-targeting drugs and 1 EPSTI1-targeting drug (Fig. 7C). Finally, gene–disease correlation analysis was performed on key genes, revealing associations of EPSTI1 and MSC with carcinoma of breast, breast neoplasms, and disease caused by 2019 novel coronavirus (Fig. 7D). These findings collectively highlight the complex regulatory networks involving TFs, miRNAs, and drug interactions associated with the key genes, providing valuable insights into their roles in disease mechanisms and potential therapeutic applications.

Expression validation of diagnostic biomarkers by RT-qPCR

The mRNA expression of diagnostic biomarkers was detected by RT-qPCR in clinical samples from 19 HIV-infected individuals and 20 healthy controls. Compared with the findings in healthy controls, EPSTI1 was markedly upregulated in HIV-infected individuals, further validating the expression of diagnostic markers in the combined dataset from public databases. Although MSC exhibited a trend consistent with predictions in clinical samples, the difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 8A–B, Supplementary Table 13). Notably, MSC failed to reach statistical significance in RT-qPCR validation. On the one hand, it was a marginally differentially identified gene in the bioinformatic analysis, carrying a potential risk of false positivity. On the other hand, the relatively small clinical sample size in this study limited the power to detect its subtle expression differences. Further validation with a larger cohort is warranted.

Discussion

T cells play a crucial role in the process of HIV infection, influencing disease progression and the efficacy of antiviral therapy. A reduction in T cell counts and impairment of their function are usually accompanied by disease progression and poor clinical outcomes [8, 9]. A major manifestation of TEX is the functional impairment of CD8 + T cells. Meanwhile, CD4 + helper T cells also exhibit functional defects during HIV infection. Although ART can effectively reduce viral loads and control HIV infection, it cannot completely reverse TEX. Therefore, determining the mechanisms that cause metabolic dysfunction and ultimately lead to TEX is crucial for developing effective and biologically reasonable immunotherapeutic interventions to control the progression of HIV infection. Currently, classic exhaustion surface markers (such as PD-1) have been extensively studied and applied in clinical practice [4–5], but they cannot fully address clinical needs. Bioinformatics research is an effective strategy to identify new approaches.

Through differential expression analysis of the training set GSE37250, 224 DEGs associated with HIV infection were identified, including 152 upregulated genes and 72 downregulated genes, suggesting that HIV infection has a widespread impact on the gene expression profile of peripheral blood. To assess associations with TEX, the 224 DEGs were intersected with a set of 683 TEX-RGs, and 54 intersecting genes were obtained as CGs [10]. This screening strategy included HIV-related genes and helped select genes potentially associated with TEX. Among the 54 screened genes, several are known to be involved in the regulation of TEX, including immune checkpoint molecules such as PDCD1, LAG3, and TIGIT and effector molecules such as IFNG and CCL5 [11]. The presence of these molecules indicates that the screening method is reliable and meaningful. Functional enrichment analysis of the screening results illustrated that the 54 CGs were enriched in GO terms such as virus response and virus defense response, as well as in KEGG pathways such as the RIG-I-like receptor signaling pathway and NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, indicating that the CGs are largely involved in the host’s antiviral response, which is also a pathophysiological feature of HIV [12]. Multiple enriched pathways are related to innate immune recognition and signaling, suggesting that innate immunity is involved in the initiation and maintenance of TEX. These hypotheses are reflected in the protein–protein interaction network. The proteins encoded by the 54 CGs formed a dense network consisting of 664 interactions, suggesting that the 54 genes might also interact with each other to coordinately regulate TEX. Using the 54 CGs, LASSO regression and SVM-RFE were used to select feature genes. LASSO regression identified 22 feature genes, whereas SVM-RFE yielded 36 feature genes. After taking the intersection of the results from LASSO regression and SVM-RFE, 13 common feature genes were obtained: CCL5, MX1, IFI27, EPSTI1, IL7R, CCR7, LAG3, RTP4, LGALS3BP, TIGIT, MSC, IFNG, and PDCD1. In LASSO regression, the penalty term L1 was introduced to select variables and simplify the model, which can overcome the “high-dimensional small-sample” problem of gene expression data. The selected feature genes were relatively stable and interpretable [13]. SVM-RFE finally generated the optimal feature set for sample recognition and discrimination. This technique emphasizes the inter-relationships among features, and it can identify the features with the most discriminative power for classification [13]. Among the two algorithms, the LASSO method stabilizes variable selection, whereas SVM-RFE focuses more on the contribution of genes to sample classification. Taking the intersection of the results of the two algorithms can reduce the possibility of false positives of a single algorithm, screen key genes with discriminative significance, and increase reliability. The results of ROC curve analysis of 13 candidate key genes indicated that nine genes, namely CCL5, MX1, IFI27, EPSTI1, LAG3, RTP4, TIGIT, MSC, and PDCD1, had AUCs exceeding 0.75. Generally, AUCs of 0.7–0.9 have diagnostic value. Thus, the identified genes have good discriminatory effects, and they represent potential biomarkers for HIV infection. Subsequently, the expression of the nine genes was verified in the training set GSE37250 and the validation set GSE160184. The results demonstrated that all nine genes had between-group differences in expression in the training set, but in the validation set, only EPSTI1 and MSC exhibited consistent performance as observed in the training set. Using EPSTI1 and MSC, we further constructed a nomogram prediction model. A nomogram is a visual prediction model that can unify multiple prediction model indicators into a scoring pattern, which has great utility value in clinical auxiliary decision-making [14]. The nomogram model assigns scores to the expression of each gene. The sum of the two scores is the total score. Higher total scores indicate a higher possibility that an individual’s T cells are exhausted in HIV infection. The overall design concept of the nomogram is also based on the idea that combining multiple indicators is superior to using a single gene. In this study, three methods, namely calibration curve, ROC curve, and decision curve analyses, were comprehensively used to evaluate the predictive efficacy of the nomogram model. The results of calibration curve analysis revealed that the slope of the calibration curve was close to 1, and the p-value of the Hosmer–Lemeshow test was 0.104, indicating that there was no statistically significant difference between the predicted probability of this model and the actual observed probability of the calibration curve, confirming the model’s fit. The results of ROC curve analysis indicated that the AUC of the nomogram was 0.867, suggesting that the nomogram had a moderately high discriminatory ability for HIV infection and potential utility as a reference for diagnosis. In decision curve analysis, the curve of the nomogram model was higher than that of each individual biomarker, indicating that the combined detection of the two genes had a greater net clinical benefit than a single gene. After comprehensive analysis using various methods such as differential expression analysis, functional enrichment analysis, machine learning-based screening, ROC curve-based selection, and independent validation, we identified MSC and EPSTI1 as potential key genes associated with TEX in HIV infection and constructed a nomogram prediction model for exploratory purposes.

MSC is located at q13.3 on human chromosome 8, and it encodes a TF that influences muscle development, lymphocyte development, and differentiation. This TF functions of the B-cell receptor signaling pathway and functions in transcriptional repression [15]. MSC has been extensively studied in tumors, inflammatory bowel disease, asthma, and allergic diseases. Some studies have found that it regulates immune activation, cellular physiological metabolism, and viral replication [16]. However, it has not been linked to HIV infection and its related pathogenesis. Our study found that MSC may be a potential functional gene involved in HIV infection and the progression of TEX. Its expression is positively correlated with the abundance of CD8 + T cells and resting NK cells and negatively correlated with that of monocytes.This study indicates that MSC is mainly located in the cell nucleus and act as transcription factors to regulate DNA replication and cell cycle pathways. In HIV infection, the down-regulation of MSC expression directly weakens the DNA repair ability of HIV-specific CD8 + T cells, leading to cell proliferation arrest and mitochondrial function damage, and driving irreversible functional disorders of HIV-specific T cells at the molecular level, which is highly consistent with the clinical exhaustion phenotype. The main cause of CD8 + TEX in HIV infection is usually the loss of CD8 + T cell function, which occurs gradually, starting with the initial loss of proliferative ability, cytotoxic potential, and restricted IL-2 production [17, 18]. Low MSC expression is associated with reduced activity of pathways related to maintaining cell homeostasis and proliferative ability, such as “DNA replication” and “cell cycle.” This result is also consistent with the clinical phenotype of TEX, as exhausted T cells exhibit loss of mitochondrial function, loss of proliferative ability, and increased genomic instability [5]. Previous reports also indicated that MSC plays an important role in regulating the functions of T cells and B cells, thereby influencing the balance between immune tolerance and inflammation [19, 20]. Therefore, these results suggest that MSC might also be an important potential participant in HIV immunoregulation. Reduced MSC expression could be related to the decreased proliferative ability and impaired metabolic function of T cells (especially CD8 + T cells), making TEX irreversible.

EPSTI1, which encodes epithelial–stromal interaction protein 1, is a widely studied oncogene that was initially discovered in breast cancer stromal cells. This gene serves as an initiator of epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and it has been extensively investigated in various cancers such as gastric cancer, liver cancer, and colorectal cancer. Its high expression significantly induces tumor cell migration, invasion, and distant metastasis. Additionally, multiple bioinformatics analyses reported the appearance of EPSTI1 in the screening of autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus and Sjögren’s syndrome. This gene is highly expressed in B cells, macrophages, dendritic cells, and activated T cells, and it can enhance the inflammatory response [21, 22], Current studies have found that EPSTI1 is a downstream target of type I (α/β) and type II (γ) interferons, and it is strongly upregulated during immune activation and inflammation. It has been confirmed as an important interferon-stimulated gene [23, 24]. EPSTI1, as a broad-spectrum ISG downstream of type I/II interferons, is elevated in immune activation triggered by various infections such as influenza and EBV, which has raised doubts about its specificity. However, the clinical stratification results of this study showed that the expression of EPSTI1 in the HIV T-cell depletion group was significantly higher than that in the non-depletion group and the ordinary immune-activated samples, and was positively correlated with the degree of depletion. Pathway enrichment further confirmed that it was specifically enriched in the HIV-related RIG-I receptor signaling pathway rather than the generalized immune inflammatory pathway. The above results suggest that EPSTI1 is unlikely to be a general marker for non-specific immune activation, and may be a specific gene participating in T cell terminal exhaustion during HIV infection. Although the specific mechanism of the direct role of EPSTI1 in HIV remains unclear, existing research has found that EPSTI1 is abnormally upregulated in HIV-infected individuals [25]. Our research results indicate that EPSTI1 is a key gene in HIV infection and TEX, and its expression is significantly correlated with the number of CD4 + T cells. In HIV infection, the main manifestations of CD4 + TEX include a decrease in HIV-specific proliferative ability and a loss of the multifunctional cytokine response centered around IL-2 production [26, 27]. At the same time, PD-1 is also expressed, and its level is associated with disease progression, viral loads, and a decrease in CD4 + T cell counts [28]. EPSTI1 may continuously amplify the inflammatory signals after HIV infection by activating the RIG-I interferon signaling axis, specifically inhibit the multifactor secretion ability of HIV-specific T cells, promote the sustained high expression of inhibitory receptors (PD-1, LAG3), and eventually push T cells into the terminal exhaustion state. Rather than a simple immune activation response. Genes related to EPSTI1 are significantly enriched in pathways closely related to virus recognition and immune response, such as the RIG-I-like receptor signaling pathway and Epstein–Barr virus infection. These findings suggest that in HIV infection, EPSTI1 might participate in driving the differentiation of T cells into an exhausted state by promoting the production of inflammatory factors such as type I interferons. Based on the results of GSEA, the core biological functions of MSC and EPSTI1 were confirmed from the perspectives of pathways and functions. Our results determined that genes highly associated with MSC were significantly enriched in the base excision repair and DNA replication pathways. Existing studies indicate that these two pathways represent the molecular basis for T cell proliferation and the maintenance of long-term functional stability [29]. MSC downregulation might lead to insufficient activity of these maintenance pathways, greatly affecting T cell replication and oxidative stress, resulting in TEX, which is characterized by loss of proliferative capacity, functional impairment, and low survival rates [5, 30, 31]. Genes highly associated with EPSTI1 were markedly enriched in the RIG-I-like receptor signaling pathway and Epstein–Barr virus infection pathways. When HIV infects T cells (mainly CD4 + T cells), the intermediate products of viral replication in the cytoplasm can be recognized by the RIG-I-like receptors on T cells [32], which can exacerbate the pathological death of infected T cells and amplify the inhibitory signals of interferon on T cell function. Epstein–Barr virus infection promotes CD8 + TEX and CD4 + T cell dysfunction [33], similar to the pathways activated by HIV infection that lead to reduced T cell proliferation, cytokine secretion, and cytotoxicity [34].

We preliminarily explored the potential biological functions of MSC and EPSTI1 based on GSEA enrichment results from the aspects of pathways and functions. The GSEA results showed that EPSTI1 was significantly enriched in the RIG-I receptor signaling pathway and the EBV infection pathway. In HIV infection, RIG-I receptors can recognize viral replication intermediates, continuously activate interferon signals, directly inhibit the proliferation of HIV-specific CD4 + T cells and the secretion of IL-2, and induce T cell exhaustion. The EBV infection pathway works in synergy with HIV, further exacerbating the loss of CD8 + T cell function. MSC was enriched in base excision repair and DNA replication pathways. Low expression of MSC can lead to genomic instability and loss of proliferation ability of HIV-specific T cells, ultimately causing irreversible T cell dysfunction.The results revealed that the enrichment functions of genes highly correlated with MSC mainly involved base excision repair and DNA replication pathways. Previous studies reported that these two pathways represent the molecular basis for T cell proliferation and long-term functional stability [34]. MSC downregulation can lead to insufficient activity of these maintenance pathways, seriously affecting T cell replication and the metabolic process of oxidative stress, ultimately leading to typical features of TEX such as T cell proliferation defects, functional defects, and low survival rates [5, 30–31]. The enriched functions of genes highly correlated with EPSTI1 included the RIG-I-like receptor signaling pathway and Epstein–Barr virus infection pathway [32]. When T cells, mainly CD4 + T cells, are infected by HIV, the cytoplasmic replication intermediates of the virus can be recognized by RIG-I-like receptors on T cells, further exacerbating the pathological death of HIV-infected T cells and amplifying the signal of interferon-mediated inhibition of T cell function [33]. Epstein–Barr virus infection can also induce CD8 + TEX and CD4 + T cell functional defects, which is similar to the mechanism of T cell proliferation inhibition, reduction of cytokines, and cytotoxicity after HIV infection [34].

Our results demonstrated that the cytoplasmic DNA sensing pathway was upregulated in HIV-infected patients with high EPSTI1 expression. This gene is a key factor promoting the inflammatory and antiviral responses [35]. Previous studies reported that the continuous activation of DNA sensing pathways such as cGAS–STING leads to chronic immunosuppression and impaired T cell function in chronic HIV infection, thereby exacerbating TEX [36]. However, in the low MSC expression group in patients with HIV infection, metabolic and synthesis-related pathways such as ribosome biogenesis and circadian rhythm system were downregulated [37, 38]. Research has found that TEX in HIV infection is associated with disorders in ribosome biogenesis. mTORC1 and mTORC2 are involved in the regulation of ribosome synthesis during T cell activation. Impairment of mTORC1 can affect ribosome production, inhibit T cell proliferation, and exacerbate TEX [39].

Drugs were selected from DSigDB, and the candidate drugs for treating diseases were obtained from the intersection of the predicted drugs for each biomarker. Potential small-molecule chemical compounds were identified using hub genes. We identified 29 drugs predicted to target MSC and a single drug that potentially targets EPSTI1. These genes were both associated with breast cancer and the 2019 novel coronavirus disease. The core problems of the pathological conditions related to these diseases and HIV infection are long-term immune activation and acquired T cell dysfunction; thus, these findings provide new potential drugs for treating tumors and infection [40, 41].

In this study, RT-qPCR was used to further validate EPSTI1 and MSC expression in clinical samples. EPSTI1 expression was significantly increased in the HIV-infected group. The expression trend of MSC was consistent with the results predicted by the bioinformatics, but the difference was not statistically significant. Several reasons could explain this result. Specifically, bioinformatics data are obtained from high-throughput sequencing or microarray technology, which detect the overall transcription level of samples. RT-qPCR is a targeted amplification relative quantification method. The technical differences between these two techniques can lead to inconsistency in expression trends [42]. In addition, the use of peripheral blood mononuclear cells in clinical samples without cell sorting might dilute the target signal [43]. The bioinformatics results were derived from samples mainly obtained from European and American populations, whereas the clinical samples for validation were obtained from Chinese people. There are functional SNPs in the promoter of MSC, and the differences in allele frequencies among different populations can also lead to inconsistent results [44]. The core analysis of this study was performed using transcriptomic data from public databases. Limited by the characteristics of public database data, problems such as inconsistent sample sources, incomplete clinical information, and differences in detection platforms must be considered. These factors might cause biases in the results of differential expression analysis and pathway enrichment analysis [45–47]. Meanwhile, this study did not conduct multicenter validation on the data from the included public databases, nor did it use the clinical cohort data of our research team for cross-validation, which to some extent reduced the reliability and specificity of the bioinformatic analysis results [48, 49].

To clarify the relationships of the two genes with TEX, we divided the T cells of HIV-infected individuals into an exhaustion group (< 0.2) and a non-exhaustion group (≥ 0.2) based on the CD4/CD8 ratio [50]. EPSTI1 was expressed at a low level in the healthy control group. Its expression was higher in the non-exhaustion group than in the healthy control group, with an even higher difference (17-fold) observed in the exhaustion group. The differences among the three groups were significant, suggesting that EPSTI1 expression is positively correlated with the degree of TEX. The significant upregulation of EPSTI1 was already observed in the non-exhaustion group even when the CD4/CD8 ratio had not significantly decreased, indicating that EPSTI1 could be the first gene to be upregulated following TEX. EPSTI1 is a type I interferon-stimulated gene, and its upregulation indicates the presence of a continuously activated type I interferon signal, which is consistent with the results of our bioinformatics analysis of interferon-related gene expression signature demonstrating the enrichment of EPSTI1 in the RIG-I-like receptor pathway, suggesting that this gene might affect TEX through the interferon signaling pathway [32]. Among the three exhaustion groups, MSC was highest in the healthy control group, whereas no difference was noted between the non-exhausted and exhausted groups. This indicates that the reduction in MSC expression occurs in the early stage of HIV infection without further changes with disease progression, reflecting the early impairment of T cell proliferation potential.

In conclusion, we identified EPSTI1 and MSC as key genes associated with TEX-RGs through a transcriptome dataset and explored their possible mechanisms of action. Further validation with clinical samples confirmed that EPSTI1 was significantly upregulated in HIV-infected individuals and positively correlated with the degree of TEX. EPSTI1 may participate in the occurrence, progression and immune regulation of HIV infection. It may act as a candidate biomarker for evaluating TEX and a tentative target for follow-up immunotherapy research. The predicted regulatory network and potential targeted drugs provide new clues for HIV immunotherapy. This study has several limitations. First, the transcriptomic analysis was based on public datasets from European/American and African populations, while clinical samples were obtained from a Chinese population. Ethnic genetic differences may affect gene expression and weaken the generalizability of the conclusions. Additionally, the public datasets lacked key clinical information such as HIV infection stage and treatment history. Second, the clinical sample size was relatively small, and the subgroup classification of T cell exhaustion relied solely on the CD4/CD8 ratio without incorporating classic markers for comprehensive assessment, limiting the precision of subgroup classification. Third, the bioinformatics prediction results, including pathway enrichment, immune infiltration, and regulatory network analyses, were not validated by in vitro or in vivo experiments, and the causal relationship between candidate genes and T cell exhaustion remains unclear. Furthermore, the constructed nomogram model and identified targeted drugs have not undergone external validation. Finally, this was a cross-sectional study without longitudinal follow-up data, precluding the analysis of dynamic changes in gene expression during disease progression. Future multi-center studies and functional experiments are needed to further validate the findings of this study.
