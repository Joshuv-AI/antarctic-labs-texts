Drug-resistant gram-negative bacterial infections in children in the Oceania region: review of the epidemiology, antimicrobial availability, treatment, clinical trial and pharmacokinetic data, and key evidence gaps
Authors: Daniel K Yeoh, Alison Boast, Sophie CH Wen, Phoebe CM Williams, Lesley Voss, Brett Ritchie, Mona Mostaghim, Flora Lutui, Alice Lei, Tony Lai, Adam D Irwin, Kiera Harwood, Thomas Ewin, Celia Cooper, Emma Best, Sarah Bannister, Amanda Gwee, KIDS DOSE group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryGram-negative bacterial infections remain a major cause of morbidity and mortality in children and neonates globally, compounded by the rise of antimicrobial resistance. Barriers to paediatric antibiotic licencing lead to reduced availability of potentially effective agents for treatment. For children and neonates in the Oceania region, specific challenges remain including a paucity of surveillance data on local rates of antimicrobial resistance, and lack of availability of newer, more costly agents. In this review, we summarise available regional epidemiological data on the WHO priority pathogens: extended spectrum B-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacterales, carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE), carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB). Paediatric clinical trial and pharmakinetic data for the antimicrobials recommended for treatment of these pathogens are reviewed, and paediatric knowledge gaps identified to inform future collaborative research.Keywords: Gram-negative, Drug-resistant, ESBL, Carbapenem-resistant, Paediatric, Children

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a major global threat to human health, with an estimated 4·95 million deaths associated with bacterial AMR in 2019.1 Gram-negative bacterial infections remain a leading cause of death globally in adults and children, particularly neonates, the latter in whom many newer antimicrobials are still not licensed.2,3 To prioritise research and development to combat AMR, the World Health Organization (WHO) has defined a list of antibiotic-resistant "priority pathogens" that pose the greatest threat globally.4 Among the critical and high priority pathogens are four multi-drug resistant (MDR) Gram-negative bacteria (GNB) that are important pathogens in children and neonates: extended spectrum B-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacterales, carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE), carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB).2

Although progress has been made in recent years in treating MDR Gram-negative bacterial infections, with the development of new antibiotics (predominantly licensed for use in adults) and the re-purposing of older agents, specific paediatric challenges remain. There have been few paediatric clinical trials to inform optimal treatment of these infections.5 Furthermore, within the Oceania region, which includes Australia, Aotearoa New Zealand (AoNZ), and the Pacific Island Countries and Territories (PICTs), only a limited number of treatments have been regionally approved for use in children.3 A lack of child-friendly formulations of the recommended oral agents further hinders management of childhood infections, particularly increasingly prevalent gram-negative infections.6,7

This review aims to summarise the epidemiology, treatment recommendations, and paediatric data published for the key antimicrobial agents used for infections caused by ESBL-producing Enterobacterales, CRE, carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa, and CRAB with a particular focus on bacteraemia, highlighting regional challenges specific to the Oceania region (see supplementary material for the search methodology and search strategy).

What is the epidemiology of drug-resistant gram-negative infections in children in the Oceania region?

In children, MDR Gram-negative pathogens can cause urinary tract (UTI) and intra-abdominal infections, pneumonia, bloodstream infection (BSI), and meningitis, the latter most commonly in neonates. Carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative infections are predominantly nosocomial, with risk factors including prolonged hospitalisation, admission to intensive care, the presence of medical devices, mechanical ventilation, and previous antibiotic exposure.8 In low- and middle-income countries, invasive neonatal infections and sepsis are now commonly caused by MDR Gram-negative pathogens from as early as the first day of life, with high associated morbidity and mortality.9

Extended spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacterales

There is variability in regional reporting for ESBL-producing Enterobacterales, likely reflective of differences in testing practices, with some studies utilising resistance to third-generation cephalosporins (G3CR) as a surrogate measure of ESBL-production. Notably, in addition to ESBLs, other mechanisms can account for G3CR (e.g. AmpC cephalosporinases and carbapenemases), whilst the latter focus of this review is treatment of ESBL-producing organisms. In Australia, 12% (99/800) of Enterobacterales bloodstream isolates from 38 laboratories between January 2020 and December 2021 were ESBL-producing; this proportion was highest in Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates (26/124, 21%).10 Similarly, in a prospective Paediatric Active Enhanced Disease Surveillance (PAEDS) study of Gram-negative BSI in hospitalised Australian children from 2019 to 2021, 22% (138/630) of Enterobacterales isolates displayed G3CR, of which 47% (65/138) were confirmed to harbour ESBL genes.11 Longitudinal data across the Australian Group on Antimicrobial Resistance for Kids (AGAR-Kids) demonstrate an increase in the proportion of Enterobacterales with G3CR from 19% (2013–2015) to 24% (2019–2021).12 A rising incidence of ESBL-producing Gram-negative infections has also been observed for Escherichia coli and K. pneumoniae in AoNZ.13 In the Auckland region, 8% (307/3787) of E. coli, and 8% (53/684) of K. pneumoniae isolates were identified as ESBL in 2022. Notably, ESBL detection was equal or higher amongst isolates from children aged <15 years compared with those aged >15 years.14

Two recent systematic reviews highlighted the lack of paediatric data on the epidemiology of drug-resistant Gram-negative infections in the PICTs region, both in neonates11 and children.15 Retrospective data from hospitalised children in Tonga showed a four-fold increase in the proportion of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales between 2018 and 2022 (10%–39%).16 Microbiological surveillance data from adult and paediatric samples across the Pacific Island Countries and Territories (PICTs) revealed varying proportions of Enterobacterales with G3CR (i.e. potentially ESBL-producing). For E. coli, the resistance rates were 6% in the Cook Islands, 9% in Kiribati, 13% in Samoa, 10% in Tonga, and 14% in Vanuatu. For K. pneumoniae, the resistance rates were 10% in the Cook Islands, 18% in Kiribati, 28% in Samoa, 26% in Tonga, and 26% in Vanuatu.17,18 Confirmatory testing for ESBL-production was not routinely performed. In astudy from Colonial War Memorial Hospital (CWMH), in Fiji, the proportion of ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae and E. coli increased markedly between 2019 and 2022 (30%–43% and 10%–23%, respectively).19

Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE)

Available data suggest that CRE remains uncommon in children in the Oceania region. However, there is a notable paucity of paediatric data from PICTs.11,15 Of 792 AGAR-Kids Enterobacterales isolates analysed by from 2020 to 2021, only two (0·3%) were carbapenem-resistant.10 Consistent with this, only 0·5% (3/630) of Enterobacterales BSI in the PAEDS surveillance study demonstrated phenotypic meropenem resistance.11 Resistance gene mutations were detected in 9 isolates which did not exhibit phenotypic resistance, IMP-4 being the most common (44%, 4/9).11 Unpublished data from the only tertiary paediatric referral centre in AoNZ showed no resistance to meropenem amongst E. coli (n = 384) and K. pneumoniae (n = 62) isolates from children <15 years in 2022. However, concerningly, in the most recent microbiological surveillance data from paediatric and adult isolates in Australia20 and AoNZ,21 carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (CPE) have increased significantly. In Australia, the predominant carbapenemase types are IMP, NDM, followed by OXA-48-like, accounting for >90% of CPE gene mutations,20,22 in contrast NDM carbapenemases (77%) are most frequently identified in AoNZ, followed by OXA-48-like.21 There are limited regional data to date reporting susceptibility of CRE isolates to novel BLBLI combinations and cefiderocol.22

Although there are little paediatric specific data from the PICTs region, laboratory susceptibility data show marked variability in the proportion of CRE amongst selectively tested isolates: 6% (3/511) of E. coli and 3% (10/381) of K. pneumoniae isolates in Samoa; 15% (17/117) of E. coli and 18% (7/39) of K. pneumoniae isolates in Kiribati18; and 0/8 and 0/20 Enterobacterales in the Cook Islands and Tonga, respectively. In the CWMH study in Fiji, 9% of selectively tested E. coli isolates (n = 1231) were meropenem resistant, with a rapid increase observed following emergence in 2021.19 There remains a paucity of sequencing data documenting genetic mechanisms underlying gram-negative resistance in the PICTs.18

Carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa

In Australia, 3% (2/61) of P. aeruginosa BSI isolates from children were carbapenem-resistant and 5% multidrug-resistant between 2020–202110, similar to proportions from longitudinal data (2013–2021).12 Concerningly, although only 6% (5/86) of P. aeruginosa isolates from hospitalised Australian children in the PAEDS study were resistant to meropenem, resistance to newer beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor (BLBLI) antibiotics was observed at higher frequencies: 19% (16/86) for ceftaolozane/tazobactam and 17% (15/86) for ceftazidime/avibactam.11 Amongst P. aeruginosa isolates from the sole tertiary paediatric referral centre in AoNZ in 2022, 2% (2/97) demonstrated resistance to meropenem. For PICTs, the proportion of selectively tested P. aeruginosa isolates demonstrating carbapenem resistance from general laboratory susceptibility data varied: 2% in the Cook Islands, 7% in Kiribati, 8% in Samoa, and 20% in Vanuatu,17,18 and 66% in Fiji.19

Carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii (CRAB)

None of the 41 Acinetobacter spp. isolates from AGAR-kids in Australia (2020–2021) exhibited carbapenem resistance according to EUCAST criteria,10 while 15% (2/13) were found to be phenotypically resistant to carbapenems in the PAED surveillance study11 with the majority of infections occurring in children with pre-existing comorbidities. Data from the national alert system for critical antimicrobial resistances from Australia (CARAlert), indicate a consistent increase in the number of CRAB isolates (combining adult and paediatric cases) reported from 2019 to 2023.20

Although there is little paediatric-specific data in AoNZ and the PICTs, laboratory surveillance data are concerning with a recent increase in the detection of CRAB, including evidence of outbreaks linked to travel-related transmission across territories within the Oceania region.23 In a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) outbreak in Fiji in 2017, 14 cases of invasive CRAB infections were identified.24 An 86% (12/14) case fatality rate was observed during this outbreak, illustrating the potentially devastating impact of this pathogen in the NICU setting.

Extended spectrum B-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacterales

Carbapenems are the agent of choice for targeted therapy of infections outside the urinary tract caused by ESBL-producing Enterobacterales.25,26,28 Meropenem and imipenem-cilastatin are the preferred carbapenems for septic shock, with ertapenem an alternative in the absence of shock.25,26 For initial treatment of complicated UTI without septic shock, intravenous (IV) fosfomycin or an aminoglycoside (for susceptible isolates) are preferred options,8,25 although IV fosfomycin is unavailable in most countries across the region, including Australia. Older BLBLI agents including piperacillin-tazobactam may also have a role in treating of uncomplicated ESBL UTI, particularly where clinical improvement occurs during empiric therapy with these agent.24,25 Depending on susceptibility testing results, oral options for treating uncomplicated UTI or targeted oral switch for other infections include quinolones, cotrimoxazole, and fosfomycin.25,26,28 Notably many ESBL-producing Enterobacterales will harbour multiple other resistance genes, potentially limiting the utility of selected oral options.11 Despite being active against ESBL-producers, the newer BLBLI combinations and the novel siderophore cephalosporin cefiderocol should be reserved for infections caused by carbapenem-resistant bacteria,25,26 and remain unlicensed in children or neonates across all countries in the Asia Pacific region.

Carbapenem resistant Enterobacterales (CRE)

For the treatment of infections outside the urinary tract caused by CRE, the newer BLBLI combinations have an increasingly important role, particularly for CPE. Ceftazidime-avibactam (for KPC-producing or OXA-48-producing CPE), meropenem-vaborbactam and imipenem-cilastatin-relebactam (for KPC-producing CPE) are considered first line.25,26 For CPE carrying metallo-β-lactamases (MBL), cefiderocol monotherapy, or ceftazidime-avibactam combined with aztreonam are recommended,25,26 with avibactam-aztreonam recently licensed in the United States and Europe for use in adults. Extended-infusion meropenem may be considered in meropenem-susceptible, ertapenem-resistant isolates without carbapenemase genes,8,25,26,28 or in IMP-type CPE isolates.29 Tigecycline should be avoided in patients with BSI and UTI, but may have a role in other CRE infections if β-lactams are contraindicated.25,26 Polymyxins (colistin and polymyxin B), previously considered part of first-line therapy,28 now have a lesser role in the treatment of CRE in general with the availability of less toxic and more effective agents,8,26 although colistin may be the only available agent in some countries in the Oceania region.

Carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa (CR-PA)

There are limited clinical data to guide directed therapy recommendations for infections caused by carbapemen-resistant P. aeruginosa, including for isolates with additional non-susceptibility to piperacillin-tazobactam, ceftazidime, cefepime, aztreonam, and quinolones, defined as ‘difficult-to treat’ resistance (DTR-PA).25,26 For severe DTR-PA infections, Ceftolozane-tazobactam (C/T) and ceftazidime-avibactam are recommended agents.25,26 Cefiderocol is an alternative when newer BLBI combinations are inactive, not tolerated, or unavailable, and is preferred for MBL-producing DTR-PA isolates.26 When using non-β-lactam agents (e.g. polymyxins, aminoglycosides, or fosfomycin), combination therapy with two drugs with in vitro activity is recommended.25,26 For non-severe CR-PA infections, monotherapy with older agents, including aminoglycosides or other antipseudomonal β-lactams may be considered based on in vitro susceptibility.26

Carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii (CRAB)

For severe infections caused by CRAB, initial combination therapy with two agents with in vitro activity is recommended, including ampicillin-sulbactam where available.25,26,30 Sulbactam-durlobactam, recently been approved for treatment of A. baumannii infections in adults, can be used in combination with meropenem for treatment of CRAB infections.30 Other agents, including polymyxins, aminoglycosides and tigecycline, may be used in combination therapy based on in vitro activity.25,26 Cefiderocol has in vitro activity against CRAB, but has shown mixed results in clinical studies and therefore should be reserved (as part of combination therapy) for cases where other agents are ineffective, or not tolerated.25,26

What clinical trials assessing treatment outcomes associated with the recommended antimicrobial agents are there in children?

Carbapenems

Limited paediatric data are available comparing the efficacy of carbapenems against other agents. However, the efficacy of ertapenem has been assessed in several randomised clinical trials (RCTs) for various infection syndromes (Table 2). An RCT comparing ertapenem and ceftriaxone for the treatment of complicated urinary tract infection (cUTI), community-acquired pneumonia and skin and soft tissue infection (SSTI) in children (aged >3 months to 18 years) reported similar safety and tolerability to those in adult trials,31 but the rate of antibiotic resistance were not described. Ertapenem has also been compared to gentamicin and metronidazole, or triple antibiotic therapy (ampicillin, gentamicin, and metronidazole) in two clinical trials of perforated appendicitis in children.32,33 The findings differed in these two trials with one reporting superior efficacy of ertapenem,32 while the other showed no difference.33 Microbiology was only reported in the latter trial, with a 12% colonisation rate of ESBL-producing E. coli or Klebsiella spp.33 Of note, the emergence of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales during therapy occurred more often in patients treated with the triple therapy than with ertapenem (30% vs. 9%, p = 0·042).33 One RCT assessed ertapenem against ticarcillin/clavulanate for complicated intra-abdominal infection and acute pelvic infections in children with 89% and 73% response rates, respectively. Data on antimicrobial resistance was not reported.34

Two RCTs comparing meropenem with C/T37,38 and ceftazidime-avibactam35 are described in the following section.

Newer BLBLI combinations and cefiderocol

There have been two phase 2 trials that evaluated ceftazidime-avibactam efficacy in children (Table 2).35,36 In a trial of complicated intra-abdominal infections (children >3 months to 18 years), ceftazidime-avibactam (with metronidazole) and meropenem had similar clinical and microbiologic response rates of ≥90%.35 Similarly, a favourable response of >80% at test-of-cure was reported when ceftazidime-avibactam was compared to cefepime for cUTI (children >3 months to 18 years).36 Across both of these studies, prevalence of ceftazidime resistance with the presence of ESBL/Amp-C enzymes was only 3% (5/178).

In a phase 2 trial assessing the safety and efficacy of meropenem and ceftolozane tazobactam (C/T) in neonates and children with cUTI,38 rates of clinical cure (96% meropenem vs. 94% C/T) and microbiological cure (86% meropenem vs. 88% C/T) were high for both treatment groups (Table 2). However, resistant Gram-negative bacteria only accounted for a small proportion of the infections, with 95% susceptibility among aerobic gram-negative bacteria to both drugs. The presence of ESBL was reported in only 5% of E. coli isolates. A phase 2 RCT of meropenem versus C/T plus metronidazole for complicated intra-abdominal infections in children37 reported cure rates of 95% and 80%, respectively (this trial included a single participant <3 months of age). Whilst the efficacy of C/T and metronidazole appeared lower than meropenem, the cure rate was comparable to that reported in adult studies (83%).41 The presence of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales was notably higher in this study 13% in C/T group and 11% in the meropenem group.37

A Phase 2/3 RCT of imipenem-cilastatin-relebactam for children (birth to 18 years) with suspected or confirmed Gram-negative infection has been recently completed with results yet to be published (NCT03969901). There are no published paediatric clinical trials assessing the efficacy of meropenem-vaborbactam, cefiderocol, ampicillin/sulbactam or sulbactam-durlobactam in the treatment of resistant Gram-negative infections in children.

Polymyxins, fosfomycin, and tigecycline

Efficacy data for older antimicrobials such as fosfomycin, colistin, and polymyxin B in children are reported mainly in cohort studies and case series.42,43 A recent RCT of standard of care (SOC) versus SOC and fosfomycin (IV then oral) in 120 neonates with suspected sepsis, did not report clinical efficacy (Table 2).39

Tigecycline, a semi-synthetic derivative of tetracycline, has been approved by the European Medicines Agency for children from the age of 8 years for the treatment of complicated intra-abdominal infections and skin and soft tissue infections, except for diabetic foot infections.44 It is not currently licensed for children under 18 years in Australia or New Zealand. A phase 2 trial of tigecycline for community-acquired pneumonia, complicated intra-abdominal infections and skin and soft tissue infections in children aged 8–11 years reported overall efficacy of 75–90% at test of cure for different dosages (Table 2).40

What paediatric dosing and pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) data are available for the antimicrobials recommended for the treatment of resistant gram-negative infections?

Carbapenems

Meropenem is the most well studied antibiotic for treating resistant Gram-negative infections in neonates and children.45 Although the pharmacodynamic (PD) target for bactericidal killing by β-lactams – time that the free drug concentration exceeds the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 40–50% (fT > MIC 40–50%) is widely accepted, for neonates, a higher target of 61%T > MIC has been proposed due to a relatively immunosuppressed state. For organisms with higher MICs (>4–8 mg/L), a target of 100% fT > MIC or 100% T > 4-5xMIC has been suggested.46,47

The currently recommended doses (Table 3) were derived from pharmacokinetic studies in infants >3 months and children, then later in neonates <3 months48, 49, 50 In the latter study, >90% of neonates achieved a T > MIC of 75% for an MIC >2 μg/ml and 50% for an MIC of >4 μg/ml.48 Extended or continuous infusion may be necessary to achieve PD targets for organisms with higher MICs (4–8 mg/L) or in critically unwell children to achieve a PD target of 40% fT > MIC for an MIC of 4 mg/L.62,63 However, there is little evidence in children to show that achieving this target with continuous infusion of β-lactams improves clinical outcome.64,65 Notably, a recent meta-analysis of adults with severe sepsis found that continuous infusion of β-lactams decreased hospital mortality.66

PK/PD studies of ertapenem in children are limited. Current dosing recommendations are based on a single-dose pharmacokinetic study in 84 hospitalised children aged 3 months to 17 years.51 In adolescents aged 13–17 years, a single 20 mg/kg (max 1 g) IV dose resulted in drug exposure comparable to adults receiving 1 g (g) and achieved a concentration >2 μg/L (susceptibility breakpoint) in all adolescents. In children aged <12 years, due to higher clearance, twice daily dosing of 15–20 mg/kg was required, with higher doses needed to achieve the PD target for bacteria with MIC values approaching 2 μg/L. Licensing information reports insufficient CSF ertapenem concentrations in 12 children (aged 3 months to 13 years) with meningitis who received a 15 or 20 mg/kg dose; hence, ertapenem is not recommended for CNS infections.67 Ertapenem has not been studied in neonates and is not recommended for infants <3 months.

Newer BLBLI combinations

The PD target for ceftazidime is as for other β-lactam antibiotics (described above). At the same time, avibactam, a β-lactamase inhibitor, must exceed a critical threshold (CT) for at least 50% of the dosing interval.68 Based on susceptibility breakpoints for Enterobacteriaceae and P. aeruginosa, 50% fT > MIC 8 mg/L (ceftazidime) and fT > CT 1 mg/L (avibactam) has been used as the PD target in adult PK-PD studies.69 The paediatric dosing regimen of ceftazidime-avibactam was derived from one phase 1 and two phase 2 studies in children aged 3 months to <18 years.35,36,52 A population PK model using combined data from these three studies reported a >90% PTA of the aforementioned PKPD target, validating the approved dosing regimen (6 months-<18 years: 50–12·5 mg/kg; 3-<6 months old: 40–10 mg/kg Q8H by 2-h IV infusion).53 Neonates and young infants (<3 months) were excluded from these studies.

Dosing for ceftolozane-tazobactam is based on a paediatric phase 1 study of 37 children aged <18 years55 and a subsequent population PK model utilising this data.56 The PD target for ceftolozane is %fT > MIC of 30% (8 hourly dosing), and for tazobactam is %fT > CT of 20%, based on established breakpoints and animal models.70,71 The PTA with a dose of 1/0·5 g for children 12 to <18 years and 20/10 mg/kg for infants from birth (postnatal age 7 days, >32 weeks gestation) was ≥90%. This dosing regimen was subsequently evaluated in a phase 2 study of 95 children <18 years of age and was found to be safe and effective.38

For meropenem-vaborbactam data from a phase 1 PK study or children aged 3 months to 18 years, and a paediatric modelling study indicate that PK targets derived from adult data may be achieved with weight based dosing via extended IV infusion.72,73 An ongoing phase 2 trial is assessing pharmacokinetics, safety and tolerability of meropenem-vaborbactam in children aged 3 months to 12 years (NCT06672978). For imipenem-cilastatin-relebactam, a phase 1 PK study of 46 children <18 years of age demonstrated adequate attainment of PK/PD targets with weight-based dosing with similar drug exposure across age cohorts.74

Aztreonam

Animal data suggests aztreonam displays time-dependent bacterial killing however, in an adult study, the 24 h AUC/MIC was associated with treatment efficacy.75, 76, 77 Several international drug formularies include dosing regimens for aztreonam.45,59 Although the product information includes reference to 612 children (aged 1 month-12 years) who received aztreonam and reports established safety and efficacy in infants and children >9 months,57 there is only one small published PK study of 29 neonates and children (2 days–11 years) who received a single dose of 30 mg/kg.58 This study found that the pharmacokinetics in children aged 2–12 years was similar to that in adults.

Polymyxins

Colistin is available as the inactive prodrug colistin methanesulfonate (CMS) (parenteral or inhalation) or as colistin sulfate (topical and oral). Both display concentration-dependent killing. Although in vitro78,79 and animal studies80, 81, 82 have shown either the free or total colistin AUC/MIC as the PD parameter associated with the bacterial killing of P. aeruginosa and Acinetobacter baumanii83 previous human studies have targeted an average steady state plasma concentration (Css,avg) of 2 mg/L.84 The currently recommended dose by the FDA and EMA of 75,000 to 150,000 IU/kg/day (∼2·5–5 mg of colistin base activity (CBA)/kg/day) has been shown to result in poor attainment of this target in neonates and children, with is significant inter-individual variability in polymyxin pharmacokinetics.85, 86, 87 A prospective population PK study of children (0·8–6·8 years (n = 79)) found a dose of 5 mg/kg/day resulted in a wide variability in the PTA in achieving the latter therapeutic target (18–63%). Attainment was affected by creatinine clearance, emphasising the importance of dose adjustment based on renal function.88 In a study of critically ill children aged 3 months to 13·75 years with carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacterial infections, higher than recommended doses were given (200,000 IU/kg day (6·6 mg CBA/kg/day), 300,000 IU/day (9·9 mg CBA kg/kg/day), and 350,000 IU/kg/day (11·6 mg CBA/kg/day). This resulted in a median Css,avg of 2·92 mg/L.85

Fosfomycin

The only study of fosfomycin PK in neonates (term, aged 0–23 days) found a dose of 150 mg/kg IV Q12H was required to achieve the PD target (AUC24/MIC for E. coli to achieve stasis (19·3) and 1 log kill (87·5) for an isolate with a MIC of 32 mg/L).39 Neonates <7 days or weighing <1500 g required a reduced 100 mg/kg IV Q12H dose. The oral bioavailability of fosfomycin was estimated at 0·48.39 There are no pharmacokinetic data in older infants and children resulting in varying dosing recommendations.89

Cefiderocol

Cefiderocol is a time-dependent antibiotic, with the target %fT > MIC based on an animal model identifying a range from 54% (Stenotrophomonas maltophilia) to 88% (Acintobacter baumanii).90 A phase II trial of cefiderocol in 53 children >3 months was recently published.60 Dosing was stratified based on weight and renal function (weight ≥34 kg normal renal function 2000 mg, weight <34 kg normal renal function 60 mg/kg (max 2000 mg). Steady-state trough plasma concentrations exceeded the breakpoint of Gram-negative organisms of 4 μg/mL (i.e. 100% T > MIC).60 The results from a phase II trial in neonates and infants <3 months are awaited (NCT06086626).

Ampicillin-sulbactam and sulbactam-durlobactam

Limited PK data is available to guide paediatric and neonatal dosing of ampicillin-sulbactam. In a PK study of 28 children aged 1–12 years, ampicillin-sulbactam (2:1) at a dose of 40–80 mg/kg Q6H for 2–6 days was given.61 Steady-state concentrations and half-life were similar in children compared to adults receiving 1–2 g ampicillin-sulbactam (2:1).61,91 A physiologically based pharmacokinetic model (PBPK) extrapolated paediatric dosing and, in simulations, found 40 mg/kg Q8H achieved 60% fT > MIC at 4 mg/L for 90% of individuals.92 However, a neonatal PK study of 15 premature and 1-term infants (mean GA 30 weeks, range 26–41 weeks) showed a longer half-life due to reduced renal clearance.93 Although sulbactam is expected to have time-dependent activity, there is limited in vivo data to define the pharmacodynamic target.94 For sulbactam-durlobactam a phase 1b paediatric trial to collect PK and safety data in children has recently been registered (NCT06801223).

Safety profile of the antimicrobial agents recommended in children

Warnings and adverse events (AE) for the selected antimicrobials are summarised below, in Table 2 and Supplementary Table 3. For many of these antibiotics, the small paediatric sample sizes warrant caution with therapeutic use until more robust safety data becomes available.

Carbapenems

Clinical AE are usually mild, typically not requiring treatment cessation.31,33,34 Ertapenem has been associated with gastrointestinal AE in children, including diarrhoea (4–5·9%) and vomiting (2%) and infusion reactions (local erythema and pain) (3–7%).31,34 Neutropenia can occur in up to 6% of children and elevated liver transaminases in 2–3%.31

Meropenem is generally well tolerated in children, with a similar AE profile to adults.37,38 AE from paediatric studies include diarrhoea (5–9%), rash (3%), eosinophilia (3%), and elevated liver function tests (LFTs) (3–5%).37,38

Newer BLBLI combinations

The most common AE associated with ceftazidime/avibactam in children are diarrhoea (3–8%), vomiting (3–15%), abdominal pain (3%), infusion site phlebitis (3–7%) and LFT derangement (1·6–3%).35,36,52 Tachycardia is an uncommonly reported but potentially serious adverse effect in children.52

From the limited evidence, ceftolozane/tazobactam use in children appears to have a similar AE profile to adult patients.37,38,55 Adverse effects in children include diarrhoea (7–17%), vomiting (1–10%), abdominal pain (2–11%), neutropaenia (4–8%), increased platelet count (9–16%), and LFT derangement (4–7%).37,38

In limited paediatric data meropenem-vaborbactam was well tolerated, although specific adverse effect data were not reported.72,73 Imipenem-cilastatin-relebactam was well tolerated in the aforementioned phase 1 paediatric study, with non-serious drug related adverse effects reported in 4% (2/46) of participants (diarrhoes, LFT derangement, and anaemia), and no drug discontinuations due to AE.74

Aztreonam

Paediatric safety data for aztreonam is limited, however, AE are uncommon and include rash (2–4%), diarrhoea (1%), and injection site pain (12%).57 Laboratory AE are generally mild and reversible, including eosinophilia (6–17%), increased platelet count (4%), and LFT derangement with ALT (7%) and ALT (4%) increase.57,58 Neutropaenia (3%) has been reported, and may be more common in children <2 years (up to 11%).57

Polymyxins

Paediatric safety data for polymyxins (Colistin/Polymyxin B) are mainly from small, retrospective case series in cohorts with frequent concomitant administration of other nephrotoxic agents. Nephrotoxicity appears less frequent in children compared to adults, with reported rates varying across studies depending on dose and age (0–22%), with adolescents seven times more likely to develop acute kidney injury than younger children.43

The incidence of neurotoxicity has declined over time in both adults and children, which may be dose-related. Neurotoxicity has not been widely observed with rare case reports in children of seizures, headaches, and paresthesias. Transient LFT derangement has been reported in up to 17% of children.43

Fosfomycin

The AE profile of fosfomycin in children is expected to be similar to that of adults. In an RCT of neonates with suspected sepsis, intravenous fosfomycin was well tolerated.39 Despite the high sodium load of the IV formulation, fosfomycin has not been associated with hypernatraemia in neonates.95 Diarrhoea and nausea has been reported in paediatric patients with the oral formulation (2%, respectively).42 However, these were not reported in neonatal studies.39,95

Cefiderocol

Cefiderocol was well tolerated in the phase II study with no serious AE or discontinuations.60 No cefiderocol related AE were observed in case studies involving extreme preterm neonates, young children, or adolescents.96, 97, 98, 99 The most commonly reported adverse effects in adult patients include diarrhoea (9%), increased LFTs (16%) and hypokalaemia (2–11%).100

Ampicillin-sulbactam

In several small paediatric studies no adverse effects were attributed to use of ampicillin/sulbactam,61,93 and it is expected that the AE profile would be similar to that reported in adult patients.101 The most commonly reported adverse effects in adults include diarrhoea (3%), rash (<2%) and infusion site pain (3% intravenous, 16% intramuscular).101

Tigecycline

Tigecycline use in children is associated with nausea (12–50%), vomiting (16–47%), headache (8%), and pancreatitis.40,44 Gastrointestinal adverse effects appear to be dose-related, occurring more frequently at doses >1 mg/kg twice daily (nausea >60%), when compared to lower doses (nausea 18%).40,44 The product information states that it is not recommended in children aged <8 years due to the risk of permanent teeth staining, however a systematic review of newer tetracyclines (doxycycline) on tooth staining have shown this risk is low.102

Regional challenges

There are few approved agents for treating children with Gram-negative infections in Oceania. Meropenem, ertapenem, and ceftazidime-avibactam are approved for use in children aged ≥3 months in Australia and New Zealand. Ceftolozane-tazobactam is approved for use in neonates and children from birth. Colistin (no lower age limit) and aztreonam (>1 week age) are also licensed for use in children in Australia and New Zealand, although specific neonatal dosing recommendations are not provided. Intravenous fosfomycin, cefiderocol, ampicillin-sulbactam, sulbactam-durlobactam, meropenem-vaborbactam, imipenem-cilastatin-relebactam, and polymyxin B have not been licensed for use in children <18 years of age throughout the region.

The management of MDR Gram-negative organisms in the region is further complicated by the availability of suitable oral formulations for children. Most oral antibiotics that may be considered for MDR Gram-negative infections are not available as child-friendly formulations. Only two agents (fosfomycin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole) are both approved for paediatric use and available as solutions or suspensions. At present, licensed use of oral fosfomycin is limited to treating acute cystitis in females >12 years of age, as such the product information does not provide adequate directions or suitable volumes for the doses recommended for younger children and infants. Of note, there is an ongoing RCT assessing Fosfomycin efficacy in children 6 months to 18 years old with UTI across the region (NCT05709028). The approved product information documents in Australia and New Zealand have inconsistent paediatric recommendations for ciprofloxacin and doxycycline, with both antibiotics available as tablets or capsules intended to deliver adult doses.

Summary & future directions

Antimicrobial resistance is a growing concern in Oceania, with ESBL-producers accounting for 1 in 5 Enterobacterales infections amongst hospitalised Australian children. There are scant published data for children in other countries within the region. In the PICTs, available data suggest a high proportion (10–43%) of K. pneumoniae are either ceftriaxone resistant and/or ESBL-producers. The increasing prevalence of MDR GNB in the region highlights the need to bolster laboratory infrastructure and technical expertise to enable timely detection of rising AMR.103 Australia and New Zealand can play a pivotal role in enhancing antimicrobial AMR surveillance across neighbouring countries by leveraging their established programs (AGAR, PAEDS surveillance) and sharing data collection frameworks that can serve as templates to develop robust AMR monitoring systems in the region.

Of the 14 recommended antibiotics for drug-resistant Gram-negative infections, only six are licensed in children aged <12 years and only two in neonates. Off-label prescribing in children results in the routine use of antibiotic dosing regimens that are extrapolated from older children or adults (as shown for fosfomycin, cefiderocol) with few dedicated paediatric studies, particularly in neonates. The neonatal population are among the highest risk group for serious Gram-negative infections, yet PK data to guide antibiotic dosing are available for only meropenem, ceftolozane-tazobactam, aztreonam, ampicillin-sulbactam, and fosfomycin. Although it is promising to see that clinical trials in children of newer antimicrobials are underwayincluding for meropenem-vaborbactam (NCT06672978), sulbactam-durlobactam (NCT06801223), and aztreonam-avibactam (NCT05639647), evidence for the use of older widely used antibiotics such as fosfomycin, colistin, and polymyxin B are largely limited to cohort studies and case reports. Therefore, future research efforts should focus on these older drugs alongside evaluating newer antibiotics.7 Finally, as licencing studies for antibiotics are often conducted using clinical rather than microbiological inclusion criteria, only a subset of patients in these trials have confirmed MDR-GNB. Platform studies such as TREAT-GNB (NCT07004049) have the potential to assess several agents simultaneously, thereby overcoming barriers inherent in conducting traditional RCTs for MDR-GNB, inclusion of children in such studies is a further priority.

Beyond the licensing restrictions faced by children, access to newer antimicrobials in the PICTs is even more limited. This lack of access can be life-threatening for those with drug-resistant Gram-negative infections, and market entry rewards, subscription models and global access programs are needed to overcome the poor economic incentives. These regulatory and infrastructure hurdles prevent access to newer medications.104

Children in Oceania face significant disadvantages compared to adults in accessing effective treatment for drug-resistant Gram-negative bacterial infections. This review underscores the urgent need for high-quality PK and clinical data for older antibiotics (fosfomycin, polymyxin B) and newer agents in paediatric populations. The scarcity of pharmacokinetic and clinical trial data in neonates and limited licensing for all but six of the recommended drugs calls for a coordinated and focused research response. Strengthening laboratory infrastructure and technical expertise in the PICTs is essential for the timely detection and surveillance of AMR threats. Ensuring equitable access to lifesaving antimicrobials for children with drug-resistant infections must remain a global health priority.
