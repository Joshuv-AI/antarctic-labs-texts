Extraction of the polysorbate 20 and 80 fingerprint via generative modeling
Authors: Peter Roelants, Reza Ranjbar Choubeh, Nico Verbeeck, Rabindranath Andujar, Torsten Schultz-Fademrecht, Patrick Garidel, Viktor Gross
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolysorbate 20 (PS20) and polysorbate 80 (PS80) are essential surfactants used to stabilize biopharmaceutical products, yet their highly heterogeneous mixtures and susceptibility to oxidation and enzymatic hydrolysis complicate routine analysis. We developed a hierarchical generative model that reconstructs entire liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) measurements to automatically interpret complex polysorbate datasets. By embedding domain knowledge of base structures, oxyethylene chain lengths, fatty acid esterification, and isotope patterns, the model resolves individual subspecies and provides molecular-level composition. Applied to PS20 and PS80, the approach distinguishes oxidative from hydrolytic degradation and yields pathway-specific fingerprints. Model outputs agree closely with manual integration while delivering greater depth and automation. This transforms polysorbate analysis from labor-intensive peak-by-peak workflows into an objective, comprehensive characterization tool suited for quality control, batch selection and degradation monitoring throughout development and manufacturing.

Introduction

The rapid development of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in recent years has revolutionized numerous industries, including pharmaceutical development. AI-assisted analytics offer promising opportunities to enhance efficiency and precision in drug development. Generative models, a subset of AI, are also transforming the development of pharmaceuticals. These models can design new molecules with desired properties, speeding up the research. (Mardikoraem et al., 2023). Moreover, automation of analysis processes using AI makes it possible to effectively handle large sample numbers or complex data structures. Generative modeling has emerged as a powerful technique for analyzing complex datasets across various fields, including analytical chemistry. Polysorbates (PS) 20 and 80, by far the most used surfactants for biological stabilization, represent heterogeneous mixtures comprising more than 600 individual substances, making them ideal candidates for generative modeling approaches (Wuchner et al., 2022a, Wuchner et al., 2022b; Evers et al., 2020). In LC-MS data analysis of PS, huge amounts of data are generated. Therefore, this approach offers a novel and comprehensive method for extracting detailed information about polysorbate composition and degradation patterns.

Unlike traditional analytical methods that identify and quantify specific peaks or regions of interest, generative modeling recreates the entire LC-MS dataset by optimizing underlying parameters, defined according to the challenge to solve. This comprehensive approach simultaneously considers all data aspects, yielding more robust analysis. By incorporating domain knowledge of polysorbate structures, mass spectrum patterns, chromatographic behavior, and potential degradation products, the model generates synthetic LC-MS data that closely matches observations. This approach enables detailed extraction of polysorbate sample composition, revealing both the presence and relative quantities of various species and their degradation products.

According to the pharmacopoeia, polysorbates comprise a hydrophilic headgroup (either sorbitan or isosorbide) ethoxylated with up to four or two polyoxyethylene (POE) chains, respectively. These POE moieties follow a normal distribution centered around 26 units for sorbitan and 13 units for isosorbide. (Evers et al., 2020). They are further esterified with up to four or two hydrophobic fatty acids (FAs) for sorbitan and isosorbide, respectively. The FA composition adds an additional layer of complexity, ranging from caproic (C6:0) to linoleic acid (C18:2) for PS20 and from myristic (C14:0) to linolenic acid (C18:3) for PS80 (Table S1). Polysorbate nomenclature derives from the predominant fatty acid present, PS20 primarily contains polyoxyethylene sorbitan monolaurate (C12:0) (Fig. 1), while PS80 contains mainly polyoxyethylene sorbitan monooleate (C18:1) (Table S1) (Kerwin, 2008). Notably, these principal structures constitute only about 20 % (w/w) of the total material, with significant variation depending on manufacturers and quality grades (Hewitt et al., 2011). Weber et al. (2023) provide a comprehensive overview of the diverse fatty acids and their potential esterification products in PS20 and PS80.

Structural heterogeneity of polysorbate. Idealized structures of PS20, with approximately w + x + y + z = 20 OE units, referring to the number of ethylene oxide (OE) moieties defined by the pharmacopoeia.

Polysorbate heterogeneity encompasses four distinct categories: (i) base molecule diversity (sorbitan/isosorbide), (ii) polyoxyethylene (POE) chain variation, (iii) esterification degree, and (iv) fatty acid composition (Kishore, 2018). This complexity increases further when considering the various degradation products that can form during pharmaceutical product storage (Borisov et al., 2015; Kishore et al., 2011).

The complexity of polysorbate mixtures and their degradation pathways (oxidation vs enzymatic hydrolysis) makes selecting appropriate analytical strategies challenging (Doshi et al., 2021; Felix et al., 2025; Gopalrathnam et al., 2018; Kozuch et al., 2023; Schultz-Fademrecht et al., 2024). While determining PS content may suffice for well-characterized processes or products, troubleshooting often requires substantially more sophisticated analytical approaches, with degradation pathway identification alone potentially requiring multiple analytical methods (Carle et al., 2024). Researchers have published numerous analytical techniques over recent years, including fluorescence detection, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and reversed-phase high performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) coupled with charged aerosol detection (CAD), evaporative light scattering detection (ELSD), or mass spectrometry (MS) to elucidate the degradation of PS (Bhargava et al., 2021; Borisov et al., 2015; Doshi et al., 2020a; Dwivedi et al., 2020; Evers et al., 2020; Kranz et al., 2019; Lippold et al., 2017; Penfield and Rumbelow, 2020; Zhang et al., 2015). MS offers distinct advantages over other detection methods by distinguishing PS species with different m/z values, providing an additional dimension that enables selective determination of co-eluting substances. This capability makes MS particularly valuable for in-depth exploration of polysorbate chemistry and identification of degradation pathways (Ayorinde et al., 2000; Evers et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2022).

Though generative modeling has been applied across various fields, its application to LC-MS analysis of polysorbates introduces a novel approach that could significantly enhance characterization of these critical compounds in biopharmaceutical formulations. This paper presents an algorithm that extracts and characterizes distinctive fingerprints of PS20 and PS80 from different vendors. A subsequent study will demonstrate methods for identifying various degradation pathways and determining oxidation degrees. To our knowledge, this represents the first algorithm capable of automatically extracting individual PS20 and PS80 sub-species and their oxidation products.

Forced oxidative degradation studies of PS20 HP and PS80 HP

To prepare oxidized surfactants, PS20 HP and PS80 HP were dissolved in ultrapure water to create 4 mg·mL−1 stock solutions, which were then sterile filtered (0.22 μm Sterivex® filter unit) and dispensed as 10 mL aliquots into 50 mL borosilicate vials. These samples underwent accelerated degradation at 40 °C / 75 % r.h. for up to 18 weeks, with degradation categorized into four scores based on the percentage of intact PS fraction (Table 2): score one (100 %), score two (approx. 80 %), score three (approx. 50 %), and score four (approx. 20 %). For testing, the stock solutions were diluted 10-fold with ultrapure water to achieve a final concentration of 0.4 mg·mL−1.

Forced hydrolytic degradation study of PS20 HP

An IgG1 antibody known to contain co-purified host cell proteins with hydrolytic activity was formulated at 50 mg·mL−1 in 20 mM histidine buffer (pH 6.0) with 0.4 mg·mL−1 PS20 HP, alongside a corresponding protein-free placebo prepared under identical conditions. Both formulations were stored at accelerated storage conditions (40 °C/75 % r.h.) to promote PS20 degradation.

Reversed-phase ultra-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry

The RP-UPLC-MS method, adapted from Lippold et al. (2017) and Evers et al. (2020), employed an Ultimate 3000® UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with an ACQUITY QDa mass detector (Waters Corporation) featuring an ESI source. (Evers et al., 2020; Lippold et al., 2017) Protein removal was achieved using a Waters Oasis®Max mixed-mode column (30 μm, 2.1 mm × 20 mm, 80 Å) while PS sub-species separation utilized a Poroshell 120 SB-C08 reversed-phase column (4.6 × 100 mm, 2.7 μm, Agilent Technologies) (Hewitt et al., 2008). The analytical gradient was performed using three solvents: 100 % acetonitrile (Solvent A), 100 % ultrapure water (Solvent B), and 100 % methanol (Solvent C), at a constant flow rate of 0.7 mL·min−1. The gradient program starts with 100 % Solvent B for 4 min. During this initial phase, the column switching valve was set to position 1–6 (diverting flow to waste) to remove proteins and excipients. After 4 min, the valve was switched to position 1–2, directing flow to the Poroshell 120 SB-C8 reversed-phase column. Following the initial phase, a linear gradient was applied to reach 36 % Solvent A and 64 % Solvent B after 6 min. For the separation of polysorbates, the mobile phase composition was further adjusted: for PS20, the gradient reached 71.2 % A, 5.0 % B, and 23.8 % C; for PS80, it reached 76.2 % A and 23.8 % B after 22 min. These conditions were maintained until the 35th min. At 35 min, the system was re-equilibrated to the initial conditions of 100 % Solvent B, which were held until the end of the 45-min run. To enhance analyte ionization prior to MS detection, 10 mM ammonium formate was continuously introduced into the mobile phase via a T-piece at a flow rate of 0.2 mL·min−1. Analysis parameters included a 2 μL injection volume, column temperatures of 50 °C for PS20 and 60 °C for PS80, a 45-min run time, and mass detection between 250 and 1250 Da in positive mode at 2 Hz sampling rate, with calibration standards ranging from 0.05 to 0.6 mg·mL−1.

Generative modeling of polysorbate 20 and 80 data

Traditional analytical methods for polysorbate analysis typically focus on identifying and quantifying specific peaks or regions of interest but fail to capture the full complexity of LC-MS datasets. To address this limitation and leverage the rich information within these complex chromatograms (Fig. S3), we developed a novel hierarchical generative model that represents a significant departure from conventional approaches by comprehensively modeling and interpreting the entire LC-MS dataset. Our approach is built upon a foundation of domain knowledge, explicitly incorporating key structural features of polysorbate molecules: diverse base structures (sorbitan, isosorbide and POE), variable fatty acid composition (Table S1), and characteristic distribution of oxyethylene (OE) chains. By integrating this structural understanding with LC-MS separation principles, our model reconstructs intricate patterns in the data, recreating the entire measurement from global system characteristics down to individual molecular species. The following sections detail the architecture of our hierarchical generative model, outlining how its components work together to reconstruct polysorbate LC-MS data with unprecedented fidelity and comprehensiveness.

Our generative model classifies the diverse molecular landscape of polysorbates into three primary categories based on core structural variations (Weber et al., 2023).

The model further captures variations in OE subunit numbers and FA compositions within each category, encompassing the full molecular spectrum contributing to polysorbate complexity (Table S1 & S2). Subsequent sections detail how our hierarchical model architecture leverages these categorical distinctions to accurately reconstruct LC-MS measurement patterns.

Hierarchical generative model

We developed a hierarchical generative model to analyze complex polysorbate LC-MS data and extract meaningful insights beyond traditional peak-based methods. Our structured approach decomposes the LC-MS measurement process into distinct levels, each with specific parameters that capture different aspects of the underlying physical and chemical phenomena (Fig. 2). This framework enables comprehensive reconstruction of the entire dataset based on fundamental principles and domain knowledge, providing more nuanced and interpretable analysis (Marty et al., 2015).

Diagram of the hierarchical generative model.

Because the model regenerates the measured LC-MS samples and all operations are differentiable, we can get values for all parameters defined by using a gradient based optimization scheme to fit all parameters to closely match the original measurement.

The following subsections define the modeled data, outline our generative goals, and detail the key components of this hierarchical architecture. Fig. 2 is a visualization of the model.

Level 1: global level parameters

Global parameters occupy the highest level of the hierarchy and are shared across a single LC-MS run, representing fundamental characteristics of both the LC-MS system and the measurement process. A key concept introduced at this level is OE-elution velocity, which describes how retention time changes with varying numbers of oxyethylene (OE) groups in otherwise identical molecules. The model incorporates the observation that as the number of OE groups increases, retention time changes in an approximate linear fashion, with this rate of change defined as the OE-elution velocity. Visually, this phenomenon appears as the characteristic “slope” of the patterns in time vs. m/z contour plots (Fig. 3). While perfectly linear relationships are not always observed in practice, the model effectively approximates these relationships using global parameters.

Global parameters related to the liquid chromatography setup and OE-elution velocity

Building on the OE-elution velocity concept, the model incorporates several specific parameters to characterize this phenomenon more precisely.

The OE-elution acceleration (aOE) accounts for the observation that as retention time increases (molecules eluting later), the OE-elution velocity itself changes. This parameter captures the rate of change of the OE-elution velocity over time, essentially functioning as the “acceleration” of the OE-elution. A negative aOE value indicates that the OE-elution process slows down as retention time increases, which we speculate results from the LC gradient profile and molecule-column interactions empirically observed in our data. While this linear velocity change is an approximation that future work may refine with more complex non-linear models, it effectively characterizes the observed behavior (Befekadu et al., 2009; Kamedulska et al., 2022).

The OE-elution intercept (bOE) represents the baseline OE-elution velocity at the beginning of the LC gradient (hypothetically at time zero) and serves as the intercept of the linear function defining how OE-elution velocity changes with retention time.

For exceptional cases where molecules lack fatty acid chains and do not follow the linear OE-elution velocity trend observed in other polysorbates, the model employs the OE-elution velocity for molecules without fatty acids (vOE_FA00), a fixed, constant parameter that accounts for these exceptions to the general rule.

Global parameter related to the mass spectrometer

Complementing the chromatography parameters, the model also accounts for mass spectrometer characteristics through the isotope peak smoothing parameter (σisotope). This parameter models the instrument-specific uncertainty of isotope peak widths in the m/z dimension. While ideally isotope peaks would be infinitely narrow and represent distinct masses, real-world limitations in mass spectrometer resolution and isotopic fine structure result in peaks with measurable width. The σisotope value represents the standard deviation of a Gaussian kernel used to convolve the theoretical isotope distribution, effectively smoothing and broadening the peaks to better match experimental LC-MS data. This convolution ensures that the generated m/z patterns are not unrealistically sharp and align more closely with the noise characteristics and resolution limitations observed in actual measurements.

Level 2: LC polymer group level parameters

This level represents the Polymer Group, also known as a homologous series h, which comprises polymers sharing the same chemical structure but differing only in their number of polymeric subunits (Loos and Singer, 2017; Mildau et al., 2022). In our context, these are collections of molecules sharing the same base structure (sorbitan, isosorbide, or POE) and fatty acid composition, but having different numbers of oxyethylene (OE) groups. The parameters at this level define the characteristic liquid-chromatography retention behavior within each polymer group and are categorized into two distinct types: fitted parameters and derived parameters. The fitted parameters are determined directly during the model's optimization process, while the derived parameters are calculated from these fitted parameters at each iteration of the optimization.

Fitted parameters

The retention time location for polymer group h (tloc h) represents the retention time for the entire polymer group by referencing a specific molecule within group h that has a representative or central number of Oxyethylene (OE) groups. This representative OE number, denoted as μOE j, is pre-defined.

The retention time spread for polymer group h (σspread h) defines the width or duration of the elution peak along the time dimension for polymer group h. It represents the standard deviation of a Gaussian function that models the elution profile of the entire polymeric family. Conceptually, a larger σspread h indicates that molecules in group h elute over a wider time range, resulting in broader chromatogram peaks. Mathematically, the elution profile for the polymer group is modeled as a Gaussian distribution in the time dimension, centered at tloc h with a standard deviation of σspread h.

The deviation of OE-elution velocity (δvOE h) allows for polymer group-specific deviations from the approximate linear OE-elution velocity relationship established by the global parameters, accommodating group-specific retention behavior.

Derived parameters

The OE-elution velocity for polymer group h (vOE h) is derived from the global OE-elution velocity parameters and the polymer group-specific retention time and deviation. This parameter represents the linear relationship between the number of oxyethylene (OE) groups and the retention time for molecules within the polymer group and is used to calculate the precise elution time for individual molecules. Level 3: liquid chromatography panini level parameters

When visualizing a molecule group with a specific ammonium adduct in LC-MS contour plots, the signal forms a distinct, elongated, and often tilted pattern that resembles the grill marks on a panini sandwich. We coined the term “Panini” for these characteristic patterns, after the distinctive Panini sandwich grill pattern (Fig. S1). Each “Panini” in the model represents a specific molecule-group (polymer group) combined with a specific ammonium adduct charge state.

The parameters at this level are specific to each Panini (indexed by j) and define its total intensity and characteristic bi-modal OE distribution, as further detailed below. Similar to the polymer group level, these parameters are categorized into two distinct types: fitted parameters and derived parameters.

Fitted parameters

Panini-level Intensity:

The total intensity parameter (Ipanini j) represents the overall abundance of molecules in a specific Panini cluster j. This parameter quantifies the cumulative signal intensity of all molecules belonging to the cluster.

Bi-Modal OE Distribution for Degradation Modeling:

The model uses either a uni-modal or bi-modal Gaussian distribution to represent the distribution of oxyethylene (OE) groups within each Panini (Fig. 8). Bi-modality is crucial for modeling polysorbate degradation and is applied specifically for POE species, since they are valuable markers of oxidative degradation. The bi-modal distribution is defined by two distinct modes of “original” and “degraded”.

In summary, our hierarchical generative model offers a powerful and flexible framework for analyzing complex polysorbate LC-MS data. By explicitly modeling the key physical and chemical processes underlying the measurement, and by organizing the model parameters in a hierarchical structure, we gain a nuanced and interpretable representation of the data, enabling detailed characterization of polysorbate composition and degradation.

Results & discussions

Characterization of PS20 subspecies generated by a generative model

In a first step, it was evaluated how comparable the model generated, and manually integrated intensities are. Therefore, the total area of the PS20 chromatogram was evaluated and differentiated into a degraded and intact fraction (Fig. S3). By neglecting matrix effects of the chromatographic system, the model shows a higher specificity by only extracting PS sub-species (Paninis) covered under the degraded and intact peak fractions. The intensities of all species covered by the degraded or intact fractions were summed up, respectively (Table S2). Based on the absolute intensity of the integrated and via model generated intensities, the relative % AUC of intact and degraded was calculated and compared (Fig. 5A, Table S3). The results show that manual integration and generation via model show a high comparability with around 79.7 % and 81.9 % of intact PS20, respectively (Table S3). Furthermore, the relative peak area percentage of the generated subspecies (paninis) was calculated (Fig. 5B, Table S4). When summing up the intensity of all POE sorbitan monolaurate (S12) charge variants (single (+), double (++), and triple (+++)), it accounts for approximately 19.52 % of the total PS20. These results align well with Hewitt et al. (2011), who describe that S12 accounts for approximately 20 % (w/w) of PS20. Considering the many co-eluting subspecies (Fig. S3), manual integration and differentiation between the subspecies become unlikely, especially in cases where PS degradation occurs. Automated profiling of polysorbate sub-species: insights into batch heterogeneity and quality differentiation

Commercially available polysorbates (PS20 and PS80) are highly heterogeneous mixtures whose composition and relative abundance of individual sub-species vary between batches and vendors due to differences in raw materials (source, grade, purity) and synthesis processes. Two main grades are available on the market: multicompendial (MC) grade, which includes high purity (HP) and super refined (SR) qualities, and ultrapure grade (Martos et al., 2017; Weber et al., 2023). Manufacturers have also developed ultra-pure grades with reduced fatty acid heterogeneity: all-oleate (AO) PS80 containing ≥98 % oleic acid and < 0.5 % other fatty acids, and all-laurate (AL) PS20 containing ≥98 % lauric acid (Brovč et al., 2020). The China grade (CG) quality for PS80, similar to all oleate (AO) quality from Croda, also claims an oleic acid content of ≥98 % (Liu et al., 2022). We evaluated our algorithm's ability to extract PS sub-species across different quality grades (HP, SR, and CG) of PS20 and PS80. The molecular distribution extracted by our model closely matched the results obtained from chromatographic analysis (Fig. 6, Fig. S3), which showed sorbitan POE mono-laurate (C12:0) as the predominant molecule (approx. 20 % of the total area for HP quality), followed by C14:0 and C16:0, while other species (C08:0, C10:0, C18:0, and C18:1) appeared in less, but similar quantities. This distribution aligns with EU pharmacopoeia specifications (40–60 % lauric acid (C12:0), 14–25 % myristic acid (C14:0), 7–15 % palmitic acid (C16:0) (Table S1) as well as with literature (Hewitt et al., 2011).

Molecular characterization of polysorbate degradation: distribution patterns and degradation mechanisms

Oxidative degradation

Oxidative PS20 degradation

We evaluated our generative model's ability to characterize PS20 and PS80 oxidation states using samples with varying oxidation scores (Table 2). Kishore et al. (2011) identified short fatty acid esters (C12:0-C16:0) as oxidative degradation products. We selected POE12 as a representative molecule to assess OE subunit-level oxidation (Kishore et al., 2011). The initial sample (score 1) displayed a characteristic Gaussian distribution with around 11–12 POE moieties (Fig. 8A). Moderately oxidized samples (score 2, approx. 80 % intact) showed increased shorter POE12 molecules centered at approximately 5–6 OE units, consistent with Borisov et al.'s (2015) findings where POE-sorbitan-laurate with approximately 24 OE units degraded to POE-sorbitan with 18–19 OE units and POE-laurate with 4–5 OE units (Borisov et al., 2015). Higher oxidation scores (3–4) exhibited further increase in these short POE-laurate (C12:0) molecules. Notably, the observed bi-modal OE distribution appears primarily driven by degradation of sorbitan and isosorbide POE12 molecules (with their various ester forms) generating POE12 esters averaging 6 OE subunits, rather than by shifting the entire POE12 distribution toward lower OE units. This aligns with the substantially larger pool of oxidizable sorbitan and isosorbide POE12 molecules, compared to the more limited number of POE12 molecules with >8 OE subunits that could shift to lower values through oxidation.

The mono-to-bimodal distribution shift occurs across all esterified POE molecules (C08-C18:0), whereas for sorbitan and isosorbide no bimodality can be observed (Fig. 8B). To simplify analysis of the complex OE subunit distribution (Fig. 8A), we developed a fdegradedj parameter (method section Level 3). This parameter, calculated for POE12, POE14, POE16, and non-esterified POE molecules (POE00) (Fig. 8C), revealed higher values for POE14 and POE16 compared to POE12 at oxidation scores 2 and 3, before approaching saturation at score 4. These findings align with Kranz et al. (2020), who observed faster degradation of higher-order esters and ester with longer fatty acid chains (Kranz et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2017, Zhang et al., 2018).

Oxidative PS80 degradation

The oxidation process of PS80 exhibits greater complexity than PS20 due to the double bond in oleic acid, which can generate a wide variety of oxidation products (Borisov et al., 2015; Hvattum et al., 2012; Weber et al., 2025). To account for this complexity, we incorporated POE 18:1 hydroxyl, a C18:1 oxidation product, into our generative model for PS80. We then compared the rate at which bi-modality develops in POE 16:0 distributions with the reduction of POE 18:1 molecules and the corresponding increase in POE 18:1-hydroxyl, a primary oxidation product (Fig. 9A-D). Our results show that POE 16:0 transitions to a bi-modal distribution, in PS80, is much slower than in PS20. This is demonstrated by a significantly faster increase in the “f_degraded_j” (describes oxidation state of the respective PS sub-species) parameter of POE 16:0 for PS20 than PS80 (Fig. 9D). In contrast, POE 18:1 molecules in PS80 show already a substantial intensity decrease at oxidation score 2 (Fig. 9B), correlating directly with increased levels of POE 18:1-hydroxyl (Fig. 9C). This rapid decline aligns with previously established vulnerability of unsaturated sites in POE 18:1 to oxidation (Brovč et al., 2020; Kishore et al., 2011; Yao et al., 2009). Notably, POE 18:1-hydroxyl exhibits a two-stage oxidation process—initially increasing for molecules centered around 11 EO subunits (scores 2–3), before transitioning to a bi-modal state centered at approximately 5 EO subunits (Fig. 9C)—a pattern attributable to the oleic acid's susceptibility to oxidation.

The oxidation process occurs in two distinct stages. In the first stage, molecules containing C18:1 fatty acid undergo oxidation to form hydroxyl derivatives (Fig. 9B-C). Specifically, POE 18:1 directly converts to POE 18:1 hydroxyl, while sorbitan and isosorbide POE 18:1 molecules transform into their respective hydroxy substitutes. During the second stage, these sorbitan and isosorbide substitutes further oxidize, as described by Borisov et al. (2015), yielding sorbitan or isosorbide molecules alongside POE 18:1 hydroxyl (Borisov et al., 2015). The bi-modal distribution emerges only after the preferential C18:1 oxidation process nears completion. This sequential process is evident when comparing oxidation score 3 results for POE 18:1 (Fig. 9B) and POE 18:1 hydroxyl (Fig. 9C), where POE 18:1 has completely disappeared while POE 18:1 hydroxyl exhibits a clear bi-modal distribution. At oxidation score 4, we observed a reduction in the POE 18:1 hydroxyl marker (Fig. 9C). This is consistent with the total decrease in intensity of the TIC chromatogram due to oxidative degradation. Additional POE and sorbitan oxidation markers (hydroxyl, hydroperoxyl, etc.) were incorporated into the model. Furthermore, Kranz et al. (2020) described universal oxidation markers, occurring in the degraded peak of PS20 and PS80, were also included into the model and are described in more detail in the following chapter.

Comparative analysis of PS20 and PS80 oxidation pathways using POE-peroxide markers

Due to the different oxidation products of PS20 and PS80, a possibility to compare their “true” oxidation states could be the use of universal oxidation markers present in the non-esterified fraction, basically the degraded fraction (Fig. S3). We therefore incorporated POE-peroxide, a universal oxidation marker described by Kranz et al. (2020), into our generative model.(Kranz et al., 2020) Fig. 10 shows the cumulative intensity of POE-peroxide molecules for both surfactants. Surprisingly, despite PS80's reported higher vulnerability to oxidative degradation, (Kozuch et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2009), POE-peroxide levels increased significantly faster in PS20 at moderate oxidation scores (score 2 & 3; Fig. 10). For PS20, the intensity increased from around 0.25 to around 1.5·107, whereas for PS80, the increase in intensity was from around 0.25 to around 0.8·107. Therefore, we hypothesise that this accelerated peroxide formation in PS20 likely results from its simpler oxidation pathway, requiring only two steps. In contrast, PS80 requires a three-stage process. Therefore, PS80 requires more oxidation steps to end up in POE peroxide formation, despite its greater overall oxidation susceptibility due to oleic acid (C18:1) (Yao et al., 2009). At oxidation score 4, PS20 and PS80 exhibit comparable levels of POE peroxide, likely attributable to the depletion of non-oxidized substitutes. The comparative oxidation susceptibility of PS20 versus PS80 raises important questions. When considering oxidation end products such as aldehydes, ketones, and short-chain fatty acids, PS80 generally requires more oxidation steps than PS20 to reach these compounds. It is unclear whether oxidation products—such as POE sorbitan 18:1 hydroxyl or other oxidized oleic acid (C18:1) derivatives—negatively impact protein stability.

Total Intensity of the universal oxidation marker POE peroxide of the different oxidation scores for PS20 HP and PS80 HP. Score one contains 100 %, score two around 80 %, score three around 50 % and score four around 20 % of the intact fraction, respectively.

In general, we have demonstrated that PS80 oxidizes more rapidly (Fig. 9B), where at oxidation score 2–3 most of the POE18:1 molecules are oxidized due to the vulnerability of unsaturated fatty acids to oxidation. However, it remains unclear whether this represents a critical oxidation state (as these species still elute in the esterified fraction (intact fraction) of PS80) or if oxidation is better characterized by universal markers such as POE peroxide and short-chain POE molecules centered at approximately 5–6 OE units.

Differences between oxidative and hydrolytic degradation patterns

Our final experiment evaluated whether the generative model could distinguish between oxidative and hydrolytic degradation pathways. We formulated mAb 1 in 20 mM histidine buffer at pH 6.0, 0.4 mg·mL−1 PS20 and at a protein concentration of 50 mg·mL−1. mAb 1 is known to be enzymatically active toward PS20 due to co-purified host cell proteins (Graf et al., 2021; Gregoritza et al., 2024; Maier et al., 2024; Weiß et al., 2025). A placebo formulation under identical conditions was used as a control. Both formulations were stored for 4 months at 40 °C. In samples containing mAb 1, we observed no increase in short POE 12 esters but rather a uniform decrease in the intensity of the monomodal distribution of OE units (Fig. 11). This observation aligned with discussions, as the presence of the high mAb 1 concentration (50 mg/mL) could potentially inhibit oxidative degradation of PS. (Bensaid et al., 2022; Brovč et al., 2020; Gopalrathnam et al., 2018; Ha et al., 2002; Klair et al., 2021). In contrast, placebo formulations exhibited a significant increase in short POE 12 esters, indicating oxidative degradation as previously discussed (Carle et al., 2024).

Conclusions/outlook

Our developed generative model effectively extracts precise fingerprints of investigated samples at the molecular level (OE unit level), by performing an aggregated statistical analysis, parameters that describe the individual polysorbate sub-species are calculated. A significant advantage of this automated approach is the elimination of manual investigation of complex LC-MS data. The model successfully differentiates between individual species without the complications of co-elution and the resulting misinterpretations. In this initial study, we demonstrated that the algorithm is capable of extracting PS20 and PS80 at a sub-species level. Furthermore, we have shown that the algorithm can thoroughly analyze PS quality variations among different vendors and distinguish them at the sub-species level. This capability enables identification of PS qualities with superior properties regarding particle formation, particularly in terms of PS sub-species composition and their esterification of long chain FA, which are generally considered more critical (Doshi et al., 2020a, Doshi et al., 2020b; Glücklich et al., 2020; Groß et al., 2025).

By analyzing at the molecular level, our model readily distinguishes between oxidative and hydrolytic degradation. The incorporation of bi-modality into the model enables easy monitoring of shifts from longer to shorter POE chains during oxidation, a feature particularly valuable for PS20, where no novel oxidation species emerge (Carle et al., 2024; Evers et al., 2020). For PS80, we integrated literature-defined oxidation markers (Borisov et al., 2015; Weber et al., 2023). This comprehensive approach allows simultaneous monitoring of both oxidative and hydrolytic degradation, enabling implementation of appropriate mitigation strategies from the earliest stages of formulation development (Bensaid et al., 2022; Doshi et al., 2021; Doyle et al., 2019; Kranz et al., 2019; Yarbrough et al., 2019). Additionally, the inclusion of the universal oxidation marker POE peroxide in our generative model facilitates more detailed comparative assessment of oxidative states between PS80 and PS20 (Kranz et al., 2020). Regarding hydrolytic degradation, the literature frequently describes specific fingerprints associated with particular hydrolases (Glücklich et al., 2021). Our model now enables investigation of these patterns at the subspecies level, potentially allowing categorization of hydrolases based on their distinctive cutting patterns.

In summary, the generative model offers several key advantages:
