Barbershop-based lung health navigation for Black men: A feasibility study
Authors: Alicia K Matthews, Suchanart Inwanna, Rohan D Jeremiah, Guy Morris, Miguel Negrete, Sage J Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveBlack men experience a disproportionate burden of lung cancer yet remain underrepresented in screening and smoking cessation programs. Community-based approaches combining culturally relevant information with patient navigation may help address these gaps.MethodsThis Phase II developmental feasibility study examined a culturally targeted, navigator-led lung health intervention to support knowledge, decision-making, and early engagement with lung cancer screening and cessation resources among Black men who smoke (March 2023–June 2024). Forty participants recruited through barbershop outreach completed three staged sessions–an in-person educational visit with targeted materials and a screening decision aid, followed by brief navigation contacts. Outcomes were assessed at baseline, post-intervention, and 6-week follow-up.ResultsParticipants demonstrated improvements in lung cancer-related knowledge, decisional clarity, and readiness to consider screening and cessation. Satisfaction with targeted materials and navigator support was high. Nearly half reported contacting healthcare providers regarding screening, and some scheduled low-dose CT examinations, while Quitline engagement remained modest.ConclusionFindings support the feasibility and acceptability of culturally targeted navigation delivered in barbershops and may strengthen early behavioral engagement along the lung health continuum for Black men. Larger controlled trials with longer follow-up are needed to evaluate sustained behavioral outcomes.Keywords: Health disparities, Lung cancer screening, Smoking cessation, Black men's health, Patient navigation, Feasibility study

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related deaths in the United States (American Cancer Society, 2025). Black men experience a disproportionate burden, with higher age-adjusted incidence and mortality rates than White men; recent data indicate mortality rates of approximately 51.0 per 100,000 among Black men compared with 44.7 per 100,000 among White men (American Cancer Society, 2025; National Cancer Institute, 2025). These disparities reflect structural and socioeconomic barriers to care, differential exposure to carcinogens, and biological mechanisms that may influence risk, including epigenetic regulation of gene expression and population variation in nicotine metabolism (Monestime et al., 2023; Murphy, 2021).

Results from the National Lung Screening Trial confirmed that low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening reduces lung cancer mortality by about 20% among high-risk adults (Aberle et al., 2011; Gudina et al., 2025). Guidelines recommend annual LDCT for adults aged 50 to 80 years with a 20-pack-year smoking history who smoke or quit within the past 15 years (US Preventive Services Task Force, 2021). Screening uptake remains low, with approximately 1 in 5 eligible adults obtaining an initial scan (Gudina et al., 2025) and half of those completing annual follow ups (Kim et al., 2025). Black men are screened at even lower rates due to barriers such as limited awareness, inconsistent provider recommendations, structural challenges, and medical mistrust (Aspiras et al., 2025; Carter-Harris et al., 2018).

Community-engaged approaches, including targeted interventions and patient navigation, are effective strategies for addressing cancer inequities (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, Medicine, 2024). Culturally targeted outreach improves knowledge, attitudes, and participation in screening (Kwok and Xu, 2025; Ochoa et al., 2020), and patient navigation increases screening completion and timely follow-up (Chan et al., 2023). However, few lung cancer prevention interventions reach Black men in trusted community settings, such as barbershops, while simultaneously addressing both lung cancer screening and smoking cessation.

This study builds on community-engaged approaches to men's health by developing and testing a staged, culturally targeted lung health intervention to support engagement in lung cancer screening and smoking cessation. Consistent with developmental intervention frameworks, the program combined education and decision support with patient navigation and structured follow-up. The study aims were to: (Aim 1) evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of delivering a culturally targeted, navigator-led lung health intervention in barbershop settings, including recruitment, retention, intervention completion, and participant satisfaction; (Aim 2) examine preliminary mechanistic trajectories associated with intervention exposure, including changes in lung cancer-related knowledge, decisional processes, and readiness for lung cancer screening and smoking cessation; and (Aim 3) characterize behavioral activation along the lung health continuum, including provider contact, screening scheduling or completion, and smoking cessation.

Methods

Study design and population

This single-arm Phase II developmental feasibility study, conducted from March 2023 to June 2024, evaluated a culturally targeted, navigator-led lung health intervention designed to support knowledge, decision-making, and engagement with lung cancer screening and smoking cessation among Black men. The study was approved by the University of Illinois Chicago Institutional Review Board (IRB#2023–0273).

Participants were a volunteer sample of Black men. Eligibility criteria included: (1) age 50 to 80 years; (2) current or former smoker; and (3) availability to attend in-person sessions. Participants were enrolled regardless of smoking pack-year history to reflect the broader population of Black men at risk for lung cancer.

This study followed the Obesity-Related Behavioral Intervention Trials (ORBIT) model for developing behavioral treatments (Czajkowski et al., 2015). It evaluated feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary mechanistic trajectories. A target sample of 40 participants was selected to estimate key feasibility parameters (e.g., recruitment, retention, and fidelity) with reasonable precision and to descriptively characterize within-person change in proximal targets, including knowledge, decision processes, and behavioral intentions. Consistent with early-phase behavioral intervention research, this sample size was intended to inform progression criteria and design parameters for a future controlled trial rather than efficacy.

The intervention was delivered at two barbershops in predominantly Black communities in Chicago, where smoking and tobacco-related mortality rates are higher than the city average (Chicago Health Atlas, 2023). Barbershops were selected based on prior community-engaged research partnerships (Negrete et al., 2024).

Staff conducted recruitment at partner barbershops and local community events. Shop owners and barbers shared study information with clients. Following eligibility determination and consenting, staff explained study procedures, administered baseline surveys, and scheduled the first intervention session.

The intervention consisted of three sessions (Fig. 1) informed by formative research, culturally targeted health education principles, and patient navigation approaches (Kreuter et al., 2003; Matthews et al., 2024). All procedures, from enrollment through final follow-up, were designed to be completed within six weeks. During Session 1, participants met in person with a trained patient navigator who delivered a standardized protocol. Navigators presented a brief (5-min), culturally adapted lung cancer screening video (https://youtu.be/eDrIFTNtO9U) followed by a cancer education session using materials adapted from the American Cancer Society (https://www.cancer.org). Navigators then guided participants through a lung cancer screening decision aid (Li et al., 2023), adapted with feedback from community citizen scientists to enhance cultural relevance for Black men who smoke. Participants established lung health goals, including scheduling a lung cancer screening appointment if eligible and enrolling in smoking cessation support through the state Quitline. Participants also received written cessation materials from the American Lung Association.

Schematic of intervention protocol for a barbershop-based lung health intervention among Black men in Chicago, Illinois, March 2023–June 2024 - This figure depicts a Phase II single-arm feasibility study of a culturally targeted, navigator-led lung health intervention delivered in barbershops. Participants completed screening, consent, and baseline assessment, followed by three navigator contacts over ∼6 weeks (two in-person, one phone). The intervention supported progression toward lung cancer screening and smoking cessation, with screening occurring outside the intervention. Outcomes included progression along the screening continuum (provider contact, scheduling, and LDCT completion), engagement with cessation services, and changes in knowledge and decisional certainty.

Session 2 occurred approximately one week later and consisted of a 15-min telephone check-in. Navigators reviewed progress toward goals, addressed logistical barriers, reinforced motivations for change, and provided additional education as needed. Session 3 was conducted in person approximately two weeks later. Navigators assessed whether participants had scheduled or completed LDCT screening and whether they had contacted the Quitline or engaged in other cessation activities. For those not meeting their goals, navigators explored barriers and provided additional support. Post-intervention surveys assessed changes in lung cancer knowledge, attitudes, decisional conflict, and satisfaction. Participants received up to $150 in compensation.

Two trained patient navigators delivered the intervention. Navigators received training from a licensed psychologist (AKM) to build rapport, facilitate discussions about tobacco use and lung cancer risk, and promote shared decision-making (Freund et al., 2014). A manualized curriculum, standardized PowerPoint slides, an educational video, and a decision aid guided the intervention. Navigators completed a fidelity checklist after each session and participated in weekly supervision meetings to enhance fidelity (Damschroder et al., 2009).

Measures

Primary outcomes

Behavioral engagement in lung cancer screening and smoking cessation. At follow-up, participants self-reported engagement in lung cancer screening behaviors, including contacting a provider, scheduling LDCT screening, and completing screening (yes/no). Engagement with smoking cessation services included self-reported contact with the Quitline and participation in other cessation activities (yes/no).

Decisional processes. Decisional certainty regarding lung cancer screening was assessed using the four-item SURE (Sure of myself, Understand information, Risk–benefit ratio, Encouragement) screening test (Légaré et al., 2010). Scores range from 0 to 4, with higher scores indicating greater certainty in the decision. Broader decisional conflict was assessed using the 16-item Decisional Conflict Scale (DCS), scored yes = 0, unsure = 2, and no = 4. Scores were summed, divided by 16, and multiplied by 25 to produce a total score ranging from 0 (no decisional conflict) to 100 (high decisional conflict). Higher scores mean more decisional conflict.

Secondary measures

Demographics. Participants self-reported age, education, employment, relationship status, health insurance coverage, and whether they had a healthcare provider.

Smoking history. Smoking behaviors were assessed using standardized self-report items (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2022), including lifetime cigarette use (≥100 cigarettes), current tobacco use, smoking frequency and intensity, time to first cigarette, age of initiation, cigarette type, use of other tobacco products, provider advice to quit, quit attempts, and cessation treatment use. Pack-years were calculated as the average number of cigarettes smoked per day, divided by 20, and multiplied by the number of years smoked. Individuals with pack year scores of greater than or equal to 20 were eligible for lung cancer screening.

Knowledge. Lung cancer symptom knowledge was assessed using 10 items that reflect common warning signs (score range: 0–10). Lung cancer screening knowledge was assessed using four items that addressed screening benefits and harms (score range: 0–4), with higher scores indicating greater knowledge.

Attitudes toward screening and smoking cessation. Readiness, confidence, and motivation were each assessed using single-item Likert scales (1 = completely disagree to 5 = completely agree). All questions range from 1 to 5, with higher scores reflecting more favorable attitudes.

Process measures

Intervention acceptability and satisfaction. Participants evaluated the lung health video (satisfaction, meets needs, ease of use) using acceptability items (Yes/No). Satisfaction with navigator knowledge, materials, responsiveness, and perceived value of navigation services was assessed using 5-point Likert scales (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), with higher scores representing more satisfaction.

Barriers and navigation needs. Participants indicated barriers to screening or cessation (e.g., transportation, motivation, confidence) and whether additional navigation support was desired (Yes/No).

Data analysis

Analyses followed an estimation-focused approach aligned with ORBIT Phase II guidance. Results emphasize descriptive summaries and mean changes from baseline (T1) to post-intervention (T2) and 6-week follow-up (T3). Continuous outcomes are reported as means (SD), with change scores calculated as post–pre (T2-T1; T3-T1); positive values indicate improvement in knowledge, decision-making, and psychosocial constructs. Behavioral endpoints (provider contact, LDCT scheduling/completion, and smoking cessation engagement) are summarized as frequencies and percentages and interpreted as indicators of early behavioral activation. Progression benchmarks reflect early-stage targets, with lower thresholds for distal outcomes (e.g., completion) than for earlier steps, and are interpreted within the USPSTF-eligible subgroup for screening outcomes (see Table 4). Analyses used available data at each time point; given the developmental focus and high retention, no imputation was performed, and denominators are reported for transparency. Observed changes were interpreted against predefined feasibility and progression criteria. Analyses used SPSS Statistics version 30.0.

Results

Sample characteristics

The mean age of participants was 59.0 years (SD = 6.69). Approximately 50% had completed high school, and were in a serious relationship (50.0%), and most were not currently working (72.5%). Health insurance coverage was high (90.0%), and 75.0% reported having a regular healthcare provider (see Supplement 1).

Table 1 summarizes the participants' smoking history. Smoking exposure was substantial: 36 of 40 participants (90.0%) reported current tobacco use, and 80.0% smoked cigarettes 6–7 days per week. Participants smoked an average of 9.3 cigarettes per day (SD = 6.69), and the mean pack-year history was 18.9 (SD = 16.46). Menthol cigarette use was common (87.5%). Over half (21/40; 52.5%) reported a quit attempt in the past year, while 17 of 40 (42.5%) reported no prior use of cessation support.

Participant flow, feasibility, and intervention engagement

A total of 40 participants enrolled, completed baseline assessment (T1), and attended the initial navigator session (see Supplement 2). Retention at the six-week assessment timepoint was high, with 37 of 40 participants (92.5%) retained. Recruitment occurred through barbershop partners (27.0%), word of mouth (40.5%), flyers (8.1%), street outreach (10.8%), and navigator networks (13.5%). Intervention delivery occurred as planned, with high session completion and strong navigator fidelity to core components.

Feasibility progression

Feasibility indicators demonstrated strong implementation performance. Enrollment targets were fully achieved (40/40; 100.0%), and all enrolled participants initiated the intervention. Intervention completion remained high, with 37 of 40 participants completing all sessions (92.5%), and six-week retention was similarly strong (37/40; 92.5%).

Preliminary signals of change across mechanistic and behavioral readiness outcomes

Descriptive outcomes for knowledge, decisional processes, and behavioral readiness are summarized in Table 2. Knowledge outcomes were largely stable across timepoints. Lung cancer symptom knowledge increased slightly from baseline (M = 4.83, SD = 3.75) to post-intervention (M = 5.00, SD = 4.18; Δ = 0.17) and follow-up (M = 5.12, SD = 4.14; Δ = 0.29). Screening knowledge showed minimal change from baseline (M = 2.25, SD = 1.15) to post-intervention (M = 2.30, SD = 0.88; Δ = 0.05) and follow-up (M = 2.24, SD = 1.16; Δ = −0.01).

Decisional processes demonstrated clearer improvement. Decisional certainty, measured using the SURE scale, increased substantially from baseline (M = 2.20, SD = 1.09) to post-intervention (M = 3.68, SD = 0.62; Δ = 1.48) and remained elevated at follow-up (M = 3.46, SD = 0.77; Δ = 1.26). Decisional conflict decreased from baseline (M = 27.58, SD = 10.13) to post-intervention (M = 23.91, SD = 5.41; Δ = −3.67) and remained lower than baseline at follow-up (M = 25.25, SD = 8.85; Δ = −2.33).

Behavioral readiness outcomes showed gradual increases over time. Screening intention rose from baseline (M = 12.43, SD = 2.33) to post-intervention (M = 13.10, SD = 1.33; Δ = 0.67) and follow-up (M = 14.16, SD = 2.04; Δ = 1.73). Similarly, intention to quit smoking increased from baseline (M = 11.80, SD = 2.95) to post-intervention (M = 12.88, SD = 2.32; Δ = 1.08) and follow-up (M = 13.84, SD = 1.98; Δ = 2.04). Overall, the largest changes were observed in decisional certainty, while readiness outcomes showed gradual upward trajectories.

Behavioral change

Screening outcomes are summarized in Table 3. Because LDCT screening behaviors are clinically meaningful only among individuals meeting established guidelines (US Preventive Services Task Force, 2021), additional descriptive analyses focused on participants who were current or former smokers aged ≥50 years with a > 20-pack-year smoking history (n = 17). At the six-week follow-up, engagement among USPSTF-eligible participants demonstrated substantial behavioral activation: 10 of 17 participants (58.8%) reported contacting a healthcare provider for screening, 8 of 17 (47.1%) reported scheduling screening, and 3 of 17 (17.6%) reported completing screening.

At the follow-up, 7 of 37 participants (18.9%) reported using formal cessation services, such as consulting a healthcare provider. Additionally, a larger proportion reported using self-directed strategies (n = 19, 51.4%). Although formal cessation uptake remained within the “Amend” range of developmental benchmarks, the observed engagement shows initial signs of movement toward cessation-related behavior change.

Barriers to screening and navigation needs

Few barriers to lung cancer screening or engaging in cessation services were reported. Scheduling challenges were the most endorsed barrier, reported by 16.2% of participants. No participants indicated a lack of confidence in the information provided as a barrier, and only 5.4% reported motivational barriers. A small proportion of participants (10.8%) expressed interest in additional navigation to address remaining barriers.

Satisfaction

All participants (N = 37, 100%) indicated that the video and decision aid were appealing, satisfactory, and met their needs. However, between 35% and 45% of participants reported that some information was difficult to understand. Navigators were universally rated as knowledgeable (100%), and 81.1% indicated they would recommend navigation services (see Table 4).

Discussion

We examined the feasibility, acceptability, and early behavioral signals associated with lung health promotion among Black men. As summarized in Table 4, recruitment, retention, implementation, fidelity, and lung cancer screening outcomes met predefined “Go” criteria, while smoking cessation engagement fell within the “Amend” range, suggesting opportunities for refinement rather than concerns about feasibility. Participants demonstrated improvements in risk factor awareness and decisional clarity, with nearly half contacting a provider about screening and approximately one-quarter scheduling an LDCT scan within six weeks. Because the intervention was designed to support lung health engagement in a broader community sample, clinically actionable screening endpoints were necessarily concentrated in the subgroup meeting USPSTF eligibility criteria. These shifts are encouraging in a population with historically low screening uptake (Jemal and Fedewa, 2017), suggesting that this approach may hold promise for supporting lung health promotion among Black men.

The staged structure of the intervention provides insight into how participants progressed through engagement phases. Knowledge gains were modest and may have stemmed from measurement issues or the need to further adapt the educational materials to include the most relevant knowledge related to lung cancer and lung cancer screening. However, improvements in decisional clarity appeared to accompany early behavioral actions such as contacting providers and scheduling screening appointments. These findings align with evidence that culturally targeted interventions and patient navigation can enhance cancer screening in underserved communities (Chan et al., 2023; Freund et al., 2014; Kreuter et al., 2003). Delivering interventions within trusted community settings, such as barbershops, may further strengthen engagement by reaching Black men who may be less connected to traditional healthcare systems (Matthews et al., 2022). In this context, early behavioral activation may represent a meaningful developmental outcome rather than an intermediate step toward predefined clinical endpoints.

Consistent with prior formative work (Matthews et al., 2022), and well-established evidence that increases in readiness and intention do not reliably translate into behavior change (Sheeran and Webb, 2016), increased readiness did not uniformly result in screening completion or sustained cessation engagement within the short follow-up period. Viewed through a Goal Attainment Scaling framework, these findings suggest accelerated progress at early engagement stages - particularly provider contact and scheduling - while screening completion reflects later developmental milestones that may extend beyond the timeframe of feasibility-stage interventions. Importantly, scheduling may signal strong motivation and willingness to engage, even when factors outside the intervention's scope influence completion.

Feasibility testing also highlighted implementation considerations. During recruitment, relatively few participants met the ≥20 pack-year threshold, consistent with prior research on screening eligibility among Black adults who smoke (Li et al., 2024; Li et al., 2019). As a result, the sample enrolled included individuals with a broader range of smoking histories (mean = 16 pack-years). Among individuals eligible for screening based on national guidelines, engagement activities were observed, including self-reported scheduling of provider and screening appointments, whereas actual screening participation was lower. Lowered screening rates may have been due to the short follow-up period of the intervention. However, some participants encountered challenges scheduling or attending screening appointments, suggesting that additional navigation or logistical support may be beneficial in future iterations. Navigation to a centralized lung cancer screening program may have improved outcomes; however, participants differed in types of insurance coverage and proximity to the participant barbershops, which may have also served as barriers. Onsight screening may have also improved screening outcomes, however the availability of mobile screening units are limited.

Prior work indicates that addressing health-related social needs through connections with community-based organizations can improve clinic attendance and adherence to care (Yan et al., 2022). These observations underscore the importance of considering social and structural context when designing community-based lung health interventions. Scheduling may reflect readiness and motivation, but additional qualitative inquiry may help clarify factors influencing follow-through.

Smoking cessation outcomes reflected a similar developmental pattern. Although formal engagement with Quitline services remained modest, a larger proportion of participants reported using self-directed cessation strategies, including reducing smoking or initiating quit attempts independently. These patterns suggest early movement toward cessation-related behavior change that may precede engagement with formal treatment services. Prior research indicates that sustained quitting is difficult without comprehensive behavioral and pharmacologic support (Rigotti et al., 2022). Because the intervention emphasized lung health education and navigation rather than intensive cessation treatment, these findings likely reflect early motivational activation rather than treatment-level engagement. Future work may examine whether integrating pharmacotherapy and structured counseling enhances engagement in evidence-based treatments and actual cessation.

Beyond feasibility, this study contributes to the literature by illustrating how a staged, culturally responsive intervention can support early engagement in lung cancer screening and smoking cessation among Black men. Integrating decision support, patient navigation, and community-based outreach within a unified framework extends prior barbershop-based models (Cole et al., 2017) toward a broader lung health strategy. The results highlight the value of eligibility-aware progression, emphasizing early behavioral activation as a developmental milestone preceding clinical completion of screening or sustained cessation. Participants generally appeared well supported in navigating screening and cessation decisions following exposure to the intervention, although some continued to benefit from targeted logistical assistance. Consistent with the study's developmental focus, reported barriers primarily reflected downstream implementation challenges rather than informational or motivational deficits, underscoring the importance of addressing system-level factors influencing follow-through after initial engagement.

This study has limitations. The single-arm design limits attribution of observed changes solely to the intervention. The lower-than-anticipated pack-year exposure raises concern that standard lung cancer screening criteria may exclude high-risk Black men because of lower cumulative smoking exposure (Li et al., 2019; US Preventive Services Task Force, 2021). Although appropriate for a feasibility pilot, the small sample size and recruitment from a single setting limit generalizability. Behavioral outcomes, including LDCT scheduling and Quitline engagement, were measured via self-report (Carter-Harris et al., 2018). Studies have documented discrepancies between self-reported and verified cancer screening behaviors (Bhattacharyya et al., 2023; Katz et al., 2022), a limitation that may be particularly relevant for lung cancer screening given limited public awareness of screening procedures (Sabour et al., 2025). Future studies should objectively verify screening outcomes, assess longer-term engagement, and validate the study-specific progression benchmarks used in Table 4.

Conclusions

Preliminary evidence suggests that a targeted, navigator-led lung health intervention is feasible and acceptable for Black men. Future research should test the refined intervention in a larger randomized trial with longer follow-up and objective outcome verification. Incorporating practical supports, such as reminders, transportation, and scheduling, may help translate early motivation into completed screening and cessation-related activities.
