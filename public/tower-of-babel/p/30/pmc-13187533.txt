Cold atmospheric plasma (CAP) stimulates melanoblast-to-melanocyte differentiation via PRKCQ upregulation and NF-κB activation
Authors: Ying Zeng, Ronghua Pan, Dong Li, Lanlan Nie, Xiaoqi Nie, Yujia Wei, Xinpei Lu, Yunhua Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryVitiligo, a chronic and recurrent skin disorder characterized by cosmetically disfiguring depigmentation, poses a significant challenge in dermatological treatment. Cold atmospheric plasma (CAP), a promising modality in biomedical applications, has been reported to promote repigmentation in vitiligo lesions. Herein, we investigate how CAP promotes repigmentation. A murine model of vitiligo was established via topical administration of hydroquinone cream and subsequently treated with CAP jet to the depigmented areas. Immortalized melanoblasts (iMC23) were used as the in vitro model, with CCK-8 assays and flow cytometry used for functional analyses. The results showed that CAP treatment enhanced melanoblast-to-melanocyte differentiation in vitiligo mice, particularly within the hair follicle bulge. In iMC23 cells, CAP promoted cell survival, melanogenic gene expression, and melanin synthesis. Mechanistically, CAP upregulated PRKCQ expression and activated NF-κB signaling. In conclusion, our findings underscore the therapeutic potential of CAP in vitiligo management, highlighting its role in driving melanoblast-to-melanocyte differentiation.Subject areas: dermatology, molecular biology, stem cells research

Introduction

Vitiligo is the most common depigmenting dermatological disorder, with a prevalence of approximately 0.1–2% in the global population.1 Clinically, it is characterized by distinct, non-scaly, depigmented macules, resulting from a progressive loss of functional melanocytes.2 These macules are predominantly located on the face and limbs and may be accompanied by leukotrichia (white hair).3 While vitiligo is not life-threatening or typically associated with severe discomfort such as itching or pain, it often leads to mental distress due to the observable signs of vitiligo lesions, which adversely impact patients' quality of life.4 Current treatment options for vitiligo include physical interventions, topical therapies, systemic medications, and surgical procedures.5,6 However, due to the disease’s refractory nature, these treatments often yield inconsistent or suboptimal outcomes, with frequent recurrence and progressive skin changes,6 underscoring the need for ongoing research into more effective therapeutic approaches.

Melanocyte stem cells (McSCs) are a group of skin stem cells resident in specific niches, including the mammalian hair follicle bulge. Generally, McSCs remain quiescent; however, during hair cycles or after injury, they can differentiate into mature melanin-producing melanocytes, which are responsible for skin and hair pigmentation and skin function maintenance.7 Melanoblasts, derived from neural crest cells, are precursors that can differentiate into melanocytes.8

Cold atmospheric plasma (CAP), a near-room-temperature ionized gas, has gained increasing attention as a versatile intervention, with applications in oncology, sterilization, and wound healing.9,10,11 It is known for generating a spectrum of biologically active species, including reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS/RNS), ultraviolet radiation, cationic ions, and free electrons.12 Evidence suggests that CAP shows great potential for use in dermatology treatment.13 For instance, CAP attenuates imiquimod-induced psoriasiform dermatitis in a mouse model.14 Ma et al. demonstrated that CAP ameliorates radiation-triggered skin damage by promoting cell proliferation and DNA damage repair.15 Importantly, a previous randomized controlled trial indicated that CAP application can induce repigmentation in vitiligo lesions, with no hyperpigmentation observed in the surrounding regions during or after application.16 Moreover, Nie et al. demonstrated that CAP can promote melanoblast differentiation into mature melanocytes.17 Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms underlying CAP-mediated repigmentation in vitiligo remain not fully understood.

This study aims to investigate the molecular mechanisms by which CAP induces repigmentation in vitiligo using a murine model and an in vitro cell model, seeking innovative approaches to address pigmentary disorders. It was speculated that CAP might exert its effect by inducing melanoblast differentiation into melanocytes by regulating downstream genes and signaling pathways.

Results

Effect of CAP treatment on hydroquinone-induced vitiligo in mice

A murine model of vitiligo was established by the topical application of 3% hydroquinone cream. Compared to control mice, mice treated with hydroquinone cream exhibited apparent depigmentation 20 days post-application. By day 30, these mice exhibited typical symptoms of vitiligo, including pale patches and white hair (Figure 1A). Notably, the depigmentation area score was significantly increased with the prolonged application of hydroquinone (Figure 1B). Paraffin-embedded skin sections were prepared for histological analysis using H&E staining and Masson-Fontana staining. These results revealed a significant reduction in melanin deposition within the hair follicles of hydroquinone-treated mice, with partial melanin loss observed in some follicles (Figure 1C), confirming the successful establishment of vitiligo in mice. After successful modeling, these vitiligo mice were given CAP treatment using a CAP jet device (Figure 1D). A group of vitiligo mice received UVB phototherapy as the positive control. Following CAP or UVB treatment, a notable increase in pigmentation and black hair growth was observed in hydroquinone-induced mice. The degree of pigmentation and hair growth was positively correlated with the duration of single CAP interventions, suggesting a pronounced therapeutic effect of CAP on skin lesions in these mice (Figure 1E). Quantitative analysis revealed that the depigmentation scores for the CAP-treated groups were significantly lower than those in the vitiligo alone group after 3 weeks of treatment (Figures 1E and 1F). Additionally, CAP treatment was more effective than UVB phototherapy in promoting repigmentation (Figures 1E and 1F). However, CAP treatment durations of 120 or 180 s induced some degree of skin and hair burning and scabbing in vitiligo mice (Figure 1E). Moreover, to evaluate potential post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation from tissue damage as a confounder, we performed RT-qPCR analysis of key inflammatory mediators IFN-γ and TNF-α in lesional skin at day 21 post-treatment. Notably, IFN-γ and TNF-α mRNA levels were markedly elevated in the hydroquinone-induced vitiligo model group compared to normal controls. CAP treatment at 60 s significantly reduced their expression. Importantly, even in the 120-s and 180-s groups, IFN-γ and TNF-α remained significantly suppressed compared to the vitiligo group, with no evidence of exacerbated pro-inflammatory response (Figure 1G). These results indicate that the observed repigmentation is primarily driven by CAP-mediated immunomodulation rather than secondary to thermal injury-induced inflammation. Additionally, a 60-s CAP treatment administered once daily for three weeks may be considered the optimal treatment duration. Repigmented skin tissues were collected from vitiligo mice undergoing 60-s CAP treatment once daily for three weeks, followed by IF staining to detect melanogenesis-related protein expression. As shown in Figure 1H, compared to those without CAP treatment, vitiligo mice treated with CAP exhibited significantly increased expression levels of tyrosinase-related protein 1 (TYRP1) and tyrosinase-related protein 2 (TRP-2) in the hair follicle bulge. TRP-2 was predominantly expressed in McSCs within the bulge, while both TYRP1 and TRP-2 were detected in mature melanocytes. These results suggest that CAP treatment induces the differentiation of McSCs in the hair follicle bulge into melanocytes, thus promoting the repigmentation of vitiligo patches.

CAP-mediated differentiation of iMC23 cells into melanocytes

The mechanism underlying CAP-induced melanoblast-to-melanocyte differentiation was further investigated in iMC23 cells. To determine the optimal treatment conditions for cellular experiments, iMC23 cells were exposed to CAP for 0, 20, 40, 60, and 80 s, followed by culture for 24, 36, and 48 h, respectively. Cellular morphology was observed under an inverted phase-contrast microscope. As shown in Figure 2A, compared to other treatment conditions, the synaptic structures of iMC23 cells were significantly increased following 60 s of CAP treatment and subsequent 48 h of culture. Given the low tyrosinase (TYR) activity in melanoblasts, the upregulation of melanocyte markers indicates their differentiation toward melanocytes. RT-qPCR was implemented to detect mRNA levels of the melanocyte markers (TRP-2, TYRPP1, TYR, and MITF) in iMC23 cells following CAP treatment. Notably, the mRNA levels of these markers in iMC23 cells were upregulated by CAP in a time-dependent manner, peaking at 60 s of CAP treatment and then declining. Of all the treatment conditions, 60 s of CAP treatment followed by 48 h of culture resulted in the highest expression of these melanogenic genes in iMC23 cells (Figures 2B–2E, Table 1), suggesting that the differentiation of melanoblasts into mature melanocytes occurred at 48 h. Consistent results were shown by western blotting (Figure 2F) and IF staining (Figure S2). In parallel, we found that CAP treatment for 60 s markedly elevated melanin content in iMC23 cells, especially after 48 h of culture (Figure 2G). As shown by TEM, there was a significant increase in the quantity of stage III–IV melanosomes in iMC23 cells after 60-s CAP treatment and 48 h of culture (Figures 2H and 2I), indicating that 60-s CAP facilitated the development of melanosomes. Moreover, as depicted by CCK-8 assays, iMC23 cell viability was remarkably increased following CAP treatment for 20, 40, 60, or 80 s and a 48-h incubation period, while the viability peaked at 60 s and decreased at 80 s (Figure 2J). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that 60-s CAP treatment increased the proportion of iMC23 cells at the G0/G1 phase (Figures 2K and 2L). Collectively, CAP treatment can promote the differentiation of iMC23 cells into melanocytes.

CAP-mediated NF-κB signaling activation and PRKCQ upregulation in iMC23 cells and vitiligo mice

iMC23 cells were randomly assigned to control and 60-s CAP treatment groups, with three biological replicates for each group. Transcriptome sequencing was employed to identify DEGs between the control and CAP-treated groups. With the screening criteria (|log2 fold-change|>0, p < 0.05), a total of 1,292 DEGs were identified, including 556 upregulated genes and 736 downregulated genes (Figure 3A and Table S1). The top 24 DEGs were displayed in the heatmap (Figure 3B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis demonstrated that the DEGs were significantly enriched in Alzheimer's disease, NF-κB signaling pathway, and fluid shear stress and atherosclerosis (Figure 3C). The NF-κB pathway has been indicated to participate in vitiligo progression.18 Therefore, this signaling pathway was considered a potential target of CAP treatment and selected for further analysis. Three DEGs were enriched in the NF-κB signaling pathway, including PRKCQ, NFKBIA, and DDX58. RT-qPCR analysis was conducted to validate their expression levels in iMC23 cells with CAP treatment. The results showed that all three genes were upregulated in iMC23 cells after 60-s CAP treatment, among which PRKCQ showed the most significant upregulation (Figure 3D). Thus, we selected PRKCQ for subsequent experiments. Western blotting was performed to evaluate PRKCQ and NF-κB p65 protein levels in iMC23 cells under CAP treatment. As depicted by the results, PRKCQ and p-NF-κB p65 protein levels in iMC23 cells treated with 60-s CAP were substantially higher than those in cells treated with CAP for 0, 40, or 80 s (Figures 4A–4C). Moreover, IF staining revealed that CAP promoted p-NF-κB p65 expression and NF-κB nuclear translocation in iMC23 cells in a duration-dependent manner, peaking at 60 s and decreasing at 80 s (Figure 4D). Additionally, we evaluated PRKCQ expression and NF-κB activation in the hair follicles of vitiligo mice. As shown in Figures S3A and S3B, p-NF-κB p65 and PRKCQ protein levels were remarkably reduced in the hair follicles of vitiligo mice compared to control mice. In contrast, CAP administration promoted NF-κB p65 phosphorylation and PRKCQ expression in vitiligo mice. RT-qPCR analysis was used to detect mRNA levels of PRKCQ and IL-6, a target gene of NF-κB signaling. As shown in Figure S3C, PRKCQ and IL-6 mRNA levels were increased in vitiligo mice with CAP treatment. The above results indicate that CAP treatment is associated with NF-κB signaling activation and PRKCQ upregulation in iMC23 cells.

CAP activates the NF-κB pathway and facilitates differentiation toward melanocytes in iMC23 cells by upregulating PRKCQ

To elucidate the involvement of PRKCQ in CAP-induced iMC23 cell differentiation into melanocytes, we knocked down or overexpressed PRKCQ in iMC23 cells using sh-PRKCQ or oe-PRKCQ, followed by exposure to CAP for 60 s and culture for 48 h. The knockdown and overexpression efficiencies of PRKCQ were confirmed by RT-qPCR analysis (Figure 5A). As demonstrated by RT-qPCR and western blotting, sh-PRKCQ transfection significantly reduced CAP-induced PRKCQ upregulation in iMC23 cells. However, this effect was reversed by oe-PRKCQ (Figures 5B–5D). Moreover, relative to the CAP alone group, knocking down PRKCQ led to decreased p-NF-κB p65 protein levels in iMC23 cells exposed to 60-s CAP, whereas simultaneous overexpression of PRKCQ significantly attenuated this effect (Figures 5C and 5D), indicating PRKCQ’s involvement in CAP-mediated NF-κB signaling activation in iMC23 cells. The CCK-8 assay revealed that compared to the CAP group, iMC23 cell viability was increased in the CAP + sh-PRKCQ group. In contrast, iMC23 cell viability was significantly decreased in the CAP + sh-PRKCQ + oe-PRKCQ group relative to the CAP + sh-PRKCQ group (Figure 5E). In parallel, PRKCQ knockdown arrested CAP-induced iMC23 cell cycle progression, as indicated by decreased cell proportion at the G0/G1 phase and increased proportion at the S phase in the CAP plus sh-PRKCQ group (Figures 5F and 5G). However, the above effect induced by sh-PRKCQ was counteracted by the combination of oe-PRKCQ (Figures 5F and 5G). Furthermore, to validate whether PRKCQ is involved in CAP-mediated iMC23 cell differentiation into melanocytes, RT-qPCR and western blotting were conducted to detect melanogenic marker expression. Additionally, α-MSH, a well-known stimulator of melanogenesis, was used as a positive control for CAP treatment. As shown by the results, 60-s CAP treatment exhibited similar effects to α-MSH in facilitating the expression of melanogenic markers (TRP-2, TYRP1, TYR, and MITF) at both mRNA (Figures 6A–6D) and protein levels (Figures 6E–6I). Notably, CAP-induced upregulation of these melanogenic markers was counteracted by PRKCQ silencing, while combined PRKCQ overexpression rescued these effects of PRKCQ silencing (Figures 6A–6I). In addition, IF staining revealed that compared to those treated with 60-s CAP alone, iMC23 cells treated with 60-s CAP plus the NF-κB signaling inhibitor (BAY11-7082) showed significantly decreased TRP-2 expression (Figure S4). Furthermore, PRKCQ silencing restored the CAP-mediated increase in melanin content in iMC23 cells (Figure 6J). However, compared to the CAP + sh-PRKCQ group, the melanin content was significantly increased in the CAP + sh-PRKCQ + oe-PRKCQ (Figure 6J), indicating that CAP treatment promoted iMC23 cell differentiation into melanocytes, possibly by upregulating PRKCQ.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that CAP treatment induced melanoblast-to-melanocyte differentiation within the bulge region of hair follicles, leading to the repigmentation of hypopigmented lesions in vitiligo mice. Moreover, CAP enhanced melanocyte maturation, TRP-2 expression, and melanin particle synthesis in immortalized melanoblasts (iMC23). The mechanism was associated with PRKCQ upregulation and NF-κB signaling activation.

To fully exploit effective therapeutic options for vitiligo, animal models are indispensable for experimental studies. Currently, mouse models of vitiligo can be categorized into four types: chemically induced, immune-induced, transgenic, and melanoma-Treg-induced, with chemically induced models being the most frequently utilized.19 In this study, we established a vitiligo mouse model through hydroquinone-induced depigmentation. Our results revealed spot-like pigment loss around day 20 and characteristic vitiligo-like changes on the dorsal skin of mice by day 30 post-hydroquinone induction. Histological analysis demonstrated a marked reduction in melanin deposition in hair follicles, aligning with the hallmark features of vitiligo.20 Previous evidence has indicated that CAP and CAP-activated hydrogels are effective and safe in promoting repigmentation in vitiligo lesions.16 Consequently, we developed a vitiligo mouse model and applied CAP to the depigmented areas. Consistent with previous evidence,16 we observed significant repigmentation and black hair growth in hydroquinone-induced mice after CAP treatment, indicating that CAP exerts a pronounced therapeutic effect on skin lesions in these mice. Furthermore, pigmentation and hair growth were positively correlated with the duration of individual CAP interventions. However, CAP treatment lasting 120 or 180 s resulted in some degree of skin and hair burning in mice. Thus, a 60-s daily CAP treatment for three weeks may be considered the optimal treatment duration.

McSCs in the outer root sheath of hair follicles typically remain quiescent, dividing only during specific periods. Some daughter cells replenish the niche and revert to a quiescent state, while other transient amplifying cells migrate to the hair bulb, differentiate into mature melanocytes, and transfer melanin to hair-forming keratinocytes.21 McSCs in the outer root sheath express TRP-2; both TYRP1 and TRP-2 are expressed when melanocytes are proliferative.22,23 In this study, abundant TYRP1 and TRP-2 expression was observed in melanocytes of the outer root sheath in vitiligo mice following CAP treatment, which is similar to the findings of Nishimura et al.21 These results suggest that CAP induces the reactivation, division, and proliferation of McSCs in the outer root sheath of hair follicles in vitiligo mice, enabling their transition from a dormant to a functional state.

Evidence indicates that CAP can induce the differentiation of various stem cells.24 However, its effects on melanoblasts have been rarely investigated. Melanoblasts are incapable of producing melanin particles due to the absence of functional TYR, a capability exclusive to mature melanocytes.8 Therefore, iMC23 melanoblasts were employed in this study to investigate the impact of CAP on melanoblast-to-melanocyte differentiation. It has been reported that dendritic melanocytes appear when melanoblasts differentiate into melanocytes in the outer root sheath of hair follicles.25 Herein, iMC23 cells were treated with CAP for different durations and then cultured for different periods. Our results indicated that iMC23 cell morphology began to change 48 h post-CAP treatment and culture. Compared to other treatment conditions, 60 s of CAP treatment followed by 48 h of culture significantly increased the synaptic structure of iMC23 cells, indicating morphological differentiation. Liu et al. proposed that stem cell arrest at the G1 phase results in the accumulation of key differentiation factors.26 Our results showed that 60-s CAP treatment markedly increased cell accumulation at the G0/G1 phase, consistent with a potential shift toward differentiation. Furthermore, the production of melanin via melanogenesis plays an important role in the pathogenesis of vitiligo. TYR is the primary enzyme that controls the production of melanin at the biochemical level. TRP-2 catalyzes melanin synthesis by forming a complex with TYR. In melanin production, specific enzymes are exclusive to melanocytes, including TYR, TYRP1, and TRP-2. Their expression is controlled by MITF, which interacts with the common M-box located in the promoter regions of these three genes.27 A previous report has indicated that CAP can promote iMC23 cell differentiation into melanocytes.17 Consistently, this study revealed that CAP enhanced TYR, TYRP-1, TRP-2, and MITF expression and melanin particle content in iMC23 cells in a duration-dependent manner, with 60-s CAP showing the most pronounced effect. These indicate that CAP may facilitate iMC23 cell differentiation into melanocytes, with 60-s treatment followed by 48 h of culture being the optimal condition to explore the differentiation of melanoblasts into mature melanocytes.

Multiple genes and signaling pathways are implicated in regulating the differentiation of McSCs and subsequent melanogenesis.28 To further investigate the mechanism by which CAP induced melanoblast-to-melanocyte differentiation, we analyzed related genes and pathways using transcriptome sequencing and functional enrichment analysis. As a result, we found that CAP could upregulate PRKCQ and activate NF-κB signal transduction. PRKCQ, a member of the protein kinase C subfamily, plays a crucial role in numerous biological processes, including cell differentiation, survival, and apoptosis.29 In addition, PRKCQ can activate the NF-κB pathway.30 Evidence indicates the activation of NF-κB signaling in the lesional skin of patients with vitiligo.31 Herein, our results showed that p-NF-κB p65 levels were reduced in hair follicles of vitiligo mice, indicating that NF-κB signaling is relatively quiescent in hair follicles. This may explain why the repigmentation in the vitiligo treatment often begins in the hair follicle units (manifested as punctate pigmented islands around the hair follicles). Moreover, 60-s CAP treatment markedly promoted PRKCQ expression and activated NF-κB signal transduction in the hair follicles of vitiligo mice and iMC23 cells. Additionally, PRKCQ silencing reversed CAP-induced NF-κB activation and iMC23 cell differentiation into melanocytes, while this effect was reversed by PRKCQ overexpression. While our knockdown/overexpression experiments support a contributory role for PRKCQ in CAP-induced NF-κB activation and differentiation, direct hierarchical evidence (e.g., via additional pathway inhibitors) would further strengthen causality.

CAP-mediated stimulation of melanoblast differentiation holds promise for clinical application in vitiligo, particularly for patients refractory to phototherapy or immunosuppressants. Recent clinical trials demonstrate CAP’s efficacy in dermatology, achieving partial repigmentation in 80% of vitiligo lesions without adverse effects at optimized doses, and modulating autoimmune responses in conditions such as atopic dermatitis and psoriasis through targeted cytokine reduction.16,32 For clinicians, this positions CAP as a non-invasive adjunct therapy, potentially integrated with existing regimens to enhance repigmentation while minimizing systemic toxicity. Additionally, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of CAP, particularly regarding clinical translation. Our study used a specific laboratory-scale CAP jet device operating under defined parameters. The field of CAP medicine currently lacks universal standardization of devices and treatment protocols. Variations in power output, plasma composition, and application methods across different CAP devices could significantly influence the biological outcomes and treatment efficacy.33 Therefore, our findings are contextual to the experimental setup employed. While no universal CAP standard exists, our setup adheres to common practices, and future standardization efforts could incorporate these parameters. The non-thermal plasma and low-current discharge ensured no adverse effects in mice, as supported by RT-qPCR analysis of inflammatory markers in treated skin tissue showing minimal inflammation. Additionally, no visible macroscopic skin burning or hair damage was observed at the optimal 60-s exposure (except at longer durations). These findings align with CAP’s established safety in dermatological applications.34 For human translation, comprehensive toxicological studies and preliminary clinical trials would be essential, particularly focusing on long-term effects. The plasma plume’s coverage area was suitable for localized lesions, with sequential applications addressing multiple sites. Scaling to larger areas, as in human vitiligo, could involve multi-jet arrays or automated scanning systems to enhance coverage and reduce treatment time. Future studies will explore these adaptations to ensure clinical feasibility.

In conclusion, the present study indicates that CAP promotes melanoblast-to-melanocyte differentiation in the hair follicle bugle of vitiligo mice and in vitro, thereby enhancing melanin synthesis and repigmentation. The mechanism is associated with PRKCQ upregulation and NF-κB activation. Our findings may provide a direction for vitiligo treatment.

Limitations of the study

Our study has some limitations. First, the mechanistic insights primarily rely on bulk RNA-seq in a single cell line (iMC23). This may obscure cellular heterogeneity and transcriptional diversity among melanocyte subpopulations or immune cells in vitiligo lesions, as demonstrated by recent single-cell RNA-seq studies.35,36 We did not comprehensively examine other NF-κB-related genes beyond the core pathway activation. Considering the autoimmune nature of vitiligo, future studies could employ single-cell RNA-seq in primary McSCs or in vivo models to validate PRKCQ and other NF-κB-related genes and integrate immune profiling to investigate whether CAP modulates the immune microenvironment to promote repigmentation. Second, the hydroquinone-induced vitiligo mouse model, while reproducible for assessing melanogenic effects, mimics chemical depigmentation via TYR inhibition and ROS accumulation but does not fully recapitulate the predominant autoimmune pathogenesis in most non-segmental human patients with vitiligo. Therefore, future studies are required to validate CAP’s effects in more immunologically faithful models. Additionally, although molecular evidence showed minimal inflammation and previous CAP studies support histological tolerability, direct histologic confirmation would provide stronger exclusion of injury as a confounder.

Experimental model and study participant details

Experimental animals

Adult female C57BL/6 mice (weighing 18−22 g, aged 6 weeks; Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) were housed in an SPF environment under a 12-h light-dark cycle (lights on at 8:00 AM) at a controlled temperature (24 ± 1°C) and humidity (50−56%), with free access to food and water. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Huazhong University of Science and Technology (No. 3334).

Method details

Mouse vitiligo model

After 3 days of acclimatization, the back of the mouse torso was shaved with a mixture of rosin/paraffin at 1:1 to expose bare pink skin (2 cm × 2 cm). During the experiment, intermittent shaving was conducted according to the hair growth cycle of mice.37 Mice were randomly categorized into control (n=6 mice) and vitiligo groups (n=30 mice). To induce vitiligo, murine depilated skin was evenly smeared with 3% hydroquinone cream (self-made), once a day for 30 days. The control mice were smeared with distilled water. The colors of murine skin and hair were photographed and recorded on days 1, 10, 20, and 30.

Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining

After euthanasia, skin tissues were collected from the back of the mouse's torso and fixed in 10% formaldehyde, followed by alcohol dehydration, xylene clearing, and paraffin embedding. The tissue samples were then sliced at 4 μm. After deparaffination and rehydration, the slices were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (C0105S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) as per the manufacturer's protocols. Then, the slices were dehydrated using gradient alcohol, cleared with xylene, and sealed with gum. The stained skin tissues were imaged using a light microscope (Leica Microsystems, Shanghai, China).

Masson-Fontana staining for melanin content

Paraffin tissues were sliced, dewaxed with xylene, washed with distilled water, and immersed in Fontana ammoniac silver solution (G2032, Solarbio, Beijing) at room temperature in the dark for 14 h. After washing with distilled water 5 times, the slices were treated with Hypo solution for 3 min, washed with tap water for 5 min, counterstained with neutral red dye for 5 min, and rinsed with distilled water. The slices were dehydrated with ethanol, dewaxed with xylene, cleared, and sealed with neutral gum. The staining results were observed under a bright-field microscope (Leica Microsystems).

CAP treatment in vitiligo mice

The CAP jet device was provided by Professor Xinpei Lu from the State Key Laboratory of Advanced Electromagnetic Engineering and Technology and School of Electrical and Electronic Engineering, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. A schematic of the device is provided in Figure S1. The device was driven by the pulse DC power supply, with a fixed applied voltage amplitude of 6 kV, a fixed pulse frequency of 8 kHz, and a fixed pulse width of 2 μs, delivering a supply voltage of 4000 V ± 100 V and a current of 6 mA ± 0.2 mA. A gas mixture of helium (He) and O2 (0.5%) was introduced at a flow rate of 1 L/min through the quartz tube, generating a plasma plume in ambient air. The plasma was applied to the depigmented skin lesions of the mice at a distance of 15 mm from the nozzle to the skin surface, maintaining a gas temperature below 40°C. As previously reported, the plasma treatment time is 4−8 min for skin melanoma and psoriasis models,14,38 and 2−3 min for the wound healing model.39 Therefore, in this study, the initial treatment time was set at 3 min. The plasma plume provided an effective coverage area of approximately 4 cm2, sufficient for individual lesions. The mice were not grounded during treatment, as the non-contact CAP jet system, with its grounded outer electrode, ensures safe delivery of ROS/RNS without requiring animal grounding. After the successful induction of vitiligo (30-day hydroquinone administration), mice with vitiligo-like lesions (n=30) were randomly divided into five groups using the random number method: vitiligo, vitiligo + ultraviolet B (UVB) phototherapy, vitiligo + CAP (60s), vitiligo + CAP (120s), and vitiligo + CAP (180s), with 6 mice per group. The mice in the vitiligo + UVB phototherapy were irradiated using a Therapie-systeme UV109B (Waldmann Group) as previously described.40 The mice in the vitiligo + CAP groups received CAP jet treatment once a day (60, 120, and 180 s for each time, respectively) for 3 weeks. For the vitiligo group, only airflow was given (without electricity) for 3 weeks. Murine skin and hair colors were imaged and recorded on days 1, 10, and 21 following CAP treatment.

Depigmentation evaluation

The depigmentation area was scored by two observers blinded to the animal grouping. The criteria are as follows: 0 point, no evidence of depigmentation; 1 point, area≤5%; 2 points, 5%<area≤25%; 3 points, 25%<area≤50%; 4 points, 50%<area≤75%; and 5 points, 75%<area≤100%. The average individual score of the mice in that group represents the vitiligo score of each experimental group.

Tissue immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Paraffin-embedded skin tissue slices were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by heat-mediated antigen retrieval in sodium citrate (pH 6.0). Then, the slices were blocked with 10% normal goat serum and incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-TYRP1 (1:100, ab190709) and anti-TRP-2 (1:500, ab74073) primary antibodies (Abcam, Shanghai). After washing with Tris-Buffered Saline (TBS) in Tween 20 (TBST) three times (5 min each time), the slices were incubated with Cy3- or FITC-conjugated second antibodies (Abcam) at room temperature for 1 h in the dark. The slices were counterstained with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 10 min to label nuclei. After washing with TBST three times, the slices were mounted with antifade medium. Finally, images were captured and observed under a fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems).

Cell culture and CAP treatment

Immortalized melanoblasts (iMC23), which are derived from the skin of newborn C57BL/6 mice and cannot produce melanin particles,41 were provided by Professor Yang Ke (Stem Cell Therapy Center of Children's Hospital of Chongqing Medical University). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (Biosharp, Hefei, China) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) in a 37°C incubator with 5% CO2. Adherent iMC23 cells were treated with CAP (He + O2) for different exposure durations (0, 20, 40, 60, and 80 seconds). Control cells were exposed to an O2 gas flow alone. In addition, iMC23 cells were treated with 100 nM α-MSH (HY-P0252, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) for 24 h as a positive control group. Post-treatment, cells were either processed in situ as adherent cultures or detached for suspension-based assays (flow cytometry for cell cycle analysis).

Transcriptome sequencing

Three biological replicates were set up for each group. Total RNA was extracted from iMC23 cells using TRIzol reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), followed by determination of RNA purity and integrity with the NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer (Implen, Munich, Germany) and Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Beijing), respectively. Library preparation was performed using the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit (#E7770, New England Biolabs, Beijing) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, poly-A-tailed mRNA was enriched using oligo-dT magnetic beads to remove ribosomal RNA and other non-coding RNAs, and enriched mRNAs were randomly fragmented in the fragmentation buffer containing divalent cations. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer priming and reverse transcriptase, followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis using DNA polymerase I and RNase H. The resulting double-stranded cDNA was subjected to adapter ligation. PCR amplification was performed, and libraries were size-selected (200–500 bp) using AMPure XP beads. Library concentration was initially quantified using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific), targeting ≥ 1.5 ng/μL. The effective library concentration (> 2 nM) was verified using RT-qPCR to ensure compatibility with Illumina sequencing. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, Beijing) using paired-end sequencing chemistry. Raw data were processed to remove low-quality reads and adapter sequences. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened using DESeq2 (v1.16.1) with the following thresholds: log2(Fold Change)| ≥ 0 and false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted p < 0.05. To account for multiple testing, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR method. Functional annotation of DEGs was performed using ClusterProfiler (v3.4.4) for Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis. Terms with adjusted p-value (p-adj) < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Cell transfection

To knock down PRKCQ, the short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting PRKCQ and a non-targeting control were cloned into the pLKO.1 lentiviral vector (#8453, Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA) to construct sh-PRKCQ/sh-NC. To overexpress PRKCQ, the cDNA sequence of PRKCQ was amplified and cloned into the pLVX-IRES-puro lentiviral vector (#128652, Addgene). iMC23 cells were infected with sh-PRKCQ or oe-PRKCQ lentiviruses at an MOI of 10 in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). After 24 h, the medium was refreshed. The transduction efficiency was validated by RT-qPCR and Western blotting.

RT-qPCR

After total RNA extraction using TRIzol reagent, approximately 1.5 μg of RNA in each sample was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the RevertAid first-strand cDNA synthesis kit (Thermo Scientific, Shanghai). Real-time qPCR was performed using the Quantagene q225 qPCR detection system (Kubo Technology, Beijing). The reaction system consisted of 6.25 μL SYBR Green PCR SuperMix, 0.25 μL forward and reverse primers, 1 μL cDNA, and 4.75 μL ddH2O. The reaction conditions consisted of 30 s at 95°C and 40 cycles of 10 s at 95°C, 30 s at 60°C, and 30 s at 60°C. After each step, the melting curve at 65°C−94°C was monitored to assess the dissociation properties of double-stranded DNA products. Each sample was tested 3 times, and samples from three independent experiments were analyzed. Normalized to β-actin, relative gene expression was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. The used primers are listed in Table 1.

Cellular IF staining

iMC23 cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min, and then washed three times with PBS. Cells were separated with 5% bovine serum albumin (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 30 min, followed by incubation with anti-p-p65 (1:100, AP0124, ABclonal, Wuhan, China) or anti-TRP-2 (1:100, ab74073, Abcam) primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. After three washes with PBS, cells were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies (Abcam) in the dark for 1 h. The nuclei were labelled by counterstaining with DAPI for 10 min. Cells were observed under the fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems) and analyzed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software. Ten fields were randomly selected to quantify the percentage of p-p65-positive and TRP-2-positive cells in the total number of cells (DAPI-labelled).

Western blotting

Protein isolation from iMC23 cells was conducted using RIPA lysis buffer (Vazyme), followed by protein concentration quantification with a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime). Protein samples were separated by 10% or 12% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and electro-transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). After blocking with 5% skim milk in 0.1% TBST, the membrane was subjected to overnight incubation with the following primary antibodies: anti-TRP-2 (ab221144, 1:1000, Abcam), anti-TYRP1 (ab235447, 1:1000, Abcam), anti-TYR (A1254, 1:500, ABclonal), anti-MITF (ab303530, 1:1000, Abcam), anti-protein kinase C Theta (PRKCQ; 1:1000, ab302891, Abcam), anti-p65 (1:5000, A19653, ABclonal), anti-p-p65 (1:500, AP0124, ABclonal), and anti-β-actin (1:80000, AC038, ABclonal) at 4°C. The membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit/mouse second antibodies (Abcam) at room temperature for 2 h, followed by three TBST washes. Protein bands were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (Abbkine, Wuhan, China), and relative band intensities were quantified with Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was examined using the cell counting kit (CCK)-8 kit (Servicebio). iMC23 cells were seeded into 96-well plates (2×103/well). After treatment according to grouping, the plates were placed in a 37°C incubator with 5% CO2. Afterward, CCK-8 solutions were added to the plates (10 μL/well) for 1.5 h of culture. The optical density (OD) values were measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific). Cell viability was determined using the following formula: cell viability (%) = (OD treatment – OD control)/OD control × 100%.

Cell cycle analysis

Cell cycle analysis was performed as instructed in the manuals of the cell cycle detection kit (Servicebio). Briefly, adherent cells were processed into a cell suspension by 0.25% trypsin digestion and then centrifuged. Afterward, cells were fixed with 1 mL of 70% ice-cold ethanol for 12 h, centrifuged at 200 g for 3 min, resuspended in PBS, and centrifuged again. Then, cells were stained with 0.5 mL propidium iodide staining solution containing RNase A at 37°C for 30 min away from light. Finally, the percentages of cells at G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases were detected by ModFit LT software.

Melanin particle detection

iMC23 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were trypsinized and dispersed into single-cell suspensions. Cells (1 × 106) were centrifuged at 1000 r/min for 5 min, and the pellet was washed twice with ice-cold PBS. The pellet was resuspended in 20 μL of 1 mol/L NaOH lysis buffer containing 10% dimethyl sulfoxide and cultured in a preheated water bath at 80°C for 2 h to obtain a melanin solution. Melanin content was determined by measuring the OD value at 405 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific). Additionally, transmission electron microscopy (Leica Microsystems) was employed for melanin particle observation in iMC23 cells.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis

Changes in melanosomes in iMC23 cells were observed after treatment with 60-second CAP and culture for 48 h. The cells were cultured using 2.5% glutaraldehyde in sodium cacodylate buffer, followed by fixation with 1% osmium acid in cacodylate buffer. After dehydration, an ultramicrotome (Leica EM UC5, Leica Microsystems) was used to prepare ultrathin sections. These sections were then stained with 1 % uranyl acetate and lead citrate. The stained samples were observed by TEM (Tecnai 12, FEI, Hillsboro, OR, USA). Melanosomes at various stages of maturation (stages I-IV) were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software. The effect sizes were estimated based on preliminary data, and the selected sample sizes for all experiments were sufficient to have 80% power with a two-sided alpha of 0.05. The data were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and for homogeneity of variances using Levene's test. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation from at least three independent experiments. Statistical differences between two groups were analyzed with the unpaired two-tailed t-test, while those among multiple groups were analyzed with one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's post hoc analysis. All the statistical details of the experiments can be found in the figure legends section. Differences were statistically significant at p < 0.05.
