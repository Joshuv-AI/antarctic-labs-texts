Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome and orthostatic intolerance in adult patients with active cancer
Authors: Ismail Hamam, Albaraa Al Holy, Omar Darwish, Batool Alkhalaileh, Ahmed Abdulelah, Shadee Shaaban, Shahed AL Qudah, Muyasar Amro, Ali Shakhatreh, Amr Alkarmi, Ibrahim Jarrad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome (POTS) is a variant of autonomic dysfunction (AD) defined by an increase in heart rate (HR) ≥ 30 bpm within 10 minutes (min) of a change from the supine to an upright position, in the absence of orthostatic hypotension. Many studies suggest that AD is common in active cancer patients and is associated with variable symptoms and decreased survival. Based on this evidence, we hypothesized that POTS can be associated with active cancer and might contribute to some of these patients’ symptoms and survival.MethodsConsecutive 220 active cancer patients aged 19–59 were enrolled. They were asked to lay flat for 5–10 min then to stand for 10 min without support. Total of 5 blood pressure (BP) and HR readings were taken (immediately before standing up then immediately after, and at 3, 6, and 10 min while standing). All patient’s symptoms were recorded.Results213 patients were included. Age was 48 ± 8, 76% were females,18% had metastasis and 43% were on chemotherapy. 55% reported symptoms of AD, with symptoms associated with standing were reported in 28%. Baseline HR was 76 ± 13 bpm, and systolic BP was 129 ± 20. Upon standing, HR increased by 10 ± 3 bpm and systolic BP dropped by 1.3 ± 0.4. Immediately after standing, 47 (22%) Patients developed symptoms.18 patients (8.5%) met the criteria for POTS, where HR increased by 37 ± 5 with an increase in systolic BP by -1.6 ± 6. Patients with POTS had higher HR upon standing (p = 0.000), HR at 3 min (p = 0.005), HR at 6 min (p = 0.002), and HR at 10 min (p = 0.000). In contrast, baseline HR and BP showed no significant difference between patients with and without POTS (p = 0.74 and 0.11). After 16.9 ± 5.5 months, there was no statistical deterrence in all-cause mortality between POTS and non-POTS patients.ConclusionPOTS and orthostatic intolerance are prevalent in active cancer, where onset of symptoms started after cancer diagnosis in many patients. These patients have a significant association with higher HR upon standing and overall prognosis comparable to that of patients without POTS.Keywords: Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), Orthostatic intolerance, Autonomic dysfunction, Cancer

Introduction

Orthostatic intolerance (OI) is defined as the inability to remain upright without severe signs and symptoms such as hypotension, tachycardia, lightheadedness, pallor, fatigue, weakness, and nausea. OI appears in many forms, including overt dysautonomia, vasovagal syncope, and orthostatic tachycardia. Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS) is a form of OI, typically characterized by [1]:

Frequent symptoms that occur with standing, such as lightheadedness, palpitations, tremor, generalized weakness, blurred vision, exercise intolerance, and fatigue.

Frequent symptoms that occur with standing, such as lightheadedness, palpitations, tremor, generalized weakness, blurred vision, exercise intolerance, and fatigue.

An increase in heart rate (HR) of ≥ 30 beats per minute (bpm) when moving from a recumbent to a standing position (or ≥ 40 bpm in individuals aged 12 to 19 years).

An increase in heart rate (HR) of ≥ 30 beats per minute (bpm) when moving from a recumbent to a standing position (or ≥ 40 bpm in individuals aged 12 to 19 years).

The absence of orthostatic hypotension (a drop of > 20 mmHg in systolic blood pressure (BP)).

The absence of orthostatic hypotension (a drop of > 20 mmHg in systolic blood pressure (BP)).

Symptoms associated with POTS are typically linked to standing, such as lightheadedness and palpitations, and are not related to specific postures [2]. The standing (or orthostatic) HR of individuals with POTS is often ≥ 120 bpm, and it tends to increase more in the morning than in the evening [3–5]. POTS is a systemic illness, with postural tachycardia being one of several criteria. Many patients with POTS occasionally faint, though presyncope is much more common.

The prevalence of POTS is approximately 0.2% (4,5,6). Most patients present with POTS between the ages of 15 and 25 years, and more than 75% are female [2–4]. POTS is also common in patients with chronic fatigue syndrome [6]. The chronic and usually systemic symptoms, along with the frustration caused by the difficulty in obtaining medical help, can significantly lower the quality of life. Little has been reported on long-term outcomes, although a few cases of patients over 50 years of age have been documented, and there have been no reports of mortality.

A number of mechanisms have been described in patients with POTS, including autonomic dysfunction (AD), hypovolemia, hyper-adrenergic stimulation, deconditioning, anxiety and hypervigilance [7, 8]. These mechanisms often coexist in patients with POTS [9, 10].

Active cancer defined as cancer diagnosed within the previous six months, recurrent, regionally advanced or metastatic cancer, cancer for which treatment has been administered within six months, or hematological cancer that is not in complete remission. Active cancer patients can share many characteristics that POTS patients have, including the presence of AD, hypovolemia, anxiety and deconditioning. We hypothesized that since these deferent mechanisms described in patients with POTS are common in patients with active cancer, POTS should also be common in these patients.

In this study, we specifically investigated the prevalence of POTS in adult patients with active cancer to determine whether POTS can coexist with cancer and whether both cancer and several types of anti-cancer therapies can be associated with POTS. We also examined POTS- related symptoms, their clinical presentation, and their progression with cancer progression and therapy.

Methods

Ethical conduct of research

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at King Hussein Cancer Center (KHCC). All procedures performed in this study involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. This article does not contain any studies on animals performed by any of the authors. Informed consent was approved by our institution and was obtained from all participants.

Study protocol

Over a period of one year (from November 2022 to October 2023), all consecutive out-patients’ adults with active cancer aged 19–59 years who presented at the KHCC cardiology clinic and met inclusion and exclusion criteria were invited to participate in this study. Inclusion criteria included: age 19–59 years, active cancer (regardless of type, site, or stage), having an official visit to KHCC cardiology clinic on the day of enrollment, agreement to participate, ability to provide informed consent, ability to do the screening test, and normal sinus rhythm at the time of the screening test. Exclusion criteria included resting HR < 50 or > 100 bpm, systolic BP < 90 or > 160 mmHg, diastolic BP < 60 or > 100 mmHg, serum hemoglobin < 10 gm/dl, estimated ejection fraction < 50%, or pregnancy.

Patients who agreed to participate, they were asked to sign a consent form. A standardized clinical form was then filled out, recording the following clinical information: demographics, tobacco use, comorbid conditions, cancer type, stage, previous treatment, concomitant medications, current POTS or AD-related symptoms, onset of symptoms in relation to cancer diagnosis, laboratory, electrocardiographic, and echocardiographic features.

All patients underwent a standardized testing protocol to screen for the diagnosis of POTS, performed by physicians involved in the study and supervised by the KHCC cardiologists. The active stand test was utilized to screen for POTS in this study. The rationale behind using this test in our studied population was the simplicity and practicality of this test compared to the formal head-up tilt test (HUTT) that requires specialized equipment and is time-consuming,

All tests were conducted during regular clinic hours (9 a.m. to 2 p.m.). Patients were asked to lay flat for 5–10 min and then stand without support for 10 min. BP and pulse readings were taken using an automated machine (DINAMAP V100 GE CARESCAPE TM USA). A total of 5 readings were taken: immediately before standing after 5–10 min of lying flat, immediately after standing, and at 3, 6, and 10 min while standing. Along with the BP and pulse readings, all patient’s symptoms were also recorded. The test was terminated immediately if patients developed severe lightheadedness, near syncope, or whenever patients requested to sit down. Patients should have HR above thresholds that are associated with symptoms in at least two consecutives measurements to be considered to have POTS. All screening test results were reviewed and analyzed by the certified electrophysiologist. Patients were followed up via chart review or by phone after 12 months or longer from enrolment to assess living status. All POTS patients were contacted during the follow-up and asked about the progression of their baseline POTS- related symptoms.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using the SPSS software package (version 25.0, SPSS Inc. Chicago, Illinois). Continuous variables were represented by mean (Standard deviation [SD]) or median (Interquartile range [IQR]) where appropriate, and the categorical variables were represented by N (%). Univar ate analyses were performed using the student’s t-test for independent samples or the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables. Categorical data were compared using Chi-Squared test (χ2). A significance level of p < 0.05 was used for all tests.

Results

Therefore, 213 patients included in the final analysis. The mean age was 48 ± 8 years; 76% were female, 26% had hypertension, 18% were diabetic, 34% were smokers, and 9% had hypothyroidism. Breast cancer was the most prevalent malignancy, reported in 54% of patients, followed by gastrointestinal cancer in 14%. Metastasis was reported in 18% of the study sample, and 34% were undergoing chemotherapy (Table 1).

On follow- up after 16.9 ± 5.5 months, there was no statistical difference in all-cause mortality between POTS and non-POTS patients. Discussion

AD may be idiopathic, occur as a complication of other conditions, or result from medications. It is recognized as a common complication in patients with diabetes mellitus, stroke, and post- myocardial infarction [11]. A small number of studies on autonomic function in patients with advanced cancer suggest that AD is common [12, 13], with cardiovascular AD detected in 52–81% of patients with advanced cancer [14]. Possible causes include decreased physical activity, treatment with chemotherapeutic agents or other medications, and paraneoplastic processes [9, 10].

Our study confirms these findings, showing that symptoms suggestive of AD are common in active cancer patients, regardless of cancer type, stage, or treatment plan. Specifically, 55% of patients reported symptoms of AD, with 36% of them experiencing these symptoms after the cancer diagnosis. However, we did not perform objective tests to confirm the diagnosis of AD in these patients, as our primary aim was to screen for POTS.

Another aspect of this study is to screen for OI in active cancer patients. OI is defined as the inability to tolerate an upright posture due to signs and symptoms relieved by lying down. OI is expected to be common in active cancer patients due the autonomic effects of chemotherapy and radiation therapy, as well as relative hypovolemia, dehydration, and prolonged bed rest during cancer treatment. Despite this, data on OI, including orthostatic hypotension and orthostatic tachycardia in cancer patients, or in patients who survive cancer, remain limited to a few small studies and case reports [15–17]. These studies have linked OI in cancer patients to chronic fatigue syndrome, various anti-cancer therapies and cancer itself.

In this study, we confirmed this association, finding that 28% of patients reported symptoms associated with standing, such as weakness, palpitations, and lightheadedness. Additionally, 22% of patients experienced lightheadedness and/or palpitations immediately after standing, which was associated with an increase in HR and a drop in systolic BP over 10 min.

The main finding of this study is that POTS is prevalent in active cancer patients. Based on our objective assessments, 8.5% of patients met the diagnostic criteria for POTS. This prevalence is significantly higher than the previously reported prevalence of 0.2% in the general population [1, 4, 5, 18], This suggests that active cancer can be associated with and may contribute to the development of POTS. In previously published data by Noor et al., POTS was diagnosed in 3 out of 24 cancer survivors tested for AD [14]. Beyond this, we could not identify any other published data describing the association between POTS and cancer.

It has been noted that conditions such as chronic fatigue syndrome, joint hypermobility, autoimmune diseases, anxiety, hypervigilance, functional gastrointestinal disorders, fibromyalgia, migraine, and concussion may coexist with POTS [19, 20]. Furthermore, active cancer and anticancer therapies involve many pathophysiological factors that may contribute to an individual’s susceptibility to POTS. These factors include AD, excessive venous pooling, hypovolemia in the setting of volume dysregulation, hyper adrenergic states, mast cell activation disorders, and autoimmunity (1,8). We believe that one or more of these mechanisms can exist in most active cancer patients, and this can explain our findings of why POTS is prevalent in cancer patients. Furthermore, cancer and its treatment can be associated with cardiovascular events, and cause cardiovascular toxicity, and seem to have an impact on cardiac autonomic control. Radiotherapy and surgery often induce more localized anatomical syndromes which can cause a localized damage to peripheral, autonomic and gland tissue. Peripheral sympathetic denervation caused by anti-cancer treatment might lead to impaired vasoconstriction and increased venous pooling in lower extremities that can cause POTS. Chemotherapy, immunotherapy and radiotherapy can result in mast cell activation disorder, inappropriate release of histamine and other mast cell mediators in response to physical activity or orthostatic stress, which may lead to orthostatic tachycardia. Many studies have demonstrated that prolonged bedrest and volume dysregulation, which are common in active cancer patients, can result in a POTS-like syndrome characterized by a tachycardic orthostatic intolerance (11–15).). Another explanation is that POTS is a disorder of autonomic nervous system, and since AD is prevalent in active cancer patients, it is likely that POTS also exists in these patients [6–8, 18].

When we compared active cancer patients with and without POTS in terms of age, gender, comorbidities, cancer type, stage and anti-cancer treatment, we found no statistically significant differences between both groups. Even baseline symptoms suggestive of AD were not different. In addition to this, the use of rate controlling medication at the time of the screening test did not influence the findings of POTS in active cancer patients. This fact reflects the complex pathophysiology nature of POTS that was not masked by the use of these medications. This indicates that factors increasing susceptibility to POTS in active cancer patients are unclear and are unrelated to the variables we studied. Farther analysis and research are required to understand why some patients develop POTS while others do not.

The prospective follow- up of our patients showed that no significant difference in mortality rate between POTS and non-POTS patients over a period of more than one year. Additionally, 59% of POTS patients reported improvement in their baseline POTS related symptoms. Previous retrospective studies have shown that the prognosis for POTS in the general population is favorable and that POTS dose not increase mortality [2, 21–23]. Our study confirms these findings, showing that even active cancer patients with POTS experience symptom improvement over time and have the same prognosis as those without POTS.

Finally, these findings suggest that POTS may be more prevalent in active cancer patients than previously recognized in the general population. This implies that POTS should be screened for, tested, and treated when appropriate. Clinicians caring for cancer patients should maintain a low threshold for evaluating AD and, specifically, POTS. We think that the early detection of these conditions could improve the quality of life of cancer patients who are already affected by numerous other factors. Additionally, these patients need to be educated about their condition and managed with both pharmacologic and non-pharmacologic measurers when appropriate.

Limitations

The main limitations of our study include the heterogeneous characteristics of our patients (active cancer patients regardless of cancer type, stage, and the various types of anti-cancer therapies involved). However, our efforts aimed to screen for POTS in the general middle-aged adult active cancer population to define the association between cancer and POTS in a broad context.

Another limitation of our study is that we did not perform an objective assessment of the autonomic function in our patients, given that our main study objective was to screen specifically for POTS and not for autonomic dysfunction in general.

We excluded older patients, as POTS is uncommon in this age group and because the ability to perform the baseline screening tests may be affected by age and multiple comorbidities. Another limitation of our study is that we did not perform a formal HUTT for all patients. Instead, we utilized the active stand test, which provides a simple evaluation for POTS. The HUTT requires specialized equipment and is time- consuming, particularly for active cancer patients, who are often occupied with numerous cancer- related diagnostic and therapeutic procedures.

The lack of calculated body mass index (BMI) and serum albumin are another limitation of our study, given that these factors can reflect volume status or nutritional state, especially in a population prone to cachexia and dehydration which might affect the presence of POTS in this population.

Conclusion

POTS is characterized by the clinical hallmark of symptomatic orthostatic tachycardia without significant changes in BP. It is prevalent in active cancer patients, irrespective of cancer type, stage, or specific anti-cancer treatment. For many of these patients, symptoms began after their cancer diagnosis.

Patients with POTS exhibit a significant association with higher HR readings upon standing, which persists over 10 min. The overall prognosis of POTS in active cancer patients is comparable to that of patients without POTS. Moreover, the majority of POTS patients reported improvement in bassline symptoms over time.
