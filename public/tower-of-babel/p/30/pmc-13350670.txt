The Standard Modifiable Cardiovascular Risk Factor Paradox Revisited
Authors: Nagendra Boopathy Senguttuvan, Judah Nijas Arul, Harilalith Kovvuri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) remain the leading cause of mortality in Asia, with approximately 11 million deaths annually.1 The incidence of CVD has always been linked to traditional risk factors.2 The standard modifiable cardiovascular risk factors (SMuRFs), namely hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and smoking, are the conventional culprits that we, as clinicians, attribute the blame to.3 Although there are numerous other risk factors for CVD, conventionally, SMuRFs are confined to the aforementioned 4 risk factors, and patients without these risk factors are referred to as SMuRF-less patients.4 In recent years, studies have demonstrated that the incidence of acute myocardial infarction (AMI) in SMuRF-less patients is paradoxically associated with higher in-hospital mortality.5 Even though such data enable us to go beyond the customary and evaluate nontraditional risk factors, it should not diminish the role of SMuRFs.

Conventional SMuRFs in Asians

The data on cardiac events among SMuRF-less patients of Asian ethnicity remain limited. A Chinese multicenter cohort study found a significantly increased 30-day mortality in SMuRF-less patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI); however, there was no difference in mortality over longer follow-up periods.6 Another study, including a multiethnic Asian study group from Singapore, also demonstrated a significantly elevated 30-day cardiovascular mortality in SMuRF-less patients presenting with AMI.7 An Indian observational study evaluating SMuRF-less patients with STEMI, however, showed no difference in in-hospital mortality and 1-year all-cause mortality compared with those with SMuRFs.8 Delayed care in the absence of conventional risk factors and poor adherence to guideline-directed medical therapy are implicated as the potential causes for the poor intermediate-term outcomes in SMuRF-less patients with AMI.9 These studies give us insights into the elevated short-term risks in SMuRF-less patients presenting with AMI; however, information regarding the long-term outcomes post discharge is still lacking in the Asian study group. Furthermore, the impact of the cumulative risk of SMuRFs on long-term outcomes or a dose–response relationship between the number of SMuRFs and recurrent ischemic events has not yet been established in the setting of acute coronary syndromes.10

Current Study

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Okita et al11 address this knowledge gap:—Does a higher number of SMuRFs negatively affect the long-term outcomes post AMI? In this multicenter, retrospective observational study from Japan, 2059 patients who survived to discharge following percutaneous coronary intervention for STEMI and non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction were included.11 These patients were categorized by the number of SMuRFs present (0-4). The investigators found no statistically significant difference in the composite primary endpoint of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), over a median follow-up of approximately 18 months. MACE was defined as a composite of cardiovascular death, rehospitalization for heart failure, recurrent AMI, and ischemic stroke. This finding is noteworthy as it suggests that the well-documented hazard observed in SMuRF-less patients in an in-hospital setting does not persist in the postdischarge phase. However, the catch is in the details; recurrent AMI clearly demonstrated a stepwise increase with each additional SMuRF, rising from just 1% in the SMuRF-less group to 5.3% in patients with 4 SMuRFs (P = 0.008). Of these SMuRFs, hypertension alone was independently associated with a 60% increased risk of MACE in multivariable analysis (HR: 1.63; 95% CI: 1.117-2.376; P = 0.011). The combination of hypertension and diabetes was also significantly associated with MACE, whereas other pairwise or higher-order combinations were not. Unsurprisingly, the incidence of multivessel disease and percutaneous coronary intervention was significantly higher among those who had a greater SMuRF burden.

Okita et al should be commended on this very well-conducted study, and several strengths must be highlighted. First, the investigators specifically analyze the postdischarge outcomes, thereby isolating the chronic phase of the disease where secondary prevention and guideline-directed medical therapy have their greatest influence. Second, the study included an East-Asian study group, which offers a germane representation of the Asian study group in terms of AMI and SMuRF burden. The study analyzed the outcomes among various SMuRFs and their combinations, which are often seen in clinical practice. Extensive procedural data were also provided, which are important in an era of highly developed interventional practice. In order to reduce the confounding bias, the use of mechanical circulatory support devices and cardiac arrest were not included in the multivariable analysis model. Nevertheless, some important limitations can obscure interpretation and may warrant further research. The retrospective design precludes causal inference, and hence, most findings should be considered hypothesis-generating. Confounding bias from various unmeasured variables, such as the duration of a SMuRF or its severity, cannot be excluded. It is well known that longer exposures to SMuRFs can have worse outcomes. To this end, the postdischarge medication adherence and control of risk factors such as blood pressure, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and glycated hemoglobin targets were not captured and should be incorporated in future studies. Another important point of emphasis is the predominance of male patients in the study (77.6%). Although the outcomes were not different among the sexes, the reduced representation of female patients introduces bias as it is well known that the female sex has a higher prevalence of SMuRFs and is associated with higher mortality post AMI.8,12 The definitions used to identify certain SMuRFs are also questionable. The study defined hypertension as systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg, whereas the LDL-C cutoff to define dyslipidemia was ≥140 mg/dL. In accordance with the recent guidelines, we believe that hypertension should be defined as a ratio of systolic blood pressure to diastolic blood pressure ≥130/80 mm Hg.13 Similarly, dyslipidemia should be diagnosed with an LDL-C cutoff of ≥100 mg/dL.14 With these more stringent definitions, there is a possibility that the actual number of SMuRF-less patients might be considerably lower.

Should SMuRFs Include Only the 4 Risk Factors?

The first study to describe the traditional risk factors for CVD was the Framingham Heart Study in 1948.15 Five decades later, the INTERHEART study found the following 9 risk factors to be associated with AMI: smoking, dyslipidemia, hypertension, diabetes, abdominal obesity, psychosocial factors, lack of consumption of fruits and vegetables, alcohol consumption, and lack of regular physical activity.4 Therefore, we believe that it is time to go beyond the conventional 4 pillars of SMuRFs to include all the risk factors identified by the INTERHEART study, which might aid in better risk stratification of patients with AMI.

What Clinicians Should Take From This Study and What We Propose

First and foremost, the absence of traditional risk factors should not invite complacency. SMuRF-less patients post AMI remain at a substantial risk of MACE (∼12.6%)

Second, the linear relationship between recurrent AMI and SMuRF count supports the importance of comprehensive risk factor modification. Third, hypertension warrants strict vigilance and aggressive target adherence in the post-AMI care and follow-up. Blood pressure optimization should be prioritized alongside lipid-lowering and antiplatelet therapy.

In these patients, the underlying processes precipitating the AMI must be evaluated, and aggressive secondary prevention should be advocated for, regardless of risk profile. SMuRFs and the Future

Could a newer scoring system that accounts for both traditional and nontraditional risk factors stratify post-AMI patients more effectively? Prospective studies incorporating SMuRFs and nontraditional risk factors, such as inflammatory markers, genetic predisposition, and psychosocial factors, may help resolve these matters and give us a better and more complete understanding of evolving risk factors.
