Glycemic variability, atherosclerosis, and arterial stiffness in persons with type 2 diabetes mellitus: A Mendelian randomization and epidemiologic study
Authors: Tsai-Chung Li, Chia-Ing Li, Chiu-Shong Liu, Chih-Hsueh Lin, Shing-Yu Yang, Cheng-Chieh Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate the relationships between visit-to-visit variability in HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and atherosclerotic markers in persons with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), employing epidemiological analyses and Mendelian randomization (MR) techniques. Between January 2001 and December 2020, 2290 persons with T2DM (1307 men and 983 women) who underwent evaluation for atherosclerosis screening were enrolled in a management program at a medical center in Taiwan. To assess potential causal relationships among the coefficient of variation (CV) of HbA1c (HbA1c-CV), FPG-CV, and markers of atherosclerosis, we conducted a 2-stage multivariable regression analysis using 22 and 21 single nucleotide polymorphisms as instrumental variables for HbA1c and FPG, respectively. A total of 2290 individuals with T2DM were included, with a mean age of 61.79 years, and 57% were male. In the epidemiological analysis, after multivariable adjustment, the odds ratios for an ankle–brachial index (ABI) < 0.9 in the third and fourth quartiles compared with the first quartile were 3.15 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.80–5.50) and 4.29 (95% CI: 2.44–7.56), respectively, for FPG-CV, and 1.67 (95% CI: 1.06–2.62) and 1.65 (95% CI: 1.01–2.70), respectively, for HbA1c-CV. In the MR analysis, comparing the fourth quartile with the first quartile, the odds ratios for ABI < 0.9 and brachial–ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV) ≥ 1800 cm/s were 2.78 (95% CI: 1.84–4.19) and 1.58 (95% CI: 1.23–2.02), respectively, for the unweighted genetic risk score predicting FPG-CV. Epidemiological and MR analyses provide evidence that FPG variability is significantly associated with atherosclerosis, as indicated by the ABI.

1. Introduction

Persons with diabetes are predisposed to the early onset of atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases and exhibit more severe clinical characteristics than persons without diabetes.[1] About two-thirds of people with diabetes die of atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases, including peripheral artery disease (PAD), coronary heart disease, stroke, and heart failure.[2] Consequently, for diabetes management and public health, determinants of surrogate endpoints that can predict the risks of atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases should be identified, and individuals who are at high risk for these surrogate endpoints should be screened for the prevention of the incidence of atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases via intervention programs or treatment regimens.

Glucose variability results from fluctuations between low and high glucose levels. Several biological mechanisms have been proposed to explain the pathophysiological effects of hyperglycemia on cardiovascular outcomes, including inflammatory cytokines,[3,4] oxidative stress,[5-8] and epigenetic changes.[9,10] By contrast, hypoglycemia is primarily associated with the release of inflammatory cytokines,[3,4] and platelet activation[6] is linked to hypoglycemia. These adverse molecular and systemic changes contribute to dysfunction and endothelial damage,[11] promoting the accumulation of plaque within arterial walls. This process leads to arterial narrowing and stiffening, ultimately resulting in cardiovascular effects such as atherosclerosis and increased arterial stiffness.[12]

Prior studies that employed traditional epidemiologic designs to examine associations between glucose variation and atherosclerosis indicators, specifically the brachial–ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV) and ankle–brachial index (ABI), were limited.[13-26] Only 1 study assessed the associations between glucose variation and ABI in persons with diabetes,[13] whereas 3 studies were conducted in the general population.[14-16] One study involving individuals with diabetes explored the relationship between annual fasting plasma glucose (FPG) variability and PAD, as determined by ABI, using a cohort approach.[13] No prior study has examined glucose variation in relation to atherosclerosis, as assessed by baPWV. Although some studies have explored the causal association between FPG and PWV and PAD via Mendelian randomization (MR),[27,28] they focused solely on glucose levels, not glucose variation, and were conducted exclusively in Western populations. Thus, this study examined whether a relationship exists between glucose variation and atherosclerosis markers of ABI and baPWV via an observational epidemiologic design and MR approach.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and subjects

A retrospective follow-up study was performed among individuals diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) who were enrolled in the Diabetes Case Management Program (DCMP) at China Medical University Hospital (CMUH). Eligibility for inclusion required a diagnosis of diabetes mellitus, defined according to the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) code 250. Exclusion criteria comprised persons younger than 30 years of age, those with a diagnosis of gestational diabetes (ICD-9-CM code 648.83) or type 1 diabetes (ICD-9-CM codes 250.x1/x3), or those lacking a minimum of 1 year of follow-up data. The enrollment period, spanning from November 2001 to June 2022, was selected to maximize the sample size and enable the calculation of 1-year glycemic variability indicators. The index date was the date on which the first measurements of baPWV and ABI were recorded. The cohort was designed as an open (dynamic) population, allowing participants to enroll in the study at various time points and to be followed for variable durations. Individuals without atherosclerosis measurements were excluded. The DCMP, established in 2001 by the National Health Insurance Agency, is a case management program initiative designed to enhance care quality and reduce the incidence of diabetes-related complications through comprehensive monitoring and improved care delivery. Initially, 14,321 individuals and 647 glucose variability-related genetic variants were included (Fig. 1). Following quality control procedures, 13,824 eligible participants with all 647 variants remained. After excluding individuals with missing data, 11,543 subjects were retained for analysis. Subsequently, after removing variants that violated MR assumptions and accounting for linkage disequilibrium (LD), 502 variants were evaluated in the final analysis. This study was approved by the Ethical Review Board of CMUH (CMUH112-REC1-007).

2.2. Data source

The data sources for this study included the Clinical Research Data Repository (CRDR) and the DCMP database of CMUH in Taichung, Taiwan. Established in 2017, the CMUH–CRDR integrates data from all clinical services to improve care quality by consolidating patient information, including physiological measurements, laboratory results, hospitalizations, prescriptions, emergency visits, surgeries, and whole-genome genotyping. Genetic data were generated using Affymetrix Axiom Genome-Wide TPM array chips (Affymetrix, Santa Clara). The DCMP includes patients diagnosed with diabetes according to the American Diabetes Association guidelines. Enrollment is physician-initiated, and only health professionals who have completed DCMP-specific clinical training are eligible to recruit patients. The multidisciplinary care team includes specialists in endocrinology, cardiology, internal medicine, family medicine, and nephrology. The program emphasizes standardized care through glucose monitoring, early detection of complications, and regular assessments. The DCMP database includes detailed records on education for self-care, quarterly physical and laboratory tests, annual eye exams, and other clinical outcomes.

2.3. Measurements

The primary predictor variable was glucose variability, which was assessed using measures such as coefficient of variation (CV) of glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and FPG, derived from annual outpatient records. Glucose variability was calculated using data from the year preceding the index date (first ABI and baPWV measurement). To account for differences in the number of measurements per individual, we weighted variability estimates by the reciprocal of √ (n/(n − 1)) following established methods. Glucose variability was calculated only for individuals with at least 2 FPG and HbA1c tests.

2.4. Covariates

Data on participants’ sex, age at DCMP enrollment, and family history of chronic conditions (i.e., hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and hyperlipidemia) were included. Lifestyle factors recorded in the DCMP dataset included exercise, alcohol consumption, smoking, passive smoking, and habitual diet. Except for dietary intake, all lifestyle variables were categorized as “yes” or “no” based on self-reports. Food consumption was assessed using 24-hour dietary recall interviews conducted by trained nurses. Macronutrient content (carbohydrates, protein, and fat) and energy intake were calculated using a food composition database. Nutrient intake was expressed as a percentage of total caloric intake and categorized into 3 groups: excessive (above tolerable upper intake), adequate (within recommended range), and deficient (below adequate intake). The recommended macronutrient distribution ranges were as follows: protein (10–20%), carbohydrates (50–60%), and fat (20–30%).

Medication use consisted of insulin and oral hypoglycemic agents. Oral agents were classified into 7 categories: meglitinides, biguanides, sulfonylureas, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, α-glucosidase inhibitors, insulin sensitizers, and other compounds. Insulin types included neutral protamine hagedorn insulin, regular insulin, insulin aspart, detemir (Levemir), and glargine (Lantus). Comorbidities (hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and obesity) and diabetic complications were recorded as binary variables (yes/no) from the DCMP dataset. Acute complications consisted of severe hypoglycemia, diabetic ketoacidosis, and hyperglycemic hyperosmolar nonketotic coma. Chronic complications consisted of stroke, peripheral neuropathy, nephropathy, retinopathy, diabetic foot, peripheral vascular disease, and amputation. Other medication use was categorized as “yes” or “no” based on electronic health records. Considered medications included antihypertensives (e.g., calcium channel antagonists), nephropathy treatments, and cholesterol-lowering medications (e.g., statins).

Body measurements included height, weight, blood pressure (BP), and body mass index (BMI), calculated as weight (kg)/height2 (m2). Weight and height were measured using an autoanthropometer (Super-View HW-666) with participants in light clothing and without shoes. BP was measured using an electronic sphygmomanometer (OMRON HEM-770A, Japan) with the appropriate cuff size, following standardized protocols. Participants rested for 5 to 10 minutes before measurement, seated upright with legs uncrossed and arms at heart level. For participants with more than one BP reading in a single day, the mean was calculated. The BP device model was validated according to the 2010 European Society of Hypertension International Protocol.

Fasting blood samples were collected from the antecubital vein in the morning after 8 to 12 hours of overnight fasting and processed within 4 hours. Analyses included FPG, HbA1c, lipid tests (triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol [HDL-C], and total cholesterol), uric acid, and serum creatinine by using a Beckman Coulter Synchron system (Lx-20, Fullerton) at the CMUH Clinical Laboratory. FPG was tested in fluoride-oxalate tubes (NAF TUBE) to inhibit glycolysis, with intra- and inter-assay CVs of 4%. HbA1c was tested via boronate-affinity HPLC (reference range: 4.6–6.5%), with intra- and inter-assay CVs of 1.09% to 2.91%. HDL and LDL levels were determined by direct enzymatic methods (CVs: HDL 4.5%; LDL 3–4.5%), and triglycerides were determined by enzymatic colorimetric assay (CVs: 5–6.8%). Urinary assessments included urinalysis for protein, 24-hour urinary protein excretion, and morning urine albumin and creatinine levels. Urinary albumin and creatinine were measured by colorimetric bromcresol purple and Jaffe’s kinetic method, respectively, by using an auto-analyzer (inter-assay CV < 3%). Urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio was calculated as a marker of albumin excretion. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was derived from the CKD-EPI equation, which incorporates age, serum creatinine, sex, and race.

2.5. Instrumental variables

2.5.1. SNP genotyping

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) used as instruments were derived from the CMUH–CRDR,[29] based on DNA samples genotyped with the TPM array and analyzed using the Axiom Genome-Wide Array Plate System (Affymetrix, Santa Clara). SNP quality control included evaluation of the Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) using PLINK v2.0, and genotype imputation was performed using IMPUTE2 with the 1000 Genomes Project as the reference panel. Candidate SNPs were selected based on published candidate gene studies and genome-wide association studies related to HbA1c[30-32] and FPG levels.[33,34] For HbA1c, we identified hundreds of loci associated via glycemic pathways (e.g., CDKAL1, DGKB, GCK, SLC30A8, MTAP, and KL) and nonglycemic pathways (e.g., SPATS2L, TRAM2-AS1, RPA2P2, KCNKS, and ARAP3). For FPG, several associated loci were identified, including HFE, MYB, ANK1, HK1, and PHB2.[33,34] All identified SNPs were cross-referenced with the CMUH–CRDR genomics dataset, and variants not present or with minor allele frequency < 5% were excluded.

2.6. Outcome variables (atherosclerosis): baPWV and ABI

baPWV and ABI were assessed employing an automated, noninvasive device (PWV/ABI; Colin Co. Ltd., Komaki, Japan) with pressure cuffs applied to the arms and ankles.[35] This device is suitable for large-scale screenings due to its ease of use, rapid measurement, and minimal operator dependence.[36,37] It simultaneously records BP, pulse wave velocity, heart sounds, and electrocardiogram data. Measurements were taken after a minimum of 5 minutes of rest, with participants in the dorsal recumbent position. Electrodes and cuffs were placed on both ankles and arms. The time delay between pulse waves at the ankle and brachial arteries (△Tba) was used to calculate baPWV, using subject height to estimate arterial path lengths. The formula for calculating the path length from the heart to the brachium (Lb) is as follows: Lb== 0.2195 × height of the person (in cm) − 2.0734. The formula for calculating the path length from the heart to the ankle (La) is as follows: La = 0.8129 × height of the person (in cm) + 12.328. baPWV is calculated as baPWV = (Lb- − La)/△Tba (cm/s). Higher baPWV indicates greater arterial stiffness. The higher value between the left and right sides was used. The method has high reproducibility, with inter- and intra-rater CV ranges of 2.4% to 8.4% and 5.8% to 13.3%, respectively, depending on the population.[38] ABI was derived as the relative value of systolic pressure at the ankle (dorsalis pedis or posterior tibial) to the higher of the 2 brachial pressures. The lower ABI value between sides was used, with ABI < 0.9 and baPWV ≥ 1400 cm/s considered abnormal.[39]

2.7. Statistical analysis

Mean ± standard deviation was presented for continuous variables, whereas frequency and proportion were used for categorical variables. Bivariate analyses employed 2-sample t-tests (continuous variables) and Chi-square tests (categorical variables). Multiple linear regression modeling followed a 2-step process. First, univariate analyses were conducted for key independent variables (glucose variability in HbA1c and FPG) and covariates, retaining those with P < .25 for further analysis.[40] Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and evidence from previous literature. These included demographic characteristics (age and sex), lifestyle factors, comorbidities, and relevant clinical variables known to be associated with glycemic control and study outcomes. Second, FPG and HbA1c variations were separately included in multivariate models with selected covariates to assess significance (P < .05). Final models estimated regression coefficients. Analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC), with 2-tailed P-values < .05 considered statistically significant.

2.7.1. MR analysis

Quality control procedures involved excluding individuals with high genotyping missingness, extreme heterozygosity, or relatedness. SNPs with low minor allele frequency, high missingness, or deviation from HWE were also excluded; HWE was evaluated in controls employing the Chi-square test. MR assumptions were evaluated as follows. To assess SNP-level assumption 1 (genetic association with glucose variability), we performed ANOVA. For assumption 3 (no association with vascular stiffness outcomes), Chi-square tests were used. Only SNPs meeting both criteria were used to construct unweighted and weighted genetic risk scores (GRSs). Associations between selected SNPs and glucose variability were further examined using linear regression under an additive model (genotypes coded as 0, 1, or 2 based on minor allele count).

Prior to GRS estimation, LD among SNPs meeting MR assumptions 1 and 3 was assessed using pairwise r2 in Haploview v4.2. For SNP pairs with r2 > 0.8, the SNP most frequently linked to glucose-related genes in the literature was retained (Fig. S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1). Only low-LD SNPs were used to derive the GRS. The weighted GRS was calculated by multiplying each SNP’s regression coefficient by its minor allele count and aggregating all retained SNPs. GRSs were classified into quartiles to evaluate linearity assumptions in regression models, and linear trends were assessed by analyzing GRS as a continuous variable.

Linear regression was used to evaluate GRS-level MR assumption 1 by testing the relationship between glucose variability and GRSs. To assess assumption 2 (absence of confounding), we performed multinomial logistic regression to examine associations between GRSs and potential covariates. Assumption 3 (no direct effect on outcomes) was tested via linear regression between GRSs and echocardiographic variables.

Causal effects of glucose variability on vascular stiffness outcomes were estimated using 2-stage instrumental variable regression with multivariable adjustment. In stage 1, glucose variability was regressed on weighted or unweighted GRSs to obtain genetically predicted glucose values. In stage 2, these predicted values were used as independent variables in linear regressions of vascular stiffness traits, adjusting for stage-one residuals, demographic and behavioral covariates violating assumption 2, and the top 10 genetic principal components. Horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using MR-Egger regression. All P-values were 2-sided with significance set at .05.

3. Results

A total of 2290 individuals with T2DM were included. The average baseline age was 61.79 years (SD = 11.62), and 57% of the participants were male. Among the study population, 247 individuals (10.79%) had abnormal ABI values, and 1101 individuals (48.08%) had baPWV ≥ 1800 (cm/s). Table 1 shows the features of study subjects stratified by abnormal ABI and baPWV status. Participants with abnormal ABI (<0.9) were older and included a greater fraction of women than those with normal ABI. They also had a lower mean value of BMI, a longer diabetes duration, a lower prevalence of oral antidiabetic drug (OAD) use, but a higher prevalence of combined insulin and OAD use than their counterparts. These individuals also had a higher prevalence of hypertension, stroke, hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease, nephropathy, peripheral neuropathy, and cardiovascular medication use. Additionally, they exhibited high mean HbA1c levels and low mean levels of HDL-C and eGFR. Similarly, individuals with abnormal baPWV were older and had a greater proportion of women than those with normal baPWV. They showed a lower prevalence of smoking and alcohol drinking, a lower mean BMI, and a longer diabetes duration than their counterparts. This group also had a low prevalence of OAD use but a high prevalence of combined insulin and OAD use. They had a high prevalence of stroke, hypertension, nephropathy, and coronary artery disease, as well as high use of cardiovascular and antihypertensive medication. In terms of laboratory findings, they had low mean levels of low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol, TC, and eGFR.

3.1. MR study

Whether individual SNPs meet MR assumptions 1 and 3 was evaluated at the SNP level by using an additive genetic model. The regression coefficients for FPG-CV and HbA1c-CV, as well as ORs for the abnormal status of ABI and baPWV for SNPs that met MR assumptions, are shown in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 2. For SNPs showing negative associations (i.e., regression coefficients < 0), allele coding was reversed (coded as 2, 1, or 0 according to the count of minor alleles) to ensure that the regression coefficients’ direction remained consistently positive across analyses.

We also examined MR assumption 1 at the GRS level, specifically the relationships between unweighted and weighted GRSs and HbA1c-CV and FPG-CV (Table 3). The results showed that weighted and unweighted GRSs were significantly positively linked with FPG-CV and HbA1c-CV, with and without covariate adjustment, thereby satisfying assumption 1. In other words, increases in the weighted or unweighted GRSs were associated with corresponding increases in FPG-CV and HbA1c-CV. All instruments demonstrated F-statistics > 30, indicating strong instrument strength. The GRS explained approximately 3.0% to 5.1% of the variance in the exposure, which is comparable to that reported in previous MR studies. Together with the high F-statistics (all > 30), this indicates that the instruments have adequate strength and are unlikely to suffer from weak instrument bias. Subsequently, we examined MR assumption 2 at the GRS level, which concerned the independence of the genetic instrument from potential confounders. Linear regression analyses examined the associations between glucose variability-related weighted and unweighted GRSs and covariates, including lifestyle behaviors, clinical and biochemical markers, sociodemographic factors, and comorbidities (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content 3). Some covariates were significantly associated with glucose variation-related GRSs, indicating the variables that violated MR assumption 2. As a result, they were not included as adjustment variables in the first-stage models. Finally, we examined MR assumption 3 at the GRS level, which evaluated whether the weighted and unweighted GRSs are significantly associated with abnormal ABI and baPWV status (Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content 4). Neither the unweighted nor weighted GRSs for glucose variability measures were significantly associated with abnormal ABI and baPWV after multivariate adjustment, thereby satisfying MR assumption 3. To assess horizontal pleiotropy, we conducted MR-Egger, Cochran’s Q, and MR-PRESSO analyses. All tests were nonsignificant (P > .05), indicating no evidence of pleiotropy or heterogeneity. These consistent results support the validity of the MR assumptions (Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content 5).

4. Discussion

This study provides integrated epidemiologic and genetic evidence on the association between glucose variability and surrogate markers of subclinical and clinical vascular changes. In the epidemiologic analysis, greater visit-to-visit variability in HbA1c and FPG was significantly associated with atherosclerosis as assessed by ABI, whereas no significant associations were observed when baPWV was used as the outcome. In the MR analysis, genetically predicted FPG variability showed significant associations with surrogate markers of subclinical and clinical vascular changes measured by both ABI and baPWV. While additional analyses supported the validity of the genetic instruments, including adequate instrument strength and no clear evidence of pleiotropy, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Overall, the MR results provide supportive, hypothesis-generating evidence that FPG variability may play a role in surrogate markers of subclinical and clinical vascular changes, rather than definitive evidence of causality.

A significant association observed in epidemiologic but not MR analyses may reflect confounding, measurement error, or reverse causality. Conversely, the lack of association in MR analyses could result from genetic instruments that do not adequately capture the true nature of glucose variability or from limited statistical power. A significant association detected in MR but absent in epidemiologic analyses may indicate a causal effect that remains undetectable in conventional observational data, potentially due to short follow-up durations, measurement limitations, or an exposure effect that only appears over a lifetime – an aspect that MR is uniquely adept at capturing. These findings highlight the value of integrating multiple analytical approaches and draw attention to the need for further studies to clarify the underlying mechanisms and improve the evaluation of glucose variability.

Few epidemiologic studies have investigated the relationship between blood glucose variability and indicators of atherosclerosis, either in populations with diabetes[13] or in the general population.[14-16] To date, no MR studies have addressed this question. One study conducted in individuals with diabetes assessed the risk of PAD, defined as ABI ≤ 0.9, but did not consider baPWV. This study reported a relationship between variability in FPG and ABI,[13] which was consistent with our findings based on epidemiologic and MR analyses. However, neither study assessed the variability of HbA1c.

One study performed in the general population examined the relationship between FPG variability and baPWV in 2002 individuals aged 40 years and older by using an epidemiological approach.[14] The study found that higher baPWV was associated with greater FPG variability, which was consistent with the findings from our MR analysis. Another case–control study involving individuals aged 50 years and older investigated the association between annual HbA1c variability and ABI.[16] The study reported that individuals with PAD, as determined by ABI, had higher annual HbA1c variability compared with those without PAD. Although this result was aligned with our MR findings, the relationship between HbA1c variability and ABI was not statistically significant in our MR analysis. This discrepancy may be due to residual confounding, measurement error, reverse causality, or limitations in the genetic instruments used, which may not fully capture the complexity of glycemic variability. To our knowledge, this work is the first study to explore the causal link between the genetically predicted glycemic variation phenotype and atherosclerosis indicators via an MR approach, employing a robust set of over 500 SNPs as instruments and data from a large group of individuals with T2DM.

This study had some limitations that should be considered. First, the study population may not accurately reflect the broad type 2 diabetes population, as participants were likely referred for ABI and baPWV assessments based on specific clinical indications. In routine clinical practice, such assessments are typically performed when vascular pathology is suspected or already diagnosed. Therefore, the individuals included in this analysis could have had preexisting risk factors or symptoms for atherosclerosis, which may limit the applicability of the findings to patients with T2DM who do not present with such clinical concerns. Second, regarding missing data, we acknowledge that the reduction in sample size due to complete case analysis may introduce bias. Unfortunately, imputation methods were not feasible in this study due to the lack of auxiliary variables required to support valid imputation. Given these constraints, we adopted a complete case approach. To assess the potential impact of missing data, we compared baseline characteristics between included and excluded participants (patients with type 1 diabetes and those younger than 30 years were excluded as they did not meet the eligibility criteria) using standardized effect sizes (Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content 6). The results showed that most baseline characteristics were comparable between included and excluded participants; however, several variables exhibited moderate differences. Therefore, the possibility of selection bias cannot be fully excluded. Third, the glucose-related variables (i.e., HbA1c and FPG) were derived from routine clinical care, resulting in non-standardized measurement frequency and timing across participants. We acknowledge that the minimum requirement of 2 measurements may be insufficient for reliably estimating variability indices such as the coefficient of variation (CV). In addition, variability assessed within the 1-year period prior to the index date may not fully capture long-term glycemic patterns. To partially address these concerns, we adjusted for the number of HbA1c and FPG measurements in the models to account for differences in measurement frequency, which may be related to disease severity. Nevertheless, residual measurement bias due to irregular visit intervals and unmeasured confounding cannot be completely excluded. Furthermore, although glucose measurements preceded ABI and baPWV assessments, the temporal window may not have been sufficient to fully reflect the longitudinal relationship between glycemic variability and atherosclerosis. Fourth, ABI < 0.9 is generally indicative of established peripheral artery disease rather than atherosclerosis, and the use of this threshold may have led to misclassification of disease stage. Although baPWV ≥ 1400 cm/s is a commonly used cutoff in epidemiological studies and has been supported by evidence from Asian populations,[39] it may still vary according to age and population characteristics. While we adjusted for age in our analyses, the use of a uniform cutoff without age-specific thresholds may introduce residual heterogeneity. In addition, both ABI and baPWV were measured at a single time point, precluding assessment of vascular progression and limiting causal inference. Although glycemic variability was assessed prior to the vascular measurements, the observational design and cross-sectional outcome assessment may introduce temporal ambiguity. Fifth, the genetic instruments used in the MR analysis were internally derived from the study population rather than from large external genome-wide association studies. This may increase the risk of bias, including overfitting and limited generalizability. However, multiple sensitivity analyses, including MR-Egger, MR-PRESSO, and leave-one-out analyses (Figs. S2–S5, Supplemental Digital Content 7), did not indicate substantial pleiotropy or undue influence from individual variants, supporting the robustness of our findings. Nevertheless, the causal interpretations should be made with caution. Sixth, this study has several problems regarding the measurement of covariates. Lifestyle factors, including smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical activity, were self-reported and dichotomized, which may have introduced non-differential misclassification and attenuated the observed associations. Dietary intake was assessed using a single 24-hour recall, which may not adequately reflect habitual intake, although this approach is commonly used in large-scale epidemiological studies for population-level estimation. Detailed information on medication exposure, such as dosage, duration, adherence, and longitudinal changes, was unavailable; thus, medication use was adjusted as categorical variables, which may not fully capture treatment effects. Several potentially important confounders, including glycemic control trajectories, hypoglycemia frequency, and socioeconomic status, were not available in the dataset. Nevertheless, we adjusted for key clinical variables, including baseline HbA1c and comorbidities, to partially mitigate confounding. As with all observational studies, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Finally, given the observational nature of this cohort study, causal inferences could not be established; the results should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of definitive causal relationships. Notably, the association between glucose variability and measures of atherosclerosis (ABI and baPWV) was not consistently observed across the epidemiological and MR analyses. In the MR analysis, this discrepancy could be partially attributed to the use of weak instrumental variables, which could compromise the strength and validity of the causal estimates.

5. Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates an independent association between glucose variability and surrogate markers of subclinical and clinical vascular changes using both epidemiologic and MR approaches. Both analyses consistently showed that greater visit-to-visit variability in FPG was associated with atherosclerosis as measured by ABI. While the MR findings provide supportive evidence for a potential role of FPG variability in the development of subclinical atherosclerosis, these results should be interpreted with caution. Further studies, particularly those evaluating interventions targeting glucose variability, are needed to clarify their clinical implications.
