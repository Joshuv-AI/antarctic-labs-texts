Group B Streptococcus male urethritis: A case report and literature review
Authors: Anthony Kanbar, Raghid El Khoury
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Streptococcus agalactiae or group B streptococcus (GBS), is an uncommon cause of male urethritis. We report a case of 38-year-old man presenting with 5 days of urethral discharge, dysuria, and pruritus. Testing for common sexually transmitted pathogens was negative. GBS was isolated as the sole organism from urethral swab and urine cultures. The patient was successfully treated with 10 days course of trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole. This case highlights GBS as a rare but potential cause of male urethritis when common pathogens are excluded. Further studies are required to clarify optimal antibiotic selection, treatment duration, and the need for partner management.

Introduction

Streptococcus agalactiae, also known as group B streptococcus (GBS), is a common colonizer bacteria of the lower gastrointestinal and genitourinary tracts, with carriage rates ranging from 30% to 50% among healthy adults.1 It is classically recognized as a significant pathogen in neonates and pregnant women.2 However, its incidence among non-pregnant adults has been steadily increasing.1,3,4 In this population, reported infections predominantly occur in elderly individuals, immunocompromised patients, and those with underlying urological abnormalities.5 The pathophysiology of these infections remains poorly understood and may be related to specific virulence characteristics of certain GBS strains, in addition to predisposing host factors.1

Despite the high rate of colonization, only 1–2% of monomicrobial urinary tract infections (UTIs) are related to GBS.6 Moreover, symptomatic urethral infection attributable to GBS is rarely documented,7 and there remains ongoing debate whether its isolation in the urethra represents incidental colonization or true pathogenic involvement.8

To our knowledge, only a limited number of case reports of GBS urethritis have been published. Here, we describe a case of GBS urethritis and review the relevant literature regarding clinical presentation, diagnostic considerations, and management strategies.

Case presentation

A 38-year-old man with no relevant medical history presented to the urology outpatient clinic with a 5-day history of whitish urethral discharge associated with penile pruritus and dysuria. He denied fever, chills, flank or suprapubic pain, testicular pain, perineal discomfort, or pain during ejaculation. The patient reported being sexually active and engaging in unprotected intercourse exclusively with his wife, who denied any genitourinary complaints. He was not immunocompromised and had no history of diabetes mellitus or urinary tract structural abnormalities.

On physical examination, a mucopurulent urethral discharge was noted. Examination of the external genitalia, testes, abdomen, and flanks was otherwise unremarkable. In the absence of high-risk sexual behavior or systemic symptoms, empiric antimicrobial therapy was withheld pending microbiological evaluation.

A comprehensive workup for sexually transmitted infections was performed, including serologic testing for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), hepatitis B and C viruses, and syphilis. Urinalysis and urine culture were obtained. Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) for Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Mycoplasma genitalium, and Trichomonas vaginalis were performed on urethral and pharyngeal swabs.

Investigations were negative for common sexually transmitted pathogens. However,Streptococcus agalactiaewas identified on urethral swab culture and in a midstream urine specimen cultured on standard agar media at a concentration of 105 CFU/mL. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing demonstrated sensitivity to beta-lactam antibiotics and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, with resistance to tetracyclines.

At the follow-up visit, a gentle digital rectal examination was performed to exclude concomitant prostatitis given the atypical pathogen. The prostate was soft, non-tender, and non-inflamed. Based on clinical, microbiological, and examination findings, a diagnosis of urethritis caused by an atypical pathogen was established.

The patient was treated with trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole for 10 days. He reported rapid improvement of his symptoms shortly after initiation of treatment and complete resolution by the end of therapy.

Discussion

Urethritis in men is classically categorized as gonococcal when gram-negative intracellular diplococci are identified on urethral gram stain smear in the presence of polymorphonuclear leukocytes. In the absence of such findings, the condition is classified as non-gonococcal urethritis (NGU), which represents the most common genitourinary tract syndrome in men.8 The global incidence of NGU has increased over recent years, largely due to the expanded use of NAATs, allowing identification of a broader range of microorganisms.8

Despite improved diagnostic capabilities, the pathogenic significance of several organisms detected in NGU remains controversial. While Chlamydia trachomatis and Mycoplasma genitalium are well-established etiological agents, the role of other bacteria remains debated. Organisms such as Ureaplasma parvum, Moraxella catarrhalis, and Streptococcus – particularly GBS - species are frequently considered part of the normal urethral microbiota, with insufficient evidence supporting their role as true pathogens in acute urethritis.8

GBS is a common commensal of the gastrointestinal and genitourinary tracts.1,9 Classically, invasive infections associated with GBS are seen in patients with impaired immunity, including elderly individuals and those with diabetes, malignancy, chronic renal or liver disease, HIV infection, or prolonged corticosteroid therapy. However, invasive GBS infections have increasingly been reported in immunocompetent nonpregnant adults, possibly reflecting the emergence of strains with enhanced virulence characteristics.1

GBS may be transmitted through close interpersonal contact, including sexual activity. Previous studies have demonstrated associations between sexual practices—particularly oral sex—and co-colonization with identical GBS strains among sexual partners, suggesting potential sexual transmission. 1,9, 10, 11, 12, 13.

Interpretation of these cases is complicated by the high prevalence of asymptomatic GBS colonization in men, which was reported to up to 30% in healthy male populations.1,10,15 In addition, studies evaluating men with acute urethritis have reported GBS prevalence rates ranging from approximately 1% to 6.7%, often in association with other established urethral pathogens.7,8,16,17 This overlap further blurs the distinction between asymptomatic colonization and true infection.1,9

In the present case, the clinical symptoms were typical of acute urethritis. Extensive microbiological evaluation was negative for common urethral pathogens, and GBS was the sole microorganism isolated from urethral secretions. This sum of the findings supported the diagnosis of GBS urethritis. Current international guidelines for the management of urethritis do not provide specific recommendations regarding antibiotic selection or treatment duration for Streptococcus agalactiae infections.18,19 In our case, the isolated strain was resistant to tetracycline, and susceptibility to azithromycin was not evaluated, although both agents are commonly used empirically for the treatment of NGU.19,20 Given the atypical etiology and the absence of evidence-based treatment recommendations, therapy was guided by antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole was selected based on antimicrobial susceptibility testing and in accordance with recommendations for urinary tract infections in men18, given the atypical presentation of urethritis and the concomitant positive midstream urine culture. The treatment duration of 10 days was chosen as an intermediate course, longer than the 7-day regimen typically recommended for urethritis18,19 but shorter than the ≥14 days generally advised for acute bacterial prostatitis.18 The patient responded favorably, with complete resolution of symptoms.

On the other hand, our patient reported exclusive sexual intercourse with a single, asymptomatic regular partner. This clinical context raises questions regarding the appropriate classification of GBS-associated urethritis—specifically, whether it should be managed as a form of non-gonococcal sexually transmitted infection or considered a distinct clinical entity related to bacterial imbalance rather than classical infection. This concept may be analogous to bacterial vaginosis in women, in which overgrowth of commensal organisms leads to dysbiosis and clinical symptoms.

In conclusion, further studies are required to clarify the pathogenic role of GBS in male urethritis, to determine whether a distinct classification is warranted, and to establish evidence-based recommendations regarding antibiotic selection, treatment duration, and the need for partner management.
