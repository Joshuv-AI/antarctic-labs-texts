Hydrogen sulfide inhibits recruitment of monocyte-derived tumor associated macrophages in glioblastoma by downregulating CXCL12
Authors: Joseph Camarano, Morgan Roque, Gabrielle Gahn, Stephen Garrett Whipple, Danielle Terrell, Charles Ronkon, Jamie Toms, Anthony Sin, Bharat Guthikonda, Khatri Latha, Yuhui Yang, Xinggui Shen, Christopher G Kevil, Ganesh Rao, Sungho Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTumor associated macrophages (TAMs) directly contribute to the dismal prognosis of glioblastoma by preventing anti-tumor immunity and promoting tumor invasion and angiogenesis. Inhibiting TAM infiltration is a potential therapeutic strategy in glioblastoma, with several chemokine antagonists in early clinical development. Hydrogen sulfide, a gasotransmitter that regulates microglial accumulation in a wide range of CNS diseases, may be a novel therapeutic target to prevent TAM recruitment in glioblastoma. In this study, hydrogen sulfide concentrations were directly measured from 14 isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH)-wildtype glioblastoma surgical samples and compared against overall survival as well as expression of TAM markers and chemokines. Effects of hydrogen sulfide donor therapy on survival and TAM recruitment were also examined in a genetically engineered mouse model of glioblastoma. High hydrogen sulfide concentrations conferred a survival benefit in IDH-wildtype glioblastoma, in association with reduced monocyte-derived TAM density and downregulation of CXCL12. These findings were validated by administering hydrogen sulfide donor SG1002 to an immunocompetent mouse model of glioblastoma, which improved survival, inhibited monocyte infiltration, and downregulated CXCL12. Finally, hydrogen sulfide donor treatment directly reduced CXCL12 expression in glioblastoma cells, diminishing their ability to recruit monocytes in vitro. Taken together, these results demonstrate that hydrogen sulfide signaling prevents monocyte-derived TAM accumulation in glioblastoma by inhibiting chemotaxis.Keywords: Hydrogen sulfide, Tumor associated macrophage, Glioblastoma

Introduction

Immunosuppressive microenvironment in glioblastoma is dominated by tumor associated macrophages (TAMs) that arise from CNS resident microglia or peripheral monocytes [1]. When compared to microglia-derived TAMs, monocyte-derived TAMs preferentially produce anti-inflammatory cytokines that prevent lymphocyte mediated anti-tumor immunity and promote tumor invasion and angiogenesis [2,3]. This abundant, non-transformed, peripherally accessible cell population represents an attractive therapeutic target. Drugs that inhibit TAM recruitment or alter TAM function in glioblastoma are currently in early clinical development [[4], [5], [6], [7]].

Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) is a gasotransmitter, generated in part by the transsulfuration pathway and mercaptopyruvate sulfurtransferase (MPST) in the brain [8]. It potently downregulates microglial activation in various CNS diseases including status epilepticus [9], Parkinson's disease [10], Alzheimer's disease [11], and hypoxic-ischemic injury [12]. In glioblastoma, H2S signaling has been mainly examined for its effect on tumor cells with conflicting results [[13], [14], [15]]. However, its effect on the immunosuppressive glioblastoma microenvironment has not yet been examined.

We hypothesized that H2S signaling influences TAM behavior in glioblastoma. In this study, associations between H2S concentrations in surgical samples, survival, and TAM recruitment were determined. Furthermore, effects of H2S donor therapy were examined in the microenvironment of an immunocompetent mouse model of glioblastoma. We show that high H2S concentrations and H2S donor therapy provide a survival advantage in glioblastoma associated with diminished recruitment of monocyte-derived TAMs and downregulation of the chemokine CXCL12. These results provide evidence for H2S as a TAM-based therapeutic target in glioblastoma.

Materials and methods

Patients

Under the LSU Health Shreveport IRB approved tumor bank protocol (#1837), informed consent was obtained from the patients or their representatives. Samples were collected at the time of surgical resection, after ensuring that there was sufficient specimen for diagnosis. A small portion of the sample was immediately processed for H2S concentration measurements, while the remainder was divided in half and flash-frozen or fixed in 10 % formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Fourteen pathologically confirmed isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH)-wildtype glioblastoma were used in this study.

H2S measurements

H2S concentrations were measured by reverse phase high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with a fluorescence detector after derivatization with excess monobromobimane (MBB; B4380, Sigma), as previously reported [16]. The redox molecular signalling core of the CRBCD Center of Biomedical Research Excellence under RRID:SCR_024778 performed analytical measurement of H2S concentrations.

Briefly, tumor tissue was homogenized in Tris-HCl reaction buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 9.5, 0.1 mM diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid). Tumor lysate was derivatized with MBB and measured by Shimadzu Prominence 20A equipment with RF-10AXL (λex 390 nm and λem 475 nm) and Eclipse XDB-C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm). H2S concentrations were normalized to total protein and reported as nM/mg protein.

Mouse blood was collected by intracardiac puncture under terminal anesthesia and separated into plasma by centrifugation. Plasma was added to the reaction buffer and processed as above. Plasma H2S concentrations were reported as μM.

H2S is additionally stored as bound sulfane sulfur. Therefore, relative sulfane sulfur concentrations were measured using SSP4 fluorescent probe (SB10, Dojindo), which emits green fluorescence upon reaction with sulfane sulfur, but not free H2S [17]. Tumor tissue was homogenized in RIPA buffer, and tumor lysates containing 1 mg protein were incubated with 10 μM SSP4 for 1 h at 37 °C. Samples were added to a 96-well plate and fluorescence was detected using a plate reader with λex 488 nm and λem 510 nm. Mouse plasma samples were processed similarly.

Immunohistochemistry

Following heat-induced citrate buffer antigen retrieval and peroxide treatment, paraffin sections of human or mice tumors were incubated at 4 °C overnight with the following antibodies: rabbit anti-human CD163 (1:200, ab87099, Abcam), rabbit anti-human/mouse CXCL12 (1:50, MAB350, R&D Systems), rabbit anti-mouse ionized calcium binding adaptor molecule 1 (Iba1; 1:500, NBP2-19019, Novus Biologicals), or rabbit anti-human hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1; 1:100, ab51608, Abcam). Next day, sections were incubated with appropriate biotinylated secondary antibodies (1:200, Vector) followed by Vectastain ABC reagent (Vector), and developed with diaminobenzidine as the chromogenic substrate and hematoxylin as the counterstain. Images were obtained using the VS200 Slide Scanner (Olympus).

For double immunofluorescence studies, slides were heated in citrate buffer for antigen retrieval, blocked, and incubated at 4 °C overnight with rabbit anti-human MPST (1:500, PA5-51548, ThermoFisher) and mouse anti-human GFAP (1:500, NBP2-29415, Novus Biologicals); rabbit anti-human cystathionine gamma-lyase (CTH; 1:100, ab151769, Abcam) and mouse anti-human GFAP; rabbit anti-human cystathionine beta synthase (CBS; 1:100, ab140600, Abcam) and mouse anti-human GFAP; rabbit anti-human MPST and mouse anti-human/mouse Iba1 (1:500, MA5-27726, ThermoFisher); or rabbit anti-human/mouse P2RY12 (1:500, ab300140, Abcam) and mouse anti-human/mouse Iba1 antibodies. Sections were subsequently incubated with appropriate Alexa Fluor conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500, Invitrogen) and counterstained with DAPI. Images were obtained using Axio Observer microscope (Zeiss).

Analysis of cell density

The total number of cells and number of CD163-positive, Iba1-positive, Iba1-positive/P2RY12-negative, CXCL12-positive, or HIF-1-positive cells were counted from 5 non-overlapping high power fields from randomly selected human or mouse tumors. The number of positively stained cells were divided by the total number of cells and expressed as a percentage. Percent of infiltrating monocyte-derived TAMs was determined by dividing the number of Iba1-positive/P2RY12-negative cells by total number of Iba1-positive cells.

RNA expression analysis

RNA was extracted from glioblastoma samples using the RNeasy kit (Qiagen), reverse transcribed into cDNA, and amplified using TaqMan probes (Applied Biosystems) specific for H2S-generating enzymes: MPST (Hs05579360_s1), CTH (Hs00542284_m1), CBS (Hs00163925_m1), and beta-actin (ACTB; Hs01060665_g1). The relative abundance of transcript levels was expressed as a fold change using the ddCt method, with ACTB as endogenous control, and compared against H2S concentrations.

The open access cBIO Cancer Genomics Portal (www.cbioportal.org) was used to obtain MPST expression for a publicly available The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) cohort of 144 patients with IDH-wildtype glioblastoma using the Onco Query language syntax. Correlations between MPST expression and overall survival as well as expression of other genes of interest (CD163 and CXCL12) were determined using the built-in comparison tools.

Mouse model

Replication-competent avian sarcoma-leukosis virus long terminal repeat with a splice (RCAS) expressing platelet derived growth factor subunit B (PDGFB) and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) were generated as described previously [18]. Immortalized DF-1 chick fibroblast cells were grown in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Invitrogen) containing 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C. Live virus was produced by transfecting RCAS-PDGFB or RCAS-STAT3 into DF-1 cells using FuGENE 6 (E2691, Promega).

Mice expressing the avian tumor virus receptor A (TVA) under the Nestin promoter (Nestin-TVA) were maintained on a B6 background. DF-1 cells transfected with RCAS-PDGFB and RCAS-STAT3 (1 × 104 cells in 1 μL PBS) were injected bilaterally into the frontal lobes of Nestin-TVA pups within 48 h of birth when Nestin-positive cells are most proliferative. Expression of PDGFB and STAT3 in glioneural precursors have been shown to generate tumors that recapitulate proneural glioblastoma [18].

Following weaning, the mice were randomly assigned to control diet or diet containing 40 mg/kg/d of H2S donor SG1002 (Sulfagenix). This previously published regimen preserved cardiac function in a mouse model of congestive heart failure and enhanced metabolism in a mice fed a high-fat diet, without toxicity [19,20]. Mice were humanely euthanized by carbon dioxide asphyxiation at 90 days after injection, or sooner if symptoms related to tumor burden were present. Brains were removed, fixed, in formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for immunohistochemical analysis.

For SG1002 pharmacodynamics studies, non-tumor bearing B6 mice at 2 months were either fed a control or SG1002-containing diet for 21 days prior to blood collection and euthanasia. All animal experiments were approved by the LSU Health Shreveport Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (#P-23-020).

In vitro studies

T98G glioblastoma cells were cultured to 80 % confluence in DMEM containing 10 % FBS and incubated with 100 μM Na2S4 (SB04, Dojindo) or equivalent volume of phosphate buffered saline (PBS) in serum-free DMEM for 24 h (Na2S4 is a H2S donor that has previously been shown to convert the thiol group in cysteine (–SH) to persulfide (–SSH) at this concentration [21].) Conditioned media (CM) was collected for CXCL12 ELISA or transwell migration assay, and cells were harvested by trypsinization.

RNA was extracted from the cell pellets using the RNeasy kit (74104, Qiagen), reverse transcribed into cDNA, and amplified using TaqMan probes specific for CXCL12 (Hs03676656_mH), CCL2 (Hs00234140_m1), CX3CL1 (Hs00171086_m1), and ACTB (Applied Biosystems). Differences in CXCL12, CCL2, or CX3CL1 transcript levels in response to Na2S4 versus PBS treatment was expressed as a fold change using the ddCt method, with ACTB as endogenous control.

A commercially available ELISA kit (DSA00, R&D Systems) was used to determine CXCL12 concentrations in CM according to manufacture instructions. Measured optical density values were fitted against the standard curve and sample CXCL12 concentrations were calculated as pg/mL.

THP-1 monocytes were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) media (Invitrogen) containing 10 % FBS and differentiated to macrophages by incubating with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate-13-acetate (PMA; Sigma) for 24 h. 2 × 105 macrophages were suspended in serum-free DMEM and seeded into 24-well transwell inserts with 8.0 μm pores (Corning). CM from T98G cells treated with PBS or 100 μM Na2S4 was added to the receiver wells. Following overnight incubation, migrated macrophages were fixed, stained with cresyl violet (Sigma), and counted from 4 high-power fields using the Axio Observer microscope (Zeiss). Serum free DMEM was used as the negative control (NC) and DMEM containing 10 % FBS as the positive control (PC).

PMA differentiated THP-1 macrophages were also separately treated with CM from T98G cells treated with PBS or 100 μM Na2S4. Relative expression levels of immunosuppressive cytokines IL10 (Hs00961622_m1) and TGFB1 (Hs00998133_m1) were compared using the ddCT method with ACTB as endogenous control, as described above.

Statistical analysis

Survival statistics were reported using Kaplan-Meier curves, which were compared using the log-rank test. Correlations between continuous variables measured from surgical samples or RNA sequencing database were assessed with Spearman's correlation coefficient. Patient characteristics were compared using the chi-squared test for discrete variables and t-test for continuous variables. Unpaired t-test was used to compare SG1002 dependent effects and T98G gene expression. ANOVA with post hoc Tukey's test was used to compare transwell migration assay results between different conditions. Statistical analysis was performed using the GraphPad Prism software.

Results

Effect of tumor-derived H2S on survival

Fourteen IDH wildtype glioblastomas from an institutional tumor bank were included in the analysis. Free H2S concentrations were measured from tumor lysates following derivation with monobromobimane, as described previously [16]. Patients with high tumor H2S concentrations (n = 7) had significantly longer median overall survival than those with low tumor H2S concentrations (351 versus 98 days, ∗∗P = 0.0075, log-rank test; Fig. 1A). Patient characteristics including sex, age at diagnosis, KPS, extent of resection, adjuvant therapy and O-6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) promoter methylation status did not significantly differ between the two groups (Supplementary Table 1).

(A) Kaplan-Meier curves showing overall survival of patients with IDH wildtype glioblastoma stratified by high (n = 7; blue; above median) or low (n = 7; red; below median) tumor H2S concentrations (∗∗P = 0.0075, log-rank test). There was a positive correlation between tumor H2S concentrations and relative MPST expression (B; n = 14, ∗∗P = 0.0037, Spearman) as well as SSP4 fluorescence, indicator of sulfane sulfur (C; n = 9, ∗P = 0.021, Spearman). Histologic sections of IDH wildtype glioblastoma were immunostained with antibodies against H2S-generating enzyme MPST (red, D and E) and pan-myeloid marker Iba1 (green, D) or tumor marker GFAP (green, E), revealing co-localization between GFAP and MPST immunofluorescence (asterisks, E), but not Iba1 and MPST immunofluorescence (asterisks, D). (F) Percent of MPST-positive/GFAP-positive cells was significantly higher in patients with high (n = 3) compared to low (n = 4) tumor H2S concentrations (∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001, t-test). Scale bar, 25 μm.

In order to determine the potential sources of tumor H2S, surgical samples were analyzed for relative expression of H2S-generating enzymes: CTH, CBS, and MPST. Only the relative expression of MPST positively correlated with H2S concentrations (n = 14, r = 0.71, ∗∗P = 0.0037, Spearman; Fig. 1B), whereas the relative expression of CTH (Supplementary Fig. 1A) and CBS (Supplementary Fig. 1B) did not. In addition, relative SSP4 fluorescence in tumor lysates, indicator of sulfane sulfur concentrations, was directly proportional to H2S concentrations (n = 9, r = 0.77, ∗P = 0.021, Spearman; Fig. 1C). Finally, histologic sections were immunostained with antibodies against MPST and pan-myeloid marker Iba1 (Fig. 1D) or MPST and GFAP, an astrocytic intermediate filament protein and a marker for tumors of astrocytic origin including glioblastoma (Fig. 1E). Co-localization between MPST (red) and GFAP (green; Fig. 1E) but not Iba1 immunofluorescence (green; Fig. 1D) provided support for tumor-specific H2S production. Greater percentage of GFAP-positive cells expressed MPST in patients with high (n = 3) compared to low (n = 4) tumor H2S concentrations (∗∗∗P < 0.0001, t-test; Fig. 1F), suggesting that MPST is the major H2S generator in glioblastoma. On the other hand, CTH and CBS expression did not co-localize with GFAP (Supplementary Fig. 1C and D).

H2S-dependent alterations in TAM accumulation and ontogeny

To determine the impact of H2S on the immune microenvironment, histologic sections from tumors with low (Fig. 2A) and high H2S concentrations (Fig. 2B) were immunostained with antibody against CD163, a TAM marker. There was a significant inverse correlation between CD163-positive TAM density and tumor H2S concentrations (n = 10, r = −0.65, ∗P = 0.044, Spearman; Fig. 2C). Next, the effect of H2S on TAM ontogeny was examined by double immunofluorescence with antibodies against the pan-myeloid marker Iba1 (green, Fig. 2D and E) and microglia-specific marker P2RY12 (red, Fig. 2D and E) [22]. High tumor H2S concentrations were associated with decreased abundance of Iba1-positive, P2RY12-negative monocyte-derived TAMs (n = 10, r = −0.66, ∗P = 0.043, Spearman; Fig. 2F).

Histologic sections of IDH wildtype glioblastoma with low H2S concentrations (A, D, and G) and high H2S concentrations (B, E, and H) were immunostained with antibody against a TAM marker CD163 (A and B), combination of antibodies against a pan-myeloid marker Iba1 (green, D and E) and resident microglia specific marker P2RY12 (red, D and E), and antibody against chemokine CXCL12 (G and H). There was an inverse correlation between tumor H2S concentrations and density of CD163-positive TAMs (C; n = 10, ∗P = 0.044, Spearman), percent of Iba1-positive, P2RY12-negative, peripherally derived TAMs (F; n = 10, ∗P = 0.043, Spearman), and density of CXCL12 expressing cells (I; n = 10, ∗P = 0.0005, Spearman). Scale bar, 50 μm.

In order to investigate the potential mechanisms behind decreased recruitment of monocyte-derived TAMs in tumors with high H2S concentrations, histologic tumor sections were immunostained with antibody against CXCL12 (Fig. 2G and H), a chemokine shown to promote monocyte infiltration in glioblastoma [23]. CXCL12 immunoreactivity was observed in tumor and endothelial cells based on morphology (Fig. 2G and H) and inversely proportional to tumor H2S concentrations (n = 10, r = −0.92, ∗∗∗P = 0.0005, Spearman; Fig. 2I). In addition, histologic tumor sections were immunostained with HIF-1 (Supplementary Fig. 2A and B), which upregulates CXCL12 in glioblastoma [24]. Tumors with high H2S concentrations exhibited significantly decreased expression of HIF-1 compared to those with low H2S concentrations (n = 4, ∗∗P = 0.0075, t-test; Supplementary Fig. 2C).

Validation using TCGA data

We further validated the association between H2S and TAMs by leveraging publicly available TCGA RNA sequencing data in IDH-wildtype glioblastoma patients. MPST expression was used as a surrogate for H2S concentration, based on the relationship shown in Fig. 1B. Consistent with our results, patients with high MPST expression (n = 42) showed increased overall survival compared to those with low MPST expression (n = 102; 15.1 versus 12.5 months, ∗P = 0.013, log-rank test; Supplementary Fig. 3A). Furthermore, MPST expression was inversely correlated with CD163 (n = 144, r = 0.23, ∗∗P = 0.0045; Supplementary Fig. 3B) and CXCL12 (n = 144, r = 0.24, ∗∗P = 0.0041; Supplementary Fig. 3C).

Effect of H2S donor therapy on survival and TAM recruitment in a mouse model of glioblastoma

RCAS-driven expression of PDGFB and STAT3 induces endogenous tumors from Nestin-positive glioneural precursors in immunocompetent Nestin-TVA mice (raised on a B6 background), which resemble proneural glioblastoma [18]. Following injection with RCAS-PDGFB/RCAS-STAT3 constructs, the mice were administered a control diet or a diet containing SG1002 (40 mg/kg/d), a H2S donor currently in clinical trials for congestive heart failure [25]. Mice administered SG1002 gained weight similarly as the control mice (Supplementary Fig. 4A) and did not show any manifestations of drug toxicity. Representative tumors from control and SG1002 groups are shown in Supplementary Fig. 4B–D.

SG1002 administration increased plasma free H2S (n = 5 per group, ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001, t-test; Fig. 3A) and polysulfide concentrations (n = 4 per group, ∗P = 0.016, t-test; Fig. 3B) compared to a control diet in wildtype animals, confirming appropriate pharmacodynamics. Tumor-bearing mice fed a SG1002 containing diet (n = 25) showed significantly improved median overall survival compared to those fed a control diet (n = 25; 74 days versus not reached, ∗P = 0.020, log-rank test; Fig. 3C), with a follow-up duration of 90 days.

(A) Wildtype mice fed a diet containing H2S donor SG1002 (40 mg/kg/d; n = 5) demonstrated increased plasma free H2S concentrations compared to those fed a control diet (n = 5; ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001, t-test). (B) Similarly, relative plasma SSP4 fluorescence (indicating sulfane sulfur concentrations) was increased in the SG1002 group (n = 4) compared to controls (n = 4, ∗P = 0.016, t-test). (C) Kaplan-Meier curves showing overall survival for Nestin-TVA mice injected with RCAS-PDGFB and RCAS-STAT3 and fed a control (n = 25) or SG1002-containing diet (n = 25, ∗P = 0.020, log-rank test). Histologic sections of tumors from control (D, G, and J) and SG1002 groups (E, H, and K) were immunostained with an antibody against Iba1 (D and E), combination of antibodies against a pan-myeloid marker Iba1 (green, G and H) and resident microglia specific marker P2RY12 (red, G and H), and antibody against chemokine CXCL12 (J and K). SG1002 administration decreased accumulation of Iba1-positive TAMs (F, n = 4, ∗P = 0.013, t-test), relative abundance of Iba1-positive, P2RY12-negative, peripherally derived TAMs (I: n = 4, ∗∗P = 0.0018, t-test) and density of CXCL12 expressing cells (L, n = 3–4, ∗P = 0.036, t-test). Scale bar, 50 μm.

To determine the effects of SG1002 on TAMs, histologic sections of RCAS-PDGFB/RCAS-STAT3 derived tumors from control (Fig. 3D) or SG1002 group (Fig. 3E) were immunostained with an antibody against Iba1. SG1002 treatment decreased overall TAM density compared to control diet (5.6 % versus 14.4 %, n = 4 per group, ∗P = 0.013, t-test; Fig. 3C). Co-staining Iba1 (green, Fig. 3G and H) and P2RY12 (red, Fig. 3G and H) revealed decreased relative abundance of Iba1-positive, P2RY12-negative monocyte-derived TAMs in the SG1002 compared to control group (12.4 % versus 42.4 %, n = 4 per group, ∗∗P = 0.0018, t-test; Fig. 3I). Finally, compared to tumors from the control group (Fig. 3J), tumors in the SG1002 group (Fig. 3K) exhibited decreased CXCL12-positive cell density (7.0 % versus 2.7 %, n = 3–4 per group, ∗P = 0.036, t-test; Fig. 3L).

H2S-mediated inhibition of macrophage chemotaxis

Transwell migration assays were performed to examine the chemotaxis of PMA-differentiated THP-1 macrophages towards CM from T98G glioblastoma cells treated with PBS or 100 μM Na2S4 (Fig. 4A). Whereas CM from PBS treated T98G cells increased THP-1 macrophage migration by 2.46-fold compared to control (n = 6 per group, ∗∗P = 0.0070, ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test; Fig. 4B), this response was abrogated with CM from Na2S4 treated T98G cells (n = 6 per group, ∗P = 0.010, ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test). In addition, CM from Na2S4 treated T98G cells inhibited IL10 but not TGFB1 expression in THP-1 macrophages, reflecting a partial reversal of immunosuppressive phenotype promoted by glioblastoma CM [26] (Supplementary Fig. 5A and B).

(A) Transwell migration of THP-1 macrophages in response to conditioned media (CM) from T98G glioblastoma cells treated with PBS or 100 μM Na2S4, as well as serum-free media (NC) or media containing 10 % serum (PC). (B) CM from PBS treated T98G cells promoted chemotaxis of THP-1 macrophages compared to NC (∗∗P = 0.0070, ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test), but this effect was abrogated with CM from Na2S4 treated T98G cells. (∗P = 0.010, ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test). Data are representative of 6 independent experiments per each condition. Scale bar, 50 μm. Na2S4 treatment of T98G cells decreased CXCL12 transcript levels (C) and CXCL12 concentrations in CM (D) compared to PBS treatment (n = 3, ∗P = 0.010, ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001, t-test).

To identify potential molecular mediators, T98G glioblastoma cells were treated with H2S donor Na2S4 (100 μM) or PBS and examined for differential chemokine expression. Relative CXCL12 mRNA expression was down-regulated 1.89-fold by Na2S4 treatment (n = 3 per group, ∗P = 0.010, t-test; Fig. 4C), whereas CCL2 and CX3CL1 transcript levels were unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 5C and D). CXCL12 protein concentrations in the CM were also significantly reduced by Na2S4 treatment compared to PBS treatment (532 versus 250 pg/mL; n = 3 per group, ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001, t-test; Fig. 4D).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the role of H2S signaling in the glioblastoma microenvironment. Higher H2S concentrations in surgical specimens, despite the small sample size (n = 14), and H2S donor therapy in a mouse model of glioblastoma were associated with improved survival and reduced monocyte-derived TAM density. Importantly, expression of chemokine CXCL12 was specifically inhibited by H2S. Taken together, these data from surgical specimens, immunocompetent mouse model of glioblastoma, and in vitro experiments confirm the beneficial role of H2S signaling in inhibiting the recruitment of monocyte-derived TAMs by downregulating CXCL12 (Graphical Abstract).

The glioblastoma microenvironment is dominated by TAMs, derived from resident microglia and infiltrating peripheral monocytes [1]. TAMs promote tumor invasion, angiogenesis, and immunosuppression by producing proteases, growth factors, and anti-inflammatory cytokines [[27], [28], [29]]. TAM ontogeny – microglia-versus monocyte-derived – has become better defined recently, with the former expressing characteristic markers such as P2RY12 [22]. Importantly, monocyte-derived TAMs are found more frequently in necrotic core and perivascular spaces in glioblastoma, with preferential production of immunosuppressive factors and angiogenic mediators compared to microglia-derived TAMs [2,3].

Therefore, inhibiting chemokine-mediated TAM infiltration is an attractive therapeutic strategy in glioblastoma. In particular, CXCL12-CXCR4 signaling is implicated in recruitment of monocyte-derived TAMs, especially following radiation, and its inhibition mitigates angiogenesis and tumor recurrence in rodent models of glioblastoma [23,24]. Plerixafor (CXCR4 inhibitor) combined with adjuvant chemoradiation improved local control rates in newly diagnosed glioblastoma compared to historical controls [7]. Treatment of incompletely resected MGMT non-methylated glioblastoma with CXCL12-neutralizing aptamer showed radiographic response in 9 out of 10 patients in a recent phase I/II trial [6]. Importantly, our results specifically showed downregulation of CXCL12 in response to H2S signaling.

Previous studies investigating H2S in glioblastoma have mostly focused on its effects on tumor behavior, with inconsistent results. Treatment of C6 rat glioma cell line with H2S donor NaHS either promotes growth or enhances apoptosis, depending on experimental conditions [30,31]. Decreased H2S concentrations in response to high fat diet in the GL261 model of glioblastoma generated more aggressive, chemotherapy-resistant tumors [14]. On the other hand, CTH knockout mice formed smaller brain tumors upon intracranial transplantation of GL261 tumor cells [15]. However, our study is the first to specifically examine the impact of altering H2S concentrations on TAMs in the glioblastoma microenvironment, based upon the clear immunoregulatory role that H2S plays in various CNS diseases, including Parkinson's disease [10], Alzheimer's disease [11,32], and hypoxic-ischemic injury [12,33].

H2S has high CNS bioavailability and inherent blood-brain barrier permeability. It is enzymatically generated from cysteine metabolism by CTH, CBS, and MPST. Interestingly, only MPST expression correlated with tumor H2S concentrations, suggesting that MPST is the primary H2S-generating enzyme in glioblastoma, in accordance with previous studies [34,35]. H2S concentrations may be modulated by multiple H2S donors currently in development, including SG1002 used in this study, which is in early clinical trials for heart failure [25]. Therapeutic strategies to boost H2S concentrations in glioblastoma merit further consideration to reduce TAM recruitment and reverse TAM-mediated immunosuppression and angiogenesis.

Future studies will focus on identifying mechanisms by which H2S downregulates CXCL12 expression in glioblastoma. In particular, specificity protein 1 (SP1) activates CXCL12 transcription [36], but is negatively regulated by H2S-mediated sulfhydration of –SH thiol group in cysteine residues to persulfide (–SSH) [37]. Another candidate is HIF-1 (Supplementary Fig. 2), which upregulates CXCL12 [38], but is inhibited by H2S-driven phosphorylation of eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2α (eIF2α) [39]. Importantly, both SP1 and HIF-1 have been implicated in immune cell recruitment by glioblastoma cells [24,40]. Additional experiments will also seek to determine whether H2S has direct effects on CXCR4 expression or function. For instance, H2S donor GYY4137 inhibits CXCR4 expression in oxidized low-density lipoprotein treated RAW 264.7 macrophages [41].

Our study has limitations. Despite the pronounced survival difference based on H2S concentrations, the institutional cohort is small. Additional specimen collection is ongoing to confirm initial findings. Furthermore, conclusions derived from surgical samples are correlational in nature. Therefore, patient derived glioblastoma stem cell lines are being generated to directly examine the consequences of altered H2S signaling. Although THP-1 monocyte express CXCR4 and migrate in response to CXCL12 [42], the proof-of-principle transwell migration assays (Fig. 4) will need to be validated using primary monocytes. Lastly, mechanisms by which H2S downregulates CXCL12 expression remains incompletely understood, and their elucidation will be the focus of our future studies. Nonetheless, we highlight the heretofore unrecognized impact of H2S on TAM recruitment in glioblastoma, which could form the basis for future immunotherapies.

Conclusions

In IDH-wildtype glioblastoma, increased tumor H2S concentrations were associated with reduced abundance of monocyte-derived TAMs and downregulation of CXCL12. In an immunocompetent mouse model of glioblastoma, H2S donor therapy inhibited infiltration of monocyte-derived TAMs and CXCL12 expression. Taken together, these results highlight H2S as a potential immunotherapeutic target in glioblastoma.
