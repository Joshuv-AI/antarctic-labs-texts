Surgical outcomes and stoma-related complications in inflammatory bowel disease in Saudi Arabia: a retrospective study
Authors: Thamer A. Bin Traiki, Sulaiman A. Alshammari, Mansoor A. Abdulla, Fayez G. Aldarsouni, Noura S. Alhassan, Maha-Hamdien Abdullah, Awadh Alqahtani, Khayal A. Alkhayal
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND: The prevalence of inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD), Crohn's (C) and ulcerative colitis (UC) has increased in Saudi Arabia during the past decade. Even though medical treatment is first-line therapy, most patients require surgery during the course of the disease. Stoma creation complications in IBD are underreported in the literature of the Middle East and especially in Saudi Arabia.OBJECTIVES: Report the postoperative, stoma and peristomal complications following stoma creation in (C) versus UC.DESIGN: Retrospective cohort studySETTINGS: Tertiary care centerPATIENTS AND METHODS: Patients with IBD who underwent stoma creation for either UC or CD between August 2015 and July 2020 were included. The diseases were compared to assess their characteristics and association to postoperative, stoma and peristomal complications. All complications were reported over a 90-day duration from the surgery. Patients younger than 14 years of age were excluded.MAIN OUTCOME MEASURES: Postoperative complications, stoma and peristomal complications in IBD patients who underwent stoma creation.SAMPLE SIZE: 50RESULTS: Of 50 IBD patients underwent stoma creation, 32 patients (64%) were diagnosed with CD and 18 patients (36%) with UC. Most of the procedures in both groups were laparoscopic and elective. Low BMI and serum albumin were more prevalent in the CD group. Postoperative complications were higher in the CD patients compared to the UC patients (CD 40.6% vs UC 11.1%, P=.028) with the most common complication being abdominal collection[a]. Stoma complications were comparable between the two groups (UC 16.7% vs CD 15.6%). However, peristomal complications were higher clinically in UC patients in comparison with the CD patients (UC 61.1% vs CD 37.5% P=.095) with the most common complication being skin excoriation (UC 44.4% vs CD 37.5%).CONCLUSIONS: CD has significantly higher postoperative complications compared to UC. Peristomal complications were high in both groups and had a negative impact on quality of life. Therefore, comprehensive stoma education and regular outpatient follow ups are recommended to improve the overall outcomes.LIMITATIONS: Retrospective and conducted in one academic institution with a small sample size.

INTRODUCTION

The prevalence of inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD) has risen in the past 30 years, and represents a significant disease burden. An initially reported rate of 79.5 per 100 000 population in 1990 has increased to 84.3 per 100 000 in 2017.1 Despite this increase, breakthroughs in medical and biological treatment options have dramatically influenced disease progression and prognosis.2 However, surgery remains a cornerstone in the treatment arsenal in 10% to 30% of ulcerative colitis (UC) patients, and up to 70% of Crohn's disease (CD) patients. The use of surgery is primarily due to failure of response to optimized medical treatment or development of life-threatening complications.3 In Saudi Arabia, IBD is progressively becoming a common disease, shifting from its previous status as a rare disease in the 1990s and early 2000s, with similar reports from other countries in the Gulf region.4 In a single center retrospective study in Saudi Arabia, 53% of patients with CD and 20% of patients with UC required surgery during treatment.5

The increased use of medical treatments such as corticosteroids and biologics in IBD possesses the ability to hinder surgical outcomes. This claim is backed by results from large studies identifying biological treatments as an independent risk factor for the development of postoperative complications.2 The use of biologics is the most debatable subject in IBD discussions with multiple studies reporting contradicting results on the role of biologics in affecting surgical outcomes.2 However, an overall effect on wound healing by vedolizumab, a monoclonal antibody has been proven during a laboratory experiment in mice.6 This ultimately led to a preference among some surgeons to create a temporary or permanent stoma during the surgical course.

The creation of stomas has been a surgical practice present for over 100 years, targeting fecal diversion in elective and emergent settings.7 The intent of surgery in CD mainly comprises stricturoplasty or resection, with a high rate of recurrence.8 Surgical options in UC include traditional Brooke type end ileostomy, continent ileostomy, ileorectal anastomosis, or ileal pouch anal anastomosis (IPAA).9 Although largely abandoned due to the risk of eventual development of carcinoma and intractable proctitis, ileorectal anastomosis was preferred by patients as it did not require stoma creation.9 The current standard in UC surgery is the IPAA with temporary ileostomy.10 Despite advances in surgical techniques, postoperative stoma-related complications are reported to be around 35% in CD and 18% in UC, with a marked effect on the quality of life.11 The aim of this study was to report the postoperative complications, stoma and peristomal complications in CD and UC patients following stoma creation at a single academic center in Saudi Arabia.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

We reviewed the electronic medical records in King Saud University Medical City in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. We included all patients with a diagnosis of either CD or UC who underwent stoma creation between August 2015 and July 2020. Pediatric patients younger than 14 years of age were excluded. Demographics, clinical data, and creation-related variables were collected at the presentation. Relevant variables were used to compare both diseases to further assess their characteristics and association to postoperative, stoma and peristomal complications. All complications were reported over a 90-day duration from the surgery. For the postoperative complications, the Clavien-Dindo classification was used for the reporting, which ranks the severity of the complications based on the intervention used to treat them.12 The assessment of the severity was reported by a single colorectal surgeon.

In our center, the surgical management of IBD patients was carried out only by a designated colorectal surgery unit. By the time of this study, the unit was composed of three colorectal consultants with more than 5 years of experience and one junior consultant with less than 5 years of experience. In the majority of cases, preoperative stoma marking was performed by specialized stoma nurses especially in difficult abdomen domains. Moreover, the stoma nurse team provided comprehensive stoma care teaching sessions to the patients and their caregivers after the surgery. All patients were followed postoperatively in the outpatient clinic by the stoma nurse team.

We reported continuous variables using means and standard deviations. If normally distributed, the t-test was used to compare subgroups. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages, and we used the chi-square or Fisher's exact test (when appropriate) to compare subgroups. Data collection was done using Microsoft Excel version 16.64, and statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS v. 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA, 2019).

RESULTS

Of 50 IBD patients who underwent stoma creation between August 2015 and July 2020, 32 patients (64%) were diagnosed with CD and 18 patients (36%) with UC. The median (minimum-maximum) age of the study population was 32 (16–61) years (Table 1). The median BMI was significantly lower in the CD group (20.0 kg/2) compared to the UC group (26.3 kg/m2) (P=.004). BMI less than 20 kg/m2 was documented in 17 patients of the CD group (53.1%) compared to 4 patients in the UC group (22.2%) (P=.032). The albumin level < 30 g/L was more prevalent in CD group when compared to UC group; 78.1% and 50%, respectively (P=.043) respectively. Perianal disease and disease recurrence presented expectedly in the CD group (18.8%, 25.8%, respectively) compared to none in the UC group (P=.05, .01, respectively). Commutant colorectal cancer rate was higher in the UC group (22.2%) when compared to 3.1% to the CD group (P=.05).

Postoperative complications were higher in the CD group compared to the UC group (CD 40.6% vs UC 11.1%) (P=.028) (Table 4). Abdominal collection was commonly reported in the CD group (18.8%) compared to none of the UC group (P=.057). However, stoma and peristomal complications were higher in the UC group in comparison to the CD group (UC 16.7% vs CD 15.6% and UC 61.1% vs. CD 37.5%, respectively). The most common peristomal complication was skin excoriation (UC 44.4% vs CD 37.5%) followed by stoma bag leakage (UC 27.8% vs CD 12.5%). Prognostic analysis of many variables toward postoperative, stoma and peristomal complications were performed, with no significant result. This is mainly due to small sample size and number of complications. Males had a higher rate of complications than females (Table 5). Malnourishment, represented by albumin being <30 g/L, was more frequent in patients with complications. The rate of end configuration in patients with stoma complications was more frequent than in patients with a loop configuration, and Ileostomies were more common than colostomies (Table 6). Ileostomies in in patients with peristomal complications were more frequent than colostomies with end configuration being more common than loop (Table 7).

DISCUSSION

Stoma creation during surgical management of IBD patients is a tool to reduce postoperative complications.11 The protective and divertive role of stomas aid in the successful fulfillment of the primary surgical intent, as evident in procedures such as IPAA for UC and end ileostomies post proctocolectomy, subtotal colectomy, total colectomy, and ileocecal resections.13 Although beneficial, stoma creation is associated with complications that can affect patient recovery and quality of life.14 This study aimed to report the postoperative complications of stoma creation in IBD patients.

With a complicated disease course like IBD, several studies worldwide have documented the preoperative risk factors associated with the development of postoperative complications.11,13 While disease-specific etiologies may vary, it has been reported that malnourishment in IBD patients predisposes to an increase in postoperative complications.15–17

This is explained by the catabolic nature and a fistulizing phenotype of CD, which results in an inability to meet adequate caloric demands during increased disease activity, eventually leading to malnutrition.15 Our study reports BMI <20 kg/m2 and serum albumin <30 g/L to be more common in CD when compared to UC. Although CD had a higher postoperative complication rate in our data, we were unable to run a regression test to determine prognostic factors due to the small sample size. A study done by Yang S et al concluded that albumin <30 g/L is an independent risk factor for postoperative complications in CD patients.17 Another multicenter study done by Cai Z et al showed that a lower BMI was an independent risk factor for short-term complications in surgical UC patients.16

Definitive proof of concept regarding the effect of biologics on surgical outcomes is yet to reach a consensus.2 Multiple large-scale studies presented conflicting verdicts regarding their association with postoperative complications in IBD patients.2,18–22 Our data showed that use of azathioprine and biologics 12 weeks prior to surgery was higher in CD patients than UC patients (59.4% vs 22.2% and 56.3% vs 38.9%), respectively. The absence of an agreement is attributed to several interpretations; mainly the difference in study designs and parameters.2 The relatively recent approval of vedolizumab and ustekinumab introduces the necessity for new studies.2 However, a scientific concept presented by Schleier et al indicated possible impaction in wound healing with the use of vedolizumab.6 Owing to its inhibition of the integrin α4-β7, it was found to reduce classical monocytes (M2 macrophages) in the systemic circulation.6 This warrants an individualized cautious approach in the clinical setting. While the indications on stopping biologics prior to surgery are still controversial, postoperative administration within 8 weeks of surgery is recommended by the American Gastroenterological Association guidelines.10,23

Corticosteroids are cornerstones of IBD therapies, but prolonged use causes the disease to be steroid-resistant as well as the inevitable relapse and flare ups.24 When administered preoperatively they are associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications, most commonly anastomotic leak and infectious complications.25 Despite observing the rate of corticosteroid use in our study for CD patients and UC patients to be comparable, the rate of overall surgical complications was higher in the CD patients. This includes abdominal collection, where the rate of CD patients to UC patients is (18.8% to 0% P=.057). This could be attributed to the effect of corticosteroids on postoperative outcomes as well as the nature of CD and its complications.24 These observations are similar to the conclusions of a meta-analysis by Subramanian et al where corticosteroids had a positive association with an increased risk of postoperative complications.25 Unfortunately, due to the small sample size in our study we were not able to assess the predictive role of corticosteroids toward complications.

The most common type of stoma created in our study was an ileostomy (CD patients, 75% vs UC patients, 94.4%) in an elective setting (CD patients, 74.2% vs UC patients, 77.8%) using a laparoscopic approach (CD patients, 54.8% vs UC patients, 66.7%). Elective stoma creation allows for preoperative stoma marking, which can contribute to improved outcomes.7 Our study categorized complications into postoperative, stoma and peristomal complications. The postoperative complication rate was higher in CD patients (CD patients, 40.6% vs UC patients, 11.1% P=.028). Stoma complications were similar in both groups, but slightly higher in UC patients (CD 15.6% vs UC 16.7%). However, peristomal complications were clinically higher in UC UC patients (CD patients, 37.5% vs UC patients, 61.1% P=.095) with skin excoriation being the most common peristomal complication in both groups (CD patients, 37.5% vs UC patients, 44.4%). These results correlate to a study done by Takahashi et al and Estrada et al, respectively. Takahashi et al found a stoma-related complication rate in IBD patients to be CD patients, 36.8% vs UC patients, 17.4%.11 The discrepancy between the rates of stoma-related complications in relation to our results can be explained by the exclusion of skin excoriations in their study. Estrada et al found stoma-related complications to be 53.7% in CD patients vs 44.4% in UC patients with categorization based on the onset of complications.13 Stomas can a have a negative biopsychosocial effect on a patient's quality of life depending on many clinical and social factors.26 Among the many possible complications, skin excoriations and dermatitis can cause pain and bad odor and lead to frequent reapplication of the collection bag. Such peristomal complications contribute to longer length of stay and rate of readmission.27 This process causes patients to feel shame and further limit them from performing daily tasks thus leading to a dissatisfaction with their life.28

This study has limitations. The study nature of a retrospective cohort study has potential bias. Furthermore, this study was conducted in one academic institution with a small sample size. Therefore, multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to generalize these results. Moreover, we lack objective measures of the immune status, especially since the majority of our patients who were on immunomodulator/suppressant medications. Considering these measures might add significance to the study.

In conclusion, the high rate of postoperative complications in CD is attributed to the nature of its complicated presentation and high malnourishment rate. Stomal and peristomal complications have a significant impact on the patients' quality of life. In order to minimize the negative effects of stomas, comprehensive stoma education and regular outpatient follow up may be beneficial.
