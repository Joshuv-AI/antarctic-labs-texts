Cetuximab combination with cytokine induced killer cells effectively inhibit conjunctival squamous cell carcinoma in vitro
Authors: Hossein Aghaei, Mehdi Khakpour, Samaneh Dehghan, Ali Ghamari, Alireza Shoae-Hassani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe explored the potential effects of the anti-EGFR monoclonal antibody Cetuximab when combined with activated cytokine-induced killer cells (CIKs) as a novel treatment strategy for human conjunctival squamous cell carcinoma (CSCCs) in vitro. The CSCC used in our study was obtained from an explant of a patient diagnosed with the condition. After molecular and pathological confirmations, the CSCC was established as proliferating and confluent cultures and was exposed continuously for seven days to different concentrations of Cetuximab (10–3-103 µg/mL) and different ratios of CIKs. CIK cells were cultured with a high dose of IL-2 and the cell population contained activated CD3 + , CD16 + , and CD56 + cells. CIK cells alone exhibited cytotoxicity toward EGFR-positive CSCC whereas Cetuximab alone exhibited limited cytotoxic efficacy. Final counts of CSCC measured on days 1 and 7 after exposure demonstrated over 95% suppression of proliferating CSCC at a Cetuximab concentration of 102 µg/mL, with a CIKs ratio of 50:01 (Effector:Target). Meanwhile, the confluent normal limbal stem cells were largely unaffected. This particular anti-EGFR monoclonal antibody could be beneficial in treating proliferative ocular epithelial conditions, including squamous cell carcinoma of the ocular surface.Keywords: Anti EGFR, CIK, CSCC, Immune Cell Therapy, Limbal Stem Cell, Ocular carcinoma

Introduction

Conjunctival squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC) falls under the category of ocular surface squamous neoplasms (OSSN). Epidemiological research indicates that environmental ultraviolet (UV) exposure is the main factor responsible for triggering the onset of this condition [1]. Additionally, other factors such as human papillomavirus (HPV), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), smoking, chemical exposure, and injuries to the ocular surface may further promote its progression [2, 3].

CSCC is characterized by tumor cells penetrating the basement membrane and invading the substantia propria [4]. Depending on the predominant atypical cell type, CSCC can present as spindle cells, epidermoids, clear cells, or muco-epidermoids [5]. Although it is classified as a low-grade malignancy relative to other forms of squamous cell carcinoma, it has an incidence ranging from 0.02 to 3.5 cases per 100,000 [6]. Surgical excision remains the standard approach for managing OSSN lesions; however, due to the potential complications associated with surgery and the rise of minimally invasive diagnostic techniques like optical coherence tomography and impression cytology, there is an increasing interest in topical medications as primary or adjunctive treatments. Various agents, such as interferon-α2b (IFN), mitomycin-C (MMC), and 5-fluorouracil (5FU), have been introduced for this purpose [7]. Nevertheless, in some instances, CSCC can result in ocular morbidity and mortality [8].

The overexpression of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) in various human cancers has been associated with a poor prognosis [9]. By interfering with the interaction between EGFR and its natural ligand, it becomes possible to impede the associated signaling pathways, resulting in anti-tumor effects, cell cycle arrest, and prevention of metastasis. Cetuximab, a monoclonal antibody (mAb), specifically targets the extracellular domain of EGFR and has demonstrated efficacy in the treatment of certain squamous cell carcinomas (SCCs). However, resistance to cetuximab can occur due to k-ras mutations that are frequently observed in SCCs [10]. Consequently, despite the approximate 90% upregulation of EGFR in SCC patients, careful patient selection for this therapy is essential [11, 12]. Absolutely, alongside patient selection, it is crucial to explore innovative strategies to improve the effectiveness of these medications. One of the most promising approaches may be to combine monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) with cellular immunotherapy. Cytokine-induced killer cells, comprising activated natural killer (NK) cells and activated T-cells, have demonstrated therapeutic advantages in adoptive immunotherapy in clinical settings [13]. In the presence of elevated levels of recombinant human interleukin-2 (IL-2), CIKs can undergo ex vivo expansion. The cytotoxic activity of NK cells and T-cells partly depends on the natural killer group 2 member D (NKG2D) and DNAX accessory molecule-1 (DNAM-1) systems [14]. Notably, NKG2D is crucial for helping NK cells overcome inhibitory signals. Moreover, NK cells can engage in antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) against cancer cells that are bound to immunoglobulins [15]. Although NK cells have not shown standalone clinical effectiveness, their combination with a monoclonal antibody (mAb) can provide therapeutic advantages through ADCC [16]. Additionally, activated and expanded CD8 + T-cells can target tumor cells using the NKG2D system independently of the T-cell receptor (TcR)-class I system.

Our hypothesis focused on the potential enhanced efficacy of a combined approach utilizing both cytokine-induced killer (CIK) cells and monoclonal antibodies (mAb), rather than relying on the individual application of each treatment alone. In this study, we examined the synergistic cytotoxic effects of cetuximab and CIKs on conjunctival squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC) to pave the way for further preclinical studies and clinical trials (Fig. 1).

Material and methods

Isolation and expansion of the CSCC

A biopsy of the resected conjunctival squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC) from a male patient who visited Rassoul Akram Hospital at Iran University of Medical Sciences was explanted to establish primary cultures following established cell culture protocols [17]. This study was conducted in compliance with the ethical standards of the Institutional Ethics Committee of Iran University of Medical Sciences, adhering to the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Ethical approval was granted by the National Ethics Committee (IR.IUMS.REC.1401.4133). Written informed consent was obtained from the patient prior to the collection of tissue and blood samples for research purposes. In brief, the 24 mm biopsy was divided into two sections. One portion was placed in 6-well culture plates (JetBiofil, Guangzhou, China) and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator set to 5% CO2, in complete media containing Dulbecco Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 15% fetal calf serum (FCS; Gibco, UK), 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 2 mM Glutamax (Gibco, UK). The explants were removed after 10 days, and the cells were sub-cultured weekly in the complete medium. The purity of the cultures was assessed by flow cytometry using a pan-cytokeratin marker (MNF-116FITC; Dako), as previously described [17].

Characterization of the CSCC

The second part of the biopsied tumor was transported to the pathology laboratory in formalin. Serial sections of tissue were cut to a thickness of 0.4 mm and prepared for histochemical evaluation as previously described [18]. Briefly, the hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining process involved steps such as tissue fixation, dehydration, and clearing, followed by staining with hematoxylin, differentiation, counterstaining with eosin, and subsequent dehydration and clearing. The sections were then mounted in an aqueous medium (Crystal Mount™; Biomeda Corp, US) and cover-slipped using DPX™ mounting (VWR International, Poole, UK). Photomicrographs were captured with a digital camera attached to an Olympus microscope at 4 × and 10 × magnification (Olympus, Sydney, Australia).

Scratch test

About 72 h after cultivation, a scratch was made in the middle of the culture surface using a sterile Pasteur pipette, and then the images were taken immediately and 24 h after this scratch by an inverted microscope.

EGFR expression in isolated CSCC

After dissociation of the CSCCs using Trypsin–EDTA (Gibco, UK) and washing in phosphate buffered saline (PBS, Gibco; UK); these cells were lysed using Trizol (Merck; Germany) to extract the total RNA of cultures cells. The cDNA synthesis was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol of the Qiagen cDNA synthesis kit (Cat no. 330451; Qiagen, Germany). The final reaction volume of each microtube was 20 µL. The EGFR gene was detected using the following primer pairs: EGFR mRNA: 5′- TGAAGGCTGTCCAACGAA-3′ (sense) and 5′- CGCATGAAGAGGCCGATC-3′ (anti-sense). Beta-actin with the following primer sequence was used as an internal control gene: 5′-TCGTGCGTGACATTA AGGAG-3′ (sense) and 5′-ATGCCAGGGTACATGGTGGT-3′ (anti-sense). The initial step of denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min followed by binding of primers at 94 °C for 10 s and finally DNA synthesis at 60 °C for 40 s completed the PCR cycle, which continued for 35 cycles. EGFR expression levels were normalized to β-actin expression to minimize sample-to-sample variation. Relative gene expression was calculated using the comparative Ct (2^-ΔΔCt) method, and fold changes were determined relative to the control group.

Expansion and activation of cytokine induced killer cells

CIK cells were generated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) obtained from a healthy donor, who provided written informed consent and was different from the donor from whom the CSCC was sourced. In summary, PBMCs were incubated with a high concentration of recombinant IL-2 (2000 U/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific, US) along with 10 μg/mL of OKT3 murine monoclonal IgG2 isotype (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US) and cultured for 14 days in RPMI to achieve sufficient expansion of CIK cells. The surface markers on the CIK cells were labeled through immunofluorescence using monoclonal antibodies (CD3-FITC, CD16-FITC, CD56-PE, NKG2D-PE; Beckman Coulter, France). CIKs were characterized by flow cytometry using antibodies against CD3 and CD56 surface markers. The proportions of CD3⁺CD56⁺, CD3⁺CD56⁻, and CD3⁻CD56⁺ cell populations were quantified and expressed as percentages of the total gated lymphocyte population. The percentage of the CD3⁺CD56⁺ population, representing the activated CIK subset, was used as the primary indicator of successful CIK expansion and characterization. Fluorescence was measured using an FC500 Beckman Coulter flow cytometer and reported as relative mean fluorescence intensity or as a percentage above the baseline.

Cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxicity assay was conducted as previously described [19]. In brief, the cytotoxic activity of CIK cells against CSCC was assessed at effector-to-target (E:T) ratios of 10:1, 20:1, 30:1, and 50:1, both in the presence and absence of cetuximab (10 μg/mL). Target CSCC cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. CIK effector cells were then added at the specified ratios, and the co-cultures were incubated for 72 h. Following the treatments, 20 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to each well and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. The medium was subsequently removed, and formazan crystals were dissolved in 200 µL of DMSO. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Biocompare, USA). All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results are presented as mean ± SD.

Antibody dependent cell cytotoxicity assay

ADCC assay was performed by Calcein-AM release (Biotium, Fermont; US) [20]. In brief, Calcein-AM release from target CSCC was quantified in the supernatant to assess cytotoxicity. Target cells (104 cells/well) were loaded with Calcein-AM for 30 min at 37 °C, washed, and co-cultured with CIK cells at indicated E:T ratios in the presence or absence of cetuximab. After 4 h incubation, fluorescence in the supernatant was measured using a microplate reader (excitation/emission: 485/530 nm). Percent specific lysis was calculated as: % cytotoxicity = [(Experimental release − Spontaneous release)/(Maximum release − Spontaneous release)] × 100, where maximum release was obtained by lysing target cells with 0.1% Triton X-100, and spontaneous release was measured in wells containing target cells only. Experiments were performed in triplicate. All the experiments were performed in triplicate and the limbal stem cell (LSC) culture (purchase from Persian Type Culture Collection, PTCC; Tehran, Iran) was used as a control.

Statistical analysis

All cytotoxicity experiments (MTT and Calcein-AM assays) were performed in triplicate and repeated at least three independent times using separate cultures. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using the Mann–Whitney U test because the data were non-parametric and the sample size was relatively small. Comparisons were conducted as pairwise analyses between the experimental groups. Where multiple pairwise comparisons were performed, the potential risk of type I error was taken into consideration during interpretation of the results.

Results

Histopathological features of CSCC

CSCC in tissue sections is characterized by a loss of typical surface features, including keratinization, a disorganized arrangement of epithelial cells with maturation abnormalities, and the presence of large polyhedral cells with hyperchromatic nuclei and prominent nucleoli (Fig. 2A). Invasive cell clusters are frequently found near the limbal transition zone, which is a common location for ocular surface tumors. The basal epithelial cells in these lesions often display a spiky morphology, indicating a phenotypic change. In contrast, normal conjunctival epithelium exhibits well-defined cell nuclei and organized epithelial layers (Fig. 2B).

The results revealed that the CSCC exhibited a significantly higher migratory potential compared to normal cells. The scratch made on the monolayer of CSCC showed a rapid and extensive closure, indicating enhanced cell migration (Fig. 3).

Epidermal growth factor receptor in CSCC

Additionally, we examined the expression of the EGFR gene in CSCC, as these effector transcripts are known to influence cell proliferation and have been implicated in related ocular surface disorders. The RT-PCR analysis revealed overexpression of the EGFR transcript in CSCC (Fig. 4A), while minimal EGFR expression was detected in normal LSC (Fig. 4A). Quantitative RT-PCR analysis indicated that EGFR mRNA levels in cultured CSCC cells were significantly elevated (approximately 8.4 ± 1.2-fold) compared to normal LSC (p < 0.01; data not shown). In contrast, EGFR transcript levels in the control cells were close to the detection limit, suggesting low baseline expression. Furthermore, we evaluated CSCC for pan-cytokeratin expression using flow cytometry. A substantial proportion of CSCC cells displayed strong intracellular pan-cytokeratin staining (Fig. 4B), whereas normal LSC did not exhibit this signal.

Effects of cetuximab on CSCC proliferation

We initially evaluated the impact of cetuximab on the viability of CSCC phenotypically (Fig. 5A-H) and subsequently performed the MTT assay (Fig. 5I). CSCC was cultured with varying concentrations of cetuximab (10⁻3, 10⁻2, 10⁻1, 10⁰, 101, 102, 103 μg/mL) for one week, with daily assessments. Treatment with cetuximab reduced the viability of CSCC, as illustrated in Fig. 4A-H, which can be attributed to the overexpression of EGFR in our established CSCC model. The results indicate that a concentration of 102 μg/mL exhibited optimal cytotoxicity for killing CSCC in vitro (Fig. 5I). However, the determination of the effective dose in future in vivo studies may vary slightly.

Effects of CIK cells in combination with cetuximab on CSCC suppression

We assessed whether cultured CIK cells exhibited cytotoxicity against CSCC either alone or in combination with cetuximab. Activated CIKs were generated by culturing PBMCs with high-dose IL-2 and OKT-3 for two weeks. The expression levels of cell surface markers FcR IIIa (CD16) and NKG2D were analyzed, as shown in Fig. 6A. The CIK cell population comprised two effector subsets: CD16 +/CD56 + NK cells and CD3 +/NKG2Dhigh + NKT cells (Fig. 6A). As demonstrated in Fig. 6B, CIK-mediated cytotoxicity increased in a dose-dependent manner, plateauing at an effector-to-target ratio of 30:1, which was established as the optimal condition for subsequent experiments. Although the cytotoxicity of CIKs alone was modest, the addition of cetuximab (at a concentration of 102 μg/mL after 96 h) significantly enhanced the cytotoxic effect of CIKs. Notably, antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) expedited the CIK cells’ cytotoxic action against the target CSCC. Microscopic examination of the CSCC cultures revealed that, in the presence of CIK cells, CSCCs were destroyed more rapidly (within 48 h) and with greater intensity (Fig. 7). The synergistic effect of cetuximab on CIK cells was evident, while control LSCs remained unaffected (Fig. 7). In ADCC assays conducted with increasing doses of cetuximab, we observed that doses ranging from 10 to 100 μg/mL significantly heightened the susceptibility of CSCC to CIK-mediated cytotoxicity (p ≤ 0.05), reaching a plateau at 100 μg/mL (Fig. 8). These findings indicate that cetuximab concentrations above 0.1 μg/mL are sufficient to achieve maximum ADCC activity.

Discussion

In this study, the CSCC expressed EGFR and was sensitive to the anti-EGFR cetuximab established by our team. The first aim of this work was to develop a model for further in vitro studies on CSCCs. The procedure used to create the cell line followed protocols that have been documented for establishing cell lines from other SCCs [21]. The isolated cells were positive for EGFR expression (Fig. 4A) and also expressed pan-cytokeratin as the general SCC marker (Fig. 4B, C). The cetuximab alone had cytotoxicity against our isolated cells. However, it has significantly enhanced cytokine-induced killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity through ADCC too, suggesting that even weak expression of EGFR is sufficient to mediate ADCC against CSCCs. A principal limitation of the present study is that all functional in vitro experiments were performed using a primary CSCC culture derived from a single patient. We acknowledge that reliance on a single patient-derived specimen restricts the generalizability of our findings because of inter-patient heterogeneity in tumor biology, EGFR expression, and immune susceptibility. However, we emphasize that this work represents a proof-of-concept investigation that demonstrates feasibility and mechanistic plausibility for combining Cetuximab and CIK cells against conjunctival SCC in vitro. Several practical and ethical constraints limited the number of available patient samples for this initial study: (i) the relative rarity of clinically resected CSCC tissue suitable for primary culture at our center during the study period, (ii) the requirement for immediate processing of fresh tissue under sterile conditions to establish viable primary cultures, and (iii) institutional ethical restrictions on excess tissue collection beyond diagnostic needs. Importantly, findings from a single, well characterized primary culture can provide meaningful proof-of-principle data that justify and inform larger follow-up studies.

Cetuximab demonstrated selective cytotoxicity toward CSCC cells but not toward normal limbal stem cells (LSCs). This selective effect is likely attributable to the markedly higher EGFR expression in CSCC cells, whereas normal LSCs exhibit only minimal or baseline EGFR expression, as shown by our RT-PCR data (Fig. 4A). Previously, Liu et al. [22, 23] demonstrated that the EGFR antibody showed stronger membrane staining in limbal basal epithelial cells than in suprabasal cells, and that the distribution of these molecules was not uniform throughout all regions of the limbus. Cetuximab acts through EGFR blockade and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) mechanisms, which depend on surface EGFR density.

Usually, OSSN therapies can be surgical or non-surgical. The gold-standard therapy of OSSN is the surgical excision with a no-touch technique [24]. Despite the equal success to the medical treatment in small tumors [25] surgery causes conjunctival scarring, symblepharon, conjunctival hyperemia, and limbal stem cell deficiency [26]. Also, it does not guarantee micro-metastasis removal which leads to 30% recurrence rates [27]. On the other hand, topical chemotherapy of OSSN including IFN, MMC, and 5-FU [7] has similar recurrence rates and considerable side effects, especially for MMC [28, 29]. Recent studies on the pathology of SCCs have shown that approximately 80–90% of SCCs are positive for EGFR overexpression [30].

However, clinical trials of EGFR-targeting therapies such as cetuximab show significant promise. Gruenberger et al. in a phase II trial concluded that a combination of gemcitabine, oxaliplatin, and cetuximab had more effective antitumor activity [31]. Cetuximab competes with natural ligands and due to higher affinity, prevents their binding to the receptor, and thus blocks the intracellular tyrosine kinase-dependent signal transduction pathway [32, 33]. Only a few studies have assessed the safety of subconjunctival cetuximab in animal models. In 2012, Tunik et al. showed the safety and efficacy of subconjunctival cetuximab in management of corneal angiogenesis in the rat model [34]. In 2023 Aghaei et al. showed the safety of subconjunctival cetuximab in a dose of 5 mg/mL in rabbits [35]. They reported transient chemosis without other significant changes. No significant conjunctival inflammation, goblet cell loss, decrease of limbal blood vessel density, or other signs of toxicity were observed in histopathologic evaluations [36].

In some studies, it has been shown that cetuximab resistance is associated with k-ras mutation [37, 38]. To address this issue, the second characteristic of the antibodies can be leveraged, which is Antibody Dependent Cell Cytotoxicity. Although previous case reports [39, 40] have shown the efficacy of systemic cetuximab, alone or combined with other agents, in patients with orbital CSCC, no study assessed its effect on OSSN. Here we hypothesized that Cetuximab is cytotoxic on CSCC which expresses EGFR with no cytotoxicity at the same doses on normal limbal stem cells (Fig. 5A-I). The absence of a clear dose-dependent response may reflect receptor saturation at relatively low concentrations of Cetuximab, a phenomenon previously observed in other epithelial tumor models. Once most EGFR molecules are bound, further increases in antibody concentration do not proportionally enhance cytotoxicity. Additionally, in vitro culture conditions and limited cell proliferation rates could reduce observable dose-dependent differences within the tested concentration range [10]. Low doses cause a sharp spike in cell death because the drug binds rapidly to vacant, high-affinity cell receptors. At higher doses, this effect flattens into a plateau because all available target receptors become fully saturated, meaning additional drug yields no extra destruction. Nowadays we know that anti-EGFR-mAb coated tumor cells can recruit cytotoxic host cells. ADCC is believed to play a vital role in cancer immunotherapy with the unconjugated antibody [11].

The CIK cells of our experiment were a heterogeneous immune cell population consisting of activated NK cells, T-cells and NKT cells (Fig. 6A). The cytotoxicity of these lymphocyte populations is at least partly depending on the NKG2D and MICA/B expression. Notably, NKG2D (Fig. 6A) induces NK cells to overcome inhibitory signals and significantly enhances the cytotoxicity of activated T-cells in targeting and lysis of CSCC independently of any interaction with TcR-I [41]. Activated expanded CD8+ cells can also lyse malignant cells through NKG2D systems, independent of the TcR-I [42, 43] demonstrating that NK, γδT- and activated expanded CD8+ T-cells can all act via the NKG2D system and independently of interactions with the TcR-HLA-I. Thus, the cytotoxic activity of CIKs (Fig. 6B) alone may be associated with NKG2D expression.

The effector-to-target ratios (10:1–50:1) were selected according to standard practice in CIK-based cytotoxicity assays [43, 44], where this range effectively demonstrates dose-dependent killing while remaining within physiologically relevant limits. The progressive increase in CIK-mediated lysis with higher ratios in our study confirms that the cytotoxic response is effector cell–dependent. We have shown that cetuximab actions via interaction with FcR enhance the susceptibility of CSCCs to the cytotoxic actions of CIK cells (Fig. 7). We conclude from this result, that the interaction between cetuximab and its receptor in CIK cell has a potential to lyse the CSCC (Fig. 8A), and thus the increased susceptibility of cetuximab-treated CSCC to CIK cells-induced cytotoxicity, may be mediated by the binding of cetuximab to FcR. Given the extensive distribution of FcR and NKG2D in activated killer cells and the antitumor significance of interactions between NKG2D and its ligands [45], it is reasonable to predict that cetuximab augments activated CIK cell immunotherapies in a shorter time (Fig. 8B). In vitro culture of LSC showed no toxicity of Cetuximab in low concentrations even after one week. Even in higher concentrations that the proliferation of CSCC was inhibited cetuximab has very low cytotoxic effect on LSCs (Fig. 5I). Despite the data ocular toxicity of cetuximab should be kept in mind especially in patients with dry eye, underlying ocular surface disease or corneal disease.

In addition to the promising anti-tumor efficacy and regenerative potential observed in this study, several clinical factors must be addressed regarding the translation of this approach into ocular surface squamous neoplasia (OSSN) management [6]. Depending on lesion dimensions, invasion depth, and prior recurrence status, localized delivery routes can be dynamically adjusted via topical eye drops, subconjunctival injections, or localized transplantation matrices. Standard-of-care medical modalities, including interferon alpha-2b and 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), are frequently limited by extended treatment cycles, distinct ocular surface toxicities (such as chemosis and severe corneal epithelial defects), or variable long-term recurrence rates [7]. In contrast, our proposed bio-targeted strategy offers an accelerated, tissue-preserving therapeutic mechanism. Furthermore, this approach presents an invaluable alternative for recurrent or unresectable OSSN, where aggressive surgical margin clearance is anatomically restricted or independently pre-disposes the patient to irreversible limbal stem cell deficiency. Consequently, this cetuximab-mediated CIK adoptive cell platform establishes a viable adjunctive or primary treatment paradigm for complex, recalcitrant ocular malignancies.

Limitation and future direction

A limitation of this study is EGFR expression analysis which was evaluated by semi-quantitative mRNA analysis, without quantification or histological localization. While findings demonstrate EGFR overexpression in CSCC relative to LSC controls, future investigations will include flow cytometry of surface EGFR, and immunohistochemical validation in tumor tissues. These analyses will provide a more comprehensive understanding of EGFR’s predictive value for therapeutic responsiveness.

Conclusion

We have shown that the use of activated cytokine-induced killer cells in combination with cetuximab has potential cytotoxic activity towards conjunctival squamous carcinoma cells in vitro. CIK cells react even with weak expression of EGFR on CSCCs and mediate efficient ADCC against CSCC cells. Finally, not only the combination of CIK cells and cetuximab can shorten the treatment time, but it can also be an alternative for tumors with mutated genes that are resistant to cetuximab alone. This combination offers a novel immunotherapy paradigm for the treatment of Ocular Carcinoma Cells.
