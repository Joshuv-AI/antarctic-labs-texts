Probiotic Bacillus subtilis QST713 improved growth performance and enhanced the intestinal health of yellow-feather broilers challenged with coccidia and Clostridium perfringens
Authors: Lu Sun, Yangbin Liu, Pan Xiao, Keying Zhang, Shiping Bai, Jianping Wang, Qiufeng Zeng, Huanwei Peng, Yadong Mu, Yue Xuan, Shanshan Li, Xuemei Ding
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, we investigated the effects of dietary supplementation with Bacillus subtilis (QST713) on the performance and intestinal health of yellow feather broilers under Coccidia and Clostridium perfringens (CP) challenge or CP alone. One-day-old yellow-feathered broiler roosters (n = 600) were randomly assigned to 5 groups (6 replicates with 20 roosters per replicate): the Con blank group, the CIC.p group (d24 Coccidia+d28-30 of CP challenge), the CIC.p + BS group (CIC.p +100 mg/kg B. subtilis), the C.p group (d 28–34 of CP challenge), and the C.p +BS group (C.p +100 mg/kg B. subtilis). The experiment lasted 80 d. The birds were evaluated for parameters such as average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), feed efficiency (F/G), intestinal lesion score, villus histomorphometry, intestinal tight junctions, inflammatory factors, and cecal microorganisms. The results revealed that 1) C.p. increased the F/G of broilers from 22 to 42 d (P < 0.05), whereas CIC.p. significantly decreased the 42 d and 80 d body weights (BW) and 22–42 d and 1–80 d ADG (P < 0.05) and significantly increased the 22 to 42 d and 1 to 80 d F/G (P < 0.05). The number of intestinal lesions significantly increased at 35 d and 42 d (P < 0.05). CIC.p significantly decreased the jejunum and ileum villus height (VH) and the ileum villus height/crypt depth (P < 0.05) at 35 d. The challenge significantly upregulated the expression of Claudin-1 and IL-4 mRNAs in the jejunum at 35 d and significantly downregulated the expression of IL-10 mRNA in the ileum at 35 d (P < 0.05); the number of unique OTUs in the challenge group decreased significantly after challenge treatment, and the relative abundances of Romboutsia at 35 d and Cladomyces and Lactobacillus at 42 d decreased significantly (P < 0.05). 2) Compared with the challenge groups, the addition of BS decreased the F/G of broilers from 22 to 42 d. Compared with the CIC group, the addition of BS significantly increased the F/G of broilers from 22 to 42 d. Compared with that in the CIC.p group, the addition of BS significantly increased the VH in the jejunum and ileum at 35 d (P < 0.05). Compared with the challenge groups, the BS groups presented significantly lower mRNA expression levels of Claudin-1 (P < 0.05) in the jejunum at 35 d. The Shannon and Chao indices suggested that BS increased the alpha diversity of cecum microorganisms in broilers. Dietary supplementation with B. subtilis can alleviate the damage to intestinal morphology and intestinal barrier function, as well as the altered cecal flora structure in broilers caused by Coccidia and C. perfringens infections.Key words: Clostridium perfringens, coccidia, Bacillus subtilis, intestinal health, yellow-colored broiler

INTRODUCTION

Necrotizing enteritis is a common intestinal disease in poultry caused by Clostridium perfringens. It usually develops in 2– to 6-wk-old broilers and adversely affects their performance and feed conversion rate (Abd El-Ghany et al., 2022). Several studies have shown that nonstarch polysaccharides, coccidia, and animal proteins can cause necrotizing enteritis in chickens. Chicken coccidiosis is a concomitant disease with necrotizing enterocolitis in chickens. Coccidia infection destroys the integrity of the intestinal epithelium, causes leakage of host plasma proteins into the lumen of the intestine to increase the permeability of the intestinal mucosa (Blake and Tomley, 2014), and provides nutrients for C. perfringens to promote bacterial growth and toxin secretion (Collier et al., 2008; Van Immerseel et al., 2009).

Probiotics can improve intestinal barrier function, regulate immunity, improve the quality of animal products, and have nutritional effects. Bacillus subtilis (BS) is a commercially available bacillus commonly used in poultry production. It can inhibit intestinal colonization by pathogenic bacteria such as E. coli (Wu et al., 2009), Salmonella (Park and Kim, 2014), and Clostridium perfringens (Wang et al., 2023). This inhibitory effect may originate from the probiotic effects of BS, such as the production of enzymes that contribute to digestion and absorption (Chen et al., 2005), competition for exclusion (Barbosa, et al., 2005), alteration of intercellular signaling among pathogenic bacteria (Hughes and Sperandio, 2008), activation of innate immune cells (Rhee et al., 2004), and enhancement of immune regulation and host response and metabolite production (Wu et al., 2011). BS can improve the production performance of broiler chickens (Keerqin et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021a), reduce the number of C. perfringens in the ileum and cecum (Whelan et al., 2019), and achieve anticoccidial effects by modulating the levels of Romboutsia, Blautia, and Butyricicoccus spp. in the intestinal tract of broilers (Cai et al., 2022).

Researchers have mostly investigated necrotizing enteritis in fast-type white-feathered broilers but have paid less attention to enteritis in yellow-feathered broilers. Few studies have evaluated the performance and intestinal health of yellow-feathered broilers affected by Coccidia and C. perfringens together or C. perfringens alone, and further assessment is needed to determine whether Bacillus cereus is effective in treating yellow-feathered broilers infected with Coccidia and C. perfringens together or C. perfringens alone. We hypothesized that B. subtilis can improve the performance of broiler chickens challenged with C. perfringens (C. P) or coccidia plus C. perfringens (CIC. P) by improving intestinal integrity (jejunal morphology, intestinal permeability, and expression of TJ-encoding genes) and by modulating intestinal inflammation (the expression of innate immune-related genes). Therefore, this study was conducted under simulated production conditions to investigate the effects of B. subtilis (QST713) on the production performance and intestinal health of yellow-feathered broilers affected by coccidia and C. perfringens together or C. perfringens alone.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials, Design, and Feeding Management

The experimental protocols used in the present study were approved by the Animal Care and Use committee of Sichuan Agricultural University, China. Bacillus subtilis (QST713) ≥ 1.0 × 1010 CFU/g was obtained from Elanco Animal Health Co., Ltd. Clostridium perfringens type A was isolated and preserved by the School of Animal Medicine, Sichuan Agricultural University. The chicken coccidiosis tetravalent vaccine was purchased from Foshan Zhengdian Biotech Co., Ltd. (Foshan City, Guangdong Province), and yellow-feathered broiler chickens were purchased from Sichuan Jumbo Star Agricultural & Animal Husbandry Co. A 2 × 2 + 1 completely randomized experimental design was used, and the trial was divided into 2 types of challenge: 2 doses of the B. subtilis additive and a normal control group. In total, 600 one-day-old yellow-feathered broiler roosters were randomly divided into 5 treatment groups with 6 replicates of 20 chickens each. The treatment groups were as follows: 1) the control group (Con, fed a basal diet), 2) the CIC.p group (10-fold anticoccidial vaccine with 11,000 coccidia/mL/bird + 28–30 d 1 mL 3 × 108 CFU/mL/bird C. perfringens challenge on d24), the CIC.p + BS group (CIC.p + B. subtilis), the C.p group (28–34 d 1 mL 3 × 108 CFU/mL/bird C. perfringens challenge), and the C.p +BS group (C.p +B. subtilis). B. subtilis (QST713, ≥1.0 × 1010 CFU/g, 100 mg/kg) was added to the basal ration based on different challenge methods. The experimental groups are shown in Table 1. A corn-soybean-type diet was administered as the basal diet, where the nutrient levels and composition were determined following a method described in another study (Council, 1994). Three feed formulations were used for the following distinct phases: starter (d 1–21), grower (d 22–42), and finisher (d 43–80). The formulation of the basal diets for the experiment is shown in Table 2.

The experiment was performed in a broiler flat-feeding circle at the teaching and research base of the Institute of Animal Nutrition, Sichuan Agricultural University. Throughout the study, the birds had ad libitum access to the feed, and standard hygienic conditions were maintained. Prophylactic vaccination was performed. The temperature was controlled at 30 to 32°C for 1 to 3 d, 29 to 31°C for 4 to 7 d, and then decreased by 2°C every week. The room temperature was maintained at 22 to 24°C for 28 d, and the birds were exposed to light for 24 h every day. The chickens were observed daily (with or without abnormalities), and the number of dead and eliminated chickens and the total amount of feed added by the unit of repetition were recorded.

Growth Performance Measurement and Sample Collection

When the age was 22, 43, or 81 d, the feed was cut off and fasted at noon, and the replicates were weighed at 8:00 a.m. The total body weight (BW) of each replicate was recorded, and the feed consumption of each replicate from 1 to 21, 22 to 42, or 43 to 80 d was also recorded accurately to calculate the average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), feed-to-weight ratio (F/G) and die-off rate. The F/G ratio was calculated as the feed intake of chickens at stage (kg)/(final weight of chickens at stage [kg] + weight of dead culled chickens [kg]—initial weight of chickens at stage [kg]).

Macroscopic Lesion Scoring of the Small Intestine

Lesions in the small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, and ileum) were scored as described previously (Dahiya et al., 2005) as follows: 0 = no lesion; 1 = thin-walled and friable intestines with small red petechiae (>5); 2 = focal necrotic lesions; 3 = patches of necrosis (1–2 cm long); and 4 = diffuse necrosis typical of field cases.

Intestinal Morphology Analysis

The animals were fasted at 12 p.m. at 35 and 42 d of age, and 1 chicken was selected from each replicate with a mean weight of 100 g and above and in good health condition for slaughtering. Midsections of the jejunum and ileum were taken after separation of the intestinal tract, and the samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde to determine intestinal histology. After fixation, the samples were sent to Sichuan Yankster Technology Co., Ltd., for block embedding, paraffin sectioning, and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. The samples were then photographed using a microimaging system. The intestinal villus height (VH) and 10-crypt depth (CD) of the samples were measured using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software. The mean values were taken to represent the observed values for the VH, CD, and CD of the observed values and to calculate the VH/CD.

Jejunal and Ileum Mucosa-Related mRNA Expression by Real-Time PCR

The jejunum and ileum were removed from the middle and posterior parts of the jejunum and ileum, dissected with a pair of sterilized scissors, and washed with saline. The mucosa was then gently scraped with a rag in a cryopreservation tube, stored in liquid nitrogen, and preserved at –80°C at the end of the sampling period. Total RNA was extracted from the jejunum and ileum by grinding the samples in liquid nitrogen and submerging them in TRIzol reagent. The extraction was performed following the methods provided with the RNAiso kit (Takara, Dalian, China). The extracted RNA was stored on ice for subsequent steps, and the concentration and purity of the extracted total RNA were determined via a NanoDrop 2000 system (Wilmington, DE) to measure the ratio of the OD values of the samples at 260 nm and 280 nm (OD260/280 in the range of 1.9–2.1). On the basis of the measured RNA concentration, DEPC water was used to dilute the RNA to the same appropriate concentration to facilitate the addition of equal amounts of RNA as required by the subsequent reaction system. cDNA was synthesized via reverse transcription using the PrimeScript RT Kit (Takara, Dalian, China), and the products obtained were stored at –20°C for later use. To conduct RT-PCR analysis, the sequences of the target genes in this study were obtained from the NCBI query and tested for primer specificity (physical barrier-related genes: OCLN, CLDN-1, CLDN-2, ZO-1, and MUC-2; immune-related genes: IL-4 and IL-10), and the primers were designed by Sangon Biotech. The primer sequences are shown in Table 3. The primers were used with a real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument (CFX-96, Real-Time PCR System, BIO-RND). The operation process was conducted according to the instructions of the SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Takara, Dalian, China) kit, and a 10 μL reaction amplification system was used, with 3 parallel samples. β-actin was selected as the internal reference gene, and the gene expression results were analyzed via the 2-ΔΔCT method (Li et al., 2024).

Gut Microbiota Analysis

The microbial profile of the cecal digesta (n = 6) was evaluated by sequencing and clustering the 16S rRNA gene via high-throughput pyrosequencing, and sequencing and bioinformatics analysis were performed by Beijing Nuohe Bioinformatics Technology Company. The methods used are described in another study (Yang, et al., 2020).

Statistical Analysis

The data were analyzed via 2-way ANOVA using the GLM procedures of SAS. The factors used were the B. subtilis dose and challenge method. In the multiple comparisons with Duncan's method, P ≤ 0.05 represented a significant difference between groups. The data from the control group were compared with those from the other groups, and all the data were analyzed by 1-way ANOVA. A P-value ≤ 0.05 represented a significant difference between groups. Beta diversity based on the weighted UniFrac distance matrices was calculated with QIIME (version 1.7.0), and cluster analysis was preceded by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA).

RESULTS

Performance

Compared with those in the control group, 42 d and 80 d BW, 22 to 42 d and 1 to 80 d ADG were significantly lower, and 22 to 42 d and 1 to 80 d F/G were significantly greater in the CIC.p group, whereas 22 to 42 d F/G was significantly greater and the 1–80 d die-off rate was significantly lower in the C.p+BS group (P < 0.05) (Table 4). The CI C.p or C.p together and the BS dosage interaction significantly affected the 22 to 42 d F/G ratio (P < 0.05), with no significant effect (P > 0.05) on the performance of other products (BW, ADG, and ADFI). Compared with F/G, CI combined with C.p tapping significantly increased F/G values at 43 to 80 d and 1 to 80 d (P < 0.05) in the C.p group. Compared with those of the control group, the dietary addition of BS significantly increased the 80 d BW, 43 to 80 d and 1 to 80 d ADG, and 43–80 d ADFI of yellow-feathered broilers (P < 0.05); the BW, ADG, and ADFI levels of the yellow-feathered broilers recovered to the same level as those of the control group.

Intestinal Morphology

As shown in Table 6 and Figure 1, compared with those in the control group, 35 d jejunal and ileal VH and 35 d VH/CD levels were significantly lower in the CIC.p combined tapping group; 35 d jejunal VH and 42 d jejunal CD levels were significantly greater; and 35 d VH/CD levels were significantly lower in the CIC.p + BS group. Additionally, 35 d ileal VH and CD were significantly greater in the C.P tapping group. The 35 d CD was significantly greater in the C.p + BS group (P < 0.05). The interaction of CIC.p or C.p tapping and BS dose significantly affected the jejunal VH and VH/CD at 35 d and the ileal VH at 42 d (P < 0.05). The addition of BS significantly increased the jejunal VH and CD at 35 d compared with those of the group without BS (P < 0.05). The 35 d ileal VH, CD, and VH/CD, as well as the 42 d ileal VH/CD, were significantly lower in the CIC.p combined challenge group than in the single C.p challenge group (P < 0.05).

DISCUSSION

Both coccidia and C. perfringens can cause damage and harm to chickens to some extent, and in this experiment we used 2 types of challenge to simulate the production of subclinical necrotizing enterocolitis challenge (SNE).Coccidia and C. perfringens infections adversely affected broiler performance, decreasing feed intake, body weight gain, and feed conversion ratio during challenge(Li, et al., 2022).Daily instillation of 1 ml of 1.0 × 108 CFU/ml Clostridium perfringens at 16 to 20 d of age reduces late body weight gain and feed conversion, leading to a decline in full-term growth performance(Tang, et al., 2022).The beneficial effects of probiotic additives based on B. subtilis on broiler performance were investigated. Adding different levels of BS (LS1-2) to rations significantly increased the performance and nutrient utilization of broiler chicks (Sen, et al., 2012). In addition to its ability to act as a growth promoter in the diet, B. subtilis can replace antibiotic additives in the diet. Chickens (21 days old) infected with 1-year-old 104 giant Eimeria cepacia oocysts presented low body weight gain, feed intake, and feed conversion ratios from 21 to 28 d, whereas these parameters recovered to the same level as those of the antibiotic-supplemented group from 21 to 28 d after the addition of 1.5 × 105 CFU/g B. subtilis to the rations (Park, et al., 2020). The addition of B. subtilis PB6 alleviated the reduction in production performance due to coccidia and NE challenge (Aljumaah, et al., 2020). In this study, the addition of B. subtilis QST713 improved the production performance of yellow-feathered broilers in the CIC.p group, whereas the improvement in the production performance of the C.p group was not significant, which matches the findings of previous studies. The differences in the reported effects of B. subtilis on the production performance of challenged broilers may be influenced by factors such as the source of B. subtilis and the method of extraction and preparation, compatibility with other dietary ingredients, breed of chickens, intestinal damage, and feeding conditions (Abd El-Hack et al., 2020).

The intestinal tract is the main location for the digestion and absorption of nutrients in livestock and poultry and functions as a natural barrier to resist damage caused by exogenous pathogenic bacteria and harmful substances. The intestinal lining consists of microvilli, villi, and folds. The intestinal morphology is one of the most important indicators for assessing intestinal function and health(He et al., 2021). Bacillus subtilis can regulate the microecological balance in the gastrointestinal tract, change the intestinal flora, promote intestinal secretion of digestive enzymes, improve intestinal morphology, and promote the digestion and absorption of nutrients (Hadieva et al., 2021). The effect of B. subtilis cannot be separated from the healthy development of the intestinal tract; the VH/CD value of the NE-infected broiler jejunum was lower, and the degree of damage to the small intestine was greater. After dietary supplementation with Bacillus coagulans, regardless of the NE challenge, the morphology and structure of the jejunum were relatively normal (Wu et al., 2018). Coccidia challenge negatively affects the morphology of the small intestine in the early stages in Ross 708 male broilers, and supplementation with B. subtilis cannot reverse the damage caused by coccidia (Poudel et al., 2021). The data of this study revealed that jejunal VH was significantly reduced in the 35 d poisoning group and that VH was significantly increased in the CIC.p group after the addition of B. subtilis, whereas no significant change was recorded in the C.p group. The reason for the lack of significant change in jejunal VH in the C.p group may be that the subsequent C. perfringens challenge continued to cause damage, affecting the repair of the jejunum. The 35 d ileal VH and VH/CD values and 42 d VH/CD values were significantly lower in the CIC.p group than in the CIC.p group, and the VH and VH/CD values in the CIC.p group were restored after B. subtilis was added, which matched the findings of previous studies. In the C.p group, no significant change in ileal morphology occurred, possibly because coccidian infection destroyed the structure of the ileum. Clostridium perfringens passes through the jejunum by gavage, resulting in a decrease in bacterial number and virulence; however, C. perfringens alone cannot cause damage.

A good mechanical barrier prevents the penetration of harmful substances, such as bacteria, into the intestinal mucosa (Tian et al., 2018). Tight junction proteins, such as ZO-1, CLDN-1, CLDN-2, and MUC-2, which are crucial for maintaining intestinal integrity and barrier function, are important components of the intestinal mucosal barrier. The expression of intestinal tight junction proteins is influenced by individual protein expression or the cause of intestinal injury (Awad et al., 2017). Intravitreal injection of B. subtilis and cotton candy significantly increased the relative expression of MUC-2 in the intestine of broilers (Shehata et al., 2022). Wang reported that adding B. subtilis DSM29784 significantly decreased the relative expression of MUC-2 compared with that in the SNE group and that a decrease in mucus secretion may reduce the localization of C. perfringens, thus providing a preventive effect against SNE (Wang et al., 2021b). The findings of this study revealed that adding B. subtilis improved the integrity of the intestinal mechanical barrier in yellow-colored broilers in the SNE state, as determined by an increase in the relative expression of MUC-2. The increase in the relative expression of Claudin-1 and ZO-1 in the jejunum at 35 d in the tapping group was inconsistent with the results of previous studies, possibly because, in the late stage of infection, the organism performs self-repair, and the expression of genes related to physical barriers increases to repair the intestinal tract after it experiences considerable damage and maintains the structural and functional integrity of the intestine. This may also be related to the dose of the tapping agent, the breed of the broilers, the type of diet, etc.

Interleukin 4 (IL-4) is involved mainly in the inflammatory response, cell proliferation, repair, apoptosis, and other processes. This cytokine causes immune dysfunction by reducing the immune response mediated by helper T-lymphocyte 1 (Th1) cells. The IL-10 gene acts as a central immune molecule in the immune system and plays a bidirectional regulatory role (Yu et al., 2017). Studies have shown that supplementation of broiler diets with B. subtilis can alleviate the inflammatory response caused by heat stress, MG, and Salmonella pumilus, and the levels of the proinflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β are significantly reduced (Wang et al., 2022). Adding B. subtilis to broiler diets increases plasma interleukin (IL)–2 and IL-4 levels (Qiu et al., 2023). The inflammatory response in Salmonella-infected broiler chickens leads to a significant decrease in the gene expression of IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ (Fazelnia et al., 2021). Bacillus tardus and B. subtilis can alleviate the effects of murine typhoid fever on broilers infected with S. pumilus. The results of this study revealed that challenge groups increased the relative expression of IL-4 in the jejunum at 35 d and increased the relative expression of IL-10 in the ileum at 42 d. The addition of B. subtilis alleviated the upregulation of IL-4 and IL-10 induced by the tapping of C. perfringens, but no significant difference was found in the combined tapping of Coccidioides immitis and C. perfringens, which might be related to the degree of intestinal damage, the breed of the chickens, the rearing conditions, and other factors.

Trillions of microorganisms parasitize the poultry gut and play important roles in digesting food and absorbing nutrients from the host diet, regulating host fat stores, stimulating intestinal epithelial renewal, and guiding the maturation of the immune system (Zhang et al., 2013). In the context of the feed ban, therapeutic or prophylactic use of antibiotics can disturb the normal microbial balance of the host (Simoneit et al., 2015). An imbalance of the intestinal flora often leads to disease. Clostridium perfringens infection can decrease the diversity of microflora in the cecum of broiler chickens (Stanley et al., 2014). In this study, the number of unique cecal microbial OTUs was significantly lower in the challenge group than in the control group, and the number of cecal microbial OTUs could be appropriately restored by B. subtilis, which matches the results of previous studies. According to the alpha diversity analysis, the addition of B. subtilis increased the Shannon index of broiler chickens in the challenge group, but the Shannon index increased significantly in the 42 d challenge group. A greater value of the Shannon index reflects greater diversity of microbial community species. In this study, infection caused by C. perfringens and Coccidioides immitis increased the diversity of microflora in the cecum of 42-day-old broilers, possibly because the colonization of the cecum by pathogenic bacteria after infection disrupts the normal microecological balance of the cecum and creates favorable conditions for the adhesion and colonization of other pathogenic bacteria.

The intestinal microorganisms are closely related to the health of the broiler intestine (Rubio, 2019).The phylum Firmicutes is the dominant phylum in the broiler intestine (Mohd Shaufi et al., 2015). Bacteroides is considered as a probiotic with the ability of degrading a variety of complex carbohydrates (Wang et al., 2024). Bacillus subtilis probiotic supplementation enriched specific gut microbiota including Streptococcus, Butyricicoccus, Faecalibacterium, and Ruminococcus. These microbiotas were found to upregulate expression of various genes such as TJ proteins occluding, ZO-1, junctional adhesion 2 (JAM2), interferon gamma, IL12-β and transforming growth factor-β4. Moreover, downregulated mucin-2 expression was involved in restoring the intestinal physical barrier, reducing intestinal inflammation, and recovering the physiological functions of damaged intestines (Ghimire et al., 2024).The relative abundance of Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes decreased, as did the relative abundance of Proteobacteria in the CIC.P group increased at 35 d, whereas B. subtilis intervention decreased the relative abundance of Proteobacteria in the cecum of broilers in the CIC. P group. Coccidioides immitis, and C. perfringens infections reduced the proportion of Firmicutes and increased the abundance of microorganisms at the level of the other phyla, thus increasing species diversity. This occurred because Proteobacteria includes many pathogenic bacteria in the bacterial classification, such as Escherichia coli, Helicobacter pylori, Vibrio cholerae, Salmonella, and other species, which are pathogenic bacteria that can easily colonize the intestines of animals to cause intestinal diseases (Cuesta et al., 2022; Waite et al., 2017). Pseudomonas spp. constitute one of the major intestinal flora, and the cecal flora composition of the 42 d challenge group was similar to that of the B. subtilis intervention group. The 35 d CIC.p group presented a significantly greater relative abundance of Pseudomonas spp. in the cecum of broiler chickens and a significantly lower relative abundance of Romboutsia. The C. perfringens infection group presented a significantly greater relative abundance of Faecalibacterium and Alistipes and a significantly lower relative abundance of Romboutsia; this may directly cause the development of intestinal diseases in broilers. The reason for the increase in the abundance of other pathogens caused by CIC.p or C.p infections may be that the pathogens disrupted the original microecological balance in the broiler intestinal tract, increasing susceptibility to adherence and colonization by other pathogens. At 42 d, the relative abundances of C. vitellogenicus-like bacilli species in the cecum and [Ruminococcus]_torques_group were significantly greater in the CIC.p group. The relative abundances of the other branching bacillus genera and Lactobacillus spp. in the challenge group were significantly lower than those in the control group. Bacillus subtilis alleviated intestinal flora disorders caused by C. immitis or C. perfringens and increased the diversity of beneficial intestinal flora.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, coinfection with coccidia and C. perfringens resulted in more severe intestinal damage in yellow-feathered broilers than infection with C. perfringens alone. Dietary supplementation with B. subtilis (QST713, 1.0 × 1010 CFU/g) alleviated intestinal morphology and intestinal barrier function impairment and altered the intestinal microbiological structure in broilers due to coccidia and C. perfringens infections.
