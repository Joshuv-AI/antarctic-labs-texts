Influenza A in the Perioperative Setting of Liver Transplantation
Authors: Dimitrios K Vlachos, Dionysios Prevezanos, Michael K Konstantinidis, Anna Paspala, Nikolaos Machairas, Georgios C Sotiropoulos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Influenza A infection is associated with significant morbidity in solid organ transplant recipients, particularly in immunosuppressed patients. In liver transplant candidates, diagnosis of respiratory viral infections remains challenging, especially in urgent settings where preoperative screening may have limited sensitivity. We report the case of a 30-year-old woman with unresectable liver metastases from a neuroendocrine tumor who underwent liver transplantation despite mild upper respiratory symptoms and negative preoperative nasopharyngeal PCR testing. In the immediate postoperative period, she developed respiratory deterioration, and PCR testing of bronchial secretions was positive for influenza A. Oseltamivir therapy was initiated, together with adjustments in immunosuppressive treatment. The patient demonstrated rapid clinical improvement, was extubated on postoperative day 1, and was discharged on postoperative day 12. A review of previously reported perioperative influenza cases in liver transplantation highlights the rarity of this condition and the variability of clinical presentation, ranging from mild disease to severe respiratory failure. Diagnostic limitations, including false-negative upper respiratory tract testing and discordance between upper and lower respiratory tract samples, may complicate perioperative decision-making. This case highlights the limitations of current preoperative screening strategies and emphasizes the importance of integrating clinical suspicion with laboratory findings in urgent transplant settings. Early antiviral treatment appears critical for favorable outcomes.

Introduction

Influenza A infection represents a clinically significant cause of morbidity in solid organ transplant recipients, in whom immunosuppression may predispose to more severe disease, prolonged viral replication, and increased risk of complications [1]. In liver transplant recipients, influenza has been associated with a spectrum of clinical manifestations, ranging from mild upper respiratory illness to severe pneumonia [2].

A major challenge in this setting is the diagnosis of respiratory viral infections in transplant candidates. Diagnostic strategies for respiratory viral infections have important limitations, which may compromise their reliability in urgent transplant settings [3]. These limitations are particularly relevant in patients presenting with mild or nonspecific symptoms, where negative preoperative testing does not reliably exclude infection and may complicate time-sensitive decisions regarding transplantation.

We aimed to evaluate the diagnostic challenges and perioperative implications of influenza A infection in liver transplantation by presenting a representative clinical case together with a focused review of the available literature.

Case presentation

A 30-year-old female with a known history of a grade 2 neuroendocrine tumor of the small intestine, previously treated with enterectomy, developed unresectable liver metastases and was listed for liver transplantation.

On the day of transplantation, the patient reported mild upper respiratory symptoms, including rhinorrhea, sore throat, and a mild cough, without fever or laboratory evidence of inflammation. Preoperative nasopharyngeal PCR testing for respiratory viruses was negative. Given her stable clinical condition and the oncologic urgency, the decision was made to proceed with liver transplantation.

The transplant procedure was uneventful. However, immediately postoperatively, while still intubated, the patient developed respiratory deterioration with desaturation and underwent urgent computed tomography of the chest, which demonstrated pulmonary edema. Diuretic therapy was initiated, and the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit for further management.

Due to persistent respiratory concerns, PCR testing of bronchial secretions was performed and returned positive for influenza A. Antiviral therapy with oseltamivir (75 mg twice daily) was initiated promptly.

Immunosuppressive therapy consisted of tacrolimus (2 mg twice daily) and corticosteroids, with a modified steroid taper (500 mg on day 1, followed by 250 mg, 125 mg, and 100 mg), adjusted in light of the viral infection. Mycophenolate mofetil was not administered due to the oncologic indication for transplantation.

The patient demonstrated rapid respiratory improvement and was successfully extubated on postoperative day 1. On postoperative day 3, she developed a large right-sided pleural effusion, which was managed with drainage (Figure 1). Her subsequent postoperative course was otherwise uneventful, and she was discharged on postoperative day 12. Antiviral therapy was continued for a total of seven days.

Discussion

A synthesis of all previously reported cases of influenza in the perioperative setting of liver transplantation (Table 1) identified a total of three cases in the literature. These cases span the entire perioperative continuum, including pre-transplant, intraoperative, and early postoperative presentations, indicating that influenza infection may be encountered at any stage of the transplant process. The clinical course appears highly variable, ranging from mild disease with favorable outcomes to severe respiratory failure requiring prolonged mechanical ventilation, underscoring the difficulty in predicting disease severity in this population.

Notably, all previously reported patients had favorable outcomes, including those with severe disease, although this observation should be interpreted with caution given the potential for publication bias and underreporting of unfavorable cases. Management strategies varied considerably across reported cases, particularly with regard to adjustments in immunosuppression, reflecting the absence of a standardized approach in this clinical setting. In parallel, antiviral therapy with oseltamivir was consistently administered, although treatment duration varied from short courses of approximately five days to more prolonged regimens exceeding 10 days.

In contrast to previously reported cases, in which influenza infection was either confirmed prior to transplantation or recognized during the perioperative period, our case uniquely describes a symptomatic patient with negative preoperative testing, with diagnosis established only postoperatively. This finding highlights the limitations of current diagnostic strategies, particularly in the early phase of infection, where the reduced sensitivity of upper respiratory tract sampling may fail to detect active disease. In our case, discordant results between nasopharyngeal and bronchial samples suggest that lower respiratory tract specimens may offer higher diagnostic yield in selected patients [5].

This diagnostic uncertainty has direct implications for perioperative decision-making. While active infection has traditionally been considered a contraindication to transplantation, emerging evidence suggests that selected patients with mild influenza may safely undergo transplantation when appropriate antiviral therapy is promptly initiated [6]. Early initiation of antiviral therapy - and even empirical treatment in patients with suspected infection - has been associated with improved outcomes, whereas delays in treatment have been linked to increased morbidity and mortality [7,8].

Beyond respiratory manifestations, influenza infection may also have implications for graft function and rejection through activation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and immune pathways [9]. However, current evidence regarding the impact of respiratory viral infections on graft outcomes in liver transplant recipients remains limited and inconsistent [10].

Conclusions

This case highlights the limitations of preoperative respiratory viral screening in urgent liver transplantation, particularly in symptomatic patients with negative nasopharyngeal testing. Integrating clinical suspicion with laboratory findings may be essential for appropriate perioperative decision-making, while prompt antiviral therapy appears critical for favorable outcomes. Although conclusions from a single case should be interpreted with caution, our findings underscore the need for further studies to better define the optimal diagnostic and management strategies in this challenging clinical setting.
