Cardiac Rehabilitation in Advanced Dilated Cardiomyopathy Within a Resource-Limited Setting
Authors: Karol G Hernández-Gutiérrez, Adolfo Calderón-Fernández, Heydi C Suarez-Jimenez, Avril S Mendoza-Gonzalez, Elva A Manjarrez-Granados, David F Cuevas-Cueto, Fernando Bátiz-Armenta, Aida R Márquez-Valdez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a major cause of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction. Cardiac rehabilitation (CR) has been shown to improve functional capacity, but its use in advanced DCM remains limited.Case SummaryA 57-year-old man with idiopathic DCM and severe systolic dysfunction (left ventricular ejection fraction = 5%) presented with acute heart failure. Cardiac magnetic resonance revealed inferoseptal fibrosis and biventricular dilation. He was treated with optimal pharmacologic therapy and an 8-week supervised CR program. The 6-minute walk test improved by 114% (280-600 m), and heart rate recovery increased from 13 to 27 beats/min.DiscussionThis case highlights the positive impact of CR in advanced DCM, showing significant functional and metabolic improvements. Integrating CR into standard care is crucial for improving patient outcomes.Take-Home MessageCR is safe and effective for patients with advanced DCM, which enhances prognosis and quality of life.Key Words: cardiac rehabilitation, dilated cardiomyopathy, exercise therapy, FITT principle, heart failure

Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a prevalent type of cardiomyopathy defined by left ventricular enlargement and impaired systolic function, either globally or regionally, that cannot be attributed solely to abnormal hemodynamic conditions such as hypertension, valvular heart disease, or coronary artery disease.1,2 An idiopathic DCM diagnosis is established when the etiology remains undetermined despite comprehensive evaluative efforts. DCM is one of the primary etiologies of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction and is linked to a heightened risk of sudden cardiac death.3,4 The prognosis is generally unfavorable under the following conditions: 1) left ventricular function is severely diminished, indicated by a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) under 25%; 2) presence of left ventricular dilation; 3) evidence of conduction abnormalities and arrhythmias; and/or 4) a reduced maximal oxygen uptake (Vo2max).5 Cardiac rehabilitation (CR) programs are established interventions that enhance patient outcomes by improving quality of life, increasing exercise capacity, and facilitating better performance in activities of daily living.4,6 Limited research has focused on CR specifically for patients with advanced DCM. We present a case demonstrating successful rehabilitation outcomes in a patient with severe DCM.

History of Presentation

A 57-year-old man presented to the outpatient clinic with respiratory symptoms. He had no significant history of hereditary-familial or degenerative chronic diseases.

Past Medical History

However, his medical history included gastroduodenitis and gastric ulcers classified as Forrest type IIc and III, as well as evidence of carbohydrate intolerance on screening studies. His cardiovascular risk, as assessed by the atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease scale, was moderate to high.

Differential Diagnosis

The patient had a history of bronchial hyperreactivity and a positive smoking history of 7 pack-years. He was diagnosed with anterior segment pneumonia in both the right upper lobe and left upper lobe, for which pharmacologic treatment was initiated. He subsequently returned to the emergency department with clinical signs of acute heart failure and progressive dyspnea, prompting hospital admission.

Investigations

Laboratory testing was performed (Table 1). Notable findings included an elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) level at 4.31 mg/L and an elevated N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) level at 1,510 pg/mL. Transthoracic echocardiography was conducted using 2D imaging and Doppler imaging, showing a 5% reduced LVEF calculated by the biplane Simpson's method. The left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) was 228 mL, and the left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV) was 217 mL, indicative of severe dilation and minimal contractile reserve. The left atrium was also markedly dilated, with a left atrial volume index of 63 mL/m2.

Right ventricular measurements showed a basal diameter of 48 mm, consistent with moderate right ventricular dilatation. Mild mitral and tricuspid regurgitation signals were noted by color Doppler evaluation, with no significant valvular stenosis. Pulmonary artery systolic pressure was estimated at 50 mm Hg. A mobile apical thrombus was visualized within the left ventricle. Cardiac magnetic resonance was performed, which demonstrated findings consistent with DCM (Figures 1 and 2). Cine sequences confirmed biventricular dilatation and severely reduced biventricular systolic function. The LVEF was 15.2% with the LVEDV and LVESV measured at 267 mL and 226 mL, respectively. The right ventricular ejection fraction was 12.6%, confirming the diagnosis of heart failure and indicating a high arrhythmogenic risk. Focal inferoseptal fibrosis was evident in the mid-myocardial layer on late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) sequences. No significant elevation in native T1 values was reported; therefore, fibrosis was defined based on LGE presence.

Coronary computed tomography angiography was also performed during the same month. It revealed a coronary calcium score of 0 AU. Nonobstructive coronary atherosclerosis was noted in the left main trunk, the proximal third of the left anterior descending artery, and the middle third of the right coronary artery.

Management

The patient was referred to the rehabilitation department, and the process of cardiac therapy was started. The patient had medically supervised 30-minute aerobic training sessions on the ergometer for a course of 8 weeks. The patient started at 50 W and 100 beats/min, increasing with weekly increments of 20 W until the patient peaked at 150 W and 110 beats/min. As per the international recommendations, the CR program was implemented as a multidisciplinary approach where the patient received 2 sessions of dietary recommendations throughout the program. One was delivered at the beginning of the program, where biometrics were registered, a dietary assessment was performed, and corresponding recommendations were given. At the end of the program, there was a second dietary session where long-term recommendations in regard to the Mediterranean diet and cardiovascular benefits were given. Pharmacologic treatment for heart failure was initiated on a once-daily basis (Table 2). The patient received sacubitril/valsartan 50 mg twice a day, empagliflozin 10 mg every day, bisoprolol 5 mg every day, furosemide 4 mg every day, and apixaban 5 mg twice a day.

According to the FITT (frequency, intensity, time, and type) principle, exercise sessions were conducted on a basis of 2 sessions per week, for a total of 24 sessions (3 months total), each of them consisting of 30 minutes of aerobic training on a recumbent bike (Schwinn 230). Intensity was determined according to heart rate and watts, based on the initial exercise test (GXT), and progressed according to the patient's variables such as Borg’s scale (6-7/10), talk test, and target heart rate range. The second half of the exercise sessions consisted of resistance training, which was prescribed initially at 60% to 70% of 1RepMax (1RM), and also progressed according to the patient's exercise perception and biweekly assessments of 1RM. Resistance training also included exercises intended for balance, proprioception, coordination, and mobility. This was with the intention of maximizing the benefit out of the resistance training exercises.

Outcome and Follow-Up

A correlation was established between the distance and heart rate covered during the physical tests conducted at the beginning and end of the CR program. The final results showed a significant improvement, with a 114% increase in the distance walked during the 6-minute walk test, from 280 m (35% of the expected value) to 600 m (82% of the anticipated value). In addition, metabolic equivalents on the stress test increased from 7.0 to 7.8, and cardiac recovery markedly improved, with heart rate recovery rising from 13 to 27 beats in the first minute (Table 3).

Discussion

CR is characterized as a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach that encompasses structured exercise training, modification of cardiac risk factors, psychosocial assessment, and evaluation of clinical outcomes.6 Mehani7 demonstrated a significant correlation between peak Vo2 and Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire scores, indicating that exercise capacity may positively influence the quality of life in patients diagnosed with DCM. Despite the documented benefits in patient outcomes, evidence supporting cost-effectiveness, and robust recommendations from clinical practice guidelines, CR continues to be underused in many patient populations.4,6,8

This case underscores the crucial importance of a structured CR program in the management of advanced DCM, a condition marked by severe systolic dysfunction and considerable functional limitations. Despite the complexities of the patient's clinical situation (including symptoms of heart failure and pulmonary edema), implementing a personalized CR program, in conjunction with optimized pharmacologic therapy, led to significant improvements in functional, metabolic, and hemodynamic outcomes.

Using the FITT principle allowed for a progressive and customized approach to exercise. This strategy led to significant improvements in aerobic capacity, evidenced by a 114% increase in the 6-minute walk test distance, and in recovery, with heart rate recovery improving from <13 to 27 beats in the first minute postexercise. These results align with the existing literature that highlights the advantages of supervised CR in boosting aerobic fitness, decreasing systemic inflammation, and encouraging ventricular remodeling in patients with DCM.4,6,8

Moreover, improvements in biomarkers, such as a significant drop in high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) levels, indicate that CR enhances functional outcomes and influences inflammatory pathways associated with DCM progression.9,10 The absence of coronary artery disease on imaging and the idiopathic nature of this patient's condition further emphasize the significance of nonpharmacologic interventions like CR in heart failure management.

Conclusions

This study presents a CR protocol that adheres to the FITT principle over a 12-week period, composed of an aerobic training regimen on a cycle ergometer, starting at 50 W with a maintained heart rate of 100 beats/min. Weekly increments of 20 W were implemented, progressing to a target aerobic load of 150 W at 110 beats/min by the program's conclusion. Each session lasted for 30 minutes and was closely supervised to ensure the safety and well-being of the patient throughout the intervention. Ongoing monitoring during CR is important to reduce risks, such as arrhythmias, and to fine-tune exercise intensity based on the individual patient's functional capacity. Integrating CR with pharmacologic treatment in severe DCM secured the most substantial clinical and functional benefits.

In Mexico, CR is not currently offered to patients with severe DCM and significant heart failure. This report details the successful implementation of a CR program for an individual with advanced DCM, demonstrating notable improvements in exercise capacity and overall quality of life. Health care professionals and clinical leaders must prioritize integrating CR into the standard care protocols for patients with advanced DCM.
