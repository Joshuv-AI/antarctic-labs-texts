Ultra-processed foods and nutrition governance in Ecuador as a public health challenge
Authors: Johanna Mora-Domínguez, Doménica P Palacios-Mora, William Calero-Cáceres
Published: 2026

In their Comment in The Lancet Regional Health – Americas, Neves and colleagues present a compelling analysis of Brazil's constitutional health tax reform and its subsequent dilution through corporate interference and legislative maneuvers.1 As they rightly note, ultra-processed foods (UPFs) constitute a principal driver of Brazil's non-communicable disease (NCD) burden and environmental degradation. While Brazil's Selective Tax offers a potential model for fiscal action, its contested implementation reveals broader regional challenges in reconciling public health priorities with powerful commercial determinants. In Ecuador, these tensions manifest not only through policy resistance but also via the institutionalization of corporate influence within national nutrition strategies.

Ecuador, like Brazil, faces a complex nutrition landscape. According to the National Survey on Child Malnutrition (ENDI), 20.1% of children under two years of age experience chronic undernutrition,2 while the ENSANUT 2018 survey reported that nearly 35% of children aged 5–11 live with overweight or obesity.3 Ultra-processed foods (affordable, heavily marketed, and widely available) are integral to this reality. Among adolescents, over 80% regularly consume sugar-sweetened beverages, snacks, and fast foods.4 Yet, unlike Brazil, Ecuador has not pursued strong fiscal measures; rather, the policy discourse has been captured by public–private alliances that position the food industry as a legitimate partner in addressing malnutrition.

Recent work by Torres et al. documents how Ecuador's official strategy against child malnutrition “Ecuador Crece Sin Desnutrición Infantil” has allowed for formal participation of food corporations in agenda-setting, resource allocation, and social responsibility campaigns.5 Despite claims of multi-sectoral collaboration, this model permits private actors to shape policy framing and weaken regulatory ambition. In practice, it conflates commercial marketing with nutritional education and delays interventions that target the upstream drivers of food system inequity. This configuration echoes what Neves et al. describe as “symbolic policy”—a pattern in which technical recommendations are acknowledged but rendered ineffective through scope restriction and inadequate enforcement.1

Such dynamics are particularly harmful in contexts of profound social inequality. In Ecuador, the double burden of malnutrition disproportionately affects low-income households, where structural barriers limit individuals’ ability to make autonomous dietary choices.6 Although the country introduced important measures such as the traffic light front-of-package labeling system in 2014, its influence on consumer behavior has been limited, and children remain widely exposed to the aggressive marketing of ultra-processed products.7 Additionally, nutrition education is seldom integrated into formal school curricula, and access to dietitians or clinical nutrition services in the public healthcare system is severely restricted. These conditions heighten the vulnerability of populations already marginalized by geography, poverty, and limited access to education.8

In contrast, recent local initiatives demonstrate the potential of food policy grounded in public interest. In 2025, Ecuador launched a pilot school-feeding program using fresh, locally sourced foods, supported by technical cooperation from the World Food Programme.9 Preliminary evaluations show increased enrollment, reduced household food expenditure, and improved dietary diversity. These results support global evidence that investment in public procurement, early nutrition, and agroecological supply chains can yield multidimensional benefits.10 Yet, scaling such programs requires strong political will and insulation from commercial influence.

Brazil's experience thus serves as both inspiration and cautionary tale. The legal establishment of a Selective Tax with extra-fiscal objectives marks a breakthrough in recognizing the health and environmental costs of UPFs. However, the rollback observed during its regulation (narrowing scope, preferential tax rates, and aggressive lobbying) mirrors a broader strategy of corporate disruption.1 If fiscal policy is to become a tool for food system transformation in the Americas, it must be accompanied by safeguards that prevent regulatory capture, mandate transparency, and prioritize independent scientific expertise.

As public health researchers and practitioners working in the Andean region, we believe Ecuador and neighboring countries must advance a nutrition agenda free from vested interests. This entails embedding food literacy in school curricula, expanding access to professional nutrition services, and strengthening state capacity to design and enforce evidence-based policy. Nutrition should not be outsourced. It is a public good—integral to health equity, human development, and democratic governance.
