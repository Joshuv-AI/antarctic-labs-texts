Gene Expression Associated With Behaviour Manipulation in Wood Ants Infected With Larval Lancet Fluke, Dicrocoelium dendriticum
Authors: Chen‐Hua Li, Cameron P. Goater, James D. Wasmuth
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMany ant species show dramatic shifts in behaviour when infected with parasites, but the molecular basis of these behavioural changes is not well understood. An example is the wood ant, Formica aserva , which serves as an intermediate host for the lancet liver fluke, Dicrocoelium dendriticum . Infected ants leave their nests during the cool hours of the day, ascend a flower and then attach themselves to a petal with their mandibles. Attached ants remain affixed to an inflorescence overnight, after which they detach, descend the plant and return to their nest. Unless eaten by a grazing mammal (the obligate next host), infected ants repeat this attach‐and‐detach cycle for the rest of the summer. We used transcriptomics to decipher the potential molecular mechanisms that underlie this reversible behaviour manipulation. Using naturally infected ants, we recreated the manipulation cycle in the laboratory and then evaluated messenger RNA from ant brains at four contrasting phases of manipulation. Among these phases, we found a total of 1349 transcripts differentially expressed between infected and uninfected ant brains. Many of these transcripts are involved in cell signalling pathways, including odorant, gustatory, vision, circadian rhythm and the production of biogenic monoamines and hormones. Metabolism, protein management and DNA repair functions might also play a role at different phases of manipulation. Our combined results are consistent with the idea that the mechanism(s) leading to the attach/detach/repeat sequence of behaviours of fluke‐infected ants is multifaceted, involving much more than temperature‐dependent contraction/relaxation of the mandibular muscles.

Introduction

The phenomenon that certain parasites may adapt to manipulate their host's phenotype, specifically their behaviour in precise ways, has frequently grabbed the public's attention (Moore 2002). The number of studies describing new host manipulation systems has increased dramatically (Herbison 2017; Poulin and Maure 2015), and these manipulative parasites cover a broad taxonomic range from single‐celled fungi and protozoans to metazoan helminths and parasitoids (Berdoy et al. 2000; Dass and Vyas 2014; Evans 1982; Evans and Samson 1984; Grosman et al. 2008; Wesolowska and Wesolowski 2014). The molecular mechanisms underlying host manipulation are beginning to be revealed through the use of –omics technologies (e.g., genomics, transcriptomics and proteomics) (Biron and Loxdale 2013; Hughes 2013; Hughes and Libersat 2018; Lefevre et al. 2009; Li and Poulin 2025; van Houte et al. 2013). In particular, transcriptomics by sequencing RNA (RNA‐Seq) allows researchers to evaluate how the expression of thousands of genes may change during different phases of host manipulation. One powerful approach is to characterise and compare transcript profiles in both manipulated and non‐manipulated hosts, ultimately leading to a list of candidate genes that may be responsible for manipulation. For example, larvae of the cestode Anomotaenia brevis manipulate various physiological and morphological phenotypes of their ant host, Temnothorax nylanderi (Stoldt et al. 2021). Comparisons of gene expression patterns in abdominal tissue of infected and uninfected ants showed that infection was associated with isoprenoid biosynthetic processes, phospholipid transport, perception of smell and metabolism. Similar approaches have been used to implicate differential gene expression in the manipulation of ant behaviours by the fungus Ophiocordyceps and the cestode Schistocephalus solidus (de Bekker et al. 2015; Feldmeyer et al. 2016; Hebert et al. 2017).

Larvae of the lancet liver fluke, Dicrocoelium dendriticum , provide an iconic example of parasite‐induced manipulation of host behaviour. Infected ant intermediate hosts leave their nest during cooler evenings, ascend adjacent vegetation and then use their mandibles to affix themselves to flower petals and leaf veins (Hohorst and Graefe 1961). Infected ants remain attached to their flower petal overnight. When the temperature exceeds approximately 18°C, infected ants detach from the flower, descend the plant, and then presumably return to their nest and assume normal worker behaviours (Anokhin 1966; Gasque and Fredensborg 2023). During attachment to a plant, infected ants are immobilised; they do not forage, avoid predators, or perform typical tasks for their colony. It is during the attachment phase when infected ants are assumed to have increased rates of accidental ingestion by the definitive host of D. dendriticum —foraging grazing mammals (Krull and Mapes 1953). To the best of our knowledge, there are no reports of uninfected ants, including those from nests that contain infected nestmates, performing these highly atypical behaviours. More profoundly, we are not aware of other examples in which the expression of a host manipulation is both multi‐faceted and reversible.

Adult D. dendriticum are hermaphroditic flukes residing in the bile ducts of the liver of grazing mammals such as deer, elk, hares and domestic stock (Otranto and Traversa 2002). Fluke eggs are passed in the faeces of grazers. Terrestrial snails, as first intermediate hosts, become infected when they encounter eggs on pasture. Larvae develop within snails to the cercariae stage, which exit the snail within aqueous slime balls. Ants, as second intermediate hosts, become infected when they encounter cercariae within ingested slime balls (Krull and Mapes 1952). Infective larvae (known as metacercariae) reside within the ant's abdomen, enveloped by a thick, double‐layered cyst wall. One, rarely two, metacercariae migrate to the ant's brain (known as ‘brainworm’), where it resides in the ventral‐anterior most region of the sub‐esophageal ganglion (SEG) (Martin‐Vega et al. 2018). The brainworm is responsible for the iconic behavioural manipulation and is considered to have zero fitness compared to the encysted metacercariae located in the abdomen (Criscione et al. 2020; Hohorst and Graefe 1961).

The mechanisms responsible for this complex, reversible manipulation are unknown. One candidate mechanism involves direct or indirect interference with host muscle function, both at the level of the mandible closure muscles (leading to attachment) and at the level of skeletal muscles (leading to host immobility on a flower). Genes and proteins related to muscle contraction, the troponin complex and actin binding are commonly identified in other parasite‐manipulated hosts (Feldmeyer et al. 2016; Herbison et al. 2019). Similarly, direct or indirect interference with an infected ant's sensory systems (e.g., those associated with odour, gustatory, or vision cues) may play a role, just as interference with environmental sensory ability may lead to immobility during attachment. Dysregulation of genes and proteins associated with odorant receptors and binding proteins has been observed in multiple host manipulation systems (Ponton et al. 2006; Will et al. 2020). Alternatively, manipulation of the biogenic monoamine neurotransmitter system is another candidate that has a known role in numerous host manipulation systems (Adamo 2005; Hojo et al. 2015; Miles and Booker 2000; Tain et al. 2006). In still other comparable interactions, precursors and enzymes involved in biogenic monoamine synthesis and the receptors of various neurotransmitters (e.g., octopamine, dopamine) have been identified among differentially expressed genes (de Bekker et al. 2015; Grecias et al. 2020; Lefevre et al. 2007; Wang et al. 2015; Will et al. 2020). Manipulation of general insect daily rhythms, such as those involved in foraging, mobility and immune responsiveness (de Bekker et al. 2017; Lee and Edery 2008), might also provide an avenue for D. dendriticum ‐induced behaviour alterations. For example, the core clock gene ‘circadian locomotor output cycle kaput’ was found significantly downregulated in two Ophiocordyceps‐ant systems during manipulation (de Bekker et al. 2015; Will et al. 2020). Intriguingly, manipulation of the insect chill‐coma phenomenon provides another candidate mechanism since it also involves a reversible, immobile paralytic state that is temperature‐dependent (Macmillan and Sinclair 2011). In sum, whereas the mechanisms underlying the multi‐faceted and reversible manipulation that characterises the ant/Dicrocoelium interaction are unknown, transcriptomic tools can be applied as an initial step to identify potential candidates.

In this study, we sequenced mRNA in D. dendriticum ‐infected and uninfected brains of Formica aserva worker ants to characterise potential molecular mechanisms underlying this reversible host manipulation. Our main aim was to characterise gene expression profiles in the brains of infected ants that were collected and processed at four phases of the attach–detach sequence of the manipulation. We find that D. dendriticum larvae affect multiple pathways that occur within the brains of infected ants, including those involved with the detection and response to cues associated with odour, vision, monoamine regulation and circadian rhythm.

Materials and Methods

Field Site and Ant Collection Protocol

Our study occurs in a region of liver fluke emergence in Cypress Hills Interprovincial Park in southeastern Alberta, Canada. vanParidon, Gilleard, et al. (2017) provide a general description of this parkland ‘Sky Island’ setting, including the proposed pathway and timing of introduction and emergence of D. dendriticum in this region from continental Europe. We collected ants adjacent to 3 nests from our ‘Staff Camp’ site for this study because background infection data was available from samples of attached ants (Martin‐Vega et al. 2018; van Paridon, Colwell, et al. 2017) and Oreohelix land snails (Dempsey et al. 2019) collected adjacent to these nests. Hundreds of infected ants have been found attached to plants adjacent to each nest each spring and summer since 2009. Furthermore, our previous and ongoing studies on behavioural and population genetic aspects of the lancet fluke/ant interaction involve ants collected from these nests (Criscione et al. 2020).

The three nests are located approximately 50 m apart from each other within a 100 m × 100 m area that contains approximately 20 visible F. aserva nests. Although it is likely that the three nests represent individual colonies, it remains a possibility that our designated ‘nests’ represent an unknown number of larger colonies with multiple, socially connected nest entrances (e.g., Scarparo et al. (2024)). Because we cannot discount this possibility, we consider our pooled collections to represent ants from a single colony. Ants were collected on 07 June 2021 between 06:00 and 09:00. Collection started by scanning the vegetation within approximately 2 m of each nest and removing 30–50 ants that were firmly attached with their mandibles to the inflorescences of goldenrod, Solidago sp. or clover, Trifolium sp. During collection, the stem of the plant was cut so that the ant and the flower it was attached to could be placed together in a plastic, mesh‐topped container. At each nest, uninfected ants were sampled by collecting the first 50–60 ants that attempted to defend the surface of the nest after it was gently disturbed. Approximately 120 infected and 120 uninfected ants were collected from the vicinity of the three nests. Following collection, containers with infected or uninfected ants were placed into a cooler with ice, transported to the laboratory at the University of Lethbridge and then transferred into a growth chamber set at 10°C to ensure that infected ants remained attached to their plant.

Experimental Design

We defined the four sequential phases of the reversible manipulation as follows. The ‘pre‐attached’ phase was defined as the period of time when a known infected ant was on a plant, opening and closing its mandibles, but not yet attached to the plant. The ‘attached’ phase was defined as the iconic immobilisation period during which an ant was attached firmly to a flower petal with its mandibles. The ‘newly detached’ phase was defined as the period when a previously attached ant had relaxed its mandibles from a flower petal but had not yet left the plant. These ants were collected and processed within 20–30 s following detachment. Lastly, a ‘detached’ phase was defined as the period when a previously attached ant had been detached from a flower petal for at least 40 min. These ants appeared to wander within their container, behaving similarly to uninfected individuals.

All ants were maintained at 10°C between 12 and 72 h prior to the start of the experiment to mimic the different behaviour phases with a 16 h:8 h light: dark photoperiod. The experiments were conducted during the day under light. For each phase, ants were randomly selected from holding containers kept at 10°C and then individually placed into customised perforated plastic containers (Unrau 2019) with infected ants still attached to their flower petals. To account for potential gene expression differences in tasks, uninfected ants of similar size to the randomly selected infected ants were chosen as controls for each phase. This provides the best possible contrast with infected ants because F. aserva ants, like most of the Formica species, lack morphologically distinct castes, and task performance is associated only with worker size (Perl and Niven 2016; West and Purcell 2020). On each day of the trial, a similar number of infected and uninfected ants from each phase were sampled. To collect samples of newly detached and detached ants, individual ants were transferred to a 25°C chamber from the 10°C chamber. Newly detached ants were sampled within 10–20 s following the relaxation of the mandibles while still remaining on the flower to which they were previously attached. In contrast, detached ants were those that had relaxed their mandibles and were wandering within their container. Samples of uninfected ants were collected at the same time as samples of infected ants at each phase of the manipulation. To collect samples of pre‐attached and attached ants, individual ants were transferred from the 10°C storage growth chamber to the 25°C growth chamber to ensure that infected ants detached from their flowers. Infected ants that did not detach at 25°C within 3 h were discarded. The detached infected ants and uninfected ants were then returned to the 10°C growth chamber. The pre‐attached ants were sampled once they showed signs of biting behaviour (i.e., opening and closing of mandibles). For the attached phase, ants were left at 10°C for at least 40 min and sampled once the infected ants were observed biting their flower. For each treatment, uninfected control ants were placed under the same conditions as the infected group and were sampled at the same time.

At each of the four conditions (pre‐attached, attached, newly detached and detached), 20 infected ants and 20 uninfected ants were sampled. There were also six infected ants that did not detach at 25°C, even after 24 h, and six infected ants that did not reattach at 10°C after 24 h. These were sampled and categorised as refused‐to‐detach and refused‐to‐attach ants, respectively.

Ant Brain Dissection and Collection

Each ant was dissected under a dissecting microscope in a drop of RNAlater Stabilisation Solution (ThermoFisher). First, fine tweezers were used to remove the cuticle from the posterior region of the head. The brain, including the sub‐ and supra‐esophageal ganglia, mushroom bodies and associated sub‐regions, was then accessed and removed intact, taking care to avoid surrounding muscles and glands. Intact brains were removed from the heads of individual ants within 1 min and then fixed in 1 mL RNAlater solution. For each phase of manipulation, samples of individual uninfected ants were selected and processed in the same manner as described above to account for the effect of temperature, which is known to affect gene expression in ant brains (Stanton‐Geddes et al. 2016; Stucki et al. 2017). All samples were stored at −20°C. The abdomens of uninfected ants were dissected to verify that they did not contain metacercariae.

RNA Extraction and Sequencing

The stored samples (one brain per sample) were thawed on ice for RNA extraction (RNeasy Mini Kit with RNase‐Free DNase Set, Qiagen) following the manufacturer's guidelines. Before extraction, the samples were rinsed twice in 500 uL of the first solution from the RNeasy kit (buffer RLT) to ensure that the RNAlater was removed from the sample. The concentration and quality of the extracted RNA were checked using the Qubit RNA HS Assay Kit (ThermoFisher) and the Agilent TapeStation using the High Sensitivity RNA assay. The extracted RNA samples were stored at −80°C until sequenced. In total, we processed 10 sample types of ants. Six sample types were the behavioural conditions for infected ants: pre‐attached, attached, newly detached, detached, refused‐to‐attach and refused‐to‐detach ants. Four types of samples were uninfected temperature‐matched control ants for four conditions: pre‐attached, attached, newly detached and detached. To ensure we had appropriate statistical power for the differential expression analysis, we sequenced six biological replicates for each of the 10 sample types of both infected and uninfected ants (60 samples in total) (Schurch et al. 2016).

RNA samples were sequenced at the Centre for Health Genomics and Informatics at the University of Calgary. Libraries were made using the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit following the manufacturer's instructions. Libraries were multiplexed and paired‐end sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq with an S2 flow cell for 200 cycles (2 × 100 bp) using the v1.5 reagent kit, following the manufacturer's instructions. The sequencing reads are accessible through NCBI within BioProject ID: PRJNA918668.

Selection of the Formica Reference Genome/Transcriptome

Because a fully sequenced genome was not available for our target ant species, F. aserva , we accessed three available Formica genomes and transcriptomes: F. exsecta (both the genome and the coding sequences file as a transcriptome, GenBank accession number: GCA_003651465.1, 24,707 transcripts), F. selysi (genome, GenBank accession number: GCA_009859135.1, no annotation information available), and a hybrid of F. aquilonia × F. polyctena genome with 19,226 coding sequences (Nouhaud et al. 2022). We selected reads from eight samples (reads L001 from samples 2021‐10C‐Unif‐1, 2021‐10C‐inf‐1, 2021‐25C‐Uninf‐1, 2021‐25C‐inf‐1, 2021‐waydown‐Uninf‐1, 2021‐waydown‐inf‐1, 2021‐wayup‐Uninf‐1, 2021‐wayup‐inf‐1) to test the mapping rates with the F. exsecta transcriptome using the RNA sequencing quantification program Kallisto (version 0.46.2) and each of the three genomes using the splice‐aware aligner STAR (version 2.7.9a) (Bray et al. 2016; Dobin et al. 2013). We also performed FeatureCounts (version 2.0.3) on the mapping results from the genomes with available annotation files ( F. exsecta and F. aquilonia × F. polyctena ) to check the percentage of reads hits to gene models (Liao et al. 2014).

Ant Differential Gene Expression Analysis

Sequence read quality was assessed using FastQC (version 0.11.9), and the reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic (version 0.39) with the trimming parameters: CROP:97 and HEADCROP:13. Trimmed reads were mapped to the Formica exsecta genome using STAR (version 2.7.9a). Transcript abundance was calculated using FeatureCounts (version 2.0.3). We performed the differential transcript expression analysis using DESeq2 (version 1.26.0) (Love et al. 2014). Pairwise comparison of transcript expression was conducted between several sample types: (1) between infected and uninfected ants in different sample types, (2) between each of the two comparisons among the four conditions for uninfected ants and (3) between each of the two comparisons of the four conditions for infected ants. We considered a false discovery rate adjusted p‐value below 0.05 as significantly differentially expressed.

For comparisons between infected/uninfected ants, pairwise assessments were performed at the pre‐attached, attached, newly detached and detached conditions. The transcript expression of refused‐to‐attach infected ants was compared to their uninfected attached condition controls, and similarly the transcript expression of refused‐to‐detach infected ants was compared to their uninfected detached condition controls. To assist with linking gene expression profiles with biological changes, we determined whether differentially regulated transcripts were uniquely associated with one of the four phases of the ant behaviour manipulation: initiation of attachment, its maintenance, its cessation and the resumption of normal behaviour (Table 1).

Gene Function Enrichment Analysis

We performed both gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. For the GO enrichment analysis, all F. exsecta transcripts were searched through InterProScan (version 5.54–87.0) to obtain the functional annotation (GO terms) for each transcript. GO enrichment analysis was performed for up‐ and downregulated differentially expressed transcripts in each comparison using the Enricher function in the clusterProfiler package in R (Yu et al. 2012). KEGG pathways were obtained for F. exsecta transcripts using eggNOG‐mapper v2 (Cantalapiedra et al. 2021). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed for up‐ and downregulated differentially expressed transcripts in each comparison using the enrichKEGG function in the clusterProfiler package in R with the species set as “fex” (Yu et al. 2012).

Results

Sequencing Results and Choice of Reference Genome or Transcriptome

A mean of 48.9 million read pairs per sample was obtained from the 60 samples. As a genome assembly for F. aserva was not available, we determined which of the available Formica resources were an appropriate proxy: F. exsecta , F. selysi and a hybrid of F. aquilonia × F. polyctena . We were able to map ~90% of reads to any of the three genomes but could map only 59%–66% of reads to a gene model or transcriptome (Figure S1). While this suggested that F. aserva ‐specific genes would be missed, we assumed that the presence of most behaviour‐related genes would be highly conserved within the genus.

Because of the better mapping rate, we used the F. exsecta genome, which contains 24,707 predicted protein‐coding genes, as the reference for the subsequent work (Table S1).

To compare differences in transcript expression between sample conditions for infected ants, it was necessary to control for the uninfected ant transcript expression associated with a change in temperature and length of time exposed at that temperature. Gene Function Enrichment Analysis

We identified enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways among the up‐ and downregulated transcripts identified in each comparison mentioned above (Files S5 and S6).

During the pre‐attached condition, upregulated transcripts in infected ant brains, compared to uninfected ant brains, were enriched in GO terms related to symporter activity, carbohydrate metabolism, hydrolase activity hydrolysing O‐glycosyl compounds, small GTPase‐mediated signal transduction, G‐protein‐coupled receptor activity and protein tyrosine kinase activity (Table S1). Additionally, these transcripts were enriched in KEGG pathways associated with cellular processes, including lysosome (fex04142) and autophagy (fex04140), as well as starch and sucrose metabolism (fex00500), and vitamin digestion and absorption (fex04977) (Table S1). Downregulated transcripts in infected ant brains enriched GO terms associated with phosphatase activity, response to oxidative stress and ion binding (Table S2).

During the attached condition, enriched GO terms in the upregulated transcripts in infected ant brains compared to uninfected ant brains include those related to neuronal functions and RNA processing. Downregulated transcripts in infected ant brains have six enriched KEGG pathways, including three related to genetic information processing (fex03050, fex04146, fex03060), two related to fatty acid metabolism (fex00071, fex01212) and peroxisome (fex04146). Enriched GO terms in the downregulated transcripts in infected ant brains, including the ones related to neuronal functions (GO:0046928 ‘regulation of neurotransmitter secretion’ and GO:0060291 ‘long‐term synaptic potentiation’), and many enriched GO terms were involved in protein synthesis and trafficking, protein glycosylation, protein degradation, metabolism and gene expression regulation.

During the newly detached condition, upregulated transcripts in infected ant brains compared to uninfected ant brains enriched three KEGG pathways in genetic information processing (fex03430 ‘mismatch repair’, fex03030 ‘DNA replication’ and fex03420 ‘nucleotide excision repair’) and five GO terms, including metallodipeptidase activity, hydrolase activity, DNA recombination, signal transduction and DNA‐binding transcription factor activity. Downregulated transcripts in infected ant brains enriched KEGG pathways involved in cell motility (fex04820, fex04814) and phototransduction (fex04745) and enriched GO terms including regulation of muscle contraction, troponin complex, glutathione metabolic process, regulation of translation and calcium ion binding.

In the detached condition, upregulated transcripts in infected ant brains, compared to uninfected ant brains, were enriched in two KEGG pathways: starch and sucrose metabolism (fex00500) and mismatch repair (fex03430). Enriched GO terms included those associated with transcription and translation, ion binding, response to oxidative stress, carbohydrate metabolic process, endocytosis and autophagy. Most of the enriched GO terms in infected ant brains with downregulated transcripts were related to microtubule functions (8 of 22) and transcription‐factors. Other suppressed functions in infected ants included lipid metabolism, phospholipid transport and monooxygenase activity (Table S8).

In the transcriptomic comparisons of infected ant brains across different conditions, while accounting for temperature variations from uninfected ant brain comparisons, the enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways are detailed in Tables S25–S36 and Tables S18–S23, respectively. Compared to the pre‐attached infected ant brains, attached infected ant brains upregulated GO terms related to chromatin remodelling and gene expression regulation, while signalling and metabolic processes were downregulated. Compared to attached infected ant brains, newly‐detached infected ant brains upregulated GO terms involved in protein management, gene expression and microtubule cytoskeleton. GO terms related to potassium transport, DNA replication and gene expression were downregulated. Compared to newly detached infected ant brains, detached infected ant brains upregulated GO terms related to motor and cytoskeleton functions, protein synthesis and modification and upregulated KEGG pathways related to cell motility (fex04820 ‘cytoskeleton in muscle cells’ and fex04814 ‘motor proteins’) and translation (fex00970). Many of the downregulated GO terms in detached infected ants are associated with transcription regulation. Compared to detached infected ant brains, pre‐attached infected ant brains upregulate GO terms involved in signalling, metabolism, potassium channel, DNA and RNA processes. Meanwhile, downregulated GO terms were primarily associated with chromatin organisation, gene expression and protein modification.

Enrichment analyses revealed that refused‐to‐attach ants showed extensive upregulation of neuronal excitability, signalling pathways, cytoskeletal dynamics and gene regulation machinery compared to the uninfected controls (File S5—Sheet 9, File S6—Sheet 7). They also had more metabolism pathways downregulated (including carbohydrate, amino acid and lipid metabolisms) compared to the uninfected controls (File S5—Sheet 10, File S6—Sheet 8). Refused‐to‐detach ants exhibited widespread downregulation of protein synthesis‐related functions and upregulation of DNA damage and repair GO terms (File S5—Sheets 11–12, File S6—Sheets 9–10).

Candidate Transcripts

We recovered candidate transcripts in support of various hypotheses from the lists of differentially expressed transcripts between various comparisons.

Odorant‐ and Gustatory‐Related Transcripts

Odorant receptors, gustatory receptors and odorant‐binding proteins were downregulated in infected ants compared to uninfected ants in pre‐attached and attached sample groups (Table S2). Among the differentially expressed transcripts at the pre‐attached samples, the following odorant receptor and odorant‐binding proteins were more than 64‐fold downregulated in the infected ant brains: ‘ejaculatory bulb‐specific protein 3‐like’ and ‘general odorant‐binding protein 56d‐like’. For those odorant‐related transcripts identified in the differentially expressed transcripts in the attached samples, the following were downregulated in infected ants: ‘general odorant‐binding protein 56d‐like’ (205‐fold decrease), ‘odorant receptor Or2‐like’ (64‐fold decrease) and ‘gustatory receptor for sugar taste 64f‐like’ (4‐fold decrease). Among these transcripts, the ‘ejaculatory bulb‐specific protein 3‐like’ transcript was downregulated specifically for the initiation of the attachment behaviour (Table S2). Besides ‘odorant receptor 22c‐like’ transcripts, there were an additional 16 and 2 odorant and gustatory receptor‐related transcripts upregulated and downregulated in refused‐to‐attached infected ants, respectively (Table S2). The ‘ejaculatory bulb‐specific protein 3‐like’ transcript was upregulated (8‐fold increase) in newly detached infected ant brains compared to attached ant brains. From the detached to the pre‐attached phase, infected ant brains downregulated 2 transcripts from the ‘putative gustatory receptor 28a’ gene and 9 transcripts associated with various odorant receptors (File S4).

Vision‐Related Transcripts

Vision‐related genes were also identified that were differentially expressed in infected ants compared to uninfected ants at different sample condition groups (Table S3). As with the odorant‐related genes, the identified vision genes in the differentially expressed transcripts at the pre‐attached and attached samples were all downregulated in infected ants compared to uninfected ants. The ‘EF‐hand domain‐containing protein 1‐like’ transcripts downregulated in the pre‐attached infected ants were also in the downregulated transcript set specifically for the initiation of the attachment behaviour (Table S3). At the attached phase, three transcripts from two ‘retinol‐binding protein pinta‐like’ genes were downregulated in infected ant brains compared to uninfected brains. In the newly detached samples, the two identified vision transcripts from the ‘rhodopsin‐like’ gene in the differentially expressed transcripts were both upregulated in infected ants compared to uninfected ants. These two transcripts were in the upregulated transcript set specifically for the cessation of the attachment behaviour (Table S3). With the between‐condition comparisons of infected ant brains, the ‘retinal homeobox protein Rx‐B‐like’ transcript was found upregulated in newly detached infected ants compared to attached infected ants, and then it was downregulated in detached infected ants compared to newly detached infected ants. The ‘transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily V member 5’ transcript was upregulated in pre‐attached infected ant brains compared to detached infected ant brains.

Biogenic Monoamine‐Related Transcripts

Several neurotransmitter‐related transcripts, including biogenic monoamines, were differentially expressed between the brains of infected and uninfected ants (Table S4). However, the pattern of up‐ and downregulated transcripts for amine‐related transcripts was a contrast to the odorant and vision‐related transcripts. In infected ants, amine‐related transcripts were upregulated in the pre‐attached and attached conditions but downregulated in the newly detached and detached conditions. In the pre‐attached samples, six ‘octopamine receptor beta‐2R‐like’ transcripts from the same gene were upregulated in the infected ants. These transcripts were part of the set upregulated specifically for the initiation of the attachment of the ant to the flower. In the newly detached samples, two transcripts were found downregulated in the infected ants, including ‘gamma‐aminobutyric acid receptor subunit beta‐like’ and ‘tryptophan 5‐hydroxylase 1’. The latter is involved in serotonin synthesis. In the detached samples, three transcripts were downregulated in the infected ants compared to uninfected ants: two ‘tyrosine 3‐monooxygenase‐like’ and one ‘gamma‐aminobutyric acid receptor subunit beta‐like’ transcripts. In addition, there were 30 neurotransmitter receptor‐related transcripts upregulated and three ‘gamma‐aminobutyric acid receptor‐related’ transcripts downregulated in refused‐to‐attach infected ants compared to the uninfected ants. For the refused‐to‐detach infected ants, the two ‘tyrosine 3‐monooxygenase‐like’ transcripts were downregulated, while a ‘gamma‐aminobutyric acid receptor alpha‐like’ transcript was upregulated. From the attached to newly‐detached phase, infected ant brains downregulated 4 transcripts from ‘dopamine receptor 2’ gene and an ‘octopamine receptor beta‐1R‐like’ transcript (File S4).

Circadian Rhythm‐Related Transcripts

Several circadian rhythm‐related transcripts were identified in the differentially expressed transcript sets in infected ant brains at different manipulation phases. Two ‘circadian locomotor output cycles protein kaput’ (‘clock’) transcripts were downregulated, and the ‘cryptochrome‐1‐like’ transcript was upregulated in newly detached infected ants compared to attached infected ants (File S4). In addition, the ‘period circadian protein’ transcript was upregulated in pre‐attached infected ants compared to detached infected ants.

Locomotion and Motor‐Related Transcripts

A transcript of the foraging gene in ants, ‘cGMP‐dependent protein kinase isozyme 1’, was downregulated in the attached and refused‐to‐attach infected ants compared to uninfected ants. In newly detached samples, while the ants are waking up from the attachment behaviour, transcripts from a wide variety of genes were downregulated in infected ants: ‘muscle‐specific protein 20‐like’, ‘actin‐5, muscle‐specific’, tropomyosin and troponin. Then, in the detached samples, infected ants downregulated transcripts for paramyosin‐like and ‘actin‐related protein 1’ and upregulated transcripts for ‘myogenesis‐regulating glycosidase‐like’. From the attached to the detached phase, infected ants downregulated 3 transcripts from ‘muscle‐specific protein 20’ and ‘muscle‐specific protein 20‐like’. From the newly detached to the detached phase, infected ants upregulated 24 transcripts for genes, including ‘actin‐5, muscle‐specific’, ‘muscle‐specific protein 20’, ‘myosin heavy chain, muscle’ and ‘muscle LIM protein MIp84B‐like’.

Chill‐Coma (Potassium Channel)‐Related Transcripts

In the attached samples, three transcripts from one ‘sodium/potassium/calcium exchanger 3’ gene were upregulated in infected ant brains (Table S5). One of these transcripts was also upregulated in the refused‐to‐detach ants. This transcript is in the upregulated transcript set specifically for the maintenance of the attachment behaviour (Table S5). In the detached condition, the ‘bumetanide‐sensitive sodium‐(potassium)‐chloride cotransporter’ transcript was downregulated in infected ants. From the attached to the newly detached phase, infected ants downregulated 16 transcripts associated with potassium channels (File S4).

Discussion

In this study, we identified changes in gene expression in the brains of ants infected with D. dendriticum . With this new knowledge, we can propose hypotheses regarding potential underlying molecular mechanisms responsible for the complex, reversible behaviour changes in infected ants. We identified a total of 8885 transcripts that were differentially expressed between infected and uninfected ants for different sample groups (including the two ‘refused‐to’ groups). Overall, this magnitude of differential expression between each of the infected and uninfected ‘ants’ pairwise comparisons within the four contrasting phases is consistent with the results of comparable transcriptomics studies involving host manipulation systems (de Bekker et al. 2015; Feldmeyer et al. 2016; Will et al. 2020). Prominent among these were transcripts implicated in key biological processes, including metabolism, gene expression, cellular repair, environmental sensing, biogenic monoamines and neuronal functions, circadian rhythm, muscle function and chill‐coma (Figure 4). Below, we discuss the biological relevance of the differentially expressed transcripts in each category.

Summary of the behaviour alterations and potential mechanisms during the four phases of the behaviour manipulation.

General Role of Metabolism, Gene Expression and Cellular Repair

Differentially expressed transcripts and proteins associated with metabolism are commonly involved in host manipulation systems (Li and Poulin 2025). In our system, energy metabolism in infected ant brains is altered in a phase‐specific manner: carbohydrate metabolism is upregulated during active phases (pre‐attached and detached), while fatty acid metabolism is downregulated during the static attached phase. This suppression of fatty acid breakdown during the prolonged static phase, when infected ants are unable to feed, may conserve energy and thus prolong host survival through the overnight attachment. Depending on the temperature, infected ants sometimes can attach upon the vegetation for days (personal observations). Pre‐attached ants may require increased energy from upregulated carbohydrate metabolism to initiate summiting behaviour. At the detached phase, infected ant brains exhibit renewed upregulated carbohydrate metabolism, alongside enrichment of GO terms related to DNA recombination, oxidative stress response and autophagy. This metabolic upregulation likely provides the energy necessary to sustain these cellular processes.

At the attached phase, there is a widespread downregulation of host genetic information processing, particularly in protein management, including synthesis, trafficking, modification and degradation. Of the enriched pathways and terms, three of six enriched KEGG pathways and 19 of 46 enriched GO terms are associated with this process. While changes in protein regulation have been observed in various host manipulation systems (Aufauvre et al. 2014; Biron et al. 2005, 2006; Feldmeyer et al. 2016; Vidau et al. 2014), the extent of downregulation observed here is notable. This suppression, combined with the substantial downregulation of metabolism‐related KEGG pathways and GO terms, may contribute to the prolonged attachment behaviour, preventing the host from “waking up”. The transition into the attached phase from the pre‐attached phase also supports this idea, with a notable downregulation of general intracellular signal transduction (GO:0035556) and carbohydrate metabolic processes (GO:0005975). From the attached to the newly detached phase, infected ants upregulate protein management GO terms, with further upregulation of protein synthesis and modification‐related GO terms in the detached phase. This pattern suggests a gradual recovery from the biting behaviour. Notably, across the behavioural cycle of infected ants, gene expression regulation remains constantly enriched in the differentially expressed transcripts, indicating its general role in the Dicrocoelium‐ant manipulation.

At the newly detached phase, the differentially expressed transcripts indicate potential cellular stress and damage incurred during the previous attachment phase. Compared to uninfected ants, newly detached infected brains upregulate KEGG pathways related to DNA maintenance, including ‘mismatch repair’ (fex03430) and ‘nucleotide excision repair’ (fex03420), alongside GO terms such as ‘DNA recombination’ (GO:0006310). This suggests that the attached phase imposed significant stress on the infected ants, promoting repair processes upon detachment. The upregulation of DNA repair also remained at the detached phase, where the ‘mismatch repair’ (fex03430) pathway remained enriched. This sustained repair activity may play a role in maintaining host cell viability following a prolonged manipulation period.

In comparisons between infected and uninfected ant brains, we observed more differentially expressed transcripts in the refused‐to‐attach and refused‐to‐detach groups than in the attached and detached groups. Most infected ants exhibited typical behaviour, attaching at 10°C and detaching at 25°C, whereas the ‘refused‐to’ groups represented a small subset displaying abnormal behaviour with unclear causes. These ‘refused‐to’ groups exhibited strong transcriptional dysregulation, including upregulation of excitability‐related genes and signs of cellular stress. The refused‐to‐detach ants remained fixed to vegetation beyond 24 h at 25°C, a state likely to be fatal. Such observations support the hypothesis that behavioural manipulation is a tightly regulated process, and deviations from this programme—whether through parasite failure, host resistance, or external factors—may result in neural instability and physiological breakdown.

Environment Sensing (Odorant, Gustatory and Vision‐Related Genes)

Uninfected F. aserva demonstrate characteristic anti‐predation behaviours when threatened. These behaviours include rapid motility (i.e., escape behaviour) and mandibular opening and closing (personal observations). In contrast, infected ants do not respond to external stimuli when they are attached to a flower petal. These anecdotal observations suggest that attached infected ants have a reduced ability to detect, or respond to, environmental stimuli. We identified transcripts that encode for receptors and binding proteins for odorant, gustatory and vision processes that were downregulated in attached infected ants compared to uninfected ants: ‘general odorant‐binding protein 56d‐like’, ‘gustatory receptor for sugar taste 64f‐like’, and ‘retinol‐binding protein pinta‐like’. Their downregulation might lead to a reduction in the ant's ability to receive and respond to environmental stimuli. If the consequence is that infected and attached ants do not escape in response to a nearby grazing mammal, this would increase the likelihood of Dicrocoelium transmission. In Ophiocordyceps‐infected ants, when infected ants exhibit the altered behaviour (seeking an optimal environment for spore release), many odorant receptors and binding protein genes were differentially expressed (de Bekker et al. 2015; Will et al. 2020). Some of the downregulated genes (e.g., ‘odorant receptor 1’, ‘odorant receptor co‐receptor’) potentially help the ants to reduce distractions from the hours‐long process of finding the optimal environment (de Bekker et al. 2015). Our transcriptomic sampling was restricted to the ant brain; therefore, we cannot directly infer changes in peripheral chemosensation (e.g., antennae, maxillary palps). Nevertheless, attached infected ants showed downregulation of a ‘sensory neuron membrane protein 1‐like’ transcript, together with reductions in transcripts associated with neurotransmitter signalling, ion channel activity and putative odorant‐degrading enzymes. In addition to receptors and binding proteins, genes that modulate neuronal physiology can influence environmental sensing and responsiveness (Kohlmeier and Billeter 2023). Central modulation of these pathways could alter responsiveness to environmental cues, but confirmation will require profiling of peripheral sensory tissues and functional assays.

In the newly detached condition, infected ants cease biting and detach their mandibles from flower petals. At this phase, the expression of two vision‐related transcripts from the ‘rhodopsin‐like’ gene was upregulated. Both were in the upregulated transcript set specifically for the cessation of the attachment behaviour. Moreover, these transcripts are different from those downregulated at pre‐attached and attached conditions. In addition, the ‘retinal homeobox protein Rx‐B‐like’ transcript was found upregulated in newly detached infected ant brains when compared to the infected ants at both the previous and later phases of the behaviour cycle (attached and newly detached). This indicates the potential role that vision‐related transcripts may play in the cessation of the attachment behaviour. Ants strongly rely on chemical and tactile cues received mainly from olfactory input, and, compared to other insects, they rely less on vision (Gronenberg 2008). However, Formica wood ants use visual landmarks and other forms of visual orientation cues for orientation purposes (Gronenberg 2008). The vision‐related genes in F. aserva likely contributed to this behaviour manipulation, as the parasite might interfere with ant vision during the attachment behaviour, which is subsequently restored when control is returned to the host.

Biogenic Monoamines and Neuronal Functions

As neurotransmitters, neuromodulators and neurohormones, biogenic monoamines modulate a wide range of insect behaviours and are commonly associated with parasitic manipulation (Adamo 2005; Hojo et al. 2015; Li and Poulin 2025; Miles and Booker 2000; Tain et al. 2006; Wang et al. 2015). At the newly detached condition, expression for the transcript for the enzyme ‘tryptophan 5‐hydroxylase’ was found to be downregulated in infected ants. This enzyme is involved in serotonin synthesis, and the downregulation of this transcript could suggest lower ant brain serotonin levels in infected ants at newly‐detached phase. Serotonin levels have been positively correlated with trail‐following and foraging behaviour in the ant Pheidole dentata (Muscedere et al. 2012). Therefore, the downregulation of ‘tryptophan 5‐hydroxylase’ transcript in newly detached D. dendriticum ‐infected ants may suggest that they do not immediately return to the nest or resume foraging after detachment. At the attached phase, GO terms ‘regulation of neurotransmitter secretion’ and ‘long‐term synaptic potentiation’ were enriched in the downregulated transcripts in the infected ant brains compared to the uninfected ones. This suggests disruption of synaptic communication and plasticity in the infected ant brain at the attached phase, likely contributing to the expression of the attachment behaviour.

In Ophiocordyceps‐infected ants, genes encoding octopamine receptors and a rate‐limiting enzyme for dopamine synthesis (‘tyrosine 3‐monooxygenase gene’) were differentially expressed when infected ants exhibit the abnormal behaviour (de Bekker et al. 2015; Will et al. 2020). Similarly, in Dicrocoelium‐infected ants, the transcript encoding ‘octopamine receptor beta‐2R‐like’ was upregulated at the pre‐attached phase. Octopamine has been associated with nestmate recognition in fire ants, Solenopsis invicta (Vander Meer et al. 2008), and with task specialisation in Formica polyctena (Wnuk et al. 2011). Considering these roles, elevated octopaminergic signalling in F. aserva may alter responsiveness to colony cues and task allocation. Whether nestmate recognition or task specialisation contributes to the behavioural alterations observed in D. dendriticum ‐infected ants remains to be tested. Additionally, transcripts of two ‘tyrosine 3‐monooxygenase‐like’ genes were identified among differentially expressed transcripts between infected and uninfected ants. However, these genes were downregulated in detached infected ants compared to detached uninfected ants and were not directly linked to attachment behaviour, suggesting that Dicrocoelium may also have effects beyond the attachment phase. This idea is further supported by the substantial downregulation of microtubule‐related functions, with 8 of 22 enriched GO terms showing decreased expression in detached infected ant brains compared to uninfected ones. Given that microtubules are critical for neuronal morphology, axonal transport, and overall brain function (Jang et al. 2024), these molecular changes may indicate underlying neurological alterations occurring at detachment phases, which could lead to previously unidentified behavioural alterations at these phases.

Circadian Rhythm

Dysregulation of circadian rhythm‐related transcripts has been reported in various host manipulation systems and could be an important component of the attachment/detachment sequence of behaviours in our fluke‐infected ants (Elya et al. 2018; Geffre et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2015; Will et al. 2020). Various insect behaviours have daily rhythms, such as foraging and flight (de Bekker et al. 2017). A daily rhythm in components of the insect immune system has also been reported (Lee and Edery 2008). The circadian clock gene ‘cryptochrome‐1’ was found downregulated in the silkworm Bombyx mori infected with B. mori nucleopolyhedrovirus (Wang et al. 2015). In Ophiocordyceps‐infected ants, homologues of two insect circadian clock genes, ‘period’ and ‘cycle’, were found to have higher expression in manipulated ants, and another circadian rhythm‐related gene ‘clock,’ was found to be downregulated in manipulated ants (de Bekker et al. 2015; Will et al. 2020). Interestingly, transcripts from these genes were identified in Dicrocoelium‐infected ants, with the expression of the ‘period’ transcript upregulated in pre‐attached infected ants compared to detached infected ants, and the expression of two ‘clock’ transcripts upregulated and the ‘cryptochrome‐1‐like’ transcript downregulated in attached infected ants compared to newly detached infected ants. These comparisons were associated with changing environmental temperatures (25°C–10°C and 10°C–25°C). This may affect the expression of clock‐controlled genes and contribute to these two comparisons (detached vs. pre‐attached and attached vs. newly detached) having the two highest numbers of differentially expressed transcripts (Table 3).

Genes Associated With Muscle Function

In other ant‐parasite systems, genes normally associated with muscle function have been implicated in behaviour manipulation (de Bekker et al. 2015; Feldmeyer et al. 2016). Anomotenia brevis‐infected ants remained relatively motionless even when the nest was disturbed. The downregulation of the muscle‐related genes (e.g., ‘actin’, ‘myosin’, ‘tropomyosin’) has been observed in parasitised ant brains (Feldmeyer et al. 2016). In Ophiocordyceps‐infected ants, infection caused muscle atrophy (Hughes et al. 2011), and genes encoding major muscle fibre proteins (e.g., ‘collagen’, ‘muscle LIM protein’) were downregulated in ant heads during the manipulated behavioural phase (de Bekker et al. 2015).

For D. dendriticum ‐infected ants, we also identified differentially expressed transcripts typically linked to muscle function across various phases. Notably, our study focused on brain tissue, where these genes may play different roles from their roles in muscle. For example, ‘actin’ and ‘myosin V’ have been suggested to play a role in intersynaptic vesicle transport, and ‘tropomyosin’ is critical for cytoskeletal function in non‐muscle cells (Venkatesh et al. 2020; Wang and Coluccio 2010). Some differentially expressed transcripts, such as ‘muscle‐specific protein 20’ and ‘actin‐5, muscle‐specific,’ raise the possibility of contamination from adhering muscle fibres during brain dissection. Despite efforts to avoid surrounding muscles during dissection, obtaining a completely pure brain sample without residual or closely associated muscle fibres remains challenging.

Moreover, the downregulated GO terms associated with muscle contraction in newly detached infected ants compared to uninfected ants, combined with the upregulation of motor and cytoskeletal‐related GO terms and KEGG pathways in detached infected ants compared to newly detached infected ants, suggest a potential role for muscle function in detachment. Newly detached infected ants may still exhibit impaired movement, whereas detached infected ants seem to regain their locomotor abilities.

Chill‐Coma Hypothesis

Insect chill coma is caused by cold‐induced depolarisation of the central nervous system cells through ion pump activity or ion permeability that leads to neuromuscular impairment (Findsen et al. 2014; MacMillan et al. 2014; Macmillan and Sinclair 2011; Robertson et al. 2017). The levels of extracellular potassium ion (K+) are associated with chill‐coma; an increase follows the paralytic state, which is reversed by a reduction to normal levels (Findsen et al. 2014; MacMillan et al. 2014). At the resting membrane potential, the intracellular K+ concentrations are higher than the extracellular concentration (Kano et al. 2019). The outward K+ flow hyperpolarises the cell membrane and decreases cell excitability.

We found that GO terms related to potassium channels were enriched in the downregulated transcripts of newly detached infected ants compared to attached infected ants and in the upregulated transcripts in pre‐attached infected ants compared to detached infected ants. However, we could not identify a clear pattern of extracellular K+ levels from the differentially expressed transcripts to directly support this hypothesis. It is possible that some ion pumps, such as sodium/potassium‐transporting ATPase, exhibit temperature‐dependent activity (Macmillan and Sinclair 2011). Although these pumps were not downregulated at the attached phase, their function might still be impaired due to cold exposure. It is possible that the SEG‐associated D. dendriticum larva triggers an early chill‐coma, and the reversal of the attachment behaviour may be caused by the recovery from the chill‐coma following temperature increase, causing the extracellular K+ levels to return to normal. Future studies could directly measure extracellular and intracellular K+ concentrations to determine whether elevated extracellular K+ is associated with the attachment behaviour and if the recovery from chill‐coma is associated with the detachment in infected ants.

Conclusions

This study uncovers potential underlying mechanisms involving an iconic, reversible host manipulation. The results of our study provide the first insight into a manipulator that does not just ‘turn‐on’ altered behaviours but also turns them off. The ant brain transcripts identified in this study related to metabolism, protein management, DNA repair, environmental sensing, biogenic monoamines and chill‐coma highlight the complexity of mechanisms that might be involved in this behaviour manipulation.
