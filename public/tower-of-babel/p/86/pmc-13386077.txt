Health-seeking behaviours and sociocultural factors influencing self-management of diabetes mellitus type 2 among women: a descriptive qualitative study from Islamabad, Pakistan
Authors: Sundus Majid, Babar Tasneem Shaikh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHealth-seeking behaviours among individuals with diabetes are complex and shaped by multiple factors, including financial constraints, cultural beliefs, attitudes towards the disease, access to healthcare and social support. This research explores the health-seeking behaviours of women with type 2 diabetes mellitus and the sociocultural factors influencing self-management of diabetes using the Social Ecological Model as a framework. The study aims to gain insight into improving the health-seeking behaviours of women with type 2 diabetes mellitus.MethodsThis study employs an in-depth descriptive qualitative approach which provides deep insights into the experiences and perspectives of diabetic women. Data collection involved using in-depth semistructured interviews from 14 type 2 diabetic women attending a public sector tertiary care hospital in Islamabad. Thematic analysis was used to identify key themes and insights from the data.ResultsThe findings revealed that multilevel factors such as individual, interpersonal, organisational, community and policy level factors influence health-seeking behaviours and self-management practices among diabetic women. There is non-adherence to self-management practices due to low motivation, financial constraints and time constraints. Medical treatment is thought to be crucial in controlling diabetes rather than bringing changes in behaviour.ConclusionsThe findings of this study will help in designing gender-sensitive and culturally appropriate interventions. Collaboration between healthcare providers and policymakers is required to design culturally sensitive interventions in order to educate and empower women to follow self-management practices.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a global public health challenge that is characterised by rising prevalence. This chronic condition requires constant attention and behaviour changes to prevent complications. Diabetes self-management refers to the daily tasks and actions that people with diabetes do to keep their condition under control. Key self-management activities include behaviour modifications in exercise, diet, medication adherence, blood glucose monitoring and foot care.1 Despite the importance of self-management in preventing complications, it is identified that recommended self-management practices are not followed.2

Diabetes management is challenging because patients with T2DM have to balance their available resources, personal values and behaviour choices to maintain a healthy diet, physical activity, treatment adherence and blood glucose monitoring.3 For women, managing T2DM can be even tougher because of various factors like affordability, access to healthcare, education, cultural expectations, family responsibilities and support systems.

Despite the known clinical benefits of self-management, individuals with T2DM often do not adhere to recommended practices. There are many factors that impact adherence to self-management practices such as patients’ behaviour behaviours including healthy eating habits and physical activity, inadequate knowledge, lack of communication with healthcare providers and psychological factors such as depression, etc.4 For effective self-management patients with T2DM need communication and coordination between themselves, their families, healthcare providers and other stakeholders.3

Health-seeking behaviour includes early recognition of symptoms, regular healthcare visits and adherence to prescribed treatments.5 Although we are aware that regular healthcare, education and medication adherence are important to prevent diabetes complications, it is also important to understand how people with T2DM seek healthcare.6 Health-seeking behaviour in people with diabetes is complex and influenced by many factors like finances, cultural beliefs, attitudes toward the disease, personal motivation, confidence in managing it, healthcare access and social support.6 Family support plays an important role in diabetes management by influencing self-care behaviours of patients with diabetes.7

Previous research works have mostly used clinical data, missing the nuanced personal and sociocultural meanings behind health-seeking behaviours.15 Qualitative research provides deeper insights into these behaviours and the factors that affect these behaviours and self-management practices.6

Women in Pakistan, who often act as primary caregivers, are disproportionately affected by the dual burden of familial responsibilities and chronic disease management. However, there is limited contextual understanding of the specific socio-cultural and structural barriers they encounter in managing T2DM, hindering the development of gender-sensitive and culturally appropriate diabetes care strategies. This descriptive qualitative study explores the health-seeking behaviours and sociocultural influences that shape how women manage T2DM. By examining women’s real-life experiences, the study aims to gain deeper insight into the obstacles they encounter, the support they rely on and how they manage day-to-day life with diabetes. Such understanding is critical for designing more effective and culturally appropriate interventions that address the unique needs of women living with T2DM.

Methodology

This was an in-depth descriptive qualitative study to understand the lived experiences and subjective meanings that women with T2DM ascribe to their condition and its management and to gain a deeper understanding of their individual experiences. In this study, it guided us to explore, in depth, how women with T2DM perceive, interpret and navigate their daily lives with the disease, focusing on the meanings they attach to illness, care and self-management. The area selected for this research was a public sector tertiary care hospital of Islamabad, that is, Pakistan Institute of Medical Sciences (PIMS). The study population consisted of women diagnosed with T2DM residing in Islamabad, Pakistan.

A purposive sampling technique was used to select participants. A total of 14 participants were interviewed until data saturation and sufficient depth of information were achieved. Participants for this study included women residing in Islamabad, aged 18 years and older, based on a confirmed diagnosis of T2DM, as documented in their medical records at the hospital and confirmed by their attending physicians. Women with T2DM and presenting with other serious comorbidities and complications (hypertension, cardiovascular diseases or ischaemic heart disease) were excluded to ensure that the findings specifically reflected health-seeking behaviours and self-management practices related to diabetes rather than being confounded by the additional burden of managing other chronic conditions. The presence of such comorbidities could significantly alter healthcare priorities, treatment complexity and self-management capacity, potentially obscuring the sociocultural factors uniquely influencing diabetes-specific behaviours.

In-depth semistructured interviews served as the primary data collection method. The interviews were conducted by the principal investigator (PI) within the outpatient department of a tertiary care hospital (PIMS). PI reached out and recruited the women who had been diagnosed with T2DM and were visiting the hospital for routine check-up or follow-up. All the interviews were conducted by the PI after taking a written informed consent. Interviews were conducted in Urdu and each interview lasted for 30 min on average. Interviews were audio recorded and additional field notes were taken after each interview. The primary data collection tool for this study was a semistructured interview guide developed based on the social ecological model (SEM) which provides a comprehensive framework for understanding the multiple levels of influence on health behaviours, including individual, interpersonal, organisational, community and policy factors. The interview guide (online supplemental file 1) included open-ended questions and the development of the interview guide was informed by a thorough review of qualitative studies on diabetes self-management. To ensure the credibility and relevance of the interview guide, a pilot study was conducted with a small sample of participants to assess the feasibility and clarity of the questions. Modifications were made based on participant feedback, particularly to ensure that language was accessible and culturally sensitive.

The data analysis for this study was conducted by the PI. The data analysis followed the six-phase inductive thematic analysis approach, which provides a flexible yet rigorous framework for identifying, analysing and reporting patterns within qualitative data.8 These phases included: (1) familiarisation with the data through repeated reading of transcripts, (2) generating initial codes, (3) searching for themes, (4) reviewing themes, (5) defining and naming themes and (6) producing the final report. The analysis was guided by the SEM, which served as a theoretical lens to organise emerging themes.

To ensure familiarisation with the data, the PI engaged in an iterative process of reading and re-reading the transcripts to achieve immersion in the participants’ experiences and narratives. Initially, all interviews were transcribed verbatim, first in Urdu and then in English. Microsoft Word was used to organise and manage the transcripts, while coding and theme development were conducted using a combination of printed transcripts and Excel spreadsheets. Each transcript was coded line by line in the margins to capture significant statements and ideas related to health-seeking behaviours, sociocultural factors, diet and physical activity in the context of T2DM self-management. Codes were then transferred to Excel, where they were sorted, grouped and categorised into potential themes. Through iterative coding and constant comparison, themes emerged from the data. These themes were organised and tabulated according to the levels of the SEM, creating a visual framework that linked codes to subthemes and main themes (see table 1). Finally, the themes were refined and interpreted in relation to existing literature and the study’s theoretical framework. The findings were presented in a narrative format, linking the themes to the research questions and illustrating how sociocultural factors and health-seeking behaviours influence T2DM self-management among women in Islamabad (For pages citations, see online supplemental file 2).

Results

This study provides insights into the health-seeking behaviours and patterns of women with T2DM and how the sociocultural factors influence these behaviours and self-management practices. Thematic analysis of the transcripts revealed five major themes based on the levels of the SEM. Health-seeking behaviours of women with T2DM and the sociocultural factors influencing self-management of diabetes were identified.

The participants were in the age bracket of 35–63 years, having literacy levels ranging from no formal education to graduate, and a majority of them were home-makers.

Individual level

Theme 1: knowledge, beliefs, practices and health-seeking behaviour towards diabetes care

Knowledge and understanding about diabetes and its management is important for effective self-management. Most of the participants had basic knowledge about diabetes and its management. Participants shared diverse beliefs about the causes of diabetes. As one participant mentioned:

The main cause of diabetes is unhealthy diet such as desserts, fast food. (age around 50, no formal education)

Others believed that diabetes occurs if you have a family history of diabetes.

My father had diabetes so I think it was in my genes. (age early40s, intermediate)

Timely diagnosis is important in order to prevent diabetic complications. The experiences of diabetes diagnosis varied among participants. Weakness and fatigue were among the most common symptoms. One participant shared their experience saying:

I felt weakness and fatigue. I went to the doctor for checkup and he did all the tests. Then I was diagnosed with diabetes. (age early 60s, grade 10)

Other common symptoms were reported as body pains, dry mouth and frequent urination. The participants highlighted their experiences as:

I felt pain in my legs and I noticed frequent urination and dry mouth. My mouth used to be very dry at night. I knew about these symptoms because I have had a family history of diabetes. I went to the hospital and was diagnosed with diabetes. (age mid 40s, grade 10)

In contrast, some participants experienced no symptoms. As one participant shared:

One day I woke up in the morning and fell down. Then I visit the hospital and the tests were done and I was diagnosed with diabetes. (age around 50, no formal education)

Effective diabetes self-management requires regular checkups. Participants revealed different behaviours regarding regular hospital visits for diabetes management. Most women perceived regular hospital visits as unnecessary and reported visiting the hospital when facing some serious medical issue. The following quote illustrates this behaviour as:

I don’t visit hospital regularly for diabetes checkup because the doctor only increases or decreases insulin dosage which I have learnt with time. (age mid 30s, graduate)

The interviews highlighted different attitudes and behaviours towards diabetes self-management. Most participants highlighted their efforts towards dietary adjustments. One participant mentioned:

I avoid sugary foods, juices and soft drinks. (age mid 30s, graduate)

Participants also highlighted difficulties in following healthy eating practices as:

I avoid eating sugary foods but sometimes it becomes difficult. When I crave for sweet foods I eat in small quantity. (age early 60s, grade 10)

The interviews showed a consistent trend of non-adherence to physical activity and blood glucose monitoring. One participant mentioned:

I do not check my blood glucose level regularly because it gives me stress if I find out that I am hyperglycemic. (age early 50s, intermediate). Another participant added:

I know physical activity is important in managing diabetes but due to household responsibilities, I don’t find time for myself. I don’t have any help, I have to do everything on my own cooking, cleaning etc. (age mid 30s, intermediate)

A clear trend was observed in treatment adherence. Almost all women emphasised the importance of medication in diabetes management and showed positive behaviours towards medication adherence.

I take medicines regularly because it is important for controlling blood glucose level. (age early 60s, grade 10)

Interpersonal level

Theme 2: influence of interpersonal relations on seeking healthcare and management practices

Families and social relations often play an essential role in shaping healthcare decisions of patients with diabetes. As one woman said:

I discussed my symptoms with my neighbor who is a diabetic patient, she advised me to consult a doctor. I went to the hospital and found out that I had diabetes. (age early 40s, graduate)

Most women mentioned that their family support them in buying medications, taking them for checkups, medication adherence and encourage them for physical activity and avoiding sugary foods.

My husband takes care of my diet and supports me in visiting hospital for regular checkups. (age early 50s, primary)

Participants highlighted healthcare providers as a key source of knowledge about diabetes management. As one participant said:

I did not know about the symptoms of diabetes and its complications. I came to know when I was diagnosed with diabetes and the doctor told me about its complications and all. (age mid 50s, no formal education)

Organisational level

Theme 3: accessibility and availability of healthcare services

Access to healthcare services impacts people’s behaviour of seeking medical care. Participants mentioned that they avoid going to the hospital regularly for diabetes management because they have to wait for long times. Some participants mentioned this as:

Whenever I visit hospital I have to wait for several hours. That’s why I avoid going to the hospital. (age mid 30s, intermediate)

Participants shared their experiences on the availability of medicines and insulin within the health facility as:

Medicines are easily available. I never faced any difficulty. (age early 60s, graduate)

Some participants mentioned the unavailability of medicines sometimes

Sometimes insulin is not available so I have to buy it from private medical stores. (age around 50, no formal education)

Community level

Theme 4: influence of cultural norms and practices on seeking healthcare and management practices

Most participants said that in Pakistani culture, people like to eat traditional foods rich in carbohydrates and fats and due to traditional dietary habits, it becomes difficult to follow the recommended diet.

It is very difficult to control diet. Our traditional foods are rich in carbohydrates and fats and one cannot avoid these foods completely. (age around 50, no formal education)

Participants mentioned that in Pakistani culture women do not give importance to their health and they are dependent on male members of the family for accessing healthcare services.

In our society women are supposed to do household chores and take care of everyone at home. They usually ignore their own health for their family. (age mid 30s, intermediate)

Policy level

Theme 5: policy barriers towards self-management

Financial burden was highlighted as a barrier towards diabetes self-management. Women mentioned that due to high costs of medicines and healthy food, it has become difficult for them to afford the recommended diet and medicines. One woman elaborated:

Insulin and medicines have become very expensive now. It is very difficult to afford medicines. The prices of medicines should decrease as everyone cannot afford. (age around 50, no formal education)

Participants mentioned that they had no idea about diabetes education programme and emphasised the need that such programmes should be conducted. Participants mentioned this as:

I have not seen any public health campaign or educational program about diabetes. Only doctors and nurses provide information about diabetes when I visit the hospital. (age mid 30s, intermediate)

Discussion

This descriptive qualitative study explores the health-seeking behaviours and socio-cultural influences that shape how women manage T2DM. Our findings reveal that women’s ability to manage diabetes is shaped by a complex interplay of multilevel factors, including individual knowledge gaps and low motivation; interpersonal supports and constraints; healthcare access barriers; cultural norms that prioritise family needs over self-care; and policy-level challenges such as medication costs and a lack of structured education programmes.

Five major themes emerged from the findings, grounded in the Social-Ecological Model.

The study revealed basic knowledge and understanding about diabetes and its management among participants. This aligns with a study conducted in Fiji that reported good understanding of diabetes self-management practices among patients.9 Most participants experienced symptoms such as weakness and fatigue, frequent urination, dry mouth and body pains that led to their initial diagnosis. However, most participants did not relate these symptoms to diabetes unless they had family history. This finding is consistent with a study from Nepal that also reported symptomatic presentation of patients at the time of initial diagnosis.10

Most women perceived regular hospital visits as unnecessary and only sought care when experiencing serious health issues. This finding aligns with a study conducted in Pakistan that also reported irregular patterns of seeking medical care.4

Findings showed mixed attitudes and behaviours towards diabetes self-management. Medication adherence was common, driven by fear of complications and the belief that diabetes cannot be controlled without medication similar to findings in Iran.11 The findings revealed participants’ efforts in controlling their diet. However, it was found difficult to follow recommended diet plans and avoid their favourite foods. This aligns with a study that reported difficulty following recommended dietary practices among patients with diabetess.12 Physical inactivity was common, as also reported by Montazeri et al.11 Many lacked regular glucose monitoring due to needle fear or no felt need, which aligns with a study that also showed non-compliance to regular blood glucose monitoring.6

At the interpersonal level, the findings revealed that women received support from family and friends in terms of finances, hospital visits and medication reminders. A qualitative study from Uganda also showed similar findings that diabetic patients’ families supported them in self-management practices and patients perceived this support as an important enabler of diabetes self-management.7 This study reported family or friends as an important source of information for diabetes symptoms.

Participants also reported unavailability of medicines or insulin within the health facility. This finding was consistent with a previous study that also mentioned shortage in supply of medicines at health facilities, increasing financial burden.13 Long waiting times at healthcare facilities affected regular hospital visits that align with a study that also reported long waiting times as a barrier in seeking regular medical care.12

Cultural practices such as traditional eating habits impacted dietary management. This finding is consistent with a study conducted previously that showed that traditional foods at events and social gatherings act as a barrier towards healthy eating.14 In Pakistani culture, women are expected to prioritise the needs of their families. In addition, women are dependent on men financially and in accessing healthcare. These cultural factors pose challenges for women to access regular care and to follow self-management practices. These findings are supported by a recent study conducted in the UK among South Asian women that also showed unique cultural barriers that women face in managing T2DM.15 These findings have highlighted the need for culturally appropriate interventions.

High costs of medicines and healthy food were barriers towards self-management, especially for low-income women. Costs of consultation, tests and transport also hindered routine checkups. Previous studies have also shown economic barriers such as medication cost, healthy diet and transport costs towards health-seeking practices.13 16 Another study from Germany also reported lack of education programmes about diabetes, and concluded that providers must educate their patients on self-examination to avoid complications.14 Our study too highlighted a lack of diabetes education programmes and emphasised the need to conduct such programmes in order to raise awareness about T2DM and its complications.

Future studies should explore and investigate how family-inclusive education can improve diabetes outcomes for women in similar settings. Healthcare providers should integrate gender-sensitive counselling, simplify education using culturally relevant dietary examples and advocate for system-level changes such as reduced wait times, affordable medication and community-led diabetes awareness programmes to empower women in their self-management journey.

A key strength of our study is its use of a multi-level SEM to uncover the complex, intertwined individual, social and systemic barriers that women with type 2 diabetes face in a specific cultural context. Nevertheless, there are a few limitations of this study. Our study included only 14 women from a single public hospital in Islamabad, which limits the generalisability of the findings to other regions or healthcare settings in Pakistan or similar contexts. Moreover, participants were recruited from a tertiary care hospital and may not represent the experiences of women who seek care in primary or private settings or those who do not access healthcare regularly. Our study relied on self-reported interview data, which is subject to recall bias and social desirability bias, especially regarding sensitive topics such as adherence to self-management practices.

Conclusions

This study explored health-seeking behaviours and socio-cultural factors influencing diabetes self-management among women with T2DM. Women face unique barriers despite the availability of healthcare, such as lack of awareness, limited access and financial constraints. Managing diabetes requires understanding social and cultural contexts. These findings support the need for gender-sensitive and culturally appropriate interventions. Health education programmes should be simple and culturally relevant, focusing on diet, activity and medication adherence. Further research is needed to examine the roles of education, income and family in self-management. Policies and systems must adapt to women’s needs. Training for healthcare workers on gender-specific challenges, accessible services and practical programmes is essential. Collaboration between public and private sectors is needed to support such initiatives. By taking these steps, we can help women better manage their diabetes and live healthier lives. This approach can also inspire solutions for managing other chronic diseases in similar communities.
