Real-time robust autofocus method enabling sustained intravital scanning light field imaging
Authors: Yuedi Wang, Jingyao Wu, Jiamin Wu, Yuan Li, Wenjin Lv, Fangfei Yu, Jun Yan, Zhi Lu, Yi Yang, Qionghai Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent advances in computational microscopy enable highspeed high-resolution intravital 3D imaging with low phototoxicity. However, inevitable sample vibration and tissue deformation in multi-cellular organisms make it extremely challenging to maintain samples stably in focus over long-term even with an extended effective depth of field. Here, we propose a real-time robust autofocus method based on scanning light-field microscopy (AFsLF), enabling sustained high-speed 3D imaging of diverse samples across several days by continuously tracking the sample focal plane without hardware modifications. Based on the intrinsic disparity of light-field angular measurements, AFsLF estimates the focal plane with less than 2 µm error over a 500 µm depth range, completing within 0.1 s, 300-time faster than previous methods. We validate AFsLF across diverse tissues and challenging conditions, including low excitation power, multichannel illumination, and large axial displacements, enabling stable, long-term, multichannel subcellular imaging of neural activities and immune responses in mouse brain and liver.Subject terms: Microscopy, Imaging

Introduction

In vivo imaging provides direct visualization and quantitative analysis for studying biological mechanisms, facilitating drug development, and advancing translational medicine1,2. Light field microscopy (LFM), with its highspeed volumetric imaging capability and exceptional photon efficiency, offers unique advantages for intravital imaging and has thus been widely adopted in neuroscience, immunology, oncology, and cell biology3–7. However, conventional LFM suffers from limited spatial resolution due to constraints imposed by the spatial-bandwidth product. The fast drift of image plane formed scanning light field microscopy (sLFM), which achieves near-diffraction-limited resolution8. Recent advances in sLFM techniques have enabled long term mesoscale intravital imaging with minimal phototoxicity and increased 3D field of view (FOV)9–16.

Despite the extended depth of field (DOF) provided by sLFM, sustained subcellular imaging often encounters intrinsic morphological changes across 3D space. Excessive axial movement exceeds the DOF of the sLFM, which degrades image sharpness and compromises 3D reconstruction quality, hindering for continuous, high-fidelity imaging. Therefore, a real-time robust autofocus method is essential to compensate for focal drift over prolonged imaging sessions. In recent years, multifarious autofocus techniques have been proposed to mitigate defocus blur. One category involves hardware-based methods, such as axial scanning to construct a focus map from Z-axis images17, or inferring defocus via additive illumination light18–25 and multi-camera systems26–28. While effective, these approaches typically increase systematic complexity, cost, and the acquisition time. Alternatively, image-based algorithms offer digital solutions for autofocus. Metrics such as the Tanimoto coefficient29 and deep learning30–32 have been applied to infer the focal plane. However, most image-based strategies require capturing multiple in-focus and out-of-focus images, which remains time-consuming. To overcome this, single-shot deep learning-based autofocus methods have been introduced33–36. For example, user-defined refocusing based on 2D microscopy images33, and MobileNetV3-based defocus estimation trained on single-frame data34, have shown promising results. A deep-learning-based offline autofocusing method transfers the defocused image into an in-focus image without any prior knowledge of defocus magnitude, direction, or point spread function (PSF)35. Despite these advances, these learning-based methods are dependent on overfitting of sample structures, leading to poor generalization and practicality. In fact, sLFM inherently possesses strong physical priors embedded in the high-dimensional spatial-angular domain, which holds promise for guiding accurate defocus estimation with broad generalization capability by analyzing the disparities among angular views. Previous exploration has been conducted with digital adaptive optics (DAO) modelling14; however, the curse of dimensionality leads to prohibitively slow computations, with each estimation taking several tens of seconds, rendering the approach impractical for real-time applications. Therefore, although a bunch of autofocus methods have been proposed, concerns persist regarding their reliability and practicality during long term volumetric imaging.

In this work, we develop a real-time, robust autofocus method for scanning light field microscopy (AFsLF), to address the defocus problem in sustained intravital imaging. By skillfully exploiting angular redundancy and simplified geometric properties to represent the underlying spatial-angular correlations, our method rapidly estimates the defocus distance through wave-optics-based analysis of light field shifts and corrects focal drift in real time using a motorized translation stage, eliminating the need for traditional Z-stack acquisition. Compared with existing DAO-based autofocus algorithm for sLFM, AFsLF reduces the computation time for defocus estimation from 33.7 s to 0.1 s. Extensive experimental results demonstrate that AFsLF can accurately distinguish between positive and negative defocus, as well as estimate and correct the defocus distance up to 500 µm. Furthermore, AFsLF is universally compatible with various commercial sLFM systems including sLFM9, confocal scanning light field microscopy (csLFM)13 and RUSH3D15, multiple imaging configurations including varying light intensities, illumination wavelengths, angular view configurations, and diverse biological samples including living mouse spleen, liver, brain and tissue slices, enabling fully automated, long-duration intravital imaging with consistent in-focus high resolution.

Results

Principle of AFsLF

A typical optical configuration of sLFM consists of an objective lens, tube lens, and a microlens array (MLA) placed at the intermediate image plane, which enables simultaneous acquisition of multiple spatial-angular images for rapid 3D imaging (Fig. 1a). A critical challenge in achieving real-time autofocus and stable long term intravital imaging at high resolution lies in efficiently extracting defocus information from the high-dimensional and redundant spatial-angular measurements. The acquired multi-angular images (x, y, u, v) are orders of magnitude larger than the required defocus distance value z, leading to substantial computational costs and a high risk of overfitting. While extensive angular information is essential for 3D reconstruction14,37, it becomes redundant and inefficient for real-time autofocus.

To address this, we investigate the physical characteristics of light propagation under both in-focus and out-of-focus conditions. Specifically, when the sample is in focus (as indicated by the green lines), light rays emitted from the same object point pass through a single microlens L(i,j), resulting in no apparent image shift between the central view (CV) and other angular views after realignment. In contrast, when the sample is out of focus (as indicated by the yellow lines), rays from the same object point diverge to neighboring microlenses L(i,j) and L(i+1,j), leading to discernible image shift exists. For example, a defocused fluorescent bead appears blurred and induces measurable displacements between angular views, which correspond to larger epipolar plane image gradient (Fig. 1b). Conversely, an in-focus bead shows negligible angular disparity, and the gradient of epipolar plane approaches 0. These observations reveal that only two angular views are sufficient to implicitly characterize the degree of defocus, minimizing angular redundancy in sLFM.

Building upon this principle, we develop AFsLF, a lightweight and robust autofocus method for sLFM. AFsLF estimates defocus distance by analyzing the relative image shift between CV and an edge angular view, which is calculated using the Enhanced Correlation Coefficient (ECC) registration algorithm, then compensates for the defocus by inversely moving the sample to the focal plane using a motorized positioning stage (Fig. 1c). The framework leverages sparse angular information for defocus estimation, substantially reducing computation burden compared to existing methods that require full angular integration and Zernike coefficient fitting (Fig. 1d-e), while using the globally shared structural information in the two views for disparity estimation, rather than requiring manually trackable puncta or a specific biological morphology. To better contextualize the advances of AFsLF within existing autofocus techniques, we have built a comprehensive quantitative benchmark that includes the previously discussed DAO-based approach15, the method using pixel difference with the Tanimoto coefficient (PDTC) on image stacks29 and our AFsLF presented in this work. Experiments are carried out on ten datasets across three axial positions with running time, CPU utilization, and defocus error quantified systematically. AFsLF achieves real-time processing at ~0.1 s per frame, representing a two-orders-of-magnitude improvement in speed over existing DAO-based autofocus methods for sLFM and also significantly outperforming the PDTC, while maintaining optimal autofocus accuracy and acceptable CPU utilization, which is essential for sustained intravital high resolution imaging (Fig. 1f-g; Supplementary Figs. 1; 2). Notably, “real-time” of AFsLF refers to feedback that is sufficiently fast to compensate biologically relevant axial drift during intravital imaging, rather than zero-latency correction or one autofocus operation for every imaging frame (Supplementary Fig. 3; Supplementary Note 1).

In practical biological imaging, the displacement between the central view (CV) and another angular view is estimated by ECC-based global image registration (Supplementary Fig. 4) rather than by tracking specific bright spots. The peripheral angular view is iteratively warped to maximize the normalized global correlation coefficient with the CV, and the converged x-direction shift is converted into the defocus distance through pre calibration. Because this strategy relies on the global structural information distributed across the full field of view, AFsLF remains broadly applicable to samples with distinct morphology, including point-like, line-like, planar, texture-dense and texture-sparse structures (Supplementary Fig. 5). This global-registration mechanism also improves robustness against intensity fluctuation, photobleaching and local structural variation during long term imaging.

Experimental characterization of AFsLF

Directly estimating the defocus distance from conventional images is inherently challenging. As the impulse response of an optical system, the PSF generally reflects the defocus through its spatial shape, in which the radius increases as the sample moves away from the focal plane. Consequently, imaging results of a defocused USAF chart become increasingly blurred (Fig. 2a). For example, line pairs and letters are clearly resolved at the focal plane, but even a defocus of 30 µm leads to noticeable degradation. The blur caused by the defocus not only deteriorates the light field images, but also makes it difficult to distinguish whether the sample is located above or below the focal plane, which is a common problem for manual focusing and image-based algorithms. Fortunately, AFsLF leverages spatial-angular priors and evaluates the disparity between angular views, enabling reliable estimation of defocus even when spatial resolution is severely degraded. Our approach facilitates accurate measurement and compensation of defocus, regardless of image blurriness.

To evaluate AFsLF performance in real biological experiments, we conducted imaging in the immune microenvironment of the mouse spleen. Images of neutrophils and macrophages acquired with and without AFsLF correction were compared. Specifically, the Ly-6G-labeled neutrophils and F4/80-labeled macrophages is the representative markers contributing to disparity estimation. AFsLF provides evident resolution enhancement, as demonstrated by the extension of the spatial frequency range and improved signal intensity (Fig. 2b-c). Among them, R0 and R1 are the cutoff frequencies of the corresponding images, respectively. Across a large dataset, consistent contrast enhancement further validates the robustness and general applicability of our method (Fig. 2d).

Next, we assessed how objective magnification affects the performance of AFsLF. Experiments were performed using two objective lenses with 20× and 60× magnifications. Higher magnification reduces the DOF, making real-time autofocusing increasingly critical for sustained imaging. Quantitatively, AFsLF accurately estimates and corrects defocus distances up to 500 µm for the 20× objective lens and 250 µm for the 60× objective lens, both of which substantially exceed the intrinsic DOF of the objectives (typically <5 µm). In both cases, the prediction error remains less than 2 µm, indicating high precision and strong generalization (Fig. 2e). Furthermore, we evaluated the runtime of AFsLF across multiple sLFM systems. Even on large-scale imaging platforms such as RUSH3D, the autofocus process remains under 0.1 s, demonstrating the real-time capability and scalability of our method (Fig. 2f).

AFsLF demonstrates strong robustness under real imaging conditions

During practical experiments, acquired sLFM images are often far from ideal due to uncertainties in imaging conditions, such as varying illumination intensities, angular view configurations, sample structures, and excitation wavelengths, which pose significant challenges to post-processing autofocus algorithms. AFsLF, however, exhibits exceptional robustness owing to its unique framework design. Firstly, AFsLF fully exploits spatial priors derived from spatial-angular constraints to infer defocused distances. These high-dimensional physical priors remain invariant under different imaging conditions and serve as a reliable guide for the autofocusing process. Secondly, the intrinsic angular redundancy in sLFM promotes computational resilience. Even when a single defocus estimation is suboptimal, it can be rapidly corrected via iterative refinement. Thirdly, unlike deep neural network-based approaches that often suffer from data dependency or overfitting, AFsLF adopts a lightweight disparity-based computation between angular views, making it broadly applicable across diverse samples and conditions.

We experimentally evaluate the robustness of AFsLF across a range of imaging conditions. During long term biological observation, light intensity needs to be continually adjusted for enhancing signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) or minimizing phototoxicity, which demands autofocus methods to remain stable under both high and low illumination. AFsLF successfully corrects out-of-focus images acquired under varying light intensities, maintaining consistent performance (Fig. 3a-b). Although minor fluctuations are observed at extremely low light levels, the estimated defocus-distance curves remained approximately linear. In both positive and negative axial shifts, AFsLF consistently provides well-focused results across multiple sLFM systems, including sLFM, csLFM and RUSH3D (Fig. 3c-e).

Additionally, different sLFM systems operate with varying angular sampling densities, necessitating angular robustness from the autofocus method. Since AFsLF estimates the defocus distance using only two angular views, we investigate how view interval impacts AFsLF accuracy. Defocus estimation accuracy increases with larger view intervals. Our results show that a view interval of 3 is sufficient to achieve high accuracy (Fig. 3f-g). Considering that sLFM inherently supports such intervals, AFsLF remains robust to changes in angular configurations.

Furthermore, AFsLF proves to be compatible with a wide range of specimen types and illumination wavelengths. Whether applied to fixed tissue slices or live organs with complex immune microenvironments, AFsLF effectively corrects defocus and restores high resolution (Fig. 3h-i). To further validate its sustained stability, we monitored autofocusing performance during multi-wavelength intravital imaging. AFsLF consistently estimated similar defocus values across multiple fluorescence channels from the same sample (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 6). Notably, even when autofocusing was performed solely on the 640-nm channel, defocus compensation remained stable for up to 6 h, with a drift of only ~1 μm, thus meeting the stringent requirements for long term intravital imaging (Fig. 3k).

AFsLF stably facilitates sustained intravital immune observations in multiple mouse organs

In mammalian organs such as the brain, spleen, and liver, diverse immune cells engage in sustained intravital migration and interaction, forming intricate immune microenvironments that defend against external threats and maintain homeostasis38. Continuous observation of these immune dynamics requires highly stable imaging techniques. However, conventional microscopy suffers from high phototoxicity and a lack of autofocusing mechanisms, both of which severely limit the duration of immune imaging. AFsLF addresses this gap by providing real-time, robust autofocusing capabilities with low phototoxicity and photobleaching.

To evaluate the performance of AFsLF under real biological conditions, we first conduct continuous one-hour imaging of the spleen in living mice. As the largest immune organ, the spleen hosts a wide variety of immune cells and intense cell-cell interactions. Using sLFM and csLFM, we capture populational dynamics of neutrophils and macrophages in vessels of the mouse spleen (Supplementary Fig. 7). Without AFsLF, immune cells rapidly drifted out of focus, with noticeable blurring observed as early as 20 minutes, and most cells disappeared from view after 1 h. In contrast, with AFsLF enabled, image quality remain consistently sharp and stable throughout the entire duration. Furthermore, we performed 12-hour long-term continuous intravital imaging of the dorsal skin of live mice in a skin injury model with csLFM, during which prominent aggregation and infiltration of neutrophils were observed within the injured skin region (Supplementary Fig. 8; Supplementary Movie 1).

We then monitor living mouse livers labelled with neutrophils, macrophages, and vasculature using sLFM. To conduct a direct comparison, we synchronize hardware acquisition such that odd-numbered frames are acquired without autofocus, while even-numbered frames undergo autofocusing with AFsLF before imaging. This strategy allows side-by-side temporal comparisons from the same sample at identical timepoints. The results show that without AFsLF, cellular details quickly blurred and disappeared. With AFsLF, macrophages, known for their low motility, remain clearly resolved and unchanged for the entire duration, verifying the effectiveness of our method (Fig. 4a-b, Supplementary Movie 2). To further evaluate AFsLF, we conduct a 6-hour continuous intravital imaging session of the immune microenvironment in living mouse livers using RUSH3D. For a controlled evaluation, AFsLF is activated during the first 3 h and deactivated during the last 3 h. With AFsLF enabled, immune cells remain well focused and morphologically intact. Upon disabling AFsLF, cells gradually defocus by ~8 μm, leading to image degradation (Supplementary Fig. 9a–c). Quantitative analysis shows a concurrent decline in the number of detectable neutrophils and macrophages in the FOV during the latter 3 h (Supplementary Fig. 9d). Moreover, long-range cellular migration trajectories can be faithfully tracked only in the AFsLF-enabled frames, which would otherwise be lost without autofocusing (Supplementary Fig. 9e).

Additionally, we observe genetically expressed microglia in the cortex of CX3CR1-GFP transgenic mice. With AFsLF enabled, we achieve large-scale, high resolution time-lapse imaging and accurate cell tracking (Fig. 4c–e, Supplementary Movie 3). Quantitatively, single-cell resolution is maintained throughout the entire imaging session, confirming the precision of AFsLF (Fig. 4f–g). Together, these results demonstrate that AFsLF effectively maintains imaging stability across multiple organs during sustained intravital imaging, enabling new avenues for cellular behavioral and functional studies in vivo over extended timescales.

Limitations

The current version of AFsLF still has a temporal boundary, although the performance is promising. Specifically, AFsLF operates with a feedback latency of 0.1 s, and the experimentally validated axial correction range reaches up to 500 μm for the 20× objective and 250 μm for the 60× objective. Accordingly, if the sample undergoes an exceptionally rapid axial displacement that exceeds the corresponding correction range within a single autofocus cycle, the subsequent update may fail to fully recover the focusing image. Such cases may occur under extreme mechanical perturbation or unusually violent sample motion. However, in our intravital imaging experiments, axial tissue motion was much slower and smaller in magnitude, and we did not observe more than 500 μm displacement within one autofocus cycle under common intravital imaging. Therefore, this limitation defines the current operating boundary of AFsLF for exceptionally fast axial motion, but does not affect the biological scenarios investigated in the present research.

To assess the boundary conditions of AFsLF, we further characterized the performance under extended axial displacements with a 20× objective lens (Supplementary Fig. 10). AFsLF remains accurate and robust focusing across an axial range of 100 to 600 μm. In contrast, the method fails to recover clear in-focus structures and estimated defocus distances deviating substantially from ground truth at axial displacements of 700 μm or greater. This failure is attributable to the sample being displaced too far from the focal plane such that angular views no longer retain sufficiently discernible structural features to support robust disparity matching, which confirms that while AFsLF offers a substantially extended practical autofocus range, the performance is inherently bounded only under extreme axial defocus.

More broadly, the performance of AFsLF fundamentally depends on whether the disparity estimation from sparse angular views is reliable. While the method exhibits robust performance with conventional biological samples under typical dynamic imaging scenarios, the effective operating range may be constrained with extreme sample properties or rare imaging conditions. For samples undergoing extremely rapid axial displacement, the system is unable to reliably track and compensate for defocus when the axial displacement during a single autofocus update cycle exceeds the effective correction range of AFsLF. For samples with extremely strong scattering, strong scattering can attenuate directional information and cause the reduction of the disparity cues used for defocus estimation, which reduces the operating range of AFsLF. For non-sparse samples, especially those with highly regular or periodic arrangements, repeated structures can cause ambiguity in view-to-view matching and limit the unambiguous range for disparity calculation. For low-SNR imaging scenarios, particularly our robustness tests under varying illumination intensities (Fig. 3a-b) validate that the algorithm remains reliable and high performance autofocus under low-SNR conditions, even keeping fully effective at the minimum output intensity of the laser system. For samples with ultra-low SNR, the inability to reliably resolve intrinsic structural features across angular views will directly compromise the precision of cross-view matching and disparity estimation, which thus reduces autofocus accuracy and robustness.

A further practical constraint of the current implementation is that the real-time performance of AFsLF depends not only on the defocus-estimation algorithm itself, but also on the mechanical characteristics of the motorized translation stage used for axial correction. Because AFsLF compensates the estimated defocus by physically translating the sample, the achievable autofocus precision and temporal responsiveness are jointly constrained by the minimum incremental motion, positioning repeatability and moving speed of the stage. In practice, these hardware specifications should be matched to the operating mode of the algorithm, such that the stage can resolve the estimated axial displacement with sufficient precision and complete the correction within the imaging interval. A stage with large axial steps, poorer repeatability or slower response would introduce motion quantization, positioning error and temporal lag, and may therefore compromise the autofocus accuracy and stability. Accordingly, the performance demonstrated in this research may not be directly reproduced on lower-precision or slower motorized stages.

Besides, the axial movement range of the motorized translation stage is a considerable factor of the performance of AFsLF. Although AFsLF can correct substantial defocus within the tested range, samples with larger and more sustained axial displacements may exceed the available stage travel range or the amount of compensation that can be completed within the imaging interval. Due to the present system relies on a high precision and highspeed motorized stage to maintain the present autofocus performance, which may increase the whole cost of hardware. Therefore, one of the important future works in the future is to address this limitation by combining lower-cost translation stages with improved control-feedback strategies to extend the practical correction range while reducing the hardware burden.

Discussion

sLFM is an emerging imaging technique that enables sustained in vivo imaging at high speed and subcellular resolution. However, axial sample drift caused by intrinsic biological motion remains a significant challenge during extended imaging sessions, often resulting in defocused imaging. To address this, we investigate the relationship between spatial-angular light propagation and sample axial displacement, revealing the presence of substantial angular redundancy in sLFM systems. Building on the physical priors, we propose AFsLF, a real-time robust autofocus method tailored for sLFM. Unlike existing DAO-based autofocus approach15 that rely on computationally intensive integration across all angular views, AFsLF implicitly captures high dimensional spatial-angular priors using only two angular views. The strategy significantly reduces the computational burden while maintaining accuracy, enabling rapid autofocus with a feedback latency of ~0.1 s, which provides a two-orders-of-magnitude speed improvement over DAO-based autofocus methods and a substantial advantage over PDTC29, while delivering acceptable CPU utilization and superior estimation accuracy.

To maximize its applicability, we validated AFsLF across a broad range of sLFM systems. Even on the RUSH3D system, which features the largest FOV at multi-millimeter scale, our method can also be executed under 0.1 s per frame. AFsLF maintained stable performance under diverse imaging conditions, including variations in light intensity, illumination wavelength and objective magnification (20× and 60×), with prediction errors consistently less than 2 μm across a 500 μm axial range. We extensively evaluated AFsLF across a variety of biological samples, including human blood, human skin, epithelium, equisetum spores, and mouse spleen, and demonstrated successful autofocus correction universally. Furthermore, AFsLF enabled over several h of continuous observation of the liver and brain in living mice. In contrast, severe image blur occurred during control experiments without autofocus, highlighting the critical need for real-time correction in long term imaging.

Despite its promising performance, the current version of AFsLF has several limitations. Firstly, long term imaging inevitably leads to accumulated light dose, potentially causing phototoxicity and photobleaching. Future work may mitigate this by combining AFsLF with low-light imaging strategies and denoising algorithms39. Secondly, sample-induced optical aberrations would deflect angular rays and affect defocus estimation accuracy. Integrating DAO with AFsLF holds promise for correcting these aberrations40. Thirdly, despite the implementation of autofocus, the imaging DOF is not intrinsically increased; thus, for thick specimens, it remains necessary to incorporate additional techniques for DOF extension41. Fourthly, AFsLF currently supports only axial correction. Future integration with motorized stages of higher degrees of freedom and intelligent algorithms may extend AFsLF to real-time 3D localization, enabling more comprehensive tracking of target structures in 3D space.

With long-term stable imaging capability, AFsLF facilitates intravital biological studies. Previous work has demonstrated that hepatic macrophages actively mediate neutrophil clearance in ischemia-stressed mouse and human livers2. However, the related researches were only able to capture the terminal state of neutrophils already engulfed within macrophages. Conventional imaging methods including the DAO-based autofocus approach cannot remain sustained focal stability and subcellular spatiotemporal resolution during long term intravital imaging of the liver due to severe respiratory motion and physiological organ drift, which causes the complete and sequential dynamic process of macrophage-mediated neutrophil engulfment in the native hepatic microenvironment has never been visualized in vivo before. With the superior sustained focal stability and subcellular spatiotemporal resolution provided by AFsLF, we successfully captured a hepatic macrophage sequentially engulfing two neutrophils in the living mouse liver metastasis model for the first time (Supplementary Fig. 11; Supplementary Movie 4). The results show the whole dynamic engulfment process via continuous intravital imaging and provide unprecedented direct spatiotemporal evidence of macrophage-mediated neutrophil clearance in the intact native liver microenvironment.

AFsLF is particularly suited to long-term intravital 3D imaging in which cumulative axial drift, tissue deformation and physiological motion progressively degrade focus over tens of minutes to h. AFsLF preserves the intrinsic advantages of sLFM, including high volumetric throughput, low phototoxicity, multichannel compatibility and subcellular resolution 3D imaging, while providing real time defocus estimation and correction with the latency of 0.1 s and an error less than 2 μm over a 500 μm range, Consequently, AFsLF is well matched to the research of immune-cell trafficking and cell-cell interactions in the spleen and liver, vascular–immune interactions, tumor-cell circulation, and neuroimmune or microglial dynamics in the cortex, and large FOV tracking of cell populations and migration trajectories at the organ level. Compared with sLFM without autofocus, AFsLF mainly improves temporal continuity and focal stability during long term imaging. Compared with point-scanning volumetric methods, AFsLF-enabled sLFM is advantageous when lower phototoxicity and sustained 3D throughput are more important than the optical sectioning or penetration depth. In dense or scattering samples, AFsLF can be combined with confocal sLFM13 to further suppress background; whereas for highly transient events dominated by motion artifact, VsLFM12 with AFsLF remains more suitable. Therefore, AFsLF serves as a general enabling module for long term, stable, in-focus intravital imaging of dynamic biological processes in living mammals.

In summary, as sLFM continues to gain traction in biological imaging, its potential is still constrained by axial defocus issues in sustained observations. AFsLF addresses this challenge and offers a reliable, efficient autofocus solution tailored to the unique properties of sLFM. With its distinctive advantages including low phototoxicity, subcellular resolution, and real-time feedback, AFsLF is poised to significantly advance long term intravital biological studies. We anticipate that the incorporation of AFsLF with sLFM will provide a powerful tool for a wide range of in vivo applications in neuroscience, immunology and oncology.

Methods

Setups of sLFM

The sLFM systems used in this study including sLFM9, csLFM13 and RUSH3D15, are commercial versions (SLiM1100, SLiM2000, and RUSH3D, respectively) provided by Zhejiang Hehu Technology Co., Ltd. Specifically, SLiM1100 consists of a conventional epifluorescence microscope (IX73, Olympus), an integrated control module, a scanning light field imaging module, a multi-channel laser (LDI-prime-488, 89 North), a highspeed highprecision motorized translation stage (QGSP600, Prior) and a complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) camera (Zyla 4.2, Andor) with a customized MLA in front of the sensor. Four laser beams illuminate the sample after passing through the scanning light field imaging module and the camera captures the images with angular resolution of 15 × 15. The 3D imaging speed of SLiM1100 is up to 90 Hz, with an optical resolution of 220 nm laterally and 400 nm axially, and a FOV of 220 micrometers. SLiM2000 is an upgrade version of SLiM1100, by combining a line confocal illumination to reject background fluorescence and improve the signal-to-background ratio. A cylindrical lens (Thorlabs, LJ1363L2-A) are inserted into SLiM2000 to generate a thin light sheet for illumination. RUSH3D is an ultra-large-scale sLFM, which provides a uniform single-cell resolution of 2.6 μm laterally and 6 μm axially across a volume of 8 × 6 × 0.4 mm3 at 20 Hz. An optical objective lens with larger spatial-bandwidth product is configurated in the RUSH3D system. The remaining components are identical to those of the SLiM1100 system.

Pre-calibration

Although AFsLF has extensive applicability for various scanning light field microscopes, the otherness of hardware from these commercial scanning light field microscopes leads to the difference of βc in Eq. (4). Hence, the pre-calibration needs to be finished prior to the first utilization. Generally, we calculate the pixel shift between two views in several distances via calibration plate to estimate βc and the pixel shift invariably enlarges as the axial position increases (Supplementary Fig. 12a). Then the accuracy of βc can be assessed by the comparison between ground truth and estimated axial position (Supplementary Fig. 12b-c).

Because the pre-calibration coefficient βc depends on the optical configuration of scanning light field microscope, it is calibrated once for each system before initial deployment by measuring the pixel shift between central view and an edge angular view at several known axial positions using a calibration plate. βc is determined by fixed hardware parameters that define the PSF and the spatial-angular mapping, these parameters include the objective magnification, objective numerical aperture, microlens size, and pixel size; therefore, recalibration is not required during routine imaging with stable mechanical alignment and fixed operating temperature, and is independent of the specimen and imaging settings such as exposure time, laser power, and acquisition frame rate. Recalibration is only needed if the optical configuration is changed, such as altering the MLA or camera alignment. The robustness of this calibration strategy is supported by the low prediction error obtained with both 20× and 60× objectives and by the stable performance of AFsLF across various illumination intensities, angular configurations, illumination wavelengths, and samples.

Data processing and analysis

The observation process including hardware control, FOV selection, parameter setting and image acquisition was achieved by an open-source software called sLFDriver38. AFsLF is evaluated using a C + + script on a desktop computer (CPU, Intel i9-10940X; RAM, 256 GB; GPU, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090). ImageJ43 is used to process the recorded images and movies, and Imaris 9.0 software provides the 3D rendering. Figures were created by the authors using Microsoft Visio and Cinema 4D, without using third-party images or previously created elements.

Antibodies

For intravital imaging of liver and spleen, Alexa Fluor 488 WGA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #Cat:W11261, 15% (v/v)), Alexa Fluor 561-anti-F4/80 (BM8, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #Cat:505-4801-82, 10% (v/v)), Alexa Fluor 647-anti-Ly-6G (1A8, Biolegend, #Cat:127610, 10% (v/v)) were used. For skin intravital imaging, Alexa Fluor 594-anti-Gr-1 (RB6-8C5, Biolegend, #Cat:108448, 20% (v/v)), and Alexa Fluor 647-anti-CD31 (390, Biolegend, #Cat:102416, 20% (v/v)) were used. All antibodies were diluted in sterile PBS.

Mice

C57BL/6 wild-type (WT) mice (SLAC Laboratory Animal Company, 7- to 8-week-old, male) were used for liver and spleen imaging. CX3CR1-GFP transgenic mice (The Jackson Laboratory, 008451, male) were used for brain imaging. All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with free access to food and water, in a temperature-controlled environment (20-22 °C) with a 12 h light/dark cycle and 50% relative humidity.

Mouse experiments

Prior to imaging, wild-type mice received an intravenous injection of a 100 μl antibody mixture (described above) and were and anesthetized them with Avertin (350 mg kg⁻¹, i.p.). During imaging, mice were placed in a customized chamber with a thermoregulator (37 °C, ThermoStar System, RWD), and the target organ was apposed to a #1.5 coverslip. Anesthesia was maintained with isoflurane (0.5% in O₂ at 0.75 L/min).

For conventional liver and spleen imaging: After anesthesia, a small laparotomy (~ 2 mm incision) was made to expose liver or spleen for direct intravital imaging.

For liver metastases model: After anesthesia, the dorsal skin was shaved and disinfected with 75% ethanol. A dorsal incision was made to expose the spleen, and a total of 100 μl of the cell suspension (2× 10⁵ cells) was slowly injected into the spleen using a 30-gauge insulin syringe. After injection, the incision was closed. Subsequently, a separate abdominal incision was made to expose the liver for imaging.

For skin injury model: After 2 h for antibody distribution, mice were anesthetized with Avertin. The dorsal skin was shaved and disinfected with 75% ethanol. A standardized 4 mm excision wound was created in the mid-dorsal region to exceed the imaging FOV, with an additional deep wound about 800 μm in diameter penetrating the dermis at one end.

For brain imaging (microglial dynamics): CX3CR1-GFP transgenic mice received no antibody injection. Under Anesthesia, a cranial window (7×5×7 mm) was surgically prepared over the somatosensory cortex. Unlike peripheral imaging, no isoflurane maintenance was used during the subsequent in vivo scanning light field imaging.
