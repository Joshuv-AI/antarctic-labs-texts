Circadian regulation of insulin secretion in transplanted human stem cell-derived pancreatic β-cells
Authors: Satish K Sen, Zenith Khashim, Sharath Shivakumar Belame, Shaimaa Hassoun, Tiana Salomon, Sean Lewis-Brinkman, Matthew R Brown, Luhui Zhang, Quinn P Peterson, Aleksey V Matveyenko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryCell replacement strategies utilizing stem cell-derived pancreatic β-cells (SCβ-cells) hold therapeutic potential for patients with diabetes. However, little is known about how endogenous (host) and exogenous (transplant) circadian systems interact to influence the engraftment and function of SCβ-cells. We report that differentiation of SCβ-cells in vitro is associated with the induction of key circadian clock genes known to regulate insulin secretion. Upon transplantation into severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID)-beige mice, SCβ-cells exhibit circadian rhythms in glucose-stimulated insulin secretion optimized to the host’s active circadian phase. Furthermore, disrupting the host’s circadian rhythms abolishes circadian regulation and the overall functional capacity of transplanted SCβ-cells. These observations suggest that the host’s circadian system entrains SCβ-cell function to optimize the circadian control of insulin secretion.

Introduction

Transplantation of stem cell-derived pancreatic β-cells (SCβ-cells) holds therapeutic potential for patients with diabetes. Various differentiation protocols have been developed to produce SCβ-cells in vitro, and proof-of-concept studies in animal models have shown promising results (Pagliuca et al., 2014; Nair et al., 2019; Peterson et al., 2020; Hogrebe et al., 2021). Despite this progress, achieving the ultimate goal of recapitulating and maintaining a mature β-cell functional phenotype, along with long-term regulation of glucose homeostasis by transplanted SCβ-cells, remains challenging (Russ et al., 2022). Thus, it is crucial to thoroughly understand the physiological mechanisms underlying the successful maturation and sustained function of transplanted SCβ-cells. In this regard, recent work highlights the critical role of maintaining physiological circadian rhythms for proper control of β-cell function, maturation, and overall glucose homeostasis (Perelis et al., 2016; Javeed and Matveyenko 2018).

The circadian regulation of insulin secretion is essential for β-cell physiology, as the circadian system modulates insulin secretion during different phases of the circadian cycle: restraining it during the inactive/sleep phase and optimizing it during the active/feeding phase (Boden et al., 1996). In mammals, the circadian system operates as a hierarchical oscillator network. Cell-specific circadian rhythms in gene expression are driven by an endogenous molecular clock, featuring a complex network of transcription factors (TFs) organized in positive-negative feedback loops. The core circadian loop involves two main TFs, CLOCK and BMAL1, which bind E-box DNA motifs to regulate the circadian transcription of various genes, including their negative regulators (Bass and Lazar 2016; Kim and Lazar 2020). Additional transcriptional rhythms are generated by clock-regulated TFs downstream of CLOCK and BMAL1, such as DBP/NFIL3, REV-ERBα/RORα, and DEC1/2 (BHLHE40/41) (Kim and Lazar 2020). This intricate molecular network enables temporal regulation of the cellular genome, influencing diverse cellular functions (Bass and Lazar 2016).

At the organismal level, cellular clocks across tissues and organs are synchronized with environmental cues through hormonal, neuronal, and behavioral signals, driven by light-induced regulation of the main circadian oscillator in the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus (SCN) (Schibler et al., 2015). This functional circadian system is crucial for β-cells, as nearly one-third of the β-cell genome is under circadian control, encompassing pathways that regulate insulin production, exocytosis, maturation, metabolism, turnover, inflammation, and responses to various stressors (Petrenko et al., 2017; Brown et al., 2021; Weidemann et al., 2024). Importantly, recent studies suggest that the induction of circadian clock gene expression in developing mouse islets correlates with functional maturation in insulin secretion (Rakshit et al., 2018). Furthermore, circadian entrainment of stem cell-derived β-cells in vitro enhances glucose-stimulated insulin secretion, underscoring the importance of circadian control for SCβ-cell maturation and corresponding function (Alvarez-Dominguez et al., 2020).

The consideration of circadian biology in cell and organ transplantation is an emerging field of research (Patlin et al., 2024). However, limited information is available on how endogenous (host) and exogenous (transplant) circadian systems interact to influence the engraftment and function of transplanted tissues (Patlin et al., 2024). Insights from solid organ transplantation studies (e.g., heart) indicate that the establishment of functional circadian rhythms in transplanted tissues correlates with improved clinical outcomes (Patlin et al., 2024). Conversely, dysfunction of the host’s circadian clock appears to compromise tissue engraftment and function (Cheng et al., 2011). These studies suggest that coordination between the recipient’s and the transplant’s circadian systems is vital for transplantation success. Yet, the circadian regulation of transplanted SCβ-cell function and the potential influence of the host’s circadian clock on transplantation outcomes remain unexplored. This is particularly important for assessment of SCβ-cell’s therapeutic potential in diabetes, as circadian regulation of insulin secretion is critical for optimal prevention of hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia.

Therefore, in the current study, we aimed to provide novel insights into the role of the circadian system in the regulation of SCβ-cell function following transplantation by addressing the following key questions. Does the transplantation of SCβ-cells affect the host’s circadian rhythms? Do transplanted SCβ-cells exhibit circadian rhythms in insulin secretion aligned (or misaligned) with the host’s active/feeding cycle? Finally, does global disruption of the host’s circadian rhythms impair the functionality of transplanted SCβ-cells?

Results

We successfully applied a previously published protocol for the stepwise differentiation of SCβ-cells from human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) (Figures 1A and 1B). Consistent with previous studies, this differentiation process resulted in the generation of functional SCβ-cells, characterized by robust glucose- (5-fold) and KCl- (15-fold) stimulated insulin secretion (Figure 1C). Prior studies have reported the spontaneous emergence of core circadian clock genes during the in vitro differentiation of hESCs (Dierickx et al., 2018). In alignment with these findings, we observed a robust stepwise induction of core circadian TFs (CLOCK and BMAL1), negative core clock regulators (e.g., PER1/2 and CRY1/2), and auxiliary loop circadian TFs (REV-ERBα, BHLHE40/41, and NFIL3) (Figure 1D). Interestingly, the D-box binding TF DBP did not show increased expression during the differentiation process, which is consistent with fasting-induced regulation of DBP expression in β-cells (Brown et al., 2021). Notably, the expression level of key circadian clock genes in stage 6 SCβ-cells was attenuated compared to cadaveric adult human islets (Figure S1).

Recent studies have demonstrated that the engraftment of human transplanted tissues (e.g., hepatocytes) can significantly alter the host’s behavioral and physiological circadian rhythms by directly affecting the SCN circadian clock output function (Delbes et al., 2023). This is especially relevant for transplanted cells or tissues that can impact the host’s glucose/insulin axis, as insulin serves as a primary entrainment signal for cellular clocks throughout the body (Crosby et al., 2019). To explore this further, we transplanted 5 million SCβ-cells under the kidney capsule of immunocompromised (severe combined immunodeficiency [SCID]-beige) mice. Following the successful engraftment of the transplanted cells (60 days post-transplant), we conducted a comprehensive assessment of the host’s circadian behavior and physiology (Figure 2A). Under standard light/dark cycle conditions, SCβ-cell transplantation did not significantly affect the circadian rhythms of the host’s SCN-mediated behavioral (e.g., activity) and metabolic (e.g., energy expenditure) outputs (p > 0.05 for all parameters compared to mock: Figures 2B and 2C). Next, we evaluated the impact of SCβ-cell transplantation on the circadian regulation of the host’s glucose and insulin axis (Figures 2D–2F). Intriguingly, both the host’s (mouse) and the transplant’s (human) plasma insulin concentrations under ad libitum conditions displayed significant circadian rhythms, with the peak phase of insulin occurring during the “dark/active” phase of the circadian cycle (∼ zeitgeber time [ZT]19, Figures 2D–2F). Additionally, SCβ-cell-transplanted mice exhibited robust circadian rhythms in ad libitum glucose concentrations, recapitulating reported circadian patterns in mice (Brown et al., 2021). Overall, the SCβ-cell-derived human insulin release under ad libitum conditions mirrors the circadian insulin release patterns characteristic of the host’s circadian cycles.

Enhanced glucose tolerance and glucose-stimulated insulin secretion during the active circadian phase is a characteristic feature of circadian physiology in rodents and humans (Javeed and Matveyenko 2018), whereas disruption of normal circadian physiology and behavior adversely impacts human health and leads to the dysregulation of glucose homeostasis and β-cell function (Arble et al., 2015; Qian and Scheer 2016). This raises the question of whether global disruption of the host’s circadian rhythms compromises the functionality and engraftment of transplanted SCβ-cells.

To investigate this, we exposed SCID-beige mice to either standard housing conditions (12-h light/12-h dark circadian cycles) or circadian disruption (CD) induced by an 8-week exposure to 24-h constant light (Figure 3A), a condition validated to disrupt global behavioral and physiological circadian rhythms in mice (Fonken and Nelson 2014; Brown et al., 2021). Control and CD mice were subsequently transplanted with 5 million SCβ-cells under the kidney capsule. CD compromised the normal regulation of global circadian rhythms in activity and energy metabolism, as expected based on the known deleterious effects of constant light on SCN function (Ohta et al., 2005) (Figures 3B and 3C). Notably, the transplanted control mice showed robust diurnal rhythmicity, characterized by significantly enhanced glucose tolerance and human insulin secretory function at ZT16 time point, corresponding to the host’s active circadian cycle (Figures 3D and 4B, p < 0.05, ZT4 vs. ZT16).

These findings suggest that transplanted SCβ-cells exhibit circadian rhythms in glucose-stimulated insulin secretion optimized for the host’s active circadian phase. In contrast, exposure to CD abrogated circadian rhythms in glucose tolerance and glucose-stimulated human insulin secretion and significantly reduced the glucose responsiveness of transplanted SCβ-cells (Figures 3E and 4C, p > 0.05 for ZT4 vs. ZT16). Notably, while 92% of SCβ-cell-transplanted mice demonstrated glucose responsiveness during the active circadian phase in control mice, only 20% of SCβ-cell-transplanted mice demonstrated glucose responsiveness under CD conditions (Figures 4D and 4E).

Discussion

Circadian control of glucose homeostasis is a hallmark feature of human physiology (Qian and Scheer 2016). However, the role of circadian physiology in cell and organ transplantation outcomes is poorly understood, and limited information is available on how endogenous (host/transplant recipient) and exogenous (transplant) circadian systems interact to influence the engraftment and function of transplanted cell products. In this regard, our studies reveal novel insights into the circadian regulation of transplanted stem-cell-derived pancreatic β-cells. Most notably, our studies demonstrate that the in vitro differentiation of SCβ-cells is associated with the spontaneous induction of key circadian clock genes known to regulate insulin secretion. Once transplanted, SCβ-cells exhibit circadian rhythms in glucose-stimulated insulin secretion optimized for the host’s active circadian phase. Moreover, disruption of the host’s circadian rhythms abrogates circadian regulation and impairs the overall functionality of transplanted SCβ-cells. These observations suggest that SCβ-cells acquire circadian control of insulin secretion in vivo, influenced by the recipient’s circadian system, which should be considered in the design of therapeutic strategies for the treatment of T1DM.

In humans, the insulin secretory response and corresponding glucose tolerance are optimized according to the specific time within the 24-h circadian cycle. Particularly, humans exhibit a several-fold increase in the insulin secretory response to glucose, mixed meals, and secretagogues (e.g., sulfonylureas) at the onset of the active circadian phase (i.e., early morning) compared to the onset of the inactive circadian phase (i.e., evening/night) (Carroll and Nestel 1973; Lee et al., 1992; Boden et al., 1996; Saad et al., 2012; Sharma et al., 2017). Notably, circadian rhythms in glucose-stimulated insulin secretion persist in vivo under glucose clamp conditions (Boden et al., 1996), and in vitro in isolated human islets (Peschke and Peschke 1998; Perelis et al., 2015; Petrenko et al., 2017), implicating regulatory control by the cell-autonomous molecular circadian clock. Indeed, core circadian TFs CLOCK and BMAL1, as well as other circadian TFs (e.g., DBP) downstream of BMAL/CLOCK, drive the rhythmic regulation of insulin secretion, partially through the genomic activation of β-cell-specific enhancers that regulate insulin exocytosis, metabolism, and insulin processing to promote maximal insulin secretory function within a specific time window, thereby optimizing glucose homeostasis (Perelis et al., 2015; Alvarez-Dominguez et al., 2020; Brown et al., 2021). The functional significance of this intricate system is reinforced by observations of impaired glucose tolerance and increased susceptibility to the development of diabetes in both whole-body and β-cell-specific clock gene mutant mice (e.g., Bmal1 and Clock) (Perelis et al., 2015; Rakshit et al., 2016) and in humans with clock gene variants (Woon et al., 2007). Our observations strongly suggest that the endogenous circadian clock system is preserved and functional in SCβ-cells following transplantation in SCID beige mice. Additionally, our study indicates that the host’s circadian system can synchronize/entrain SCβ-cell function to optimize the circadian regulation of glucose-stimulated human insulin secretion.

Previous studies demonstrate that ES cells express core circadian clock genes, albeit at significantly lower levels than fully differentiated cells (Yagita et al., 2010; Dierickx et al., 2018). These insufficient levels of clock gene and protein expression potentially explain the lack of a functional circadian clock reported in murine and human pluripotent ES cells. Importantly, studies also demonstrate enhanced clock gene expression and the spontaneous emergence of circadian clock function following the directed differentiation of ES cells toward mature/adult cell lineage (Dierickx et al., 2017). The exact mechanisms underlying the emergence of clock gene expression and function during the ES cell differentiation process are poorly understood; however, studies suggest that growth factors (e.g., retinoic acid) used during the differentiation process potentially promote the emergence of the circadian clock (Yagita et al., 2010). Our studies also demonstrate a spontaneous emergence of circadian clock gene expression during ES to SCβ-cell differentiation, with most clock genes exhibiting a several-fold increase in expression from the first to the last stage of the differentiation process. Notably, recent work by Alvarez-Dominguez and colleagues elegantly demonstrated the potential role of CLOCK and BMAL1 as pioneer TFs critical for SCβ-cell maturation (Alvarez-Dominguez et al., 2020). Consistently, in vitro, entrainment of circadian rhythms in SCβ-cells was shown to enhance genomic, epigenetic, and functional β-cell maturity (Alvarez-Dominguez et al., 2020). It is also important to note that these observations are consistent with in vivo studies in primary rodent and human islets, which demonstrate the emergence of circadian clock expression and function during the early postnatal period (postnatal day 15–30 in rodents) (Jacovetti et al., 2017; Rakshit et al., 2018). Importantly, embryonic β cell-specific deletion of Bmal1 or early-life exposure to CD impairs the maturation of glucose-stimulated insulin secretion in vivo and promotes glucose intolerance (Rakshit et al., 2018). Collectively, these observations highlight the importance of the β-cell circadian system for the optimal design of effective SCβ-cell transplantation strategies.

Our results also suggest that circadian rhythmicity of human insulin secretion in transplanted β-cells are established as a result of SC-β cell engraftment/maturation and corresponding synchronization by transplant host’s circadian system. This raises a question regarding the physiological mechanisms regulating the circadian entrainment of transplanted SCβ-cells. The exact physiological mechanism by which the central SCN clock synchronizes peripheral oscillators remains an area of active investigation. However, studies suggest that the circadian regulation of fasting/feeding cycles plays a major role in the entrainment of the endogenous islet transcriptome, epigenome, and insulin secretory function (Brown et al., 2021). Indeed, fasting/feeding cycles appear to regulate the circadian expression of islet transcripts involved in the control of the circadian clock and glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (Brown et al., 2021). The molecular mechanisms responsible for the entrainment of circadian insulin secretion have been attributed to the fasting-induced cyclical activation of the PAR bZip TF DBP. Consistently, DBP-bound active enhancers display circadian expression in vivo and are enriched for pathways essential for glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (Brown et al., 2021). Intriguingly, in contrast to other components of the molecular clock loop, SCβ-cell DBP expression did not change as a result of the ES differentiation process in vitro. This is consistent with the notion that fasting/feeding cycles are the primary drivers of β-cell DBP expression through modulation of FOXO1 signaling and suggests that the maintenance of normal fasting/feeding cycles is important for the functionality of SCβ-cells.

It is important to acknowledge some limitations of our studies. Technical challenges have precluded us from the assessment of circadian gene expression in SC-β cells following transplantation into SCID mice. Thus, future studies will be required to examine mechanisms regulating circadian control of gene/protein expression in grafted SC-β cells under standard LD and circadian disrupted conditions. In addition, we evaluated in vivo glucose tolerance and human insulin secretion at two diurnal time points (ZT4 and ZT16), which were chosen based on previously observed diurnal differences in glucose tolerance and insulin secretion in mice. More frequent sampling periodicity may be required to further define circadian rhythms in transplanted SC-β cell function. Finally, we utilized exposure to constant light to model CD in mice. Although previous studies have shown that constant light exposure in rodents recapitulates many aspects of metabolic and β-cell abnormalities present in humans exposed to CD (Fonken and Nelson 2014), its severity potentially limits translational relevance of our approach.

Disruption of normal circadian physiology is a common occurrence in today’s society, characterized by daily disruptions in light/dark, rest/activity, and fasting/feeding cycles (Gerhart-Hines and Lazar 2015; Bass 2016; Lunn et al., 2017). Importantly, circadian rhythm disruptions are associated with dysregulation in glucose homeostasis and compromised β-cell function in vivo (Scheer et al., 2009; Buxton et al., 2012; Morris et al., 2015; Sharma et al., 2017; Chellappa et al., 2021). Moreover, studies in rodents have noted that global CD can accelerate the loss of functional β-cell mass in mice predisposed to development of diabetes (Gale et al., 2011; Qian et al., 2015). This is attributed to the uncovered roles of β-cell circadian clocks in regulating the transcriptional response to ER and oxidative stress (Lee et al., 2015), inflammation (Weidemann et al., 2024), and DNA damage (Rakshit et al., 2016). Our data demonstrate that CD also compromises the functional capacity of transplanted SCβ-cells. As stem cell islet replacement therapy in humans becomes a reality (Butler and Gale 2022), our studies suggest that the host’s circadian health should be considered to optimize the function of transplanted β-cell products. In this regard, future studies will be needed to address whether novel chronotherapeutic and chrononutrition approaches can promote the secretory function and survival of transplanted SCβ-cells.

Methods

Differentiation and functional assessment of SC-β cells

Human pluripotent stem cell maintenance and differentiation were carried out as previously described (Pagliuca et al., 2014). Stem cells were adapted to a 3D culture system using 500 mL spinner flasks and maintained using mTeSR media (Stem Cell Technologies Inc., 24243). Suspension cultures were performed by seeding 150 million cells in mTeSR media with 10 μM Y27632 (R&D Systems, 4448) and maintained at 70 rpm in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2. mTeSR media was changed every 48 h and passaged every 72-h using Accutase (Sigma Aldrich, A6964-500ML). Freshly split cells were seeded into fresh mTeSR with Y27632. SC-β cells were directed sequentially to functional β cells as previously described (Pagliuca et al., 2014).

Assessment of SC-β cell function in vitro

SC-β cells were washed twice in low-glucose (3 mM) Krebs Ringer (KRB) buffer and then loaded into 24-well transwell inserts and incubated in low-glucose KRB for 1 h at 37°C. Clusters were then transferred to high glucose KRB (17 mM) for 1 h at 37°C and then transferred to low-glucose KRB with 30 mM KCl for 1 h at 37°C. After each transfer, the supernatant was collected and stored at −20°C until analysis. Collected supernatants were analyzed by ELISA for human insulin (ALPCO, 80-INSHUU-E10) concentrations and normalized for cell number.

Immunofluorescence/flow cytometry analysis of SC-β cells

For immunofluorescence staining, stage 6 SC-β cell clusters were fixed in 4% PFA at 4°C overnight and embedded in Histogel. Sections were then immunostained for insulin (Cell signaling, 4590S, 1:250) and mounted using Fluoromount-G with nuclear counterstain DAPI (4′,6- diamidino -2-phenlindole). Slides were viewed and imaged using a Zeiss Axios Observer Z1 microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy, Oberkochen, Germany) and ZenPro software (Carl Zeiss Microscopy). For flow cytometry analysis, cells were collected at each differentiation stage. After dispersal using TrypLE Express (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 12563029) at 37°C for 10 min, the enzyme was neutralized with S3 media. The cells were counted and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Millipore Sigma, 15710) and stored at 4°C until staining. Permeabilization was carried out in a blocking solution containing PBS, 0.1% Triton X-100, and 5% donkey serum for 45 min. Cells were incubated with primary antibodies in the blocking solution for 1 h at room temperature (RT) or overnight at 4°C, followed by three washes with PBST. Secondary antibody incubation was performed for 1 h at RT, followed by three additional washes. Flow cytometry was conducted using Attune instruments, with data processed in FlowJo v.10 and Attune NxT v.4.2.6 software. For the final differentiation stage (Stage 6), insulin was detected using a C-peptide antibody (DSHB, GN-ID4, 1:250), and NKX6.1 was detected using an antibody against its epitope (DHSB, F55A12, 1:100). Secondary antibodies included donkey anti-rat Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen A-21208, 1:1,000) and donkey anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 647 (Invitrogen A-31571, 1:1,000).

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted following RNeasy mini (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) kit protocol and eluted RNA samples were further digested for genomic DNA using TURBO DNA-free kit (Invitrogen). Complement DNA (cDNA) was transcribed from 400 ng of RNA with the iScript cDNA Synthesis kit (Bio-Rad) and the resulting cDNA was mixed with SYBR Green Master Mix (ABI) with gene-specific primers. RT-qPCR analysis was performed using the ABI StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System and the results were normalized to β-actin housekeeping gene.

Animals and experimental study design

All animal procedures were approved by Mayo Clinic Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. In total, this study utilized 43 male immunodeficient SCID Beige mice (16–18 weeks old) (Taconic Biosciences). All mice were housed within external light-proof cabinets (Phenome Technologies, Lincolnshire, IL) and fed a standard chow diet (14% fat, 32% protein, and 54% carbohydrates; Harlan, Indianapolis, IN). As an initial 2-week acclimatization period, all animals were entrained under standard lighting conditions: 12-h light and 12-h dark with ad libitum access to water and food. Following the 2 weeks, the animals were randomly assigned to one of two experimental groups for 4 weeks: (1) control: 12-h light/12-h dark cycle with ad libitum access to water and chow diet and (2) CD: exposure to 24 h constant light conditions (>100 lux light intensity) with ad libitum access to water and chow diet. Control and circadian-disrupted mice were subsequently transplanted with 5 × 106 SC-β cells injected under the left kidney capsule as previously described (Pagliuca et al., 2014), and allowed an additional 60 days for engraftment during which animals were maintained under either Control or circadian-disrupted lighting conditions. The “mock” transplanted mice underwent identical surgical and recovery procedures without SC-β cell transplant. By convention, ZT0 was denoted as the onset of the light/day cycle under control conditions and ZT12 was denoted as the onset of dark/night cycles, before experimental CD.

Assessment of physiological and behavioral circadian rhythms

Assessment of circadian rhythms in activity, food intake, and energy expenditure was performed using the comprehensive laboratory animal monitoring system (CLAMS equipped with an Oxymax Open Circuit Calorimeter System, Columbus Instruments, Columbus, OH) as previously described (Brown et al., 2021; Rakshit and Matveyenko 2021). V̇co2, V̇o2, activity, and food intake were monitored for 48 h, and energy expenditure (kcal.h-1.kg-1) was calculated using Oxymax Software V.5.40.14 (Columbus Instruments).

Assessment of diurnal glucose tolerance and glucose-stimulated human insulin secretion

To assess diurnal glycemia and insulinemia blood was drawn from mice at 4-h intervals during the 24-h circadian cycle (ZT2, 6, 10, 14, 18, and 22). To assess diurnal glucose tolerance, mice received 2 g/kg body weight of 50% dextrose solution via intraperitoneal injection performed on 1,000 h (ZT4, day/light) and 2,200 h (ZT16, night/dark). Blood glucose was measured at 0, 15, 30, 45, 60, and 90 min post-dextrose injection. At 10 min post-glucose injection, blood was sampled via saphenous venipuncture into microcentrifuge tubes for collection of plasma for determination of insulin using an ultrasensitive Human and Mouse Insulin ELISA (Alpco Diagnostics, Salem, NH).

Statistical analysis and calculations

Statistical analysis was performed by either two-tailed Student’s t test for comparison of two independent groups or one-way ANOVA and/or two-way ANOVA with repeated measures for comparison of three or more groups (GraphPad Prism v.10, San Diego, CA). Parametric test normality was evaluated using Shapiro-Wilk test and equal variance using F-test (GraphPad Prism v.10). Assessment of circadian rhythmicity in behavioral and physiological parameters was performed using Cosinor analysis with SigmaPlot 13 software (SPSS, Chicago, IL), as previously described in detail (Grosbellet et al., 2015; Sen et al., 2017). Values were fitted to the regression: y = a + b·cos(2π[x – c]/24), where a is the mean level, b is the amplitude, and c is the acrophase of the rhythm. Data in graphs are presented as mean ± SEM and assumed statistically significant at p < 0.05.
