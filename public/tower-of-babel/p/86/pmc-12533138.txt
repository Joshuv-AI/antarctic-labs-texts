State-of-the-art assessment-based review of boiling heat transfer and friction factor correlations for NH3–H2O, NH3–LiNO3 and NH3–LiNO3–H2O mixtures in a plate heat exchanger
Authors: Vikash Kumar Singh Chauhan, A. Sathyabhama, Ramakrishna N. Hegde
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The increasing demand for energy-efficient and environmentally sustainable cooling technologies has led to a renewed focus on ammonia and lithium nitrate-based absorption refrigeration systems, particularly those utilizing Plate Heat Exchangers (PHEs). Despite their importance, reliable predictive models for boiling heat transfer and frictional pressure drop in PHEs using ammonia and lithium nitrate mixtures, such as NH3–LiNO3 and NH3–LiNO3–H2O, remain limited and often suffer from structural deficiencies. This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of existing correlations for boiling heat transfer and friction factor in PHEs, specifically focusing on ammonia-based mixtures (NH3–H2O, NH3–LiNO3, and NH3–LiNO3–H2O). More than 20 correlations for boiling heat transfer coefficient and friction factor were critically analysed and adjusted to account for the unique thermophysical behaviors of multi-component salt mixtures. The study reveals that many correlations fail to accurately predict boiling heat transfer in NH3–H2O mixtures due to inadequate sensitivity to heat flux. Scaling these correlations led to notable improvements in prediction accuracy, underlining the significance of appropriate scaling for different PHE configurations. Additionally, the study validates the assumption that lithium nitrate remains in the liquid phase in NH3–LiNO3 and NH3–LiNO3–H2O mixtures, supporting its exclusion from latent heat calculations. Friction factor correlations that include positive exponents for Reynolds and Weber numbers were found to be structurally inconsistent, resulting in inaccurate predictions. The analysis further highlights that many correlations are overly empirical or based on narrow experimental conditions, limiting their applicability to diverse heat exchanger geometries. A key contribution of this work is the unique visual comparison of the correlations, providing a detailed depiction of their structural characteristics and offering more precise insights than those available in previous studies.

Introduction

The study of boiling heat transfer correlations is crucial for designing and optimizing Plate Heat Exchangers (PHEs) in Absorption Refrigeration Systems (ARS). While significant advancements have been made in understanding NH3–H2O mixtures, the introduction of ternary mixtures, such as NH3–LiNO3–H2O, brings both challenges and new opportunities.

This ternary mixture offers several advantages over conventional NH3–H2O systems. Notably, it reduces generator temperature requirements and eliminates the need for a rectifier, making it particularly well-suited for solar-driven cooling applications1,2. Additionally, while the high viscosity of the NH3–LiNO3 binary mixture typically hampers heat and mass transfer efficiency, the presence of water in the ternary system helps to mitigate this issue, improving overall performance.

However, despite these benefits, the boiling behavior of NH3–LiNO3–H2O inside PHEs remains poorly understood. Most existing heat transfer models focus on simpler systems involving pure or binary refrigerants, making them insufficient for predicting the complex dynamics of ternary mixtures. Unlike binary mixtures, flow boiling in ternary systems is influenced by both nucleate and convective boiling irrespective of working fluid, creating additional complexities that existing correlations fail to capture.

To address this gap, this study systematically evaluates and adapts existing boiling heat transfer correlations originally developed for binary mixtures to better predict heat transfer in NH3–LiNO3–H2O systems. A key novelty of this work lies in the visual assessment approach: each correlation’s predictive behavior is presented through trendline plots against experimental data, offering clear insights into how various terms in the correlations influence the results: not seen in previous studies. The assessment is performed first for the NH3–H2O binary system and then extended to the NH3–LiNO3 binary and the NH3–LiNO3–H2O ternary mixtures. Furthermore, this study includes a detailed analysis of friction factor correlations under boiling flow conditions, which is essential for understanding pressure drop and overall system performance. This study tries to identify the limitations of current models and enhances our understanding of flow boiling in multi-component mixtures.

History of flow boiling correlation

Chen3 provided a semi-empirical method for predicting the boiling heat transfer coefficient (BHTC) for flow boiling by combining two mechanisms of heat transfer: convective heat transfer and nucleate boiling. The liquid only heat transfer is estimated by the Dittus-Boelter correlation4 and the nucleate boiling is estimated by the Forster and Zuber5 method. Dittus and Boelter’s4 correlation is used to estimate the convective heat transfer coefficient in turbulent flow inside pipes and has been used extensity for estimation of liquid only and convective heat transfer coefficient term in boiling heat transfer studies. The correlation remains consistent and is widely used in heat transfer calculations.2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${h}_{l}= 0.023 R{e}_{l}^{0.8}{\text{Pr}}_{l}^{0.4}\left(\frac{k}{{D}_{h}}\right)$$\end{document}

The Lockhart-Martinelli parameter is used in two-phase flow to characterize the relative contributions of liquid and gas (or vapor) phases to the flow. It is particularly useful in calculating pressure drop and analyzing heat transfer in systems involving two-phase flow in pipes and channels. The Lockhart-Martinelli parameter (X) is defined as:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ={\left(\frac{{\Delta P}_{l}}{\Delta {P}_{v}}\right)}^{0.5}$$\end{document}where:

ΔPl: Pressure drop if only liquid were flowing at the same mass flux.

ΔPv: Pressure drop if only gas (or vapor) were flowing at the same mass flux.

However, for the assessment of correlations in this assessment the Lockhart–Martinelli parameter is defined by the mass flux and fluid properties:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\chi }_{tt}={\left(\frac{1-x}{x}\right)}^{0.9}{\left(\frac{{\rho }_{v}}{{\rho }_{l}}\right)}^{0.5}{\left(\frac{{\mu }_{l}}{{\mu }_{v}}\right)}^{0.1}$$\end{document}

Some researchers, including Thonon et al.9, Donowski10, Palmer et al.11, Hsieh and Lin12, Táboas et al.13, and Koyama et al.14, have developed models to characterize the effective heat transfer coefficient during flow boiling by relating the effective coefficient to the liquid-only heat transfer coefficient, incorporating adjustments based on parameters such as the boiling number or the Lockhart-Martinelli parameter.

Zeotropic mixtures offer the ability to control boiling points at constant pressure through concentration adjustment, resulting in temperature glide. Although this feature allows the utilization of low-quality heat in various applications, it significantly reduces the BHTC compared to pure substances15–19. This reduction primarily stems from additional mass transfer resistance due to the preferential evaporation of the more volatile component, which increases diffusion resistance for the less volatile component and further alters heat transfer and pressure drop characteristics.

Conventional correlations developed for pure fluids often neglect these effects, leading to large prediction errors when applied to mixtures20–22. Jung et al.23 demonstrated the full suppression of nucleate boiling beyond transition qualities, with BHTC values for mixtures up to 36% lower than ideal predictions. Other studies have reported that pure fluid-based correlations overpredict BHTC by up to 50% when applied to mixtures without accounting for mass transfer resistance and temperature glide24,25. Shin et al.26 and Boissieux et al.27 further observed that BHTC for mixtures is highly dependent on heat flux at low vapor qualities but becomes independent at higher qualities. Thome28 emphasized the need to modify BHTC models to account for mass diffusion resistance and property variations, while Kandlikar29 proposed suppression factors specifically for binary mixtures considering mass diffusion penalties. Further corrections by Thome30, Cavallini et al.31, Gungor and Winterton32 and Thome et al.33 have shown improved agreement with experimental data.

These effects of mixtures have increasingly been incorporated into suppression factors and enhancement parameters in more recent models, enabling more accurate predictions. Additionally, the detailed documentation of mixture properties in recent studies has led to more comprehensive assessments of BHTC using equivalent parameters that account for vapor quality and phase interaction effects.

Margat et al.34 observed that the effect of vapor quality plays an important role which supports an alternative section of correlation formulation inspired by equivalent parameters derived from vapor quality. This equivalent parameter is rooted in the concept of equivalent mass flux (Geq), which normalizes the complex two-phase flow of liquid and vapor into an analogous single-phase flow35. During flow boiling conditions, Geq simplifies the analysis of flow characteristics by integrating vapor quality, the densities of the liquid and vapor phases, and the actual total mass flux of the mixture. It is defined mathematically as:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${G}_{eq}=G\left[\left(1-x\right)+x{\left(\frac{{\rho }_{l}}{{\rho }_{v}}\right)}^{0.5}\right]$$\end{document}

By integrating the equivalent mass flux into the Boiling and Reynolds numbers, the model effectively incorporates the impact of vapor bubbles on convective heat transfer. This methodology has been explored extensively, by researchers such as Han et al.36, Park and Kim37, Kim et al.38, and Kim et al.39 simplifying the equations to depend solely on the effective Reynolds number, Boiling number, and liquid Prandtl number. Unlike Chen-type correlations, this approach enables the influence of heat flux into the correlation, due to the inclusion of the Boiling number.

It is Important to mention that the effect of heat flux variation plays a relatively minor role in the heat transfer coefficient variation than the effect due to geometry variation. Therefore, the strategy of modelling the correlation constant based on heat exchanger geometry is attempted by Amalfi et al.40, Ayub et al.41, and Khan et al.42.

Table 1 presents the correlations used to analyse the BHTC during flow boiling. The correlations were selected based on their relevance to ammonia-based mixtures, where ammonia is the primary refrigerant and the dominant vapor-phase component in the absorption refrigeration cycle, and for those correlations that were developed for PHEs, as exchanger geometry significantly influences flow boiling behavior. Correlations validated for NH3–H2O mixtures or fluids with similar thermodynamic properties were also selected to improve applicability and minimize predictive errors. Some of the correlations that were originally developed using other working fluids were included due to the limited availability of established correlations for ammonia-salt mixtures in PHEs, as a non-volatile salt presents additional challenges, as critical thermophysical properties such as reduced pressure, diffusion coefficient, and surface tension are not available for these systems and cannot be reliably calculated. This methodology is inspired by the work of Palm and Claesson55, who investigated flow boiling of R22 and R134a in PHE and applied a scaling factor of 1.5 to Cooper’s pool boiling correlation to improve predictive accuracy.

Subsequently, we evaluated the percentage of data points that fell within 10%, 20%, and 30% error margins for both the original and scaled correlations. The results show that several correlations, which initially failed to predict experimental BHTC within the 30% accuracy range, exhibited significantly improved predictive capability after scaling. For example, Danilova’s et al.43 correlation, which initially failed to predict any values within a 30% margin, improved to 27.37% within 10%, 67.60% within 20%, and 84.36% within 30% after scaling. Similar trends were observed for other correlations, underscoring the effectiveness of scaling in improving prediction accuracy.

This scaling and resulting enhancement follow the predefined trends and sensitivities to heat flux, mass flux, vapor quality, and mass fraction, which are embedded in the original structure of the correlations. Therefore, scaling primarily influences the magnitude of the predicted BHTC while preserving the original functional dependencies. This suggests that the improved performance does not arise from a shift in the physical modeling, but rather from a magnitude adjustment that aligns better with the experimental data. This improvement also points to limitations in the existing correlations when used in different conditions. Traditional parameters such as length scale and fluid properties, although fundamental, do not solely govern the accuracy of BHTC predictions. Many of the existing correlations were developed for conventional tube-type heat exchangers, whereas this study focuses on PHEs, which have distinct flow characteristics and interaction patterns. Moreover, the complex, non-ideal thermodynamic behavior of the NH3–H2O mixture adds additional uncertainty. It is also likely that certain system-specific or unaccounted-for parameters influence heat transfer mechanisms, especially when the working fluid or exchanger configuration deviates from the conditions under which the original correlations were formulated.

Assessment of flow boiling of NH3–LiNO3 mixture solution in PHEs

In this section, the selected correlations from Table 1 are evaluated to determine their suitability in predicting the BHTC for the NH3–LiNO3 Binary mixture in PHEs. NH3–LiNO3 is a better alternative to NH3–H2O in ARS due to its non-volatile absorbent (LiNO3), eliminating the need for a rectifier. It operates at lower generator temperatures, enabling the use of low-grade heat sources such as waste or solar heat, which results in a higher COP and reduced system complexity. Additionally, NH3–LiNO3 offers better thermal stability than NH3–H2O mixture which makes it a more efficient choice for such applications.

The presence of LiNO3 introduces unique challenges that differentiate this system from conventional binary NH3–H2O mixtures. Unlike NH3–H2O, where both components undergo a phase change, lithium nitrate remains entirely dissolved in the liquid phase, significantly increasing the viscosity and density of the solution and affecting heat and mass transfer performance. A previous study by Sathyabhama58 has shown that the addition of salts can influence heat transfer characteristics, with LiNO3 enhancing heat transfer under specific conditions. However, most existing correlations do not explicitly account for the presence of dissolved salts, requiring modifications for improved predictive accuracy. Additionally, LiNO3 raises the boiling point of the mixture59,60, reducing the effective driving force for phase change, which suppresses nucleate boiling and shifts the dominant heat transfer mechanism toward convective boiling. This shift makes correlations that primarily rely on nucleate boiling effects prone to overprediction. Furthermore, the altered thermal conductivity, specific heat, and vapor–liquid equilibrium behavior of the NH3–LiNO3 binary mixture influence bubble departure, vapor–liquid interaction, and pressure drop characteristics. When used in PHEs, where surface effects, flow distribution, and flow geometry play a crucial role, these fluid property changes further complicate heat transfer predictions, necessitating adjustments to existing correlations.

The thermophysical properties of the liquid phase, including density, viscosity, and heat capacity, are evaluated using data from Libotean et al.61. The enthalpy of vaporization, which directly influences the boiling number is modeled using the assumption that lithium nitrate is non-volatile, it remains in the liquid phase throughout the boiling process62. Consequently, the phase change occurs only for ammonia (NH3), while the total mass flow includes the lithium nitrate content. Analysis of friction factors

In addition to evaluating BHTC, the analysis of friction factors plays an essential role in understanding the flow behavior and overall performance of working fluids in PHEs. Unlike conventional tube heat exchangers, PHEs operate with narrow channels and corrugated plates that promote enhanced turbulence, but also introduce complex flow characteristics and increased pressure drop. As a result, frictional losses become a critical parameter influencing both energy consumption and heat exchanger sizing. The accurate prediction of friction factors is especially important in the context of ARS, where performance depends not only on effective heat transfer but also on minimizing pressure drop to ensure efficient circulation of working fluids. For multi-component mixtures such as NH3–LiNO3 and NH3–LiNO3–H2O, the influence of viscosity, concentration, and channel geometry further complicates the flow behavior, making it necessary to evaluate the applicability of existing friction factor correlations.

Typically, friction factor correlations for flow through PHEs are developed based on either empirical fitting of experimental data or semi-empirical approaches that incorporate dimensionless groups such as Reynolds number, chevron angle, and channel geometry parameters. Many of these correlations were initially formulated for single-phase flows but have since been extended to account for two-phase flow characteristics under boiling conditions. In this study, the correlations proposed by Yan et al.66, Hsieh and Lin7,12, Han et al.36, Park and Kim37, Lee et al.48, Amalfi et al.41, Zhang et al.50, and Kim et al.51 were selected for evaluation. These models were chosen due to their relevance to PHE applications and their prior use in studies involving refrigerants and ammonia-based mixtures.

The thermophysical properties required for friction factor calculations—including viscosity, density, and surface tension—were determined following the same methodology outlined in the calculation of BHTC. The experimental data used for friction factor analysis were also sourced from the same studies that provided BHTC data, ensuring consistency in the evaluation methodology.

To assess, the friction factor predictions using the selected correlations, a scaling procedure was applied using experimental data for all three working fluid systems: NH3–H2O, NH3–LiNO3, and NH3–LiNO3–H2O. The comparison was carried out by evaluating the CoV, determining appropriate scaling factors, and calculating the percentage of data points that fall within ± 10%, ± 20%, and ± 30% error ranges after scaling. As can be seen, most of the scaling factors were either very high or very low and thus none of the correlations were able to predict the data within an acceptable range hence the non-scaled data is not presented here. A general trend observed in most studies is that the friction factor decreases with increasing mass flux, as higher flow rates typically reduce flow resistance. However, as shown in Fig. 25 for the correlation proposed by Amalfi et al.41, a different trend is evident—within their model, the friction factor increases with increasing mass flux across all mixture combinations. Specifically, the friction factor curve corresponding to 100 kg/m2s consistently lies above the curve for 50 kg/m2s, contrary to the expected behavior. This suggests a fundamental limitation in the correlation. In their formulation, the Weber number—proportional to the square of the fluid velocity—is assigned a negative exponent, introducing an inverse dependency that likely contributes to this unusual trend.

A similar deviation from expected trends is also seen in the behavior of the friction factor with respect to mean vapor quality for correlations proposed by Zhang et al.50 and Lee et al.52. It is well established that the friction factor generally decreases as vapor quality increases, due to the higher vapor content reducing the effective viscosity and density of the two-phase mixture, which in turn lowers the pressure drop. However, the correlations proposed by Zhang et al.50 and Lee et al.52 exhibit an opposite trend by predicting increasing friction factor values with rising vapor quality. This behavior, like that of the previous model, arises from the structure of the equations: both Zhang et al.50 and Lee et al.52 formulations include a Reynolds number term with a positive exponent, in contrast to most other models where the exponent is negative. The positive exponent implies that the friction factor increases with more turbulent flow, which is inconsistent with experimentally observed behavior in flow boiling through PHEs. Consequently, these models tend to overpredict the friction factor at higher vapor qualities and mass fluxes, reducing their reliability for pressure drop estimation in PHEs.

Reviewing the performance of all models, most correlations tend to overpredict the friction factor at low vapor quality. Among them, only a few including those by Hsieh and Lin7,12, Han et al.36, and Lee et al.47 were able to reasonably replicate the experimental trend across the full range of conditions. The scaled correlation from Lee et al.47 delivered the most consistent and accurate predictions. This model exhibited a low scaling factor and one of the lowest CoV among all the evaluated correlations, suggesting a high level of agreement with the experimental data. These results point to Lee et al.47 as the most reliable correlation for estimating friction factors in flow boiling of NH3–H2O, NH3–LiNO3, and NH3–LiNO3–H2O mixtures in PHEs.

Future efforts may focus on the development of dedicated correlations that inherently account for salt concentration effects and non-ideal fluid interactions under two-phase flow conditions in PHEs.
