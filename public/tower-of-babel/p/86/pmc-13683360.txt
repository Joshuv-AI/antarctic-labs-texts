Virus-mediated prokaryotic community adaptation dynamics under thermal stress in municipal organic solid waste microbiomes
Authors: Xinyue Kang, Pinjing He, Hua Zhang, Fan Lü
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Temperature influences microbial metabolic activity, which is crucial for biotechnological processes and bioproducts stabilization. However, temperature-driven responses of complex viruses and prokaryotic communities, and the modulatory role of viruses in prokaryotic community within environmental biotechnology systems, remain poorly understood. We developed a continuous thermal stress system with temperature gradients and high-resolution temporal sampling of metagenomics and metatranscriptomics, using municipal organic solid waste as a biological model. An optimized meta-omics pipeline integrating genomic potential and activity was applied to investigate the adaptive dynamics of complex prokaryotic and viral communities. Continuous thermal stress triggered stress responses in paired virus-hosts within the system. Thermal stress exerted distinct effects on temperate and virulent viruses. Viruses formed quasi-symbiotic alliances with their hosts to withstand thermal stress by integrating protein folding genes, stress response, and metabolic function genes, shaping host adaptability under thermal pressure. Equipped with multiple defense and counter-defense systems, viruses accelerated the accumulation of beneficial mutations under thermal stress, enabling them to escape host immunity and intensify competition with prokaryotic communities. This study demonstrates how viruses accelerated both the restructuring and adaptive responses of prokaryotic communities under thermal stress, advancing our understanding of phage-based therapeutic strategies in temperature-variable engineering applications.

Introduction

As parasites or symbionts, viruses drive microbial predator-prey dynamics and play key roles in microbial mortality and shaping biotechnology functions1,2. Viruses can selectively lyse dominant populations, triggering an arms race3,4, or adopt lysogenic replication, conferring novel phenotypes and forming a persistent ecological guild with hosts5. Viral and microbial communities are exposed to a range of environmental stressors6–8, and such fluctuations can disturb the dynamic equilibrium between viruses and microbial hosts, initiating cycles of competition and co-adaptation, which may, therefore, reshape ecosystem functions. Previous studies have shown that viruses can enhance host fitness under environmental stress6,9,10. As a key environmental parameter, temperature affected microbial and viral genome stability11,12 and virus–host interactions13,14. Temperature was widely applied to regulate industrial production efficiency15, maintain bioreactor stability16, and ensure product stabilization17 in the biotechnology system. However, isolation-based laboratory studies on viruses and prokaryotes adaptation overlook the extensive viral and microbial diversity present in environmental communities, thereby disconnecting these findings from real-world scenarios. A growing consensus emphasizes shifting studies of virus–host dynamics from laboratory settings to complex natural communities18,19. Current research on thermal variations in biotechnology systems primarily focuses on prokaryotes16,20,21, while the modulatory role of viruses in complex prokaryotic communities under thermal variations has generally been disregarded.

Despite the current advancements in omics technologies, capturing virus–host interactions in complex environments remains a significant technical challenge. Although the reference-independent metagenomics (MG) is powerful for recovering the majority of community members, the reliability of the DNA-based sequencing approach for investigating virus–host dynamics remains uncertain, as the temporal signals of the community can be significantly dampened by the presence of extracellular DNA in complex environments10,22,23. The metatranscriptomic (MT) approach, representing gene activity, was combined with MG sequencing, which represents genomic potential24,25, offering the potential to capture ongoing virus–host interactions.

Municipal organic solid waste (MOSW), as a key component of energy recovery, harbor diverse native viral and microbial communities and stress-resistance genes1,26. Here, we developed a temperature gradients stress system using MOSW as a biological model, integrating temperature gradients with high-resolution temporal tracking. A multi-level de novo assembly strategy, integrating genomic features from MG and MT and maximizing the recovery of rare taxa (particularly viruses), was employed to comprehensively resolve the dynamic responses of prokaryotic and viral communities to temperature stress. The results deepened our understanding of the plasticity of virus-mediated prokaryotic community adaptability under temperature variability and provided a basis for engineering phage therapy.

Results

Meta-omics of the thermal stress system

The large-scale bioinformatics analysis of MT-single, MG-single, and co-MT-MG de novo assembling resulted in 1.22 × 107 MT contigs, 1.44 × 106 MG contigs, and 1.06 × 107 co-MT-MG contigs (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1). From MG-single- and co-MG-MT-assemblies, 124 MAGs and 2010 viral operational taxonomic units (vOTUs) were retained after dereplication and quality filtering, representing the high-coverage genome pool (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Within the high-coverage genome pool, 21 RNA vOTUs and 11 putative RNA (pRNA) vOTUs were detected, likely originating from RNA virus assembly in the MT data. Via MT-single-assembly, 1.38 × 106 clustered contigs were annotated as prokaryotic and 5036 as viral, including 777 RNA vOTUs, 170 pRNA vOTUs, and 4089 DNA vOTUs, together representing the active genome pool (Supplementary Data 1). The median mapping rates for the high-coverage genome pool were 63.88% in MG (n = 23) and 53.27% in MT (n = 41; Supplementary Fig. 3). For the active genome pool, the median rates were 69.12% in MG (n = 23) and 84.91% in MT (n = 41). A total of 6445 unified vOTUs were obtained from the entire dataset after clustering the vOTUs from the two genome pools. The gene-sharing network revealed low similarity between unified vOTUs in this study and NCBI reference viral genomes, indicating substantial viral novelty (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Decoupling of the high-coverage and active genome pools

The quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) results revealed that the gene concentration of total DNA (both extracellular and intracellular DNA) was significantly higher than that of intracellular DNA in PMAxx-treated samples (p < 0.001, n = 186; Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6), emphasizing the necessity of integrating both high-coverage and active genome pools to accurately capture the community’s temporal dynamics. Members of phyla Actinobacteriota and Firmicutes dominated the high-coverage genome pool (Supplementary Fig. 7a). In contrast, Firmicutes, Firmicutes_A, and Proteobacteria became active following heat treatment, as indicated by MAG-centric alignment of transcripts from the active genome pool (Supplementary Fig. 7b), highlighting the inequivalence between genetic potential and transcriptional activity. Additionally, independent of MAGs-centric alignment, taxonomic classification of prokaryotic transcripts based on the NCBI nr database revealed that the phyla Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteriota were dominant (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Simultaneous mapping of MG and MT reads to the same genomes provided an intuitive contrast across the two pools (Supplementary Fig. 8). A similar trend was observed in the viral community, where only 289 DNA vOTUs from the high-coverage genome pool exhibited active transcription, most of which belonged to a novel family of Caufoviricetes (Supplementary Fig. 9). Exclusive reliance on either genome pool could lead to biased interpretations.

A total of 1920 vOTUs and 118 MAGs were linked in the entire dataset (Supplementary Data 1). Approximately half of the vOTUs (47.86%) were predicted to infect multiple hosts. The dominant host phyla were Firmicutes, Firmicutes_A, and Actinobacteriota. Based on the correspondence of vOTUs between the two genome pools, the global virus–host networks were reassigned to their respective genome pools. Significant nonlinear effects of combined temporal and thermal gradients on virus–host interaction frequency were revealed based on both homology similarity and CRISPR-Cas loci matching approaches in the active genome pool (Supplementary Fig. 13), indicating that the constructed thermal stress system drove the dynamic changes in paired virus–host interactions. However, the effects were not significant in the high-coverage genome pool, likely due to the presence of relic DNA, which may have masked the temporal signals of community dynamics.

Among the 289 DNA vOTUs with transcriptional activity, 109 were assigned to 53 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs). To analyze the dynamics of paired virus–host interactions, vOTUs or MAGs with abundances below 0.01 TPM in more than 80% of samples were excluded, resulting in 51 MAGs retained for analysis. The fold change (FC) of treatment groups was then computed using the 0 h VTR as a baseline, followed by a logarithmic transformation (logFC). Metabolic potential of key hosts actively infected by viruses

To investigate whether viral infection patterns were influenced by host metabolic versatility, 51 active hosts were evaluated using metabolic niche breadth (MNB) and metabolic connectivity (MC). No significant correlation was observed between the number of active virus–host linkages and either MNB or MC (Fig. 3a, b), indicating that overall metabolic completeness and participation in cross-feeding were not the primary determinants of viral host selection in this system. Fine-grained functional profiling, encompassing CAZymes, methanogenesis, and short-chain fatty acids conversion pathways, revealed pronounced functional specialization among hosts with high infection intensity (12 transiently infected hosts). These hosts were significantly enriched in enzymes for degrading mixed-linkage glucans and xyloglucan (Fig. 3c), and the pattern persisted among the top five most frequently infected core hosts (Fig. 3d). The representative host C50-24-bin.3, targeted by three distinct active DNA vOTUs, possessed a complete central carbon metabolism, with near-complete glycolysis (0.89) and TCA cycle (0.88) pathways, indicating robust capacities for organic matter degradation and energy conversion. The presence of a high-affinity cbb3-type cytochrome c oxidase (completeness 1.0) suggested an ability to sustain efficient aerobic respiration under microaerophilic conditions. Divergent impacts of thermal stress on viral lifestyles and transcriptional loads

A total of 769 DNA vOTUs after quality filtering were used for lifestyle classification, of which 133 vOTUs (17.3%) were identified as temperate viruses, 512 vOTUs (66.5%) as virulent viruses, and the remaining 124 vOTUs (16.1%) could not be determined (Supplementary Data 1). At temperatures below 65 °C, the normalized expression ratio of lytic genes in temperate viruses remained relatively stable (Fig. 4a). However, at 65 °C, the ratio exhibited a significant increase compared to other temperature conditions. Elevated temperature triggered a shift of temperate viruses toward a lytic lifestyle at 65 °C. To exclude the possibility that the observed increase in lytic signals of temperate viruses was due to a generalized surge in viral infection, we compared the transcriptional responses of virulent viruses. In contrast, no corresponding increase in viral lysis was observed (Fig. 4b). This divergent response provided evidence that the increased lytic signal in temperate viruses at 65 °C was not driven by a general increase in viral infection efficiency, but rather by a specific prophage induction mechanism that mediated a lifecycle transition. Auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs) in the viral community under thermal stress

A total of 1004 AMGs were identified in DNA vOTUs, participating in 145 metabolic pathways, 19 of which were functionally active (Fig. 5a–c and Supplementary Fig. 14). In the high-coverage genome pool, the average number of AMGs per functional viral genome (AMG intensity) remained statistically consistent across all temperature groups (Supplementary Fig. 15a). In contrast, the proportion of viral lineages carrying AMGs (AMG prevalence) increased significantly at 60 and 65 °C (Supplementary Fig. 15b), indicating that the elevated temperature imposed selective pressure favoring vOTUs harboring AMGs within the system. Unlike in other ecosystems6,9,10, genes directly associated with thermal stress (such as rpoH, dnaK/J, clpB, htpG, fabA/B, recA, and hrcA) were absent in the viral genome. Amino acid metabolism, metabolism of cofactors and vitamins, and transporters were the most prevalent AMG functions. Prokaryotic defense systems, energy metabolism, and amino acid metabolism were the most active and frequent AMG functions. Genes involved in protein folding, sorting, and degradation pathways were actively expressed. The stress system was classified into mild, moderate, and intense levels according to the interaction frequencies between the virus and host communities (Supplementary Table 2). AMGs associated with prokaryotic defense systems exhibited the highest transcriptional activity in the active genome pool, but no significant variation was observed in their expression under different stress conditions (Fig. 5d). AMGs involved in protein folding, sorting, and degradation pathways showed significant upregulation under moderate and intense stress compared to mild stress, suggesting that viruses assist in maintaining host protein homeostasis (Fig. 5e). A large number of stress response-related AMGs were detected. Prophage of C65-120-bin.4 (Acinetobacter baumannii) evolved SOS response-related gene (SOS-response peptidases, Supplementary Fig. 16), which may enable viruses to sense stress and regulate the lysogenic-lytic switch for precise control of lysis timing, potentially modulating the host’s stress response threshold. DNA repair gene (dinI) was detected within ALL-bin.69 (Escherichia coli) prophage suggested that the prophage may play a role in enhancing the host’s DNA damage repair mechanisms, potentially aiding the host in responding to thermal stress. The viral–host arms race driven by the CRISPR-Cas defense system

Recovery of CRISPR-Cas elements was performed for all MG and MT datasets. The number of spacers in precursor CRISPR RNA (pre-crRNA) was significantly and positively correlated with that of protospacer-containing viral contigs (PSCVC) in DNA vOTUs within the active genome pool (adjusted R2 = 0.63, p < 0.0001; Supplementary Fig. 17a and Supplementary Data 1), in contrast to the DNA-level spacer acquisition stage (p > 0.01, Supplementary Fig. 17b). Functionally complete Cas operons were identified on 13 scaffolds, seven of which were active (Supplementary Fig. 17c and Supplementary Data 1). Despite the detection of a high-quality CRISPR array in C50-24-bin.3, the absence of a complete Cas operon prevented the assessment of CRISPR-Cas system activity. A paired analysis of MAGs and viruses with active CRISPR-Cas systems revealed an immune evasion event in the viruses targeting the CRISPR-Cas system of co-C50-72-bin.73 (Pediococcus acidilactici). P. acidilactici encoded a complete, active type II-A CRISPR-Cas locus. It targeted 18 vOTUs (11 unique; Fig. 6a), expanding to 66 vOTUs with CRASS-derived spacer linkages, 51 of which were uniquely targeted (Supplementary Table 3). No anti-CRISPR-Cas (Acr) genes were detected in targeted viral genomes. The protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sequences of vOTUs uniquely targeted in the high-coverage genome pool were identified by aligning 5-base pair (bp) sequences adjacent to the protospacer regions. Apart from a conserved adenine at the fifth position, no conserved PAM sequence was observed, with the trinucleotide AGG being the most frequent motif (Fig. 6b). We tracked the single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) in viral PAM and protospacer regions that presented in over five temperature-time samples. Consistent PAM and protospacer sequences in vOTU C50-120.5740 were identified across individual samples (MG: C-2, C50-72, C50-120, C55-12, and C55-72) and validated by three co-assemblies (co-C-2, co-C50-72, and co-C55-12), with a single mismatch between protospacer and spacer sequences outside the protospacer seed region (a 10–12 bp sequence immediately adjacent to the PAM30, Fig. 6c). In sample C55-336, however, one mismatch in the PAM region and three in the protospacer seed region were identified. SNV frequency analysis revealed four consistently observed low-frequency variants (coverage > 10×) across samples, including C-to-T (2910), C-to-A (2913), G-to-A (2916), and A-to-G (2919; Fig. 6d and Supplementary Table 4). The A2919G transition was reliably detected in sample C55-336, while the C2910G variant may be obscured due to suboptimal sequencing depth. Results supported genuine intra-population genetic variation rather than assembly artifacts. Multi-layered counter-defense systems in the viral community

The viral community encoded eight types of counter-defense systems to counteract the host defense system. The most frequent systems in the DNA vOTUs were anti-restriction modification (anti-RM), anti-CRISPR, anti-Thoeris, and anti-TA systems. In the active genome pool, the most frequent systems were anti-RM, anti-Thoeris, anti-CRISPR, and anti-RecBCD systems (Fig. 7a). Anti-RM and anti-RecBCD systems were the predominantly expressed counter-defense mechanisms in the active genome pool (Fig. 7b). Discussion

This work provided an overview of the adaptive dynamics of prokaryotic and viral communities under thermal stress. An optimized de novo assembly strategy was implemented by integrating genomic features from MG and MT datasets to maximize the recovery of rare taxa, particularly viruses. The intersection and union of the two genome pools generated were fully explored to depict complementary virus–host dynamics at the community level, in contrast to MG-centric transcript-mapping studies17,31,32, which inevitably excluded a substantial fraction of transcripts from metabolically active community members. A decoupling between genomic potential and active transcription was observed, which may be attributed to interference from relic DNA (as confirmed by qPCR), microbial dormancy under stress conditions33, or nonlinear transcriptional amplification during metabolic reprogramming25,34–36.

In both the high-coverage and active genome pools, viral community dynamics were consistently related to those of the prokaryotic host community. Notably, RNA virus remained high abundance even under elevated temperatures (65 and 70 °C), which contrasted with recent findings during hyperthermophilic composting, where few RNA viruses were detected during the thermophilic phase17. The discrepancy may reflect differences in RNA virus composition between studies or variations in detection methods. Active predation was one of the ways viruses contributed to shaping community function under thermal stress. Our findings demonstrated that active viruses in the system preferentially infected hosts occupying specialized functional niches. Hosts with the ability to degrade mixed-linkage glucans and xyloglucan, complex hemicelluloses commonly found in organic waste37, were targeted more frequently by active viruses. Such primary degraders performed the initial rate-limiting hydrolysis that released resources for downstream microbial populations. By focusing on key functional nodes, viruses exerted a top-down influence on the prokaryotic cross-feeding network. Compared to virulent viruses, temperate viruses exerted more complex regulation on the host's metabolic activity38. Although prokaryotic hosts were equipped with diverse defense systems, the integration of temperate viruses into host genomes was widespread in natural environments39–42. Hosts faced dynamic trade-offs between maintaining defense systems, acquiring evolutionary benefits from prophages, and mitigating the risk of cell lysis resulting from prophage induction43,44. In extreme environments, as the main threat to survival shifted from exogenous DNA invasion to environmental stressors, the pattern became more pronounced40–42. The shift of temperate viruses toward the lytic cycle at 65 °C was likely associated with either disruptions in the host intracellular environment or intrinsic stress responses of the viruses, which exhibited narrower thermal tolerance than their prokaryotic hosts13. However, as temperature increased, the number of vOTUs carrying AMGs rose significantly, accompanied by upregulation of protein folding, sorting, and degradation pathways, indicating that the surviving virus–host pairs exhibited an enhanced quasi-symbiotic effect, with selective pressure boosting the net fitness conferred by the integration of beneficial traits.

Multiple active virus–host pairs based on the CRISPR-Cas system were captured in the thermal stress system, with immune evasion by the viral community confirmed at 55 °C within 14 days, highlighting that thermal stress drove CRISPR-Cas immune adaptation and escape. The viral community was equipped with a wide range of defense and counter-defense systems. In addition to typical anti-defense systems, viruses commonly integrated host defense homologs to form a multi-layered counter-defense system, enabling them to counteract host immunity and inhibit competing viruses45–47.

Overall, viruses accelerated both the restructuring and adaptive responses of the prokaryotic community under thermal stress, highlighting the plasticity of virus-mediated prokaryotic responses within complex communities. Although multiple lines of evidence for virus–host interactions were provided by capturing both active and genomic potential dynamics, future studies should integrate higher-resolution approaches, such as single-cell sequencing, to offer direct and real-time insights into virus–host interactions and regulatory mechanisms under thermal stress.

Methods

Setup of the temperature gradient stress system

MOSW collected from a full-scale composting plant located in Zhejiang Province, China, was transported to the laboratory on ice and stored at 4 °C prior to use. Pretreatment of material can be found in Supplementary Note 1. The thermal stress system was set up at temperature gradients of 40, 45, 50, 55, 60, 65, and 70 °C with time durations of 4, 12, 24, 48, 72, 120, 168, 240, and 336 h. Two extra internals of 504 and 720 h were added for groups 40 and 45 °C. A total of 201 sterile 50 mL polypropylene tubes, each containing 25.00 g homogenized material, were heated in a water bath to the target temperatures with an accuracy of ±0.1 °C (PC200, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Processing time was counted till the core of the material reached target temperatures (come-up time: 5–12 min). Air exchange was performed every 24 h. Triplicate tubes were withdrawn of each predetermined temperature-time point and labeled as temperature-time (X°C-Xh). After heat treatment, half of the material was subjected to snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and maintained at −80 °C till RNA extraction, the other part of the material was intended for total DNA extraction, PMAxx treatment, and physiochemical properties measurements, sample processing workflow was shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Materials collected prior to heat treatment were used as the initial samples. Electrical conductivity and pH were measured in 1:10 (w/v) compost to ultrapure water extracts using an electrical conductivity meter (DJS-1, LEICI, China) and a pH meter (PXSJ-216F, Shanghai Precision and Scientific Instrument Co., LTD, Shanghai, China). The supernatant was filtered using 0.45 µm polytetrafluoroethylene filters for the measurement of dissolved organic carbon, dissolved inorganic carbon, and dissolved nitrogen using the TOC meter (Supplementary Data 1; SHIMADZU, Japan).

Nucleic acid extraction, PMAxx treatment, and quantitative polymerase chain reaction

For the material that was not snap-frozen, 0.2 g sub-samples were weighed out in parallel. The first sub-sample underwent PMAxx treatment. The PMAxx dye (20 mmol/L, Biotium Inc, USA) was prepared as a 2 mmol/L stock solution using ultrapure water prior to use. A 0.2 g sample from each sampling point was suspended in 20.0 mL of sterile PBS and gently shaken for one minute to ensure homogenization. A volume of 10.53 µL of the PMAxx stock solution was added to 200 µL of the material/PBS slurry, achieving a final concentration of 100 µM23,48,49. The mixture was incubated in the dark for 10 min with gentle agitation. After incubation, the mixture was exposed to intense visible light (60 W, 465–475 nm, blue light) for 15 min using a PMA-lite LED photolysis device (Biotium Inc, USA)50. Following photolysis, the mixture was used for the extraction of PMAxx-treated DNA. The samples used for total DNA and PMAxx-treated DNA extraction were listed in Supplementary Table 1. A total of 64 samples, represented by 192 PowerBead Pro Tubes, were used for total DNA extraction using PowerSoil™ DNA Isolation Kit (Qiagen, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Additionally, 62 PMAxx-treated samples, represented by 186 PowerBead Pro Tubes, were processed for PMAxx-treated DNA extraction using the same kit and protocol. All extracted DNA and PMAxx-treated DNA were stored at −80 °C prior to qPCR. A total of 69 samples, represented by 207 PowerBead Tubes, were processed for total RNA extraction using the RNeasy PowerSoil™ Total RNA kit (Qiagen, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and concentration of all extracted nucleic acids were assessed using the Agilent 2100 (Agilent, Germany) and the Qubit 3.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

qPCR

The qPCR was performed on total DNA (representing the entire microbial community) and PMAxx-treated DNA (representing intracellular, cell-associated microbes) using a Mastercycler® ep realplex2 (Eppendorf, Germany). 16S rRNA genes were quantified to estimate the abundance of microbial populations, with total DNA reflecting both intracellular and extracellular DNA, while PMAxx-treated DNA selectively represents live or intact cell-associated microbes. Primer pairs targeting the V3-V4 region of the prokaryotic 16S rRNA, 341F (5′-CCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 515R (5′-ATTACCGCGGCTGCTGGCA-3′)51,52, were used.

MG and MT sequencing

For MG sequencing, 0.2 μg of total DNA per sample was used to construct libraries using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. A total of 24 DNA samples were processed, resulting in 23 successfully constructed MG libraries (Fig. 1a). For MT sequencing, total RNA was first depleted of rRNA using the Ribo-Zero Magnetic Kit (Illumina, USA), and 2 μg of rRNA-depleted RNA was used for library preparation with the NEBNext® Ultra™ II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, USA). Among 52 RNA samples, 41 MT libraries were successfully constructed (Fig. 1a). Library quality was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, USA), and qualified libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform using a paired-end 150 bp strategy at Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China; Supplementary Data 1).

De novo single-sample assembly

After reads clipping and trimming using fastp v.0.23.453, MG paired-end reads of each sample were assembled using metaSPAdes v.3.15.554. MT paired-end reads were further filtered to remove ribosomal RNA reads using SortMeRNA v.4.3.655 and assembled with Trinity v.2.15.156 individually (Supplementary Note 2).

De novo co-assembly

The preprocessed MG and MT paired-end reads from the same sample were co-assembled using metaSPAdes v.3.15.554 or MEGAHIT v.1.1.357, depending on memory requirements (Supplementary Data 1). Meanwhile, all the preprocessed MG and MT paired-end reads obtained were combined into a single dataset and co-assembled using MEGAHIT v.1.1.357, resulting in a total of 18 co-assemblies. Read coverage of assemblies were assessed by mapping preprocessed MG and MT reads back to assemblies with Bowtie2 v.2.4.458.

Binning, abundance estimation, and taxonomy of metagenome-assembled genomes

Eighteen co-assemblies and 23 MG single-assemblies were binned using CONCOCT v.1.0.059, MaxBin2 v.2.2.660, and MetaBat2 v.2.12.161. Only the recovered bins with at least 50% completeness and contamination of less than 10% assessed by CheckM v.1.0.1262, were screened for downstream analysis. Quality-filtered bins were dereplicated at 95% average nucleotide identity (ANI) by dRep v.3.5.063 to generate representative MAGs. Chimeric assemblies were assessed and controlled using GUNC v.1.1.064, with a clade separation score threshold of <0.45. MAGs were taxonomically classified using GTDB-Tk v.2.1.165 with the Genome Taxonomy Database (classify_wf, release 207). MAG coverage in both MG and MT data was calculated using CoverM v.0.6.1 (https://github.com/wwood/CoverM) in genome mode, and expressed as transcripts per kilobase of exon model per million mapped reads (TPM). Antiviral defense systems in MAGs were identified using DefenseFinder v.2.0.166.

The functional versatility of each MAG was evaluated using MNB, which was calculated as the total number of unique combinations of functional categories (Func1) and subcategories (Func2) within each MAG, reflecting the overall functional coverage of the genome. The potential of a MAG to serve as a central node within the community metabolic network was assessed using MC, defined as the total number of unique functional subcategories (Func2) present in each MAG. All functional annotations and calculations were performed using METABOLIC v.4.067. Metabolic pathway reconstruction and detailed functional profiling of MAGs were conducted using DRAM v.1.5.068.

Transcripts annotation

Assembled MT contigs sharing ≥95% ANI and ≥85% alignment fraction were clustered using CD-HIT-EST v.4.8.169. Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted using Prodigal v.2.6.370. Predicated transcripts were taxonomically annotated by aligning against the NCBI non-redundant (nr) protein database using the lowest common ancestor algorithm (LCA) with Diamond BLASTp v.2.1.8.16271 and MEGAN CE v.6.21.172. Further functional annotation was performed against UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot (release 2023_05)73, Pfam74, and eggNOG v.5.0.275, all with an e-value cut-off 1e−5. Gene expression level was estimated using Salmon v.1.10.376.

Linkage of transcripts to specific MAGs

To link the de novo assemblies originated from MG and MT data, transcripts were associated with target MAGs based on the following criteria: (1) transcripts exhibited ≥90% BLASTp sequence similarity (alignment length × identity/query length, e ≤ 10−5) with target MAGs; or (2) for transcripts with 80–90% BLASTp sequence similarity (e ≤ 10−5) to target MAGs, the LCA taxonomy based on the NCBI nr protein database was consistent with the GTDB-based taxonomy of MAGs at the species level; or (3) for transcripts with 80%–90% BLASTp sequence similarity (e ≤ 10−5) to target MAGs, taxonomic annotations from UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot (release 2023_05)73 and from eggNOG v.5.0.275 were consistent with the taxonomy of MAGs at the species and genus or family levels, respectively. Transcripts without a matching MAG that met quality and completeness thresholds were taxonomically annotated using LCA analysis against the NCBI nr protein database.

Viral genomes recruitment and classification

Contigs longer than 5.0 kb were screened from 23 single-assemblies and 18 co-assemblies of MG data. Tools, including Virsorter2 v.2.2.477, DeepVirfinder v.1.078, and CheckV v.1.0.179, were applied to minimize false positives of viral contigs. Contigs were selected for CheckV estimation if they met one of the following criteria: (1) VirSorter2 score ≥ 0.95; (2) DeepVirFinder score ≥ 0.9 (p < 0.05); or (3) DeepVirFinder score between 0.7 and 0.9 (p < 0.05) and also detected by VirSorter2. Contigs annotated as “not-determined” by CheckV were discarded1. Retained contigs were clustered using CD-HIT-EST v.4.8.169 with an ANI of 95% and an alignment coverage of 80%, generating the non-redundant set of vOTUs. Abundance of vOTUs was quantified using CoverM v.0.6.1 (https://github.com/wwood/CoverM).

The same procedure above was conducted on 41 MT single-assemblies with a contig length cut-off of 1.0 kb. RdRp-scan workflow80, with minor modifications, was applied to expand the ribovirus dataset. After searching the hypothetical amino acid sequences against the RdRp_HMM_profile database, all HMM-RdRp matches were subject to A, B, and C motifs detection with PalmScan v.2.081,82. RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) protein structures of sequences containing A, B, and C motifs were further validated in Phyre2 sever83 with batch option. Best hits with greater than 90% confidence matching with the PDB_virRdRp_acc list provided80, or those without any confident match to the Protein Data Bank were considered as RdRp-like candidates from the orphan contigs. Viral genomes were clustered using CD-HIT-EST v.4.8.169 with an ANI of 98% and an alignment fraction of 85%84. Abundance/expression of vOTUs were quantified using Salmon v.1.10.376.

The non-redundant vOTUs obtained from MG and MT data were subjected to secondary clustering using CD-HIT-EST v4.8.169 with 98% ANI, generating unified vOTUs from the entire dataset (Supplementary Fig. 2a). ORFs were predicted using Prodigal v.2.6.370. Gene-sharing network between unified vOTUs obtained and NCBI reference viral genomes were conducted using vContact385 with v232 database and visualized with Cytoscape v.3.10.286. The unified vOTUs dataset was categorized into RNA vOTUs, putative RNA vOTUs (pRNA vOTUs), and DNA vOTUs. vOTUs identified in the active genome pool were categorized as either RNA or DNA vOTUs. vOTUs containing RdRp proteins, as confirmed by the RdRp-scan workflow, were classified as RNA vOTUs. vOTUs that were annotated as RNA viruses by DRAM-v68 or eggNOG75 but did not support by the RdRp-scan workflow for validation were classified as pRNA vOTUs. vOTUs not identified as either RNA or pRNA vOTUs were labeled as DNA vOTUs. Similarly, in the MG datasets, vOTUs that shared ≥98% ANI with RNA vOTU were labeled as RNA vOTU. pRNA vOTUs and DNA vOTUs were categorized the same as in the MT datasets.

To establish cross-omics correspondence across datasets, non-redundant vOTUs obtained from MG and MT data were systematically aligned against the unified vOTU dataset using BLASTn v2.14.087, with a sequence identity threshold of ≥98%, coverage of ≥80%, and an e-value cutoff of ≤10−5. Corresponding viral genomes and transcripts were accordingly linked between the MG and MT datasets.

DNA vOTUs that were identified as complete or high-quality by CheckV v.1.0.179, circular by VirSorter2 v.2.2.477, or longer than 10 kb were used for the prediction of temperate and virulent viruses using BACPHLIP v.0.9.388. The identification of lytic and lysogenic genes was performed using PHROG89 annotations.

Viral genome annotation and AMGs identification

vOTUs identified as complete or high-quality by CheckV v.1.0.179, or as full by VirSorter2 v.2.2.477, from MG and MT data were retained for functional analysis. Microbial genes in the viral genome were removed using CheckV v.1.0.179. A gene was considered a viral hallmark or viral-like if it had an e ≤ 10−5 and a bit score ≥50 in at least two of the following databases: VOGdb, CheckV v.1.0.179 viral-specific HMM profiles, or VirSorter2 v.2.2.477 viral HMM profiles. Non-viral ORFs were manually identified as potential AMGs if flanked by viral hallmark or viral-like genes90, while genes within a stretch of more than five consecutive non-viral genes flanked by viral hallmark or viral-like genes were discarded as potential microbial contamination (Supplementary Figs. 2b and 2c). Viral genomes were annotated using DRAM-v v.1.5.068 and eggNOG v.5.0.275 (e ≤ 10−5). AMGs identified by the automatic pipelines DRAM-v v.1.5.068 and VIBRANT v.1.2.191 were included. The abundance and expression level of AMGs were substituted with that of vOTUs carrying AMGs to avoid interference of microbial genes.

Virus–host linkage prediction

Approaches of CRISPR spacers-protospacers matches, tRNAs similarity, and genome nucleotide sequence homology were employed to link viruses to putative prokaryotic hosts. CRISPR array were identified according to Arbas et al.22 with minor modifications. CRISPR elements, i.e., spacers, repeats, and flanking sequences, were recovered from preprocessed data of MT and MG using CRASS v.1.0.192 and predicted in all assembled contigs using MetaCRT93. Non-redundant spacers were queried against the unified vOTUs dataset using BLASTn v.2.14.087 to identify protospacers. Non-redundant flanking sequences and repeat sequences were aligned to MAGs using BLASTn v.2.14.087 to link MAGs with specific CRISPR loci and corresponding spacers. Linkages between PSCVC and MAGs were established by specific spacers-protospacers matching (Supplementary Fig. S2d). Further inspection of CRISPR elements and cas genes in MAGs was applied using CRISPRCasFinder v.4.2.1894. Spacers in CRISPR arrays with evidence level higher than 3 were used for protospacers matching with BLASTn v.2.14.087 (-dust no, -word_size 7). Results obtained were filtered with an 80% sequence similarity cutoff and ≤2 mismatch. MAGs and vOTUs with the same tRNA genes (BLASTn, identity = 100, coverage = 100) identified using tRNA-SE v.2.0.1295 or shared ≥ 70% BLASTn identity at coverage ≥ 75% (e ≤ 10−3, bit score ≥ 50) in the entire dataset were included for virus–host linkages prediction (Supplementary Fig. S2e). Virus–host pairs identified by at least one of three methods that co-occurred in a single sample were identified as valid virus–host interactions. In both the high-coverage and active genome pools, a host was considered present or expressed if its abundance exceeded 1.00 TPM, and a virus if its abundance exceeded 0.01 TPM. The number of valid virus–host interactions was then determined for each sample.

Analysis of sequence variants and prophages identification

Samtools mpileup96 was used to assess the variants and their frequencies at each position in the viral genome based on read mapping data (BAM files). Prophages in MAGs and corresponding attachment sites were identified using PHASTEST97 and VIBRANT v.1.2.191. ORFs from the MT dataset were aligned to prophage genomes using BLASTp with ≥95% sequence similarity and an e-value cutoff of ≤10−5 (Supplementary Fig. S2f).

Statistics and reproducibility

Data normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Statistical analyses were performed in R v4.3.2, Python v3.7.3, and GraphPad Prism v9.5.0 as appropriate. Community diversity metrics were calculated using the Vegan v 2.6-6.198 package in R. Spearman’s correlation analyses were conducted in Python using Pandas v1.3.5 and SciPy v1.7.399. Linear regression was performed using GraphPad Prism v9.5.0. Generalized additive models were fitted using the mgcv v1.9-3100 package to evaluate nonlinear effects of temporal and thermal gradients on virus–host interactions and viral lifecycle dynamics. The model incorporated tensor product smoothers and appropriate distributional assumptions for overdispersed data, with smoothing parameters estimated using restricted maximum likelihood. Model performance was evaluated using adjusted R2, residual diagnostics (gam.check), and effective degrees of freedom.

Replicates and sample sizes were analysis-dependent due to differences in data types and analytical objectives across the high-coverage genome pool, active genome pool, and virus–host interaction datasets. Biological replicates were defined as independently collected and processed microcosm samples. Technical replicates were not used for statistical inference. The sample size (n) for each analysis corresponded to the number of biologically independent samples or paired observations used in the respective dataset and was specified in the relevant figure legends.
