Comparable efficacy and safety for bicuspid aortic valve stenosis patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement with balloon-expandable or self-expanding valves using Wei’s sizing method
Authors: Yung-Tsai Lee, Wei-Hsian Yin, Tien-Ping Tsao, Kuo-Chen Lee, Huan-Chiu Lin, Chun-Ting Liu, Ming-Chon Hsiung, Jeng Wei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The present study aimed to investigate whether proper sizing can improve the procedural and clinical outcomes of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) with new-generation self-expanding valves (SEVs) and balloon-expandable valves (BEVs) for treating bicuspid aortic stenosis (BAS).Methods:We retrospectively evaluated consecutive patients who underwent TAVR with Sapien 3 valves (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA) or Evolut R/PRO valves (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) for severe BAS from 2017 to 2022. The primary endpoints were device success rate and major adverse cardiac and cerebral events (MACCEs), including mortality, nonfatal myocardial infarction or disabling stroke, transcatheter heart valve failure, or clinically relevant valve thrombosis during follow-up. Our team used a complementary approach of supraannular sizing in addition to the conventional annular sizing method (Wei’s method) to guide the TAVR procedures.Results:We recruited a total of 75 consecutive patients, of whom 43 (57%) were treated with BEVs from October 2017 to June 2021, and 32 (43%) were treated with SEVs from July 2021 to December 2022. Device success was similar between the BEV and SEV groups (93% vs 94%; p > 0.99), and no cases of annular rupture occurred in either group. Similar rates of moderate-to-severe paravalvular leak were observed in the BEV and SEV groups (5% vs 6%, p > 0.99). At a median follow-up of 464 days, the MACCE rates were comparable between the two groups. In multivariate analysis, the presence of previous percutaneous coronary interventions (hazard ratio: 5.43; p = 0.039) and New York Heart Association functional class III/IV heart failure at 30 days of follow-up after TAVR (hazard ratio: 9.90; p = 0.037) were independently associated with long-term MACCEs.Conclusion:Our results demonstrated comparable efficacy and safety for BAS patients undergoing TAVR using either BEVs or SEVs when using Wei’s sizing method.Keywords: Balloon-expandable valve, Bicuspid aortic stenosis, Procedural outcome, Self-expandable valve, Transcatheter aortic valve replacement, Valve sizing

1. INTRODUCTION

The use of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) for treating bicuspid aortic stenosis (BAS) remains a subject of ongoing debate due to the unique challenges posed by BAS compared to tricuspid AS.1–7 Advances in the design of sealing skirts in newer-generation transcatheter heart valves (THVs) have led to increased procedural success and survival rates similar to those of patients with tricuspid valve AS undergoing TAVR.8–10 Moreover, previous studies on TAVR for BAS have shown no significant differences in outcomes between balloon-expandable valves (BEVs) and self-expanding valves (SEVs). However, higher rates of annular rupture and post-TAVR transaortic valve pressure gradient (PG) have been reported in patients treated with BEVs, while a higher rate of moderate-to-severe paravalvular leak (PVL) has been reported in those treated with SEVs.3,6,11–14

To aid in device selection and valve sizing, various sizing methods, algorithms, and even computer simulations have been proposed.15–26 Previous studies, including our own, have identified the presence of heavily calcified leaflets and a calcified raphe in type 1 BAS as significant determinants of procedural and clinical outcomes, presenting technical challenges during the TAVR procedure.24–28 At our institution, to address the issues of severe eccentric calcification and calcified raphe, we have applied a complementary approach of supraannular sizing in addition to the conventional annular sizing method (Wei’s method) for TAVR in BAS patients since 2016.28 Our previous study suggested that Wei’s method is beneficial in guiding safer TAVR procedures in BAS patients treated with BEVs.28

In the present study, we hypothesize that proper sizing is pivotal for improving procedural and clinical outcomes with both newer-generation SEVs and BEVs in the treatment of BAS.

2. METHODS

2.1. Patient population

Between January 2017 and December 2022, consecutive patients who underwent TAVR with balloon-expandable Sapien 3 valves (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA) or self-expanding Evolut R/PRO valves (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) for severe BAS were retrospectively recruited. Our institution follows a shared decision-making approach for all patients considering aortic valve replacement, ensuring that the patients’ goals and preferences are incorporated into the final decision-making process. Approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Cheng Hsin General Hospital No. (769) 109A-09, and the requirement for individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

2.2. Choice of device

The decision on whether to perform TAVR and the selection of the prosthesis type and size were at the discretion of the heart team. The balloon-expanding Sapien 3 valve was the most commonly used default device, and it was implanted into 43 patients from October 2017 to June 2021 (the BEV group). To evaluate the effectiveness of Wei’s method in improving outcomes of TAVR with SEVs in BAS patients, we applied this sizing method in 32 consecutive patients who underwent TAVR using new-generation self-expanding Evolut R/PRO valves as the default device from July 2021 to December 2022 (the SEV group).

2.3. Wei’s method for valve sizing, positioning, and deployment

To address the intricate anatomical complexities of the aortic root and valve in BAS patients along with the frequently encountered severe eccentric calcification, we developed Wei’s method at our institution to select the most appropriate valve size.28 As illustrated in Fig. 1, this comprehensive approach integrates both supraannular and annular sizing considerations. Initially, we identify a supraannular plane at end-systole with the minimum aortic leaflet opening to predict the anchoring point for the THV prosthesis. The THV size is determined based on the calculated average diameter, which takes into account both the maximum and minimum diameters at this level. Subsequently, in addition to supraannular sizing, the THV is also sized following conventional annular sizing, based on annular area-derived (for BEVs) or perimeter-derived (for SEVs) diameter. If there is a discrepancy between the proposed THV sizes from the two methods, the prosthesis is ultimately sized according to the plane (annular or supraannular) with the smaller derived diameter. After this, the aortic root, valve anatomies, and membranous septum length are surveyed to assess the risk of aortic root injury, conduction disturbances, and coronary obstruction, and to determine the appropriate implantation depth. During valve deployment, we use a two-step safer implantation approach: we deploy the THV with a size identical to the measured diameter (anchoring), and then, if necessary, we perform postdilatation with or without overfilling to enhance conformity and reduce PVL (optimization).

Wei’s method for valve sizing in bicuspid aortic stenosis. A, After identifying the virtual annular ring, the image is evaluated from the outflow tract to the sino-tubular junction using an MPR view. The supraannular plane with the most severely calcified leaflets and/or raphe is identified, typically located between 2 and 8 mm above the virtual ring. This plane is crucial for anchoring the implanted valve. The intercommissural distance (long diameter) and shortest distance between the leading edge of the calcium and opposite aortic wall (short diameter) are measured at this plane. The mean diameter, which is the average of the long and short diameters, is then calculated. Subsequently, the valve is also sized using conventional annular sizing, based on the annular area-derived diameter for a balloon-expandable valve, or the perimeter-derived diameter for a self-expanding valve. If there is a discrepancy between the proposed valve sizes from the two methods, the prosthesis is ultimately sized according to the smaller derived diameter from either the supraannular or annular plane. B, In case 1 (type 1 bicuspid aortic valve with calcified raphe), the supraannular plane with the most severely calcified leaflets and raphe is 6.8 mm above the virtual ring (upper panel). At that plane, the intercommissural distance (long diameter) is 27 mm, and the shortest distance between the leading edge of the calcium and the opposite aortic wall (short diameter) is 20 mm. The mean diameter, which is the average of the long and short diameters, is 23.5 mm (middle panel). The valve is also sized using conventional annular sizing. The area and perimeter of the virtual annular ring are 552 mm² and 84 mm, respectively, with an area-derived diameter of 26.5 mm, which is much larger than that of supraannular sizing (lower panel). Therefore, the valve is ultimately sized according to the smaller mean supraannular diameter of 23.5 mm in this patient with a “tapered bicuspid configuration.” C, The measurements would be different in patients with a type 0 bicuspid aortic valve with symmetrical, heavily calcified leaflets, as demonstrated in case 2. In this particular case, the most calcified plane is 4.8 mm above the virtual ring (upper panel). At that plane, the long diameter is 27 mm, and the shortest distance between the two heavily calcified leaflets (short diameter) is 17 mm. Therefore, the average diameter is (27 + 17 mm)/2 = 22 mm (middle panel). The area and perimeter of the virtual annular ring are 415 mm² and 73 mm, respectively, with an area-derived diameter of 23 mm, which is similar to that of supraannular sizing (lower panel). The proposed valve size between the conventional annular valve sizing and supraannular sizing methods is in concordance in this patient with a “tube bicuspid configuration.” D, To better understand how to measure the long and short diameters on the supraannular plane under various asymmetrical calcification distributions, additional illustrations of bicuspid aortic stenosis are provided for reference. 2.4. Follow-up and data collection

Echocardiography and clinical follow-up were conducted in all patients before and after the operation according to established criteria.29,30 The risk of mortality after TAVR was assessed using the Society of Thoracic Surgeons Predicted Risk of Mortality (STS-PROM) score. The patients were followed up by the heart valve team through telephone interviews and office visits, with data prospectively collected and entered into our heart valve replacement database.

2.5. Definitions

The primary endpoints were device success rate and major adverse cardiac and cerebral events (MACCEs), including mortality, nonfatal myocardial infarction or disabling stroke, device failure, or clinically relevant valve thrombosis during follow-up. Device success was defined as the absence of procedural mortality and correct positioning of a single prosthetic heart valve into the proper anatomical location with intended performance of the valve (mean PG <20 mmHg, peak velocity <3 m/s, Doppler velocity index ≥0.25, and less than moderate aortic regurgitation).

The distribution of calcification was described based on its anatomical location on the leaflets and commissures according to a TAVR-directed, simplified, nonnumerical classification system for bicuspid aortic valves.15 If the calcium distribution was asymmetrical on the leaflets and/or commissures, it was classified as asymmetrical distribution. Excessive leaflet calcification plus a calcified raphe was defined according to the definition in reference 27.

2.6. Statistical analysis

Data from the database were imported into SPSS version 18.0 for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD and analyzed using the Student’s t test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were presented as percent frequency and compared using Pearson chi-square test or Fisher exact test.

For survival analysis, the patients were divided into two groups based on the occurrence of MACCEs during follow-up. Independent predictors of MACCEs in the study patients were identified using multivariate Cox proportional hazards analysis. Variables with a p < 0.1 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model, in addition to the consideration of BEVs vs SEVs. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Major intraoperative complications did not occur in either group. However, one (2%) BEV patient required implantation of a second valve due to initial implant malpositioning. Significant PVL of at least a moderate degree after TAVR was found in two (5%) patients in the BEV group and two (6%) patients in the SEV group (p > 0.99). Two (5%) patients in the BEV group had a postprocedural transvalvular PG of >20 mmHg. Overall, the device success rates were 93% in the BEV group and 94% in the SEV group (p > 0.99).

At 30 days post-TAVR, both groups had significant improvements in New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class. All successfully treated patients had a notable reduction in prosthetic valvular PG and increase in prosthetic aortic valve area (AVA). Although the estimated AVA was similar between the two groups (BEV vs SEV = 1.9 ± 0.3 vs 1.9 ± 0.4 cm2, p = 0.705), the mean PG was significantly higher in the BEV group compared to the SEV group (11.1 ± 5.2 vs 7.4 ± 3.3 mmHg, p = 0.001). The final implantation depth below the annulus was significantly shallower in the BEV group compared to the SEV group (2.3 ± 1.2 vs 3.9 ± 2.5 mm, p = 0.001), however the incidence of permanent pacemaker implantation due to complete heart block was similar in both groups at 30 days (BEV vs SEV = 5% vs 9%, p = 0.731).

During a median follow-up period of 464 days (interquartile range: 149-812 days), the clinical outcomes between the two groups remained comparable, with MACCE rates of 12% in the BEV group and 13% in the SEV group (p > 0.99). There was no significant difference in mortality between the BEV and SEV groups (2% vs 6%, p = 0.793). One patient (2%) in the BEV group had a nonfatal myocardial infarction, and one patient in each group had a nonfatal stroke (BEV vs SEV = 2% vs 3%, p > 0.99). Three patients (7%) in the BEV group and one patient (3%) in the SEV group had clinically relevant valve thrombosis necessitating anticoagulant therapy. In addition, valve failure requiring reintervention occurred in one patient (2%) in the BEV group.

3.5. Independent prognostic determinants of long-term MACCEs by univariate and multivariate analyses

Kaplan-Meier analysis showed that the event-free survival rate was similar between the BEV and SEV groups (log-rank test, p = 0.604) (Fig. 2). In multivariate Cox proportional hazards analysis, the presence of previous percutaneous coronary interventions (hazard ratio: 5.43; 95% CI, 1.09-27.00, p = 0.039) and the presence of NYHA functional class III/IV heart failure at 30 days of follow-up after TAVR (hazard ratio: 9.90; 95% CI: 1.15-85.21, p = 0.037) were identified as independent factors associated with long-term MACCEs (Table 5).

4. DISCUSSION

The main findings of this study are as follows: (1) Wei’s method can improve the safety of THV implantation with similar device success rates for both BEVs and SEVs; (2) no patient experienced annular rupture, and the rate of moderate-to-severe PVL was similar in both groups; (3) although the estimated AVA was similar in both groups, the mean post-TAVR trans-AV PG was significantly higher among the patients treated with BEVs compared to those treated with SEVs; (4) no difference in the rate of permanent pacemaker implantation was observed, even though the implantation depth was significantly deeper in the SEV group compared to the BEV group; and (5) the presence of concomitant coronary artery disease needing interventions and the presence of significant heart failure symptoms at 30 days after TAVR were independently associated with long-term MACCEs.

Recent data from large registries have shown that the use of newer-generation THVs may lead to better outcomes in patients with BAS compared to early-generation devices.8–10 However, BEV-treated patients may have higher rates of annular rupture and post-TAVR transaortic valve PG, while SEV-treated patients may have a higher incidence of moderate-to-severe PVL. These findings underscore the importance of tailoring TAVR strategies to the unique anatomical and pathological characteristics of BAS patients.

Indeed, several studies, including ours, have consistently identified the significance of unfavorable aortic and leaflet anatomies as the most critical factor impacting device success and long-term clinical outcomes in BAS patients.24–28 Importantly, the presence of a calcified raphe and the heterogeneous distribution of calcium in BAS can impede the optimal expansion of the THV stent frame during TAVR procedures, resulting in elliptical implantation, malapposition, migration, and significant PVL.24–28 Therefore, despite advances in TAVR technology and techniques, addressing the challenges posed by a calcified raphe and eccentric calcium distribution in BAS remains paramount for ensuring successful procedural outcomes. In this regard, the development of comprehensive sizing methods, such as Wei’s method, has proven beneficial in improving the safety of TAVR procedures for BAS patients.28 The key outcomes of the present study include similar device success and moderate-to-severe PVL rates between the BEV and SEV groups, with no cases of annular rupture in either group. These findings underscore the effectiveness of Wei’s method in guiding TAVR procedures for BAS patients, regardless of the type of valve used, thereby enhancing procedural safety and clinical outcomes.

While some studies have suggested that supraannular sizing may not offer significant benefits due to its less reproducible nature and lack of standardized measurement techniques,31–33 our findings suggest otherwise. Our study and others have demonstrated the incremental value of supraannular sizing in certain cases of BAS.9,24–28 Specifically, supraannular sizing, which selects a smaller THV than suggested by annular sizing in about one-fifth of patients with a tapered configuration, can help avoid the risks associated with oversizing. This is particularly relevant for BAS patients with heavier calcification, as they are more likely to have a tapered or funnel anatomy. Moreover, compared to other sizing methods advocated by expert panels such as the circle method,26 CASPER algorithm,25 and LIRA method,24 Wei’s method offers advantages in terms of ease of application, time, and precision in calculating the THV diameter, potentially ensuring safer implantation for BAS patients undergoing TAVR. Moreover, in the present study, we found that patients in the BEV group had significantly larger ascending aorta dimensions and a higher prevalence of aortopathy compared to those in the SEV group. Because we collected consecutive cases in two different periods, with BEV procedures being performed in one period and SEV procedures in the other, the higher prevalence of aortopathy during the BEV period may be a random occurrence.

The use of predilatation in patients with heavy calcification may facilitate crossing of the delivery system and ensure appropriate expansion of the THVs. In addition, the use of contrast injection during predilatation allows for visualization of leaflet behavior in relation to the coronary ostia and aortic wall, helping to mitigate the risks of annular or aortic rupture and coronary obstruction. Moreover, we used predilatation for sizing purposes in the patients whose proposed valve size fell into a borderline range. In doing so, a pigtail catheter was placed above the predilatation balloon, and the C-arm was moved until the coaxial implantation view was obtained. A contrast injection of 15 mL for 2 seconds after partial balloon inflation was performed to achieve optimal visualization of the cusps, assuming that the distance between the cusp hinge points correlated with the annulus diameter. A balloon was placed across the virtual aortic annulus and fully expanded under rapid pacing. If the balloon reached the hinge points, its size corresponded to the valve size. Otherwise, a larger THV could be chosen. At our institution, we choose the size of the predilatation balloon to be 1 to 2 mm smaller than the valve size proposed by Wei’s method. Sizing adjustments are rarely made after predilatation with this approach.

During valve deployment, we adopt a two-step safer implantation approach at our institution: we deploy the THV with a size identical to the measured diameter (anchoring) and, if necessary, perform postdilatation with or without overfilling to enhance conformity and reduce PVL (optimization). By performing postdilatation with or without overfilling, we may improve THV conformity and reduce PVL, thereby optimizing procedural outcomes. Moreover, the higher mean post-TAVR transaortic valve PG observed in the BEV group aligns with previous reports.3,6,11–14 The long-term implications of this difference in PG on hemodynamics and valve durability warrant further investigation through extended follow-up studies.

Previous studies have reported a higher rate of pacemaker implantation in BAS patients undergoing TAVR due to asymmetric THV expansion resulting from a resistant calcific raphe and leaflet fusion.27,34 Nevertheless, we found no significant difference in the rate of permanent pacemaker implantation between the BEV and SEV groups, despite the deeper implantation depth in the SEV group. This could be because we have used the cusp overlap technique since 2021 at our institution, which aims to standardize SEV implantation and achieve more consistent implantation depths. Moreover, our strategy of aiming for a higher implantation depth in BAS patients while balancing the risk of THV malpositioning and embolization is also noteworthy. This approach underscores the importance of careful planning and precise execution in TAVR procedures for BAS patients.35,36

During follow-up, the rates of MACCEs were similar between the two groups. Two independent predictors of long-term MACCEs were identified: the presence of concomitant coronary artery disease requiring interventions, and the persistence of NYHA functional class III/IV heart failure at 30 days after TAVR. Given that BAS patients are typically younger with longer life expectancy and may require additional interventions in the future, these findings highlight the need for comprehensive management strategies to optimize outcomes in BAS patients with multiple comorbidities.

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size in both groups and the single-center nature of the study limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the retrospective design and lack of randomization introduce the potential for selection bias and unmeasured confounders. Third, the study spanned a 5-year period from 2017 to 2022, during which there were significant advances in TAVR techniques, devices, patient selection, and the experience and technical refinements of our heart team. These temporal changes could also have influenced the outcomes. Consequently, caution should be exercised when interpreting the results. Future larger multicenter randomized controlled trials conducted over a shorter and more homogeneous time frame are warranted to provide more definitive conclusions regarding the optimal management of BAS with TAVR.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrated comparable efficacy and safety for BAS patients undergoing TAVR using either BEVs or SEVs when using Wei’s sizing method.
