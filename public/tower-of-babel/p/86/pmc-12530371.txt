Translation and validation of the Malay Patient Measure of Safety-10 (M-PMOS-10) to enhance patient safety in Malaysia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mohd Hanif Mohd Nawawi, Mohd Ismail Ibrahim, Najib Majdi Yaacob
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to translate and validate the Malay version of the Patient Measure of Safety-10 (M-PMOS-10) for use in Malaysian healthcare settings. The study also sought to establish its content and face validity to ensure cultural relevance, psychometric validity and reliability in assessing patient perceptions of safety.DesignA cross-sectional validation study.SettingThe study was conducted in tertiary-level care at a teaching hospital in Kelantan, Malaysia.Participants30 hospitalised patients participated in face validity assessment, while another 100 patients were involved in confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) for construct validation. Eligible participants were adults aged 18–60, fluent in Malay, clinically stable and without a formally diagnosed psychiatric illness.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was the psychometric validation of the M-PMOS-10, assessed through CFA and internal consistency reliability (Raykov’s rho). Content validity was determined using expert evaluations, and face validity was assessed through cognitive interviews with patients. Secondary outcomes included descriptive statistics of patient safety perceptions.ResultsThe M-PMOS-10 demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Raykov’s rho=0.929) and strong factor loadings (majority >0.70). CFA supported a revised single-factor structure (Comparative Fit Index=0.986, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation=0.067). Scale-Level Content Validity Index/Average method (Ave) (0.96) confirmed the relevance of the translated items, while Scale-Level Face Validity Index/Ave (0.87) indicated that patients found the questionnaire items clear and comprehensible. The mean total M-PMOS-10 score was 42.98 (SD=6.14), indicating positive patient safety perceptions, though communication regarding care plans showed room for improvement.ConclusionsThe validated M-PMOS-10 is a reliable and culturally appropriate tool for assessing patient safety perceptions in Malaysia. Its strong content and face validity reinforce its utility in patient safety research and clinical applications. Implementing this tool can help healthcare institutions identify safety gaps, refine staff training and improve communication strategies. Future studies should explore its applicability in different healthcare settings and assess its responsiveness to interventions to enhance patient safety.Keywords: Cross-Sectional Studies, Patient Care Management, Patient Participation

Introduction

Patient safety has become a cornerstone of high-quality healthcare ever since the landmark Institute of Medicine report, To Err is Human, spotlighted the prevalence of preventable medical errors and their devastating impact on patient outcomes.1 In the decades that followed, healthcare systems worldwide adopted extensive measures aimed at reducing adverse events and enhancing patient safety.2 Despite these efforts, patient harm remains a significant global challenge.

Substandard medical care ranks among the top 10 causes of mortality and morbidity worldwide, with an estimated 10% of patients in high-income countries experiencing harm annually.3 4 Among the primary sources of harm are diagnostic errors, medication errors and therapeutic complications, a substantial proportion of which are preventable.5 Meanwhile, in low- and middle-income countries, unsafe care is responsible for over 2.6 million deaths every year.6 Although numerous adverse events occur across all healthcare settings, a meaningful subset is potentially preventable.7

Patient safety is a national priority in Malaysia, with initiatives such as the Malaysian Patient Safety Council and the Malaysian Patient Safety Goals (MPSGs) launched in 2013 to enhance healthcare quality. The MPSGs identify critical domains in patient safety, including medication errors, patient falls and clinical management issues, and require mandatory implementation across healthcare facilities. These initiatives reflect Malaysia’s commitment to fostering a culture of safety and transparency.8 Despite progress, the Malaysian healthcare system faces ongoing challenges. For example, the Malaysia Incident Reporting System documented adverse events such as surgical mishaps, transfusion errors and retained foreign objects, highlighting areas for improvement.9

Capturing the patient’s perspective is increasingly recognised as a vital component of patient safety strategies. Patients offer unique insights into safety threats that may go unnoticed by healthcare professionals, making patient-reported outcome measures an invaluable tool in identifying and addressing safety gaps.10 One such tool is the Patient Measure of Safety (PMOS), designed to evaluate the safety aspects most relevant to patients.11 Recognising the need for a concise yet reliable measure, researchers developed the PMOS-10, a shortened version with strong psychometric properties, including internal consistency, construct validity and responsiveness.12

Given Malaysia’s multilingual and multicultural landscape, adapting the PMOS-10 for local use requires a rigorous translation and cultural adaptation process to ensure semantic, conceptual and functional equivalence.13 14 Such adaptation is essential to preserve the instrument’s validity while addressing context-specific nuances that may influence patients’ interpretations of safety. To ensure the translated instrument retains conceptual and functional equivalence, this study rigorously assessed content and face validity through expert evaluations and cognitive interviews with patients. Content validity ensures that the questionnaire items adequately cover the intended construct, while face validity assesses whether the items appear relevant and comprehensible to participants.

To date, no validated Malay version of the PMOS-10 exists, limiting the ability to capture patient safety perceptions in Malaysia. Given the importance of culturally adapted safety measures, this study aims to translate and validate the Malay version of the Patient Measure of Safety-10 (M-PMOS-10) using a rigorous cross-cultural adaptation process. By ensuring semantic, conceptual and functional equivalence, this research provides a robust tool for healthcare stakeholders to identify safety gaps and enhance patient-centred care.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional validation study was conducted in January 2025 at a teaching hospital in Kelantan, Malaysia. Using a stratified random sampling approach, participants were recruited from medical, surgical, orthopaedic, obstetrics and gynaecology wards. Eligible participants were adult inpatients aged 18–60 years, fluent in Malay, clinically stable and without a formally diagnosed psychiatric illness. Exclusion criteria included patients younger than 18, critically ill patients and those unable to provide informed consent. The survey was administered in paper format during the inpatient stay. The first author personally distributed the questionnaires at the bedside once patients had fulfilled the eligibility criteria and were clinically stable, and patients completed the survey independently before hospital discharge. No postal or home-based surveys were conducted.

Ward administrative personnel assisted in generating a list of admitted patients, and a stratified random sampling approach was used to identify individuals for participation. Before data collection, each participant confirmed eligibility and provided written informed consent. In total, 100 eligible inpatients were approached (25 per department identified via stratified random sampling), and all 100 consented and completed the paper survey during their admission (response rate 100%).

The PMOS-10 instrument

The PMOS was originally introduced in a 44-item format to capture a broad spectrum of patient perspectives on ward safety.11 Although this initial version showed favourable validity and reliability, its length posed challenges for routine clinical use. Through a two-stage process comprising (1) secondary data analyses, for example, examining SD and inter-item correlations and (2) consensus exercises, 14 items were eliminated, producing the 30-item PMOS (PMOS-30), which significantly reduced negatively worded items from 57% to 33%. PMOS-30 exhibited strong internal reliability (α=0.89), yet further refinements were performed based on corrected item-total correlations, culminating in a more concise 10-item version (PMOS-10).

Developed and validated by Louch et al, the PMOS-10 preserved the core psychometric strengths of the longer PMOS measures while offering enhanced usability and reduced respondent burden. Analysis of the PMOS-10 indicated commendable internal reliability (Cronbach’s alpha=0.79), and the tool maintained robust validity in reflecting patients’ evaluations of ward safety.12 The PMOS-10 consists of 10 items, each measured on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (‘strongly disagree’) to 5 (‘strongly agree’), resulting in a total mean score between 1 and 5. Higher mean scores suggest that the patient perceives their care environment as safer, with better communication, clearer instructions, a safe care environment and well-managed procedures. By providing a short, self-contained questionnaire, the PMOS-10 is well-suited for continuous and routine measurement of ward safety performance, offering actionable insights into areas that warrant improvement.

Translation and cultural adaptation

The process of translating and adapting the PMOS-10 into Malay adhered to a comprehensive 10-step methodology adapted from Wild et al (2005) and guided by cross-cultural adaptation principles.15 Formal permission for translation was obtained from the original developers of the PMOS-10 before beginning the study.

Preparation

During the preparation phase, the research team selected two qualified translators and established a timeline for the adaptation process. One translator was a public health medicine specialist experienced in patient safety research, while the other had specialised training in linguistics at Universiti Sains Malaysia. Both translators were thoroughly briefed on the aims of the PMOS-10, relevant patient safety constructs and relevant project deadlines.

Forward translation

Two translators independently converted the PMOS-10 from English into Malay. Translator 1, a healthcare professional, emphasised preserving the conceptual depth of each item, particularly regarding patient safety, while Translator 2, a language specialist, focused on linguistic accuracy and cultural appropriateness. Both translators kept detailed notes on any unclear terms or phrasing during the translation.

Reconciliation

Next, the research team reviewed Malay drafts and merged them into a single preliminary version. An expert panel comprising professionals in clinical care, patient safety and hospital quality management, as represented in table 1 refined this consolidated version. They also checked for face validity and item clarity to ensure they aligned with local cultural contexts.

Back translation

Two additional bilingual translators, unfamiliar with the original PMOS-10, back-translated the reconciled Malay version into English. This procedure was key to uncovering inconsistencies or shifts in meaning. Approximately 2 weeks were allocated for this phase to allow for a detailed linguistic review.

Back translation review

A review committee, which included some members of the original expert panel, compared the back-translated drafts to the original PMOS-10. They scrutinised any conceptual or contextual mismatches and revised the Malay version to correct those discrepancies.

Harmonisation

All versions (original English PMOS-10, Malay draft and back-translated drafts) were compared closely. The remaining inconsistencies were resolved to produce a reconciled Malay version suitable for pretesting. This step ensured the final draft captured the same patient safety constructs embedded in the original PMOS-10.

Cognitive debriefing

A convenience sample of 10 Malay-speaking inpatients took part in the cognitive debriefing phase, using the draft M-PMOS-10. They provided feedback on any unfamiliar or ambiguous words and suggested alternative language where appropriate. Their insights guided refinements to enhance cultural relevance and clarity.

Review of cognitive debriefing results and finalisation

The research team examined all participant feedback from the cognitive debriefing sessions. Adjustments were made based on consensus, resulting in the finalised M-PMOS-10. Emphasis was placed on ensuring that each item remained faithful to Louch et al’s original conceptual framework.

Proofreading

A meticulous proofreading process followed, led by the expert panel. Minor modifications were made to address any typographical or grammatical errors, ensuring the final M-PMOS-10 was linguistically coherent and accurately conveyed the intended meaning of the original items.

Final report

A comprehensive final report detailing each stage of translation and adaptation was compiled. The report documented the justifications for all revisions, summarised expert and participant feedback and provided a transparent reference for future validations or further adaptation efforts. The finalised Malay version of the PMOS-10 is available as online supplemental file 1.

Pretesting

The finalised M-PMOS-10 was subjected to a pretesting exercise at the same teaching hospital to evaluate its clarity and ease of use. A total of 30 patients from the same wards, none of whom had participated in the cognitive debriefing, were randomly selected to complete the scale. All participants received a succinct overview of the study’s purpose and provided informed consent before proceeding.

Pretesting feedback on instructions and item phrasing was minimal, reflecting that the target population readily understood the translated PMOS-10. Minor suggestions for rewording certain phrases were considered and incorporated before embarking on the main data collection phase.

Validity assessment

Content validity was assessed first by a panel of experts, followed by face validity testing with patients. This order ensured that items were conceptually reviewed and refined by subject-matter experts before evaluation of clarity and comprehensibility by patients, consistent with best practices in instrument validation.

Content validity

The content validity of the M-PMOS-10 was assessed using a four-point scale to evaluate item relevance: 1=not relevant, 2=somewhat relevant, 3=quite relevant and 4=highly relevant. A panel of 10 experts, including specialists in public health, clinical practice and patient safety, evaluated each item’s suitability based on their expertise, training and knowledge of patient safety. Experts were purposively selected to ensure representation across disciplines (public health, clinical care, patient safety/quality), and none of the experts participated in the face validity or CFA patient samples, preserving independence across phases.

The evaluation was conducted individually using a self-administered approach. Experts were allowed to provide comments, discuss the clarity of items and recommend modifications. Their feedback was reviewed in a subsequent team discussion, and suggested revisions were incorporated into the questionnaire. The Item-Level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) and Scale-Level Content Validity Index/Average method (S-CVI/Ave) were calculated using the expert ratings. Items rated as 3 or 4 were recoded as 1 (relevant), while those rated as 1 or 2 were recoded as 0 (not relevant).16 The I-CVI for each item was determined using the formula:

I-CVI = (number of experts rating the item as relevant (score 3 or 4))/(total number of experts).

S-CVI=summation of all I-CVI.

S-CVI/Ave = (S-CVI)/(number of items).

A minimum threshold of 0.78 for I-CVI and 0.90 for S-CVI/Ave was used to determine excellent content validity.17 18 All calculations were performed using Microsoft Excel.

Face validity

For face validity testing, we used stratified random sampling to recruit 30 adult Malay-speaking inpatients across medical, surgical, orthopaedic, and obstetrics and gynaecology wards; these participants were independent of the CFA cohort to avoid overlap between phases. Face validity was assessed using a self-administered questionnaire, where patients rated the clarity, relevance and comprehensiveness of each item based on their own experiences with patient safety. Participants rated each item using a four-point scale: 1=the item is not clear and understandable, 2=the item is somewhat clear and understandable, 3=the item is clear and understandable, 4=the item is very clear and understandable. Any ratings of 3 or 4 were recoded as 1 (clear), while ratings of 1 or 2 were recoded as 0 (unclear).16 Participants' misconceptions or suggestions were analysed and incorporated into the final version of the questionnaire.

The Item-Level Face Validity Index (I-FVI) and Scale-Level Face Validity Index (S-FVI/Ave) were calculated using the following formulas:

I-FVI = (number of participants rating the item as clear)/(total number of participants).

S-FVI=summation of all I-FVI values.

S-FVI/Ave = (S-FVI)/(total number of items)

An I-FVI of at least 0.80 was considered acceptable for each item.19 20 Additionally, an S-FVI/Ave of 0.83 or higher was considered indicative of satisfactory face validity for the scale.16 17 All calculations were performed using Microsoft Excel.

Data analysis

Data collected during the pretesting and main study phases were initially organised and analysed using SPSS (V.29) for preliminary assessments. Descriptive statistics, including means, SD, frequencies and percentages, were used to summarise participants' demographic and clinical characteristics. The dataset was then imported into R (V.4.4.2) for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and composite reliability estimation using Raykov’s rho.21 CFA was conducted using the lavaan package with the Weighted Least Squares Mean and Variance Adjusted Weighted Least Squares Mean and Variance Adjusted (WLSMV) estimator to account for the ordinal nature of the Likert-scale items. As the PMOS-10 has an established latent structure validated in the original English and in other language adaptations, we used CFA to test whether the hypothesised structure held in the Malay context. Exploratory factor analysis is more appropriate when no prior structure exists; hence, CFA was selected in line with cross-cultural validation best practices. The sample size of 100 participants adhered to the 1:10 participant-to-item ratio, a commonly cited benchmark for robust CFA results.22,26 Beyond the rule-of-thumb ratio, simulation studies indicate that samples of ≈100 cases provide adequate power and stable solutions for simple CFA models with moderate loadings (≥0.50) and low model complexity; accordingly, n=100 was considered sufficient for the unidimensional PMOS-10 structure.27 28

To assess the internal structure of the M-PMOS-10, CFA was performed using fit indices recommended by Schreiber et al.29 Model fit was evaluated based on the χ² goodness-of-fit test (p >0.05), Comparative Fit Index (CFI ≥0.95), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI ≥0.95), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA≤0.08) and Standardised Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR ≤0.08). Model refinement was guided by standardised residuals (≥|2.58|) and modification indices (≥3.84), ensuring theoretically justified modifications to improve fit.30 Items with factor loadings ≥0.30 were retained,31 while correlation coefficients above 0.85 were examined for multicollinearity concerns. The Akaike Information Criterion and Bayesian Information Criterion were used to compare alternative models, with lower values indicating better model performance.30 Reliability was assessed through Raykov’s rho (≥0.70), indicating acceptable internal consistency.31 Test–retest reliability was not conducted because most participants were hospitalised for short periods, preventing readministration during follow-up. In practice, all 100 participants completed the survey in full, so no imputation was required. Our prespecified plan was to apply Multiple Imputation (MI) (m=5) with Expectation–Maximization (EM) for cases ≤20% missing, excluding cases with >20% missing values.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to compare findings between complete-case and imputed datasets. Given the stratified random sampling strategy, the analytical approach accounted for a proportional representation of patient groups from different wards. Additional sensitivity analyses included outlier detection via Mahalanobis distance and model respecifications to ensure robust conclusions. This rigorous statistical approach supports the validity and reliability of the M-PMOS-10, ensuring its applicability in the Malaysian healthcare context.

Patient and public involvement

Patients contributed to face validity testing (n=30), providing feedback on clarity and cultural relevance of the Malay translation of the PMOS-10. Patients and the public were not involved in defining the research question, study design, recruitment, data analysis or manuscript preparation. Results will be disseminated to participants and stakeholders through hospital briefings and academic presentations.

Results

Content validity

All 10 items of the M-PMOS-10 demonstrated strong content validity. The I-CVI values ranged from 0.80 to 1.00, with all items exceeding the minimum threshold of 0.78 (see online supplemental table S1). Item 10 (‘Staff seemed to struggle to get help when they needed it’) received an I-CVI of 0.80, while all other items scored ≥0.90. The S-CVI/Ave was 0.96, indicating strong overall scale validity. Experts suggested minor modifications, including simplifying medical jargon for better cultural and contextual appropriateness, which were incorporated into the final version.

Face validity

The M-PMOS-10 demonstrated strong face validity. The I-FVI values ranged from 0.80 to 1.00, meeting or exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.80 for all items (table 2). The S-FVI/Ave was 0.87, surpassing the recommended 0.83 threshold. Participants confirmed the instrument’s clarity, with the majority endorsing the items as ‘clear’ or ‘very clear’.

Item‐level analysis revealed that Item 10, which states ‘The staff seemed to struggle to get help when they needed it’ (negatively worded, then reverse‐scored), achieved the highest mean (4.40, SD=0.75). This indicates that most participants disagreed that staff had difficulty obtaining help, reflecting a perception that resources or additional expertise were readily accessible. In contrast, Item 1 (‘I got answers to all the questions I had about my care’) received the lowest mean (4.12, SD=0.89), although most patients still selected ‘agree’ or ‘strongly agree’. Notably, a small proportion (5%) reported ‘strongly disagree’, underscoring a minority who felt that their questions were not fully addressed.

A closer look at the response distributions further emphasises the generally high ratings given to most statements. For example, across items like Item 5 (‘there was always someone available to deal with every aspect of my care’), Item 6 (‘The staff were always able to carry out tasks that they should be able to do’), and Item 8 (‘When I needed treatment, there was always someone available who was trained to do it’), more than 90% of responses fell into the ‘agree’ or ‘strongly agree’ categories. These findings highlight broad confidence in staff capability, availability and responsiveness to patient needs.

In terms of negative statements (Item 2: ‘The staff did not always know when a doctor changed my plan of care’; Item 7: ‘I felt that the attitude of the staff towards me was poor’; Item 10: ‘The staff seemed to struggle to get help when they needed it’), the mean scores remained above 4.0 after reverse‐scoring. Such consistently elevated means imply that participants largely disagreed with statements suggesting inadequate communication, poor staff attitudes or struggles seeking assistance. For example, Item 7 (‘I felt that the attitude of staff towards me was poor’) had a mean of 4.39 (SD=0.68), with almost half of the participants reporting ‘strongly agree’ (once reversed), pointing to a widely shared perception that staff attitudes were respectful and patient-centred.

Although the overall results suggest a positive outlook, variations in SD (ranging roughly from 0.68 to 0.90) indicate that not every patient experienced the same level of communication or care responsiveness. Specifically, areas such as answering patient questions (Item 1) or ensuring seamless communication about changes in the care plan (Item 2) showed slightly more variability. Nonetheless, the small number of participants who expressed dissatisfaction, evidenced by responses in the lower range (1–3), provides meaningful insights into potential areas for improvement.

Taken together, these descriptive statistics highlight a generally favourable perception of safety among patients, with high scores on key domains such as staff competency, availability, communication and responsiveness. At the same time, the spread in responses emphasises the importance of ongoing quality‐improvement efforts, particularly around ensuring all patients feel fully informed and supported throughout their care.

CFA of the PMOS-10

The initial single-factor model (Model 1) demonstrated inadequate fit (χ² = 439.79, df=35, p<0.001, CFI=0.617, TLI=0.507, RMSEA=0.340) (see online supplemental table S2). However, after model refinement by correlating three pairs of residuals (PMOS-2 and PMOS-8, PMOS-4 and PMOS-7, PMOS-6 and PMOS-10) as shown in the path diagram of M-PMOS-10 CFA in figure 1, the revised model (Model 2) exhibited a significantly improved fit (χ² = 46.46, df=32, p=0.047, CFI=0.986, TLI=0.981, RMSEA=0.067, SRMR=0.045). The significant χ² difference test (χ² diff=393.33, df=3, p<0.001) confirmed the superiority of the revised model.

Factor loadings and reliability

The standardised factor loadings and their 95% CIs for all M-PMOS‐10 items are summarised in table 5. All items were significantly loaded onto the latent factor (Factor 1), with standardised estimates ranging from 0.548 (95% CI 0.405 to 0.690) for PMOS‐10 7 up to 0.913 (95% CI 0.872 to 0.953) for PMOS‐10 6. Although Item 7 (‘I felt that the attitude of staff towards me was poor’) loaded at 0.548, this exceeds the commonly accepted ≥0.50 threshold; it was retained for its conceptual importance in capturing staff–patient attitudes. The loadings were generally acceptable to excellent, indicating that each item substantially contributes to the underlying construct. Items with particularly high loadings, such as PMOS‐10 6 (0.913) and PMOS‐10 10 (0.878), emphasise their strong association with Factor 1.

Discussion

The findings confirm that the M-PMOS-10 is a valid and reliable tool for assessing patient safety perceptions. With a high internal consistency (Raykov’s rho=0.929) and strong factor loadings (0.548–0.913), the Malay version aligns with the psychometric properties of the original PMOS-10,12 supporting its suitability for Malaysian healthcare settings. Despite generally positive safety perceptions, variability in responses, particularly regarding communication like Item 1 (‘I got answers to all the questions I had about my care’), highlights the need for targeted interventions. Similar trends have been reported in other PMOS validation studies, highlighting communication as a key area for quality improvement.32 These findings reinforce the importance of patient–provider dialogue in enhancing safety outcomes.11 32

The M-PMOS-10’s psychometric properties are comparable to those of the original English version and other translated versions of the PMOS-10 with similarly high levels of internal consistency and construct validity.12 Our findings also align with recent research emphasising the importance of cultural adaptation in patient safety tools. Several studies highlighted that culturally adapted instruments are more effective in capturing patient experiences, particularly in multicultural settings like Malaysia.33,35 The high content validity (S-CVI/Ave=0.96) and face validity (S-FVI/Ave=0.87) suggest that the M-PMOS-10 is both conceptually sound and user-friendly. These results align with other studies on translated patient safety instruments, such as the Malay Safety Attitudes Questionnaire35 and the Turkish PMOS-10 adaptation,32 which also reported high validity indices. These findings reinforce the tool’s appropriateness for measuring patient safety perceptions in Malaysia.

The final one-factor model of the M-PMOS-10 is illustrated in figure 1, displaying standardised factor loadings and correlations between residuals. Overall, the factor loadings support a unidimensional construct, with estimates ranging from 0.548 to 0.913. The relatively lower loading for Item 7 may reflect cultural sensitivity around reporting negative staff behaviours; nevertheless, it indexes a salient safety domain and was retained on conceptual grounds. These values align with the strong psychometric properties observed in prior PMOS-10 studies.12 The correlated residuals among specific items such as PMOS-10 2 and PMOS-10 8, PMOS-10 6 and PMOS-10 10, and PMOS-10 4 and PMOS-10 7 indicate shared variance not fully explained by the overall factor. In practice, this means some items capture related facets of staff communication, responsiveness or coordination of care. Despite these correlations, the unidimensional structure remains robust, as evidenced by improved model fit indices (χ² = 46.46, df=32, p=0.047; CFI=0.986; TLI=0.981; RMSEA=0.067; SRMR=0.045).

Residual correlations were introduced only when conceptually defensible, specifically where items touched overlapping domains such as staff communication and responsiveness, and not solely on the basis of modification indices. This approach balances statistical fit with theoretical plausibility and is consistent with recommended psychometric refinement practice. These results highlight the coherence of the M-PMOS-10 and confirm that minor item-level overlaps do not undermine the scale’s overall validity or reliability. From an applied perspective, administrators or researchers wishing to use the PMOS-10 can be confident in its capacity to measure a unified construct of patient safety perception, while also noting that certain item pairs may tap into closely related aspects of care.

The availability of a validated M-PMOS-10 provides healthcare institutions in Malaysia with a practical tool for routinely assessing patient safety perceptions. This tool can inform targeted interventions, such as staff training programmes focused on improving communication and responsiveness, as well as policy changes aimed at enhancing patient-centred care. Moreover, the PMOS-10 can be integrated into existing quality-improvement frameworks, such as the MPSGs, to monitor progress and identify areas for enhancement. This aligns with global trends towards patient-centred care, where patient feedback is increasingly recognised as a critical component of safety improvement strategies.10 36 37 While these applications are promising, their clinical and policy impact should be confirmed through implementation and outcome studies that evaluate changes in patient outcomes and safety events over time.

Specifically, hospital administrators can use the PMOS-10 results to guide policy development, enhance staff training and optimise resource allocation, as implementation of targeted policy refinements is informed by identifying items or domains with lower scores, which may indicate communication gaps, unclear care plans or related challenges highlighted by patient feedback, while PMOS-10 data also inform staff training programmes by pinpointing areas such as communication or staff availability that requires focused attention, thereby reinforcing a patient-centred safety culture, and repeated assessments over time allow administrators to track long-term trends in safety perceptions and make informed decisions on resource distribution that ultimately foster safer care environments through adjustments in staffing levels or acquisition of essential equipment.

This study also adds to the growing body of literature on patient-reported safety measures and their role in improving healthcare quality. Future research could explore the use of the M-PMOS-10 in different healthcare settings, such as primary care or outpatient clinics, to assess its generalisability. Additionally, longitudinal studies could investigate the impact of interventions informed by PMOS-10 data on patient outcomes, such as reduced adverse events or improved patient satisfaction. Such studies would provide valuable insights into the long-term effectiveness of patient-centred safety interventions.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including a rigorous 10-step translation and cultural adaptation process that ensured semantic, conceptual and functional equivalence, consistent with guidelines by Beaton et al, and widely endorsed in cross-cultural research.13 14 The involvement of a multidisciplinary expert panel enhanced the cultural relevance and face validity of the M-PMOS-10. Robust psychometric evaluation, including CFA and reliability testing, demonstrated strong validity and reliability, aligning with findings from the original PMOS-10 and other validated translations.12

However, limitations should be acknowledged. The single-centre design in a teaching hospital in Kelantan may limit generalisability, and future studies should include diverse settings such as rural clinics or private hospitals. In addition, the sample was predominantly Malay and drawn from a publicly funded tertiary hospital; therefore, findings may not generalise to other ethnic groups, private facilities or regions with different care delivery contexts. Self-report bias, including social desirability bias, may have influenced responses, as patients might hesitate to report negative experiences, a common challenge in patient safety research.38 Such bias can be amplified in hierarchical care environments, where deference to clinicians and concern about repercussions may suppress negative reporting.39 40 To mitigate this risk, surveys were self-administered and confidentiality was emphasised; nonetheless, residual social desirability bias cannot be excluded. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences or assessment of changes over time, necessitating longitudinal studies to evaluate the tool’s responsiveness.

Furthermore, convergent, discriminant and criterion validity, as well as external validation against existing instruments and cross-validation approaches, were not examined in this initial study due to feasibility constraints. Future research should address these validity types to further strengthen the psychometric evidence for the M-PMOS-10. Test–retest reliability was also not assessed because of the short duration of inpatient stays, which precluded readministration; future longitudinal or outpatient studies should examine temporal stability. Additionally, the study did not explore differences in safety perceptions based on demographic or clinical characteristics, such as age, gender or hospitalisation type. Future research should investigate these factors to identify vulnerable populations requiring targeted interventions.

Conclusion

The translation and validation of the M-PMOS-10 represent a significant contribution to patient safety research in Malaysia. The study demonstrates that the M-PMOS-10 is a reliable and valid tool for assessing patient perceptions of safety, with strong psychometric properties and cultural relevance. The findings highlight the importance of patient-centred approaches to safety improvement and provide healthcare institutions with a practical tool for identifying and addressing safety gaps. The availability of a validated M-PMOS-10 has several implications for practice and research. It enables healthcare providers to routinely monitor patient safety perceptions, inform targeted interventions and enhance the quality of care. Moreover, it provides a foundation for broader national implementation, reinforcing Malaysia’s commitment to fostering a patient-inclusive approach to healthcare improvement.

The validated M-PMOS-10 offers a culturally adapted, psychometrically sound tool for assessing patient safety in Malaysia. Its strong validity and reliability emphasise its utility in hospital settings, allowing healthcare providers to systematically identify and address safety gaps. Future research should expand its application to primary care and outpatient settings, ensuring broader generalisability. By incorporating patient feedback into safety initiatives, Malaysian healthcare institutions can strengthen their commitment to patient-centred care and continuous quality improvement. Future research should also extend validation to include convergent, discriminant and criterion validity, as well as test–retest reliability and sensitivity to change, to build a more comprehensive psychometric evidence base for the M-PMOS-10.
