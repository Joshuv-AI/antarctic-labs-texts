Burden of veno-occlusive disease in tigray region of ethiopia: Evidence from community-based survey
Authors: Mekonnen Haileselassie, Hagos Degefa Hidru, Yimer Mulugeta Agga, Mulugeta Woldu Abrha, Brhane Ayele, Hailay Gebretnsae, Ataklti Gebretsadik, Hayelom Kahsay, Tsegay Hadgu, Nega Mamo, Fisseha Ashebr, Mebrahtom Hafte Ameha, Amanuel Haile, Tesfay Belay Reda, Alem Reda, Daniel Woldemichael, Feyisa Regessa, Geremew Tasew, Getachew Tollera, Mesay Hailu, Abrha Tesfay, Samuel Aregay Gebreslassie, Abiy Girmay Haddis, Senait Tekeste Fekadu, Patrick Okumu Abok, Mussie Alemayehu, Afework Mulugeta

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatic veno-occlusive disease refers to a toxic liver injury leading to obstruction and significant morbidity and mortality. Pyrrolizidine alkaloids are among the commonest causes of toxins. In the Tigray region of Ethiopia, this disease linked to toxic plant Ageratum conyzoides. Disruption interventions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic and the Tigray war, potentially leading to escalated reports of the disease. Therefore, this study aims to assess the burden and distribution of hepatic veno-occlusive disease in the northwestern zone of the Tigray region of Ethiopia.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conduct from January to February 2025, involving 2845 households and study participants were selected using multistage random sampling techniques. The data was collected from a defined population within a specific community at a point in time using Open Data Kit tool and exported to SPSS version 25.0 for cleaning and analysis. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify the association of factors with hepatic veno-occlusive disease. Statistical significance was reported using adjusted odds ratio along with a 95% confidence interval, and at p-value of <0.05.ResultOverall, 6.9% (95% CI: 6.0, 7.9) of households were affected, with 180 cases per 10,000 population. About 250 cumulative deaths per 10,000 population and a notable cumulative case fatality rate (CFR) of 58.1% were found since 2002. This showed with considerable differences among the various administrative districts in the zone. The risk was significantly increased among age groups above thirty, having a larger family size, and believing supernatural force was a cause for the disease.ConclusionUnevenly distributed, high morbidity, mortality, and case fatality rate due to hepatic veno-occlusive disease were found in Tigray region.

Introduction

Hepatic veno-occlusive disease (HVOD) is a type of toxic liver injury that causes blockage of the very small veins. This causes blood to back up in the liver and flow out of the liver, which results in severe morbidity and death [1]. Damage to sinusoidal endothelial cells and hepatocytes in zone three of the liver acinus is believed to be the cause of hepatic veno-occlusive disease [2,3]. Due to the prevalence of sinusoidal blockage, sinusoidal obstruction syndrome (SOS) has been given a different or supplementary name [4]. Abdominal pain, jaundice, and weight gain brought on by the development of hepatosplenomegaly and abdominal fluid retention (ascites) are the hallmarks of HVOD [5]. Doppler ultrasonography can confirm the diagnosis of HVOD, even though histological liver biopsy is the gold standard for doing so. The diagnosis is typically made using the standard case definition, which takes into account the patient's signs and symptoms after ruling out other conditions that could mimic [6,7].

Among the most frequent causes are ingestion of pyrrolizidine alkaloids, use of some medications that can occasionally cause liver toxicity, such as azathioprine and cyclophosphamides, radiation therapy, a reaction following bone marrow or stem cell transplantation, and a number of antineoplastic medications [8,9]. Since pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs) have been found in honey and plant material such tea, herbs, vegetables, cereals, salads, and spices, contamination of food products and plant-based medications with PAs has recently become a major global concern [10]. Pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs) are toxins found naturally in a wide variety of plant species, and they are widely distributed and affect wildlife, livestock, and humans [11,12]. >8000 cases of PA-induced poisoning have been documented in different nations, including the US, Russia, India, Afghanistan, Jamaica, South Africa, and Australia [13]. Furthermore, one or more of the harmful PAs are present in about 3% of flowering plants worldwide. Harvesting wild weeds alongside grain fields and consuming flour tainted by these plants can be the cause of HVOD [14,15].

Depending on the kind of PA and the dosage, the burden of VOD varies. Human acute and short-term toxicity is linked to doses of at least 1 mg PA/kg body weight per day. According to reports, the lowest amount linked to longer-term harm is 15 μg PA/kg body daily [11]. These alkaloids have since been linked to a number of documented outbreaks and cases of human poisoning, the most significant of which involved over 2000 individuals in Afghanistan [10]. In addition to endemic poisoning instances in Jamaica, PA-caused liver illnesses that result in death have been reported in Pakistan, India, and Afghanistan [12]. When VOD is mild, survival is high; however, when it is severe and accompanied by an increase in the hepatic venous pressure gradient of >20 mmHg, death is around 90% [7]. Death from the acute form of the disease can happen anywhere from two weeks to more than two years after exposure, indicating a significant mortality rate [16].

The ingestion of bread prepared from wheat tainted with seeds of a weed known locally as charmac was the cause of the worst outbreak ever recorded in Gulran district, western Afghanistan, which affected an estimated 7800 individuals and resulted in over 1600 deaths [17]. 14 patients were admitted as a result of another VOD epidemic in northern Iraq [18]. In 1975, a VOD outbreak was reported in India's Sarguja area due to the consumption of cereals containing pyrrolizidine alkaloids from plant (Crotalaria sp.) seeds [19].

In Ethiopia, the northwestern zone of the Tigray region, an unknown liver disease with unknown etiology, was first identified in 2002 in the mountainous Tahtay koraro district [20]. A study from this specific area indicated that histopathological examination on the animal liver test result showed severe hepatic necrosis, and the serum sample test result indicated a raised level of some clinical markers that are highly significant for detecting liver abnormalities of toxic origin [21]. These findings highlighted the hepatotoxic potential of pyrrolizidine alkaloids present in Ageratum conyzoides, a plant widely prevalent in the region, known for its capacity to induce liver injury and dysfunction through mechanisms such as hepatic venous-occlusive disease.

The Tigray war which were resulted (80.6%) health posts, (73.6%) health centers,(80%) primary hospitals,(83.3%) general hospitals, and (100%) specialized hospitals damaged and/or vandalized either fully or partially [22]. Despite the initial efforts to mitigate Ageratum conyzoides-related hepatic veno-occlusive disease (HVOD), the high clinical severity leading to hepatosplenomegaly, ascites, pain, and its catastrophic mortality rate, particularly severe forms, those with elevated hepatic venous pressure are significantly increased. The situation has drastically worsened due to the COVID-19 pandemic, which had diverted essential health resources away due to the global crisis and the devastating Tigray war, which halted all intervention activities, leaving the current burden and geographical distribution of the disease entirely unknown. Due to the devastated Tigray war, which has looted majority of the health facility in the region, scarcity of essential services and materials including those had been used for laboratory diagnosis are highly suffered. Given the documented history of sever toxic liver disease in the region and the total disruption of public health surveillance by war and pandemic, there is a critical knowledge gap regarding the current prevalence of HVOD. Due to long time existence of the diseases with in the community and its severity, the community and community health workers are easily identified the characteristics nature of the disease. Therefore, we have conducted community-based survey using the standard case definition to determine the current burden of the disease in the post-conflict Northwestern zone of Tigray in order to re-establishing evidence based life-saving intervention strategies.

Methods and materials

Study area

The study was conducted in the northwestern zone of the Tigray region. The region is divided into seven administrative zones, comprising 93 districts (57 rural and 36 urban) of these, 15 of the districts were found in the northwestern zone (four districts inaccessible due to security issue). The estimated population of the region is to be more than seven million people, with the majority of rural residents [23]. The Northwestern zone of Tigray was specifically targeted for this study due to its high burden of HVOD [24]. This zone borders Eritrea to the north, the Amhara region to the south, the central Zone to the east, and the western zone to the west. The zone consists of various public health facilities, including health posts (108), health centers (38), primary hospitals (4), and general hospitals (2). Economically significant activities in these districts include smallholder mixed crop-livestock agriculture with the most common crops such as sorghum, teff, millet, sesame, niger seed, and wheat barley alongside livestock such as cattle, goats, sheep, donkeys, and chickens, which are prominent features of the local economy. Study design and period

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from January to February 2025.

Target and study population

The target population was the population of the northwestern zone of the Tigray region. The randomly selected districts from the accessible districts of the zone were the source population and the randomly selected tabiyas (smallest administration unit) from the selected districts were the sample population. The selected village from the selected Tabiyas were the study population and all households of the selected village were our study units.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

A multi-stage simple random sampling method was used to obtain the desired population. A minimum 30% representative of districts/ tabias based on the simplified general method for sample surveys of health in developing countries were used to select the tabias [25]. First, five districts were selected using a simple random sampling technique from the eleven accessible districts. Second, a 30% representative of, tabiyas (n = 15) and villages (n = 15) sub-unit of tabiyas were selected randomly from the selected districts and selected tabieas, respectively. Finally, data was collected from all the households residing in these selected villages (n = 15). The study employed a total enumeration sampling strategy, targeting every household within the designated villages. While the primary respondents were heads of households, a critical exception was made for residences containing one or more suspected cases. In these instances, all individual having the suspected cases were interviewed to account for the dose-response relationship of the disease and due to variability in individual consumption patterns. Consequently, a formal sample size calculation with a non-response adjustment was not required. This exhaustive coverage census) approach within the target villages might be eliminated the potential for non-response bias typically associated with sampling-based designs. Therefore, the final sample size for this study was used 2,845 (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Schematic representation of sampling procedure selecting study participants, Northwest zone, Tigray region of Ethiopia, 2025.Fig 2: dummy alt text

Study variables

Dependent variable

Suspected HVOD (yes/no)

Independent variables

Scio demographic characteristics, knowledge and practice related characteristics, and a detailed description of the cases using their socio demographics, diagnosis and management, healthcare utilization, behavioral and perception-related factors were included as independent variables.

Standard case definition of HVOD

Suspected VOD case: is defined as a person with abdominal distention and either a household member sick with similar symptoms or abdominal cramp/pain for at least two weeks. Possible HVOD case: is defined as a person who meets the suspected case definition and has hepatomegaly or splenomegaly. Probable HVOD case: is defined as a person who meets the possible case definition and has a serum alkaline phosphatase greater than or equal to twice the upper limit of the normal [26]. The local community's long-standing experience with the illness, known locally as ‘Guaka’(abdominal swelling), influenced the definition of the suspected case. Local community health professionals are highly skilled at distinguishing the disease from other causes of abdominal distension, like schistosomiasis or malaria, because of its severe nature and unique appearance. We used a strict symptom method and local healthcare workers who had enough knowledge about the disease's natural history to reduce misclassification.

Data collection tool and procedures

Data were collected through structured survey tool and face-to-face interviewer-administered-questionnaires using an open data kit tool (ODK) installed in personal android mobile phones. The questionnaire which adapted from different literatures [24,25] and surveillance data elements of the regional health bureau were included different parts like, socio-demographic, knowledge and practice, and a detailed description of the cases. The characteristics of the disease is well known by the local community and community health workers called as ‘Guaka’ by their local language (abdominal distension) and they are easily differentiated from other common cause of abdominal distention like that of schistosomiasis, malaria, leshimeniasis, hepatitis etc. because of its long time stayed with them, its severity, and other nature of the diseases. As a result, heads of the households from the selected village were participated in the interviewed. The outcome variable (prevalence of suspected HVOD) was determined, if individuals (one or more) suspected cases was found in the household. Then each suspected HVOD cases were interviewed for their socio demographics, diagnosis and management, healthcare utilization, behavioral and perception-related factors. If the age of the case was found below 18 years old, we had interviewed the head of the households about their condition.

Data quality control

Fifteen public health professionals fluent in the local language (Tigrigna) were recruited as data collectors and were trained for two days on the objective of the study, instructions for the method, how to obtain informed consent, how to approach participants, ethical procedures, and general information on the standard case definition of HVOD. The questionnaire was checked using ODK for its completeness, clarity, wording, logical sequence, and skip patterns with the data collectors before their field visit. Daily data collection location, accuracy, and completeness were monitored by the data manager looking at the ODK server-centered. In addition, every day the filled-out questionnaire was checked by data collectors and supervisors.

Data processing and analysis

The collected data was exported to SPSS version 25.0 for cleaning and statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics was performed to summarize categorical data using frequency, tables, percentages, and graphical representations such as bar graphs and pie charts, while mean and standard deviation were used for continuous variables. The association of the independent variables with the outcome variable (suspected HVOD) was investigated using binary logistic regression. Variables having p ≤ 0.15 on the binary logistic regression analysis were candidates for the multivariable logistic regression analysis to control possible confounders. A multi-collinearity test was conducted to see any correlation between the independent variables using the co-linearity diagnostic test (VIF and tolerance test).The assumption for Hosmer Lemshow model for goodness of fit were also checked, and fitness was declared at p-value ≥ 0.05. Statistical significance was reported whenever p-value ≤ 0.05, and the strength of the association was quantified using the adjusted odds ratio (AOR) along with a 95% confidence interval.

The respondents were largely composed of married individuals (82.2%), with near two-thirds (63.1%) living in households of less than five people. More than half proportion (59%) of the respondents were unable to read and write. The population was overwhelmingly rural, with 90.8% identifying as farmers and 83.6% of the respondents represented males. Characteristics related to knowledge and practice

Most of the respondents (81.4%) had heard about HVOD, more than one-third (39.3%) and (89.8%) of the respondents thought as the disease was infectious and not cured respectively. About half (49%) of the respondents were thinking as the disease is not preventable and only half (49.4%) know the prevention mechanism about HVOD. Below half (44.7%) of the respondents used any traditional remedies to treat symptoms of HVOD and above two-thirds (69.3%) of the respondents did not follow the medical advice given by health professionals. The majority (95.5%) of the respondents did not discuss about HVOD in the community, similarly (94.4%) did not participate in any campaign or health education program. In addition, only half (50.1%) of the respondents were aware that ageratum weed is linked to HVOD in the community, and above 17% of the respondents believed a supernatural force (spirit, curse) was the cause of HVOD. Moreover, only half (51.8%) of the respondents were avoiding and willing to avoid the ageratum weed from their farming areas.

Burden and distribution of HVOD

From the total surveyed households, 196 households were found to have suspected HVOD cases, yielding a prevalence of 6.9% (95% CI: 6.0, 7.9). Among these households, 248 (1.8%) individuals were identified as having suspected HVOD cases and 344 (2.49%) cumulative deaths were reported since 2002 (when the index case had been identified in the zone), yielding a cumulative case fatality rate (CFR) of 58.1% and the districts based case fatality rate is attached in (Fig. 3). This suggests suspected burden of HVOD were found 1800 per 100,000 population, with a cumulative mortality rate of 2500 per 100,000 population. Socio-demographic characteristics of the suspected cases

The mean age of the cases was 39.21 years (SD±18.39), with 27.8% falling within the age group 46–60 years. Males constituted two-thirds (65.7%) of the cases, a similar proportion of the cases were unable to read and write. Most cases (98.4%) were farmers, and nearly two-thirds (63.3%) were married, while over half (56.9%) had family sizes exceeding five.

Healthcare utilization of the cases

Most of the cases, 215 (86.7%) had visited health facilities, with nearly three-fourths, 152 (71%), visited more than one facility. From the total, 33 (13.3%) cases that did not visit health facilities, more than half 19 (57.6%) of them responded lack of money was the primary reason for not accessing healthcare services. The mean duration of symptoms was 9.8 years (SD±9.5 years). Two-thirds, 144 (65.6%), were hospitalized at least once, with most, 116 (80.5%), undergoing multiple investigations. Additionally, 20% of cases were admitted to general hospitals. Below half (46.8%) of the cases were used treatment or prevention mechanism currently.

Most, 215 (86.7%) of the cases have not diagnosed with hepatitis. Even though around half, 118 (47.6%) of the cases had consumed ‘Suwa’ (a local drink) in the past and currently, the majority, 207 (83.5%), 225 (90.7%), and 197 (79.4%) of the cases had never consumed any alcohol (beer), ‘teji’, and areki, respectively. Similarly, 243 (98%) of them had never smoked cigarettes. The majority, 244 (98.4%), of the cases did not have additional chronic diseases. More than half, 148 (59.7%) of the respondent did not consume animal products before they develop their cases. The majority, 237 (95.6%), of the cases have not diagnosed with kala-azar, whereas 138 (55.6%) have diagnosed with malaria (Table 2). Nearly two-thirds, 156 (62.9%) of the respondents reported that the progress of their disease outcome had either deteriorated (17.7%) or shown no change (45.2%) since started of their symptoms (Fig. 5). More than half 129 (52%) of the cases thought as HVOD is non-infectious disease but the majority 228 (91.9%) and above two-third 171 (69%) of the cases thought was the disease as a fatal and not cured respectively. Factors significantly associated with suspected HVOD

Twelve variables (districts, age, family size, thought as the disease was infectious and fatal, utilized traditional remedies, following the medical advises, discussed with the community, participated in campaign or health education program specifically about HVOD, believing supernatural force responsible for cause, knowing where to seek treatment, and willing to avoid ageratum weed) were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. Four variables (thought, as the disease was fatal, follow the medical advises, know where to seek treatment, and willing to avoid ageratum weed) were associated with the outcome variable.

Districts such as, Zana, T/koraro, and Asgede were found 6.2, 3.3, 2.9 times more significantly affected by case burden, respectively as compared to districts with low caseload (M/adyabo). Households having family size greater or equal to five were 1.6 times more likely had HVOD as compared to households with family size less than five (AOR=1.6; 95% CI: 1.12, 2.25). Age groups below the mean (43.4 years) were by 40% less likely to develop HVOD as compared to age groups above 43.4 years (AOR=0.6; 95% CI: 0.43, 0.88). Respondents thought as the disease was non-infectious were 50% less likely to had HVOD as compared to those thought as HVOD was infectious (AOR=0.5; 95%CI; 0.34, 0.73). In addition, those did not used any traditional remedies to treat symptoms of HVOD were found 80% less likely develop HVOD as compared to those used any traditional remedies for treating symptoms of HVOD (AOR=0.2; 95%CI: 0.13,0.35). Participants who did not discussed about HVOD were 60% less likely affected by HVOD as compared to those discussed about the case with in the community (AOR=0.4; 95% CI: 0.26, 0.73). Similarly, those who did not participated on health education or campaign programs related HVOD were 60% less likely affected by HVOD as compared to those participated in health education or campaign related the HVOD with the community (AOR=0.4; 95%CI:0.22, 0.62). On the other hand, respondents who believed supernatural force (spirits, curse) was responsible for cause of HVOD were 3.3 times more likely affected by HVOD as compared to those not related the Discussion

This study assessed the burden and distribution of hepatic veno-occlusive disease (HVOD) in the northwestern zone of the Tigray region. Overall, the prevalence in the households was found to be 6.9% (95% CI: 6.0, 7.9), 1800 per 100,000 population were affected by the disease. In addition, 2500 per 100,000 populations were lost due to the disease since 2002, yielding a cumulative case fatality rate (CFR) of 58.1% ranged from 9% to 65.3% across the districts. The burden is significantly high among some districts, those with a large family size, among those believed supernatural force was a cause. However, it occurred less likely among those thought as the disease was non-infectious, those not used any traditional remedies to treat symptoms of HVOD, and those not discussed and participated on health education or campaign programs specifically related to HVOD.

The burden of HVOD in our study revealed that there was a high number of morbidity, mortality, and case fatality rate (CFR). CFR in this finding is higher than studies done in one district of Tigray region (46.3%) [26], western Afghanistan Gulran District (20.5%) [17], western Tigray region (27.6%) [27] and India (42%) [28], but lower than the study conducted in China (88.6%) [29]. This disparities might be due to the devastated Tigray war, from the total 106 health facilities visited by Médecins Sans Frontières / Doctors Without Borders (MSF) teams between mid-December and early March, nearly 70 percent had been looted and >30 percent had been damaged. Moreover, only 3.6% of all health facilities (n = 1007), 13.5% of all hospitals and health centres (n = 266), none of the health posts (n = 741), are functional [30]. This might be limited better and quality healthcare access for diagnosis and treatment of HVOD, including timely intervention and supportive treatments can significantly affect outcomes, which may resulted in a high morbidity and mortality rate. In addition, different in geographical areas may have varying levels of exposure to ageratum conyzoides, regions with high prevalence of this plant may experience higher toxicity levels. Moreover, it might be due to variability in sample size of study populations can influence how exposed to the toxins, leading to differences in disease severity, mortality rates, and which lead to different case fatality rate outcomes.

The burden and distribution across the districts in the zone were found significantly varied, with T/koraro the district where cases had been reported since 2002 which is highly affected to Makel adiyabo district, which is less affected. This variant could be the epicenter of what was formerly known as Hirmi Valley Liver Disease has historically been identified as Tahtay Koraro, especially the mountainous regions of the Hirmi Valley [20,31].Additionally, the district's unique microclimate and soil conditions have led to a higher density and long-term establishment of Ageratum conyzoides, the main putative source of pyrrolizidine alkaloids [29]. Toxic weeds may therefore have been deeply ingrained in the local agricultural ecology in regions where cases have been present for >20 years, resulting in long-term, low-level exposure through contaminated staple cereals like wheat and teff [31,32]. It might also be, prior to 2020, targeted mechanical weeding and health education campaigns were concentrated in high-burden districts like Tahtay Koraro [20].The cessation of these activities during the Tigray war allowed the toxic weed to re-encroach on farm fields unchecked [27,31]. However, variations in altitude, farming methods, or recent changes in land use might be the reason for the decreased incidence in Maekel Adiyabo. While the flatter, more arid lowlands of Maekel Adiyabo may be less conducive to the weed's growth, historical data suggests that Ageratum conyzoides flourishes in certain damp, disturbed highland soils typical of the Tahtay Koraro highlands [20,28].

Households with larger family sizes, particularly those having more than five members, were found 1.6 times more significantly associated with HVOD (AOR=1.6, 95% CI: 1.12, 2.25). This might be because large households require stretching scarce resources to feed more people, especially in post-conflict or economically fragile areas like Tigray's Northwestern Zone. This frequently results in distress purchasing, where households choose less expensive, inferior, or unprocessed grains that might not have been thoroughly cleaned to get rid of harmful weed seeds like Ageratum conyzoides [29]. Bigger families might put quantity ahead of the grain's safety and purity, which would raise the total amount of pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs) that each member consumes. The prevention of HVOD relies heavily on the mechanical removal of "charmac" or A. conyzoides seeds from wheat and teff before milling. In households with many members, the sheer volume of grain required for daily subsistence increases the labor burden on those responsible for food preparation (typically women and children). Higher levels of contamination in the finished flour may result from a less thorough cleaning process if the demand for labor exceeds the household's capacity [29].

Age group above 30 years were found highly affected by the disease, which covered near two-thirds (62.1%) of the burden. This finding is in line with finding reported from one districts northwestern zone of the Tigray region (29 years) [25] and 30 years [24], the reason could be that adults tend to consume more alcohol and eat raw meats, such as animal liver, which increases their risk of developing the disease. Similarly, in this finding, males were affected by two-thirds (65.7%) of the burden. This finding is similar with the finding conducted from September 2006 to August 2016 in Tahtay koraro district, northwestern Tigray, Ethiopia (61.5%) [24], the analysis conducted from 2002 to 2012 in northwestern Tigray of Ethiopia (70%) [26], the result from China (64%) [33]. However, our finding is slightly higher than from the study conducted in China (51.4%) [34], and northern Iraq (57%) [17]. These sex disparities might be due to males are drinking more alcohol, which is a known hepatotoxin and can contribute to liver damage though it is not considered as the primary cause of HVOD. Thus, it is possible that the combination of PA exposure and alcohol consumption could worsen the severity of liver damage.

The belief in supernatural forces (spirits) was a cause of HVOD found significantly associated with the disease, this influence health behaviors and outcomes in the affected communities. This might be due to the reason that communities lacking scientific health literacy frequently blame supernatural origins rather than environmental poisons when a disease manifests with obvious, dramatic symptoms such abdominal swelling (ascites), a characteristic of HVOD [27,31]. Sudden illnesses affecting the stomach or liver are often seen as the product of a curse or evil spirit in rural Ethiopia [35]. Due to this misattribution, households first turn to religious leaders or traditional healers for assistance, which causes significant delays in accessing hospital-based care. Participants who believed in supernatural causes were far less likely to take part in weed-eradication initiatives or health education campaigns, according to a study conducted in the Tigray region.

Individuals, who do not use traditional remedies, not discussed about veno-occlusive disease with the community, and those not participated in any health education and campaign program specifically with HVOD in the community were found less likely to be associated with the disease. This might be due to the unevenly distributed of the ageratum weed among the districts and Tabiyas, these communities may situated in areas having low coverage and exposure of these toxic plants or harmful environmental factors which reduced risk of developing HVOD, as a result, HVOD is not their concern. In addition, communities harvested their crop fields from these less risky areas with ageratum conyzoid toxics were access to safer food sources, reducing the likelihood of consuming harmful substances like raw animal liver or other toxic foods. On the other hand, those did not used traditional remedies may indicate a preference for or trust in conventional medical practices, leading to better health outcomes and proactive health management and they may aware about the scientific prevention mechanism of these toxic plants.

Similarly, communities did not participate in health education programs specifically about HVOD were 60% less likely to cause HVOD. This may be due to the geographical, geological, and environmental factors were not conducive for the weed to grow among their farming crop fields and animal grazing areas. Thus, this may less likelihood the community exposed to the toxic plants and may not develop HVOD due to less dose consumptions, as a result these communities might be less likely participated in any health education or campaign programs specifically about this disease.

Individuals who perceive HVOD as a non-infectious disease were found 50% less likely to be associated with it. This may those who think HVOD as non-infectious may believe that it does not spread from person to person, individuals may focus on dietary and environmental factors that contribute to the disease, such as avoiding toxic plants from their crops used for food recipes, raw animal liver, milk, honey, egg, or excessive alcohol consumption and other harmful food practices. This knowledge can lead to healthier lifestyle choices and proactively can mitigate risk. In addition, this may be more participate to health education initiatives that emphasize prevention through lifestyle changes, reducing their likelihood of developing the disease.

Strength and limitation

This study offers timely and crucial insights into the burden and distribution of suspected HVOD (Hepatic Veno-Occlusive Disease) within the communities of the Northwest Zone of Tigray. Conducted as a community-based cross-sectional survey, it directly addresses a region severely impacted by the catastrophic Tigray war, where HVOD has been a recognized concern since 2022. A significant strength lies in its large sample size, allowing for a robust assessment of the disease's magnitude in the war's aftermath. This research provides much-needed evidence from an area where health data has been profoundly disrupted. However, it is important to acknowledge certain limitations; while symptom-based identification is highly effective in this specific endemic context due to the distinct nature of 'Guaka,' which is able to easily identify by the community, we acknowledge the inherent limitation of lacking gold-standard diagnostic tools like Doppler ultrasound or biopsy. This may lead to over-reporting while unable to rule out other causes of abdominal swelling or under-reporting of early-stage cases where symptoms are sub-clinical. Because symptom-based identification is poor specificity and sensitivity as many clinical manifestations like hepatomegaly, ascites, or fever are non-specific and might be indistinguishable from viral hepatitis, cirrhosis, chronic malaria, leshimeniasis, or schistosomiasis. Furthermore, the calculation of cumulative death and case fatality rates since 2002 may be subject to length bias, potentially distorting long-term trends, which might lead underestimation of the total death and case fatality rate. Given the sensitive nature of severe HVOD and the associated fear of community stigmatization, social desirability biases may have influenced reporting, particularly among the most affected. In addition, while we employed multistage sampling, the sample size was determined based on a 30% representativeness threshold for districts and tabias rather than a standard power-based calculation. Consequently, a design effect was not incorporated into the analysis. This may result in narrower confidence intervals, and the findings should be interpreted with the understanding that the effect was not statistically adjusted for in the final model.

Conclusion and recommendation

Even though unevenly distributed across districts, significantly high morbidity, mortality, and case fatality rate veno-occlusive disease were found. Above six out of ten cases were prone to death though there is variation among districts from 9% to 65.3%. The burden is significantly higher in those with large family size, believing supernatural as cause for HVOD. Two-thirds of the cases were affected males and the mean age group of the cases were 39.21 years. Near to half of the cases thought as the disease was infectious and non-preventable. Two-thirds of the cases claimed as their progress from the disease were deteriorate and no change of improvement. Thus, it is recommended to implement culturally sensitive health education programs that emphasize the risks associated with these factors and promote safe dietary practices. Additionally, further research using a One Health approach is necessary to clarify community misconceptions about the root causes of HVOD and develop comprehensive strategies for its prevention and management.
