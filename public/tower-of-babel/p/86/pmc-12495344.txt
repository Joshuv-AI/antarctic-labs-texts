Improving consenting practice in trauma and orthopaedics: A single centre original mixed methods study
Authors: Kevin Joseph Jacob, Mohammad Naeem Mangal, Jack W M Lamb, Ahmad Abdallatif, Keshav Mathur, Mohammed Elmajee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionInformed consent represents a cornerstone of ethical and legal surgical practice across numerous healthcare systems, including that of the United Kingdom. Despite its recognised importance, achieving consistency and comprehensiveness in the consent process remains a persistent challenge, particularly within busy and time-constrained clinical environments. This study critically evaluates existing consenting practices in the Department of Trauma and Orthopaedics at Worcestershire Acute Hospitals. It also presents the department’s experience with implementing electronic consent (e-consent) forms as a replacement for traditional handwritten documentation.MethodologyA two-phase study was conducted at a single National Health Service (NHS) trust. In the first phase, 102 handwritten surgical consent forms were retrospectively audited against national guidelines to assess compliance in areas such as timing of consent, legibility, provision of information, and documentation of risks and alternatives. The second phase involved a structured survey of clinicians within the orthopaedic department to explore their experiences with the current process and their perceptions of electronic alternatives.ResultsThe audit revealed major shortcomings in the handwritten consent process: only 21 % of forms were completed well in advance of surgery, fewer than half provided adequate procedural information, and just 17 % documented all available treatment options. Over 60 % were poorly legible, and most lacked complete documentation of risks and benefits. Survey results indicated widespread clinician dissatisfaction and a strong preference for e-consent systems to improve legibility, clarity, and patient engagement.ConclusionThe study highlights significant deficiencies in handwritten consent practices and supports adopting standardised e-consent systems to enhance communication, patient comprehension, and medico-legal protection.

Introduction

In the United Kingdom's National Health Service (NHS), thousands of surgeons engage daily in complex discussions with patients to determine the most appropriate operative treatments. Central to these discussions is the principle of 'informed consent', which mandates that surgeons provide patients with all material information regarding their procedures while ensuring comprehension, thereby enabling them to make an informed choice. The ethical interplay between beneficence, non-maleficence, and patient autonomy presents a significant challenge, particularly within the constraints of a time-pressured healthcare environment[1,2]. Optimising patient comprehension of surgical indications and procedures is associated with increased patient satisfaction and reduced preoperative anxiety. Conversely, inadequate patient engagement in the consent process can erode trust and heighten the risk of litigation in cases where postoperative complications arise[3].

A considerable proportion of malpractice claims and allegations of patient harm pertain to factors that precede the surgical intervention. These commonly include issues related to diagnosis, treatment selection, and crucially communication. Effective communication extends beyond verbal interactions between surgeons and patients; it also encompasses the substantive content of preoperative discussions. These discussions should ensure that patients fully understand the risks and complications associated with a procedure, as well as the availability of alternative treatment options[[4], [5], [6]]. When communication is inadequate or documentation is insufficient, patients may feel misinformed or inadequately prepared, which can contribute to dissatisfaction and legal disputes.

The legal framework surrounding informed consent has evolved significantly. The United Kingdom (UK) Supreme Court has established that the materiality of a risk is determined by whether "a reasonable person in the patient's position would be likely to attach significance to the risk, or the doctor is or should reasonably be aware that the particular patient would be likely to attach significance to it"[7]. These ruling underscores the necessity for consent discussions to be highly individualised, addressing not only the physical implications of treatment but also the emotional and psychological concerns of each patient. Identifying which risks are particularly significant to an individual is essential for upholding ethical and legal standards in medical practice.

Among the limited published guidelines on best practices for informed consent, the Royal College of Surgeons (RCS)[8] has outlined four key principles that should underpin consent discussions:1. Despite these recommendations, the practicality of their implementation within the resource-limited NHS setting remains an area requiring further evaluation. To date, there is a paucity of published studies assessing the consent practices of NHS surgeons. This study seeks to improve the standards of the consenting process within the Department of Trauma and Orthopaedics at Worcestershire NHS Acute Hospitals. The primary objective is to evaluate current practices related to obtaining consent for elective orthopaedic procedures, with a particular emphasis on transitioning from handwritten to electronic consent (e-consent) forms. E-consent offers several advantages, including enhanced clarity, accessibility, and standardisation of information, aligning with the guidelines set forth by authoritative bodies such as the Royal College of Surgeons, the British Orthopaedic Association and the General Medical Council[[28], [29], [30], [31]]. These organisations stress the importance of delivering detailed, appropriately timed, and procedure-specific information to patients, thereby ensuring informed decision-making and reducing medico-legal risks.

By critically evaluating current consenting practices and exploring the potential of e-consent systems, this study aims to contribute to the ongoing efforts to enhance patient understanding, improve compliance with legal and ethical standards, and mitigate the risk of litigation within the NHS.

Methodology

Study design and setting

This study was conducted within a single acute hospital trust, which comprises three main sites and operates a total of 750 licensed beds. The study was designed in two distinct phases to evaluate the adequacy and compliance of handwritten surgical consent forms with national guidelines and to explore clinicians' perspectives on the consent process. The sample size was determined based on pragmatic considerations, including the availability of data and the aim to achieve a representative sample of the target population. CHERRIES (Checklist for Reporting of Internet E-Surveys) Tool was used because of emphasis on the importance of documenting the processes used to improve the questionnaire with pilot testing, expert review, and adjustments based on feedback. These steps were taken to ensure that the questionnaire is valid and appropriate for the senior doctors in Trauma & Orthopaedics[27].

The questionnaire was developed based on a review of existing literature and similar questionnaires on informed consent practices (handwritten vs. electronic) and input from subject-matter experts. Draft items were generated to capture clinicians’ attitudes, perceived advantages, and potential barriers. The draft questionnaire was then reviewed by our clinicians in our department for face and content validity. Minor revisions were made for clarity and comprehensiveness. Prior to dissemination, the questionnaire was pilot tested with few clinicians. Feedback from this pilot phase was incorporated into the final version. The final instrument consisted of the items as per the attached appendices. Good response overall achieved.

For transparency and replicability, the full questionnaire has been appended as a Supplementary Appendix.

Part 1: Retrospective Cross-Sectional Audit

The first phase of this study was a retrospective, cross-sectional audit designed to evaluate the completeness and compliance of handwritten consent forms with established national guidelines. Specifically, documentation was assessed against best practice recommendations from the Royal College of Surgeons (RCS), the British Orthopaedic Association (BOA), and the General Medical Council (GMC). The aim was to identify deficiencies in the consenting process and highlight areas for improvement.

Data collection

Handwritten consent forms were randomly selected from surgical records at our hospital over a six-month period, together with the corresponding preoperative clinic letters. The following parameters were extracted and analysed:1. Timing of consent: whether consent was obtained sufficiently in advance of surgery, in accordance with best practice. National guidelines (GMC, RCS, BOA) recommend that consent should be taken at least 24 h before the scheduled procedure, except in emergencies[29].2. Provision of patient information: whether patients were given relevant educational materials (e.g., procedure-specific leaflets). Preoperative clinic letters were cross-referenced; absence of documentation was assumed to indicate no materials provided.3. Review of consent on the day of surgery: whether the consent discussion was revisited on the day of surgery when obtained in advance.4. Legibility of consent forms: assessed independently by two medically trained clinicians. Words were deemed illegible only if both reviewers agreed, reflecting potential difficulties patients may face in understanding the information.5. Documentation of risks and benefits: comparison of recorded information with standardised orthopaedic consent templates to evaluate whether procedure-specific risks and benefits were comprehensively documented. Compliance was defined as inclusion of all expected risks and benefits.6. Discussion of treatment alternatives: whether the consent forms documented discussion of alternative treatment options, including conservative management or opting for no intervention.

Part 2: Clinician Survey

The second phase of the study aimed to gain qualitative insights from clinicians regarding their experiences with the current consenting process. A structured online questionnaire was developed using Google Forms and distributed to all consultants and registrars within the orthopaedic department. The survey comprised a combination of multiple-choice and open-ended questions designed to capture clinician perspectives on key aspects of the consent process. A copy of the full questionnaire is provided in the Supplementary Appendix.

Quantitative data from the consent form audit were analysed using descriptive statistics. Compliance rates were calculated for each parameter, including adherence to best practice recommendations on documentation of risks, benefits, and treatment alternatives. A theoretical comparison of handwritten versus electronic consent forms was also undertaken, drawing on published literature and standardised orthopaedic consent templates. This comparison evaluated key aspects such as completeness, legibility, and usability, highlighting the potential advantages of transitioning to an electronic consent system.

Responses to open-ended and closed-ended questions were analysed thematically and descriptively (frequencies and percentages), following Braun and Clarke’s (2006) approach[36]. Recurring themes included the need for standardisation, concerns regarding legibility, and issues related to the timing of consent. These findings were then contextualised with the audit results to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the challenges faced by clinicians.

Ethical considerations

As this study involved a retrospective review of clinical documentation and an anonymous survey of healthcare professionals, it was classified as a service evaluation and quality improvement rather than research requiring formal ethical approval. Institutional approval was obtained from the hospital trust’s audit and governance committee. No patient-identifiable information was collected or analysed, ensuring compliance with data protection regulations.

Discussion

Informed consent is a cornerstone of ethical medical practice, ensuring that patients fully understand the nature of their treatment, associated risks, and available alternatives before planning. However, this study highlights significant shortcomings in the informed consent process, particularly in the context of handwritten and generic consent forms commonly used in UK NHS Trusts. Many of these forms feature blank spaces for procedural details but lack standardized, procedure-specific explanations, leaving patients vulnerable to receiving incomplete or unclear information.

Staff survey assessing current practice and views on electronic consent forms in our hospital were answered by 40 % of the clinicians in the form of an online questionnaire. Semi-structured qualitative questionnaire (refer to supplementary appendix) with Multiple choice questions (MCQs) and short answers was conducted in the trauma & orthopaedic department where varying levels of experience (from registrars to experienced consultants) was selected within Worcestershire Acute NHS hospitals. A survey conducted as part of this study collected responses from clinicians involved in the surgical consent process (n = 16). The results, summarised in the ‘clinical survey’ section, highlight variations in current practices, preferences, and perceived barriers to the adoption of electronic consent forms. Participants were approached by personal contact by the study lead or by direct email. Reminders were sent only once if emails were not answered.

The omission of critical details regarding the procedure, potential complications, and alternative treatment options can lead to significant patient confusion and uncertainty. The historical context of rehabilitation, manual medicine, physiotherapy, balneotherapy and many other conservative modalities for musculoskeletal disorders is key to the development of conservative treatment for ‘high risk’ patients and those unwilling for surgical treatment[25]. When patients do not fully understand their choices, the risks involved, or the rationale behind their treatment plan, it may lead to a diminished sense of autonomy and an erosion of trust in their healthcare providers[32].

Furthermore, the lack of a standardised approach to informed consent can contribute to inconsistencies in the quality of information provided to patients. Guidelines from the GMC, RCS and other national UK-based bodies on consenting process states that “Timing of consent acquisition” refers to documentation occurring at a sufficient time (at least 24 h) before the scheduled procedure[[28], [29], [30]]. While the GMC, RCS and other UK-based bodies emphasize the importance of timing for consent, the 24-hour timeline is more of a guideline instead of a rigid rule. The key principle is that consent is obtained when patients had enough time and opportunity to understand and make an informed decision. In some cases, discussions may be rushed due to time constraints, and verbal explanations may lack sufficient detail. This variability can result in an inequitable consent process, where some patients receive a more thorough understanding of their procedure than others, depending on their clinician’s approach[33].

Achieving truly informed consent is inherently complex, as patients vary widely in their willingness and ability to engage with medical information. The absence of comprehensive preoperative education may impact patient understanding and decision-making, underscoring the need for improved information dissemination practices. The hand-written consent form consists of written information given to most patients and represents suboptimal documented evidence[19]. Healthcare professionals must emphasize the patient's active participation in decision-making and avoid creating any sense of coercion to agree with the clinician's recommendation[24]. Additionally, the retention of medical information over time can be highly variable, meaning that even patients who receive thorough explanations may struggle to recall essential details when required to reaffirm their consent closer to the time of surgery[10,11].

To address these challenges, several authors have advocated for the integration of supplementary educational materials into the consent process. Strategies such as written information sheets, preoperative educational classes, and video-based instruction have been proposed to enhance patient understanding and engagement[12,13]. For instance, Smith et al. [14] demonstrated that trauma patients who received supplementary written materials had significantly better recall of surgical risks compared to those who relied solely on verbal explanations. Greater attention should be given to the development and use of simplified consent forms, multimedia formatting, personal discussion, and comprehension assessments[21]. This underscores the importance of providing clear, accessible, and standardized information as part of the consent process to improve patient comprehension and informed decision-making.

Effective communication and meticulous documentation during the consent process are therefore critical in mitigating medico-legal disputes. By ensuring that all aspects of the procedure—risks, benefits, and alternatives—are clearly explained and recorded, healthcare providers can protect both patient rights and legal integrity. Notably, Andrew et al. [15] demonstrated that the implementation of the ‘Orthoconsent’ form significantly improved patient comprehension and reduced the incidence of successful medico-legal claims against orthopaedic surgeons. ‘Orthoconsent’ adheres to the parameters of consent, and they intend to constitute a more efficient mode of information provision, with up-to-date descriptions of each procedure including its benefits, risks, and alternatives in simple layman's terms[26].

By embracing ethical best practices with genuine collaboration across interdisciplinary teams, the healthcare community can maximise the full potential of AI in e-consent forms to emphasise personalized data-driven health care which promotes patient care[16]. Acknowledging the diverse demographic within healthcare settings, incorporating multilingual consent forms and culturally tailored educational content could address potential language barriers and improve understanding for patients from different cultural backgrounds.

Introducing a system for patients to provide feedback on the consent process identifies gaps in understanding and help fine-tune future consent practices with real-time feedback. Use of an online survey platform accessed via QR codes for feedback was less expensive, yielded higher response rates, timelier response and quicker analysis of the improvements required[18]. 40 % of the clinicians responded to the online questionnaire which indicated a remarkably high response rate, strong participant engagement and willingness to contribute. This high level of participation resulted in a comprehensive and diverse dataset, which enhanced the reliability and validity of the findings.

With the growing use and benefits of modern technology with enhancement, trends are emerging regarding improvements in patient knowledge and satisfaction with respect to the use of audio/visual tools in terms of their understanding, satisfaction, willingness to participate, and anxiety or other psychological distress [17]. Some patients might benefit from having information provided in various formats. Using audio or video explanations in addition to written content could cater to patients with varying literacy levels and preferences for how they consume information.

According to St John et al. [19] ‘completion of hand-written consent forms suffers from wide variation and is frequently suboptimal. Electronically produced, procedure-specific consent forms can improve the quality and consistency of consent documentation’. The process of writing out and explaining handwritten consent forms can be labour-intensive for healthcare providers, particularly in busy clinical settings and if a patient consented on the day of surgery. This can lead to rushed or incomplete explanations, further reducing the clarity of the consent process.

Legibility to the patient population should be ensured to produce the best results of their understanding and minimising variability of procedure-specific elements and required information from the electronic patient record[20]. From our study, illegible words within the consent body were 60.78 % (illegible) compared to 39.22 % (legible) when presented to 102 patients. It was clearly evident that this ensures reduction in misinterpretations or misunderstandings while going through the information explained to them. The role of readability when enrolling the elderly, illiterate, or disadvantaged minority communities who may have lower reading levels, adds the utmost importance to implement the need for e-consent forms[21].

Missing or incomplete consent is the most common reason for first case delay and patient confidentiality. Physical handwritten forms are prone to being misplaced or lost, leading to potential issues with documentation and legal integrity. Handwritten consent forms often suffer from space constraints that prevent clinicians from providing detailed, procedure-specific information[34]. This limitation can result in overly simplistic or generic consent forms that fail to cover the range of risks, benefits, and alternatives relevant to the patient. This poses a challenge in retrieving patient records when needed for follow-up care or legal reasons. Digital records, by contrast, offer more secure and efficient storage[22].

With respect to the legal implications of consent forms, insufficient prior information provided to the patient and validity of informed consent are considered elements of liability for healthcare professionals which may lead to compensation for damage with the risk of being charged with damages causing harm and breach of the right to self-determination. Benefits and risks of the treatment mentioned within the consent body was 59.8 (not mentioned) compared to 40.2 (mentioned) for 102 patients. If handwritten consent forms are not filled out legibly or comprehensively, this could become a legal issue if a patient later claims that they were not properly informed[23]. A handwritten signature without sufficient detail may be harder to defend in court, whereas e-consent systems can automatically log each step of the process, ensuring transparency and clarity[35].

Study limitations

While this study provides valuable insights into the shortcomings of the current consent process, it is not without limitations. One notable limitation is the subjective nature of assessing handwritten consent forms for legibility. To minimize bias, two medically trained reviewers independently evaluated the forms, and any illegible entries were carefully reassessed before a final determination was made. Nevertheless, subjective differences in interpretation may still have influenced the results.

Additionally, the retrospective design of this study limited the ability to collect real-time patient feedback regarding their experience with the consent process. While this does not necessarily undermine the validity of the findings, future research incorporating direct patient perspectives would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the impact of consent practices on patient understanding and satisfaction.

Another potential limitation is that the study was conducted within a single NHS hospital, which may affect the generalizability of the results. However, given that district general hospitals (DGHs) across the NHS typically follow similar consent protocols, the findings are likely applicable to comparable healthcare settings. Future multicentre studies could further assess whether these issues are widespread across different hospital trusts and specialties.

Conclusion

The findings of this study underscore the pressing need to improve the informed consent process for surgical procedures in NHS hospitals. The current reliance on generic, handwritten forms without standardized explanations risks leaving patients under informed and vulnerable to miscommunication. Enhancing the consent process through structured, procedure-specific forms, supplemented by educational materials, can improve patient comprehension, strengthen trust between patients and clinicians, and reduce the risk of legal disputes. Addressing these deficiencies through policy reform and improved communication strategies should be a priority to ensure that all patients receive the information necessary to make truly informed decisions about their care.

Ethical approval

This study did not involve human participants or animals and therefore did not require ethical approval. It’s a retrospective study of existing patients’ data. The consent forms were anonymized by removing names, hospital ID numbers, and any direct patient identifiers, in compliance with institutional data protection protocols.
