Robotic Insertion of Cochlear Implants and Vestibular Function in Children
Authors: Enrica Fabiano, Baptiste Grenier, Marine Parodi, Daniele De Seta, Isabelle Rouillon, Vincent Couloigner, François Simon, Natalie Loundon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:To analyze the impact of robotic-assisted electrode array insertion on vestibular function after cochlear implantation (CI) in children.Methods:This single-center retrospective study included 80 ears from pediatric patients who underwent CI: 42 in the robotic-assisted group (2021-2024) and 38 in the manual group (2015-2020). All surgeries were performed by the same surgeon. Vestibular function was assessed preoperatively and postoperatively using objective tests, including cervical vestibular evoked myogenic potentials (cVEMP) and video head impulse test (vHIT). Subpopulations were defined based on the availability of complete vestibular data: 68 ears were included in the cVEMP analysis and 74 in the vHIT analysis. Statistical analysis was performed using the Fisher exact test and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.Results:Overall mean age at CI was 56±51 months and post-CI vestibular assessment was performed at a mean delay of 18±13 months. In the robotic group, 100% of the sample (n=36) preserved saccular function (cVEMP), compared with 85% (n=27/32) in the manual group (P=0.02). vHIT analysis revealed no statistically significant differences between groups in canal function preservation; 92% (n=36/39) of the robotic group and 100% (n=35/35) of the manual group maintained normal canal responses postoperatively. Per-canal delta-gain analysis for vHIT showed no significant changes for any canal (anterior: P=0.75; lateral: P=0.06; posterior: P=0.92).Conclusions:Robotic-assisted electrode array insertion had significantly higher post-CI cVEMP preservation compared with manual techniques after pediatric cochlear implantation. This may suggest that robotic slow electrode array insertion is less traumatic for the vestibule and helps preserve saccular function.

Introduction

Cochlear implantation (CI) has become a widely used and effective treatment for restoring hearing in children with profound deafness. While its benefits for hearing are well established, its impact on vestibular function—essential for balance and spatial orientation—remains an area of investigation. This is particularly relevant in children, who may have difficulty describing dizziness, compensate quickly through central mechanisms, and face challenges in completing objective vestibular assessments1,2.

Vestibular dysfunction in early childhood, particularly if bilateral, can significantly impact motor development, delaying milestones such as sitting, standing, and walking. It can also affect cognitive functions, including reading and spatial orientation3,4. Studies indicate that vestibular dysfunction is common in children with hearing loss, with a reported prevalence ranging from 20% to 70%5,6. After cochlear implantation, vestibular abnormalities occur in 20% to 80% of patients, with vertigo reported in 2% to 35% of cases. Approximately one-third of patients develop significant vestibular disturbances, underscoring the potential impact of CI on inner ear function7,8. CI-related vestibular dysfunction, especially saccular function, can result from various mechanisms, including direct trauma from electrode array misalignment, disruption of inner ear fluid homeostasis, inflammatory responses, or unintended electrical stimulation of vestibular receptors9. In addition, foreign body reactions may lead to labyrinthitis or vestibular fibrosis, further impairing function10,11.

To minimize trauma and preserve both vestibular function and residual hearing, modern CI surgical techniques focus on reducing the impact on inner ear structures. Surgical strategies to enhance preservation include the use of flexible, atraumatic electrode arrays with small-diameter tips, insertion through the round window instead of cochleostomy, intraoperative lubricants or corticosteroids, and soft surgical techniques to minimize cochlear trauma during electrode array placement12. A slow insertion speed has been associated with better residual hearing preservation and may also help reduce vestibular damage12,13. Robotic systems allow for very slow and controlled electrode array insertion, maintaining an optimal trajectory that follows the cochlear basal turn. By reducing human limitations such as hand tremors, motion inconsistencies, and variability in manual techniques, robotic systems improve surgical accuracy and reduce intraoperative trauma14,15.

Only 2 studies in adults have examined its impact on vestibular function. Although the vertigo patient reported outcomes were significantly improved after robotic-assisted CI, no objective vestibular tests or postural stability improvement were reported compared with manual insertion techniques16,17.

The objective of this study was to assess the impact of robotic-assisted CI surgery on vestibular functions in children.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective, monocentric study was conducted in a tertiary reference pediatric cochlear implant center. All patients met the clinical eligibility criteria for CI defined by the French National Authority for Health18. Two groups of pediatric patients who underwent cochlear implantation were included: one group with robotic electrode array insertion and a control group with the standard manual insertion technique. The robotic group consisted of consecutive patients implanted between September 2021 and May 2024 using the RobOtol® system (Collin ORL, Bagneux, France), introduced in the center in 2021. The manual group included consecutive patients implanted between March 2015 and June 2020, before the availability of the RobOtol® system. Ears were excluded from the analysis if they presented with preoperative vestibular areflexia or if they had undergone cochlear reimplantation or explantation. In addition, ears were included only if both preoperative and postoperative vestibular assessments were available. Patients were classified based on the etiology of hearing loss and its potential correlation with vestibular involvement. Based on this classification, 2 groups were defined: the vestibular involvement (VI) group, including patients with conditions known or suspected of affecting vestibular function, and the no vestibular involvement (NVI) group, including diseases where vestibular impairment is absent or extremely rare.

All procedures were performed by the same experienced surgeon following a standardized protocol, which included a retroauricular incision, mastoidectomy, posterior tympanotomy, and electrode array insertion through the round window. In the robotic group, electrode array insertion was performed at a controlled speed of 0.3 mm/s using a semi-automated system, with prior calibration of the insertion axis. All patients received straight, atraumatic electrode arrays from major cochlear implant manufacturers (Cochlear, MED-EL, or Advanced Bionics). No precurved electrode arrays or devices intended for ossified or fibrotic cochleae were used.

Vestibular function assessment

Preoperative and postoperative vestibular function was assessed using the video head impulse test (vHIT) and cervical vestibular-evoked myogenic potentials (cVEMP)19,20. Other tests could be performed, but were not assessed in this study.

cVEMP

The cVEMP test was used to evaluate saccular otolithic function using an age-appropriate recording method. Stimuli consisted of 500 Hz tone bursts delivered via bone conduction (BC below 4 years of age) or air conduction (AC above 4 years of age) at 95 or 105 dB nHL, without threshold-seeking, with impedances <5 kΩ. Electromyographic (EMG) activity was monitored to confirm adequate muscle contraction. A response was classified as present when a reproducible biphasic waveform (P1–N1) was observed in at least 2 averaged trials.

vHIT

High-frequency semicircular canal function was assessed using the vHIT with the Synapsys system (Inventis, Padova, Italy), employing a remote-camera system. Gain values were considered within normal limits when they were ≥0.77 for the lateral semicircular canal (LSCC) and ≥0.65 for both the anterior (ASCC) and posterior semicircular canals (PSCC)21.

A partial vestibular deficit was defined as an altered gain in 1 or 2 canals, whereas a complete vestibular deficit was diagnosed when function was absent in all 3 semicircular canals.

Statistical analysis

To optimize the analysis of vestibular data, patients were divided into 2 subpopulations: the vHIT group and the cVEMP group, allowing for variability in data collection, as some patients had only undergone one of the 2 tests. Results were presented as mean ± standard deviation [minimal–maximal] for continuous variables, and as number (percentage) for categorical variables. Groups were compared using the Wilcoxon rank test for continuous variables and the χ2 or the Fisher exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. A 2-sided P-value ≤0.05 threshold has been used to define statistical significance. All analyses were performed using R Software 4.

Ethics

The agreement of the hospital ethics committee and General Data Protection Regulation registration (registration number N° 20250708180525) was obtained for this study.

Results

No patients in our study experienced intraoperative or postoperative complications or reported any clinical abnormalities. In addition, no cases of dizziness, instability, or bilateral vestibular deficit were observed after surgery.

The mean age at implantation was 67±51.8 months in the robotic group and 44±46.6 months in the manual group, with an overall mean of 56 ± 50.5 months. Inner ear malformations were identified in 15% of the sample (12/80), with a prevalence of 17% in the robotic group and 11% in the manual group. The etiology of hearing loss remained unknown in 64% of cases, while conditions with frequent or possible vestibular involvement (VI) were observed in 16% of patients, with a higher prevalence in the robotic group (24%) compared with the manual group (8%) (P=0.07).

The groups were overall comparable: statistical analysis did not reveal significant differences between the groups for categorical variables such as sex, side of cochlear implantation, etiology prone to VI, or inner ear malformations (P>0.05). Electrode array characteristics were also similar across groups; no formal statistical comparison was performed due to the limited sample size.

However, 2 statistically significant differences were observed: the mean age at implantation was significantly higher in the robotic group compared with the manual group (69±53.0 mo vs. 46±47.7, P=0.03, Wilcoxon test), while the mean post-CI vestibular follow-up delay was significantly longer in the manual group compared with the robotic group (P=0.01, Wilcoxon test).

Vestibular test execution and subpopulation selection

Among the total sample, 85% of ears (68/80) had analyzable presurgery and postsurgery cVEMP data, while 93% (74/80) had analyzable presurgery and postsurgery vHIT data. According to predefined exclusion criteria, n=12 were excluded from cVEMP analysis and n=6 from vHIT analysis. The final distribution included 39 robotic and 35 manual ears for vHIT (n=74) and 36 robotic and 32 manual ears for cVEMP (n=68) (Fig. 2).

cVEMP: primary analysis

Among the 68 ears included in the cVEMP analysis, 7% (5/68) exhibited an absence of saccular response in the postoperative period. All ears in the robotic group (36/36) maintained a normal cVEMP response, whereas in the manual group, 15% (5/32) experienced a loss of saccular response. This difference reached statistical significance, suggesting a possible association between the type of insertion and saccular function loss, with a trend favoring the robotic group (P=0.02, the Fisher exact test, Table 2). No other significant associations were observed with categorical variables.

Postimplantation cVEMP assessments were performed at a mean delay of 19±13.6 months (2.6 to 62.6). Timing differed between groups, with later testing in the manual group (25±15.9 mo) compared with the robotic group (14±8.6 mo; P = 0.001). No significant difference in testing delay was found between ears with and without postoperative cVEMP loss (P>0.05).

The mean age at implantation for the 27 ears in the manual group that preserved saccular function was 49 months, while the 5 ears that lost cVEMP responses belonged to younger patients (aged 16, 44, 26, 23, and 16 mo, respectively). Among these 5 cases, 2 involved potential vestibular fragility related to either the underlying etiology or a malformation-associated condition, such as prematurity or a global inner ear malformation (P=0.53, Fisher exact test).

An exploratory subgroup analysis was performed in high-risk patients (inner ear malformations or vestibular-related etiology). Within this subgroup, postoperative cVEMP loss occurred in 2/6 ears in the manual group and in none of the 14 ears in the robotic group (Fisher exact test, P=0.079), with no differences in age at implantation or follow-up interval between groups (Supplemental Table 3, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/MAO/C492).

Regarding electrode array characteristics, all 5 cases shared comparable physical properties, with relatively small dimensions (eg, 20 to 23 mm in length and basal diameters of 0.6 to 0.8 mm), and no distinctive features were identified.

vHIT: secondary analysis

Among the 75 ears included in the vHIT analysis, 4% (3/75) exhibited a partial loss of canal function following cochlear implantation. However, no cases of complete canal impairment were detected in the cohort. vHIT analysis did not reveal any statistically significant differences in canal function preservation between the 2 groups. Specifically, 92% (36/39) of the robotic group and 100% (35/35) of the manual group maintained normal canal responses postoperatively (Table 2). All ears with postoperative partial canal deficits exhibited involvement of the lateral semicircular canal (LSCC), with gain values of 0.76, 0.68, and 0.42. In addition, one case also showed a deficit isolated to the posterior semicircular canal (PSCC) (gain=0.64). All head/eye velocity recordings were reviewed and showed reproducible reduced eye velocity as well as overt saccades.

These deficits were detected at 12-, 17-, and 15-month postsurgery, with an average age at implantation of 62 months (136, 42, and 9 mo, respectively). Among ears with postoperative LSCC deficits, one had IP2-Mondini malformation, while none had an etiology associated with vestibular weakness (VI). Interestingly, in 5 cases, postoperative vHIT was normal, although an abnormal gain had been found preoperatively.

Per-canal delta-gain analysis

The preimplant and postimplant vHIT analysis of vestibular gain across the anterior, lateral, and posterior semicircular canals did not show statistically significant differences (anterior: P=0.75, lateral: P=0.06, posterior: P=0.92, Wilcoxon test). Although the lateral canal gain showed a trend toward significance, the distribution of values in the box plots (Fig. 3) suggests substantial overlap between preoperative and postoperative measurements. Interestingly, the postoperative mean gain for the lateral canal was slightly higher than the preoperative value, which may reflect random variability or sample size limitations. However, the interpretation of these results is limited by the small number of ears with complete preimplant data, particularly in the manual group, where only 4 (anterior), 7 (lateral), and 5 (posterior) patients had available data.

Discussion

In this study, all ears in the robotic group retained post-operative cVEMP responses compared with 84% in the manual group (P=0.02). Conversely, canal function showed no significant differences between groups and none of the patients in either group had complete canal function loss. Delta-gain analysis across the three canals confirmed that there were no significant canalar changes.

The present findings suggest a potential advantage of robotic-assisted insertion in preserving saccular function in pediatric cochlear implantation. Although statistically significant, this difference should be interpreted cautiously. The 2 groups were overall comparable in terms of general sample characteristics. The only significant differences observed were related to age at implantation and vestibular follow-up. The robotic group had a higher implantation age than the manual group due to the fact that, for ethical reasons, the robotic system had initially been used in our center for older children, as the robot had been previously validated in adults15.

In terms of vestibular follow-up, additional timing analysis showed that cVEMP assessments were performed significantly earlier in the robotic cohort than in the manual group (P=0.001), leaving less time for potential spontaneous recovery of vestibular function. Taken together, these differences should be considered when interpreting the results. However, the shorter interval to postoperative testing in the robotic cohort likely acts against it and may therefore reinforce the observed trend while mitigating the comparative limitation between groups.

Post-CI vestibular function: general findings in the overall sample

Cochlear implantation is frequently associated with vestibular function changes, with postoperative dizziness reported in 2% to 35% of cases, while vestibular abnormalities are detected in 20% to 80% of pediatric patients7. Two meta-analyses on both pediatric and adult populations have identified caloric tests, cervical and ocular evoked myogenic potentials (oVEMP) as the most affected vestibular assessments postimplantation, while vHIT responses tend to be more variable and generally preserved11,22. A retrospective study on 34 children found higher deterioration rates in cVEMP (53.3%) and oVEMP (52.0%) compared with vHIT results (P<0.05), with caloric tests showing hypofunction in 50% of postoperative cases23. In our study, we decided not to consider caloric test results, as several factors may influence their reliability in post-cochlear implantation assessment. The presence of the electrode array, fibrin glue, and muscle grafts filling and covering the round window can modify the tympanic cavity volume and alter heat movement, potentially affecting the consistency of the results and leading to a higher rate of false negatives. In our manual insertion sample, 15% of patients exhibited a loss of cVEMP responses, while no patient showed evidence of canal hypofunction on vHIT, which aligns with previous reports.

Impact on cVEMP

Previous studies highlight the sacculus as particularly vulnerable due to its proximity to the cochlea, with reported postoperative impairment rates ranging from 21% to 100%24. Surgical technique plays a crucial role in its preservation, such as slow insertion speed, as in our study, saccular function impairment was exclusively observed in the manual insertion group. Existing literature on robotic insertion’s impact on vestibular function primarily focuses on adults and reports no significant differences compared with standard surgical approaches regarding objective vestibular tests (cVEMP, vHIT, caloric tests), subjective visual vertical deviations, or posturography findings16,17. However, some studies suggest a beneficial effect of robotic insertion on postoperative vertigo symptoms, likely due to a more controlled insertion speed13. Notably, Derieppe et al.16 found a higher incidence of postoperative dizziness in the manual group compared with the robotic group (P<0.05). This protective effect could be due to several key factors: optimizing the insertion speed helps reduce mechanical stress on the inner ear, reducing the risk of inflammation and fibrosis. Also, slow insertion could reduce perilymphatic volume displacement caused by electrode array insertion in the scala tympani, which could directly affect the sacculus via the scala vestibuli. Consistent with this hypothesis, previous studies using robotic-assisted insertion systems have demonstrated improved hearing preservation following cochlear implantation25–27. Although hearing and vestibular outcomes reflect different aspects of inner ear function, both may be influenced by reduced insertion-related trauma.

CVEMP responses are particularly vulnerable in the early postoperative weeks, with the most significant reduction occurring within the first 5 days24. However, some studies report recovery within 2 months, while follow-ups extending up to 11 years highlight individual variability, with cases of permanent deterioration28. In our cohort, 5 ears in the manual group showed absent cVEMP responses at an average of 25 months postsurgery, consistent with a potential long-term saccular impairment rather than an early, transient postoperative alteration. This distinction is clinically relevant in children, as persistent saccular dysfunction may impact motor and postural development. Importantly, patients with cVEMP loss were not tested earlier than those with preserved responses (P>0.05). Among the patients with absent responses, 2 had additional risk factors, such as inner ear malformations (IP2-Mondini) or premature birth, which could have influenced the outcomes regardless of the surgical approach. However, an exploratory subgroup analysis in high-risk patients (inner ear malformations and/or vestibular-related etiology) still showed a consistent trend toward better cVEMP preservation in the robotic group. In addition, these 5 ears belonged to patients who were younger than average at the time of implantation compared with those without vestibular impairment, although this finding should be interpreted with caution. Previous studies suggest that early implantation is more of a protective factor than a risk factor, and the literature has not identified a correlation between age and objective cVEMP alterations29. An alternative hypothesis is that lower compliance in younger patients may have made vestibular testing more difficult, potentially affecting the results.

Impact on vHIT

In our study, 8% (3/39) of ears that underwent robotic-assisted insertion exhibited partial postoperative canal loss, whereas vestibular function remained intact in the manual insertion group. No statistically significant differences were found between the 2 groups, concerning the proportion of abnormal SCC function or in gain variation. Notably, no patient experienced complete vestibular loss, aligning with existing literature that suggests SCCs are less susceptible to surgical trauma than otolithic structures23. The 3 cases with postoperative deficits all had lateral semicircular canal (LSSC) involvement, with one also showing posterior semicircular canal (PSSC) impairment. This is consistent with the literature, where Wang and colleagues reported a 9.4% deterioration rate for the LSSC in vHIT, compared with 2.9% for the SSCC and 3.0% for the PSCC, with caloric tests showing greater LSCC impairment (P<0.05). Interestingly, 5 ears showed spontaneous SCC recovery from partial deficits to normal function after surgery, possibly due to vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR) maturation or improved compliance with testing, with preoperative false negatives9. This also highlights the reproducibility issues of vHIT in infants, where variability in test performance, especially for vertical canals, a smaller number of head impulses per canal and interpretation may influence results. Vestibular assessment in children remains challenging, as Verrecchia et al.30 suggest, a vestibular deficit should be confirmed by multiple tests. Repeated testing over time is essential to confirm deficit stability.

Despite contributing to the understanding of robot-assisted insertion’s impact on pediatric vestibular function, our study has limitations. The variability in follow-up durations and the lack of standardized preoperative and postoperative assessment time points make direct comparisons between groups challenging, as vestibular conditions are evaluated at different postsurgical intervals. Another limitation is the reproducibility of vestibular tests, highlighted by cases of canal function recovery in some patients. Lastly, because only 5 patients exhibited cVEMP loss, the number of events was insufficient to allow a meaningful multivariate analysis adjusting for age or postoperative vestibular testing delay. The results of this retrospective study should be interpreted with caution, and a prospective controlled analysis is required to confirm the protective nature of robotic insertion for vestibular function in children.

Conclusion

Robotic-assisted electrode array insertion showed a significantly higher post-CI cVEMP preservation compared with manual insertion, which suggests an increased saccular function preservation. Canal function remained largely preserved across both groups. These observations suggest a potential protective effect of slow robotic insertion, which could be less traumatic for the vestibule and especially for the saccule. These promising preliminary results for an improved organ preservation strategy in children's robotic CI provide a basis for further prospective controlled trials to confirm the trend.
