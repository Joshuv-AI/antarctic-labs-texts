Association between internet addiction and musculoskeletal disorders in Iranian medical sciences students
Authors: Mohammad Amin Nochian, Seyedhamidreza Mousavi, Hamed Khosravi, Mohammad Hasan Basirinezhad, Seyedmohammad Mirhosseini
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe utilization of the Internet can lead to adverse outcomes in students’ lives. Prolonged Internet usage may result in academic failure, an elevated risk of musculoskeletal problems, and the emergence of issues impacting the lives of students and their peers. This study aimed to explore the relationship between Internet addiction and musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) among students in the field of medical sciences.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, 314 medical sciences students in Semnan, Iran, were assessed using the census sampling method. The participants’ Internet addiction status and the presence of MSDs were determined through Young’s Internet Addiction questionnaire and the Nordic self-report questionnaire, respectively. Data underwent analysis employing logistic regression test to ascertain the relationship between Internet addiction and MSDs.ResultsAmong the 314 participating students in this research, about 21% and 1.6% of the participants were lay open to and suffered from Internet addiction, respectively. Internet addiction was associated with the presence of pain, discomfort, burning, or numbness in the neck, wrist, upper back, hips, and thighs. For each unit increase in the Internet addiction score, the chance of neck and wrist pain rises by 3% compared to a 2% increase in upper back and hip/thigh pain.ConclusionThe findings of this study indicate that excessive Internet use is linked to a heightened risk of MSDs in medical sciences students. Consequently, it is imperative to raise awareness about these complications and promote the adoption of proper posture when using the Internet. Further research is also warranted among diverse student populations to investigate the impact of Internet addiction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-024-07780-9.Keywords: Internet addiction, Musculoskeletal disorders, Students

Background

Nowadays, the Internet has become a crucial tool for fostering interaction, social communication, and daily activities, especially for students. It plays a pivotal role in meeting the basic needs of individuals, to the extent that life without the Internet would be unimaginable [1]. Approximately half of global Internet users reside in Asian countries [2]. In Iran, mobile phone and Internet usage have surged to 85% and 35%, respectively, marking a 25-fold increase in Internet users compared to the last decade [3]. This growth is notably higher than in other Middle Eastern countries [4]. Students utilize the Internet for various educational and academic purposes, such as accessing information from electronic databases, engaging with teachers and peers, participating in online courses, and undertaking learning activities. Beyond academics, they also leverage the Internet for communication, entertainment, leisure, and social media interactions. These diverse uses have contributed significantly to the escalating Internet usage among students [5, 6].

With the heightened usage among students, the potential emergence of Internet addiction, defined as the “uncontrolled and harmful use of the Internet,” becomes a concern [7]. Despite its numerous advantages, the Internet can introduce new problems or exacerbate existing ones. The term “addiction” is no longer confined to substances like drugs but has extended to behavioral patterns, including Internet usage. This behavioral addiction is primarily driven by the excessive use of mobile phones, laptops, tablets, and computers, contributing to its classification as a social issue affecting both Eastern and Western societies [8]. This growth in the prevalence of internet addiction is mainly caused by smartphone usage [9]. The previous study found that smartphone addiction is increasing significantly in many countries around the world [10]. There has been an apparent increase in Internet addiction during the COVID-19 pandemic [11]. With young people having more free time and courses being conducted through cyberspace and the Internet, the conditions have become conducive to the exacerbation of this addiction [12]. Various studies have reported the prevalence of Internet addiction among students to range from 5 to 25% [13]. Study on students in Mexico and Spain have reported an average Internet addiction rate of 11.75% [14]. In North India, medical students showed a significantly higher Internet addiction rate, reaching 56.5% [15]. An Iranian study focused on medical students indicated a 44% addiction rate to social networks [16]. The consequences of Internet addiction, characterized by an excessive increase in Internet usage hours, can be detrimental. These negative outcomes encompass academic failure among students [17], manifesting in careless behaviors, inattention, and behaviors that may displease friends and families [18]. The impact of Internet addiction extends beyond behavioral aspects, causing physical problems such as changes in weight and damage to vision, as well as psychological issues, including anxiety and depression [19–21]. With the rise of mobile technology, people are increasingly using their smartphones and tablets to access the internet for a wide range of activities, including communication, social networking, and messaging [22]. Students, who represent a significant and influential demographic, are particularly reliant on mobile internet access for their studies, research, and educational pursuits [23].

People especially university students spend significant amounts of time on their smartphones for various purposes (such as communication, social media, texting, and etc.) can caused musculoskeletal disorders [24]. A systematic review provided evidence that neck flexion, the frequency of phone calls, texting, and gaming are related to musculoskeletal complaints among mobile device users [25]. Several studies have also highlighted that prolonged Internet use can contribute to musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) [26, 27]. Additionally, it is crucial for health specialists to be aware of the impact of excessive use of digital devices on physical health. When individuals use digital devices like smartphones, laptops, tablets, and computers, their typical posture involves the device being positioned lower than eye level, necessitating a downward gaze. This usage pattern often leads to the adoption of poor posture, particularly the bending of the neck, which is frequently sustained for extended periods [25]. Research indicates that the prolonged and repetitive use of these digital devices, coupled with frequent movements of the upper limb in unfavorable positions or maintaining an unsuitable posture for extended periods, can result in musculoskeletal symptoms [28]. Users of smartphones and laptops for internet communication purposes commonly experience discomfort and pain as manifestations of these symptoms [29]. Musculoskeletal pain encompasses acute or chronic discomfort affecting bones, muscles, ligaments, tendons, and even nerves, making it a prevalent medical and socioeconomic challenge globally [30]. This pain is not confined to the neck but extends to various parts of the body, including shoulders, eyes, elbows, arms, wrists, hands, thumbs, and fingers [29]. It is essential to avoid maintaining a single body position for more than an hour, as prolonged repetitive alignment can lead to musculoskeletal pain syndromes. According to Sahrmann’s theories, the sustained adoption of a specific body position or repetitive movements can contribute to the development of musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) [31]. Individuals addicted to the Internet often assume poor physical postures during usage, which can have adverse effects, including musculoskeletal abnormalities [25].

As one of the key and influential groups of society, students are Internet users for various research, educational, and study reasons [9, 32]. As mentioned above, the growing trend of technology as well as the outbreak of COVID-19, the quarantine of cities, the closure of universities, and online education at universities have increased the internet use rate. Therefore, identifying and introducing the risks of excessive Internet use and the consequences of Internet addiction to society can play a significant role in the prevention and reduction of these consequences. This research aimed to investigate the relationship between Internet addiction and MSDs among medical science students.

Methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Semnan University of Medical Science from January to August 2023 in Iran.

Study participants

The research population comprised all Semnan University of Medical Sciences students aged 18 or older with Internet and smart devices (laptops, tablets and smartphone) access. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary, and students were selected through the census method based on specific inclusion and exclusion criteria. Participants were briefed on the study objectives, provided informed consent, and completed questionnaires. Ultimately, 314 students actively took part in the study. Inclusion criteria involved participants being engaged in theoretical, practical, and clinical educational programs within university settings. Exclusion criteria encompassed students with muscle and joint diseases, peripheral and central nerve conditions, a history of trauma, injury, or fractures in the back, neck, and shoulder, as well as those who incompletely filled out the questionnaire.

The sample size was determined by considering the standard deviation of the Internet addiction score (equal to 12.1), from a previous study based on the following formula [33].

Based on the formula, the sample size for this study was determined to be 349 medical science students, considering a significance level of 95%, a power of 80%, and a margin of error of 1.3 considering the possibility of drop out. A total of 35 medical science students were excluded due to a history of trauma and injury.

Data collecting tools

In this study, demographic information forms, the Young Internet Addiction Test (IAT), and the Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire (NMQ) were employed. The demographic information form included items such as age, gender, marital status, field of study, and BMI, which were completed online by the participating students.

The Young Internet Addiction Test (IAT), developed by Kimberly Young, consists of 20 items scored on the Likert scale. The scores are used to categorize individuals into three groups reflecting their internet usage patterns: (i) those with normal internet use (scoring 20–49), (ii) those experiencing problems due to excessive use (scoring 50–79), and (iii) those with sever internet addiction, suggesting a need for treatment (scoring 80–100). The IAT assesses various aspects of Internet addiction and gauges how excessive Internet use may impact different facets of an individual’s life [34]. Yang et al. reported an internal validity value of > 0.92 for the IAT [35]. The questionnaire was translated into Persian by Alavi et al. (2010) in Iran, demonstrating a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.88 for the entire test [36]. In the present study, the reliability of the IAT was found to be satisfactory, as indicated by an internal consistency assessment with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.78.

The Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire (NMQ) stands out as one of the most comprehensive tools for assessing the prevalence of Musculoskeletal Disorders (MSDs). Devised by Kuorinka et al. in 1987, the NMQ consists of three parts. The first section comprises demographic questions, while the second delves into inquiries related to the identification of bodily complications and discomforts. Respondents are prompted to specify which of the nine parts of their bodies (neck, shoulder, elbow, hands, back, thighs, knees, and feet) experienced pain or discomfort in the past year. The third segment of the NMQ encompasses 12 questions related to discomfort in the pertinent organs. This tool is recognized for its validity and reliability in measuring MSDs, boasting an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.07. The questionnaire was translated into Persian in Iran, and its reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha method, confirming a reliability coefficient of 0.91 by Ozgoli et al. [37].

Data were collected using an online survey platform that ensures adherence to all data protection regulations and laws. General reminders were sent weekly to the participants during the data collection period.

Data analysis

The data analysis for this study was conducted using descriptive and inferential statistics through SPSS26 software, with a significance level set at 0.05. The research data were elucidated, categorized, and compared using relative and absolute frequency, mean, and standard deviation. Inferential statistics analysis was done by logistic regression analysis.

Ethical considerations

The current study received approval from the ethics committee of Semnan University of Medical Sciences with the ethics code IR.SEMUMS.REC.1401.224. Prior to participation, the participants were briefed on the study’s objectives and nature, and the confidentiality of their data was assured. Informed consent was obtained from all students who willingly participated in the study.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study aimed to explore the relationship between Internet addiction and Musculoskeletal Disorders (MSDs) in various body parts among medical science students. Overall, 20.1% of participants exhibited moderate Internet addiction, while 1.6% showed severe addiction. These findings are comparable to or slightly exceed values reported in other studies utilizing the same assessment tool [38, 39]. Discrepancies in Internet addiction rates among students across studies may be attributed to cultural diversity in different regions, advancements in science and technology, the escalating demand for technology, the digitalization of societies, and the influence of the COVID-19 pandemic, during which students often spent more time at home, leading to increased Internet usage. Additionally, the rate of Internet use has risen with the implementation of online teaching at universities [40].

The findings of this study indicated that the average score of Internet addiction was higher in boys than in girls, aligning with the results reported by Karimi et al. [1]. The excessive use of the Internet has evolved into an addictive behavior. Based on our results, it becomes imperative to address Internet addiction in students and conduct educational sessions to impart strategies for preventing Internet addiction. The improper use of the Internet can give rise to numerous complications, including the established connection between excessive Internet use and an elevated risk of musculoskeletal symptoms, as supported by previous studies [41, 42]. Individuals with moderate and severe levels of Internet addiction report a higher percentage of Musculoskeletal Disorders (MSD) symptoms compared to others. Determining the prevalence rate and pattern of MSDs and pains serves as the initial step in the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of such disorders [42]. According to our results, the majority of complaints and expressions of musculoskeletal pain were centered around the neck, waist, and upper back area over the past year and week. Based on participants’ self-reports, the most prevalent pain or discomfort occurred in the upper back, shoulders, and wrists/hands in the last 12 months, to the extent that it necessitated rest, reduced work activity, or hindered the performance of activities at work or home. These findings are in line with other similar studies conducted among students and young individuals [42, 43]. Previous studies have delved into the exploration of musculoskeletal pain and abnormalities associated with varying levels of digital device usage for internet connection. Reported findings encompass the repercussions of prolonged adoption of incorrect body positions while using these devices, leading to discomfort and musculoskeletal pains, particularly in the neck, shoulder, head, and wrist areas [29, 41]. Additional evidence suggests that the prolonged use of electronic devices and continuous engagement of muscles, such as holding a computer mouse or a smartphone, serve as risk factors for the development of Musculoskeletal Disorders (MSDs) in the upper limbs [44].

This study identified a direct and significant relationship between an increased Internet addiction score and the presence of pain and discomfort in the neck, upper back, wrists, hips, and thighs. Complaints of Musculoskeletal Disorders (MSDs) in these areas demonstrated a noteworthy increase with the rise in Internet addiction scores among participants. The precise cause of the association between Internet addiction and MSDs remains unclear. As previously mentioned, a plausible explanation suggested by the authors is that users often maintain a fixed position while using the Internet or Internet devices (e.g., smartphones, tablets, or laptops), particularly during activities such as online text messaging or gaming. For instance, prolonged smartphone use involves extended wrists, bent elbows, and a downward-facing head. Such poor posture can exert pressure on tendons, muscles, and discs, eventually leading to pain in the neck, shoulders, elbows, and wrists/hands. Prolonged daily Internet use emerges as a risk factor for back pain and sedentary-related back pain. Long-term immobility and incorrect posture are recognized as significant risk factors contributing to back pain [45].

The impact of the Internet on people’s lives remains a controversial issue [46]. Considering the extensive use of the Internet by students and the current findings highlighting the adverse effects of excessive Internet use on physical well-being, pain, and Musculoskeletal Disorders (MSDs), it becomes imperative to take measures to inform and educate individuals, families, and education/health authorities about the negative consequences associated with the prolonged use of this tool. Given the structural and anatomical differences between boys and girls, similar research examining the gender-specific effects is recommended. Furthermore, conducting a follow-up study with the same participants after graduation is suggested. Such a study could offer conclusive evidence on whether the drawbacks of Internet addiction significantly impact individuals’ performance over time.

Limitation of the study

A limitation of this study is its restriction to students at the university where the research was conducted, rather than encompassing all Iranian students, thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the use of a cross-sectional design makes it challenging to discern causal relationships between variables and understand changes in variable scores over time. Consequently, a longitudinal study is recommended for investigating cause-and-effect relationships in future research. The tools utilized in this study were time-specific, and the subjects under investigation were not subjected to validation, introducing the possibility of errors in the study results. Given that data collection relied on self-reporting tools, there is a potential for response bias, thereby constraining the external validity of the study.

Conclusions

This study reveals the prevalence of Internet addiction among students and establishes a significant link between Internet addiction and Musculoskeletal Disorders (MSDs). As we navigate the early stages of widespread Internet use, addressing associated issues responsibly is crucial. It is imperative to comprehensively assess all facets of Internet usage, considering its positive and negative implications, and leverage research findings for informed policymaking. Recognizing Internet addiction as a potential health issue, particularly affecting the young generation, emphasizes the need for preventive measures, especially among students who shape the future. Further investigations across diverse student populations are warranted to delve deeper into the impact of Internet addiction, and future research could explore the factors connecting Internet addiction and MSDs through prospective cohort or intervention studies.

Clinical implications

These findings have important implications for clinical practice, especially in healthcare settings where students are receiving training and preparing for future careers.
