Short-term temporal variation in the early-life gut microbiota links maternal clinical phenotypes to neonatal jaundice
Authors: Hailing Zhang, Haojun Yao, Ke Zhang, Chunfang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMaternal clinical phenotypes shape the neonatal gut microbiome and influence infant health outcomes, yet the microbial mechanisms linking maternal conditions to neonatal jaundice remain largely unknown. To investigate this, we characterized bacterial community profiling in prenatal maternal feces, meconium, and postnatal Day 3 neonatal feces from a mother–infant cohort, and integrated microbiota–phenotype association analyses and mediation models.ResultsWe found that meconium microbiota showed slightly lower divergence from maternal fecal microbiota than neonatal fecal microbiota did. Canonical correspondence analysis (CCA) showed that maternal HbA1c consistently contributed to early-life gut microbial community structure, explaining 7% of the variation in meconium microbiota and 11% of the variation in neonatal fecal microbiota. Neonatal transcutaneous bilirubin levels on Day 3 were also associated with early-life microbial variation, with a stronger association observed in neonatal fecal microbiota than in meconium. In addition, maternal HbA1c significantly correlated with maternal urinary bacterial counts. The abundance distribution patterns of meconium ASVs related to maternal diabetes status and urinary bacterial detection status were also observed in the corresponding taxa of neonatal gut microbiota and were associated with subsequent neonatal jaundice. Notably, specific differentially abundant ASVs affiliated with Bifidobacterium, Clostridium_T, and Rothia mediated the interconnected rather than independent effects of maternal HbA1c and urinary bacterial counts on neonatal jaundice.ConclusionsThe results suggest that meconium microbiota features associated with maternal diabetes-related phenotypes influence early neonatal gut microbial community structure and may contribute to neonatal jaundice. This study highlights the potential role of early-life gut microbiota shifts in mediating the effects of maternal physiological variation on neonatal health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05383-z.

Background

Neonatal jaundice is one of the most common conditions in early life and can lead to neonatal morbidity when severe or prolonged [1–4]. As bilirubin metabolism plays a central role in the pathogenesis of neonatal jaundice, identifying factors that influence this process may provide insights into the regulatory mechanisms underlying neonatal jaundice. Maternal clinical and metabolic characteristics during pregnancy have been shown to influence neonatal health outcomes [5–7]. Previous studies have shown that mothers with poor glycemic control or hypertension often exhibit evident metabolic disturbances, and their offspring have an increased risk of neonatal hyperbilirubinemia [8–11]. In addition, higher maternal mean corpuscular volume has been associated with an elevated risk of pathological jaundice in the offspring [12]. However, the underlying mechanisms linking maternal clinical phenotypes to neonatal jaundice have not been fully elucidated.

In early life, the gut microbiota plays an important role in metabolic regulation and host development [13, 14]. Current evidence indicates that maternal conditions, including inflammatory bowel disease, gestational diabetes mellitus, and obesity, are associated with varying degrees of gut microbiota dysbiosis in offspring [15–17]. In addition, the neonatal gut microbiota has been increasingly linked to neonatal jaundice [14, 18–20]. Certain gut microbiota can contribute to bilirubin reduction through microbial bilirubin reductase activities, thereby promoting intestinal elimination of bilirubin [21]. Conversely, microbiota-associated β-glucuronidase activity can deconjugate conjugated bilirubin and enhance enterohepatic circulation, thereby increasing bilirubin reabsorption and exacerbating neonatal jaundice [22, 23]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the neonatal gut microbiota may serve as a putative mechanistic link between maternal clinical phenotypes and neonatal jaundice. Nevertheless, the mediating role of the gut microbiota in this association remains largely unexplored, particularly in the early neonatal period.

Early life is a critical period characterized by rapid and dynamic establishment of the neonatal gut microbiota [24, 25]. Meconium collected on the day of birth may provide insight into the earliest detectable microbial signals in the neonatal gut environment and may reflect early microbial exposures associated with maternal physiological and metabolic conditions [26–29]. However, as meconium is a low-biomass sample and the existence of prenatal microbial colonization remains debated [30–32]. In contrast, neonatal fecal microbiota is further shaped by postnatal exposures such as feeding practices and clinical care, reflecting ongoing microbial maturation and community remodeling during early postnatal period [29, 33]. Analyses based on a single time point may overlook the dynamic nature of the neonatal gut microbiota. This underscores the need to investigate whether and to what extent maternal clinical phenotypes shape the temporal dynamics of meconium and neonatal gut microbiota, and whether these microbial dynamics contribute to neonatal jaundice.

In this study, we used microbiome profiling to investigate the potential mediating role of early-life gut microbiota in linking maternal clinical phenotypes to neonatal jaundice. We addressed this objective through three sequential analyses: (1) comparing the overall community composition of the maternal fecal microbiota, meconium microbiota, and postnatal Day 3 neonatal fecal microbiota; (2) identifying key clinical phenotypes associated with the variations in the meconium and the neonatal fecal microbiota; and (3) characterizing differentially abundant taxa that may contribute to the maternal clinical phenotypes-meconium microbiota-neonatal fecal microbiota-neonatal jaundice axis.

Methods

Ethics approval and collection and definition of clinical data

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (Approval No. KY2024126) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating pregnant women. For newborn participants, informed consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians prior to inclusion in the study. Clinical data were retrieved from the hospital electronic medical record system and summarized in a Supplementary Table (Clinical data.xlsx). Prenatal clinical information, including maternal HbA1c levels, was collected concurrently with fecal sampling, while postpartum clinical data were obtained within 24 h after delivery. Diabetes status was determined from hospital medical records. Gestational Diabetes Mellitus was diagnosed according to International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups criteria using a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test at 24–28 weeks of gestation, defined as any of the following: fasting plasma glucose ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, 1-hour post-load glucose ≥ 10.0 mmol/L, or 2-hour post-load glucose ≥ 8.5 mmol/L. Pre-existing Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus was defined as a documented pre-pregnancy diagnosis or by fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or HbA1c ≥ 6.5% prior to pregnancy. Gestational and Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus were combined into a single diabetes group for analysis.

Maternal urinary bacterial counts were measured as cells/µL. In correlation and mediation analyses, urinary bacterial counts were treated as a continuous variable. For subgroup analyses, if maternal urinary bacteria were detected, samples were classified as present; otherwise, they were classified as absent. For each neonate, transcutaneous bilirubin levels on postnatal Day 2 and Day 3 were measured at the forehead, inner canthus, and anterior chest of each neonate using a transcutaneous jaundice meter (Nanjing Nanke Huagong Technology Development Co., Ltd.), and the mean value was used for subsequent analysis. Neonates were classified into higher-risk and lower-risk groups using a postnatal Day 3 transcutaneous bilirubin cutoff of 9.8 mg/dL, corresponding to the lowest value observed among neonates who subsequently required phototherapy or bilirubin-lowering treatment. All clinical data and samples were anonymized prior to analysis.

Sample collection, DNA extraction and sequencing

A total of 20 pregnant women with singleton pregnancies at 36–41 weeks gestation and their corresponding neonates were enrolled in this study. Participants were recruited using a consecutive convenience sampling strategy. Participants with conditions that could potentially affect maternal or neonatal microbial composition were excluded, including maternal–fetal blood group incompatibility, neonatal hepatobiliary diseases, neonatal asphyxia, and severe neonatal infections. All eligible pregnant women presenting during the study period were consecutively invited to participate until the target sample size was reached. Fecal samples were collected at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University between February 20 and May 31, 2024.

Maternal fecal samples (Maternal; n = 20) were collected during the late third trimester, primarily within 3 days before delivery. Meconium samples (Meconium) were collected from all 20 newborns within the first day after delivery. In addition, third-day neonatal fecal samples (Neonatal) were collected from 13 of the 20 enrolled newborns using simple random sampling. Maternal fecal samples were self-collected immediately after spontaneous defecation using sterile, DNA-free fecal collection tubes with integrated sampling scoops (Labshark, Hunan BKMAN Holding Co., Ltd., China). Aliquots were preferentially obtained from the inner portion of the stool to minimize environmental contamination. Newborn diapers were monitored regularly by trained nursing staff and caregivers, and Meconium samples were collected immediately after spontaneous defecation by trained medical staff using the same sterile collection tubes while wearing sterile gloves and masks. At least 10 g of feces were collected per sample. Fecal samples were initially frozen at -20 °C within one hour after collection and transferred to liquid nitrogen within 12 h after collection for long-term storage until DNA extraction. Samples were thawed only once on ice immediately prior to DNA extraction.

After thawing, each fecal sample was thoroughly homogenized by vortex mixing to ensure uniformity. Approximately 0.2 g of the homogenized stool was then collected as a representative subsample for DNA extraction using the OMEGA Soil DNA Kit (M5635-02; Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration and quality were evaluated using a NanoDrop NC2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively. Extracted DNA was amplified targeting the 16 S rRNA gene V3–V4 region using primers 338 F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’) with sample-specific barcodes. PCR reactions were prepared following standard protocols, and thermal cycling conditions were 98 °C for 5 min, followed by 25 cycles of 98 °C for 30 s, 53 ℃ for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were purified using VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and quantified with the Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Purified amplicons were pooled in equimolar amounts and subjected to paired-end 2 × 250 bp sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq platform (Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China).

Data analysis

Raw sequence data were processed using QIIME2 (version 2022.11) following the official tutorials [34]. Briefly, sequences were demultiplexed using the demux plugin and primers were trimmed with the cutadapt plugin using default parameters [35]. Quality filtering, denoising, read merging, and chimera removal were performed with the DADA2 plugin using default parameters [36]. The resulting unique sequences were defined as amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Singleton and low-abundance ASVs (< 0.001% of the total sequencing reads across all samples) were removed prior to downstream analyses. Raw sequencing reads and final ASV counts for each sample are provided in a Supplementary Table (Sample sequencing statistics.csv). ASVs were aligned with MAFFT [37], and a phylogenetic tree was constructed using FastTree2 [38]. Taxonomic assignment was performed using the classify-sklearn naïve Bayes classifier in the QIIME2 feature-classifier plugin against the SILVA database (release 138) with a confidence threshold of 0.7 [39].

To assess whether sequencing depth was sufficient to capture microbial diversity within each sample, rarefaction curves were generated in QIIME2 (Fig. S1). Sequencing reads were randomly subsampled at 10 rarefaction depths, and alpha diversity indices were calculated at each depth. To minimize bias introduced by unequal sequencing depth across different fecal samples, the ASV table was rarefied using QIIME2 to a uniform depth of 33,236 reads per sample, corresponding to approximately 95% of the minimum sequencing depth across all samples after quality control. The rarefied ASV table was used for alpha and beta diversity analyses. The non-rarefied ASV table was used for canonical correspondence analysis (CCA). Differentially abundant taxa were identified using the linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) based on both rarefied and non-rarefied datasets, while MaAsLin2 was performed using the non-rarefied ASV table.

Taxonomic composition was summarized at phylum and genus levels, and displayed using bar plots (Fig. S2). ASV-level alpha diversity indices, including Observed species, Pielou’s evenness, Shannon, and Good’s coverage, were calculated using the vegan package and visualized with the ggplot2 package in R (v4.3.3). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Differences in alpha diversity indices among groups were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test, followed by Dunn’s post hoc multiple comparison test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction using the FSA package in R (https://fishr-core-team.github.io/FSA/). To further account for potential within-family correlation, additional analyses were performed using linear mixed-effects models implemented in the lme4 package in R [40], with fecal sample type included as a fixed effect and family ID included as a random intercept. Overall group differences were evaluated using analysis of variance for each fitted model. Estimated marginal means and post hoc pairwise comparisons were obtained using the emmeans package in R (https://rvlenth.github.io/emmeans/). The shared and unique bacterial ASVs among Maternal, Meconium, and Neonatal fecal samples were depicted with the Venn package.

Beta diversity analyses were performed using Bray–Curtis, Jaccard, weighted UniFrac, and unweighted UniFrac distance metrics. Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) was conducted for each distance matrix using the vegan package in R to visualize microbial community variation (https://vegandevs.github.io/vegan/). To evaluate potential within-family or within-individual clustering effects, group differences were assessed using both standard and grouped permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA), with family ID included as strata to account for the paired design.

Mantel tests were performed to assess correlations between bacterial community structures across different sample types. When sample sizes were unequal, only matched samples were included in the analysis. Bray–Curtis distance matrices were calculated using the vegdist function in the vegan package, and Mantel correlations were computed using Spearman’s method with 999 permutations. In addition, pairwise Bray–Curtis distances between maternal and meconium fecal samples and between maternal and neonatal samples were separately calculated and compared using permutation tests with 999 randomizations.

Additional prevalence filtering (10%) was performed within each subgroup dataset (Maternal, Meconium, and Neonatal datasets) before CCA to reduce sparsity and minimize the influence of rare taxa. Hellinger-transformed microbial data were used in CCA to evaluate the relationships between microbial community structure and key clinical factors. The significance of CCA models was assessed using permutation tests with 999 permutations in the vegan package, including global tests to evaluate the overall model significance and marginal tests to assess the contribution of individual explanatory variables. Variance inflation factors (VIF) were assessed for variables included in the final CCA models. To validate the robustness of the CCA results, we conducted PERMANOVA and tests for homogeneity of multivariate dispersions (PERMDISP) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrices. Variable selection was conducted prior to CCA to minimize model overfitting. Pairwise associations among categorical variables were assessed using Cramér’s V. Pairwise correlations among continuous variables were analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation, with each mother–infant family contributing a single independent observation (n = 20). The p values from both analyses were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction.

To evaluate the influence of key clinical variables on neonatal jaundice, transcutaneous bilirubin levels across different clinical groups were visualized using boxplots generated with the ggplot2 package. For comparisons of transcutaneous bilirubin levels between two groups, homogeneity of variances was evaluated using an F-test. When the assumptions of normality and equal variances were satisfied, Student’s t-test was used; otherwise, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied.

Differentially abundant ASVs associated with each clinical factor were identified using LEfSe method implemented in the microeco package [41], applying significance thresholds of α = 0.05 and |LDA| > 3. ASV-level differential abundance analysis was also performed using both unadjusted MaAsLin2 models and MaAsLin2 models adjusted for clinically relevant covariates [42]. ASVs with |coefficient| > 1 were retained and ranked by ascending p value. The taxonomic information, read counts, and ASV counts for these differentially abundant ASVs within each clinical group were further visualized using the metacoder package. To compare the abundances of differentially abundant ASVs across neonatal jaundice groups, mean read counts of each ASV were depicted using heatmaps generated with the pheatmap package. Statistical comparisons of read counts among differentially abundant ASVs associated with different clinical groups at each jaundice level were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as the data were not normally distributed according to the Shapiro–Wilk test. These analyses were performed separately for bacterial samples collected from Meconium and Neonatal groups. The proportions of samples with urinary bacteria present or absent within each maternal diabetes group were visualized using stacked bar plots generated with the ggplot2 package.

To investigate whether differentially abundant ASVs mediated the influence of key clinical variables on neonatal jaundice, mediation analysis was conducted using the mediation package [43]. To address potential confounding among the exposure, mediator, and outcome variables, mutual adjustment analyses were performed within the mediation framework by including a covariate in both the mediator and outcome models. For each variable in the mediation models, a one-dimensional ecological feature was derived using a distance-based ordination approach. Briefly, the ASV abundances and maternal urinary bacteria counts across neonatal samples were first adjusted by adding a small pseudocount (0.001) to avoid zero-only profiles. Bray-Curtis dissimilarities among neonatal samples were then calculated for each variable, followed by PCoA. The first three PCoA axes were weighted by their corresponding eigenvalues and summed to represent each variable. The distribution patterns of the derived ecological features and the observed values of the corresponding variables were visualized using heatmaps implemented in the pheatmap package.

Results

Bacterial community diversity and similarity across different fecal samples

Pairwise microbial community distance analyses did not reveal significantly smaller within-family or within-individual distances compared with within-group distances, indicating limited clustering effects in this cohort (Fig. S3). PCoA based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity revealed clear separation of Maternal, Meconium, and Neonatal fecal samples (PERMANOVA R² = 0.12, p < 0.001). Similar patterns were observed using Jaccard, weighted, and unweighted UniFrac distances, confirming the robustness of beta diversity patterns across different distance metrics (Fig. S4 and Table S1). Alpha diversity analysis of the fecal bacterial communities showed that Maternal samples exhibited significantly higher Observed species, Pielou’s evenness, and Shannon index compared with both Meconium and Neonatal samples, whereas Good’s coverage was significantly lower in Maternal than in Meconium and Neonatal samples (Fig. 1A). Although no significant differences in bacterial alpha diversity indices were observed between Meconium and Neonatal samples, Meconium bacterial communities showed higher mean values of Observed species, Pielou’s evenness, and Shannon index, along with a lower mean Good’s coverage, compared with Neonatal bacterial communities. Further linear mixed-effects model analyses for alpha diversity indices were consistent with the above results (Tables S2 and S3). Analysis of shared and unique ASVs among the three groups revealed that Maternal samples harbored the largest numbers of both total and unique ASVs, whereas Neonatal samples exhibited the fewest total and unique ASVs (Fig. 1B). Overall, Maternal fecal microbiota displayed significantly higher alpha diversity than Meconium and Neonatal microbiota.

Mantel tests indicated a moderate correlation for both the overall (Mantel r = 0.349, p = 0.008) and shared bacterial communities (Mantel r = 0.348, p = 0.013) between Meconium and Neonatal samples (Table S4). Weak correlations were observed for both overall (Mantel r = 0.097, p = 0.165) and shared communities (Mantel r = 0.102, p = 0.137) between Maternal and Meconium samples; however, these associations were not statistically significant. Moreover, the mean Bray-Curtis distance between Maternal and Meconium samples was significantly lower than that between Maternal and Neonatal samples (0.969 vs. 0.990; permutation test: p < 0.001; Fig. S5), although both comparisons showed high compositional dissimilarity. Collectively, Meconium and Neonatal microbiota were more correlated with each other than with Maternal microbiota, and Maternal microbiota showed greater divergence from Neonatal microbiota than from Meconium microbiota.

Associations between clinical phenotypes and fecal microbiota

We performed CCA analysis to identify potential clinical factors associated with fecal bacterial community variation. Given the relatively small sample size, only a limited number of biologically relevant and non-collinear variables were retained in the final CCA model, including delivery mode, maternal HbA1c, and neonatal transcutaneous bilirubin on Day 3 (Figs. S6 and S7; Table S5). CCA identified maternal HbA1c and neonatal transcutaneous bilirubin on postnatal Day 3 as key clinical factors associated with meconium microbial community structure, explaining 7% and 8% of the community variation, respectively (Table 1). In Neonatal fecal microbiota, both variables were also significantly associated with community structure, each explaining 11% of the total variation. PERMANOVA analysis further showed differences in neonatal fecal microbial community composition across groups stratified by maternal HbA1c and neonatal transcutaneous bilirubin on Day 3, although neither association was statistically significant (HbA1c: R2 = 0.12, p = 0.055; bilirubin: R2 = 0.10, p = 0.077; Tables S6 and S7). In Meconium samples, significant differences in microbial community composition were observed only for maternal HbA1c (R2 = 0.08, p = 0.039), but not for neonatal transcutaneous bilirubin on Day 3. Together, these findings suggest that maternal HbA1c was consistently associated with early-life gut microbial community structure, whereas associations with neonatal bilirubin levels were more pronounced in Neonatal fecal microbiota than in Meconium microbiota.

Differentially abundant ASVs related to maternal clinical phenotypes and neonatal jaundice

To investigate whether maternal clinical parameters influencing Meconium microbiota have subsequent effects on neonatal jaundice, we analyzed the relationships among maternal clinical factors, Meconium microbiota, and neonatal jaundice. In the FDR-corrected correlation analysis, maternal HbA1c was not significantly associated with any continuous variables (Fig. S7A). However, an exploratory unadjusted analysis showed a positive correlation between maternal HbA1c and maternal urinary bacterial counts (r = 0.46, nominal p = 0.043; Fig. S7B). Therefore, maternal diabetes status and urinary bacterial detection status were retained for subsequent analyses. LEfSe analysis identified eight differentially abundant Meconium ASVs associated with maternal diabetes status (Figs. 2A-C). Five ASVs were enriched in the diabetes group, including ASV_9753 (genus Bifidobacterium), ASV_35833, and ASV_3106 (genus Clostridium_T), whereas three were enriched in the control group. After adjustment for maternal urinary bacterial detection status, these differentially abundant ASVs remained consistently associated with maternal diabetes status (Tables S8 and S9). The relative abundances of these eight differentially abundant ASVs in Meconium and Neonatal samples exhibited similar associations with neonatal jaundice (Fig. 2D). Specifically, in the lower-risk jaundice group, the ASVs enriched in the maternal control group tended to show higher relative abundances than those enriched in the maternal diabetes group. Consistently, neonates born to mothers with diabetes exhibited significantly higher transcutaneous bilirubin levels on both postnatal Day 2 and Day 3 compared with neonates born to mothers without diabetes (Fig. 2E). These results indicate that Meconium differentially abundant ASVs associated with maternal diabetes status may influence subsequent neonatal jaundice.

Neonates born to mothers with detectable urinary bacteria showed no significant differences in transcutaneous bilirubin levels on postnatal Day 2 or Day 3 compared with neonates born to mothers without detectable urinary bacteria (Fig. S8A). However, neonatal samples from mothers without diabetes showed a lower occurrence of maternal urinary bacteria than those from mothers with diabetes (Fig. S8B). LEfSe analysis further identified differentially abundant Meconium ASVs associated with maternal urinary bacterial detection status, revealing two ASVs, including ASV_31447 (genus Rothia), enriched in the urinary bacteria-present group and one ASV enriched in the urinary bacteria-absent group (Figs. 3A and S9). After adjustment for maternal diabetes status, these urinary bacterial detection status-associated ASVs were consistently identified (Tables S8 and S9). For Meconium samples, the ASVs specific to the maternal urinary bacteria-absent group have significantly higher relative abundance than those specific to the bacteria-present group in the lower-risk jaundice group (Fig. 3B). These findings suggest that differentially abundant Meconium ASVs associated with maternal urinary bacterial detection status may be related to subsequent patterns in neonatal jaundice.

Differentially abundant ASVs-mediated maternal phenotypes–neonatal jaundice associations

To examine which Meconium differentially abundant ASVs contribute to the influences of maternal diabetes and urinary bacteria on neonatal jaundice, we performed mediation analyses using the one-dimensional ecological features of variables. Consistent distribution patterns were observed between the original variable values and their corresponding ecological features, except for neonatal transcutaneous bilirubin levels on postnatal Day 2, which exhibited an inverse pattern (Fig. S10). ASV_31447 showed the most robust mediation effect in the association between HbA1c and jaundice on Day 2 (Fig. 3C; Tables S10 and S11). The average causal mediation effect (ACME) remained significant after adjustment for urinary bacterial counts (ACME p = 0.032), whereas the average direct effect (ADE) remained non-significant, suggesting that ASV_31447 predominantly mediated the association independently of urinary bacterial counts. Although ASV_31447 was enriched in subjects with urinary bacterial presence, its mediation effect could not be fully explained by urinary bacterial counts alone.

ASV_9753, a diabetes-associated differentially abundant ASV, also showed a significant mediation effect between HbA1c and neonatal jaundice in the unadjusted model (ACME p = 0.034; Tables S10 and S11). However, after adjustment for urinary bacterial counts, the mediation effect was attenuated and no longer significant (ACME p = 0.238), while the direct effect remained significant. These findings suggest that the observed mediation effect of ASV_9753 was at least partially influenced by confounding from urinary bacterial counts. For the diabetes-associated differentially abundant ASV_3106, a significant mediation effect was observed between urinary bacterial counts and neonatal jaundice in the unadjusted analysis (ACME p = 0.033; Tables S10 and S11). However, this association became non-significant after adjustment for HbA1c (ACME p = 0.090), indicating limited robustness of the mediation effect and possible confounding by HbA1c. Collectively, these findings suggest that specific microbial taxa may participate in the link between maternal metabolic dysregulation and neonatal jaundice, while the attenuation of mediation effects after covariate adjustment indicates potential confounding relationship between maternal HbA1c and urinary bacterial counts.

Discussion

We observed significantly higher microbial diversity in Maternal fecal samples than in Meconium and Neonatal fecal samples, with a non-significant trend toward lower diversity from Meconium to Neonatal feces. Previous studies have shown that gut microbial diversity generally increases and community complexity expands with age as environmental exposures accumulate [44–46]. These findings suggest that the long-term trajectory does not preclude a short-term decline in microbial diversity during the earliest postnatal window before subsequent expansion and maturation. Such a pattern may be consistent with ecological filtering as a potential explanatory hypothesis during early-life microbial development. Low-diversity microbial communities provide limited functional redundancy and are therefore less resilient to environmental perturbations, suggesting that the meconium microbial community is comparatively fragile and can be rapidly reshaped during the early postnatal period by short-term environmental exposures [47–51]. The slightly lower compositional divergence between Maternal fecal microbiota and Meconium microbiota than that between Maternal and Neonatal fecal microbiota was observed, although both distances indicated high overall dissimilarity. Collectively, these findings suggest that the meconium microbiota exhibits an intermediate microbial pattern between maternal and neonatal microbial states and may undergo rapid changes during the early postnatal period.

Our results indicated that maternal HbA1c explained a modest proportion of the variations in the Meconium microbiota. Maternal hyperglycemia and diabetes-associated metabolic dysregulation can reshape maternal microbial communities across multiple body sites, including the urinary tract, vagina, perineum, and gut [52–59]. Mechanisms include altered nutrient availability and pH homeostasis, impaired immune defenses, and metabolic perturbations, may collectively influence the pool of microbes transmitted to neonates during delivery [52–60]. In addition to modifying microbial sources, maternal dysglycemia can alter the nutritional landscape of the meconium. Emerging meconium metabolomics studies suggest that maternal glycemic dysregulation is associated with broad shifts in meconium metabolites [16, 61], which may reshape nutrient availability and physicochemical selective pressures encountered by early colonizers. Collectively, maternal hyperglycemia may be associated with variation in the meconium microbiota, potentially reflecting combined influences of maternal microbial and metabolic environments.

Neonatal jaundice levels measured on postnatal Day 3 showed a stronger association with variation in neonatal fecal microbiota collected at the same time point than with meconium microbiota collected shortly after birth. This difference may be attributed to the shift in bilirubin homeostasis before and after birth. In utero, fetal unconjugated bilirubin is primarily transported across the placenta into the maternal circulation, where it is metabolized and cleared by the maternal liver and biliary excretion [62]. After birth, the maintenance of bilirubin homeostasis transits to neonate itself, at which stage the gut microbiota increasingly participate in bilirubin metabolism [63, 64]. Beneficial taxa with high bilirubin reductase activity can promote bilirubin reduction and clearance [21] Certain opportunistic gut taxa such as some Clostridium-related groups exhibited strong β-glucuronidase activity, which can promote bilirubin deconjugation and the enterohepatic circulation, thereby exacerbate neonatal jaundice [22, 23, 65–67]. In contrast, the potential contribution of Rothia to neonatal jaundice remains largely unclear. Previous studies have suggested that Rothia may be associated with diabetes-related alterations in maternal microbial communities and early microbial transmission [68]. Therefore, the detection of Rothia in meconium may reflect microbial shifts associated with maternal metabolic conditions and be involved in the association between maternal hyperglycemia and neonatal jaundice. Our findings underscore early-life gut microbial dynamics as a clinically relevant dimension of neonatal jaundice metabolism.

Higher maternal HbA1c during pregnancy is generally associated with elevated neonatal bilirubin levels, consistent with the significant direct effect observed in our partial mediation model [10, 69, 70]. However, the indirect effect indicated that Bifidobacterium-affiliated taxa mediated a pathway that partially offset the detrimental direct association between maternal HbA1c and neonatal jaundice, suggesting a potential protective role of specific early-life microbes. Bifidobacterium-affiliated taxa can acidify the gut environment and competitively suppress the bacteria with strong β-glucuronidase activity, thereby reducing overall bilirubin deconjugation and facilitating bilirubin clearance [71–73]. On this basis, we speculate that although maternal diabetes predisposes neonates to higher jaundice burden, concomitant shifts in specific gut microbial taxa could exert a protective indirect effect that partially mitigates the adverse influence of maternal hyperglycemia on neonatal jaundice.

In the mediation models, inconsistency between the differentially abundant ASV grouping criterion and the phenotype driving the mediated effects, along with attenuation after covariate adjustment, suggests a reciprocal or potentially confounded mediation structure involving maternal HbA1c and urinary bacterial counts. Prior evidence indicates that poor glycemic control increases urinary glucose availability, and diabetes-related alterations in renal tubular reabsorption may also shift the urinary amino acid profile [74–76]. Such diabetes-induced changes in the urinary nutritional milieu could plausibly reshape the microbial community around the urethral [77, 78]. Therefore, maternal HbA1c and urinary bacterial counts may reflect a diabetes-associated maternal milieu that is linked to variation in meconium microbiota composition and neonatal bilirubin metabolism.

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. The relatively modest cohort size may limit statistical power and increase the risk of type I error. Low-biomass nature of neonatal samples also increase susceptibility to technical variation and background contamination. Moreover, study based on 16 S rRNA gene sequencing provides limited taxonomic and functional confirmation. Although maternal HbA1c and urinary bacterial counts were prioritized based on clinical relevance and additional analyses were performed to partially account for confounding effects, residual confounding from unmeasured clinical variables cannot be excluded. Future studies with larger independent cohorts and higher-resolution multi-omics approaches could be considered to validate and extend these exploratory findings.

Conclusions

In this mother-infant cohort study, we identify the meconium microbiota as a transitional community between maternal and neonatal microbial states. Importantly, our findings support the notion that early-life gut microbial colonization can serve as a biologically plausible link between maternal clinical phenotypes and neonatal jaundice. Maternal HbA1c and urinary bacterial counts were associated with specific microbial abundance features in meconium that were further linked to subsequent neonatal gut microbiota development and potentially contributed to neonatal jaundice heterogeneity. Collectively, this work provides a new perspective on how maternal clinical phenotypes shape early-life microbial colonization and influence neonatal jaundice, offering a rationale for early neonatal jaundice risk stratification.
