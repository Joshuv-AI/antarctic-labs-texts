Knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) of self-medication and healthcare-seeking behavior among residents in the vicinity of a tertiary healthcare center in Madhesh Province, Nepal
Authors: Lokeshwar Chaurasia, Hem Shankar Yadav, Kshitiz Shreshta, Suruchi Poudel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectives:Self-medication (SM) is the consumption of medicine by oneself for health-related issues without any prescription, while healthcare-seeking behavior refers to any action taken by individuals when they experience a health-related issue, with the goal of obtaining an appropriate solution. This study aimed to assess the knowledge, attitude, and practice of SM and healthcare-seeking behavior among residents in the vicinity of a tertiary healthcare center in Madhesh Province, Nepal.Materials and methods:A quantitative cross-sectional study was conducted among residents in the vicinity of a Medical College in Madhesh Province, Nepal, during the period of December 2024 to May 2025. A convenient sampling technique was used for the selection of a total of 453 respondents who were willing to participate. A pretested structured questionnaire, tailored to the local context, was used to collect data from the respondents. Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 24.0. The Chi-square test was performed to compare different groups. Binary logistic regression was conducted to observe the associated factors. The level of significance was set at a 95% confidence interval (CI), and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistcally significant.Results:Out of 453 participants included in the study, with a mean age of 36.06 ± 11.99 years, the majority of respondents (n = 389) reported SM. Twenty-seven percent of them had good knowledge, while 73% demonstrated poor knowledge, with a significant association between occupation and knowledge (P = 0.034). A positive attitude was exhibited by 82.3% of the respondents, and a significant association was identified between knowledge and attitude (P = 0.001). Headache was the most common ailment (84.5%), and allopathic medicines were preferred (96.6%), while pharmacy shops were the predominant source of medicine (95.4%). The main reasons for SM were a desire for quick recovery (68.6%), though 81 participants (20.8%) reported side effects. Sixty-seven point seven percent of respondents preferred nearby hospitals; however, 21.1% consulted traditional healers for health-related issues, and family and friends (61.8%) were the primary sources of health-related information.Conclusions:This study showed that residents near a tertiary health center in Madhesh Province commonly used both tertiary healthcare services and traditional healers, with limited awareness of services contributing to delays in seeking formal treatment. SM was highly prevalent, driven by poor knowledge, especially among agricultural workers, and easy access to medicines through pharmacies. Sociodemographic factors further shaped healthcare choices. Targeted health education, improved service awareness, and stronger pharmacy regulation are essential to promote safer and more timely healthcare utilization.

Introduction

Self-medication (SM) is a prevalent global phenomenon, with significant variations across regions and populations. According to various studies on SM, the prevalence is approximately 67% worldwide[1]. The prevalence of SM in Asia was 53%, with regional variations[2]; in Europe, it was 40.8%, and it was highest (74%) in Eastern Europe[1]. Madhesh Province covers an area of 9661 km2, accounting for about 6.5% of Nepal’s total land area, and has a population of over 6.1 million, according to the 2021 census[3]. Despite being the smallest province by area, it is the most densely populated. Geographically, it borders India to the south and west, Koshi Province to the east, and Bagmati Province to the north. The province consists of eight districts, from Parsa to Saptari, and plays an important role in Nepal’s economy due to its fertile plains, agricultural productivity, small industries, and cross-border trade. The health system in Madhesh Province includes government hospitals, primary healthcare centers, and private clinics, though health resources are unevenly distributed. Urban areas generally have better facilities and specialist services, while rural regions depend on basic health posts with limited staff and equipment. Access to healthcare is further affected by transportation difficulties, financial limitations, and cross-border health-seeking practices with nearby Indian towns[4]. Dunn’s classification scheme described three types of healthcare systems. He made a distinction between the regional system, which consists of codified traditional healthcare services like Ayurvedic, Unani, and Chinese medicines administered by qualified local professionals, and the local system, which consists of primitive, folk practitioners and healthcare services[5]. The third type of healthcare system clearly referred to the professional, bureaucratic, modern, or worldwide system of healthcare services. The healthcare system in Nepal is pluralistic (i.e., there are a number of competing systems related to healthcare, each of which is distinctive by its set of ideas, practices, methods, and treatment materials)[6]. In Nepal, particularly in the Madhesh region of the southern Terai, traditional medicine plays an important role in community health practices. People commonly use herbal remedies prepared from locally available plants to treat conditions such as fever, digestive problems, skin diseases, and respiratory infections[7].

Faith-based healing practices are also prevalent. Traditional healers, such as Dhami, Jhankri, or Ojha, perform rituals, chanting, and spiritual healing to treat illnesses believed to be caused by supernatural forces, spirit possession, or the evil eye. Religious practices, including temple visits, prayer, fasting, and offerings, are also used as healing methods. These approaches remain popular because they are culturally familiar, easily accessible, and relatively inexpensive.

Traditional medicine interacts with the modern healthcare system in both complementary and competitive ways. Many individuals initially seek help from traditional healers and later visit health facilities if symptoms persist. However, strong reliance on traditional practices can sometimes delay the timely use of modern medical services, creating a pluralistic healthcare system where multiple models of care coexist[8].

In less developed countries, where both private and public healthcare systems are basic, SM is often the single most important form of healthcare[9]. SM is often driven by financial constraints and a lack of time to visit a medical practitioner[10]. Factors such as sociodemographic characteristics enabling health needs have been found to be associated with health-seeking behaviors, including SM[11]. Moreover, the prevalence of SM has been reported to interfere with prompt healthcare seeking from hospitals[12]. In addition, health literacy has been identified as a determining factor in safe SM behaviors[13]. SM practices in Nepal were reported to be high, ranging from 69.6% to 78%, with common ailments treated being headaches and colds[9–11,14]. SM and healthcare-seeking behavior in community members in the vicinity of a medical college are influenced by various factors, such as the availability of health facilities, motivation, and the ability of individuals to seek medical care[15]. The decision to seek healthcare is influenced by various barriers, including a preference for informal consultations, concerns about confidentiality, and a preference for self-diagnosis[16]. Additionally, the quality of patient-doctor relationships and public education has been highlighted as important factors that may foster healthcare-seeking behavior[17]. It is important to note that healthcare-seeking and avoidance behaviors, when applied maladaptively, may negatively impact an individual’s functioning and the public healthcare system[18]. It is quite concerning that, with previous health issue experience, the same medicine was used again the next time without proper diagnosis[19]. Besides the positive aspects, there is a potential risk of multiple drug interactions and the development of antibiotic resistance with the haphazard use of medicines[20,21].

While the majority of existing literature on SM and healthcare-seeking behavior has focused predominantly on rural or remote populations, where physical distance from healthcare facilities is considered the primary barrier, comparatively little research has examined communities residing in close proximity to tertiary care centers. This represents a critical gap in the literature, as geographic proximity to a healthcare facility does not necessarily confer adequate health literacy, awareness of available services, or timely care-seeking behavior. Studies conducted within tertiary care settings in Nepal have demonstrated that even populations with close access to medical institutions exhibit significant knowledge gaps and engage in unsafe SM practices, suggesting that accessibility alone is insufficient to promote rational health behavior[22,23]. Furthermore, growing evidence from low- and middle-income country contexts indicates that non-geographic barriers, including low health literacy, socioeconomic constraints, reliance on informal information networks, and poor awareness of available services, represent independent and persistent determinants of delayed care-seeking and inappropriate SM, irrespective of physical proximity to healthcare facilities[24,25]. These findings collectively underscore the need for studies specifically targeting peri-urban and semi-urban populations near tertiary healthcare centers, where the assumption of better health knowledge and access remains largely unexamined and, as this study demonstrates, empirically unsubstantiated.

To the best of our knowledge, the dynamics of SM and healthcare-seeking behaviors in the residents of the vicinity of Janaki Medical College, Dhanusha, Madhesh Province, Nepal, are still unknown, despite comparatively better access to healthcare services. Thus, the objective of this study was to assess the knowledge, attitude, SM practices, and health-seeking behaviors of residents in the vicinity of our Medical College, Dhanusha, and also to investigate the association between demographic characteristics, knowledge, and attitudes. This cross-sectional study has been reported in line with the STROCSS 2025 guidelines[26].

Materials and methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study design was employed. The study was conducted from December 2024 to May 2025 in the vicinity of Janaki Medical College and Teaching Hospital, located in Kshireshwornath Municipality.

Setting and timeframe

The study was carried out in an urban–semiurban setting within Wards 1–4 of Kshireshwornath Municipality. According to the National Population and Housing Census 2021, the municipality has a total population of 51 075, with ward populations of 3784, 3254, 3936, and 3211, respectively[3]. Data collection was done through household visits during the specified 6-month period.

Study population and eligibility criteria

The study population included adults aged 18–60 years residing in the selected wards. Individuals with severe medical illnesses, known mental disorders, or those affiliated with healthcare professions were excluded. Only participants who consented and completed the questionnaire were included in the final analysis.

Participant recruitment

Recruitment process and participant flow

A total of 600 individuals were approached. Of these, 453 participants met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis, yielding a response rate of 75.5%.

Sampling technique

Convenience sampling was used to select the households.

According to the National Population and Housing Census 2021, the total households in the included Wards 1–4 were 2291, with households of 636, 535, 564, and 538, respectively[3]. One hundred and fifteen households were selected from each ward in this study.

Development and validation of questionnaire

A structured questionnaire assessing knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) on SM and healthcare-seeking behavior was developed using previously published studies[28–30]. The tool was adapted to the local context, considering the proximity to a medical college.

Content validity was ensured by a panel of three experts in medicine and public health. A pilot study was conducted in a similar setting using 5% of the expected sample to assess clarity and feasibility. Necessary modifications were made prior to the final administration.

Internal consistency was measured using Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded a score of 0.764, indicating acceptable reliability.

Measurement of KAP

The classification thresholds used for KAP were determined based on a modified Bloom’s cut-off criterion. A score representing ≥60% of the total attainable points was conventionally categorized as good or positive to indicate sufficient knowledge, a positive attitude, or good practice, while a score ≤60% was categorized as poor or negative[31].

Knowledge assessment

Knowledge was evaluated using six structured items. Correct responses were scored as 1, and incorrect responses were scored as 0. Total scores ranged from 0 to 6 and were categorized as:

Good knowledge: 4–6

Poor knowledge: 0–3

Attitude assessment

Attitude was assessed using four statements rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Total scores ranged from 4 to 20 and were categorized as:

Positive attitude: 13–20

Negative attitude: 4–12

Practice assessment

Practice was assessed using five structured questions. Safe practices scored 1; unsafe practices scored 0. The total practice score ranged from 0 to 7 and was categorized as:

Good practice: 5–7

Poor practice: 0–4

Data collection procedure

Data were collected via face-to-face interviews at participant’s homes. The study's purpose, procedures, and implications were explained, and written informed consent was obtained prior to participation.

All 453 eligible participants completed the healthcare-seeking behavior questionnaire. Among these, 389 individuals (85.9%) reported practicing SM and, therefore, completed the KAP questionnaire related to SM.

Data entry and statistical analysis

Collected data were entered into Microsoft Excel and checked for completeness. Incomplete records were excluded. Data analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 24.0.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. Chi-square tests were used to compare categorical variables. Binary logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with SM practices. A P-value of < 0.05 at a 95% confidence interval was considered statistically significant.

Health-seeking behavior

Family and friends (61.8%) and healthcare centers (60.3%) were the primary sources of health-related information. A total of 67.7% of participants preferred nearby hospitals for treatment when they were ill. Notably, 21.1% also consulted traditional healers, reflecting the influence of cultural practices on health behavior. Among the 104 respondents (22.9%) who experienced barriers to healthcare access, the most common barrier was a lack of knowledge about available services (51.9%). The majority of participants reported infrequent consultation, with “sometimes” being the most selected response (n = 210, 46.4%). Attitude toward SM

The attitude of the respondents toward SM is presented in Table 5. The univariable analysis indicated that occupation was significantly associated with attitude (χ2 = 16.364, P = 0.012). In the multivariable logistic regression, occupation was confirmed to be a significant predictor of attitude. Discussion

This cross-sectional study investigated the healthcare-seeking behavior and KAP of SM among residents in the vicinity of a tertiary healthcare center in Madhesh Province, Nepal. A complex interplay of traditional practices, service accessibility issues, and a high prevalence of SM, which collectively influence health outcomes in this setting, was revealed in this study.

Almost all respondents (99.8%) in this study sought physical healthcare when they felt ill, indicating a high overall propensity to seek help. However, the nature of this help was pluralistic, as 60.3% of the respondents consulted healthcare centers, while a notable minority of 21.1% also consulted traditional healers. This concurrent utilization confirms a highly pluralistic healthcare ecosystem, wherein residents often pragmatically use both traditional and modern systems, sometimes sequentially or simultaneously. These findings strongly align with established epidemiological patterns and public health literature from other parts of Nepal and the wider South Asian region, consistently affirming the deep-rooted and resilient influence of cultural practices on individual health behaviors[8]. In the context of evolving health information landscapes, the emergence of artificial intelligence (AI)-driven tools, particularly large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT, presents both opportunities and challenges for healthcare-seeking behavior in resource-limited settings. A recent machine-learning-driven global-scale bibliometric analysis by Guo et al identified that the accuracy and safety of ChatGPT in health information recommendation constituted the most impactful global research cluster, while patient education, health literacy, and communication emerged as its core thematic nodes[32]. These findings are particularly relevant to our study, where family and friends (61.8%) constituted the primary source of health-related information, reflecting a heavy dependence on informal and potentially unreliable networks. Additionally, the study showed that self-diagnosis is a declining yet alarming trend in ChatGPT-related medical research, indicating a growing global awareness of the risks associated with eschewing formal healthcare in favor of unreliable information sources. This pattern is reflected in our finding that 47.2% of respondents postponed seeking medical care by at least one day. Importantly, Guo et al also underscored the promising potential of LLMs in strengthening primary healthcare services and teleconsultation, which could help address the information gap identified in our study, where 51.9% of respondents who faced barriers to healthcare access cited lack of knowledge of available services as the primary impediment[32].

Among the 104 (22.9%) respondents who reported experiencing barriers to healthcare access, a critical finding of lack of knowledge about available services (51.9%) was found, which suggests that the physical proximity to a tertiary health care center does not automatically translate to accessible or satisfactory healthcare. Instead of a lack of physical infrastructure, the primary impediment is an information gap, a barrier frequently reported in studies across low- and middle-income countries[33,34]. The possible reasons for these findings could be the free and easy access to primary healthcare centers in the proximity of the study area. Additionally, the residents preferred to visit the tertiary healthcare center only for those healthcare services that were not provided at the primary healthcare centers. This highlights the penetration of government-owned health service centers at the community level. A study by Wu et al found that patients with higher education and income prefer tertiary hospitals, while chronic disease patients are more likely to utilize community care[35]. Furthermore, the finding that nearly half of the participants (47.2%) reported waiting at least a day before seeking medical help is concerning, as delayed care-seeking can lead to poor health outcomes, especially for acute conditions. This rate contrasts with a study conducted by Hunde et al in Ethiopia, focusing on childhood illness, where the rate was 74.5%[36].

The primary sources of health-related information were family and friends (61.8%) and health centers (60.3%). While health centers are a key source, the reliance on informal networks like family and friends underscores the need for targeted community-based health education to ensure the dissemination of accurate information[37]. The prevalence of SM among the study population was high, with 85.9% (389 out of 453). This figure is slightly higher than the 78% reported in a study conducted by Ghimire et al in Kathmandu Metropolitan City[38], suggesting that SM is pervasive across both urban and semi-urban/rural settings in Nepal.

KAP of SM

Knowledge of SM

The study revealed a significant gap in knowledge, with 73% of respondents having poor knowledge regarding SM. This low level of knowledge is a major public health concern, as poor understanding of drug use, dosage, and potential side effects can lead to adverse drug reactions and the development of antimicrobial resistance[39]. LLMs like ChatGPT are among the AI-driven tools and digital health technologies that have proliferated so quickly in recent years that they are starting to change how people look for and access health information worldwide. These technologies hold considerable promise for bridging health information gaps, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. However, a recent machine-learning-driven global-scale bibliometric analysis by Guo et al revealed that ChatGPT, despite showing a favorable development trend in medicine (Growth Rate Per Month: 26.97%) and strong international cooperation (International Co-authorships: 25.09%), remains highly unevenly distributed across the globe[32]. Developed regions such as the USA, UK, Australia, and Canada demonstrated the highest attention and application of ChatGPT in medicine, while underdeveloped and resource-poor regions remained significantly underrepresented, a disparity the authors warned would exacerbate global health inequalities. Critically, the study identified that regions with higher education levels and stronger digital infrastructure had significantly greater engagement with ChatGPT in medicine, whereas lower development indices and poor digital literacy were the primary barriers to its adoption in resource-limited settings. Furthermore, using the Walktrap algorithm, the study found that medical education and clinical decision support—both highly relevant to improving health literacy and rational medicine use—remain among the most underdeveloped areas of ChatGPT application, highlighting their urgent future research potential. These findings are particularly pertinent to our study population in Madhesh Province, Nepal, where 73% of respondents demonstrated poor knowledge of SM, and primary health information was sourced predominantly from informal networks. The convergence of low health literacy, limited digital infrastructure, and weak regulatory frameworks in such settings not only perpetuates unsafe SM practices but also widens the gap between communities in Madhesh Province and the transformative potential of emerging digital health technologies, underscoring the urgent need for targeted health literacy interventions and stronger policy frameworks at both local and global levels.

The finding that occupation, specifically being engaged in agriculture, was associated with 6.42 times higher odds of poor knowledge (AOR = 6.421; 95% CI: 1.835–22.471) is a novel and important finding. This suggests that public health campaigns need to be specifically tailored to reach and educate the agricultural workforce in this region, who may have limited access to formal education and health information compared to those in business or service sectors.

Attitude toward SM

The general attitude toward SM was found to be positive, which is a common trend observed in other studies in Nepal and South Asia[40]. The multivariable analysis indicated that occupations such as housewives, business, and students were associated with lower odds of having a negative attitude. This suggests that individuals in these groups may perceive SM as a convenient and effective solution for minor ailments, possibly due to time constraints or the perceived high cost of formal healthcare.

Practice of SM

The most common sources for SM were pharmacy shops (95.4%) and family/friends (15.9%). This highlights the critical role of community pharmacies as the primary point of access for non-prescription drugs and the need for strict enforcement of drug dispensing regulations to reduce the sale of prescription-only medicines without a valid prescription[41].

The most common ailments leading to SM were headache (84.5%), cough, cold, flu (75.3%), and fever (72.7%). These findings align with other studies conducted in South Asia, which consistently reported minor, self-limiting conditions as the main triggers for SM[38,42]. The main reasons cited were quick recovery (68.6%), low cost (61.2%), and previous experience (38.6%). These reasons directly reflect the perceived inefficiencies and costs associated with the formal healthcare system, reinforcing the earlier finding of this study regarding the lack of available services as a barrier.

A particularly noteworthy finding is the association between religion and SM practice, where Hindu participants had significantly lower odds of having good practice compared to Muslim participants (AOR = 0.361). While the study does not explore the underlying reasons, this difference may be attributed to varying sociocultural norms, health beliefs, or access to different types of traditional or faith-based healing practices between the two communities, a phenomenon that warrants further qualitative investigation[43].

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its focus on a specific, under-researched population: residents in the vicinity of a tertiary healthcare center in Madhesh Province. This provides valuable, localized data that can inform regional health policy.

However, the study is subject to several limitations. The self-reported nature of the data is susceptible to recall bias and social desirability bias, particularly concerning sensitive topics like SM and consultation with traditional healers. Finally, the findings are specific to the peri-urban area near the tertiary center and may not be generalizable to other regions of Madhesh Province, Nepal.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study highlighted a highly pluralistic healthcare-seeking pattern and a high prevalence of SM among residents living near a tertiary healthcare center in Madhesh Province. Although nearly all individuals sought care when ill, many continued to rely on traditional healers alongside modern health services, reflecting the enduring influence of cultural practices on health behavior. Despite close proximity to a tertiary facility, access barriers persisted, driven largely by limited awareness of available services. The need for better health communication and community engagement is further highlighted by the significant delay in seeking formal care.

A major public health concern is the pervasive practice of SM, which is exacerbated by a lack of knowledge about safe medication use, particularly among those working in agriculture. Pharmacies serve as the primary gateway for SM, emphasizing the need for stronger regulatory oversight. The variations observed across sociodemographic groups, including the influence of occupation and religion, point to deeper sociocultural dynamics that shape health decisions in this region.

To improve public health outcomes, we recommend: Targeted health education and service promotion: Developing and implementing health literacy programs focused on the safe use of medicines and the risks of SM, specifically targeting the agricultural workforce and using accessible communication channels. Given that family and friends (61.8%) constitute the primary source of health information and that 73% of respondents demonstrate poor knowledge of SM, targeted health literacy interventions are critically needed. However, for such interventions to be effective in Madhesh Province, they must be designed in alignment with the existing communication landscape and sociocultural context of the community. Community radio, which enjoys wide listenership in rural and semi-urban settings of the Madhesh region, should be leveraged to broadcast regular health education programs in the local Maithili and Bhojpuri languages, addressing safe medicine use, recognition of prescription drugs, and the importance of timely healthcare-seeking. Places of worship, including temples and mosques, represent trusted community spaces that attract large congregations across diverse religious groups; given the significant association between religion and SM practices identified in this study, faith-based health education outreach through religious leaders could be a particularly effective strategy for reaching both Hindu and Muslim communities. Marketplaces and weekly grocery markets, frequently visited by the agricultural workforce identified in our study as having 6.42 times higher odds of poor knowledge, offer additional high-footfall venues for community health workers to distribute culturally adapted information materials and conduct brief educational sessions. Free health camps in the vicinity of the medical college should be conducted from time to time with the aim of reducing SM practices and promoting visits to nearby healthcare facilities in case of any illness. This can be achieved through awareness and by educating residents near the college.Regulation of pharmacies: Enforcing strict regulations on the dispensing of prescription-only medicines by community pharmacies. The findings of this study underscore the urgent need to strengthen pharmacy regulation in Madhesh Province, particularly given that 95.4% of respondents obtained medicines directly from pharmacy shops, often without a valid prescription. Regulatory authorities should consider implementing a multi-pronged approach to address this gap. First, mandatory training and continuing education programs for pharmacy personnel should be established, with a specific focus on rational drug dispensing, recognition of prescription-only medicines, and counseling skills for common self-limiting conditions. Second, a standardized record-keeping system for the sale of prescription drugs at community pharmacies should be introduced, enabling health authorities to monitor dispensing patterns, detect misuse, and identify high-risk medicines being dispensed without prescriptions. Third, community-based pharmacy monitoring mechanisms should be explored, wherein trained community health volunteers or local health committees conduct periodic assessments of pharmacy practices and report violations to district health offices. Regulation of pharmacies: Enforcing strict regulations on the dispensing of prescription-only medicines by community pharmacies. The findings of this study underscore the urgent need to strengthen pharmacy regulation in Madhesh Province, particularly given that 95.4% of respondents obtained medicines directly from pharmacy shops, often without a valid prescription. Regulatory authorities should consider implementing a multi-pronged approach to address this gap. First, mandatory training and continuing education programs for pharmacy personnel should be established, with a specific focus on rational drug dispensing, recognition of prescription-only medicines, and counseling skills for common self-limiting conditions. Second, a standardized record-keeping system for the sale of prescription drugs at community pharmacies should be introduced, enabling health authorities to monitor dispensing patterns, detect misuse, and identify high-risk medicines being dispensed without prescriptions. Third, community-based pharmacy monitoring mechanisms should be explored, wherein trained community health volunteers or local health committees conduct periodic assessments of pharmacy practices and report violations to district health offices. These measures, if implemented in a coordinated manner, could significantly reduce the indiscriminate dispensing of prescription medicines and curb SM practices at the community level.

Further research: Conducting studies to explore the sociocultural and religious factors influencing the observed differences in SM practices, as well as the reasons behind the high consultation rate with traditional healers.
