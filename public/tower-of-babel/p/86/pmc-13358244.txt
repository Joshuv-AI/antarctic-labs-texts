High unmet need for modern contraception among married women of reproductive age (15–49 years) in Mogadishu, Somalia: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Abdulwahab M Salad, Abdi Gele, Rhoda K Wanyenze, Marian Muse Osman, Amanda Cleeve, Elin C Larsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionUnmet need for modern contraception is measured as the proportion of fertile women who do not use contraception despite wanting to delay or limit their childbearing. The persistently high unmet need for modern contraception among ever-married women of reproductive age in Somalia highlights the importance of understanding the individual determinants of this unmet need. This study aims to identify the unmet need for modern contraception and examine the factors associated with this need among married women of reproductive age in Mogadishu, Somalia.MethodsA community-based, cross-sectional study with 462, randomly selected, married women of reproductive age (15–49 years) was conducted from October 2024 to January 2025. A pretested questionnaire was used to collect data from the participants. Descriptive and logistic regression analyses were employed using Stata V.18.ResultsA total of 117 (25%) had an unmet need for modern contraception. Illiterate women had significantly higher odds of unmet need for modern contraception compared with those with higher education levels (Adjusted odd ratio(AOR)=3.6, 95% CI 1.64 to 7.88). Similarly, women whose first pregnancy occurred at the age of ≤18 had significantly higher odds of unmet need for modern contraception compared with those whose first pregnancy occurred after 18 years (AOR=1.8, 95% CI 1.10 to 2.98). Furthermore, women who did not receive antenatal care (ANC) during their last pregnancy had higher odds of unmet need for modern contraception (AOR=1.5, 95% CI 1.01 to 2.44) compared to those who did.ConclusionThe unmet need for modern contraception among married women of reproductive age in Mogadishu remains very high. Literacy, age of first pregnancy and ANC attendance were shown to be important predictors of unmet need for modern contraception. Improving women’s education, preventing teenage pregnancy and enhancing ANC attendance would play an important role in increasing the use of modern contraceptives in Somalia.Keywords: Sexual Health, Reproductive History, Public Health

Introduction

Modern contraceptives include injectables, subdermal implant, oral contraceptive pills, condoms, intrauterine devices and systems, emergency contraceptive pills, patches, diaphragms and cervical caps, spermicidal agents, sterilisation, vaginal rings and sponge.1 The use of modern contraceptives for spacing and limiting births is crucial for the health, survival and overall well-being of women and children globally.2 Modern contraceptives also promote gender equality, contribute to women’s social and economic empowerment and give women more control over their reproductive choices.3 4 In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), modern contraceptive use significantly reduces maternal and neonatal mortality.5 6 Despite these documented benefits of modern contraception, approximately 214 million women globally have an unmet need for modern contraceptives.7 This unmet need remains high in LMICs, at 24.7%.8

Consequently, more than 85 million unintended or mistimed pregnancies occur annually, contributing to high rates of induced abortions, maternal morbidity and mortality.7

Global initiatives—such as Sustainable Development Goals (SDG)9 targets 3.7 and 5.6 and the Family Planning 2020 (FP2020)9 Agenda—aim to ensure universal access to sexual and reproductive healthcare and rights, including the availability of essential family planning services.10 The FP2020 Agenda aimed to ensure access to modern contraceptives for 120 million additional women and adolescent girls in 69 poorest countries in the world by 2020.8 During this period, the use of modern contraceptives in Africa has gradually increased from 23.9% in 2012 to 28.5% in 2017.11 However, the overall prevalence of an unmet need for modern contraceptives among FP2020 target countries during the same time was 21.6%, with most countries in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) reporting rates exceeding 25%.11 This high-rate unmet need for modern contraception impedes progress towards achieving the SDG indicator 3.7.1, which emphasises the proportion of women of reproductive age whose unmet need for family planning is satisfied with the modern methods.12 13

Somalia has one of the highest fertility rates in the world, at 6.2 births per woman compared with the SSA average of 4.8 births per woman, and the global average of 2.4.7 This high fertility rate may be attributed, in part, to the extremely low use of modern contraceptives, at 1% among married women of reproductive age, as well as the high unmet need for modern contraception, at 37% among the same population group.14

The association between high fertility rates and low contraception use in Somalia is associated with a multitude of factors: limited access to healthcare and family planning services, cultural and religious beliefs favouring large families, and low levels of education, particularly among women.7 Despite interventions aimed at increasing the awareness of and the accessibility to contraception in Somalia, the deeply rooted societal norms and the lack of resources still contribute to the country’s low use of modern contraception.7 Comprehensive strategies that address these barriers are essential for advancing family planning and reproductive health in Somalia.

Today, there is a lack of evidence about the factors associated with the unmet need for modern contraceptives in urban areas of Somalia. Therefore, this study aimed to identify factors associated with unmet need of modern contraception among married women of reproductive age in Mogadishu, the capital city of Somalia. Enhancing our knowledge of this matter can guide the development of culturally sensitive policies and practices, thus improving access to and acceptance of modern contraception in Somalia.

Methods

Study design, setting and population

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from October 2024 to January 2025 among married women of reproductive age (15–49 years) in Mogadishu, the capital city of Somalia.15 Mogadishu has a population of 1.7 million and is administratively divided into 17 districts.15 16

The city’s health service delivery system operates at three essential levels: national referral hospitals, which provide advanced care including comprehensive delivery and family planning services; regional and district hospitals, which offer full deliveries, family planning services and postnatal care, with complicated cases referred upward; and health centres, which deliver basic primary care such as antenatal care (ANC) services, normal deliveries, and family planning and immunisation services.17 At all levels, healthcare services are mainly financed out-of-pocket, except at health centres where services are provided free of charge.17 Study respondents were married women of reproductive age who lived in the study area during the data collection period. Women who were ill or declined to sign the consent form were excluded from the study.

Sample size and sampling method

The sample size was calculated using a single proportion formula with the assumption of a 95% CI, 5% margin of error and an assumed 49% prevalence of unmet need from a previous study in Mogadishu.7 To account for a 20% non-response rate, the final sample size was determined to be 462 participants.

A two-stage sampling method was used to select the study participants. First, two districts were randomly selected from the 17 districts in Mogadishu. Next, two zones were randomly chosen from each of the two districts. For this study, we counted the number of households in selected zones in the two districts, and a number was given to each household using a geographic information system, supported by Nova Technologies company in Mogadishu. The sample size was then proportionally allocated based on the total number of households in each zone.

To select the first household from each zone, an interval number was calculated by dividing the total number of households in each zone by its allocated sample size; then, a random number household within this interval was selected, and subsequent households were selected at the same interval by applying a systematic random sampling technique until the required sample was achieved. If a selected household did not have an eligible participant or was not at home, the next household to the right was approached. Only one respondent per household was included.

Data collection

A semistructured questionnaire was developed based on existing literature and instruments.714 18,21 The questionnaire included questions such as demographic characteristics, reproductive health characteristics and knowledge about and use of contraceptive methods. The questionnaire was initially prepared in English and subsequently translated into Somali. The Somali version was then back translated into English by the first author of this paper, who is proficient in both languages, to ensure accuracy and consistency of the questionnaire.

The questionnaire and data collection procedure were piloted on 10% (46) of other participants from the study area, who were not included in the final study sample. After piloting, a minor adjustment regarding the language and content of the questionnaire was made to improve its clarity and understanding. The final version of the questionnaire was used for data collection using the online KoboCollect application.22

A team consisting of four females, led by a female field supervisor, all of whom had a bachelor’s degree in medicine, was recruited as research assistants to collect the data. A 2-day training organised by the first author was provided to the data collectors covering the purpose, relevance, ethical perspectives and interview techniques pertaining to the study. The interviews were held face-to-face at participant’s home between 8 AM and 5 PM and the research assistant filled out the questionnaire. Each interview lasted about 30–40 min. Interviews were carried out in a quiet, private area of the home to ensure confidentiality and avoid distractions.

Measures and outcomes

The primary outcome was unmet need for modern contraception, measured as the proportion of fecund and sexually active women who did not want any more children or desired to delay the next child by at least 2 years but were not currently using any modern contraceptive method. It also included pregnant women whose last pregnancy was mistimed.23 The denominator included women who were at risk of being pregnant and those who were (1) married, (2) sexually active and (3) fecund. Among this population, women were categorised as modern contraceptive users and non-users of modern contraception.23

The secondary outcome was use of modern contraception, measured as the proportion of women who reported themselves as currently using at least one modern contraceptive method.1

Covariates included measures known to be associated with contraceptive use: sociodemographic characteristics such as age, highest level of education, occupation, family income and reproductive health characteristics, parity and husband’s educational level.

Data analysis

We exported the data from Kobo Toolbox to Excel to check completeness and cleaning and thereafter exported it to Stata V.18 for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the distribution of the study variables using frequencies, percentages and measures of central tendency. Afterwards, univariable analysis was conducted and the crude OR with 95% CI and p values were recorded. All variables that were significant in the univariate analysis (p<0.05) were entered into the multivariate analysis using a logistic regression model to control for potential covariates.

Patient and public involvement

This study did not involve patients and the public in its design, data collection, or dissemination plans.

Results

Sociodemographic and reproductive health characteristics

A total of 462 married women of reproductive age were interviewed. Background and reproductive health characteristics are shown in table 1. More than one-third of the participants (N=197, 43%) were aged between 25 and 34 with a median age of 29 years (IQR 24–36). Approximately two-thirds of the participants (N=299, 65%) either lacked formal education or were illiterate. Among the participants, 377 (82%) were unemployed, while 415 (90%) of their husbands were employed. Nearly half of the participants (N=226, 49%) had a monthly family income of $US$200 or less. Furthermore, 344 (74%) of the participants were married at ≤18 years old, and 306 (66%) became pregnant at an age ≤18 years.

Most participants (N=210, 45%) had five or more living children. Nearly half of the women (N=202, 44%) did not attend ANC during their last pregnancy. Among women who attended ANC during their last pregnancy, 113 (43%) attended private hospitals. More than one-third of the women (N=149, 34%) delivered at home during their last pregnancy, and a similar percentage, 150 (34%), were assisted by traditional birth attendants. Nearly two-thirds of women (N=29, 62%) who were assisted by a healthcare provider during their last delivery did not receive counselling on the use of modern contraceptives. 325 (71%) of the women indicated that they would prefer to have more than six children if given the choice.

Knowledge and prevalence of contraceptive use

95% of participants reported having heard of at least one type of modern contraceptive method. The implant was the most known contraceptive method, at 423 (92%), followed by oral pills, at 400 (87%), and injectables, at 323 (70%). Knowledge of emergency contraceptive and female condom methods was the lowest, at 88 (19%) and 49 (11%), respectively.

Among the participants, 156 (34%) reported currently using any type of contraceptive method, with only 45 (10%) using modern methods. The main reasons reported by the respondents for not using modern contraceptives were fear of side effects, at 85 (18%), personal choice not to use, at 64 (14%), and opposition from their husbands, at 62 (13%).

Unmet need of modern contraceptives by respondents’ background and reproductive health characteristics

As shown in figure 1, unmet need for modern contraception was calculated as the total number of women who were not using modern contraceptive methods and wished to delay or avoid pregnancy (n=98), plus currently pregnant women whose pregnancy was unintended (n=19). Therefore, the unmet need for modern contraception was 117 out of 462 women, which equals 25%.

How modern contraception was calculated.

As shown in table 2, the unmet need for modern contraception was higher among illiterate women (34%) than among those who obtained secondary education and university (14%). Women who married by the age ≤18 years had greater unmet need (30%) than those married >18 years (13%). A similar pattern was reported for the age at first pregnancy, where respondents who became pregnant by age ≤18 had a greater unmet need for contraception (29%) than those whose first pregnancy occurred >18 years of age (17%). Unmet need was higher among women who had five or more living children than among those with one to two children (39%). Similarly, the unmet need for contraception was greater among women who did not attend ANC during their last pregnancy (31%) than among those attending ANC (21%). Women who delivered at home (34%) or in public hospitals (34%) had a higher unmet need for contraception than those who delivered at private hospitals (14%).

Factors associated with unmet need for contraception

Education and family income were adjusted for unmet need of modern contraception.

Illiterate women had significantly higher odds of unmet need for modern contraception compared with those with secondary education and university (AOR(Adjusted odd ratio)=3.6, 95% CI 1.64 to 7.88). Likewise, women whose first pregnancy occurred at age ≤18 had significantly higher odds of unmet need compared with those whose first pregnancy occurred after age 18 (AOR=1.8, 95% CI 1.10 to 2.98). Women with one to two living children (AOR=0.16, 95% CI 0.08, 0.33) and those with three to four children (AOR=0.39, 95% CI 0.22, 0.68) had significantly lower odds of having an unmet need for contraception compared with those with five or more children. Finally, women who did not attend ANC during their last pregnancy had 1.5 times higher odds of unmet need for contraception (AOR=1.5, 95% CI 1.01 to 2.44) compared with those who did.

Discussion

The rate of unmet need for modern contraception identified in this study, 25%, is comparable to that reported in SSA involving both married and cohabiting women, which found an unmet need of 28.7%.24 However, we found that the rate of 25% is lower than the one reported 37% by the Somali Health Demographic Survey in 2020.14 This lower rate may be attributed to the city’s better healthcare infrastructure, the higher density of health facilities and the greater availability of skilled providers than other regions of Somalia.17

We found that the rate of unmet need was higher among women who married at the age of ≤18, which might be attributed to the Somali cultural norms where women are often considered marriageable starting at the age of 15. Our study corresponds to that of Ahinkorah, which shows that early marriage is significantly associated with unmet need for contraception, primarily due to early sexual debut and limited autonomy in decision-making.25 Similarly, a study conducted in Ethiopia found that married adolescent girls aged 15–19 had a higher unmet need for modern contraception.9 For meaningful progress in family planning efforts in Somalia, there must be a concerted effort to shift societal norms by delaying marriage, allowing young women to pursue personal, educational and professional goals before entering matrimonial commitments. This delay not only allows young girls to mature emotionally and financially but also fosters an environment that prioritise informed choices, ultimately leading to healthier families and communities.

The unmet need for contraception in this study was higher among illiterate women and among those with low levels of education as demonstrated in other studies in LMICs in Asia and SSA.826,28 Another study in SSA also showed a positive association between women with no formal education and unmet need for modern contraception.29 Evidence proves that achieving universal access to contraceptive services will not be possible without increasing women’s educational attainment.30 In Somalia, as in many other LMICs, women experience lower social standing and less autonomy. Therefore, enhancing women’s education is crucial to improving their social status and empowering them to make decisions about contraception independently.

In our study, a higher number of living children was significantly associated with a higher likelihood of unmet need. This finding verifies that of a study conducted in Kenya.31 A probable reason for that is that as women have more children, they become more likely to want to use modern contraceptives.29 Another probable reason may be that women with a large number of children might find themselves overwhelmed with handling household duties, which can restrict their ability to visit health facilities for maternal, newborn and child health services, including access to contraceptives.3 29 Therefore, modern contraception service providers must pay attention to these women.

The low prevalence of modern contraceptive use—only 1 in 10 women—reported in this study may be attributed to deeply ingrained pronatalist social norms that emphasise cultural expectations for couples to have many children.7 Such cultural pressures often deter women from spacing their children or from controlling the number of children they may have.32 Furthermore, the use of modern contraception is frequently discouraged within the Somali community, largely due to misconceptions that are inaccurately linked to religious and cultural beliefs.7 This cultural stance is compounded by the fact that many women, who lack adequate education, find themselves compelled to adhere to and accept these prevailing societal norms. In countries like Somalia, widespread misinformation surrounding contraception, particularly for women, contributes to low use of modern contraceptives, contributing to high fertility rates.33 This situation underscores the urgent need for culturally sensitive educational initiatives that can clarify misconceptions and empower women with the knowledge needed to make informed health decisions, ultimately challenging and changing the entrenched norms that limit their reproductive choices.

In Mogadishu, where most of the country’s healthcare facilities are located,17 only 43% of women attended ANC during their last pregnancy, significantly limiting the opportunities for dialogue on women’s reproductive health rights, including postnatal family planning with healthcare providers.34 Limited access to quality ANC services has been seen to reduce women’s opportunity to receive essential education and counselling about modern contraception. By increasing ANC attendance, healthcare facilities can enhance women’s exposure to contraception-related information, dispel myths and misconceptions, and empower women to take control of their reproductive health.

Furthermore, the fact that 51% of women were not aware of their husbands’ fertility preferences underscores a significant gap in spousal communication about reproductive intentions. In Somalia, where societal values place high importance on fertility and where men often dominate reproductive decisions,35 this gender gap explains that many women may not take family planning decisions without men’s permission. This is in line with studies conducted in several SSA countries, showing that couples’ discussions have a strong and positive correlation with women’s contraceptive use.36,38

This study has important implications, as it brings attention to the persisting challenges of limited contraception use, and a high unmet need for modern contraception, driven by a complex web of sociocultural issues, illiteracy, early pregnancies and marriage practices. Reasons for these associations are not yet established.

Strengths and limitations

The cross-sectional design of this study limits the ability to establish causal relationships between covariates and outcomes. The study focused exclusively on married women of reproductive age, thereby excluding unmarried women and those outside this age range. As a result, the findings may not be generalisable to all women. We believe that the unmet need would be even larger in that group. Since the study was conducted in Mogadishu, the capital city of Somalia—the results primarily reflect an urban context and may not adequately represent rural or nomadic populations. Nonetheless, the findings provide valuable context-based data to inform national family planning policies and support Somalia’s broader goals of improving reproductive health and achieving Universal Health Coverage.

Conclusion

Despite access to healthcare being higher in Mogadishu compared with other regions in Somalia, the high rate of unmet need and low prevalence of modern contraceptives remain a pressing concern. Illiteracy, experiencing the first pregnancy before the age of 18, and not attending ANC during the most recent pregnancy were found to be significantly associated with the unmet need for contraception. Addressing the factors significantly associated with the unmet need for contraception requires a multifaceted approach. Enhancing literacy programmes tailored for women can empower them with knowledge and decision-making abilities. Promoting adolescent health education by integrating comprehensive sexual and reproductive health education into school curricula is crucial for raising awareness about the benefits of delaying pregnancies. Expanding access to ANC and family planning services, across both public and private facilities, is essential for reducing unmet need and improving reproductive health outcomes in Somalia.
