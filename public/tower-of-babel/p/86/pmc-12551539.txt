Unravelling IPAF, VEDOSS and connective tissue diseases classifications through the mixed connective tissue disease spectrum
Authors: Kevin Chevalier, Benjamin Thoreau, Marc Michel, Bertrand Godeau, Christian Agard, Thomas Papo, Karim Sacre, Brigitte Bader-Meunier, Raphaele Seror, Xavier Mariette, Patrice Cacoub, Ygal Benhamou, Hervé Levesque, Cécile Goujard, Olivier Lambotte, Bernard Bonnotte, Maxime Samson, Félix Ackermann, Jean Schmidt, Pierre Duhaut, Isabelle Koné-Paut, Jean-Emmanuel Kahn, Thomas Hanslik, Nathalie Costedoat-Chalumeau, Benjamin Terrier, Alexis Regent, Bertrand Dunogue, Pascal Cohen, Véronique Le Guern, Eric Hachulla, Benjamin Chaigne, Luc Mouthon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMixed connective tissue disease (MCTD) has long been debated as an early nonspecific phase/symptom of differentiated connective tissue diseases (dCTD), similarly to interstitial pneumonia with autoimmune features (IPAF) and very early diagnosis of systemic sclerosis (SSc) (VEDOSS).ObjectiveWe aimed to evaluate the predictive value of IPAF, VEDOSS and dCTD classification criteria variables in MCTD patients.MethodsWe conducted an observational study within the French MCTD cohort. IPAF, VEDOSS and current dCTD classification criteria were used to classify patients.ResultsThree hundred and twenty-four MCTD patients were included and followed for 8 (3.3–13) years. Among them, 111 (34.3%) progressed into a dCTD, that is, 50 (15.4%) SSc, 40 (12.3%) systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and 11 (3.4%) Sjögren’s disease. At diagnosis, 38 (11.7%) patients fulfilled IPAF criteria, among which 15 (39.5%) progressed into a dCTD (vs 75 (26.2%) in patients who did not fulfil IPAF criteria; p=0.09). At diagnosis, 293 (90.4%) patients fulfilled VEDOSS criteria but did not progress significantly more frequently to SSc than MCTD patients without VEDOSS criteria (46 (15.7%) vs 4 (12.9%); p=0.8). At baseline, SSc classification criteria did not predict evolution toward SSc, whereas antiphospholipid antibodies and low C3 and/or C4 were predictive of an evolution toward SLE (p=0.01 and p=0.04, respectively).ConclusionAt MCTD diagnosis, fulfilment of IPAF and/or VEDOSS criteria was not predictive of evolution toward SSc, whereas antiphospholipid antibodies and low C3 and/or C4 were predictive of an evolution toward SLE. This suggests that MCTD patients should be excluded from IPAF and VEDOSS.Keywords: Classification; Connective Tissue Diseases; Scleroderma, Systemic; Lupus Erythematosus, Systemic

Introduction

Connective tissue diseases (CTD) classification criteria have been developed to standardise groups of patients with the same diagnosis for inclusion in clinical studies.1,4 While these classifications have become increasingly sensitive and specific over time, they are unable to capture patients with early or very early-stage disease. To address this unmet need, the concept of a ‘pre-CTD’ state has emerged in the literature. Several entities have been defined to correspond to this ‘pre-CTD’ state, such as very early diagnosis of systemic sclerosis (VEDOSS),5 6 which corresponds to a ‘pre-systemic sclerosis (SSc)’. One goal of these entities is to identify patients—and therefore, which factors—are at risk of progressing to a dCTD, allowing for efforts to predict and prevent this progression or manage its complications, which could negatively affect functional outcomes, vital prognosis, or quality of life and increase the therapeutic burden.7 In addition, some manifestations are suggestive of autoimmune features but may be present in the absence of a differentiated CTD (dCTD). Along these lines, the Interstitial Pneumonia with Autoimmune Features (IPAF) criteria were established to identify patients with interstitial lung disease (ILD) who exhibit features suggestive of an underlying autoimmune process but do not meet the criteria for a dCTD.8 9

Our group has recently described the largest retrospective cohort of mixed connective tissue disease (MCTD, or Sharp syndrome) patients, highlighting this syndrome as a distinct entity characterised by clinical features shared with other CTDs (such as Raynaud’s phenomenon, puffy fingers, arthralgia, myalgia, ILD) and the presence of autoantibodies (Abs) directed against the ribonucleoprotein U1 (anti-U1RNP). However, since its first description, several authors have argued that MCTD is not a distinct entity but rather an early, nonspecific phase of a dCTD, including idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM), rheumatoid arthritis, Sjögren’s disease (SjD), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) or SSc.10,13 In fact, approximately 25% of MCTD patients will meet the classification criteria for at least one other CTD after a median follow-up of 8 years.14 In line with this, some authors have suggested that MCTD patients could belong to the IPAF or VEDOSS groups, given their clinical similarities and the presence of anti-U1RNP antibodies in the IPAF criteria definition.5 9 Therefore, the controversial status of MCTD as a ‘pre-CTD’ and its relatively frequent progression to a dCTD present a unique opportunity to evaluate the predictive value of dCTD classification, IPAF and VEDOSS criteria. By leveraging the French MCTD cohort, which includes more than 300 well-classified patients (4), we conducted an observational study to address these objectives.

Methods

Study design

We conducted an observational retrospective multicentre cohort study within the French MCTD cohort, a retrospective cohort of patients with MCTD followed in 15 rare disease reference and competence centres, including two paediatric centres, across France. Detailed information about the characteristics and formation of this cohort has been previously reported.14 Briefly, inclusion criteria for the MCTD cohort required patients to meet, at the time of disease diagnosis, at least one of the four existing MCTD diagnosis classification criteria: Sharp,15 Alarcon-Segovia,16 Kahn17 or Kasukawa criteria.18 Patients were excluded if, at disease onset, they fulfilled the classification criteria for any other CTD, including the American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European alliance of associations for rheumatology (EULAR) 2019 classification for SLE,2 the ACR/EULAR 2013 classification for SSc,1 the ACR/EULAR 2010 criteria for rheumatoid arthritis,19 the ACR/EULAR 2017 classification for IIM,4 or the ACR/EULAR 2016 classification for SjD.3

Data collection

Data, including demographics, date of MCTD diagnosis and first symptoms, clinical features, nailfold capillaroscopy, radiological and biological findings (including immunological data) at MCTD onset, during follow-up, and at the last known follow-up, as well as the date of progression to another CTD (if applicable), were collected.

Definitions

Progression to a dCTD was defined according to the current classification criteria (see above). To analyse VEDOSS, we considered that patients fulfilled VEDOSS criteria if they had Raynaud’s phenomenon and at least one other criterion, including positive antinuclear Abs (ANA), positive, puffy fingers, SSc-specific Abs positivity (anti-centromere, anti-Scl70, or anti-RNA polymerase III), or abnormal nailfold capillaroscopy as performed previously.5 For IPAF, we used the definition proposed in 2015 by the European Respiratory Society/American Thoracic Society.8 9

Abnormal nailfold capillaroscopy was defined by an SSc pattern, that is, the presence of giant capillaries, capillary rarefaction and/or haemorrhages.1

Pulmonary arterial hypertension was defined according to the 2022 European guidelines, as a mean pulmonary artery pressure >20 mmHg at rest, with pulmonary arterial wedge pressure ≤15 mmHg and pulmonary vascular resistance >2 Wood units on right heart catheterisation.20 ILD was defined by the presence of at least one of the following imaging findings: honeycombing, traction bronchiectasis, ground-glass opacity, or reticulations on chest high-resolution CT scan.21

Ethical approval

The French MCTD cohort study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the French organisation dedicated to privacy, technology and civil rights (Commission Nationale de l’Informatique et des Libertés, reference 225 396 v0). Ethical Review Committee approval was granted on 30 January 2022; number AAA-2022–08008.

Statistical analyses

Data were reported as numbers (n) and percentages (%) for categorical variables, compared using the χ‐square or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Quantitative variables were expressed as the median and interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis test as appropriate. Two-tailed p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Multivariate analysis using logistic regression was performed to assess the association between dCTD classification criteria variables, IPAF and VEDOSS criteria, and progression to a dCTD. Variables with a p value <0.1 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate analysis. Results were expressed as odd-ratio (OR) and confidence interval (CI). Statistical analyses were conducted using STATA (V.14, Data Analysis and Statistical Software) and R (V.4.4.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Patients’ characteristics

Three hundred and twenty-four patients were included and followed up for a median (IQR) duration of 101 (48–176) months. Two hundred and eighty-four (87.7%) patients were females, with a median age at diagnosis of 34 (24–45) years. Of these, 98 (30.2%) were of Afro-Caribbean ancestry, 21 (6.5%) were of Asian descent, and 205 (63.3%) were Caucasian. At the time of MCTD diagnosis, 293 (90.4%) patients had Raynaud’s phenomenon, 141 (43.5%) had puffy fingers, 217 (67.0%) experienced arthralgia, 71/151 (47.0%) had abnormal capillaroscopy, and 38 (11.7%) had ILD. During follow-up, 303 (93.5%) patients had Raynaud’s phenomenon, 188 (58.0%) had puffy fingers, 275 (84.9%) experienced arthralgia, 120/202 (59.4%) had abnormal capillaroscopy, and 88 (27.2%) had ILD. Among these 324 patients, 90 (27.8%) fulfilled at least one dCTD classification criteria during follow-up, including 50 (15.4%), 40 (12.3%) and 11 (3.4%) patients fulfilling SSc, SLE or SjD classification criteria, respectively.

IPAF, VEDOSS and SSc criteria

IPAF criteria

At MCTD diagnosis, 38 (11.7%) patients had ILD, and all of them fulfilled the IPAF criteria (table 1). Comparison between MCTD patients fulfilling IPAF criteria (MCTD-IPAF+) and other patients (MCTD-IPAF−) revealed that MCTD-IPAF+ patients had less frequently Raynaud’s phenomenon (n=28, 73.7% vs n=265, 92.7%; p=0.001), more frequently pulmonary arterial hypertension (n=5, 13.2% vs n=5, 1.7%; p<0.0001), anti-CCP (n=2/20, 10.0% vs n=1/149, 0.7%; p=0.04), anti-SSA (n=16, 42.2% vs n=35/282, 12.4%; p<0.0001), and anti-SSB (n=6, 15.8% vs n=2, 0.7%; p<0.0001) Abs. During follow-up, 15 (39.5%) MCTD-IPAF+ patients fulfilled at least one CTD classification criterion (including 8 (21.1%), who fulfilled SSc classification criteria) compared with 75 (26.2%) MCTD-IPAF− patients (including 42 (14.7%), who fulfilled SSc classification criteria) (p=0.09).

VEDOSS criteria

At MCTD diagnosis, 293 patients (90.4%) fulfilled the VEDOSS criteria (MCTD-VEDOSS+) (table 2). All of them had Raynaud’s phenomenon and ANA. One hundred and thirty-two patients (45.1%) had puffy fingers (vs 10, 32.3% in MCTD-VEDOSS− patients; p=0.02), 68/146 (46.6%) had abnormal nailfold capillaroscopy (vs 3/5, 60.0% in MCTD-VEDOSS− patients; p=0.1), and 1 (0.3%) had an SSc-specific antibody (vs 0 in MCTD-VEDOSS− patients). After a median follow-up of 101 (48–176) months, 46 (15.7%) of the 293 MCTD-VEDOSS+ patients progressed to SSc, compared with 4 (12.9%) of patients not fulfilling VEDOSS criteria (p=0.8). The number of VEDOSS criteria was not associated with progression to SSc (p=0.8). Among MCTD-VEDOSS+ patients, an abnormal nailfold capillaroscopy at MCTD diagnosis was significantly associated with progression toward SSc (p=0.01).

Since all patients were ANA positive, we performed a second analysis without requiring ANA positivity to meet VEDOSS criteria. With this change, 163 patients on 324 (50.3%) met VEDOSS criteria (online supplemental table 1). All of these patients had Raynaud’s phenomenon, 125 (76.7%) had only one other VEDOSS criterion, and 38 (23.3%) had two VEDOSS criteria. As expected, patients fulfilling these VEDOSS criteria had more frequent abnormal nailfold capillaroscopy (n=68/106, 64.2% vs n=3/45, 6.7%; p<0.0001) and puffy fingers (n=131, 80.4% vs n=10, 6.2%; p<0.0001). Thirty-one (19.0%) of the 163 MCTD-VEDOSS+ patients progressed to SSc, compared with 19 (11.8%) of the MCTD-VEDOSS- (p=0.07). Ten (26.3%) MCTD-VEDOSS+ patients with two criteria, in addition to Raynaud’s phenomenon, progressed to SSc, compared with 21 (16.8%) MCTD-VEDOSS+ patients with only one criterion (p=0.08). Among patients fulfilling the VEDOSS criteria, lung involvement (p<0.05) at MCTD diagnosis was statistically independently associated with progression towards SSc (onlinesupplemental tables 1 2).

SSc criteria

We next evaluated whether SSc classification criteria variables were associated with progression towards SSc during follow-up. Among the 50 (15.4%) patients who progressed to SSc, only 20 (40.0%) had six points or more on the ACR/EULAR 2013 SSc classification criteria at MCTD diagnosis (online supplemental table 3). Univariate analysis revealed that a higher median duration between the first clinical manifestation and MCTD diagnosis (21 (5–36) vs (8 (3–26) months; p=0.04), abnormal nailfold capillaroscopy (n=19/29, 65.5% vs 52/122, 42.6%; p=0.02), and an ACR/EULAR 2013 SSc classification criteria ≥6 points at MCTD diagnosis (n=20, 40.0% vs 45, 16.4%; p<0.0001) were associated with evolution towards SSc (table 3). However, none of these remained significantly associated with progression to SSc in multivariate analysis. Fulfilling VEDOSS criteria was also not associated with progression to SSc in multivariate analysis with or without considering ANA positivity (p=0.9 and p=0.8, respectively) (table 4).

SLE criteria

Forty (12.3%) patients progressed to SLE. Among them, 14 (35%) had seven points or more on the ACR/EULAR 2019 SLE classification criteria at MCTD diagnosis (online supplemental table 6). We found that at MCTD diagnosis, patients with antiphospholipid (APL) Abs (ie, anti-cardiolipin or anti-ß2GP1 Abs or lupus anticoagulant; n=4/22, 18.2% vs n=8/164, 4.9%; p=0.04), low complement component 3 (C3) and/or low complement component 4 (C4) (n=4/18, 22.2% vs n=8/145, 5.5%; p=0.03), or an ACR/EULAR 2019 SLE classification criteria score ≥7 points (n=14; 35.0% vs n=55, 19.4%; p=0.02), were more likely to progress to SLE than other patients (table 5). In multivariate analysis, APL Abs (OR=8.6, 95% CI 1.6 to 45.5, p=0.01) and low C3 and/or low C4 (OR=4.9, 95% CI 1.1 to 22.8, p=0.04) remained significantly associated with progression to SLE (table 6).

SjD criteria

Eleven (3.4%) MCTD patients progressed to SjD, fulfilling SjD ACR/EULAR 2016 classification criteria. At MCTD diagnosis, 64 patients scored three points on the ACR/EULAR 2016 SjD classification criteria (of these, 51 (15.7%) patients scored points due to the presence of anti-SSA Abs, and 13 (4.0%) because of positive labial salivary gland biopsy results). However, none of the SjD ACR/EULAR classification variables was associated with progression to SjD including labial salivary gland biopsy positivity (n=1/2, 50.0% vs n=12/56, 21.4%; p=0.4), anti-SSA Abs (n=1/11, 9.1% vs n=50/309, 16.2%; p>0.99), abnormal Schirmer’s test (n=0/2, 0% vs n=4/25, 16.0%; p>0.99) and parotid swelling (n=2, 18.2% vs n=10, 3.2%, p=0.06). During follow-up, the occurrence of classification criteria variables associated with progression to SjD were labial salivary gland biopsy positivity (n=8, 72.7% vs n=14, 4.5%; p<0.0001), anti-SSA Abs (n=3, 27.3% vs n=14, 4.5%; p=0.02), abnormal Schirmer’s test (n=3, 27.3% vs n=3, 1.0%; p<0.0001) and parotid swelling (n=2, 18.2% vs n=8, 2.6%; p=0.04) (online supplemental table 9).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the predictive value of dCTD classification, IPAF and VEDOSS criteria in a cohort of patients with MCTD.

We present the first such study in a large cohort of MCTD patients with strict exclusion criteria and more than 8 years of follow-up. The main findings of our study are as follows: (1) Over 90% of MCTD patients fulfilled VEDOSS criteria, but only 15.7% of them progressed to SSc (almost half of the patients, if ANA positivity is not required, with only 19% of them progressing to SSc), (2) MCTD patients with ILD fulfilled IPAF criteria, without differing from other patients in progression to dCTD and (3) MCTD patients with APL Abs and/or low C3 and/or low C4 at MCTD diagnosis were more prone to progress to SLE.

In the French MCTD cohort, we previously demonstrated that MCTD is a distinct entity, with only 25.6% MCTD patients progressing to a dCTD after more than 8 years of median follow-up.22 Although we found that an abnormal pattern on nailfold capillaroscopy and parotid swelling were associated with progression to dCTD in multivariate analysis (OR=2.44, 95% CI 1.11 to 5.58) and (OR=3.86, 95% CI 1.31 to 11.4), these parameters were not associated with specific dCTD (notably SSc or SjD) but could reflect a lack of power.14 Consequently, we sought to evaluate whether baseline dCTD classification criteria and scoring were associated with CTD evolution in MCTD patients. Interestingly, we found that high classification criteria scores were not associated with CTD progression, further highlighting the distinct phenotype of MCTD patients. Along the same lines, we did not find any SjD or SSc classification criteria independently associated with SjD or SSc evolution. However, we did find that an APL Abs and low C3 and/or low C4 were associated with SLE evolution. Antiphospholipid Abs are items of many SLE classification criteria, including the systemic lupus international collaborating clinics (SLICC) and the ACR/EULAR 2019 classification.223,25 However, APL abs can also be found in healthy blood donors and in various pathological conditions, including infections and other CTDs, such as SSc (6.4%–57.5% of patients) and MCTD (13.3%–29.8% of patients).26 Additionally, APL Abs can be transient. It will thus be of interest to evaluate the impact of persistent APL Abs on the classification criteria for SLE and other CTDs.27 28 Low C3 and/or low C4 was also added in the SLICC classification criteria in 2012, increasing sensitivity from the previous 1997 ACR criteria but decreasing specificity.29 The 2019 ACR/EULAR classification criteria do include low C3 and/or low C4, but partly because of expert opinion rather than statistical modelling.2 30 Thus, if low C3 and/or low C4 is frequent in SLE (54%–62% of SLE patients),30 31 it depends on the activity of the disease (less frequently in very early and milder disease) and seems not to be predictive of a progression to SLE. Indeed, in a study of probable lupus who fulfilled only three ACR criteria, only 36% had low C3 and/or low C4 historically, even though 30% transitioned to SLE at follow-up.32 In addition, low C3 and/or low C4 is not specific to SLE and can be found in 14% to 24% of SSc patients,33,35 10% to 25% SjD patients,36 and up to 42% in MCTD.37

CTDs have benefited from recent, modern classification criteria, such as ACR/EULAR 2013 classification criteria for SSc,1 ACR/EULAR 2019 classification criteria for SLE,38 ACR/EULAR 2017 classification criteria for IIM4 or ACR/EULAR 2016 classification criteria for SjD.3 These classifications were designed to define homogenous patient groups or inclusion in research studies and trials,2 39 with high sensitivity and specificity, and are widely used in daily clinical practice to diagnose CTDs.1 38 Over time, these classifications have been reassessed to improve their performance.1 2 However, it was found that they could fail to identify patients with very early disease or those at risk of developing CTD. To address this, ‘pre-CTD’ states had emerged in the literature, such as VEDOSS. Along these lines, the status of MCTD as a dCTD or a pre-CTD has been debated since its first description. Some physicians recognise MCTD as a distinct and well-defined entity, while others consider MCTD to be a pre-CTD.1040,46 This debate has been fuelled by older studies reporting a progression rate to dCTD of 34%–90% in MCTD patients.1112 42 47,49 However, more recent studies have highlighted a progression rate of less than 30%, including a 25% progression rate in the French MCTD cohort.1422 50,52 The French MCTD cohort thus presented a unique opportunity to evaluate the performance of pre-CTD criteria in the context of MCTD.

The VEDOSS project aimed to identify parameters that could lead to the early diagnosis of SSc and, thus, early treatments.53 These criteria rely on the presence of three ‘red flags’ (Raynaud’s phenomenon, puffy fingers, ANA) to prompt patient evaluation, including the search for SSc-specific Abs and microvascular alteration detected by nailfold capillaroscopy. To determine the clinical value of the VEDOSS criteria, a worldwide prospective study was conducted by Bellando-Randone et al.5 In this study, among 1150 patients with Raynaud’s phenomenon who did not fulfil the 1980 ACR54 or the 2013 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for SSc, or any other ACR or EULAR classification criteria for other definite connective tissue diseases (ie, overlap syndromes), 764 (66.4%) fulfilled the VEDOSS criteria (ie, ANA positive, puffy fingers, SSc-specific Abs positive, abnormal nailfold capillaroscopy). Out of these, 254 patients were followed for 5 years, and 133 (52.4%) of them fulfilled the ACR/EULAR 2013 SSc classification criteria. The authors also showed that the risk of fulfilling SSc classification criteria varied according to the type of manifestation associated with Raynaud’s phenomenon and the number of VEDOSS criteria. In our study, MCTD patients fulfilled VEDOSS criteria in 90.4% of the cases. However, only 46 (15.7%) of them progressed to SSc after a median of 8.4 years, which is much lower than the progression observed in the Bellando-Randone et al study. Furthermore, even when we did not take ANA positivity into consideration for the VEDOSS criteria, 163 (50.2%) patients still fulfilled these criteria, with only 31 (19%) of them progressing to SSc. In addition, in our cohort, MCTD-VEDOSS+ patients with more VEDOSS criteria were not more likely to progress to SSc during follow-up. These differences in prognosis suggest that VEDOSS and MCTD patients represent distinct patient populations, and that VEDOSS criteria cannot be applied to MCTD patients. Moreover, in Bellando-Randone et al study, the presence of Abs other than SSc-specific Abs, especially anti-U1RNP Abs, was not assessed. It could suggest that VEDOSS patients with anti-U1RNP Abs do not follow the same trajectory as other VEDOSS patients, and thus, VEDOSS patients should be followed according to their antibody profile rather than relying solely on ANA.55

Another concept is IPAF.8 The goal of the IPAF criteria is to identify patients with ILD who exhibit clinical or serologic autoimmune features but do not meet the current international CTD classification criteria, allowing for early identification and potential initiation of immunosuppressive treatment to stabilise or improve lung function.56 57 Since the IPAF criteria include the presence of anti-U1RNP, it was of interest to evaluate the overlap between MCTD patients with ILD and those meeting IPAF criteria with anti-U1RNP. It is known that 12% to 26% of IPAF patients fulfil at least one CTD classification criterion after 4 to 6 years of follow-up, mostly SSc, SjD or IIM.57,62 In our study, all MCTD patients with ILD fulfilled IPAF criteria, and 15 (39.5%) of them progressed to a dCTD, a rate higher than that reported in IPAF cohorts. Importantly, we found that fulfilling IPAF criteria in MCTD patients was not associated with progression to dCTD. These findings support the original IPAF criteria proposal,8 which excluded MCTD patients from being classified under IPAF.

Our study emphasises classification criteria variables developed during follow-up that are associated with the evolution towards a dCTD. These criteria included SSc classification criteria variables (sclerodactyly, skin thickening extending proximal to the metacarpophalangeal joints, pitting scars, telangiectasia, lung involvement), SLE classification criteria variables (mucocutaneous manifestations, serositis, APL Abs, SLE-specific Abs), and SjD classification criteria variables (positive salivary labial gland biopsy, anti-SSA Abs, abnormal Schirmer’s test) and parotid swelling. These classifications have high sensitivity (91%, 96% and 96% for SSc, SLE and SjD, respectively) and high specificity (92%, 93% and 95% for SSc, SLE and SjD, respectively),1,3 so it is not surprising that these variables are associated with dCTD progression in MCTD. Most of our patients (67% at MCTD diagnosis and 84.9% cumulatively) had arthralgia, which accounts for six points in the ACR/EULAR 2019 SLE classification criteria. Thus, only one other SLE classification criteria variable could be necessary to meet the 10-point threshold to fulfil the ACR/EULAR 2019 SLE criteria. Similarly, most of our patients had ACR/EULAR 2013 SSc classification criteria variable at MCTD diagnosis (Raynaud’s phenomenon in 90.4% of patients, puffy fingers in 43.5%, abnormal capillaroscopy in 47% and an ILD in 11.7%), or cumulatively (Raynaud’s phenomenon in 93.5% of patients, puffy fingers in 58%, abnormal capillaroscopy in 59.4% and an ILD in 30.9%). Therefore, one additional classification criterion variable was sufficient to fulfil the ACR/EULAR 2013 SSc classification criteria. Moreover, very few MCTD patients were included in the derivation and validation cohorts used for development of the SSc and SLE classification criteria. Specifically, SSc derivation and validation cohorts included 9 MCTD patients out of 100 patients and 14 MCTD patients out of 137 patients, respectively.1 Similarly, SLE derivation and validation cohorts included 9 MCTD patients out of 500 and 15 MCTD patients out of 574, respectively.2 No MCTD patients were included in the establishment of SjD classification criteria.3 Thus, one could argue that these classifications were not designed to be applicable to MCTD patients. Overall, our work confirms the sensibility of classification criteria during MCTD patient follow-up. However, it also supports the idea that MCTD is a distinct entity that should be taken into account when developing future CTD classification criteria.

Our work has several limitations. First, it is retrospective, and data collection may be subject to potential biases. Second, the CTD classification criteria are not tailored for diagnosis and thus may not capture the full spectrum of the concerned CTDs, leading to potential misclassification. Third, due to the low number of patients progressing to a dCTD in our cohort, we cannot exclude a lack of statistical power for our analysis. Fourth, in the absence of consensus diagnostic criteria for MCTD, we used four different sets of criteria to include patients with MCTD, but only one set of criteria to classify dCTD. Lastly, we did not evaluate in this study the impact of treatment on dCTD evolution since we previously showed that it did not influence progression to a dCTD.63

Conclusion

This work demonstrates that the presence of SSc, VEDOSS and IPAF classification criteria or variables at MCTD diagnosis is not predictive of progression toward SSc. However, the presence of APL Abs or low C3 and/or low C4 is predictive of an evolution toward SLE. It confirms the sensibility of dCTD classification criteria during follow-up of MCTD patients but suggests excluding MCTD patients from the VEDOSS and IPAF definitions. It also proposes that VEDOSS criteria could benefit from including Ab specificity rather than relying solely on ANA positivity.
