The association between social environment and school bullying among middle school students: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Yuxin Zhu, Xiyu Lin, Chengzi Jiang, Xiaotong Cui, Jiasheng Li, Liping Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescent bullying does not occur in a vacuum but is tied to experiences across peer, family, school, and broader social contexts. Employing an ecological perspective, this study used a mixed-methods approach to examine the associations among ecological environmental factors and bullying victimization (BV), bullying perpetration (BP), and bystander emotional reactions (BR) among Chinese middle school students.MethodsWe adopted a partially sequential mixed-methods design with a complementary purpose. Quantitative data were collected via questionnaires distributed to 411 students (378 valid cases) at a single middle school, with instruments measuring BV, BP, BR, and ecological environmental factors. Given the sparse distribution and strong right skew of BV and BP data, the current study adopted hurdle-type models to distinguish the occurrence and severity of bullying behaviors, with the false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied to coefficient-level statistical tests. BR was analyzed as a continuous outcome variable, and exploratory moderation analyses were conducted to further explore whether family support moderated the associations between negative peer experiences and bullying-related outcomes. Semi-structured interviews with 16 students were analyzed using inductive coding approach informed by grounded theory principles. Both quantitative and qualitative findings were integrated narratively during the interpretive stage of analysis.ResultsThe overall quantitative models predicting BV occurrence, BP occurrence, and BR were statistically significant. For BV and BP, several ecological variables showed significant unadjusted associations with the outcomes, but most of these associations failed to retain significance following FDR correction, and no stable associations were identified in the severity models. For the BR model, gender, parental academic expectations, school environment, and social environment remained significantly associated with BR after FDR correction. An exploratory interaction effect between negative peer experiences and family support was detected only for BP occurrence, which requires cautious explanatory framing. Qualitative findings revealed that students reported bullying-related experiences including peer, family, school, and broader social contexts. Their accounts highlighted peer conflict, social exclusion, behavioral imitation, family relationships, parental expectations, and teacher responses, while also revealing substantial variability in students’ family support and school experiences.ConclusionsBased on data collected from a single school in China, ecological environmental factors demonstrated outcome-specific associations across bullying-related experiences. BR exhibited more consistent ecological correlates than BV or perpetration, suggesting that students’ emotional responses to bullying situations warrant attention alongside their behavioral involvement. The qualitative data further illustrate how peer interactions, family expectations, school climate, and broader social influences intersect in students’ accounts of bullying-related situations. These findings reflect correlational relationships, and further multi-site and longitudinal studies are needed to assess the robustness and generalizability of the present results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28656-8.Keywords: School bullying, Ecological Systems Theory, Peer victimization, Bystanders, Mental health, Chinese adolescents, Injury and violence prevention

Introduction

School bullying occupies a troubling intersection of public health and educational concern. It can detrimentally affect adolescents’ physical safety, psychological well-being, peer relationships, and capacity to engage productively with schooling [1]. Evidence suggests that bullying involvement is prevalent among school-aged children and adolescents, though prevalence rates vary by country, age group, measurement tools, and definitions [2]. Following Olweus’ seminal definition, bullying is characterized by intentional harm-doing, repetitiveness, and a power imbalance [3]. This behavior manifests in physical, verbal, relational, or cyber forms and should be viewed not merely as individual behavior but as a complex phenomenon embedded within the social contexts of adolescents.

Ecological frameworks have proven particularly generative for researchers trying to explore the relationship between bullying and adolescents’ surrounding social environments. In a widely cited review, Hong and Espelage employed an ecological framework to catalogue risk and protective factors at the individual, peer, family, school, and community levels [4]. This approach recognizes that no single tier of influence is sufficient to account for bullying involvement since child and adolescent development contains multiple social contexts, ranging from immediate interpersonal interactions to broader cultural influences. Therefore, using an ecological perspective highlights the connections among peer interactions, family dynamics, school atmosphere, and broader social environments concerning bullying involvement and their reactions. In the present study, Ecological Systems Theory was adopted as a conceptual framework to organize the examination of multiple ecological factors associated with bullying-related outcomes, rather than as a fully tested developmental model.

Empirical research has examined bullying across a range of ecological domains, including peer relationship quality, family functioning, school climates, and broader societal contexts [4–9]. Peer relationship quality has been connected to bullying involvement [5], with negative peer experiences such as rejection and conflict identified as risk factors [8]. Family dynamics, such as family structure, parental education, parenting styles, and family support, are believed to influence adolescents’ susceptibility to bullying by affecting emotional security, behavioral regulation, and the availability of adult guidance [6]. Additionally, the school climate and teacher support have been linked to outcomes related to bullying, indicating that students’ perceptions of safety, fairness, and support within schools may play critical roles in both the prevalence of bullying and the responses to bullying incidents [7, 8]. Research on Chinese adolescents has underscored the significance of class atmosphere, peer relationships, and school climate in comprehending bullying and its associated psychosocial impacts [5, 6, 9].

More recently, researchers have moved beyond identifying isolated correlates of bullying and toward understanding how ecological factors may interact with various bullying-related outcomes across multiple social contexts. Within Chinese adolescent samples specifically, researchers have probed, for instance, the potential mediating role of teacher-student relationship quality in the association between bullying victimization (BV) and psychological symptoms, emphasizing the importance of school relationships in linking bullying experiences with adolescent adjustment [10]. Other studies investigated how factors related to social media are linked to aggressive behaviors and how these associations may vary by gender, which shows that broader social and online contexts are relevant to aggression-related risks in ways that are dependent on the context [11]. However, several gaps persist. Firstly, current findings are often scattered across studies that focus on specific ecological domains, predictors, or outcomes, making it challenging to examine ecological factors across measures of bullying involvement and emotional reactions within the same empirical setting [4–9, 12, 13]. Secondly, although bystander behavior has been integrated into comprehensive models of bullying involvement and explored in Chinese school environments, students’ emotional reactions to bullying situations have garnered comparatively less attention than victimization and perpetration, despite their significance in understanding how students perceive and assess such situations [14, 15]. Thirdly, since bullying-related associations may differ across relational, cultural, and school contexts, context-specific evidence from Chinese secondary school settings remains relatively scarce [12]. These gaps underscore the need for a context-sensitive investigation of multiple ecological domains and bullying-related outcomes within a well-defined school setting.

Therefore, the present study investigated the associations among peer, family, school, and broader social environmental factors, bullying victimization (BV) and bullying perpetration (BP), and bystander emotional reactions (BR) among students at a secondary school in Shantou, China. Employing a mixed-methods design, the quantitative component examined patterns of association across ecological domains, while the qualitative component delved into how students articulated and experienced peer conflict, family support and expectations, teacher interventions, school climate, and broader social influences in bullying-related situations. Within this single-school sample, the study aimed to provide preliminary insights into the associations between students’ ecological environments and various bullying-related outcomes, which may inform future research on the mechanisms that link social contexts with bullying experiences in the Chinese context.

Methods

Ethics statement

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Scientific Research at Shantou University (approval No. STU202503010). Written informed consent was obtained from all participating students and their guardians before data collection commenced. To ensure confidentiality, all qualitative transcripts were anonymized, and identifiable details were removed prior to analysis.

Participants and eligibility criteria

Participants were recruited from a secondary school located in Shantou, China, hereinafter referred to as School P. Eligible students were 12-15 years old, enrolled at School P, able to understand study-related information and communicate normally, and willing to participate voluntarily with written consent provided by both themselves and a parent or guardian. Students were excluded from participation if they failed to comprehend the informed consent process, declined study involvement, or had recently experienced a major life event that rendered their participation inappropriate. For the quantitative component, questionnaires were distributed to four intact classes available for research participation. For the qualitative phase, interview participants were recruited from the pool of eligible students who consented to participate in semi-structured interviews.

Study design

A mixed-methods design integrating a cross-sectional survey and semi-structured interviews was adopted. The quantitative and qualitative components aimed to addressing related yet distinct dimensions of the research question. Data collection and analysis for the respective component were performed independently, and the respective findings were synthesized during interpretation. This study employed a partially sequential mixed-methods design for complementary research purposes. Rather than verifying or testing individual statistical associations, the qualitative component aimed at offering contextual depth and providing more insights into the ecological domains investigated in the survey.

The quantitative component examined the associations among ecological factors at the peer, family, school, and broader social levels and three bullying-related indicators: BV, BP, and BR. Prior to formal data collection, a small-scale pilot test was conducted with 46 Grade 8 students from a separate school with an age range comparable to that of the main study sample. The pilot test was implemented for assessing the clarity of questionnaire items and interview questions. Students’ on-site feedback was utilized to revise item wording, refine the interview guide, and adjust scoring rules. Pilot data were excluded from the final analyses. Following this pilot process, the interview guide underwent iterative refinement to support the in-depth exploration of students’ ecological experiences. Critically, the guide was not derived from specific statistical models, hypothesis tests, or preliminary quantitative results, and no further modifications were made once formal data collection commenced. Consequently, this study does not align with the conventional explanatory sequential mixed-methods design.

The qualitative component focused on the exploration of students’ experiences and interpretations of peer interactions, family expectations, school climate, and social or media influences in everyday life. Transcripts were analyzed through a multi-stage coding procedure informed by grounded theory principles. These procedures were applied to organize and interpret participants’ accounts, with the goal of description and interpretation rather than formal theory-building.

Integration took place at the interpretive stage of the study. Quantitative results and qualitative themes were compared narratively and organized through an ecological lens. Qualitative themes were subsequently organized around distinct ecological levels to examine how factors identified across both strands interrelated within students’ daily school experiences. Placing statistical patterns alongside participants’ firsthand accounts allowed a more comprehensive understanding of the ecological contexts surrounding school bullying.

Quantitative analysis

Participants

The quantitative component was carried out at School Pin October 2024, drawing on a convenience sample of intact classrooms. Four eligible classes participated, with two Grade 7 classes and two Grade 8 classes, and questionnaires were distributed across all four during a single data collection period. A total of 411 completed questionnaires were returned. Because the survey was administered anonymously, no classroom identification information was recorded alongside individual responses. Responses were excluded when key demographic fields were left blank or when bullying-related responses failed to satisfy the prespecified scoring criteria. After this screening, 378 students were retained for the primary quantitative analyses.

It should be noted that analytic sample sizes varied slightly across variables and models, as composite scores were computed only from valid item responses, and each multivariable model relied on complete case analysis for all variables entered into that model. The resulting sample size was considered sufficient for detecting moderate bivariate associations. By contrast, statistical tests involving interaction terms usually demand larger samples. For this reason, all moderation analyses in the present study were treated as exploratory and interpreted conservatively.

Demographic assessment

The demographic information collected in this study included gender, place of residence, only-child status, left-behind status, grade, age, family structure, and parental education and occupation. Left-behind status referred to whether a student was identified as a left-behind child in the context of parental labor migration. All questionnaire items and corresponding response options are presented in Additional file 1: Table 1, and the detailed demographic distribution of the quantitative sample is reported in Additional file 1: Table 2.

Assessing BV, BP and BR

Module B was employed to assess bullying-related behaviors. BV and BP were measured using the Peer Experiences Questionnaire (PEQ) adapted from Giovazolias et al. (2010) [16]. Specifically, the victimization of self (VS) and victimization of others (VO) subscales of the PEQ were utilized to assess victimization and perpetration, respectively [17]. The original English version of the PEQ was translated into Chinese by the authors of this manuscript. A back-translation procedure was subsequently performed by an independent bilingual researcher, who translated the Chinese version back into English. Following the back-translation, we rigorously compared this new English draft against the original English version. Whenever discrepancies emerged, our research team thoroughly discussed and resolved them to guarantee true semantic equivalence. Strictly adhering to established protocols for cross-cultural adaptation, we finalized the Chinese version of the adapted PEQ in the present study. The resulting instrument encompasses 18 items, evenly split so that 9 items capture BV and the other 9 measure BP. All items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 1 to 5 corresponding to the response categories: Never, 1–2 times/month, Several times/month, Once/week, and Several times a week. Raw summed scores for each of the BV and BP subscales range from 9 to 45, with higher scores indicating a higher frequency of perpetration or victimization experiences. For statistical analyses, raw or prorated summed scores were transformed by subtracting the minimum possible score to form a 0–36 scale, on which a score of 0 represented no endorsed bullying-related experiences and higher scores reflected greater frequencies of bullying-related experiences. For descriptive reporting only, self-reported BV and BP experiences were summarized based on an item-level reference criterion: a score of ≥ 2 on at least one item of the corresponding scale, which indicates the occurrence of the relevant experience at least “1–2 times per month.” This criterion was adopted solely to provide an intuitive summary of sample distribution and was not applied to define formal analytical groups. Correlation analyses relied on the continuous 0–36 composite scores. We employed hurdle-type models that distinguished zero transformed scores from non-zero transformed scores for regression analyses for BV and BP and further examined the severity of bullying experiences among students with non-zero scores. BR was treated as a continuous outcome variable. Reliability and sampling adequacy indices for all scales, including Cronbach’s α, McDonald’s ω, KMO, and Bartlett’s test results are summarized in the supplementary materials.

To capture bystander attitudes within a behaviorally specific framework, this study constructed a scenario-based BR instrument. This measure was derived from the victimization scenarios outlined in the PEQ, guided by relevant conceptual models. We rephrased the original items to explicitly target participants’ emotional reactions. As an illustration, the BV item “A student teases another student in a mean way” was revised to “Seeing this situation makes me feel uncomfortable.” Participants rated their emotional reactions, including discomfort, anger, and sadness, using a 5-point Likert scale. Raw summed BR scores ranged from 9 to 45. To retain the consistent transformed score range applied to BV and BP, BR scores were transformed by subtracting the minimum possible score, resulting in a scale range of 0 to 36, with higher scores indicating stronger negative emotional reactions to scenario-based bullying situations. BR was treated as a continuous outcome variable in the main analyses. Internal consistency indices for the BR scale were high in the current sample; however, given the scenario-based design of the measure and potential item content overlap, these indices were interpreted with caution. Accordingly, BR-related findings should be interpreted as students’ negative emotional reactions to witnessed bullying situations, rather than as evidence supporting a fully validated bystander behavior construct. Detailed reliability and sampling adequacy indices are presented in the supplementary materials.

Assessing other modules

The peer, family, school, and broader social environment modules were developed using items adapted from several well-established instruments for measuring social support and environmental influences. All items were reviewed and modified to ensure their relevance to the present study. Consistent with Section B, items in Modules C, D, E, and F were also rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 1 to 5 corresponding to the response categories: Completely Disagree, Somewhat Disagree, Neutral/Uncertain, Somewhat Agree, and Completely Agree. Item scoring directions were verified based on item content, and seven items were reverse-scored before psychometric analysis and composite score calculation: D3, D7, D10, D11, D12, and D13 from the Family Environment Module, and E6 from the School Environment Module.

The Student peer relationship module (C) is adapted downward from the original questionnaire developed by Asher (1984) [18].

For the Family environment module (D), we drew upon the theoretical dimensions of the Family Environment Scale established by Moos (1987) [19].

The School environment module (E) is measured using core items selected from the Delaware School Climate Survey-Student (DSCS-S) [20] to ensure feasibility within a school-based data collection setting.

The Society environment module (F) was constructed as an exploratory module to capture broader community and online contextual factors that were insufficiently covered by the adapted instruments. Reliability and sampling adequacy indices, including Cronbach’s α, McDonald’s ω, KMO, and Bartlett’s test results, are summarized in the supplementary materials.

Before conducting factor analyses, we carefully reviewed item wording to establish appropriate scoring directions. We reverse-coded several items so that higher scores consistently reflected higher levels of the target construct. Specifically, within the Family environment module, reverse-scored items included those measuring limited family communication, lack of closeness, family interference, family conflict, and restrictive academic expectations (items D3, D7, D10, D11, D12, and D13). In the School environment module, item E6 (assessing fear of bullying) was reverse-scored while the remaining items in this module primarily reflected positive school rules, campus safety, peer climate, and teacher support.

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was then carried out using principal axis factoring with oblique rotation (oblimin), as correlations among the peer, family, school, and broader social environmental constructs were expected. Decisions regarding factor retention were guided by parallel analysis, which is a more rigorous criterion than the conventional eigenvalue-greater-than-one rule. Item retention was evaluated based on factor loadings, cross-loadings, and the consistency of loading directions with the intended construct following reverse-coding.

The results of parallel analysis and EFA suggested that most scales were adequately represented by a stable single-factor structure. The Family environment module differed from this pattern and showed evidence of a two-factor solution, which was labelled Family support and Family restriction. Items D8 and E6 were removed because, after reverse coding, their loading directions did not align with the intended construct. Item D11 was excluded because it showed substantial cross-loadings on both family factors. Although item D13 did not load distinctly on either of the two primary family factors, it retained theoretical relevance to parental academic expectations and was preserved as a single-item indicator to be analyzed independently. The finalized scale structure was used to generate the variables for correlation, regression, and moderation analyses.

To further verify the internal consistency and preliminary construct validity of the retained scales, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), composite reliability (CR), average variance extracted (AVE), Cronbach’s α, and McDonald’s ω were further calculated and reported in the supplementary materials. Because the process of item refinement and psychometric assessment was conducted within a single sample, these results should be viewed as preliminary and require validation in independent samples.

Data collection and management

The survey was administered by trained research personnel, and all data were completed independently by two researchers to reduce input errors. Descriptive analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Psychometric testing and model-based analyses were conducted in Python 3.12.7 (Anaconda distribution; Anaconda, Inc., Austin, TX, USA), primarily using the packages including pandas 2.2.2, NumPy 1.26.4, SciPy 1.13.1, statsmodels 0.14.2, semopy 2.3.11, matplotlib 3.9.2, and factor_analyzer. The main procedures are described below.

Missing data and score construction

Each of the BV, BP, and BR subscales comprised nine items. A subscale score was calculated when at least eight items had been validly completed. If only one item was missing, the mean of the completed items was prorated to estimate the total nine-item score. When two or more items were missing, the corresponding subscale score was treated as missing and excluded from analyses involving that outcome. Raw or prorated nine-item totals were transformed by subtracting the theoretical minimum value of 9, yielding final scores ranging from 0 to 36. For modules C, D, E, and F, composite scores were calculated as the mean of valid responses across retained items, with missing items excluded from the denominator. Item scoring directions were checked before score construction. Within the Family environment module, items D3, D7, D10, D11, D12, and D13 were reverse-scored. For the School environment module, item E6 was reverse-scored. On the basis of parallel analysis, EFA, and item content evaluation, items D8 and D11 were ultimately removed from the Family Environment module, whereas item D13 was retained as a single-item indicator of lower parental academic expectations after reverse scoring. The final family-related composites were Family support (D1, D2, D4, D5, D6, D9, and D12) and Lower family restriction (reverse-scored D3, D7, and D10), with higher scores indicating less perceived family restriction. Item E6 was excluded from the School environment module since its factor loading direction was not consistent with the validated positive school environment construct. Cronbach’s α, McDonald’s ω, CFA, CR, and AVE were further examined and reported in the supplementary materials.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were first calculated for all bullying-related outcomes and environmental variables, including means, standard deviations, ranges, skewness, and kurtosis. For BV and BP, descriptive proportions were summarized using the item-level reference criterion described above. In subsequent inferential analyses, Spearman correlation analyses were conducted using the continuous 0–36 composite scores. Regression analyses for BV and BP were based on hurdle-type models, which partitioned zero and non-zero transformed scores for the occurrence component and modeled non-zero scores as continuous severity outcomes. BR, by contrast, was treated as a continuous outcome throughout.

Given that several variables, especially BV and BP, showed non-normal and highly right-skewed distributions, Spearman’s rank-order correlation coefficients were employed to assess bivariate associations among bullying-related outcomes, demographic variables, and ecological environmental factors.

Before fitting the regression models, the distributions of dependent variables and regression assumptions were evaluated using residual plots, Q-Q plots, heteroscedasticity tests, and influence diagnostics including leverage values and Cook’s distance. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were used to assess multicollinearity. Given the highly skewed and zero-inflated distributions of BV and BP, these two outcomes were analyzed with a two-step hurdle-type strategy. First, logistic regression models were applied to distinguish students who reported no bullying-related experience from those who reported at least one such experience. Second, among students with non-zero scores, regression models with heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors were fitted to examine variations in bullying severity. For BR, which displayed a less sparse distribution, linear regression with heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors was applied.

Model results were presented in terms of regression coefficients, standard errors, and 95% confidence intervals, together with model-level fit indices where appropriate, including the adjusted R2 for linear models and pseudo-R2 for logistic models. To reduce the risk of false-positive findings from multiple testing, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was implemented using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure at the level of coefficient-specific tests within each model. Both unadjusted and FDR-adjusted p values were taken into account when interpreting the results.

Moderation analyses were treated as exploratory since interaction effects generally require larger samples, and the present data came from a single school. All continuous predictors were mean-centered before interaction terms were created. Any interaction findings were interpreted conservatively and were not used to support firm mechanistic or causal claims.

Qualitative analysis

Participants

Sixteen students from School P took part in the semi-structured interviews. Quota sampling was used to ensure balance across gender and grade. The final interview sample included eight Grade 7 students (four boys and four girls) and eight Grade 8 students (four boys and four girls). Participants were drawn from eligible survey respondents who volunteered for the qualitative phase and for whom valid student and parental consent had been obtained. Selection followed the predefined grade-by-gender quota and was not based on bullying scores, teacher nominations, or other outcome-related characteristics. The sample size was determined in light of the exploratory purpose of the study and the need to maintain balanced representation across the two grade levels and genders. Subsequent data analysis and full-dataset coding showed that ecological domains of interest were adequately covered and a broad range of student experiences had been captured. Each interview lasted approximately 15–20 min. Since the interview guide was focused and structured, students were able to provide information relevant to the research aims within a relatively brief session. All interviews were conducted by trained researchers who had no teaching, supervisory, or evaluative role in relation to the participants, thereby reducing potential power-related influences.

Interview and data collection

After the completion of the quantitative survey, the research team reviewed the ecological domains represented in the questionnaire, the preliminary descriptive findings, and the pilot interview feedback. On that basis, the interview guide was revised iteratively so that students’ experiences of peer interactions, family expectations, school climate, and social or media influences could be explored in greater depth. No further revisions were introduced once formal data collection had begun.

All interviews took place in a quiet and private room on school grounds, with only the interviewer and the participant present. Audio recordings were obtained for every session, and field notes were taken at the same time. All interviews were transcribed verbatim, and two researchers independently checked each transcript against the original recording to ensure completeness and accuracy. Every stage of the data collection process was implemented in compliance with approved ethical requirements.

Qualitative analysis

Interview transcripts were analyzed inductively through a multi-stage coding approach informed by principles commonly associated with grounded theory. The goal of this analysis was to organize and interpret students’ accounts of bullying-related experiences. During the initial coding stage, transcripts were read line by line, and concepts were derived directly from participants’ statements. In the subsequent category-development stage, these concepts were grouped into higher-order categories based on semantic similarity and contextual connections. When a concept could reasonably belong to more than one category, the research team discussed its primary placement while retaining links to other relevant categories in order to preserve the complexity of students’ experiences. In the final stage, higher-order categories were compared and integrated into broader qualitative themes that reflected recurring patterns in how students understood and experienced bullying across different domains of daily life.

Coding decisions were refined through multiple rounds of team discussions, which helped clarify category boundaries and reduce individual interpretive bias. Attention was also given to within-category variation and divergent cases that diverged from dominant patterns, so that the analysis would better reflect the diversity and complexity of participants’ experiences. Representative quotations were selected to illustrate the connections among original participant statements, emergent concepts, and the categories developed during analysis. Throughout the process, the team engaged in ongoing reflection on assumptions and interpretive choices that could influence data interpretation. Coding and data management were completed in NVivo 15.0, and all transcripts were revisited in a final review to ensure full thematic coverage across the dataset. As the qualitative sample came from a single school and formal theoretical sampling was not employed, the analysis aimed for adequate thematic coverage within the existing dataset rather than full theoretical saturation.

Trustworthiness of qualitative findings

Several steps were taken throughout the analytical process to strengthen the trustworthiness of the qualitative findings. First, coding and category development were discussed among multiple members of the research team to improve consistency and reduce individual bias. Second, convergent, divergent, and deviant cases were examined systematically to preserve within-category variation and avoid overgeneralizing the results. Third, coding and theme development were repeatedly checked against the original transcripts so that the analysis remained grounded in students’ own accounts. Finally, the research team utilized ongoing discussions about assumptions and potential influences on data interpretation throughout the study.

Coding framework

The coding process generated a set of descriptive codes, higher-level categories, and overarching qualitative themes that informed the interpretation of students’ bullying-related experiences. A detailed coding framework demonstrating the progression from original statements to codes and categories is provided in Additional file 2.

Mixed-methods integration

Integration was conducted at the interpretation stage. Qualitative themes were narratively compared with quantitative findings and then mapped onto distinct ecological levels. In this way, the study examined how factors identified across the two analytical strands were distributed across, and interconnected within, their everyday school experiences. By placing statistical patterns alongside students’ own accounts and contextual descriptions, the qualitative findings added interpretive depth to the associations identified in the quantitative analysis. The purpose of this integration was to enrich and extend interpretation, rather than to establish causal mechanisms or to directly validate statistical results.

Results

Quantitative results

Participant characteristics and descriptive statistics

A total of 411 questionnaires were collected in this study. After excluding cases with missing key demographic information and participants whose Module B responses did not satisfy the prespecified scoring criteria, 378 students were retained for the quantitative analyses. The final analytical sample consisted of 183 boys (48.41%) and 195 girls (51.59%), including 206 Grade 7 students (54.50%) and 172 Grade 8 students (45.50%). Most participants were rural residents, non-only children, non-left-behind children, and came from intact families. The full questionnaire is presented in Additional file 1: Table 1, and detailed demographic distribution is reported in Additional file 1: Table 2. To give a more intuitive overview of the sample distribution, self-reported BV and BP experiences were summarized using an item-level reference criterion: a score of ≥ 2 on at least one item of the corresponding scale, which corresponded to reporting the experience at least “1–2 times per month”. Based on this descriptive rule, 26.72% (n = 101) of students reported experiencing at least one form of BV, 8.73% (n = 33) reported at least one form of BP, and 5.56% (n = 21) reported both victimization and perpetration behaviors. This criterion was adopted solely for describing sample distribution and was not treated as a formal classification of bullying roles. Correlation analyses were performed using continuous composite scores ranging from 0 to 36. In the regression analyses, BV and BP were modeled using hurdle-type approaches that separated zero from non-zero transformed scores and then examined severity among non-zero cases. BR was analyzed as a continuous outcome variable.

Descriptive statistics for the principal study variables are shown in Table 1. BV, BP, and BR (negative emotional reactions to witnessing bullying) were all transformed to a 0–36 scale, while the ecological environment variables were calculated as the mean of validly answered items. The mean score for BP was low, and the distribution was highly right-skewed and leptokurtic. These results indicated that most students reported little or no perpetration and only a small number reported relatively frequent involvement. BV showed a similarly low and highly skewed distribution, but its mean score and the descriptively summarized proportion based on item-level reference criteria exceeded those of perpetration, suggesting that victimization was relatively more common than bullying others in this sample. By contrast, BR had a higher mean value and a more dispersed distribution, indicating greater variability in how strongly students reacted emotionally when witnessing bullying. Among the ecological variables, the supportive environment dimension exhibited a moderately high level, whereas the mean score for peer negative experiences was relatively low. These distributional characteristics provide an informative foundation for subsequent correlation and multivariate model analyses.

Preliminary psychometric evaluation and scale construction

Prior to conducting correlation and regression analyses, the present study examined the psychometric properties of each multi-item scale. The results demonstrated that all KMO values fell within the acceptable range, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity reached statistical significance for each scale, supporting the feasibility of subsequent factor analysis. Findings from the parallel analysis and principal axis factoring indicated that most scales yielded a single-factor structure, with the family environment module as the sole exception, which was optimally characterized by a two-factor structure. Details about KMO test results, Bartlett’s test outcomes, reliability estimates, and factor retention results are shown in Additional file 1: Table 3.

The exploratory factor analysis for the family environment module, conducted using principal axis factoring with an oblique rotation, suggested a two-factor structure. One factor reflected supportive family experiences and was labeled Family support; the other captured a relatively less restrictive and less intrusive family climate and was labeled Lower family restriction. Family support included items D1, D2, D4, D5, D6, D9, and D12, whereas Lower family restriction was formed by the reverse-scored items D3, D7, and D10, with higher scores indicating more open family communication and less perceived family restriction or interference. Item D8 was excluded due to its empirical loading direction contradicted the theoretical construct, while D11 was discarded after displaying substantial cross-loading across the two family factors. Item D13 failed to load clearly onto either of the two primary family factors, yet its content addresses parental academic expectations. Rather than discarding it, we reverse-scored D13 and kept it as an independent, single-item measure (Lower parental academic expectations). Treated this way, higher values on this indicator denote less stringent or more relaxed academic demands from parents. Complete details regarding item loadings and retention decisions for the family environment module are shown in Additional file 1: Table 4.

Regarding the School environment module, item E6 was ultimately removed since its loading direction was inconsistent with the validated positive school environment construct. The remaining items were then combined to form a single School environment composite score. Following these psychometric evaluation procedures described above, the following ecological environment variables were incorporated into subsequent quantitative analyses: Peer positive experience, Peer negative experience, Family support, Lower family restriction, Lower parental academic expectations, School environment, and Social environment.

CFA-based reliability and validity indices are provided in Additional file 1: Table 5. Most retained multi-item scales exhibited acceptable CR and AVE values. The Lower family restriction dimension yielded less favorable CFA-based indices relative to other retained scales, with CR exceeding 0.60 but an AVE value below 0.50. According to Fornell and Larcker’s criterion, an AVE value below 0.50 does not necessarily invalidate a construct, so long as the construct maintains acceptable CR and theoretical relevance [21]. Accordingly, Lower family restriction was retained as an exploratory dimension, given that it was derived from EFA results and comprised three theoretically coherent, reverse-scored items that reflect more open family communication and reduced family restriction and interference. All findings associated with this dimension were interpreted with appropriate caution.

Qualitative results

Theme 1: Peer negative experiences as a pathway to bullying involvement

Rather than viewing bullying as an isolated event, students consistently portrayed it as a process that developed through everyday peer interactions. Their narratives revealed how repeated interpersonal experiences within peer groups gradually shaped involvement in bullying-related situations. These accounts illustrate how negative peer experiences shape students’ everyday social interactions and provide context for understanding the patterns observed in the quantitative data. Students emphasized that bullying rarely emerged from a single event. Repeated interpersonal conflicts, social exclusion, and peer influence seemed to create the conditions in which bullying-related involvement gradually took shape.

Many participants spoke first about conflict. Such conflict was not limited to physical confrontation, and it also included verbal disagreements, minor play-related tensions, and disagreements over schoolwork or opinions. One student explained that “Sometimes we play around and there may be some conflicts.” Another participant recalled that disagreements could arise during academic discussions: “We had different opinions about a question and started arguing. Later, we asked the teacher for help, and after getting the answer, the conflict was resolved.” In some cases, conflicts were more persistent and affected peer relationships. As one student stated, “Two classmates who used to be very close became unfriendly after something happened. They no longer talked much, and when they had to communicate, they spoke to each other in an unpleasant tone”.

Beyond open conflict, students also highlighted the role of social exclusion and peer conformity in bullying processes. Their descriptions suggest that rejection often spread through the peer group rather than remaining a one-to-one issue. One student explained, “There was a student in our group whom many people disliked because of some things he did in primary school, so many classmates started excluding him.” What begins as a negative view held by a few classmates may therefore harden into a shared group position, making exclusion seem normal or justified.

In addition, some students also noticed how aggressive behavior could be copied. One participant noted that “After an incident happened, some students would deliberately bump into him during breaks and laugh at him.” This kind of imitation matters because it shows that bullying-related behavior may normalize aggression and encourage similar actions among other students.

At the same time, participants were equally articulate about the protective side of peer relationships. Mutual assistance, emotional support, and collaborative conflict resolution were also emphasized. For example, one participant stated, “If my friend is misunderstood by others, we will help him communicate with them.” Another explained, “If I am misunderstood, my friends will first try to understand the situation and then help me resolve the conflict.” Emotional support surfaced repeatedly as well, with one student noting that “My friends comfort me when I do poorly on an exam.”

Taken together, the picture that emerged was one in which peer relationships function simultaneously as sources of risk and protection. Negative peer interactions, exclusion, and behavioral imitation are described as processes that can contribute to conflict escalation and bullying involvement. Supportive peer relationships by contrast, offer both emotional coping and practical conflict-resolution resources.

Theme 2: Family support and behavioral regulation as protective resources

Family emerged across interviews as a domain of considerable importance to how students navigated peer difficulties. These experiences highlight how family support influences students’ coping with peer conflicts and complements patterns observed in the quantitative analyses. Interview data further illustrates how family support functions as a protective resource through emotional support, positive family relationships, and behavioral guidance.

One student simply stated that “my parents fully support me.” Parental attentiveness came up frequently, and it went beyond monitoring academic performance. One student offered a quiet but telling observation: “My parents once saw me walking home with a friend. When I did not walk home with that friend the next time, they asked why I was no longer walking with him.”

Positive family relationships were also commonly mentioned. Students described harmonious family environments in ways that suggested a baseline sense of security. One participant stated that “I have a good relationship with my parents,” while another reported that “I do not feel uncomfortable or unwanted at home.” Such accounts suggest that supportive family environments provide adolescents with a sense of security and belonging that may help them handle interpersonal stress at school.

Not every participant described such an environment. Some noted stricter parenting styles or limited parental involvement in their daily peer interactions. These accounts indicate that family influences vary across households. Even so, the accounts of more engaged, supportive parenting outnumbered those of distance or restriction.

Beyond emotional support, participants emphasized the role of parental guidance in shaping adolescents’ appropriate social behaviors. One student explained, “My parents teach me to identify who is responsible when a conflict occurs. If I am wrong, I should apologize first. If the other person is wrong, I can seek help from teachers or parents.” Another participant reported that “My parents teach me to be open-minded, not overly sensitive, to interact well with classmates, and to control my temper.”

The more nuanced accounts involved parents helping students develop perspective-taking and independent problem-solving. As one student stated, “My parents listen to my perspective and try to understand what happened. They teach me to understand my classmates and solve problems on my own rather than interfering too much.”

Synthesizing these qualitative narratives, family support manifest via both emotional and behavioral pathways. Supportive family environments supply adolescents with emotional security, conflict-resolution guidance, and interpersonal skill cultivation, enabling them to cope with peer difficulties and respond to interpersonal conflict in more constructive ways.

Theme 3: School climate and teacher intervention in shaping bystander responses

All interviewees underscored that teachers and the broader school environment jointly govern adolescents’ cognitive and behavioral reactions to bullying situations. Students described teachers as reliable adults capable of detecting conflicts, delivering timely intervention, and providing consistent emotional comfort. Such lived experiences imply that positive teacher-student rapport paired with a healthy campus ecosystem jointly shape how students seek support, report bullying, and respond to peer conflict.

The majority of interviewees demonstrated high levels of reliance on teachers to address bullying situations. One participant stated, “If bullying really happened, I think teachers are trustworthy.” Another reported that “if I or one of my classmates were bullied, I would first talk to a teacher.” These responses verify that school instructors are perceived as core sources of support and protection.

Participants further recounted educators’ proactive efforts to identify and address conflicts among students within daily campus life. One student explained that “when conflicts occur between classmates, teachers step in and intervene.” Another stated that “if bullying occurred in our school, teachers would be able to notice it.” Importantly, students highlighted that teacher involvement often continues after the immediate incident. As one participant noted, “After dealing with a bullying incident, teachers continue to check whether the victim has experienced bullying again.”

A minority of students also pointed out that teacher responses vary across situations. Severe peer friction would trigger thorough follow-up and bilateral mediation, whereas minor conflicts only received brief reminders or informal conversations. Such differentiated feedback indicates that the effectiveness of teacher intervention may depend on individual circumstances and available resources.

Beyond targeted intervention, participants also documented teachers’ dual emotional and academic scaffolding functions. One student shared anxiety over mathematics examinations and anticipated disciplinary criticism from the teacher, yet received consistent comfort and encouragement instead, which made the student “feel cared for.” Such experiences contribute to students’ perceptions of teachers as supportive adults who are concerned about their psychological and academic well-being.

Students further stressed the pivotal function of school-level policies and preventive measures. Participants reported that schools regularly host anti-bullying lectures and thematic activities, maintain clear anti-bullying policies, and encourage students to report bullying incidents. Two student quotations capture institutional practice: “the school has clear anti-bullying policies, and bullying incidents are handled with clear consequences.” Another noted that “the school encourages students to report bullying behavior and tell teachers or parents immediately.”

Apart from written institutional regulations, participants frequently highlighted the formative value of a positive school climate. One student noted that “the school has created a positive and friendly learning atmosphere,” while another stated that “the school has done a good job of creating a friendly environment.” Extracurricular clubs and group activities were also cited as key platforms to foster constructive peer communication and mitigate hostile interpersonal dynamics.

Collectively, participants considered teacher support, effective intervention, and a positive school climate as key school-level resources that influence their help-seeking willingness, bullying disclosure behavior, and adaptive coping strategies upon witnessing peer victimization.

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings

The integration of quantitative and qualitative findings yields a more holistic understanding of the factors associated with school bullying involvement. While the quantitative analyses revealed distinct association patterns across BV, BP, and bystander emotional responses, the qualitative thematic coding supplied situated, experiential context regarding adolescents’ daily encounters with peer, familial and campus ecological systems.

A high degree of convergence was observed in the peer domain. Both quantitative and qualitative findings confirmed the central role of peer relationships in shaping bullying involvement. Quantitative models identify adverse peer interactions as one of the strongest predictors of BV and perpetration. Correspondingly, interview respondents recounted how repeated peer conflicts, social exclusion, group conformity, and behavioral imitation could gradually escalate into formal bullying incidents. Interviewees stressed that peer victimization rarely stems from one-off disputes but accumulates through prolonged relational tension. Meanwhile, supportive peer relationships characterized by emotional support, mutual emotional comfort and collaborative dispute resolution appeared to buffer against bullying involvement. Taken together, these findings suggest that peer relationships function as a proximal ecological context in which both risk and protective processes operate.

Parallel consistent results also emerge within the familial ecological layer. Statistical and qualitative analyses uniformly validate the protective role of family support in reducing bullying involvement. As for the quantitative findings, family support was associated with lower levels of bullying involvement. Interview data further elaborated that supportive families provided emotional security, guidance in conflict resolution, and opportunities to develop interpersonal skills. Adolescents repeatedly recounted how parents assist them with affective regulation, perspective-taking and adaptive dispute management. Such lived narratives help explain how family support may reduce vulnerability to bullying involvement by strengthening emotional and behavioral coping resources.

Consistently, statistical correlation results and student interview narratives jointly underscored the significance of teacher support and positive school environments in reducing bullying involvement and promoting bystander responses. Participants described teachers as reliable adult mediators capable of proactively detecting relational friction, delivering targeted intervention, and offering sustained follow-up care for victimized adolescents. Students additionally acknowledged the protective value of anti-bullying policies, preventive education, reporting mechanisms, and positive school climate, which collectively cultivate a sense of safety and accountability among students. These findings suggest that supportive school environments may encourage students to seek help, report bullying incidents, and engage in active defending behaviors.

The integration process also yielded findings that extended beyond direct convergence. Statistical models reveal stringent parental academic expectations correlate with lower levels of bullying involvement, as well as lower levels of active bystander responses. While this pattern was less explicitly evident in student interview content, several participants recounted parents’ heavy focus on academic achievement and educational performance. A reasonable interpretive inference holds that rigorous academic standards cultivate adolescent self-regulation and deter aggressive peer conduct; concurrently, overemphasis on academic performance may dissuade teenagers from intervening in peer disputes, even when victimized peers require assistance. This finding highlights that certain family influences may carry mixed protective and constraining influences on bullying-related behaviors.

Partial inconsistent results exist between the statistical dataset and interview thematic data regarding macrosystem social factors. While broader social and media-related influencesfailed to register as prominent predictors in the quantitative analyses, participants frequently confirmed that digital content, social media exchanges and public conversations about bullying shaped their understandings of bullying and appropriate responses. This suggests that macrosystem influences operate more strongly through the formation of attitudes, norms, and interpretations than through direct behavioral involvement.

The qualitative findings not only corroborated several quantitative associations but also provided insights into the social processes through which these ecological factors influence bullying involvement and bystander behavior. Collectively, the integrated findings support an ecological interpretation of school bullying. Peer relationships represent the most immediate context shaping bullying involvement, whereas family support, teacher support, and school climate serve as key protective resources. The integration of quantitative and qualitative evidence indicates that bullying behaviors arise from the interactions of multiple ecological systems, rather than being attributable to any single factor (Table 7).

Discussion

Main findings and ecological interpretation

Based on data from one secondary school in Shantou, China, this mixed-methods study examined the associations among peer, family, school, and broader social environmental factors and three bullying-related outcomes: BV, BP, and BR. Overall, the three outcomes exhibited distinct association patterns. Self-reported scores of BV and perpetration were generally low and highly right-skewed, indicating that most students in this sample had limited involvement in bullying behaviors as measured in this study; this distribution aligns with the occurrence pattern of bullying experiences documented in previous studies of Chinese adolescent samples [22]. In contrast, scores of BR presented a wider distribution, reflecting substantial individual differences in students’ negative emotional responses to scenario-based bullying situations.

A key implication of this distinct pattern is that BV, BP, and BR should not be regarded as interchangeable bullying-related outcomes. While several ecological environmental variables were correlated with BV and perpetration in bivariate analyses, most of these associations failed to remain significant after adjusting for other variables and conducting FDR correction. Accordingly, the findings regarding BV and perpetration should be interpreted with caution. In comparison, BR demonstrated relatively consistent associations with school environment, social environment, gender, and parental academic expectations. This pattern is broadly consistent with ecological perspectives, which propose that bullying-related experiences are correlated with peer, family, teacher, school, and broader sociocultural factors [4, 23].

In Chinese middle school contexts, students spend the majority of their school time in relatively stable class groups with frequent peer interactions. As such, class atmosphere, teacher-student relationships, peer relationships, and school rules are closely relevant to how students perceive, report, and emotionally respond to bullying situations [15, 24]. Recent studies have also indicated that, within the Chinese educational context, a competitive school climate is associated with school bullying behaviors and students’ anti-bullying attitudes [12, 25]. Collectively, these findings indicate that peer, family, and school environments should be regarded not as isolated background variables, but as interrelated constituents of their everyday school experiences. This mechanism may account for why certain ecological variables exhibited significant associations in bivariate analyses yet failed to surface as robust independent predictors in multivariable models.

This interpretation aligns with the ecological perspective that bullying behaviors are situated within intersecting peer, family, school, and broader social contexts [4, 26]. Existing literature also demonstrates that adverse peer relationships and peer conflict are associated with BV and perpetration [5], whereas peer relationships and school climate facilitate adolescents’ psychological adaptation [8]. Family structure, parental educational attainment, and family support additionally shape bullying risk via influencing factors such as emotional security, behavioral regulation, and access to adult social support [6]. Hence, elucidating the mechanisms underlying the associations between ecological factors and bullying involvement constitutes a pivotal avenue for future bullying research.

The present findings advance this issue in two respects. First, this study employs a mixed-methods design to supplement quantitative association analyses with contextual insights drawn from students’ everyday school lives. Second, this study investigates BR alongside BV and perpetration to examine whether distinct bullying-related outcomes display divergent ecological association patterns. The results reveal that, following adjustments in hurdle-type multivariable models and FDR correction, many associations between individual ecological environmental variables and BV or perpetration do not remain consistently significant. Rather than interpreting this finding as evidence that ecological factors are unimportant, a more appropriate interpretation is that the relationships between ecological environmental factors and bullying involvement may not manifest as a set of direct effects that can be independently compared with one another. Future research may therefore benefit from larger and more diverse samples, more refined measures of peer, family, school, and broader social or online environments, and longitudinal or multilevel designs to further examine the potential mechanisms underlying these relationships.

BR as a distinct outcome

Existing research on bystanders has predominantly focused on bystander roles, helping or defending behaviors, and students’ judgments and behavioral responses in bullying situations [14, 15]. Although several studies have examined emotional or psychological responses associated with bystander experiences, students’ negative emotional reactions to scenario-based bullying situations have received relatively limited attention as a distinct outcome [27, 28]. BR do not necessarily indicate whether students are directly involved in bullying incidents; instead, they capture students’ emotional evaluations of specific bullying situations, that is, how students perceive such situations at the emotional and affective level. Their association patterns may therefore differ from those of actual bullying involvement, yet this does not diminish the research value of this outcome. On the contrary, it may provide insights into students’ emotional sensitivity, initial appraisal, and potential bystander responses in bullying situations, which constitute critical processes for future research on the maintenance, escalation, and interruption of bullying incidents. After adjusting for other ecological variables, school environment and social environment were associated with higher BR scores. Previous studies have indicated that school climate is correlated with BV and other bullying-related outcomes [7], while school climate and peer relationships are also associated with adolescents’ psychological adjustment [8]. Based on these findings, a tentative and prudent interpretation is that school and social environments may shape how students recognize, evaluate, and emotionally respond to bullying situations; however, the specific mechanisms underlying these associations remain to be further investigated.

Contextualizing family-related and cross-level ecological findings

Family-related findings and exploratory interaction effects warrant circumspect interpretation when situated within a broader ecological context. The specific rationale is presented below. As ecological perspectives underscore, individual experiences are nested within interrelated systems comprising peers, families, schools, and broader social environments [4, 26]. First, the association between parental academic expectations and BR ought not to be construed as evidence that parental academic expectations directly promote or inhibit BR. In the present study, parental academic expectations were assessed through a reverse-scored single item with elevated scores denoting lower or less rigorous parental academic expectations. Consequently, this variable may capture not a unitary dimension of family education but may instead be entangled with multiple processes, including family rules, responsibility norms, students’ self-expectations, academic pressure, and parent-child communication. Existing studies have also indicated that parental stress, family resilience, parenting styles, social support, and emotion regulation among school-aged populations are intimately linked to bullying involvement and relevant psychological processes [29–31]. Hence, the link between more stringent parental academic expectations and stronger negative BR should be regarded as a family-related indicator meriting further investigation, rather than as a conclusive causal mechanism.

Similarly, in the exploratory moderation analysis, the Peer negative experience × Family support interaction remained significant only in the BP occurrence model and did not emerge consistently in the BV or BR models. Accordingly, this finding does not support the simplistic interpretation that family support buffers the impacts of negative peer experiences, nor can it be taken as evidence that family support deterministically amplifies or attenuates the effects of negative peer experiences. A more prudent interpretation is that this interaction may reflect complex relationships among students’ peer experiences, family support, problem exposure, and the recognition of bullying-related events. For instance, higher family support may be accompanied by greater family attention and an increased likelihood of problem recognition, or it may indicate stronger family responsiveness after students encounter peer conflict or bullying-related situations. Given the single-school cross-sectional design of this study and the limited number of non-zero BP cases, the detected interaction effect may also be influenced by sample size and instability in model estimation. This finding should thus be treated as exploratory.

The qualitative data further suggest that weak or unstable independent associations of individual ecological variables in the models do not necessarily indicate that these ecological environments are trivial. The interview data demonstrate that students’ perceptions and descriptions of bullying-related situations are embedded within daily peer interactions, family relationships, teacher interventions, school climate, and online and broader social environments. Noticeable differences were also observed within the same ecological domain. For instance, within a single classroom, some students reported that teachers can identify and resolve interpersonal conflicts in a timely manner, while other students perceived that teachers only provide superficial reminders. Such inconsistent perceptions indicate large individual discrepancies in how adolescents judge campus and familial environmental quality. Prior empirical work has also established that middle school students’ subjective perceptions of campus atmosphere, peer victimization, social backing and coping tactics are critical to understanding school bullying contexts [32]. From a theoretical perspective, students’ personal experiences, problem recognition styles, and contextual interpretations jointly shape how multi-layered ecological systems exert real effects. Accordingly, thematic qualitative outputs are not meant to verify or interpret specific statistical coefficients. Instead, they contextualize bullying incidents within adolescents’ bullying-related scenarios and clarify that the ecological environments encountered by students cannot be treated as isolated external variables but inherently interact with individuals’ subjective feelings and risk recognition processes. This mechanism may explain why certain ecological variables exhibit significant associations in bivariate analyses yet fail to maintain stable and consistent independent associations following multivariable modeling and FDR correction. Future research should move from single-factor effect comparison frameworks and prioritize variations in students’ lived experiences and cognitive appraisals, which can produce more nuanced understanding of context-sensitive bullying prevention and intervention designs [33].

Strengths and limitations

The present investigation carries several strengths. Primarily, this work differentiates three bullying indicators: victimization (BV), perpetration (BP) and scenario-triggered bystander affective responses (BR), rather than merging all bullying indicators into a unified latent construct. This design allowed the study to compare ecological patterns across behavioral involvement measures and emotional-reaction measures. The findings revealed that BR showed an association pattern distinct from BV and perpetration, which indicates that students’ emotional sensitivity and initial appraisal when confronting bullying situations may constitute critical dimensions for understanding bullying-related developmental processes. Second, this study adopted a mixed-methods design that combines a quantitative survey and semi-structured interviews. Quantitative statistical modeling unifies peer, family, school, and broader social environmental factors under one analytical framework to compare predictive effects across diverse bullying-related outcomes. Meanwhile, qualitative thematic analysis supplements contextual information from students’ perspectives regarding how these multi-layered environments are perceived in daily schooling. This two-pronged approach proves ecological covariates never act as isolated steady predictors; their influences are deeply embedded within adolescents’ concrete school-life experiences and problem-recognition processes. Last, customized statistical pipelines were deployed to the distribution characteristics of research outcomes, including hurdle-type analyses for BV and BP, robust linear regression for BR, and FDR correction for multiple testing, which effectively avoids biased conclusions caused by mismatched models and repeated significance testing.

Several limitation exist within this research. First, participants were recruited from intact classes at a single secondary school in Shantou, China, yielding a single-school convenience sample with limited variation in the school context. Accordingly, the findings cannot be directly generalized to students from other regions, school types, or age groups, nor can they be regarded as representative of Chinese middle school students as a whole. As the questionnaires were completed anonymously, no class identifiers were recorded. Thus, the study failed to estimate within-class correlation or to use multilevel models or cluster-robust standard errors to address potential classroom-level clustering effects. Future research should incorporate samples from multiple schools and regions and record class- or school-level identifiers to enable more appropriate handling of the nested structure of the data.

Second, the study used a cross-sectional design and can only describe associations between ecological environmental factors and bullying-related outcomes. It cannot determine temporal order or causal direction. For example, negative peer experiences may be associated with bullying involvement, but this study cannot determine whether peer conflict is an antecedent or consequence of bullying involvement, or whether both are embedded within more complex peer relationship processes. Similarly, associations among the school environment, family support, and BR should not be interpreted as causal effects. Longitudinal research is needed to examine the dynamic relationships between ecological factors and bullying-related outcomes.

Third, all quantitative variables relied on student self-reports and may therefore be affected by recall bias, social desirability bias, and common method bias. Because both predictors and outcomes were measured via questionnaire responses from the same source at the same time point, some associations may have been inflated by shared measurement methods. BP may be particularly prone to underreporting due to its sensitive nature. Future studies could combine student self-reports with peer nominations, teacher reports, and school records to improve measurement stability and interpretability.

Fourth, several measures in this research were revised or developed specifically for the current research context. Although preliminary reliability and validity testing was conducted, further validation using independent samples remains necessary. Specifically, the BR scale measured students’ negative emotional reactions to scenario-based bullying situations rather than actual bystander intervention behavior. The low family restriction dimension remained exploratory and exhibited relatively weaker psychometric indicators. Low parental academic expectations was assessed via a single item. Findings involving these variables should therefore be interpreted with caution. Future research could further differentiate BR, intervention intentions, and actual bystander behavior, and adopt more refined measures for family academic expectations and family pressure.Furthermore, the qualitative and quantitative strands of this study were integrated primarily during the interpretation stage. The interview protocol was not formulated on the basis of statistical model outcomes. Therefore, the qualitative data should not be regarded as a direct explication or corroboration of specific quantitative associations.

Fifth, the moderation analyses are best regarded as exploratory. Interaction effects typically necessitate larger sample sizes and robust a priori theoretical assumptions. The limited number of non-zero BP cases, combined with the single-school sample, constrained the stability of the interaction estimates. Accordingly, the Peer negative experience × Family support interaction requires cautious interpretation. The current study cannot ascertain its underlying mechanism or directionality, and this finding is best understood as associational evidence within a particular school setting, rather than direct evidence for an ecological mechanism or intervention pathway. The present study utilized a predicted probability plot to illustrate the model-predicted relationship between negative peer experiences and the occurrence of BP across different levels of family support. Nonetheless, this figure should not be taken as confirmatory evidence of a moderation mechanism and is intended principally to characterize the exploratory interaction pattern.

Conclusion

This single-school mixed-methods investigation of Chinese middle school students identified distinct ecological association patterns for BV, BP, and BR. Associations between ecological environmental factors and BV or perpetration were generally weak and warrant cautious interpretation. Conversely, BR displayed relatively more consistent associations with school environments, social environments, gender, and parental academic expectations. These findings imply that students’ emotional reactions to bullying situations constitute a valuable bullying-related affective measure that can be examined alongside behavioral involvement indicators. Qualitative findings further indicate that students’ bullying-related experiences and interpretations are situated within everyday peer interactions, family relationships, teacher responses, school climate, and broader social environments. Taken together, the findings indicate that bullying-related phenomena within this school context are embedded within overlapping ecological environments, as opposed to representing isolated individual behaviors. In light of the cross-sectional design and single-school context, the findings should be interpreted as context-specific and correlational in nature. Future multi-site, longitudinal studies with more refined measurement tools are warranted to examine how peer, family, school, and broader social environments correlate with bullying-related experiences and bystander responses over time.
