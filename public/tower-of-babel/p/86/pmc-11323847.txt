Aggressive giant dumbell-shaped thoracic vertebral hemangioma: illustrative case
Authors: Matteo Gionso, Ali Baram, Mario De Robertis, Massimo Tomei, Daniele Ajello, Umberto Cariboni, Federico Pessina, Maurizio Fornari, Gabriele Capo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDVertebral hemangioma is the most common benign tumor of the spine, diagnosed incidentally in most cases. In 0.4% of patients, the lesion is considered aggressive, causing neurological deficits. This subtype of hemangioma is characterized by strong postcontrast enhancement, cortical lysis, and epidural extension causing myelopathy and/or radiculopathy.OBSERVATIONSA 52-year-old man presented with myelopathy symptoms, namely lower-limb hypoesthesia up to the T4–5 sensory level, right leg hyposthenia, and urinary incontinence. Imaging studies revealed a giant dumbbell-shaped lesion causing spinal cord compression, associated with signal alteration of the T3 vertebral body. The diagnosis of schwannoma was not certain given the radiological features, so a biopsy was planned and confirmed the diagnosis of vertebral hemangioma. Preoperative embolization, spinal fusion, and gross-total resection of the extravertebral component of the lesion were performed.LESSONSThis report should raise awareness of the differential diagnosis of dumbbell-shaped spinal tumors and the therapeutic strategies available for aggressive vertebral hemangiomas, a rare lesion that should be managed in a multidisciplinary setting. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE24190

Vertebral hemangiomas (VHs) constitute 5%–12% of vascular lesions of the spine and have an estimated prevalence of 11%, although recent studies suggest that the actual prevalence is much higher, considering the benign nature of these lesions. VHs are typically diagnosed most commonly in the 5th decade of life with a slight female preponderance (female/male ratio of 1:1.5). The thoracic and lumbar spine are the most commonly affected segments.1

VHs can be classified radiologically into 3 types: 1) typical VHs, 2) atypical VHs, and 3) aggressive VHs (AVHs). This radiological classification reflects the histopathological and clinical features of hemangiomas: typical VHs are asymptomatic, have a benign clinical course, and are histologically characterized by a higher adipose content relative to the vascular component.2 Only 1% of VHs are classified as atypical and aggressive, and they are usually symptomatic at presentation, with little or no adipose component and a higher vascular content.3 AVHs are lesions that commonly expand beyond the vertebral body to enter the vertebral canal and usually present with neurological deficits at diagnosis. Regarding their radiological appearance, they often exhibit atypical features. On computed tomography (CT) scans, cortical lysis or extracortical extensions may be observed. On magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), VHs are hypointense on T1-weighted sequences and strongly hyperintense on T2-weighted sequences.4 The atypical findings on CT and the strong postcontrast enhancement on T1-weighted sequences make the differential diagnosis between AVHs and other neoplastic lesions of the spinal cord challenging.5 Here, we report the case of a giant dumbbell-shaped aggressive hemangioma of the thoracic spine, presenting with medullary cord compression and intrathoracic expansion. The case was managed in a multidisciplinary setting with CT-guided biopsy, preoperative embolization, excision, and postoperative radiological follow-up.

Illustrative Case

History and Presentation

A 52-year-old male patient with no significant medical history reported hypoesthesia and paresthesia in his right foot starting 3 months before presentation. The sensory disturbance progressed in a distoproximal fashion, initially involving the entire right lower limb, then spreading contralaterally to the left lower limb, and finally reaching approximately the T4–5 sensory level on the trunk. In addition, the patient reported the onset of motor impairment in the right lower limb (Medical Research Council [MRC] 3/5 on right foot dorsiflexion), a paraparetic gait, and frequent episodes of urinary incontinence. The patient did not complain about axial pain. A cervical-dorsal CT scan and MRI scan with contrast showed a giant dumbbell-shaped lesion (7.5 × 7 cm) with a voluminous extravertebral component and single-level bilateral epidural extension at T3 (Figs. 1 and 2). The lesion caused spinal cord compression and was highly suspicious for a nerve sheath tumor. An associated alteration of the vertebral body characteristic of VH with a high vascular component was found at T3. The CT scan showed the typical “polka dot sign” (Fig. 2). After a multidisciplinary discussion, a CT-guided biopsy was performed. The histological results—capillary angiodysplasia with cavernous aspects—confirmed the diagnosis of AVH. According to the Enneking staging system of benign bone primary tumors, this lesion was classified as stage 3 and warranted surgical treatment.6 Multimodal treatment consisted of preoperative embolization and two-stage resection of the lesion.

Preoperative Embolization

After the administration of local anesthesia, an angiographic map of the arterial feeders was obtained. One main feeder trunk was identified as a hypertrophic bronchial artery on the right side, arising at the T5 level. A 0.027-inch Renegade HI-FLO microcatheter (Boston Scientific) was used to navigate the target vessel. Once a wedged position was reached and no backflow was recognized, Contour PVA embolization particles (150–200 µm, Boston Scientific) were infused until optimal devascularization of the tumor was achieved (Fig. 3). The patient left the angiography suite without new neurological deficits. Due to the development of fever (> 38°C), the surgical procedure was delayed. A second embolization procedure was performed before the rescheduled surgical intervention. Surgical Procedure

A two-stage surgical procedure was performed by the neurosurgery and thoracic surgery teams on the same day. First, a posterior approach was performed. Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring and CT scan–based navigation were used. After exposing the thoracic vertebrae from T1 to T6 and positioning pedicle screws in T1, T2, T4, and T5, we performed a laminectomy at T3 and a partial laminotomy at T4, exposing the lesion, which appeared grayish and hypervascularized. To fulfill a complete excision of the epidural component, bilateral pediculectomy and right nerve root clipping were performed. This approach allowed us to tangentially visualize the anterior bilobed component of the lesion, reducing the ventral blind working angle. The lesion was excised using a piecemeal technique, reaching into the intraforaminal component. Moderate bleeding was controlled with hemostatic agents.

For the second stage, the patient was in the left lateral decubitus position. Thoracic surgeons approached the intrathoracic component through a right posterolateral thoracotomy at the fourth intercostal space and a transpleural route. The lesion appeared as a 7-cm, rounded soft mass dissociated from the posterior thoracic wall, with no evidence of intrathoracic adherence or infiltration. The neurovascular peduncle originating from the vertebral body was sectioned, and the intrathoracic retropleural component of the aggressive hemangioma was removed en bloc.

Postoperative Course and Outcome

The postoperative course was characterized by progressive improvement in right lower-limb motor function and sensory disturbance, as well as resolution of urinary incontinence. The motor deficit in right foot dorsiflexion observed preoperatively improved from 3/5 to 4+/5 on the MRC scale in the first postoperative days. With the aid of daily physiotherapy, we observed complete resolution of the paraparetic gait. The patient was discharged on the 10th postoperative day in good clinical condition, with residual noninvaliding light paresthesia in the right lower limb and hypoesthesia at the T5 level in the right hemithorax. The final histopathological analysis confirmed the previous biopsy results. After a multidisciplinary discussion, it was decided that no radiation therapy (RT) should be performed on the surgical field, as the postoperative MRI showed radiological gross-total resection (GTR) of the extravertebral component of the tumor. Follow-up MRI was performed at 2 months, 6 months, and 1 year and showed no tumor recurrence (Fig. 4). Discussion

Observations

AVHs represent a heterogeneous pathology. They may present with diverse radiological and clinical features that require different therapeutic approaches. A clear consensus on their management has not yet been reached. In our case, the radiological characteristics of the lesion, namely the dumbbell shape and the presence of a component inside the spinal canal with a retropleural intrathoracic expansion through the right neuroforamen at the T3 level, were more indicative of a spinal schwannoma than an AVH. Furthermore, preoperative imaging (Figs. 1 and 2) revealed enlargement of the neuroforamen, a characteristic more commonly associated with slow-growing lesions, such as spinal schwannomas, rather than aggressive lesions.7 Following a multidisciplinary discussion, we chose to perform a CT-guided biopsy of the intrathoracic part of the lesion. Indeed, the histopathological results suggested a hemangiomatous lesion, demonstrating how relying only on neuroimaging can be misleading. To our knowledge, only a few reported cases of dumbbell-shaped AVHs mimicking spinal schwannomas have been reported in the recent literature, underlining the rarity of this subtype of AVHs.8–13 In all these reported cases, the primary radiological suspect was spinal schwannoma, and patients were referred for surgery without a preoperative diagnosis. Therefore, we believe that CT-guided biopsy should be included as a key step in the preoperative workup for patients presenting with dumbbell-shaped lesions with atypical vertebral body characteristics. Indeed, knowing the histological subtype before surgery enabled us to tailor a multimodal operative strategy accordingly. AVHs can result in high intraoperative bleeding, and the latest studies agree on selective embolization of tumor feeders.14 For this reason, we decided to perform preoperative embolization, resulting in an estimated blood loss (EBL) of 150 mL during surgery, a relatively low amount if compared with recently published data, where the mean EBL was between 400 and 700 mL.15, 16 Following the embolization procedure, the patient developed a fever, consistent with postembolization syndrome.17 As a result, the planned surgical procedure had to be postponed. Evidence suggests that surgery should be performed within 3 days following preoperative embolization; hence, a second embolization procedure was planned before the rescheduled tumor resection.18

Recent data suggest that surgery is the recommended treatment for AVHs presenting with neurological deficits at diagnosis.14 The choice of approach and surgical technique primarily depends on the localization of the hemangioma, the presence of associated neurological deficit, and the risk of fracture.19 However, no clear indications exist regarding the optimal extent of resection and oncological strategy. If we were to follow oncological principles according to the Enneking staging system,20 a stage 3 benign aggressive tumor would require at least a wide resection, given that an intracapsular or marginal resection can result in tumor recurrence. This would have meant performing a vertebrectomy with subsequent 360° reconstruction,4 as described by other previous studies.21–23 We chose not to strictly adhere to oncological principles because of 2 main factors. First, as stated in the largest systematic review analyzing surgically treated AVHs, en bloc spondylectomy is associated with higher surgical morbidity compared to aggressive intralesional excision and has no significant benefit in terms of local control and progression-free survival.24 Second, Pratheep et al. recently demonstrated in a retrospective study of 21 patients with AVH that less extensive procedures, such as posterior instrumented decompression and intralesional tumor resection, can achieve good clinical and neurological outcomes with low recurrence rates.25

Recent evidence considers vertebroplasty an important step in the management of AVHs, to be performed together with posterior decompressive surgery in order to achieve mechanical support and to treat axial pain.26–28 However, to our knowledge, no advantage in terms of recurrence or outcome has been attributed to vertebroplasty. In this case, we chose not to perform it for 2 reasons. First, pedicle screw fixation already provides substantial mechanical stability, and the patient did not present any back pain symptomatology. Second, the presence of a large-caliber afferent vessel originating from the vertebral body raised concerns regarding the risk of cement leakage.

An adjuvant therapy thoroughly discussed in the literature is postoperative RT. RT alone can be considered for patients harboring atypical VHs presenting with isolated back pain and/or mild neurological compromise; this approach has shown good results in terms of pain control.29, 30 However, if patients present with a worsening neurological picture and spinal cord compression, surgery with or without adjuvant RT is the most common treatment. Piper et al. suggest that it should be employed as a valid adjuvant option in cases in which there is residual or recurrent compressive tumor after surgery.14 In our reported case, GTR of the extravertebral epidural component was achieved. In addition, the 6-month and 1-year MRI follow-up showed no evidence of residual tumor growth or recurrence of the intracanalicular component, which would have been a criterion in favor of adjuvant RT; hence, RT was not performed.

Limitations

The rarity of AVHs, coupled with the diversity in treatment protocols among institutions, limits the validity of any conclusions drawn regarding treatment strategies and patient outcomes. Multi-institutional studies could provide clearer and more founded therapeutic criteria for these patients. Additionally, the limited duration of our postoperative follow-up restricts our ability to confirm the absence of future recurrence and the long-term effectiveness of our team’s treatment strategy.

Lessons

The differential diagnosis of dumbbell-shaped lesions developing at the vertebral neuroforamen can be broader than what is thought. AVHs, although rare, should always be considered alongside more common spinal schwannomas, especially when there are alterations involving the adjacent vertebral body. In such cases, it is of pivotal importance to manage the patient in a multidisciplinary setting and to perform a preoperative biopsy to plan the best treatment strategy. Preoperative embolization helps in reducing intraoperative blood loss and facilitates surgery. Excision may not strictly adhere to oncological principles, considering that an intralesional and partial resection has a low risk of recurrence and notably lower morbidity. Vertebroplasty serves as a useful tool to provide mechanical support to the vertebral body but may be avoided in cases where posterior fixation is performed. Radiotherapy is indicated in recurrent tumors and can be considered for residual tumor.
