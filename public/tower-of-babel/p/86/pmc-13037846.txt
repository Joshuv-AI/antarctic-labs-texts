Pedro A. Sanchez (1940–2026), a social soil scientist
Authors: Meine van Noordwijk, Generose Nziguheba, Thomas P. Tomich, Mary Scholes, Fahmuddin Agus, Leigh Winowiecki, Joyce Kasyoki, Peter A. Minang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cuban-born Pedro A. Sanchez (1940-2026) became the face of tropical soil science at Cornell University and the North Carolina State University. His combination of process-based understanding of soils, emphasis on farmer choices and conducive policy environments earned him the 2022 World Food prize. After leadership roles in international agroforestry research in Kenya, he returned to academia at Columbia University and the University of Florida, and as a member of the U.S. National Academy of Sciences.

On January 12, 2026, Pedro A. Sanchez passed away, nearly two years after his spouse, colleague soil scientist and sparring partner Cheryl Palm. Authors of this retrospective met Pedro in different stages of their own career (student, postdoc, researcher) and different decades but all found Pedro and Cheryl to be extremely personable individuals. Many friendships had a lasting influence on making soil science more social, more ecological, and more development oriented. Throughout his career Pedro wove three strands together: A. Soils and their sustainable management, B. Locally adapted farming systems, and C. Conducive policy environments, all contributing to his 2002 World Food Prize award (1). Tropical soils differ from temperate-zone soils in aspects B and C rather than A, as the basic processes are universal. Soils are central to the interplay of agriculture, health, environment, and infrastructure and can be better understood through a digital soil map of the world that he helped to shape with a system of “fertility capability” soil classification as a tool to assess soil quality (2).

Pedro’s contribution to soil science started with PhD research on puddled rice soils in the Philippines, but especially when he worked up his lecture notes for undergraduate teaching at the University of the Philippines to become the “Properties and management of soils in the tropics” textbook (3) in 1976. With all the work Pedro initiated since it rapidly became out of date. The second edition in 2019 (4) was enriched with a much deeper understanding of soil biological relationships, land use across the agriculture–forestry spectrum and policy connections as a means to scale impacts—including the backgrounds to his World Food Prize in 2002.

Pedro started life on a family farm near Havanna (Cuba) that grew avocados and mangos for export. His father worked as an agricultural extensionist and had a fertilizer shop, so he learned what can put money in farmers’ pockets. After he graduated from high school in 1958 (the year of the success of the anti-Bautista Cuban revolution), Pedro, oldest of four children, followed his fathers’ footsteps to Cornell University. He scraped by financially as a student but continued with a master’s degree and a PhD in soil science in 1968. The soil science he studied was largely descriptive and classification-oriented, but he added a life-long interest in appropriate diagnostics, pedotransfer functions, and digital soil maps. He obtained a faculty position at North Carolina State University (NCSU) in Raleigh, initially focused on rice research in Peruvian Ultisols and soybean cultivation on the Brazilian Cerrado Oxisols. Fine-tuning liming and fertilizer inputs could make cropping systems of acid-soil tolerant varieties possible on half a million ha (5). But beyond the choice of fertilizer, adequate organic input was essential for maintaining soil conditions (6), as well as avoiding the soil compaction of mechanical land clearing. He stayed at NCSU for the next 23 years. Several of the graduate students at NCSU became part of the inner circle for Pedro and Cheryl with life-long friendships and research links.

“There is no direct information on soil fertility and conservation under agroforestry systems in the humid tropics of Latin America.” In 1979, Pedro started his contribution to an expert meeting in Nairobi at the newly constituted International Council for Research in Agroforestry (ICRAF) with this statement (7). He reviewed evidence that slash-and-burn was agronomically superior to the bulldozer land clearing method that had become a “sign of progress.” His paper foreshadowed important directions for agroforestry research and practice. Under his leadership of the NCSU TropSoils program in Yurimaguas (Peru), a foundational set of agroforestry experiments was started. The Nairobi meeting launched Pedro’s long-term connection to the international agroforestry and tropical soil biology and fertility community.

With his scientific credentials, he was selected in 1991 to lead ICRAF in its transformation from a communication and advocacy council into a research -oriented member of the international agricultural research centres (CGIAR). He strongly supported a process-based and hypothesis testing research culture without loss of relevance for the farmers who see trees as an essential part of their farms (8). His trademark comment, in a loud bass voice from the back of the room of any seminar was the question “so what?.” ICRAF’s priorities shifted from descriptions of agroforestry systems and on-station trials to on-farm collaboration with small-scale farmers and a solutions-oriented agenda driven by their needs.

For Africa, he saw a strong need for “soil fertility replenishment,” emphasizing use of local sources of rock phosphate and the expectation that at systems level nitrogen can be obtained from biological nitrogen fixation (9). Part of this was the “improved fallow” research (10) that he viewed as an alternative to the alley-cropping (hedgerow intercropping) that had been promoted before but failed to gain traction as managing the tree–soil–crop interactions in simultaneous agroforestry required too much labor.

His start as Director-General of ICRAF coincided with the Rio Conventions and the global interest in climate change, biodiversity loss, and land degradation as key concerns that link farmers to global policy. He found support for a large initiative to transform crop production in tropical forest margins through Alternatives to Slash-and-Burn (ASB). ASB initially was modeled on the Yurimaguas experience of sustainable cropping systems with positive spinoffs for forest protection. As the first diagnostic phase found important alternatives to the original ASB-alternative (continuous annual cropping), the program broadened to what is now seen as social–ecological systems research (11) within a boundary-spanning organization (12). When social scientists were discussing the finer details of distinctions of participatory action research at the start of the ASB program, Pedro concluded that “whatever you call it, talking with farmers is always good.”

Pedro led efforts to get the CGIAR international agricultural research community connected to the climate change expertise that was still largely concentrated in the countries that were the primary cause of climate change but not the countries that suffered most from the consequences (13). As he recalled in reflecting on his ICRAF years (14), one of his largest disappointments was when key donors rejected the proposals for international agricultural research to engage with climate change as “mission creep.”

Among the honors he received that he was most proud of was being made, at his farewell in Nairobi in August 2001, a “Luo elder” (named Odera Kang’o) for his assistance in eliminating hunger from many villages in Western Kenya. After their decade in Kenya, Cheryl, and Pedro found a new home at Columbia University in New York where he was active in UN-related efforts (incl. as Co-Chair of the UN Millennium project task force on hunger) while linking the new insights back to a more academic soil science agenda (15) including his magnum opus the second edition of the soil management book, a legacy to future generations (16). Subsequently relocating to the University of Florida (Gainesville) made it easier to close another circle and support efforts to help Cuban agriculture transform.

His vision for an Africa-specific green revolution, beyond the selected crop cultivars, irrigation, and heavy application of synthetic N fertilizers of the Green Revolution of lowland Asia and Latin America, had soil health as a primary intervention. These efforts sparked new initiatives including the Alliance for the Green Revolution in Africa and the Millennium Villages Project (17), which was coconceived with Cheryl. While his earlier publications reported that use of the yield-improving fertilizer levels was economically justified in Peru and Brazil, he became a strong supporter of significant price subsidies for fertilizers for African smallholders to offset high transport costs due to poor infrastructure. He subsequently advised the government of Malawi in 2005 to restore fertilizer subsidies that had been abolished by “structural adjustment” programs of international financial institutions. Discussion on the merits and long-term impacts of such policies continues.

Pedro obtained a MacArthur foundation fellowship. He was elected as member of the US National Academy of Sciences in 2012 in section 64 (Human Environmental Sciences), with secondary affiliation in section 63 (Environmental Sciences and Ecology). A truly social–ecological systems soil scientist, he changed tropical soil science for all future generations. Thank you, Pedro, for friendship and inspirational discussions.
