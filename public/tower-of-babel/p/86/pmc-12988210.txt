A hybrid machine learning approach for reliably predicting surface roughness in CNC turning operations
Authors: Hakan Yurtkuran, Güven Demirtaş, Feyyaz Alpsalaz, Hasan Uzel, Ievgen Zaitsev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Surface roughness in CNC turning is a pivotal quality metric shaping functional performance, service life and production cost. This study investigates data-driven prediction of arithmetic mean surface roughness (Ra) during the turning of AISI H13 steel under both new-tool and progressively worn-tool conditions. Several machine learning models including k-Nearest neighbors (KNN), random forest (RF) and extra trees (ExT) are evaluated and compared with a stacking ensemble model that integrates these base learners using a linear regression meta-learner. The input variables consist of cutting speed, feed rate, depth of cut and triaxial cutting force components. The results show that the KNN model exhibits limited predictive accuracy whereas the RF and ExT models achieve competitive performance. The proposed stacking ensemble consistently outperforms all individual models achieving a coefficient of determination (R²) exceeding 0.98 along with substantial reductions in root mean square error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE) under tool-wear conditions indicating strong generalization capability. To enhance model transparency SHapley additive exPlanations (SHAP) and local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME) are employed. The interpretability analyses identify feed rate as the dominant factor influencing surface roughness while the importance of cutting forces and the interaction between depth of cut and feed rate increases as tool wear progresses. Overall the findings demonstrate that the proposed stacking-based hybrid model provides an accurate, robust and explainable framework for surface roughness prediction in CNC turning offering practical potential for in-process quality monitoring and decision support applications.

Introduction

Machining is one of the most widely used manufacturing processes in modern industry. Process is used not only to improve existing products but also to produce new ones1. H13 tool steel is commonly used for both hot and cold work tools2 a hot-work tool steel. Essentially this steel material is a chromium-molybdenum alloy hot work steel. During hot work repeated heating or repeated cooling cycles in tool applications cause thermal fatigue cracks in the cutting tool. The hot hardness (hot strength) of H13 is resistant to this condition3,4. H13 tool steel is commonly used for wear-resistant parts such as injection molds that are exposed to high operating temperatures and fatigue5,6. Machinability refers to the ability to remove material from a workpiece efficiently while achieving the desired surface quality7. Turning is a basic material removal process that involves workpiece rotation and tool movement to obtain the required geometry. Most turning operations use a single-point cutting tool that maintains consistent contact with the workpiece during machining except at the beginning and end of the cut8. The correct selection of cutting parameters ensures a high-quality machining process particularly when forming difficult-to-machine materials that require experience such as hot-work tool steels. At the same time determining the cutting parameters of the cutting tool to be used according to the material being cut is essential for a correct machining process.

The emergence of machine learning offers a transformative approach to modeling advanced precision processing. Machine learning models such as artificial neural networks (ANNs), support vector machines (SVMs) and convolutional neural networks (CNNs) show promise in industrial applications. These models can learn from data, determine processing conditions and make highly accurate predictions9. Machine learning (ML) is used in speech and image recognition the Internet of Things (IoT) and in fields such as healthcare, aviation, space and the military. With its rapid pace of development machine learning has the potential to revolutionize many industries10. Machine learning regression techniques have emerged as powerful tools for understanding and predicting the complex relationships that exist within production processes11. A pure algorithm is a single technique used to solve a problem from start to finish. In contrast a hybrid algorithm combines multiple techniques from different fields to solve a problem or improve the performance of a single algorithm. Hybrid methods leverage the strengths of each algorithm while mitigating their limitations by integrating different methodologies. Thus a hybrid algorithm can be considered a combination of ideas and methodologies that compensates for weaknesses while exploring the potential of different approaches12.

Chengli Jing et al. examined studies on the machinability of AISI H13 tool steel and the contribution of machine learning methods to machinability. They conducted a study on cutting performance and machining economy by hardening AISI H13 steel. According to the results high cutting force and temperature were obtained under dry cutting conditions. Tool life is short and significant tool wear occurs13. In another study surface roughness of cold work tool steel was found to be initially affected by feed rate and subsequently by cutting speed, cutting depth was found to have no significant effect. Since the main objective of the study was to examine surface integrity and quality the lowest measured value for Ra was 0.98 μm14. Balasuadhakar et al. stated that the CATBoost (CATB) model demonstrated superior performance with an accuracy rate of 90.8% surpassing other ML models. Based on these results the researchers concluded that the CATB model is the most effective machine learning framework for predicting surface roughness values in the context of this material when considering various cutting parameters, machining processes and cooling conditions15. Mahdi S. Alajmi and Abdullah M. Almeshal conducted a study on estimating and optimizing surface roughness in a turning process using the ANFIS-QPSO method. The estimated surface roughness values were matched with the measured values demonstrating the effectiveness of the ANFIS-QPSO method. The predicted results were found to closely agree with the experimental values16. In energy-based modeling and prediction approaches for surface roughness in turning operations experimental results show that the energy-based prediction approach can achieve satisfactory prediction accuracy with a mean root mean square error (MRE) of 4.86% and a maximum root mean square error (RE) of 10.09%. This indicates that energy consumption is a reasonable and useful method for predicting surface roughness17. Jannis Yakup et al. sought to address the challenge of predicting surface roughness values based on in-process sensors during hard turning of 16MnCr5 steel using ensembles of machine learning models. They performed hyperparameter optimization using a random grid search to improve the models’ performance. Results showed that the Ensemble Regressor achieved a 5.65% improvement in performance compared to the best individual model based on RMSE evaluation resulting in highly accurate surface roughness prediction18. Another study estimated surface roughness using vibration signal-based Hauss process regression. It noted a significant advantage of this approach: its ability to accurately predict surface roughness for complex workpieces without relying on complex simulations or laborious physical measurements. The researchers reported that this capability could facilitate quality control in production by reducing costs and time. It could also help manufacturers improve production methods, enhance surface quality and minimize waste19. Long Bai et al. developed a surface roughness prediction model based on hybrid physical data for ultra-precise machining. The model achieved 83.4% prediction accuracy on the test set. This result is 8.7% higher than that of a data-driven model that does not use ERD or reference roughness features20. Another compilation study was conducted to distinguish the production methods and specific surface roughness requirements covered in the articles. It has been demonstrated that in addition to numerical data image data obtained from product measurements plays an increasingly important role21. Recent studies have applied machine learning techniques to complex machinability problems across different materials and cutting environments. For instance Şap et al. investigated the machinability of Incoloy 800 superalloy under varying cutting and cooling conditions using artificial intelligence models to predict surface roughness, tool wear and cutting temperature with promising accuracy22. Similarly Şap et al. examined the milling machinability of Cu–Gr composites and predicted multiple performance metrics using machine learning-based models, demonstrating the applicability of data-driven approaches for diverse materials and cutting outputs23.

In recent years researchers have shown significant interest in the reliable prediction of surface roughness in CNC turning operations in terms of both production quality and cost optimization. Traditional statistical methods have long been used to determine the relationship between cutting parameters and surface quality; however their predictive capability is limited due to the nonlinear and multivariate nature of machining processes. In this context machine learning approaches, particularly hybrid and ensemble-based models have gained increasing attention in the literature. Existing hybrid approaches differ in both methodology and application scope. For instance Bober et al. integrated material certification data within a reverse engineering framework to enable pre-production surface roughness prediction24. More generally hybrid machine learning studies can be categorized into: (i) integrating cutting parameters with signal-based features, (ii) combining statistical models with learning algorithms, (iii) incorporating meta-heuristic optimization techniques and (iv) hybridizing physical knowledge with data-driven models. Despite these advances limitations remain regarding model robustness across different tool conditions and the interpretability of prediction mechanisms. Addressing these gaps the present study proposes a stacking-based hybrid learning framework that integrates machining parameters, multi-axis cutting force components and an interaction term between depth of cut and feed rate (ap·f) and systematically evaluates its performance under both new tool and controlled tool-wear conditions. Furthermore explainable artificial intelligence techniques namely SHAP and LIME are employed to provide both global and local insights into the prediction process enabling an interpretable analysis of how the influence of machining parameters and cutting forces on surface roughness evolves with tool wear. This integrated perspective enhances the reliability, generalizability and industrial applicability of surface roughness prediction in CNC turning operations.

Material method

This section discusses the dataset, prediction algorithms the proposed hybrid model, performance metrics and explainable artificial intelligence methods used in the study. The study used surface roughness data obtained from CNC turning experiments taking into account cutting parameters and tool wear conditions. To facilitate comparison, KNN, RF and ExT algorithms were employed in the prediction process. A Stacking hybrid model incorporating a Linear Regression-based meta-learner was developed to leverage the strengths of these algorithms. The performance of the models was evaluated using determination (R²), mean square error (MSE), MAE and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) metrics to comprehensively analyze accuracy and error levels. To enhance the model’s interpretability SHAP and LIME methods were employed to elucidate the decision-making processes at the global and local levels.

Dataset

This study utilized a dataset obtained from CNC turning experiments published on the Kaggle platform25. The dataset was originally generated within a Master’s dissertation conducted at the Competence Center in Manufacturing (CCM) of the Aeronautics Institute of Technology (ITA) and is based on controlled turning experiments performed on AISI H13 steel under cutting fluid conditions.

The overall experimental scheme and signal acquisition process are illustrated in Fig. 1 which presents the turning configuration the relative positioning of the cutting tool and workpiece and the acquisition of surface roughness and machining force signals. The structure of the experimental design and the applied machining conditions for both experiments are schematically summarized in Fig. 2.

The experimental campaign consisted of two distinct experiments: Experiment 1 (Exp1) and Experiment 2 (Exp2). Experiment 1 (Exp1) followed a full-factorial design of experiments (DoE: 3³) conducted under theoretically new tool conditions. The depth of cut (ap) was varied at three levels (0.25, 0.5 and 0.8 mm) the cutting speed (Vc) at three levels (310, 350 and 390 m/min) and the feed rate (f) at three levels (0.07, 0.11 and 0.13 mm/rev). Each experimental condition was replicated twice resulting in a total of 54 machining runs. A new cutting tool was used for each experimental group. After each machining run surface roughness measurements were performed at six different locations on the machined surface yielding a total of 324 observations.

Experiment 2 (Exp2) was designed to investigate the influence of tool wear on surface roughness and included the tool condition as an additional experimental factor. In this experiment the cutting speed (Vc) was kept constant at 350 m/min. The depth of cut (ap) was varied at two levels (0.25 and 0.5 mm) the feed rate (f) at four levels (0.07, 0.09, 0.11 and 0.13 mm/rev) and the tool condition (TCond) at three levels defined by flank wear width (VBB): new tool (0.0 mm) mid-life tool (0.1 mm) and end-of-life tool (0.3 mm). The cutting tools corresponding to these wear states were prepared in advance during a dedicated tool preparation phase. Exp2 consisted of 48 machining runs with two replicas and surface roughness was again measured at six locations per condition resulting in 288 observations.

Experiment scheme and signal acquired25.

In both experiments multiple surface roughness parameters were measured after each machining operation including arithmetic mean roughness (Ra), skewness (Rsk), kurtosis (Rku), mean width of profile elements (Rsm) and total height (Rt). Machining forces were recorded using a Kistler Type 9265B dynamometer connected to a Kistler Type 5070 charge amplifier and Dynoware 2825 A acquisition software. Although cutting forces were measured in three orthogonal directions only the resultant cutting force (F) was used as an input variable in the proposed prediction model. The experiments were carried out on AISI H13 steel workpieces with an average hardness of 200 HV using a ROMI E280 CNC turning center (maximum spindle speed: 4000 rpm; nominal power: 18.5 kW). A Sandvik Coromant ISO TNMG 16 04 04-PF 4425 insert mounted on an ISO MTJNL 2020 K 16M1 tool holder was employed. A water-based cutting fluid (Blaser Swisslube Vasco 7000, 8% concentration, pH ≈ 8) was applied during machining. Surface roughness was measured using a Mitutoyo Surftest SJ-210 and tool wear was evaluated using a Dino-Lite AM4113ZT digital microscope.

Although the present study does not involve newly conducted experimental trials the detailed description above together with the schematic representations in Figs. 1 and 2 provides a transparent and technically grounded experimental context for the dataset employed. Accordingly the primary contribution of this work lies in the development and evaluation of a hybrid machine learning-based surface roughness prediction model using a well-documented and systematically generated experimental dataset.

Prediction algorithms

KNN is a non-parametric algorithm that classifies or regresses examples based on their distance to their nearest neighbors. In regression the example to be predicted is determined by the average of the k nearest examples in the training set and in classification, it is determined by the majority of the k nearest examples. Despite its simple structure, KNN’s performance in high-dimensional datasets is sensitive to distance measures26. RF (random forest) is an ensemble learning method based on the aggregation of multiple decision trees. Each tree is trained using randomly selected subsets of features and samples and the results are combined to produce the final prediction. This structure reduces the risk of overfitting and effectively captures non-linear relationships27. Similar to RF the ExT algorithm uses multiple decision trees; however, node splits are performed with a higher degree of randomness. This completely random selection of split points reduces the model’s variance and improves its generalization ability. This method provides faster computation and a structure that is more resistant to overfitting28. Linear regression is a classic statistical method that uses a linear model to explain the relationship between a dependent variable and one or more independent variables. It offers the advantages of simplicity, interpretability and fast computation. However, it may be inadequate for complex, nonlinear relationships. In this study linear regression is employed as a meta-learner in the stacking approach to generate the final prediction based on the outputs of various base models29.

All machine learning models were trained within a supervised learning framework using a 5-fold cross-validation strategy to ensure robust performance estimation and to reduce the risk of overfitting. The hyperparameter settings of each model were explicitly defined based on preliminary experiments and cross-validated performance. For the KNN regression model the number of neighbors was set to k = 5, uniform weighting was applied and the Euclidean distance metric was used. This configuration allows the model to capture local patterns while maintaining prediction stability. For the RF regression model the number of trees (n_estimators) was set to 200 and the maximum tree depth was limited to 10 to control model complexity and avoid overfitting. Similarly the ExT regression model was trained using 200 trees with a maximum tree depth of 10, introducing additional randomness while maintaining controlled model growth. The outputs of the KNN, RF and ET models were combined using a stacking ensemble approach, where a linear regression model was employed as the meta-learner. This ensemble structure integrates the complementary strengths of individual base learners and enhances generalization performance. All hyperparameter values were selected based on cross-validated performance within the same 5-fold cross-validation framework, ensuring fair comparison, reproducibility and robustness of the proposed hybrid modeling approach.

Recommended stacking hybrid model

In the context of this study the term hybrid model refers to a learning framework that combines multiple heterogeneous machine learning algorithms within a stacking ensemble structure, as well as the integration of different types of input information for surface roughness prediction. Similar to hybrid approaches reported in the literature, which often merge complementary models or data sources to improve robustness and accuracy the proposed method integrates distance-based and tree-based learners and combines machining parameters with process-related features. This hybrid formulation enables the model to capture both local and global data patterns, leading to improved predictive performance and generalization. This study proposes a stacking-based hybrid ensemble learning model for reliable surface roughness estimation. The goal of stacking is to overcome the limitations of models used alone by combining the strengths of different algorithms30. In this context the KNN, RF and ExT algorithms were used as base learners. The outputs obtained from each model were then combined and fed into a linear regression-based meta-learner. This structure leverages KNN’s ability to capture local patterns, RF’s high accuracy based on decision trees and ExT’s randomness approach, which reduces the risk of overfitting, along with the simple, generalizable structure of linear regression. Thus the weaknesses of the individual models are balanced and stronger prediction performance is achieved. To construct the stacking ensemble, a 5-fold cross-validation scheme was adopted. In each fold the base learners were trained on the training folds and generated predictions for the validation fold, resulting in out-of-fold predictions for the entire training set. These predictions were subsequently used to train the linear regression meta-learner. This cross-validation-based stacking strategy prevents information leakage and improves the robustness of the final model.

SHAP

The SHAP method was used to make the Stacking hybrid model more interpretable. Based on the concept of Shapley values in game theory, SHAP calculates the contribution of each input to the model by distributing it fairly. Developed to demonstrate the basis of predictions in machine learning models, SHAP has become one of the most widely used explainable artificial intelligence tools in recent years. Using SHAP in this study ensures high accuracy and makes the decision-making mechanism transparent and interpretable.

LIME

Another method used to improve model interpretability is LIME. Short for “Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations,” LIME is a model-agnostic explanation technique that can work independently of any machine learning model. It explains a model’s prediction for a single observation at the local level. In other words, it shows which inputs influence the prediction in a given example. Thus, not only is the model’s decision mechanism understandable at the general (global) level, it is also understandable through individual examples.

The methodology of the study

Figure 3 shows the steps involved in developing the proposed hybrid model. First the data set obtained from CNC turning experiments was loaded and then preprocessing was performed. Unnecessary columns were removed from the dataset, missing or erroneous observations were checked and all numerical variables were converted to a standard format. At this stage the machine learning models utilize machining and process-related parameters as input variables, including cutting speed (vc), feed rate (f), depth of cut (ap) the resultant cutting force (F) and the interaction term between depth of cut and feed rate (ap·f). The target output variable of all models is the arithmetic average surface roughness (Ra), which is predicted based on the given input variables. Then, feature engineering was performed. To more accurately represent the relationships between cutting parameters the interaction term (ap f) between step depth (ap) and feed rate (f) was added to the dataset. This allowed the model to capture the complex interactions between the parameters. Furthermore, quantile-based stratification was applied to the target variable, surface roughness (Ra), to balance the distribution in the training-test split. Next the dataset was split into training and test sets. Scaling operations (normalization/standardization) were then performed to obtain more reliable results in the modeling process. This improved the performance of distance-based algorithms, such as KNN. Three different algorithms were defined as base learners in the modeling process: KNN, RF and ET. Since these algorithms have different learning logics, each can capture different dimensions of the data. The models’ outputs were combined using the stacking approach and final predictions were generated through a linear regression-based meta-learner. This structure mitigates the limitations of individual models while leveraging their strengths. During training the model ran up to 1,500 iterations to ensure a balance between loss and error metrics. Then the performance evaluation phase was conducted to calculate metrics such as R², MSE, MAE and MAPE. A paired t-test was then applied to test the statistical significance of the differences between the models. Finally, SHAP and LIME analyses were performed to evaluate the model in terms of both accuracy and interpretability. SHAP revealed which parameters contributed most to the predictions across the entire dataset and LIME explained the model’s decisions for individual examples. Figure 3 is therefore not just a flowchart, but rather, it presents a holistic methodology from data preparation to model development, statistical performance testing and ultimately, explanation of the decision-making process.

Experimental results

This section compares the performance results of individual machine learning algorithms with those of the proposed Stacking hybrid model on data obtained from CNC turning experiments. First, we examined the statistical properties of the experimental data and the relationships between parameters. Then, we compared the accuracy levels and error metrics of the models. The superiority of the proposed model over the individual algorithms was verified through statistical tests. Additionally, SHAP and LIME analyses were used to make the model’s decision mechanism interpretable.

Figure 4 shows the correlation matrices for Experiments 1 and 2. In Experiment 1 the highest correlation is observed between Ra and F (r = 0.76). Additionally there is a strong correlation between Ap and F (r = 0.90), while the relationship between Ap and Ra is weak (r ≈ -0.06). Figure 9 shows the SHAP-based variable importance levels for the KNN, RF, ET and Stacking hybrid models in Experiments 1 and 2. In both experiments, f is the most dominant parameter. This finding underscores the critical role of the feed rate in predicting surface roughness and aligns with existing literature. Furthermore the F variable reaches a high importance level, particularly in the RF and hybrid models. This indicates that surface quality is closely related to cutting forces. Although the ap parameter contributes at a low level in Experiment 1, it is notable that its importance and that of the ap·f interaction term increase in Experiment 2 with the onset of tool wear. This suggests that, as tool wear progresses the combined effect of cutting depth and feed rate on surface quality becomes stronger. In the hybrid model in particular the prominence of the ap·f interaction variable, in addition to f and F, demonstrates that the model can more successfully capture the complex relationships between parameters. In conclusion, SHAP analyses are key evidence explaining the hybrid model’s superior performance. The model represents not only the effects of individual variables, but also the interactions between them thereby achieving high accuracy in Ra estimation.

Conclusion

This study compared different machine learning algorithms for reliably estimating surface roughness in CNC turning operations. A stacking hybrid model was developed by integrating KNN, RF and ET base learners with a linear regression meta-learner. Experiments were conducted on AISI H13 steel under new tool conditions and tool conditions with varying degrees of wear. The findings revealed that KNN offered low accuracy levels, while RF and ET demonstrated satisfactory, albeit limited, performance. In contrast the proposed hybrid model achieved the highest R² values and the lowest error metrics in both experiments, outperforming all the individual algorithms. Paired t-test results confirmed that the hybrid model’s superiority was statistically significant. Furthermore, SHAP- and LIME-based explainability analyses revealed that the feed rate (f) parameter dominates surface roughness. As tool wear increases the importance of cutting forces and the ap·f interaction term also increases, indicating the model’s ability to capture complex parameter interactions. These results demonstrate that the proposed hybrid model is a powerful approach in terms of both predictive accuracy and interpretability. In conclusion the developed stacking hybrid model is a promising approach that can be integrated into decision support and quality control systems for industrial applications, enabling reliable surface roughness prediction in CNC turning processes. However, it should be noted that the experimental dataset is limited to a specific material and a defined range of machining conditions, which may restrict the direct generalization of the model to other materials or cutting environments. In this regard the proposed model contributes to increasing production efficiency, managing tool life more effectively and reducing costs within the investigated domain. Future studies can enhance the model’s generalizability by conducting experiments on different materials and broader machining conditions. Additionally, integrating real-time sensor data may further support its application in online quality monitoring systems.
