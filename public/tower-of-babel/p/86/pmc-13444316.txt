Oncolytic adenovirus incorporating TetO sites in the E1A promoter for controlled replication in TetR-expressing mesenchymal stem cells
Authors: Ramon Alemany, Silvia Torres-Manjon, Laia Traveset, Rafael Moreno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOncolytic viruses are therapeutic agents that combine self-amplification, lytic activity, and immunostimulatory properties. However, their systemic administration is limited by inefficient tumor delivery. Although mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-based carrier systems have been proposed to overcome these barriers, their efficacy is restricted by rapid MSC lysis following viral replication, which limits the time available for tumor homing. Here, we describe a tetracycline operator-regulated oncolytic adenovirus designed to temporally control viral replication within MSC carriers and enhance intratumoral viral delivery. In this system, menstrual blood-derived MSCs (MenSCs) are engineered to stably express the tetracycline repressor, while the adenoviral genome incorporates TetO sites upstream of the E1A promoter, enabling reversible pharmacological control of viral replication through tetracycline or doxycycline administration. TetO incorporation did not compromise viral fitness, and viral replication was effectively repressed in TetR-expressing MenSCs, with restoration upon inducer addition. Importantly, once released within the tumor microenvironment, viral progeny replicated unrestrictedly in TetR-negative cancer cells. In lung adenocarcinoma model, TetO-regulated oncolytic adenoviruses delivered by TetR-MenSCs achieved enhanced intratumoral viral accumulation and improved antitumor efficacy. Overall, these findings establish a controllable MSC-based oncolytic adenovirus delivery strategy that addresses a key limitation of systemic virotherapy and supports its clinical translation across solid tumors.Keywords: cancer, oncolytic adenovirus, TetR, mesenchymal stem cell, CELYVIR, virotherapy, cell carrier

Introduction

Oncolytic viruses (OVs) have emerged as a promising class of anticancer immunotherapies that preferentially infect, replicate within, and lyse tumor cells, while exhibiting limited activity in normal cells.1 Beyond direct oncolysis, OVs can act as potent in situ vaccines, promoting immune infiltration and enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapeutic combinations. Oncolytic virotherapy has been evaluated in hundreds of clinical trials worldwide across multiple viral platforms, and several oncolytic agents have received regulatory approval, including talimogene laherparepvec for melanoma and H101 for head and neck cancer.2 However, despite these encouraging developments, systemic administration of OVs still suffers from important limitations. Rapid neutralization by circulating antibodies, sequestration by liver and spleen, and the presence of stromal and endothelial barriers collectively restrict virus delivery to tumors, resulting in insufficient therapeutic potency.3

To address these barriers, substantial efforts have focused on the use of cell carriers to transport OVs to primary tumors and metastatic sites. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are particularly attractive for this purpose because of their immune-privileged phenotype, stromal plasticity, and intrinsic ability to home toward inflammatory and tumor-associated niches.4 MSCs are being evaluated as OV-delivering carrier cells in clinical trials, reflecting their translational readiness and unique biological advantages.5 In this context, our group has pioneered the co-development of CELYVIR, an advanced therapy combining MSCs with the oncolytic adenovirus (OAd) ICOVIR5, and later ICOVIR15. CELYVIR has undergone extensive clinical evaluation, including one compassionate-use study, a completed phase 1 trial, and multiple ongoing trials in pediatric and adult patients with refractory extracranial solid tumors, metastatic uveal melanoma, and newly diagnosed or relapsed DIPG or medulloblastoma.6 Across studies, CELYVIR has demonstrated an excellent safety profile and signs of clinical benefit, with objective responses including complete tumor regressions in some cases.7

However, despite its favorable clinical behavior, CELYVIR also revealed a key limitation: only small amounts of virus reach the tumor after systemic administration. Analysis of tumor biopsies from patients treated across trials showed detectable adenovirus in only a minority of cases and at low levels.8 This limited intratumoral accumulation likely arises from multiple biological and anatomical barriers, including the filtering of infused MSCs by organs, such as the lung, liver, and spleen, the challenges of extravasation into tumor tissue, and the structural complexity of solid tumors that limits MSCs tumor spread. Importantly, these obstacles are further constrained by the limited viability window of MSCs following OAd loading. Once infected, MSCs are lysed as the adenovirus completes its replication cycle within 48–72 h, leaving limited time for efficient tumor homing and intratumoral spreading. Thus, a major limitation of CELYVIR is not the potency of the virus but the insufficient time during which MSCs can function as effective delivery vehicles.

To overcome this bottleneck, we developed a tetracyclin operator (TetO)-regulated OAd (CONAN) designed to improve the performance of MSC-based delivery. MSC carriers isolated from menstrual blood (MenSCs) are genetically modified to stably express the tetracycline repressor (TetR), while the adenoviral genome is engineered to contain seven TetO sites upstream of the E1A promoter TATA box. In the absence of tetracycline or doxycycline, TetR binding to the TetO sites suppresses E1A expression, thereby preventing viral replication within MSCs and extending the time frame available for tumor homing. By enabling pharmacological, reversible activation of E1A expression through administration of tetracycline or doxycycline, this system allows virus replication to be triggered only after MSCs have accumulated within the tumor, thereby enhancing the therapeutic payload delivered to the tumor microenvironment. Although tetracycline-regulated systems have previously been incorporated into recombinant adenoviruses, primarily to control transgene expression and, in some cases, adenoviral replication,9,10,11 our design, which combines a Tet-regulated system with OAds and MenSCs carrier cells, enables for the first time temporal control of viral replication during systemic trafficking while preserving unrestricted replication of viral progeny after release. Accordingly, once TetO-regulated adenoviruses are released from lysed MenSCs within the tumor, they undergo unrestricted replication in cancer cells, which lack TetR expression, thereby behaving as conventional OAds. The modular design of the system could enable incorporation into serotype 5-based oncolytic adenoviral backbones and use with carrier cell types engineered to express TetR, thereby expanding its translational versatility.

In this study, we demonstrated stable TetR expression in MenSCs, successful incorporation of TetO elements into the adenoviral genome without compromising viral fitness, and regulation of viral replication in TetR-positive cells with restoration upon doxycycline addition. In a murine model of human lung adenocarcinoma, the TetO-regulated virus within TetR-MenSCs achieved superior intratumoral viral delivery and antitumor efficacy compared with classical CELYVIR, supporting its clinical potential. Collectively, these results demonstrate that our adenoviral replication control system could represent a clinically translatable strategy designed to overcome a fundamental limitation in systemic virotherapy and to improve patient outcomes across a broad range of solid tumors.

Results

Effect of TetO incorporation into the E1A promoter on virus fitness and replication control

The OAd ICOVIR15 was engineered to incorporate either two or seven TetO repeats within the E1A gene promoter by replacing the 24 bp immediately downstream of the TATA box, resulting in ICOVIR15-R2 and ICOVIR15-R7, respectively (Figure 1A). ICOVIR15-Luc, an arming ICOVIR15 virus expressing the luciferase gene under the control of the adenoviral major late promoter and therefore, dependent on viral replication, was also modified in a manner analogous to ICOVIR15 by incorporating seven TetO repeats into the E1A gene promoter, thereby generating ICOVIR15-R7-Luc (Figure 1A).

We sought to determine whether the number of TetO repeats incorporated into the E1A promoter influenced the control of virus replication and A549 host-cell lysis, a reference cell line for adenovirus production. To this end, A549 cells and A549 cells stably expressing the (TetR were infected with the parental ICOVIR15 or modified viruses, ICOVIR15-R2 and ICOVIR15-R7, at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1 and the total virus production was assessed three days post-infection. As shown in Figure 1B, parental ICOVIR15 and the TetO-modified viruses exhibited comparable virus yields in unmodified A549 cells (left). However, in TetR-expressing A549 cells (right), virus production was reduced by 72% for ICOVIR15-R2 and by 90% for ICOVIR15-R7 relative to the parental virus. We also assessed the ability of the different viruses to induce lysis of the producer cells. To this end, a similar experimental setup was used, and the percentage of viable cells was determined three days post-infection. As shown in Figure 1C, the cytolytic potential was comparable among all three viruses in A549 cells (left). In contrast, in A549-TetR cells, only the parental virus ICOVIR15 exhibited cytotoxicity, resulting in a 42% reduction in cell viability, whereas cells infected with the TetO-modified viruses retained near-complete viability (right). Taken together, these results indicate that increasing the number of TetO repeats enhances the degree of adenovirus replication control, with seven TetO repeats exhibiting the strongest repression.

Next, we examined whether incorporation of seven TetO repeats into the E1A promoter affected virus fitness. To this end, dose-dependent cytotoxicity assays were performed not only in A549 cells but also in MenSCs. As shown in Figure 1D, the inhibitory concentration 50 (IC50) values (defined as the amount of virus required to reduce cell viability by 50%) were comparable for ICOVIR15-R7-Luc and ICOVIR15-Luc in A549 cells (0.0722 vs. 0.1195, not significant). In MenSCs, ICOVIR15-R7-Luc exhibited a slightly lower IC50 than ICOVIR15-Luc (102.3 vs. 225.8, not significant).

Finally, we sought to compare the ability of the modified and parental virus to express replication-dependent transgenes. To this end, luciferase expression was measured in A549 cells infected with ICOVIR15-Luc or ICOVIR15-R7-Luc at different time points post-infection. Figure 1E shows that both viruses exhibit nearly identical luciferase expression kinetics over time. Collectively, these results indicate that incorporation of seven TetO repeats into the E1a gene promoter do not affect ICOVIR15-Luc virus properties in non-modified cells.

Control of TetO-modified adenovirus replication by TetR-expressing MenSCs in vitro

To evaluate the capacity of our system to control adenovirus production in a CELYVIR-like setting, complementary in vitro experiments were performed to assess the ability of TetR-expressing MenSCs to regulate 7xTetO-engineered adenoviral replication. Since MSCs have been reported to silence transgene expression through methylation of different promoters,12,13 the ability of MenSCs to express the Tet repressor under the control of alternative promoters was fist evaluated. To this end, MenSCs were transduced with lentiviral vectors expressing TetR under either the CMV or hEFH, which has been reported to support long-term and high-level transgene expression in modified MSCs,13 and TetR transcript levels were subsequently quantified. The results showed that TetR expression levels in MenSCs were not influenced by the promoter used (Figure S1). Therefore, we continued using the same lentiviral vector employed in A549 cells, which drives TetR expression under the control of the CMV promoter.

MenSCs and MenSCs modified to express Tet-repressor (MenSCs-TetR) were infected with ICOVIR15-Luc or with ICOVIR15-R7-Luc. Then, early (E1a) and late adenovirus proteins production were determined by western blot. As shown in Figure 2A, while the kinetics of adenovirus protein expression was similar in MenSCs infected with both viruses, ICOVIR15-R7-Luc proteins expression was markedly repressed in TetR-expressing MenSCs, although a low level of protein expression leakage became detectable at later time points, starting at 48 h post-infection. This repression was not observed for ICOVIR15-Luc.

In a second experimental setting, MenSCs and TetR-expressing MenSCs were infected with ICOVIR15 or ICOVIR15-R7, and the number of virus particles per cell was quantified daily for up to 9 days post-infection. In unmodified MenSCs, both viruses displayed comparable virus production kinetics (Figure 2B left). In contrast, in TetR-expressing MenSCs, ICOVIR15-R7 exhibited delayed replication kinetics and reduced final virus yield compared with ICOVIR15 (Figure 2B right). However, daily administration of tetracycline (20 μg/mL), starting on day 4 post-infection, restored ICOVIR15-R7 replication to levels at day 9 comparable to those observed for the same virus in unmodified MenSCs (Figure 2C). Phase-contrast microscopy analysis, performed daily throughout the experiment, confirmed the differential cytopathic effect associated with TetO/TetR-mediated control of viral replication. At the endpoint (day 9 post-infection; Figure 2D), TetR-expressing MenSCs infected with ICOVIR15-R7 in the absence of tetracycline maintained a normal cellular morphology, despite detectable, albeit reduced, viral replication. In contrast, tetracycline administration restored the cytopathic effect in TetR-expressing MenSCs infected with ICOVIR15-R7, consistent with pharmacological reactivation of viral replication. In the absence of tetracycline, all other infected culture conditions also exhibited a clear cytopathic effect compatible with advanced viral replication and extensive cell death. The full daily imaging series is shown in Figure S2.

Finally, MenSCs and TetR-expressing MenSCs were infected with ICOVIR15-Luc or ICOVIR15-R7-Luc. The production of viral particles, viral transcripts (E1A), and luciferase expression, used as a replication-dependent transgene readout, were monitored at multiple time points post-infection. MenSCs-TetR infected with ICOVIR15-R7-Luc exhibited effective, although not complete, control of E1A transcript production (Figure 2E), luciferase expression (Figure 2F, left), and virus replication (Figure 2G, left) throughout the course of the experiment. This resulted in delayed replication kinetics and reduced viral production compared with the other experimental groups. However, upon addition of tetracycline at 96 h post-infection, ICOVIR15-R7-Luc restored luciferase expression (Figure 2F, right), and viral replication to levels comparable to those observed in the control groups (Figure 2G, right).

Overall, these results indicate that our system effectively limits progression of the adenovirus replication program, and that this restriction is sufficient to preserve a normal cellular phenotype while effectively controlling the expression of replication-dependent transgenes.

MenSCs-TetR infected with ICOVIR15-R7-Luc present virus replication control and enhanced antitumor efficacy in vivo

To evaluate in vivo control of ICOVIR15-R7-Luc replication by MenSCs-TetR and the antitumor efficacy of the combined treatment, immunodeficient NSG mice bearing large subcutaneous human A549 tumors (800 mm3) were treated intraperitoneally (day 0) with MenSCs or MenSCs-TetR, labeled with a fluorescent dye and previously infected with ICOVIR15-Luc or ICOVIR15-R7-Luc, receiving intraperitoneal administration of doxycycline on days 3, 4, 5, and 6 (Figure 3A). Intratumoral cell homing and virus replication were monitored daily using an in vivo image system, and tumor volume determined twice a week. As observed in Figure 3B, the number of injected cells reaching the tumor (fluorescence level) was comparable across most groups, with the exception of the MenSCs + ICOVIR15-R7-Luc group, which showed a slight reduction in the number of detected cells. Notably, although the MenSCs-TetR + ICOVIR15-R7-Luc group displayed the largest difference compared to the MenSCs + ICOVIR15-R7-Luc group, no differences were observed between the MenSCs-TetR + ICOVIR15-R7-Luc and the MenSCs + ICOVIR15-Luc groups. Intratumoral virus replication, assessed by luciferase activity, showed a higher tumor luciferase expression during the first days after treatment for all treatment groups except MenSCs-TetR infected with ICOVIR15-R7-Luc (Figure 3C). Notably, intratumoral luciferase expression levels of ICOVIR15-R7-Luc derived from MenSCs-TetR remained close to baseline from the first day of treatment until three days after the initial doxycycline administration, reaching a lower peak of tumor luminescence intensity than the other groups but sustained over a longer period of time. Finally, the group that showed a greater ability to control tumor growth was ICOVIR15-R7-Luc-loaded MenSCs-TetR, being this difference greater after doxycycline administration (Figure 3D).

To further confirm the greater antitumor efficacy of the viral controlled group (MenSCs-TetR + ICOVIR15-R7-Luc), a second in vivo experiment was carried out. Immunodeficient NSG mice bearing smaller subcutaneous human A549 tumors (250 mm3) were treated as previously indicated but doxycycline was administered added in the drinking water at day 4 (Figure 3E). Again, the group of MenSCs-TetR infected with ICOVIR15-R7-Luc exhibits the greater antitumor potential (Figure 3F).

Altogether these results confirm that it is possible to regulate the replication of 7xTetO-engineered OAd from cells that express the TetR, both in vitro and in vivo, and that this control allows increasing antitumor efficacy using OAd in vivo.

Discussion

OAds have been clinically evaluated for more than two decades as systemic anticancer agents, but their therapeutic efficacy has remained modest throughout multiple clinical trials. Several studies using intravenous administration of OAds, including our own experience with ICOVIR5,14 have demonstrated excellent safety but limited objective responses in most treated patients. These results reflect a broader pattern observed across the field: despite strong preclinical potency, systemic administration of adenoviruses is frequently associated with limited intratumoral accumulation, thereby constraining therapeutic efficacy.15

This limited clinical effectiveness is largely attributable to the intrinsic biological barriers that adenoviruses face in patients. Systemically administered OAds are rapidly sequestered in the liver and spleen, neutralized by preexisting antibodies, or cleared by innate immune components.16 Adults typically exhibit high seroprevalence of anti-Ad5 antibodies, acquired through natural infection, and such immunity can markedly impair adenoviral biodistribution and transduction efficiency.17 Furthermore, structural and endothelial barriers restrict extravasation into tumor tissue. Indeed, it has been reported than less than 0.001% of intravenously administered virions reached tumor sites within 24 h,18 underscoring the substantial barriers to effective viral delivery following systemic infusion.

To overcome these barriers, cell carriers have emerged as a promising strategy to facilitate tumor-directed delivery of OAds. MSCs, in particular, display immunomodulatory properties and tumor-homing capacity, making them attractive vectors for OAds delivery to primary and metastatic tumor sites.19 Our group has extensively explored this approach through the co-development of CELYVIR, in which MSCs are used as cellular carriers for ICOVIR5. Clinical trials of CELYVIR across multiple tumor types have provided valuable translational insights, demonstrating a favorable safety and tolerability profile and evidence of clinical activity in a subset of patients. In addition, these studies have enabled the assessment of MSC-based viral delivery under clinically relevant conditions.6

Despite these advantages, the clinical efficacy of CELYVIR has remained limited, and accumulating evidence suggests that insufficient MSC homing to tumors could represent a major contributing factor according to completed,7 and on-going (NCT05047276) clinical trials. Only a small percentage of systemically administered MSCs successfully reach tumor tissue, and even fewer survive long enough to release their viral payload. A critical factor likely contributing to this limitation is a mismatch between the kinetics of adenovirus replication and the time required for MSC accumulation within tumor tissue. Once infected with a replicating OAd, MSCs undergo lysis within 48–72 h, frequently before they have had sufficient time to extravasate and localize in tumor microenvironments. As a result, much of the viral production occurs away from the intended target, thereby diminishing the therapeutic impact.

In addition to limiting MSC viability, viral replication may also compromise key functional properties of these cells, including tumor-directed migration. In our recent work, transcriptomic profiling of MenSCs infected with an OAd revealed the rapid downregulation of a gene metacluster (M1) enriched in genes associated with cell migration, adhesion, and trafficking.20 More broadly, viral infection is known to induce profound changes in host-cell physiology, including metabolic reprogramming, mitochondrial perturbation, and activation of cellular stress responses, ultimately contributing to loss of cellular fitness and cytopathic effect.21,22,23 These findings suggest that active viral replication can impair the molecular programs underlying MSC homing capacity. In this context, transient repression of viral replication may help preserve the transcriptional integrity and functional properties of MSCs during the carrier phase, thereby potentially enhancing their tumor-targeting efficiency prior to viral release.

In this work, we present a strategy designed to address this fundamental limitation. CONAN represent a controlled-replication version of CELYVIR, based on a TetR/TetO regulatory system,24 engineered into the MSCs and the oncolytic adenovirus ICOVIR15. Our approach relies on two components: (1) constitutive expression of TetR in the MSC carrier, and (2) insertion of seven TetO sites immediately downstream of the TATA box in the E1A promoter of the OAd. This configuration enables TetR-expressing MSCs to actively repress E1A transcription, thereby preventing entry into the adenoviral replication program, preserving MSC viability and extending the window during which MSCs can migrate toward tumor tissue. Replication can subsequently be induced pharmacologically through administration of tetracycline or its clinically approved analog doxycycline, which binds TetR and disrupts its interaction with TetO, thereby restoring E1A transcription and permitting initiation of the viral replication program.

The choice of doxycycline as the inducer is particularly advantageous for clinical translation. Doxycycline is widely used in humans, exhibits a well-established safety profile, induces TetR dissociation at low doses, and provides rapid, reversible regulation of gene expression.25,26,27,28 These pharmacologic properties, combined with ease of administration, render doxycycline an attractive inducer for in vivo control of OAd replication. In this context, the development of doxycycline-dependent OAdsaligns naturally with established clinical practice and regulatory familiarity.

Other groups have explored conditional replication systems for adenoviruses, but each approach presents limitations that restrict broad applicability. For example, the group of Dr. Song engineered MSCs to express adenoviral E1B55K under tetracycline control to complement an E1B55K-deleted OAd.29 While this enhances replication in MSCs, the newly produced viral particles lack E1B55K and therefore cannot replicate in subsequent target cells, limiting the spread of infection and reducing therapeutic potential. Similarly, Dr. Poller’s group generated a bidirectional doxycycline-dependent cassette enabling lung cancer-specific expression of rtTA-SM2, which activates E1A transcription via TetO sites.30 However, this system requires continuous doxycycline administration for replication, preventing the virus from functioning autonomously after the initial induction. Finally, Dr. Chiocca’s group developed a dual-virus system in which a replication-defective HSV expressing FLP recombinase activates an FLP-dependent OAd by excising a GFP transcriptional blockade.31 Although elegant, the requirement for coinfection of the same cell with two distinct viruses makes systemic delivery highly inefficient.

In contrast, the system described here combines several distinctive properties, including control of adenovirus replication from the moment MSCs are infected, pharmacologically tunable activation of viral replication through doxycycline administration, autonomous virus propagation thereafter without the need for continued doxycycline exposure, and the generation of fully replication-competent virus progeny capable of infecting and lysing tumor cells.

The ability to delay virus replication until MSCs have reached the tumor represents a conceptual and practical advance for systemic oncolytic virotherapy. By decoupling virus kinetics from MSC migration, this strategy directly addresses one of the principal bottleneck identified in prior CELYVIR studies. Our in vitro and in vivo data indicate that the modifications introduced into our OAd do not compromise viral fitness. Moreover, the system provides effective, although not complete, control of viral replication in the A549 cell line.

Regarding the performance of CONAN in MSCs, our original experiments (data not shown), performed using FBS-supplemented medium, revealed residual viral replication and replication-dependent transgene expression despite the absence of exogenously added tetracycline. This observation suggested that TetR-mediated repression was not complete under the experimental conditions used. One possible explanation was that commercial FBS preparations contain trace amounts of tetracycline or related compounds capable of partially relieving TetR-mediated repression. Repeating the experiments under serum-free conditions substantially reduced basal viral replication, E1A transcription, and transgene expression while preserving the ability of doxycycline to rapidly restore all three parameters upon induction. Although a low level of residual viral activity could still be detected, viral replication remained markedly delayed and did not compromise cell morphology or viability during the carrier phase. Other biological factors may also play a role in the residual leakiness observed under serum-free conditions and in the persistence of low-level E1A transcription in the absence of induction, including heterogeneity in TetR expression among individual MenSCs, despite comparable average TetR transcript levels between promoter configurations. In addition, differences in chromatin accessibility at the TetO-containing E1A promoter region, the episomal organization of the adenoviral genome, or intrinsic basal activity of the modified E1A promoter may contribute to low-level E1A transcription in the absence of induction. These possibilities were not directly assessed in the present study and therefore represent relevant aspects for future optimization of the platform. These findings indicate that the residual activity observed in MSCs is considerably lower than initially estimated and further support the ability of CONAN to maintain effective, although not complete, control of viral replication. From a translational perspective, these observations are particularly relevant, as they highlight the importance of carefully controlling culture conditions and reagent composition during manufacturing.

Finally, in vivo assays demonstrate that the level of control achieved by our system translates into enhanced intratumoral viral delivery and improved antitumor efficacy, underscoring its superiority over the classical CELYVIR approach (without cellular or viral modification). In a first in vivo experiment, performed in mice bearing large tumors and with doxycycline administered intraperitoneally, we observed a stronger antitumor effect in the group receiving MenSCs-TetR loaded with ICOVIR15-R7-Luc. Although this group appeared to diverge from others prior to doxycycline administration, a similar early tumor growth pattern was also observed in additional groups, including MenSCs + ICOVIR15-R7-Luc and MenSCs-TetR + ICOVIR15-Luc, suggesting that these initial differences are more likely attributable to intrinsic variability in tumor growth dynamics rather than a specific effect of the TetO-regulated system. Notably, following doxycycline administration, the MenSCs-TetR + ICOVIR15-R7-Luc group displayed the most pronounced tumor growth control, supporting the role of inducible viral replication in driving therapeutic efficacy. At the same time, in this in vivo experiment, no evidence of enhanced early trafficking of MenSCs-TetR + ICOVIR15-R7-Luc was observed. However, it is important to consider that MSC homing to subcutaneous tumors in murine settings is typically rapid and may mask potential differences between groups. In contrast, clinical experience with CELYVIR suggests that MSC tumor homing in patients is a slower and more complex process, highlighting the potential relevance of delayed viral replication to extend MSC viability and improve tumor targeting in more physiologically relevant contexts. In a second independent in vivo experiment, conducted in mice bearing smaller tumors and with doxycycline administered via drinking water, the enhanced antitumor activity of the MenSCs-TetR + ICOVIR15-R7-Luc group was consistently reproduced. In this experiment, a PBS-treated control group was included as a reference for tumor growth, whereas non-infected MenSCs have previously been shown by our group to lack antitumor activity.32 At this point, it is important to note that both in vivo experiments were performed in immunodeficient mice bearing human tumor xenografts. Human adenoviruses are highly species-specific and replicate efficiently only in human cells, making this experimental setting necessary to evaluate viral replication and antitumor activity in vivo. Consequently, the contribution of immune-mediated mechanisms to therapeutic efficacy, as well as the impact of antiviral immune responses on viral persistence, biodistribution, and MSC trafficking, cannot be adequately assessed in this model. Future studies in more immunocompetent and clinically relevant systems will therefore be required to further characterize the interaction between CONAN, the host immune system, and the tumor microenvironment.

Together, these findings establish CONAN as a technology with clear translational potential for regulated OAd delivery. Finally, although this work has been performed using MSCs as carrier cells and the OAd ICOVIR15 as a model backbone, this approach is not inherently restricted to this specific combination. CONAN could, in principle, be applied to other therapeutic cell vehicles, such as neural stem cells currently used in combination with OAds to treat brain tumors,33 and to alternative adenovirus serotype 5-based backbones through appropriate adaptation of the regulatory elements. This flexibility allows preservation of full replicative competence in tumor cells while improving carrier-mediated tumor colonization and intratumoral viral delivery.

Material and methods

Cell lines and viruses

The cell lines A549 and HEK293 were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection and maintained with Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 37°C, 5% CO2. Cells were routinely tested for mycoplasma presence.

Isolation of human MenMSCs: menstrual blood (1–5 mL) was collected from healthy female donors, aged 23–42 years, on the first 3 days of the menstrual phase using a menstrual cup (Enna cycle, Spain). The protocol and cell donation for research purposes was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee and written consent was signed for each donor. Blood samples were transferred to a sterile 50 mL centrifuge tube with conical bottom, filled with sterile phosphate buffered saline (PBS, Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 5 min. The pellet was resuspended in α-MEM (Life Technologies) containing 20% Fetal Bovine Serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 1% gentamicin/amphotericin B (all from Life Technologies), and seeded overnight in culture flasks (Corning Inc., Corning, NY) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Next day, cell monolayer was washed once with PBS to remove non-adherent cells and cellular debris and replaced with fresh medium. Finally, MenSCs were amplified for 7–24 days (passage 0), substituting the culture medium every 3–4 days, until 75%–90% confluent, and then passaged periodically by detachment using TrypLE Express (Life Technologies) after achieving a subconfluent monolayer.

A549-TetR and MenSCs-TetR (cells expressing the Tet repressor) were generated by sorting A549 and MenSCs cells transduced with a lentiviral vector pHIV7-TetR-IRES-eGFP encoding the TetR and GFP under the control of the CMV promoter.

The OAd ICOVIR15 had been previously described.34 Briefly, ICOVIR15 was created by inserting four E2F-binding site hairpins and one Sp-1-binding site following nucleotide 415 in the E1a promoter of AdΔ24-RGD, a recombinant adenovirus which has a 24-bp deletion in the CR2 of the E1A gene and a fiber modified by incorporating an integrin binding RGD-4C motif into the HI loop.35 ICOVIR15-Luc contains a splicing acceptor signal connected to the firefly luciferase gene inserted after the fiber gene of ICOVIR15.36 The replication-controlled viruses ICOVIR15-R2 and ICOVIR15-R7 were generated by incorporating 2 or 7 TetO2 repeats respectively in the promoter of the E1a gene of ICOVIR15, specifically by replacing the 24 base pairs immediately after the TATAbox of E1a (bases 476–499 of the Ad5 adenovirus reference material GenBank AY339865). For this purpose, the 2 or 7 TetO2 repeats were amplified from the plasmid pTRE2Luc,37 kindly provided by Dr. Cristian Smerdou from CIMA (Navarra, Spain), and cloned by homologous recombination at position 476 of the Ad5 adenovirus reference material. ICOVIR15-R7-Luc was generated similarly by incorporating seven TetO repeats into the E1A gene promoter of ICOVIR15-Luc.

Viral plasmids were transfected into HEK293 cells, and the generated viruses amplified in A549 cells and purified on a CsCl gradient according to standard protocols. Viral titers were determined in triplicate by an antihexon staining-based method in HEK293 cells.38

Virus cytotoxicity assay

A549 and A549-TetR cells (5 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates and infected at an MOI of 1 in triplicate. At 72 h post-infection, plates were washed with PBS, and total protein content was quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Pierce Biotechnology, Rockford, IL), with absorbance measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell survival was calculated by normalizing total protein content to that of uninfected control cells, which was set to 100% viability.

For dose-dependent cytotoxicity assays, 3 × 104 A549 or 7.5 × 103 MenSCs were seeded in 96-well plates and infected in quadruplicate with serial dilution of 1/2 or 1/3 of the indicated viruses starting from 200 TU per cell for A549 and 2.000 TU per cells for MenSCs. 96 h later, plates were washed with PBS, stained for total protein content and absorbance was quantified. The IC50 was calculated with GraphPad Prism v.6.02 (GraphPad Software Inc.) by a dose-response nonlinear regression with a variable slope.

Virus replication, transcription, and transgene expression assays

A549 cell line

A549 and A549-TetR cells (1 × 105 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates and infected in triplicates with ICOVIR15, ICOVIR15-R2, or ICOVIR15-R7 at a MOI of 1 TU/cell. Four hours post-infection, medium was removed, cells were washed three times with PBS, and incubated with fresh medium. At 72 h post-infection, virus production was quantified by determining the number of infectious viral particles present in the supernatant using an anti-hexon staining-based assay.

To evaluate replication-dependent transgene expression, A549 cells (1 × 105 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates and infected in triplicate with ICOVIR15-Luc or ICOVIR15-R7-Luc at a MOI of 10 in a final volume of 200 μL. Luciferase expression was determined at the indicated time points by adding 20 μL/well of D-Luciferin (L-8200 (Biosynth) 1.5 mg/mL in PBS) and measuring luciferase activity with Multilabel Plate Reader Victor 5× (PerkinElmer).

MenSCs

To determine virus replication control and the phenotype of the producer cell, 1.5 × 104 MenSCs or MenSCs-TetR were seeded in 48-well plates and infected with ICOVIR15 or ICOVIR15-R7 at a MOI of 100 transduction units (TU) per cell (six wells per condition). This assay was performed in unsupplemented Opti-MEM, as the presence of tetracycline or related compounds in commercial FBS could not be ruled out. Four days post-infection, tetracycline was added to half of the wells in each condition at a final concentration of 20 μg/mL for four consecutive days. Supernatants (SN) were collected daily until the end of the experiment (9 days), and cell morphology was monitored by phase-contrast microscopy. To quantify virus production, virus DNA was extracted from supernatants using QIAamp DNA Mini and QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kits (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Adenovirus genome copies were quantified in triplicate by quantitative real-time PCR using primers specific to the adenoviral hexon gene (forward 5′-CTT CGA TGA TGC CGC AGT G-3′ and reverse 5′-ATG AAC CGC AGC GTC AAA CG-3′). PCR conditions consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 1 min, and 72°C for 7 s. Reactions were performed using LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I Master (Roche) in a LightCycler 480 Instrument II (Roche). Adenovirus genome copy numbers were calculated using a standard curve generated from serial dilutions of pAd5wt, a plasmid containing the complete adenovirus type 5 genome.

To simultaneously monitor viral replication, viral transcription, and replication-dependent transgene expression, MenSCs and MenSCs-TetR (7.5 × 103/well) were also seeded in 96-well plates and infected with ICOVIR15-Luc or ICOVIR15-R7-Luc at a MOI of 50 TU/cell (6 well per condition). Four hours post-infection, medium was removed, and cells were washed three times with PBS and fresh unsupplemented Opti-MEM was added. Tetracycline (20 μg/mL) was added to half of the wells at 96 h post-infection. At the indicated time points (0, 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, 144, 168, and 192 h post-infection), luciferase activity was measured directly in the culture wells as a surrogate marker of replication-dependent transgene expression (as indicated previously). Subsequently, aliquots of supernatant were collected for viral genome quantification (as described previously), and cells were harvested for virus transcript analysis. Total RNA was extracted using the Maxwell RSC simplyRNA Tissue Kit (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA samples were treated with the TURBO DNA-free Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to remove residual genomic DNA and reverse-transcribed using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using a LightCycler 480 Instrument II (Roche) and SYBR Green I Master Mix (Roche). PCR conditions consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 1 min, and 72°C for 7 s. Virus E1a transcript levels were quantified using the primers E1a-F (5′-ATCGAAGAGGTACTGGCTGA-3′) and E1a-R (5′- CCTCCGGTGATAATGACAA-3′). β-actin was used as the reference gene and amplified using ACTB-F (5′-CTGGAACGGTGAAGGTGACA-3′) and ACTB-R (5′-GGGAGAGGACTGGGCCATT-3′). E1a expression levels were normalized to β-actin expression. Non-reverse-transcribed RNA samples equivalent to the amount of cDNA used in each reaction were included as negative controls to exclude genomic DNA contamination.

Western blot

3 × 105 MenSCs or MenSCs-TetR were seeded in 6-well plates and infected with ICOVIR15 or ICOVIR15-R7 at a MOI of 50 TU/cell. Supernatants and whole-cell protein extracts were harvested 6, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h post-infection. Protein extracts were resolved by electrophoresis on an 8% acrylamide gel and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane by standard methods. Then, membranes were immunoblotted with polyclonal anti-E1a (Clone 13S-5, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA) primary antibody for virus early E1a protein detection and with polyclonal anti-Adenovirus 5 (Ab6582, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) primary antibody for virus late proteins. As housekeeping a monoclonal mouse anti-vinculin antibody was used (V9264, Sigma-Aldrich, Bozeman, MO, USA). Membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C and secondary labeled with correspondent anti-rabbit or anti-mouse HRP-linked (Dako Laboratories, Glostrup, Denmark) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Finally, SuperSignal West Femto substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was added, and bands intensity were visualized using a Chemi-doc (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

In vivo tumor-homing, OAdv tumor delivery, and antitumor efficacy studies

In vivo studies were performed at the ICO-IDIBELL Animal Facility (Barcelona, Spain) AAALAC unit 1155 and approved by IDIBELL’s Ethical Committee for Animal Experimentation.

Lung adenocarcinoma xenograft tumors were established by implanting 5 × 106 A549 cells subcutaneously into both flanks of 8-weeks-old male. When tumors reached 800 mm3, mice (experimental day −1) were randomized (5 animals per group) into the following groups: MenSCs+ICOVIR15-Luc (previously infected with ICOVIR15Luc at MOI 50 for 24 h and labeled with IVISence 680 Fluorescent cell labeling Dye (PerkinElmer NEV12000) according to the manufacturer’s protocol); MenSCs-TetR + ICOVIR15Luc (previously infected with ICOVIR15Luc at MOI 50 for 24 h and labeled with IVISence 680); MenSCs+ICOVIR15-R7-Luc (previously infected with ICOVIR15-R7-Luc at MOI 50 for 24 h and labeled with IVISence 680); and MenSC-TetRs+ICOVIR15-R7-Luc (previously infected with ICOVIR15-R7-Luc at MOI 50 for 24 h and labeled with IVISence 680). The next day (day 0), animals received a single intraperitoneal administration of 1.8 × 106 specific cells according to their group. On days 3, 4, 5, and 6 mice received an intraperitoneal administration of 50 mg/kg body weight of doxycycline. To monitor cell biodistribution and virus replication, mice were intraperitoneally injected with 120 mg/kg body weight of luciferin (Biosynth, Staad, Switzerland), 15 min before imaging. Both, fluorescent (using 675 nm excitation and 696 nm emission filters for IVISence 680 determination) and bioluminescent imaging analysis were performed daily using IVIS Lumina bioimaging system (PerkinElmer). Images were analyzed with IVIS Living Image (PerkinElmer) software. Regions were manually drawn around the tumors. Tumor volume was also calculated periodically according to the equation V (mm3) = π/6 × W2 × L, where W and L are the width and the length of the tumor, respectively. Data are expressed as tumor growth (tumor size relative to the size at the beginning of the therapy).

A second in vivo experiment was performed using the same tumor cell and animal model but initiating the therapy when tumor reached 250 mm3, without labeling cells with a fluorescent dye and including a control group treated with PBS (5 animals per group). Moreover, doxycycline was administered via drinking water (200 mg per 100 mL) starting on day 4 of treatment.

Statistical analysis

Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. For comparison of more than two groups, Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn post-hoc test was used. Statistical significance was established as p < 0.05. Data are presented as the mean ± SD or SEM. All statistical analysis were calculated with GraphPad Prism software.
