Spatial distribution of sexual network locations used by long-distance truck drivers along the Northern Corridor highway, Kenya
Authors: Cyrus Mutie, Salome Kairu-Wanyoike, Susan Mambo, Reagan Ngoge, John Gachohi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn Kenya, long-distance truck drivers (LDTDs) using the Northern Corridor highway have a high prevalence of HIV and other sexually transmitted infections (STIs) due to their risky sexual networks. However, the spatial distribution of the sexual network locations used by LDTDs is not well understood. Consequently, healthcare stakeholders have found it difficult to provide spatially targeted HIV/STI interventions among LDTDs. Thus, the study sought to establish the spatial distribution of sexual network locations used by LDTDs along the Northern Corridor highway, to inform efficient distribution and use of limited HIV/STI-prevention resources.MethodsA cross-sectional study design was used. The study adopted a systematic sampling technique. 296 LDTDs were interviewed using interviewer-administered questionnaires at the Mlolongo weighbridge in Kenya. The LDTDs listed their history of sexual interactions and highway stopovers used during the week preceding data collection. Geospatial modelling techniques, using R statistical software packages for spatial mapping, were employed. Shapefiles were created and overlaid over a map of Kenya using R statistical software to create maps of sexual networks.ResultsForty-two highway stopovers used by LDTDs were spatially distributed along the highway, from the Kenya coast to the Kenya–Uganda border. In general, LDTDs’ sexual network hotspots were restricted to the outskirts of major cities along the Northern Corridor highway (Nairobi, Mombasa and Nakuru) as well as the Kenya–Uganda international border.ConclusionsOn the Northern Corridor highway, stopovers situated proximal to major urban areas, as well as those at international border points, frequently serve as sexual network hotspots among LDTDs and their sexual partners. Thus, healthcare stakeholders should improve access to HIV/STI-prevention services targeted for LDTDs at the sexual network hotspots identified in this study.Keywords: casual sexual partners, hotspots, regular sexual partners, sexual interactions, sexually transmitted infections

Introduction

Globally, long-distance truck drivers (LDTDs), who transport goods and services within and across different countries, are highly vulnerable to risky sexual networks.1 These sexual networks include sexual linkages and the behaviours that LDTDs create with their sexual partners.2 Evidence shows that sexual networks among LDTDs are characterized by concurrent sexual interactions with casual and regular sexual partners.3 The sexual networks are risky due to multiple sexual partners, risky sexual behaviours like poor condom use and substance use, contributing to the transmission of new HIV and sexually transmitted infections (STIs) among LDTDs and their sexual partners.4,5 Denser sexual networks have been shown to have a higher risk level of HIV and STI transmission among LDTDs.4,5

Existing literature shows that specific locations along road networks serve as hotspots of sex work and transmission of STIs, including HIV.6,7 The main road network of the Northern Corridor highway runs from Mombasa Sea Port through Kenya and Uganda to Kigali in Rwanda, Bujumbura in Burundi and Kisangani in the Democratic Republic of Congo.1 The highway is characterised by road stopovers with diverse key population groups, among them female sex workers (FSWs) and LDTDs.1,8 The FSWs, and their sexual partners, most of whom include LDTDs,8 among other key population groups, accounted for 62% of all new HIV infections globally in 2019.9 Thus, LDTDs are classified among the most at-risk populations.10

Geographic information system techniques have proven to be resourceful in presenting health-related data for simple visualisation.11 While the sociodemographic, socioeconomic and biological factors influencing the distribution of HIV have been extensively studied, geospatial techniques remain inadequately utilised in most developing countries.12 This has seen poor use of scarce health resources in burdened and underfunded healthcare settings.12 Therefore, if well utilised, geospatial techniques can guide healthcare agencies in creating spatially targeted interventions.13,14 This would, in turn, guide prioritisation in the distribution and allocation of scarce and limited health resources.12 In HIV/STI programming, spatial data can provide information on the distribution of sexual networks and risk behaviours that can be used to guide targeted interventions.15 Indeed, spatial data have previously guided spatially targeted interventions like condom distribution, HIV testing and STI treatment among LDTDs, hence reducing new infections and enhancing the cost-effective use of scarce health resources.10,16–18

While the mapping of transactional sex has been previously conducted, revealing 5600 FSWs at 39 key hotspots on the Kenyan Northern Corridor highway, the sexual network dynamics among LDTDs are not well understood.8 Similar to other developing countries, there are limited studies on the spatial distribution of the sexual networks among Kenya's LDTDs.8 To achieve the global goal of ending AIDS by 2030,9 more research is needed on vulnerable groups, particularly LDTDs. This was part of a larger study whose aim was to establish sexual network mapping and utilisation of HIV/STI-preventive services.19,20 Here, this study sought to identify the distribution patterns of the sexual network locations among LDTDs operating along the Northern Corridor highway in its Kenya segment. The purpose is to help health policymakers strengthen existing policies focused on curbing HIV and STIs among LDTDs and the general population.

Methods

Study design, site and population

This was a cross-sectional study conducted at the Mlolongo weighbridge, Kenya, in July and August 2020. A weighbridge is a railroad scale that is mounted on the ground and is used to weigh heavy trucks as they pass by.1 At the Mlolongo weighbridge, trucks typically have to wait for 30–60 min to be served.1 However, the time may differ based on the size of the truck or the number of trucks awaiting service at a specific moment.1 The Mlolongo weighbridge is situated in Mlolongo Township, in Machakos County, Kenya. The town is one of the major stopovers for LDTDs along the Northern Corridor highway, where they experience delays because of the weighbridge bureaucracies.8 Moreover, the town has diversified stopover roles. Besides taking the weight of the trucks, the town is a key destination for commercial sex work and offers restful accommodation services.8 Therefore, this was a suitable site for our study, given that almost all LDTDs have to make a stopover here.

The study population was those LDTDs who had a stopover at the Mlolongo weighbridge. The target population was those LDTDs operating along the Northern Corridor highway.

Sample size and sampling technique

The study involved 296 LDTDs. A systematic sampling technique was adopted using a daily sampling size of 21 and a sampling interval of 20. The first participant was randomly selected from within the first sampling interval using simple random sampling (lottery technique), while the rest were selected by adding the sampling interval value to the initial random figure.

Inclusion criteria

LDTDs were eligible if they were present at the weighbridge, willing to participate, able to write or speak English and Swahili and had been involved in the transit of goods or services along the Northern Corridor highway during the previous week and had not been interviewed in the pretest survey. The study was open to LDTDs both genders. However, during our data collection, we did not encounter a female LDTD.

Data collection tools

The data collection tool was an interviewer-administered and structured questionnaire in either English or Swahili, depending on the language best understood by the participant. The questionnaire was designed through the guidelines of questionnaire development.21 In general, the questionnaire asked about the nature of sexual interactions and the stopover at which LDTDs and their sexual partners interacted in the week preceding data collection. Two interviewers, a male and a female, were initially trained on how to administer data collection tools for 1 day.

Data collection

Before starting every interview, LDTDs were asked whether they had been involved in the same survey before. Those who reported having been previously interviewed in our survey were excluded to reduce the biases that would occur when one LDTD is interviewed twice. Eligible LDTDs were given detailed information about the study. Thereafter, they were taken through an informed consent form, which they signed before the start of the interview. To ensure confidentiality, the anonymity of LDTDs was maintained throughout the data collection exercise, analysis and archiving.

The interviews were conducted by the two interviewers mentioned above. The male interviewer was a registered community health nurse, while the female interviewer was a registered public health officer.

A convenient place, which ensured the privacy and comfort of both the LDTD and the interviewer, was identified for the interview. Most LDTDs agreed to be interviewed inside the truck driver's cockpit, while others opted to be interviewed at the roadside parking lot. To avoid disruption to the normal flow of trucks and other operations, the LDTDs were approached at the terminal exit points of the weighbridge for interviewing. Each interview session took 20–30 min on average. All the LDTDs who were interviewed were male. There were no LDTDs who self-identified as transgender.

The LDTDs' locational and demographic data were collected using interviewer-administered questionnaires. The LDTDs listed locations (towns) used as stopovers, sexual interactions and the type of sexual partners (casual and regular) on each day of the week preceding the interview while at each stopover.

Data management and analysis

Data from questionnaires were checked for completeness by the principal investigator who also supervised the entire study process. Thereafter, the data were transferred to Microsoft Excel to create a database, which was then exported to R statistical software (R: R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria)22 for analysis. This was performed by the principal investigator assisted by a statistician.

Descriptive analyses were used in the analyses of the individual sociodemographic and socioeconomic characteristics. Locational data were generated cumulatively for each stopover as given by the truck drivers during the interviews. Geographical coordinates of the listed stopovers were extracted using the Google Earth pro tool.23 Geospatial modelling techniques, using R statistical software packages for spatial mapping,24 were employed. This generated shapefiles that were overlaid a map of Kenya using R statistical software22,24 to generate sexual network location maps along the Northern Corridor highway.25 The number of days spent in a given town along the highway by each LDTD was added to generate cumulative person-days for the different stopovers. The stopovers in which there was an elevated number of sexual network traits compared with others were mapped as hotspots. The hotspots were coded with a red colour in the maps to distinguish them from other stopovers.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants

A total of 296 LDTDs participated in the study, representing a response rate of 100%. The mean age of the respondents was 38.4 years, while the range was 24–57 years. The majority (78.7%) of study participants were Kenyans, while the others were from neighbouring East African countries. Most study participants (65.9%) had attained secondary school education, with only 2.0% having achieved a college education. Altogether, 80.4% were married, while in terms of religion, most (78.0%) of the respondents were Christians (Table 1).

Distribution of the sexual network locations used by LDTDs

LDTDs reported a total of 42 highway stopovers along the Northern Corridor highway. The highway stopovers extend along the Northern Corridor highway from the Kenyan coastal city of Mombasa to the Kenya–Uganda border points. The stopovers are distributed as follows: three in Kwale, two in Kilifi, three in Taita Taveta, 13 in Makueni, two in Machakos (Nairobi Metropolitan), six in Nakuru, three in Uasin Gishu, two in Kericho, four in Nandi, three in Bungoma and one in Busia County, Kenya (Figure 1).

Map showing reported stopovers along the Northern Corridor highway, Kenya, 2020.

Cumulative person-days spent by study participants per stopover

The average cumulative number of person-days spent by LDTDs per stopover was 30.6. The median cumulative days per stopover was 18, with a minimum of 2 and a maximum of 184 days. Four clusters of hotspot stopovers (>58 cumulative number of person-days) were identified as follows: coastal cluster with hotspots in Kilifi and Taita Taveta, one cluster in Machakos (Nairobi Metropolitan) with two hotspots, another in Nakuru with one hotspot and the Kenya–Uganda border cluster with two hotspots in Busia and Bungoma counties. There were seven other stopovers, in which study participants spent 21–58 cumulative person-days. In 23 stopovers, there were <20 cumulative person-days reported during the 7 days (Figure 2).

Map showing the cumulative number of person-days spent by long-distance truck drivers per stopover.

Number of sexual interactions per stopover

Three clusters of hotspot stopovers for sexual interactions (≥13 interactions) were identified as follows: coastal cluster with one hotspot in Kilifi, Machakos (Nairobi Metropolitan) cluster with one hotspot and the Kenya–Uganda border cluster with two hotspots in Bungoma and Busia. There were four stopovers in which 9–12 sexual interactions were reported. In 10 stopovers, there were no sexual interactions reported at all. In the remaining 24 stopovers, 1–8 sexual interactions were reported (Figure 3).

Map showing the number of sexual interactions per stopover.

The average number of sexual interactions per stopover

The average number of sexual interactions (duration per stopover divided by the number of sexual interactions reported in that specific stopover) per stopover was 5.7. Two clusters of hotspots (13 and an above-average number of sexual interactions) were identified in the following regions: the coastal cluster with hotspots in Kilifi and Taita Taveta and the other cluster in Kericho with two hotspots. In 11 stopovers, there were 7–12 sexual interactions, whereas, in 17 stopovers, there were ≤6 sexual interactions reported (Figure 4). In the remaining 10 stopovers, no sexual interactions were reported at all.

Map showing the average number of sexual interactions per stopover.

Casual and regular sexual partners interacted with per stopover

The average number of casual and regular sexual partners engaged by LDTDs per a given stopover over the 7 days was 4 and 0.9, respectively. One cluster of hotspots for casual sexual partners (≥11 partners) was identified at the Kenya–Uganda international border. Six clusters of hotspots for regular sexual partners (≥3) were situated in the following regions: coastal cluster with one hotspot in Kilifi, four clusters in Makueni, Machakos (Nairobi Metropolitan), Nakuru and Uasin Gishu, with one hotspot each, and the Kenya–Uganda cluster with two hotspots. In eight clusters of stopovers, 6–10 sexual interactions with casual partners were reported. There were 21 stopovers in which study participants reported sexual interactions with 1–5 casual partners. In the remaining 11 stopovers, there were no reported sexual interactions between LDTDs and casual partners. In nine stopovers, there were interactions with 1–2 regular partners, while in the remaining 26, LDTDs had no regular sexual partners (Figure 5).

Map showing the number of casual and regular sexual partners interacted with per stopover.

The average number of sexual acts per sexual interaction per stopover

The average number of sexual acts per interaction was 2.1. Six hotspots were identified in which ≥2 sexual acts per sexual interaction were reported. Five clusters of hotspots (>2 sexual acts per sexual interaction) were situated in the following regions: one in Machakos (Nairobi Metropolitan) with one hotspot, Makueni cluster with two hotspots, Nakuru cluster with one hotspot, Uasin Gishu with one hotspot and the last one in Nandi with one hotspot. In 26 stopovers, an average of 1–2 sexual acts per sexual interaction was reported (Figure 6). In the remaining 10 stopovers, there were no sexual interactions reported, thus no sexual acts.

Discussion

This study aimed at identifying the spatial distribution of the sexual networks among LDTDs. The study revealed that clusters of sexual network hotspots were restricted to the stopovers situated proximally to the major urban areas, and the international border points of Kenya and Uganda. Further, the findings revealed a significant variation in the spatial distribution of key components of the sexual networks among LDTDs, including the type of sexual partners, sexual interactions, number of sexual acts and preferred highway stopovers. The variability in the distribution of sexual networks among LDTDs highlights the need for varied HIV/STI-preventive services along the Northern Corridor highway. This implies that different stopovers along the Northern Corridor highway need spatially targeted HIV/STI-preventive interventions15 to effectively curb the spread of HIV and STIs among LDTDs and their sexual partners.

Our study found that the intensity of different traits of sexual networks like the number of sexual acts and frequency of sexual interactions differed significantly in some highway stopovers. This suggests that the geographic distribution of sexual risk behaviours could predict the intensity of HIV prevalence in different regions. This is consistent with the findings from studies in sub-Saharan African countries26 and another in Malawi,27 which found that sexual risk behaviours could predict the intensity of HIV prevalence in different regions. Thus, the hotspots of sexual networks identified in our study may equally suggest stopovers that have significant levels of HIV and STI transmission among LDTDs and their sexual partners. Therefore, intensified HIV testing services, STI screening and treatment are needed in the hotspots that were identified in this study.

The study revealed 42 highway stopovers distributed along the 900-km long Northern Corridor highway, comparable with a previous study,8 which established 39 highway stopovers on the same highway. Literature indicates that highway stopovers among LDTDs naturally arise in response to the need for food and other refreshments, vehicle maintenance such as tire change for their freight trucks, accommodation and soliciting for commercial sex.28 Interestingly, none of the 42 highway stopovers were reported in Mombasa, Nairobi or Kisumu cities, similar to the previous study performed on the highway.8 It is possible that the fact that major cities are the major destination points for most imported goods transported from Mombasa's port city disincentivizes LDTDs in using them as stopovers.

The hotspots with the highest cumulative number of person-days spent by LDTDs in a specific stopover were approximately 30 km proximal to major cities and towns such as Nairobi, Nakuru, Eldoret and the Kenya–Uganda international border points. The choice of highway stopovers among LDTDs could be influenced by the availability of sex workers, security and affordable accommodation facilities all available in the outskirts of these towns and cities. Furthermore, the long period of days spent by LDTDs waiting for coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) clearance at the Kenya–Uganda international border towns may explain the hotspot clusters at those points.29 Therefore, stakeholders involved in HIV programming may find an opportunity to offer more awareness on safe sexual behaviours and preventive services at stopovers where LDTDs spent longer durations.

One of the four hotspots in which the highest number of sexual interactions were reported (≥13 sexual interactions per stopover) is located in the outskirts of Nairobi city. Given the close link between risky sexual behaviours and the risk of HIV infection, our findings may explain the high prevalence of HIV reported in peri-urban and densely populated regions of Kenya cities that are home to many settled FSWs who avoid the very expensive urban life.17,27 To corroborate our hypothesis, distance to major urban areas in Malawi predicted high HIV prevalence.16

Notably, two other hotspots in which the highest number of sexual interactions were reported (≥13 sexual interactions per stopover) are located at the Kenya–Uganda international border towns. Our findings are consistent with studies carried out in Zambia and Tanzania, showing that delays at the international border points might create an enabling environment for sexual interactions among LDTDs and their sexual partners.30,31 Additionally, a study in Bangladesh found that many LDTDs engaged in sexual interactions with regular and casual sexual partners at the international border points.32

In contrast to the highest number of sexual interactions being reported in the peri-urban and densely populated outskirts of cities, four hotspots characterised by average sexual interactions per stopover were located away from the densely populated and major urban areas. The distribution of the average number of sexual acts per sexual interaction per stopover also follows a similar pattern. This implies that the mobility of LDTDs may lead to the sustained introduction of HIV and associated behaviours from regions of high to low prevalence.28

The distribution of hotspots of casual sexual partners was restricted to the Kenya–Uganda border points. Research shows that LDTDs could spend as long as 10 days at the Kenya–Uganda border points waiting for COVID-19 test results during the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic.29 Delays at the Kenya–Uganda border points meant there was more time for LDTDs to establish casual sexual partnerships, contributing to casual partners' hotspots at those points. The distribution of hotspots of regular sexual partners was widely spread across the Northern Corridor highway. The regularity of travel among LDTDs along the highway could mean regularity of sexual partners, hence the hotspots of regular sexual partners. Because this study involved participants from all of the East African countries, and that these countries share almost similar socioeconomic characteristics, the findings on spatial variability in the distribution of the sexual networks among LDTDs are generalizable to other segments of the Northern Corridor highway.

Study limitations

Because our study was cross-sectional, we cannot draw causal-effect inferences from our findings. The study relied on self-reporting from the study participants, hence there is likely to be recall bias in the responses given. To minimise the effect of recall bias, we limited the history of sexual networks among LDTDs to the week preceding the interview.

Conclusion

On the Northern Corridor highway, stopovers situated proximal to major urban areas, as well as those at the international border points, frequently serve as sexual network hotspots among LDTDs and their sexual partners. In addition, there exists a significant variation in the spatial distribution of key components of the sexual networks among LDTDs, including the type of sexual partners, sexual interactions, number of sexual acts and preferred highway stopovers. Therefore, healthcare stakeholders in Kenya should enhance spatially targeted HIV/STI-preventive services for LDTDs and their sexual partners in the sexual network hotspots identified in this study.
