Severe Aortic Regurgitation Due to Quadricuspid Aortic Valve
Authors: Aya Elalfy, Santiago F Galeano-Lovera, Rana Rashwan, Mays Ali, Awais Malik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCase SummaryQuadricuspid aortic valve is a rare congenital malformation with prevalence of <0.05%, often manifesting in mid- to late adulthood with aortic regurgitation (AR). We present a case of a 60-year-old man with chronic severe AR due to phenotype F quadricuspid aortic valve and mild to moderate functional mitral regurgitation. Cardiac catheterization revealed multivessel coronary artery disease. The patient underwent successful surgical aortic valve replacement with a bioprosthesis and 2-vessel coronary artery bypass graft surgery. Echocardiography at 1-month follow-up showed improved left ventricular volumes, normal aortic bioprosthesis, and trivial mitral regurgitation. This case highlights the role of echocardiography in diagnosing quadricuspid aortic valve and guiding management.Take-Home MessagesQuadricuspid aortic valve is a rare cause of AR that can be readily diagnosed on echocardiography. Surgical aortic valve replacement is an effective treatment for severe symptomatic AR due to quadricuspid aortic valve.Key words: acute heart failure, aortic regurgitation, aortic valve, coronary artery bypass graft, diastolic heart failure, echocardiography, valve replacement

Case Summary

A 60-year-old man with hypertension, hyperlipidemia, chronic kidney disease stage 3a, and a 30-pack-year smoking history presented with 6 months of progressive dyspnea, orthopnea, and leg swelling. On presentation, his blood pressure was 180/50 mm Hg, heart rate was 76 beats/min, and respiratory rate was 20 breaths/min. Physical examination was remarkable for an early decrescendo diastolic murmur at the right sternal border, bibasilar lung crackles, and 1+ bilateral lower extremity pitting edema. N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide was significantly elevated at 24,939 pg/mL, and high-sensitivity troponin T was mildly elevated and nondynamic (from 18 ng/L to 19 ng/L). Electrocardiogram showed normal sinus rhythm with minimal left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy and ST-segment and T-wave changes (Supplemental Figure 1). Chest x-ray revealed cardiomegaly and pulmonary edema (Supplemental Figure 2). The patient was admitted for acute heart failure, and intravenous diuresis was started.

On admission, transthoracic echocardiogram demonstrated a severely enlarged LV (LV internal dimension in diastole 67 mm and volume index 180 mL/m2) or LV with preserved ejection fraction at 56%. Quadricuspid aortic valve was noted with a central coaptation defect causing aortic regurgitation (AR) and holodiastolic flow reversals in the descending thoracic aorta (Figure 1F). Transesophageal echocardiogram confirmed these findings (Figures 1A and 1C, Videos 1 and 2) with a central AR jet occupying >50% of the LV outflow tract (Figure 1D, Video 3) and pressure half-time of 314 ms (Figure 1E). Effective regurgitant orifice area as measured by planimeter was 0.31 cm2 (Figure 1B). Together, these findings were consistent with severe AR.1,2 Mild to moderate functional mitral regurgitation (MR) was also present.

(A) Transesophageal echocardiogram showing midesophageal short-axis view of quadricuspid aortic valve in diastole with 4 distinct cusps and a central coaptation defect. (B) Two-dimensional effective regurgitant orifice showing an area of 0.31 cm2 as measured by a planimeter. (C) Long-axis transesophageal echocardiogram image highlighting the central coaptation defect in diastole. (D) Color Doppler showing central aortic regurgitation jet occupying >50% of left ventricular outflow tract. (E) Continuous wave Doppler across the aortic valve demonstrating pressure half-time of 314 ms. (F) Pulsed wave Doppler in the descending thoracic aorta showing holodiastolic flow reversals.

The most likely explanation for this presentation was heart failure exacerbation secondary to chronic severe AR from the quadricuspid aortic valve. Other differential diagnoses included ischemic and/or end-stage hypertensive cardiomyopathy. Mild to moderate MR was not thought to be sufficient to contribute to LV dysfunction.

As part of the heart team approach, the cardiothoracic surgery team was consulted. Coronary angiography revealed 30% left main and 60% proximal left anterior descending artery disease with fractional flow reserve of 0.78 and instantaneous wave-free ratio of 0.76. The right posterolateral branch had 70% stenosis. The patient underwent surgical aortic valve replacement with a 25-mm Inspiris bioprosthetic valve (Edwards Lifesciences). He underwent concomitant 2-vessel coronary artery bypass graft surgery with the left internal mammary artery to left anterior descending artery and a saphenous vein graft to right posterolateral branch, along with left atrial appendage ligation. Postoperative transesophageal echocardiogram showed no prosthetic or periprosthetic regurgitation (Videos 4 and 5).3

At 1-month follow-up, the patient was recovering well and denied symptoms of heart failure. Electrocardiogram showed normal sinus rhythm and LV hypertrophy with repolarization abnormality (Supplemental Figure 3). Transthoracic echocardiogram revealed improvement in LV size (LV internal dimension in diastole 56 mm, volume index 129 mL/m2), stable ejection fraction 54%, normally functioning aortic bioprosthesis, and trivial MR. Chest x-ray showed cardiomegaly without pulmonary congestion and left pleural effusion Supplemental Figure 4. The patient was advised to resume normal activities and start cardiac rehabilitation. He continued to be asymptomatic at 6-month follow-up visit.
