Diagnostic familiarity with osteochondroma among primary physicians: a retrospective analysis of 195 referred cases
Authors: Manabu Hoshi, Masanari Aono, Yoshitaka Ban
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOsteochondroma or exostosis is the most common benign bone tumor and is typically diagnosed based on its characteristic radiographic features. While it is generally familiar to orthopaedic surgeons, the level of diagnostic familiarity among primary physicians remains unclear.Materials and methodsThis retrospective study reviewed 195 patients diagnosed with osteochondroma/exostosis who were referred to a tertiary orthopaedic oncology center. Inclusion criteria included radiological confirmation of osteochondroma/exostosis and the availability of referral documents. Diagnostic accuracy was assessed by classifying initial impressions as “osteochondroma/exostosis” or “other diagnoses.” Primary physicians were categorized by institutional affiliation (hospital or clinic) and specialty (orthopaedic or non-orthopaedic). Temporal analysis showed no significant improvement over time: 50.0% in Period 1 and 62.4% in Period 2 (p = 0.22).ConclusionsDiagnostic familiarity with osteochondroma/exostosis among primary physicians was moderate but varied by anatomical site and physician background. Introduction

Osteochondroma or exostosis is the most common benign bone tumor, typically arising during childhood or adolescence [1]. It predominantly affects the metaphyseal regions of long bones, with the distal femur, proximal tibia, and proximal humerus being the most frequent sites [2]. Radiographically, it appears as a well-demarcated bony outgrowth that maintains corticomedullary continuity with the host bone (Fig. 1)—a hallmark finding recognized by the WHO classification [3].

Epidemiologically, osteochondroma/exostosis account for approximately 20 to 50% of all benign bone lesions [4]. Although often asymptomatic and discovered incidentally, they may become symptomatic due to mechanical irritation, cosmetic deformity, or compression of adjacent neurovascular structures, particularly when located near joints [5].

In most cases, plain radiography is sufficient for diagnosis due to their characteristic appearances. Osteochondroma/exostosis is therefore a familiar entity in general orthopaedic practice. These patients are occasionally referred to tertiary centers specializing in orthopaedic oncology, often due to limited diagnostic familiarity and to difficulty in follow-up from primary physicians.

To date, few studies have systematically assessed the level of diagnostic familiarity regarding osteochondroma/exostosis among non-specialist physicians. The present study aims to address this familiarity by retrospectively reviewing referral documents accompanying osteochondroma or exostosis cases referred to our institution.

Patients and methods

A total of 230 patients diagnosed with osteochondroma or exostosis between September 2005 and December 2024 were retrospectively identified from our institutional database. Among them, 35 patients were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria: lack of available medical records, missing radiographic images, or absence of referral documents at the initial visit. After applying the predefined inclusion criteria—(1) availability of medical records and radiographic images, (2) radiological diagnosis confirmed by three board-certified orthopaedic oncologists, and (3) accessible referral documents submitted by primary physicians—195 patients were finally included for comparative analysis.

Demographic and clinical data were extracted from the medical records, including age, sex, presenting symptoms, anatomical location of the lesion, number of lesions, presence of multiple hereditary exostoses (MHE), laterality, referring institution type (clinic or hospital), and medical specialty of the primary physician.

In this study, diagnostic familiarity in this study refers to the referring physician’s documented recognition of osteochondroma in the referral letter. To evaluate the familiarity of primary physicians with osteochondroma or exostosis, we retrospectively reviewed medical records of patients who were referred to our institution with a correctly suspected diagnosis of osteochondroma or exostosis. The initial primary physicians were classified according to their affiliated institution (hospital or clinic) and their specialty (orthopaedic surgeon or non-orthopaedic surgeon). The initial diagnostic impression made by these physicians was recorded and categorized as either “osteochondroma/exostosis” or “non-osteochondroma/exostosis.” “Unqualified bone tumor” was categorized as unfamiliar, because it does not reflect recognition of osteochondroma/exostosis specifically.

In this study, a clinic was defined as a community-based medical facility primarily offering outpatient care, typically staffed by a limited number of healthcare professionals and equipped with fewer than 20 beds. A hospital was defined as a larger medical institution with multiple clinical departments and a minimum capacity of 20 inpatient beds.

To evaluate whether diagnostic patterns changed over time, we performed a temporal subgroup analysis by dividing the study period into two intervals: Period 1 (2005–2014) and Period 2 (2015–2024). Diagnostic familiarity rate was compared between these two periods using the same criteria described above.

This study was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Osaka City General Hospital. This study was a retrospective chart review; thus, consent for participation was waived, and approval of this waiver was obtained by the institutional review board of Osaka City General Hospital.

Statistical analyses

Fisher’s exact probability test was performed to statistically assess the relationship between physician characteristics and diagnostic accuracy. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and analyses were performed using Excel Statistics for Windows (version 2025; SSRI Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

The most common presenting symptom was pain, reported in 91 cases (46.7%), followed by palpable mass in 45 cases (23.1%), deformity in 30 cases (15.4%), and incidental detection in 22 cases (11.3%).

The anatomical distribution of lesions included the femur (57 cases), tibia/fibula (57), rib (18), humerus (12), radius/ulna (12), fingers (12), and toes (11). Multiple lesions were identified in 64 patients (32.8%), and multiple hereditary exostoses were diagnosed in 20 patients (10.3%).

Primary physicians were affiliated with hospitals in 73 cases (37.4%) and clinics in 122 cases (62.6%). The majority of referrals were made by orthopaedic surgeons (161 cases, 82.6%), followed by pediatricians (11 cases, 5.6%).

The familiarity with osteochondroma/exostosis varied significantly depending on the anatomical location (Fig. 4). The radius/ulna had the highest familiarity rate at 72.7%, followed closely by the tibia/fibula (69.0%) and the femur (68.4%). These long bones were generally associated with higher levels of familiarity among primary physicians. Moderate familiarity was observed for the humerus (58.3%), whereas the rib and finger regions showed substantially lower familiarity rates, both at 33.3%. The toe had the lowest familiarity rate at 27.3%. These results suggest that osteochondromas/exostoses occurring in major long bones are more familiar to primary physicians, while those in smaller or less typical sites such as the toes, fingers, and ribs tend to be less well known.

The primary physicians affiliated with hospitals demonstrated a higher likelihood of correctly diagnosing osteochondroma compared to those practicing in clinics (p < 0.05). Moreover, diagnostic accuracy was significantly greater among orthopaedic surgeons than among non- orthopaedic physicians (p < 0.01). These findings suggest that both the institutional setting and the physician’s specialty play a critical role in the accurate clinical suspicion of osteochondroma/exostosis.

A temporal comparison of diagnostic familiarity revealed no significant difference between the two time periods. In Period 1 (2005–2014), diagnostic familiarity was observed in 15 of 30 cases (50.0%), whereas in Period 2 (2015–2024), familiarity was documented in 103 of 165 cases (62.4%). However, this difference was not statistically significant according to Fisher’s exact test (p = 0.22), indicating that referral patterns and diagnostic recognition did not substantially change over the 19-year study period. The unequal distribution of cases between Period 1 and Period 2 is attributable to a gradual increase in referral volume during the later study period. This increase likely reflects institutional expansion and strengthened regional referral networks, rather than changes in diagnostic criteria or study design.

Discussion

This study investigated the diagnostic familiarity with osteochondroma/exostosis by retrospectively analyzing referral documents from a large cohort of patients. Overall, diagnostic familiarity was moderately high, with over half of the cases accurately identified as osteochondroma or exostosis by primary physicians. However, familiarity varied substantially depending on anatomical site and institutional setting, indicating room for improvement in recognition among non-specialist physicians.

The diagnostic familiarity rate was 60.5%, indicating that nearly 40% of referral documents lacked a specific or correct diagnostic impression of osteochondroma/exostosis. While osteochondroma osteochondroma/exostosis is a commonly encountered and radiographically distinct benign bone tumor in orthopedic practice, our findings suggest that familiarity among primary physicians, particularly those outside orthopaedic specialties, tends to be limited, which may be understandable given their relatively infrequent exposure to bone lesions in daily practice [6, 7]. Although only two cases were referred with a suspicion of malignancy, such as osteosarcoma, limited familiarity can still lead to unnecessary anxiety [8].

Osteochondroma/exostosis typically presents as a pedunculated or sessile bony outgrowth with corticomedullary continuity, features that are generally diagnostic on plain radiographs [6, 9]. However, correct interpretation requires an understanding of both imaging findings and clinical context. While orthopaedic surgeons are more likely to recognize these patterns due to routine exposure, general practitioners and pediatricians may use broader or vague descriptors such as “bone tumor” or “bony protrusion,” reflecting limited exposure to musculoskeletal tumors during training [10].

One of the most striking findings of this study was the significant variation in familiarity depending on lesion location. Osteochondromas/exostoses arising in long bones—especially the femur, tibia/fibula, and radius/ulna—were more frequently recognized, with diagnostic familiarity exceeding 68%. In contrast, familiarity rates dropped markedly for lesions in the toes, fingers, and ribs, with the toe showing the lowest recognition rate at 27.3%. This discrepancy likely reflects the more common involvement of long bones in osteochondroma/exostosis and the greater visibility of such cases in educational materials and clinical practice [11, 12]. Conversely, osteochondroma/exostosis in atypical locations may present with non-specific or misleading symptoms—such as digital deformity or rib discomfort—that are more likely to be misattributed to trauma, arthropathy, or soft tissue masses [11, 13]. Additionally, radiographs of the hands and feet often pose interpretation challenges due to overlapping anatomy or smaller lesion size [10].

The institutional setting of the primary physician was also significantly associated with diagnostic accuracy. Physicians affiliated with hospitals demonstrated a higher rate of correct diagnosis than those in clinics. Several factors may account for this difference, including greater access to diagnostic imaging, consultation with orthopaedic or radiology specialists, and a higher volume of musculoskeletal cases in hospital settings [14]. Hospital-based physicians are also more likely to participate in multidisciplinary case discussions or tumor board conferences, which may further enhance their familiarity with benign bone tumors and their characteristic radiographic patterns. In contrast, clinic-based physicians—especially those in community practices—often operate with limited imaging modalities and may face broader, non-specialized case loads, reducing their familiarity with rare orthopaedic conditions [14, 15].

These observations highlight potential targets for improving diagnostic accuracy among non-specialist physicians. Educational strategies may include the development of standardized referral guidelines, imaging-based teaching modules, and decision-support tools that assist primary physicians in distinguishing benign bone tumors from other musculoskeletal conditions. Additionally, incorporating bone tumor pattern recognition into general orthopaedic and primary care training programs may help reinforce familiarity with common benign lesions such as osteochondroma/exostosis. Such measures may streamline referral pathways, reduce unnecessary investigations, and support more timely and appropriate specialist evaluation.

Most osteochondromas/exostoses are benign, and even when not explicitly recognized, physicians still tend to refer patients with bone lesions—thus treatment delay is uncommon. Nevertheless, misinterpretation as malignant or infectious disease often leads to unnecessary imaging (CT/MRI) or referrals to inappropriate departments, increasing psychological and financial burden. Missed or unrecognized lesions are difficult to quantify in a referral-based study, but literature suggests that unrecognized osteochondromas may be observed without appropriate follow-up, occasionally leading to delayed detection of complications (e.g., fracture, neurovascular impingement, bursa formation) [1, 2]. The diagnostic familiarity matters not because outcomes worsen, but because diagnostic uncertainty increases unnecessary tests, referrals, and patient anxiety.

The temporal analysis showed no significant change in diagnostic familiarity between Period 1 (2005–2014) and Period 2 (2015–2024) (50.0% vs. 62.4%, p = 0.22). This suggests that, despite advances in imaging and broader dissemination of musculoskeletal knowledge, the diagnostic performance of primary physicians has remained largely stable over time. The lack of improvement may reflect the low frequency with which non-specialists encounter bone tumors, as well as the continued reliance on plain radiography—which has not changed substantially during the study period—as the primary diagnostic tool. These findings indicate that passive, time-dependent changes are insufficient, and targeted educational interventions are needed to meaningfully improve diagnostic familiarity.

Another noted observation in this study was the dual usage of the terms “exostosis” and “osteochondroma.” In our cohort, both terms were frequently used by primary physicians, with “exostosis” appearing slightly more often. Although both refer to the same pathological entity, their usage may reflect differing levels of diagnostic familiarity or educational background. “Osteochondroma” is the preferred term in academic and orthopedic literature and is used in WHO classifications [3]. The World Health Organization specifically recommends the use of “osteochondroma” rather than “exostosis” as the appropriate terminology [3]. In contrast, “exostosis” is a more generic and less specific term that is often used in clinical practice or in older medical educational contexts [2, 3]. Its usage may indicate a less precise diagnostic impression or unfamiliarity with formal medical nomenclature. However, it should be noted that the use of terms such as “subungual exostosis” is well established in clinical practice and refers to a distinct pathological entity; therefore, such terminology is appropriate and not subject to the same standardization concerns.

From a clinical perspective, limited diagnostic familiarity may still have disadvantages for patients, even when referral to a specialist ultimately occurs. Diagnostic uncertainty can lead to unnecessary imaging studies, referrals to inappropriate departments, and increased patient anxiety. In addition, a lack of disease-specific recognition may delay appropriate counseling regarding prognosis, follow-up strategies, and potential complications such as pain provocation, neurovascular compression, or bursa formation. Although most patients in the present study were eventually referred without substantial treatment delay, these findings suggest that improving diagnostic familiarity among primary physicians could reduce clinical inefficiency and patient burden, thereby providing a rationale for enhanced musculoskeletal education.

This study has several limitations. First, it was retrospective in design and limited to a single institution, which may affect generalizability. Second, the diagnostic terminology used in referral letters may not fully reflect the referring physician’s level of understanding, particularly if terms were simplified for communication purposes. We now clearly state that this operational definition may underestimate true familiarity, and that diagnostic terminology in referral letters may not fully reflect the physician’s actual knowledge or clinical reasoning. Third, the study did not evaluate the long-term outcomes of patients based on initial diagnostic accuracy. Fourth, the findings should not be generalized to healthcare systems with different referral structures. While healthcare systems vary, the phenomenon of diagnostic unfamiliarity with benign bone tumors among non-specialists is reported internationally, suggesting broader relevance. Fifth, this study may also underestimate the true level of diagnostic familiarity, as our cohort included only patients who were referred to our institution. Benign osteochondromas that were correctly recognized and managed locally by primary physicians were not captured, and therefore the actual familiarity rate in the broader clinical community is likely higher than reported. Furthermore, the referral-based design inherently introduces selection bias, as physicians who were confident in their diagnosis may have chosen not to refer, whereas those uncertain about the lesion were more likely to seek specialist evaluation. Another limitation of this study is that physician-specific experience could not be assessed. Because referral documents often included only the referring physician’s name without sufficient identifiers, we were unable to evaluate whether diagnostic familiarity was influenced by individual case volume. Further multicenter or prospective investigations are needed to determine whether repeated exposure to osteochondroma/exostosis improves diagnostic recognition among non-orthopedic physicians. Despite these limitations, our findings provide valuable insight into diagnostic patterns and highlight potential areas for educational improvement.

In conclusion, while familiarity with osteochondroma/exostosis among primary physicians was moderately high, it varied significantly based on lesion location and physician background. Osteochondromas/exostosis in typical long bone sites were more readily identified, whereas lesions in atypical locations were often under-recognized. Hospital-based physicians and orthopaedic surgeons demonstrated greater diagnostic accuracy. These findings highlight the need for enhanced musculoskeletal education among primary care providers to improve early recognition, reduce unnecessary testing, and streamline referrals for benign bone tumors.
