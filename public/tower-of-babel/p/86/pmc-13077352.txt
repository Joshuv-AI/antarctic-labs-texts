Association between albumin-corrected anion gap and in-hospital mortality in ICU patients with lung cancer: a retrospective cohort study based on the MIMIC database
Authors: Yunfan Wang, Lijie Yang, Miaojuan Zhu, Xiu Shi, Runhua Xiong, Hanxiang Nie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis retrospective cohort study investigates the association between albumin-corrected anion gap (ACAG) and in-hospital mortality (IHM) among critically ill patients with lung cancer (LC).MethodsACAG was calculated using the formula: ACAG (mmol/L) = anion gap (mmol/L) + [4.4 − albumin (g/dL)] × 2.5, based on the first laboratory measurement within 72 hours of ICU admission. Data were extracted from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database, including 458 LC patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU). These patients were stratified by IHM status and ACAG levels into T1, T2, and T3 groups. Key variables were selected using cross-validated least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were employed to assess the relationship between ACAG and IHM. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were used to evaluate potential non-linear dose-response relationships.ResultsResults showed that higher ACAG levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of IHM when treated as a continuous variable [model 3: hazard ratio (HR) =1.077, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.032–1.123, P<0.001]. RCS analysis revealed a linear association between rising ACAG levels and increased risk of all-cause IHM (model 3: P for non-linearity =0.506). Kaplan-Meier survival curves indicated better cumulative survival in the low-ACAG group compared to the high-ACAG group. Subgroup analyses demonstrated consistent effects across various subgroups, with no significant interactions between ACAG and other covariates. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the findings.ConclusionsIn conclusion, elevated ACAG is independently associated with a higher risk of IHM in LC patients admitted to the ICU, suggesting its potential utility as a prognostic biomarker in this population. Clinically, integrating ACAG into routine ICU assessments may facilitate the early identification of high-risk patients, enabling more personalized metabolic monitoring and timely therapeutic interventions. These findings highlight the importance of acid-base and metabolic parameters in risk stratification for LC patients admitted to the ICU.Keywords: Albumin-corrected anion gap (ACAG), lung cancer (LC), in-hospital mortality (IHM), metabolic acidosis, Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV database (MIMIC-IV database)

Highlight box

Key findings

• This retrospective study of 458 intensive care unit (ICU) lung cancer patients from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database found that higher albumin-corrected anion gap (ACAG) levels were significantly associated with increased in-hospital mortality. Each unit increase in ACAG raised mortality risk by 10.2% in unadjusted models, and this association remained significant after multivariable adjustment [hazard ratio (HR) =1.048, P=0.019]. Kaplan-Meier analysis confirmed that higher ACAG levels correlated with lower survival rates.

What is known and what is new?

• It is known that ACAG reflects unmeasured anions and predicts mortality in conditions like stroke and sepsis. However, its role in ICU lung cancer patients was unexplored.

• This study newly establishes ACAG as an independent prognostic marker in this population.

• This work provides the first evidence linking elevated ACAG to poor outcomes in lung cancer patients admitted to the ICU, highlighting its potential as a simple, accessible biomarker for risk stratification.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Clinicians should consider integrating ACAG into the routine assessment of ICU lung cancer patients to identify high-risk individuals early. Future research should validate these findings prospectively and explore mechanisms linking ACAG to lung cancer pathophysiology.

Introduction

Lung cancer (LC) remains a prevalent malignancy across the globe and is a leading cause of cancer-associated death (1). In the intensive care unit (ICU), LC patients often experience a high risk of in-hospital mortality (IHM) due to disease complexity and multiple comorbidities (2,3). Previous studies (4-6) have identified several prognostic factors related to poor outcomes in LC patients and patients with thoracic or cardiovascular diseases admitted to the ICU, including respiratory comorbidities, poor performance status, need for mechanical ventilation or vasopressors, and elevated scores on the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation, Simplified Acute Physiology Score (SAPS), and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA). However, the aforementioned indicators mainly reflect clinical phenotypes and macroscopic organ function. Given that LC patients in the ICU frequently present with comorbid hypoalbuminemia due to tumor-related consumption and malnutrition (7,8), this pathological alteration often masks high anion gap (AG) metabolic acidosis, limiting the prognostic value of traditional markers such as lactate or the standard AG (9). In contrast, the ACAG adjusts for serum albumin levels. It not only more accurately reflects the presence of unmeasured anions but also effectively eliminates the underestimation of the AG caused by hypoalbuminemia, thereby objectively revealing the true severity of metabolic acidosis (10). Prior studies demonstrate that elevated ACAG values are positively related to a higher death rate among critically ill individuals with conditions such as acute ischemic stroke (AIS) (11) and acute pancreatitis (12), suggesting its potential as a prognostic marker of poor clinical outcomes. Despite these findings, the relationship between ACAG and the risk of IHM among LC patients admitted to the ICU is still unclear. This research seeks to examine the relationship between ACAG and risk of IHM among ICU patients with LC, and to determine whether ACAG can act as an independent marker for assessing clinical outcomes in this population, thereby informing treatment strategies and prognostic management (13). We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-aw-2340/rc).

Methods

Data source

The Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV [MIMIC-IV, version 3.1 (https://physionet.org/content/mimiciv/3.1/)] is a publicly available clinical database that contains anonymized health-associated information from patients admitted to the ICUs of the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2022. One author (Y.W.) of the present research successfully completed the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative Program and obtained access to the MIMIC-IV database (Certification Number: 68458001) and conducted data extraction. Given that all the MIMIC-IV data are anonymized, additional ethical approval and informed consent were not required (14). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Study population

Inclusion criteria encompassed: (I) individuals recorded in the MIMIC-IV database, (II) a primary diagnosis of LC identified by ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes, and (III) admission to the ICU (explicitly defining the study population as “critically ill”). Exclusion criteria encompassed: (I) age ≤18 years, (II) hospitalization duration less than 24 hours or more than 30 days, (III) missing or incomplete records of serum AG and albumin during ICU stay, and (IV) several ICU admissions (only data from the first admission were retained). After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 458 individuals were included (Figure 1).

Flowchart illustrating the patient selection process. ICU, intensive care unit; MIMIC-IV, Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV.

Data extraction

Structured query language (SQL) in PostgreSQL (version 17.4.1) was utilized to collect data from the MIMIC-IV database (13). The collected information included: personal history, vital signs, demographic data, laboratory parameters, LC-related pharmacotherapy, disease severity scores, comorbidities, life-sustaining interventions, admission and discharge times, and clinical outcomes. Demographics encompassed gender, body mass index (BMI), race, and age. Personal history is primarily focused on smoking history. Vital signs were defined as the mean values of diastolic blood pressure (DBP), systolic blood pressure (SBP), heart rate (HR), body temperature, and respiratory rate measured multiple times within the first 24 hours of ICU admission. LC-related pharmacotherapy comprised chemotherapy (platinum or non-platinum), targeted therapy (such as EGFR mutation, ALK rearrangement, BRAF V600E mutation, RET fusion, MET exon 14 skipping), anti-angiogenic agents, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors). Leveraging the database's precise timestamps, we reviewed all measurement records and selected the initial value obtained within 72 hours of admission for laboratory parameters with multiple assessments. Specific parameters included platelet count, hemoglobin, absolute lymphocyte count, white blood cell (WBC) count, absolute neutrophil count, albumin, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, AG, base excess, serum bicarbonate, serum potassium concentration, serum calcium concentration, serum chloride concentration, arterial blood lactate, blood pH, and blood urea nitrogen (BUN). Disease severity scores encompassed the Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) score, Acute Physiology Score III (APSIII), Logistic Organ Dysfunction System (LODS) score, SOFA, Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS), and SAPSII. Comorbidities, including hypertension, respiratory failure (RF), acute kidney injury (AKI), chronic kidney disease, and diabetes, were identified based on ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnostic codes. Life-sustaining interventions encompassed continuous renal replacement therapy and ventilation status, such as tracheostomy and various invasive or non-invasive oxygen delivery methods.

To minimize selection bias due to sample exclusion, missing data were handled proportionally. For variables with less than 20% missing data, multiple imputation was performed. Continuous variables with missing data exceeding 20% were converted into categorical variables based on reference ranges. To evaluate the potential bias introduced by imputation, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by comparing the distributions of variables between the original and imputed datasets using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Grouping and clinical outcome

The risk of IHM within 30 days after ICU admission among LC patients served as the primary clinical outcome. Individuals were classified into survival and non-survival groups accordingly (Figure 2). ACAG was calculated via the formula: ACAG (mmol/L) = AG (mmol/L) + [4.4 − albumin (g/dL)] × 2.5 (15). Individuals were divided into three groups based on the tertile distribution of maximum and minimum ACAG values (Figure 2): T1: ACAG ≤15.8 mmol/L (n=157); T2: 15.8< ACAG ≤19.2 mmol/L (n=148); T3: 19.2< ACAG ≤39.8 mmol/L (n=153). The follow-up period was determined as the time from hospital admission to discharge or the occurrence of the outcome.

Statistical analysis

Medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) were used to represent continuous variables, and between-group comparisons were conducted via the Mann-Whitney U test. Counts and percentages were used to represent categorical variables, and between-group comparisons were made via the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. To estimate the independent prognostic utility of ACAG for worse clinical outcomes among ICU patients with LC, three Cox regression models were established with stepwise adjustment for confounders. Model 1 included ACAG alone. Model 2 was further controlled for race, gender, and age. Model 3 was further controlled for covariates selected by the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, excluding those with multicollinearity. The proportional hazards assumption was tested and satisfied for all Cox regression models. ACAG was incorporated into the Cox models as both a categorical variable (T1–T3) and a continuous variable, and trend tests were carried out to assess potential dose-response relationships between categorical ACAG and death risk. To compare the predictive efficacy of the ACAG model with established prognostic scoring systems (including SOFA, OASIS, LODS, and APSIII) and the traditional metabolic marker (lactate), receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated. The area under the curve (AUC) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated. Furthermore, the DeLong test was employed to perform pairwise comparisons between the ROC curves of different models to evaluate the statistical significance of differences in predictive performance. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A potential nonlinear relationship between ACAG values and the risk of IHM was estimated through restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis. Kaplan-Meier (K-M) survival analysis was carried out to compare cumulative survival among ACAG groups. Subgroup analyses were carried out to examine whether the findings remained stable among populations with different characteristics, such as race, gender, age, and ventilation status. Data were analyzed via the R software (version 4.4.3). All P values were two-sided, and a P value less than 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Sensitivity analysis indicated no statistically significant differences between the original and imputed datasets (all P>0.9) (Table S1), confirming the robustness of the results. In total, 458 individuals were enrolled based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Of these, 205 (45%) were female, and 307 (67%) were White. The age range was 63–78 years, with a mean age of 70 years. As illustrated in Table 1, individuals were classified into a survival group (0) and a non-survival group (1) according to ICU mortality. Among the 458 individuals, 331 were enrolled in the survival group and 127 in the non-survival group, yielding a 30-day mortality rate of 27.7%. There were no significant differences in age (P=0.24) or sex distribution (P=0.31) between the two groups. In comparison to the survival group, the non-survival group exhibited a lower RF incidence but a significantly greater incidence of AKI (P<0.001), along with markedly lower albumin levels and mean SBP (P<0.001). Moreover, the non-survival group exhibited notably higher mean HR, laboratory parameters (BUN, WBC counts, and ACAG levels), and severity scores (SOFA, APSIII, OASIS, SAPSII, SIRS, and LODS) (P<0.001) (Table 1).

Predictor selection and construction of the proportional hazards model

LASSO regression was applied to all variables listed in Table 1, excluding age, gender, race, and ACAG, to further screen predictors and remove variables with multicollinearity. The largest λ within one standard error of the lowest mean cross-validation error was chosen as the optimal regularization parameter, specifically lambda.1se =0.04724618, corresponding to a minimum error at lambda.min =0.01066356. Regression coefficients of all variables were evaluated based on the optimal λ value. Eight variables with non-zero coefficients were ultimately retained for the construction of Cox model 3. As shown in Figure 3, these variables included albumin, ALP, OASIS, LODS, APSIII, SAPSII, ventilation status, and RF (Figure 3).

Variable selection by LASSO regression model using clinical data. (A) Coefficient profiles of 38 candidate variables; (B) tuning parameter (λ) selection via tenfold cross-validation in LASSO logistic regression. LASSO, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator.

As presented in Table 3, when ACAG was treated as a continuous variable, an increase in ACAG values was significantly linked to a greater risk of IHM in Cox model 1 [hazard ratio (HR) =1.102, 95% CI: 1.062–1.143, P<0.001]. This positive relationship was significant after adjustment for demographic factors in model 2 (HR =1.104, 95% CI: 1.062–1.147, P<0.001) and all potential confounders in model 3 (HR =1.077, 95% CI: 1.032–1.123, P<0.001). When ACAG levels were categorized into three groups (T1, T2, T3) with T1 as the reference, the risk of IHM was significantly increased in the highest ACAG group (T3) in the partially adjusted models (model 1 and model 2) with HRs of 2.112 and 2.023, respectively (P<0.01). The risk difference between T3 and T1 was statistically significant in model 3 (fully adjusted) (HR =1.683, P<0.05). Consistent results were observed in sensitivity analyses using clinically established and optimal cutoffs (Table S2). Furthermore, trend tests suggested a significant linear association between ACAG and the risk of IHM in models 1 and 2 (P<0.01). Although the linear trend was no longer significant in model 3, it remained statistically meaningful (P=0.036).

Predictive performance of ACAG compared with established prognostic indicators

To evaluate the prognostic value of ACAG relative to established scoring systems and lactate, ROC curve analysis and the DeLong test were performed (Figure S1, Table S3). The results indicated that the ACAG model achieved the highest AUC of 0.781 (95% CI: 0.733–0.828) among all evaluated metrics. The DeLong test further confirmed that the predictive performance of the ACAG model was significantly superior to that of the SOFA score (AUC =0.717, P=0.002) and lactate (AUC =0.637, P<0.001). Meanwhile, ACAG demonstrated predictive accuracy comparable to more complex scoring systems. No statistically significant differences were observed between ACAG and OASIS (AUC =0.760, P=0.49), LODS (AUC =0.751, P=0.25), or APSIII (AUC =0.747, P=0.17). These findings suggest that by correcting for the confounding effect of hypoalbuminemia on the AG, the simple and accessible metric of ACAG is more accurate than traditional lactate and non-inferior to complex physiological scores.

Subgroup analyses

Based on the Cox proportional hazards regression models, subgroup analyses based on race, gender, age, and ventilation status were carried out. A positive relationship between ACAG values and the risk of IHM among ICU patients with LC was found in several subgroups, including those aged >60 years, females, White patients, and those receiving invasive ventilation/tracheostomy, no ventilatory support, or oxygen therapy. There were no significant interactions between ACAG and the subgroup variables (Figure 6).

Discussion

This study reveals that elevated serum ACAG levels are independently associated with an increased risk of IHM among LC patients admitted to the ICU. This association remained robust after adjustment for potential confounders, where higher ACAG levels corresponded to progressively lower survival probabilities. Furthermore, the prognostic utility of ACAG was consistently observed across various subgroups. The associations were pronounced particularly in high-risk populations, such as patients aged >60 years and those requiring invasive mechanical ventilation. These results suggest that ACAG may serve as a sensitive indicator of severe metabolic and physiological derangement in this specific cohort.

The occurrence of acid–base imbalance in LC patients involves multiple pathophysiological mechanisms, including disruptions in metabolism, respiration, and renal function. LC cells predominantly rely on glycolysis rather than mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation for energy production, even in the presence of oxygen [the Warburg effect (16)]. This metabolic phenotype increases lactate production (17), resulting in metabolic acidosis. In ICU patients with advanced LC, the substantial tumor burden and vigorous local proliferation, as well as distant metastasis, may lead to tissue hypoxia (18), which further activates anaerobic glycolysis and elevates lactate generation. Local or systemic hypoxia stimulates the expression of hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), such as HIF-1α (19). This, in turn, exacerbates acidosis by upregulating lactate transporters such as monocarboxylate transporter 4 (20), inhibiting carbonic anhydrase and mitochondrial respiratory chain function, and altering fatty acid and amino acid metabolism. Moreover, pulmonary tumors can disrupt the structural integrity of the lungs’ gas exchange units and obstruct the bronchi, leading to ventilation-perfusion mismatch and reduced ventilatory capacity. These changes result in carbon dioxide retention and respiratory acidosis, further disturbing acid-base homeostasis. Additionally, certain LC therapies, such as cisplatin, are known to be nephrotoxic (21). These medications may cause AKI and impair the kidneys’ ability to excrete hydrogen ions and reabsorb bicarbonate, thereby inducing metabolic acidosis. Furthermore, in terminal ICU patients, cancer-related cachexia is marked by extensive catabolism of muscle and adipose tissue. This process leads to increased production of metabolic byproducts, such as amino acids and ketone bodies (22,23), which further contribute to metabolic acidosis. The ACAG reflects the difference between unmeasured anions (including lactate and ketone bodies) and unmeasured cations in the body (10). In conditions where organic acid accumulation (such as lactate or ketone bodies) exacerbates acidosis, a concomitant increase in ACAG is typically observed and often indicates worsening clinical outcomes. This may represent a key mechanism by which elevated ACAG levels reflect poor prognosis in ICU patients with LC.

In addition, infiltration of various inflammatory cells within the tumor microenvironment (24) (including tumor-associated macrophages and cancer-associated fibroblasts), along with the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (25), including IL-1β, TNF-α (13), and IL-6, may contribute to a chronic inflammatory state in LC patients (26). Critically ill patients often experience multiple infections that can further activate acute inflammatory responses (27). The exacerbation of inflammation, together with oxidative stress mediated by reactive oxygen species (28), is frequently associated with poor prognosis. Prior research has demonstrated that the severity of inflammation is negatively linked to serum albumin levels (29). Given the calculation formula of ACAG, decreased albumin levels can result in elevated ACAG. This may represent another potential mechanism linking high ACAG values to worse outcomes among ICU patients with LC.

Prior studies reveal that higher ACAG values are linked to elevated mortality risk among critically ill patients with various conditions, including liver cirrhosis with sepsis (30), AIS (11), acute pancreatitis (12), coronary artery bypass grafting (31), and AKI (32). Specifically regarding severe respiratory diseases, Mohan Giri (33) and Shidong Wang (34) independently observed a positive relationship between increased ACAG values and a greater risk of death among severely ill patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and ICU patients with asthma, respectively. Notably, the ACAG thresholds linked to increased mortality vary by disease. For instance, the cutoff was 18.95 mmol/L in ICU asthma patients, 18.3 mmol/L in AIS, 19.62 mmol/L in older patients with sepsis-induced or concurrent AKI (35), and reached 20.27 mmol/L in individuals with cardiac arrest (36). In the present study, the RCS analysis identified a threshold ACAG level of 17.47 mmol/L, which is slightly lower than those reported in other disease contexts. Several factors may account for this discrepancy. First, LC patients often experience chronic systemic inflammation (27) and immune dysregulation, making them susceptible to infections. These combined processes induce inflammatory responses and result in the production of large quantities of inflammatory mediators. Meanwhile, cancer progression causes substantial nutritional depletion (23), thereby leading to significantly reduced serum albumin levels. Since ACAG is corrected for albumin, a lower albumin level alone may elevate the ACAG value, even in the absence of notable changes in other anions. Second, LC patients are often affected by concurrent renal or hepatic dysfunction (37,38), particularly when treated with certain chemotherapeutic agents such as platinum compounds (39). These treatments may further impair albumin synthesis and increase albumin loss, ultimately exacerbating hypoalbuminemia. This, combined with the accumulation of lactate and other organic acids, may contribute to a lower calculated ACAG threshold in this patient population.

This research is the first to examine and establish the relationship between ACAG values and the risk of short-term IHM among ICU patients with LC. All data were derived from real-world clinical records, and rigorous statistical approaches were employed to enhance the robustness and reliability of the results. Nevertheless, some limitations must be noted. First, as a retrospective research, inherent limitations exist in the study design, including missing data, lack of randomization, and the inability to confirm causal relationships. Specifically, regarding sample selection, although 1,212 LC patients admitted to the ICU were identified, only 458 were included in this study based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Although we handled missing data to minimize selection bias, the potential for residual bias cannot be entirely ruled out. Secondly, the data were predominantly drawn from White patients in the United States, which might cause selection bias and restrict the generalizability of the conclusions. Thirdly, owing to data limitations within the MIMIC-IV database, several variables of interest, such as LC histological subtype, TNM staging, relevant gene mutations, detailed comprehensive treatment regimens, as well as dietary and lifestyle information, were unavailable and thus excluded from analysis. Finally, the sample size (n=458) was relatively limited, which may reduce the statistical power and external validity of the conclusions. To overcome these limitations, future studies should consider prospective cohort designs or randomized controlled trials to more rigorously validate these findings. Additionally, multicenter collaborations with greater sample sizes and more comprehensive variable inclusion are also recommended to enhance the representativeness and statistical robustness of the results.

Conclusions

This research revealed that elevated ACAG values were positively related to a greater risk of IHM among ICU patients with LC. This finding highlights the potential utility of ACAG in clinical practice. Future research is required to further elucidate the mechanistic function of ACAG in tumor-related pathophysiology and to investigate its value in informing optimized individualized therapeutic strategies.
