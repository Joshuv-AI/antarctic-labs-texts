The P-D-T anatomical classification system for supraclavicular artery perforator flap: technical optimization and clinical validation in 78 head and neck reconstructions
Authors: Jing Zhou, Tao Li, Yiming Ding, Tingyao Ma, Zheng Yang, Hongbo Xu, Xiaohong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe supraclavicular artery perforator (SAP) flap represents an excellent option for head and neck reconstruction; however, anatomical variability of the supraclavicular artery (SCA) impedes surgical standardization. This study aimed to establish a P-D-T (Pectoral-Deltoid-Trapezius) anatomical classification system based on vascular trajectory, validate the “point-line antegrade dissection” (PLAD) technique, and evaluate the impact of classification types on shoulder function and flap tolerance to prior radiotherapy.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 78 patients (80 flaps) undergoing SAP flap reconstruction between June 2016 and June 2025. Follow-up was censored on December 31, 2025. The P-D-T classification was established through cadaveric dissection of 20 hemibodies. Corrected pharyngocutaneous fistula rate was calculated among 65 digestive tract reconstructions. Radiation analysis included 59 patients (irradiated: 16; non-irradiated: 43). Shoulder function was assessed using Quick-DASH in 48 patients with minimum 6-month follow-up.ResultsThe 80 flaps were classified as Type I (Pectoral, 16.25%), Type II (Deltoid, 72.5%), and Type III (Trapezius, 11.25%). Overall flap survival was 98.75% (79/80), with corrected fistula rate of 6.2% (4/65). No significant difference in complication rates was observed between irradiated and non-irradiated groups (12.5% vs. 9.3%, P = 0.658). Quick-DASH scores differed significantly among types (P = 0.019); Type II showed higher scores than Type I (16.2 ± 7.3 vs. 9.1 ± 2.8, P = 0.017), but this difference (7.1 points) remained below the MCID threshold of 12–15 points.ConclusionsThe P-D-T classification combined with the PLAD technique enables precise SAP flap harvest. Preliminary findings support the feasibility of SAP flap in irradiated necks, demonstrating a complication profile comparable to non-irradiated cases, though validation in larger cohorts is warranted. Type II flaps demonstrate statistically significant but clinically mild shoulder functional impact.Keywords: Supraclavicular artery perforator flap, Anatomic Variation, Plastic Surgery Procedures, Radiotherapy, Shoulder

Introduction

Radical resection of head and neck malignancies frequently results in complex composite tissue defects requiring reconstruction to restore swallowing, phonation, and protection of vital structures [1]. Although free tissue transfer is regarded as the “gold standard,” its demanding requirements limit applicability in patients with prior radiotherapy, compromised vasculature, or poor general condition [2, 3]. The supraclavicular artery perforator (SAP) flap is a regional axial pattern flap that has regained clinical attention. According to the Gent consensus [4], Pallua and Wolter [5] proposed the term “SAP flap” as the nomenclature-compliant designation; the traditional term “supraclavicular artery island flap (SCAIF)” remains widely used and both refer to the same flap. First described by Lamberty in 1979 [6], early high necrosis rates led to abandonment until Pallua’s systematic anatomical studies in the 1990s enabled its “renaissance” [7, 8]. This flap offers thin pliable tissue, excellent color match, and elimination of microvascular anastomosis [9].

Two challenges impede widespread adoption. First, the supraclavicular artery (SCA) exhibits considerable anatomical variability [10, 11], and traditional “epaulette-style” design disregards vascular axis variations, risking ischemic necrosis. Second, previous studies lack refined shoulder function analysis based on anatomical classification [12], and systematic data on radiotherapy tolerance remain lacking.

Building upon our previous work proposing the “point-line antegrade dissection” (PLAD) technique [13] and evaluating shoulder function [14], this expanded study (2016–2025, 78 patients) aimed to: (1) establish the P-D-T (Pectoral-Deltoid-Trapezius) anatomical classification system; (2) validate PLAD technique safety; and (3) systematically compare shoulder function among classification types and analyze radiotherapy tolerance.

The P-D-T classification system proposed herein differs from previous anatomical descriptions of SCA variability (e.g., Abe et al. [9]) bin that it translates trajectory variations into an actionable, intraoperatively applicable classification with specific surgical implications for each type. Unlike Spiegel et al.‘s shoulder function assessment [12], which did not stratify outcomes by vascular anatomy, our study provides the first P-D-T–stratified functional analysis, enabling targeted rehabilitation recommendations.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical statement

This study employed a single-center retrospective cohort design comprising two components: an anatomical investigation and a clinical application study. All clinical data were collected retrospectively through review of medical records; P-D-T classification was assigned intraoperatively by the senior surgical team (X.H.C. and J.Z.) based on observed SCA trajectory. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University (Approval No.: TRECKY2023-045). For the clinical retrospective component, as it involved only review of existing medical records without identifiable personal information, the ethics committee granted a waiver of informed consent. This study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for reporting observational studies [15].

Study Population

Inclusion criteria: (1) Patients who underwent supraclavicular artery perforator (SAP) flap reconstruction for soft tissue defects or luminal stenosis resulting from head and neck malignancy resection or tumor-related complications (e.g., pharyngocutaneous fistula, esophageal stricture) between June 2016 and June 2025; (2) all procedures performed by the senior attending surgeon (X.H.C.) using the standardized “point-line antegrade dissection” (PLAD) technique for flap harvest; and (3) complete clinical data available, including operative records, pathology reports, and postoperative follow-up data. Follow-up was censored on December 31, 2025.

Exclusion criteria: (1) History of ipsilateral shoulder surgery or severe shoulder pathology (e.g., adhesive capsulitis, rotator cuff injury); (2) intraoperative discovery of supraclavicular artery absence or hypoplasia necessitating use of an alternative flap; and (3) incomplete clinical data.

The patient selection flowchart is presented in Fig. 1. A total of 78 patients with 80 flaps were ultimately enrolled, including 2 patients who underwent bilateral flap reconstruction. The mean follow-up duration for the entire cohort was 38.2 ± 23.8 months (median 33.5 months, IQR 19.1–52.6, range 6.3–100.2 months).

Anatomical study and establishment of the P-D-T classification system

Ten cadavers (20 hemibodies) fixed in 10% formalin solution were subjected to meticulous dissection. Specimens were positioned supine with the shoulder elevated and the head rotated toward the contralateral side. A skin incision was designed in the supraclavicular region 2 cm superior to the clavicle, followed by sequential incision through skin and superficial fascia. Dissection steps included: mobilization of the posterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle (SCM) with identification and preservation of the external jugular vein; identification of the transverse cervical artery trunk deep to the jugular venous angle and tracing of its branches to confirm the origin of the supraclavicular artery (SCA); and antegrade dissection of the SCA trunk to the subcutaneous tissue. The origin, external diameter, trunk length, and spatial relationship of the SCA to surrounding muscles (pectoralis major, deltoid, and trapezius) were measured and documented.

Based on the predominant muscular region traversed by the SCA trunk, the P-D-T classification system was established as follows: Type I (Pectoral): the SCA trunk courses inferomedially after crossing the clavicle, extending toward the pectoralis major surface; Type II (Deltoid): the SCA trunk courses posterolaterally via the acromion, extending toward the deltoid surface; Type III (Trapezius): the SCA trunk courses posteriorly along the anterior border of the trapezius or within its superficial layer.

“Point-line antegrade dissection” (PLAD) surgical technique

The core principle of this technique is “vessel-guided flap design,” which differs fundamentally from the traditional “flap-first, vessel-second” strategy. The procedure is performed as follows (Fig. 2):

A: Surface landmarks: localization of the transverse cervical vascular triangle and external jugular vein. B: Identification of the transverse cervical artery (yellow arrow). C: Antegrade dissection to locate the supraclavicular artery (yellow triangle: origin of the supraclavicular artery; blue arrow: transverse cervical artery; yellow arrow: supraclavicular artery and its trajectory). D: Projection of the supraclavicular artery trajectory (the “line”). E: Completion of flap islanding. F: Transfer of the island flap to the recipient site

Step 1: Localization of the “Point”

The patient is positioned supine with the shoulder elevated and the head turned to the contralateral side. An exploratory incision is made within the supraclavicular triangle (bounded by the posterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle, the superior border of the clavicle, and the omohyoid muscle). Under direct visualization, the transverse cervical artery is dissected and traced distally to locate the origin of the supraclavicular artery from the transverse cervical artery (the “point”). When multiple branches are present, the dominant trunk is identified based on vessel caliber and pulsation intensity.

Step 2: Determination of the “Line”

Following confirmation of the SCA origin, antegrade dissection is performed to expose approximately 1–2 cm of the SCA trunk and ascertain its initial trajectory. This direction is projected and marked on the skin surface; this line represents the true hemodynamic axis of the flap (the “line”). Intraoperative classification is then assigned based on the observed SCA trajectory.

Step 3: Dynamic design and flap harvest

The skin paddle is designed centered on the established vascular axis, with dimensions tailored to the recipient-site defect configuration, ensuring that the vascular axis remains central to the longitudinal axis of the flap. The flap is elevated in retrograde fashion within the plane superficial to the deep fascia; the deep fascial layer must be preserved intact over the vascular trajectory to protect the vascular network. The vascular pedicle is judiciously skeletonized to increase the arc of rotation, and the flap is transferred to the recipient site via a subcutaneous tunnel or open approach. The donor site is closed primarily or reconstructed with local flap transposition or skin grafting.

Data collection and outcome measures

Corrected calculation of pharyngocutaneous fistula rate

To accurately reflect complications specifically associated with luminal reconstruction, a corrected pharyngocutaneous fistula rate was employed. Calculation included only cases involving oropharyngeal, hypopharyngeal, or esophageal luminal reconstruction (n = 65) as the denominator, excluding cases of simple skin coverage, laryngeal functional reconstruction, or other non-luminal repairs without fistula risk (n = 15).

Primary outcome measures

Flap survival was categorized into three groups: complete survival, partial necrosis (distal epidermal necrosis not requiring secondary surgery), and complete necrosis (extensive necrosis requiring debridement or secondary reconstruction). The flap survival rate was defined as the proportion of cases with complete survival or partial necrosis relative to the total number of cases.

Radiation-related analysis

Patients were stratified into irradiated and non-irradiated groups based on whether they had received prior cervical radiotherapy. Fifty-nine patients with documented radiation history (irradiated group: 16; non-irradiated group: 43) were included in the radiation-related analysis, while 19 patients with unknown radiation history were excluded (Fig. 1). Overall complication rates (including flap necrosis and donor-site wound dehiscence) and flap survival rates were compared between groups.

Donor-site shoulder function assessment

Patients meeting the following criteria were selected from the entire cohort for Quick-DASH (Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand, shortened version) assessment: (1) postoperative follow-up duration ≥ 6 months; (2) absence of cerebrovascular accident or other systemic diseases affecting limb mobility; and (3) completion of the questionnaire with consent to participate in functional evaluation.

A total of 48 patients (48 flaps; one bilateral flap patient contributed only one side) completed the Quick-DASH assessment with mean follow-up of 15.3 ± 6.7 months (median 14.9 months, IQR 9.9–17.5, range 6.3–33.0 months), comprising 9 Type I, 31 Type II, and 8 Type III cases (Fig. 1). The remaining 32 patients were excluded due to death from disease progression, loss to follow-up, or inability to cooperate with assessment. The Quick-DASH questionnaire contains 11 items, with scores ranging from 0 to 100; higher scores indicate greater functional disability [16].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD and compared using the independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test as appropriate based on normality testing; categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated to quantify effect sizes. Quick-DASH scores were compared among the three classification types using the Kruskal-Wallis H test, with post-hoc pairwise comparisons performed using the Mann-Whitney U test and Holm correction for multiple testing. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical methods employed in this study can be performed using SPSS 29.0.

Results

Patient and flap baseline characteristics

A total of 78 patients with 80 flaps were ultimately enrolled in this study, including 2 patients who underwent bilateral flap reconstruction. The cohort comprised 69 males (88.5%) and 9 females (11.5%), with ages ranging from 18 to 76 years (mean 59.2 ± 9.9 years). The primary etiology was predominantly malignant neoplasms (64 patients, 82.1%), including hypopharyngeal carcinoma (n = 34), laryngeal carcinoma (n = 16), and oropharyngeal carcinoma (n = 9); benign lesions and complication repairs accounted for 14 cases (17.9%), including esophageal stricture, pharyngocutaneous fistula, and hypopharyngeal stenosis. Sixteen patients (20.5%) had a history of preoperative radiotherapy, and 56 patients (71.8%) underwent concurrent neck dissection. Supraclavicular artery anatomical variations and the P-D-T classification system

Cadaveric dissection (20 hemibodies) demonstrated that the supraclavicular artery (SCA) originated from the transverse cervical artery (TCA) in all specimens; no direct origin from the subclavian artery was observed. Based on the trajectory of the SCA trunk, the P-D-T three-type classification system was established. In the anatomical study, Type II (Deltoid) was the most prevalent (75.0%), followed by Type I (Pectoral, 15.0%) and Type III (Trapezius, 10.0%). The distribution of classification types in clinical application was consistent with anatomical findings, with Type II accounting for 72.5% (58/80).

Vascular characteristics and surgical parameters differed among the three classification types. Type II flaps provided the largest flap dimensions (7.4 ± 0.9 × 14.1 ± 1.8 cm); Type I and Type III flaps had relatively shorter pedicles and more limited flap dimensions. Flap harvest time differed significantly among the three types (F = 8.747, P < 0.001), with Type III requiring the longest duration (52.1 ± 8.1 min), attributable to its deeper anatomical location and the need for accessory nerve preservation. Anatomical parameters: pedicle length and flap dimensions

In our cadaveric dissection of 20 hemibodies, the SCA external diameter at origin measured 1.09 ± 0.20 mm (range 0.8–1.5 mm). The distance from TCA origin to SCA origin was 4.35 ± 0.62 cm (range 3.5–5.8 cm). The distance from SCA origin to the fascial penetration point varied by P-D-T type: Type I (Pectoral) 2.73 ± 0.12 cm, Type II (Deltoid) 3.61 ± 0.50 cm, and Type III (Trapezius) 2.65 ± 0.07 cm. In our clinical series, mean flap dimensions were 13.6 ± 2.0 cm (length) × 7.1 ± 1.0 cm (width).

Flap survival and complications

Flap survival

Of the 80 flaps harvested using the PLAD technique, 77 achieved complete survival (96.25%), 2 experienced partial necrosis (2.5%), and 1 underwent complete necrosis (1.25%). The overall flap survival rate was 98.75% (79/80). The single case of complete necrosis occurred in a patient with recurrent hypopharyngeal carcinoma following radiotherapy, in whom severe wound infection led to pedicle thrombosis. All cases of partial necrosis involved distal epidermal necrosis that healed with dressing changes alone, requiring no secondary surgical intervention.

Recipient-site and donor-site complications

The predominant recipient-site complication was pharyngocutaneous fistula. Among the 80 flaps, 65 cases involved luminal reconstruction of the oropharynx, hypopharynx, or esophagus, while the remaining 15 cases comprised simple skin coverage, mucosal repair, or laryngeal functional reconstruction. Among the 65 luminal reconstruction cases, pharyngocutaneous fistula developed in 4 patients, yielding a corrected fistula rate of 6.2% (4/65). Of the 4 fistula patients, 3 had concurrent flap necrosis (partial necrosis in 2, complete necrosis in 1), suggesting an association between fistula formation and compromised flap perfusion.

Donor-site complications primarily consisted of wound dehiscence, occurring in 3 cases (3.8%), all at high-tension sites near the acromion; all healed with conservative wound care. The perioperative complication profile is detailed in Table 4.

Impact of radiotherapy on flap safety

Nineteen patients with unknown radiation history were excluded from this subgroup analysis because external hospital records could not be retrieved to verify prior treatment. Fifty-nine patients with documented radiation history were included in the radiation-related analysis (Fig. 1), comprising 16 in the irradiated group and 43 in the non-irradiated group. The overall complication rate in the irradiated group was 12.5% (2/16), including complete necrosis (n = 1) and donor-site wound dehiscence (n = 1); the corresponding rate in the non-irradiated group was 9.3% (4/43), including partial necrosis (n = 2) and donor-site wound dehiscence (n = 2). The difference between groups was not statistically significant (Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.658; OR = 1.39, 95% CI: 0.23–8.46). The wide confidence interval spanning 1 and the small irradiated sample (n = 16) indicate limited statistical power; thus, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence suggesting that SAP flap appears safe in previously irradiated necks rather than definitive proof of equivalence.

Quick-DASH scores differed significantly among the three classification types (Kruskal-Wallis H = 7.955, P = 0.019). Post-hoc analysis with Holm correction revealed that Type II flaps were associated with significantly higher scores compared to Type I (16.2 ± 7.3 vs. 9.1 ± 2.8, P = 0.017). However, the absolute difference of 7.1 points falls below the established minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 12–15 points for Quick-DASH as determined by the 2024 meta-analysis by Galardini et al. [17], indicating that while statistically significant, this difference represents mild functional impairment unlikely to be perceived as meaningful by patients. Type III scores (13.4 ± 9.1) were intermediate and did not differ significantly from either Type I (P = 0.497) or Type II (P = 0.497) after correction.

To assess whether shoulder function changes over time, we stratified the 48 patients by follow-up duration: 6–12 months (n = 17), 12–24 months (n = 25), and > 24 months (n = 6). Quick-DASH scores showed no significant difference across these time intervals (Kruskal-Wallis test, H = 1.321, P = 0.516), with mean scores of 13.4 ± 7.8, 14.4 ± 6.8, and 17.4 ± 9.3 for the three groups, respectively. Discussion

Through an integrated approach combining anatomical and clinical investigation, this study systematically established the P-D-T classification system based on supraclavicular artery trajectory variations and validated the clinical utility of the “point-line antegrade dissection” technique in an expanded cohort of 78 patients (80 flaps). Our results demonstrated an overall flap survival rate of 98.75% and a corrected pharyngocutaneous fistula rate of 6.2%, both comparable to or superior to data reported in the literature [18–20]. More importantly, this study represents the first systematic comparison of donor-site shoulder function among the three anatomical classification types and provides preliminary evidence supporting SAP flap application in previously irradiated necks, thereby establishing a standardized technical pathway for broader clinical adoption of this flap.

Anatomical basis and clinical significance of P-D-T classification

The inter-individual variability of the supraclavicular arterial system has been well documented [10, 11]. While the underlying developmental mechanisms remain incompletely understood, the clinical reality is that SCA trajectory varies predictably into three predominant patterns. Abe et al. [9] established an “origin-based classification” of the SCA, addressing the challenge of vessel identification during exploration. In contrast, the P-D-T system proposed herein establishes a “trajectory-based classification,” addressing the challenge of flap design optimization. These two classification approaches are complementary: the former guides surgeons to locate the vascular pedicle, while the latter guides skin paddle orientation relative to the vascular axis. Together, they constitute a comprehensive anatomical framework for SAP flap harvest.

The clinical implications of each P-D-T type differ substantially. Type II (Deltoid) flaps provide the longest vascular pedicle and the broadest applicability, representing the “workhorse” configuration suitable for the majority of head and neck reconstruction scenarios. Type I (Pectoral) flaps course anteriorly and are well-suited for lower, midline defect reconstruction. Type III (Trapezius) flaps course posterolaterally and can adequately address laryngeal, hypopharyngeal, and esophageal reconstruction needs. Both Type I and Type III flaps face limitations in reaching the oropharynx or high cervical regions. Surgeons should consider these anatomical constraints during preoperative planning and be prepared to employ extended TCA dissection when additional pedicle length is required.

The clinical significance of P-D-T classification lies in its fundamental resolution of the mismatch between vascular axis and geometric flap center inherent in traditional flap design. Previous literature has reported SAP flap partial necrosis rates of 4.2%–17.6% and complete necrosis rates of 0%–11.7% [18–20], largely attributable to surgeons attempting to apply a uniform “epaulette” design to different vascular patterns. Our PLAD technique, with its “vessel-first, flap-second” strategy, achieved a complete necrosis rate of only 1.25% (1/80), validating the effectiveness of individualized, trajectory-guided design.

Role of preoperative imaging

Multiple imaging modalities are available for preoperative assessment of the supraclavicular artery, each with distinct advantages and limitations [21–23].

CT Angiography (CTA): CTA achieves 60% perforator site identification and 77% SCA detection rate [21, 23]. It is best suited for complex cases with prior neck dissection or radiation. Limitations include inability to define perfusion territories, radiation exposure, and positional shift between imaging and surgery.

Handheld Doppler Ultrasonography: This modality offers the highest perforator detection rate (80%) with immediate bedside availability and minimal cost [23]. However, it cannot identify which specific vessel produces the signal and provides no information about vessel course. It is best suited for confirming pedicle patency rather than trajectory mapping.

Color Doppler Ultrasonography (CDU): CDU achieves superior resolution (detecting vessels as small as 0.5 mm) with real-time anatomical and hemodynamic information [23]. However, it cannot generate three-dimensional vascular architecture and is operator-dependent. Neither digital subtraction angiography nor MR angiography is recommended for routine planning due to invasiveness or poor performance.

Our Clinical Perspective: No single imaging modality provides complete preoperative information, and static imaging cannot account for positional changes during surgery. For previously untreated patients, the PLAD technique’s “Point” exploration, requiring only 5–10 min, provides definitive real-time assessment of vessel course, caliber, and P-D-T classification; routine preoperative CTA is unnecessary. However, for patients with prior level IV/V neck dissection or cervical radiotherapy, CTA remains the gold standard for excluding proximal SCA occlusion or thrombosis before committing to this flap. We therefore recommend a tiered approach: Treatment-naive patients: PLAD intraoperative exploration ± handheld Doppler confirmation; no preoperative CTA required. Previously treated patients (prior neck dissection involving level IV/V, or prior radiotherapy): Preoperative CTA or CDU to confirm vascular patency before surgery. This algorithm balances the convenience of PLAD for straightforward cases with the safety requirements of complex, previously treated patients.

Anatomical parameters and flap design

Safe flap design requires understanding of pedicle length, vessel caliber, and maximum flap dimensions. Our cadaveric measurements provide the anatomical foundation for design optimization. The distance from TCA origin to SCA origin (4.35 ± 0.62 cm) represents the potential gain from extended proximal dissection. The distance from SCA origin to fascial penetration varies by type, with Type II providing the longest native pedicle (3.61 cm) within the skin paddle territory. With extended TCA dissection, total effective pedicle length can reach approximately 7–8 cm.

Most authors recommend limiting flap length to approximately 22 cm; in Kokot et al.‘s series, length > 22 cm was associated with increased necrosis risk (P = 0.02) [24]. The combined reach with extended pedicle plus flap length reliably addresses reconstruction from the oral cavity to the cervical esophagus.

We recommend the following design algorithm: (1) mark SCA trajectory preoperatively with color Doppler ultrasonography when available; (2) confirm trajectory and assign P-D-T classification during “Point” exploration; (3) orient flap axis according to classification; (4) limit flap length to 22 cm maximum; (5) if reach appears inadequate, consider extended TCA dissection. Radiotherapy tolerance

The favorable outcomes observed in our irradiated cohort (12.5% vs. 9.3% complication rate, P = 0.658) are consistent with published experience suggesting that prior radiotherapy does not universally preclude SAP flap use [25]. Several factors may contribute to this tolerance. First, contemporary intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) delivers elective nodal doses of 50–60 Gy to level Vb (which encompasses the TCA/SCA system), substantially lower than the 66–70 Gy delivered to gross disease. Second, according to Taylor’s angiosome theory [26], chronic ischemia may promote compensatory dilation of choke vessels connecting adjacent angiosomes—a mechanism analogous to “physiological delay” [27]. However, we emphasize that this mechanistic explanation remains speculative and requires validation through dedicated dosimetric studies correlating pedicle dose with outcomes.

The small irradiated sample (n = 16) and wide confidence interval (OR 0.23–8.46) preclude definitive conclusions. Our findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence that SAP flap can be considered in irradiated patients when preoperative Doppler or CTA confirms TCA patency and the PLAD technique enables intraoperative pedicle assessment. The single complete necrosis in our irradiated group occurred in a patient with extensive cervical fibrosis, underscoring the importance of case selection and intraoperative assessment of tissue quality.

Shoulder function: statistical vs. clinical significance

The significantly higher Quick-DASH scores in Type II flaps (16.2 ± 7.3) compared to Type I (9.1 ± 2.8) reached statistical significance (P = 0.017) but must be interpreted in clinical context. According to the 2024 meta-analysis by Galardini et al. [17], which pooled data from 7 anchor-based studies (n = 1,440 patients), the MCID for Quick-DASH is 11.97 points (95% CI: 9.60–14.33), with a recommended practical threshold of 12–15 points. The 7.1-point difference observed in our study falls below this threshold, indicating that patients are unlikely to perceive this as meaningful impairment. Furthermore, all three groups demonstrated scores within the “mild disability” range (< 25 points).

The greater functional impact of Type II flaps likely reflects several factors: the largest harvest dimensions (7.4 × 14.1 cm), extensive deep fascia dissection over the deltoid muscle that may promote scar adhesion, and complete transection of supraclavicular nerve intermediate and lateral branches (C3–C4) causing shoulder numbness and paresthesia [14]. Type III scores (13.4 ± 9.1) were intermediate, likely reflecting accessory nerve traction during dissection along the trapezius border.

We acknowledge that functional assessment was completed by only 48 of 80 flaps (60%), introducing potential selection bias. Patients with worse outcomes may have been more likely to die from disease or be unable to complete assessment. Nevertheless, based on our findings, we recommend classification-specific rehabilitation: Type II patients should begin early shoulder abduction exercises at 2–3 weeks postoperatively to prevent scar adhesion; Type III patients should focus on shoulder shrug strengthening to promote accessory nerve recovery.

Comparison with alternative flaps

Compared with free flaps, the SAP flap offers distinct advantages for selected patients. Our mean harvest time of 43.0 ± 9.3 min is substantially shorter than free flap procedures, which require additional time for microvascular anastomosis [2, 3]. Our corrected fistula rate of 6.2% is comparable to reported rates for RFFF (3%–10%) [28]. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of 794 patients reported a pooled fistula rate of 34% (95% CI: 23–47%) for pectoralis major myocutaneous flap (PMMF), significantly higher than other reconstruction techniques [29]. A recent meta-analysis of 402 patients found no significant differences between SAP flap and free tissue transfer in major complications, flap necrosis, or fistula formation [30]. The SAP flap is particularly suited for elderly or medically compromised patients who cannot tolerate prolonged microsurgical procedures, patients requiring rapid reconstruction, and settings where microsurgical expertise is limited.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study, selection bias is inherent; surgeons may have preferentially chosen SAP flap for patients with favorable tissue conditions, potentially underestimating complication rates. Second, the irradiated cohort (n = 16) provides limited statistical power, and conclusions regarding radiotherapy tolerance should be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Third, Quick-DASH assessment was completed by only 60% of flaps, with potential attrition bias from deaths and non-responders. Finally, sample sizes for Type I (n = 13) and Type III (n = 9) subgroups limit statistical power for these comparisons. Future multicenter prospective studies with standardized classification protocols and complete functional follow-up are needed to validate these findings. Additionally, formalin fixation may cause tissue shrinkage, potentially underestimating actual pedicle length in our cadaveric measurements; fresh cadaveric or intraoperative measurements may yield longer values.

Conclusions

Through an integrated approach combining anatomical and clinical investigation, this study systematically established the P-D-T (Pectoral-Deltoid-Trapezius) anatomical classification system based on supraclavicular artery trajectory variations and validated the clinical utility of the “point-line antegrade dissection” technique. The SAP flap application appears safe in previously irradiated necks, although the small sample size warrants cautious interpretation. Type II (Deltoid) flaps demonstrate statistically significant but functionally well-tolerated impact on shoulder function, with Quick-DASH scores remaining within the mild disability range. The SAP flap represents a reliable option for head and neck defect reconstruction, particularly suitable for complex cases in patients who cannot tolerate prolonged microsurgical procedures.
