Cross-sectional survey investigating knowledge, attitudes and reasons for use of food supplements in rheumatological diseases: the IntegraRE study protocol
Authors: Francesca Iacoponi, Erica Cardamone, Augusto Pastorelli, Matteo Rosetti, Umberto Agrimi, Marco Silano, Paolo Stacchini, Rosita Laurenti, Daniela Marotto, Raffaella Gargiulo, Concetta Boniglia, Silvia Di Giacomo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRheumatological diseases represent a widespread heterogeneous group of disorders, united by chronic musculoskeletal inflammatory processes. Despite the increasing effectiveness of new therapies, the lack of adequate treatments for certain conditions and the occurrence of adverse drug reactions have led to the need for alternative strategies, including food supplements. These products are perceived by the patient as a valuable aid without adverse effects. However, adverse reactions to food supplements have been reported, although their incidence cannot be established due to the lack of consumption data. In this context, the IntegraRE project aims at collecting information on the use of food supplements in rheumatology, considering both physicians and patients.Methods and analysisA survey-based cross-sectional study has been designed into two consecutive phases: Phase 1 aims to investigate rheumatologists’ knowledge, attitudes and recommendations to patients regarding food supplements, while Phase 2 focuses on estimating patients’ intake of food supplements, including the specific products used, unit consumed and reasons for their consumption. Questionnaires will be collected from at least 287 rheumatologists and 2000 patients, geographically distributed in proportion to the Italian population. These will gather information on sociodemographic variables, lifestyle factors, knowledge, clinical practices and supplement consumption.Ethics and disseminationThe study protocol has been approved by the Ethics Committee of the Italian National Institute of Health (approval no. AOO 0032395 on 24 July 2024). The results, which will be widely disseminated through conference presentations, peer-reviewed publications and dedicated project webpage, will provide an understanding of the use of food supplements in rheumatology and allow guidelines to be drawn up on their correct use in the clinical practice of rheumatological diseases. Overall, this survey will increase people’s awareness of the effects of food supplements and encourage their safe and conscious use.Keywords: EPIDEMIOLOGY, PUBLIC HEALTH, NUTRITION & DIETETICS, Surveys and Questionnaires, RHEUMATOLOGY, Safety

Introduction

Background

The European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) and the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) define rheumatological diseases as a heterogeneous group of disorders that usually affect the joints but can occur in any organ of the body.1 They are characterised by chronicity, worsening over time, pain and functional limitation. In the most severe cases, they can cause significant disability and have a major impact on life quality and expectancy.1 2 Currently, more than 200 rheumatological diseases have been identified, affecting both children and adults. Those with the highest global prevalence include osteoarthritis (7.6%), gout (3.3%), spondylarthritis (0.5%–2%), rheumatoid arthritis (0.2%) and systemic lupus erythematosus (0.003%–0.5%).1,7 Other conditions include connective tissue and bone diseases, systemic vasculitis, extra-articular rheumatism and fibromyalgia.2 Several factors are involved in the development of rheumatic diseases, including immune system dysfunctions, genetic variations and mutations, which can result in acute or chronic damage to joints, muscles, bones and blood vessels.1 Additionally, these diseases are associated with increased comorbidities (eg, infectious, neoplastic, endocrine and neurological disorders), resulting in a further reduction in quality of life and survival.1

Nowadays, several drugs are available for the treatment of autoimmune and inflammatory rheumatological conditions. Particularly, along with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and corticosteroids, conventional synthetic (eg, methotrexate), targeted synthetic (eg, tofacitinib) and biologic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (eg, infliximab) have been developed to increase the therapy effectiveness with prevention of disability and comorbidity.1 8 However, the occurrence of adverse effects (eg, cardiovascular, teratogenic, carcinogenic, gastro-intestinal)8 and the lack of effective therapy for some rheumatological conditions9 make the management of rheumatology patients challenging. Therefore, the resorting to non-pharmacological approaches to control symptoms, including potentially disabling pain, is often necessary to improve overall patient care.2 3

Among the possible alternative strategies for the management of rheumatology patients, the importance of nutrition has recently emerged. Indeed, several studies have shown that diet can directly influence inflammatory processes and, therefore, could play a potential role in various rheumatological conditions, such as rheumatoid arthritis and osteoarthritis.10,12 In addition, the diet is also involved in the onset of comorbidities associated with rheumatological conditions, such as cardiovascular disease, obesity and diabetes, which greatly contribute to reducing patients’ life expectancy.13 Also, patients have shown an interest towards diet, being perceived as a ‘natural’ way of improving their health and allowing them to play an active role in the management of their disease. For this reason, they are increasingly asking their physicians for advice on nutritional strategies as an alternative to or in support of pharmacological treatment. Among these, food supplements play a prominent role.

In accordance with the European Union law, food supplements are defined as ‘food products intended to supplement the normal diet and which constitute a concentrated source of nutrients, such as vitamins and minerals, or other substances with a nutritional or physiological effect, including but not limited to amino acids, essential fatty acids, fibres and plant extracts, either mono-components or multi-components, in pre-dosed forms’.14 15 Recently, their market has increased significantly due to the common belief that they can be a valuable aid without side effects, because of their natural origin. However, it is difficult to estimate their actual use due to the lack of consumption data. Indeed, although they may be recommended to patients by physicians, food supplements do not require a prescription and can be freely purchased through both traditional (pharmacies, parapharmacies, large-scale distribution, etc) and alternative channels (Internet).

The use of food supplements without medical supervision represents an issue of particular concern because it could lead to the occurrence of adverse effects, for example through misuse of the product or its interactions with drugs. Recent studies have highlighted cases of intoxication due to the overuse of supplements containing vitamin D, E and A.16 17 In addition, interactions between vitamin K and oral anticoagulants have been reported too.18 The situation becomes even more complex when supplements contain botanical ingredients. In this regard, cases of hepatotoxicity have been ascribed to Curcuma longa,19 while the potential risk of pharmacological interactions of supplements containing Saint John’s Wort (Hypericum perforatum) and conventional drugs is well known.20 However, although the risk of adverse reactions to food supplements is well-known, their incidence remains unquantifiable due to insufficient population consumption data.

Finally, another important issue is that, even when patients seek guidance from their physicians, the latter often lack adequate training and clear reference guidelines to provide effective advice, placing them in a difficult position.

Considering these findings and the strategic role that physicians’ recommendations have on patients, the ‘IntegraRE’ project has been designed to explore knowledge, attitudes, clinical practice and consumption of food supplements in rheumatology.

Methods and analysis

Study design and setting

The project is a cross-sectional survey structured into two phases: the first (Phase 1), lasting 6 months, involves rheumatologists, while the second (Phase 2), spanning 18 months, focuses on their patients. The participants will be recruited at national level through the Italian Rheumatologists College (CReI), a non-profit scientific society bringing together rheumatology specialists working in hospitals and in the community. Data will be collected through two questionnaires, one designed for rheumatologists and the other for their patients, ad hoc developed by the Italian National Institute of Health (ISS). Between the first and second phase, a training day for rheumatology physicians will be organised by ISS with the support of CREI, and researchers from both institutions will act as moderators and relators. Particularly, key regulatory, nutritional and safety aspects of food supplements will be addressed, with particular attention to potential benefits and risks of these products. During the meeting, the preliminary data collected in Phase 1 and the objectives of Phase 2 of the project will be presented. In addition, the method of administering the questionnaire to patients will be explained. Overall, the training day will allow strengthening rheumatologists’ awareness about the appropriate use of food supplements in clinical practice.

The study will last for 2 years, with participant recruitment for Phase 1 and data collection having begun on 9 October 2024. Figure 1 shows the flowchart of the two study phases.

Population

The target population for the first phase of the study consists of rheumatology physicians (territorial, hospital and/or university) practising in Italian public and private healthcare facilities and registered as CReI members. Specifically, the survey will be sent to 1134 physicians distributed as follows: North 454 (40%), Centre 250 (22%), South 271 (24%) and Islands 159 (14%). Therefore, to obtain a representative sample of the population, assuming that 50% of the survey population is familiar with supplements, setting a confidence level of 95% and a margin of error of 5%, the required sample size is estimated to be 287 physicians.21 Therefore, a 25% response rate is anticipated for the survey, given the generally low adherence to such studies.22

In the second phase of the study, the target population will be represented by the patients who have requested a specialist consultation with the rheumatologists involved in the first phase of the survey, without any limitation of gender and age. The final sample of patients will be at least 2000.23 To guarantee the national representativeness of the sample, the number of patients to be enrolled at the regional level was calculated based on the total number of rheumatologists in Italy as of 31 January 2021 and their regional distribution.24 The minimum number of questionnaires to be collected was set equal to 30 in the case of smaller regional numbers, as shown in figure 2.

Phase 1

Data will be collected through a questionnaire made available on the Microsoft Forms platform. The link to the questionnaire will be sent through email by ISS to the CReI and by the latter to each physician member, accompanied by information on personal data processing and a request for the relevant consent. Reminder emails (monthly, or biweekly if required) will be sent by CReI to physicians to encourage their participation during the survey period. To assess the reliability of the questionnaire (test-retest), participants will be asked in the information sheet to be contacted for a second administration after 20 days. After this period, a second mailing will be sent to the entire target population, allowing physicians to complete the questionnaire again on their own initiative.

The Phase 1 was specifically designed as a KAP (Knowledge, Attitudes, Practices) survey to assess rheumatologists’ knowledge and attitudes, as well as their clinical practices regarding food supplements. Particularly, the questionnaire includes five sections: one focused on collecting sociodemographic data, one on knowledge about supplements, another on attitudes and a fourth on clinical practice. The last section is aimed at generating the alphanumeric code. Physicians will be asked for demographic information such as age, gender, lifestyle information (smoking habits, sports practice, adherence to special diets and use of food supplements), type and location of their clinic or facility, years of work experience and average number of patients seen per year. The 12 questions in this first section will mainly involve closed-ended answers. To investigate the level of knowledge of rheumatologists about food supplements, 20 questions will be formulated with answers on a Likert scale (from one completely disagree to five completely agree). Examples of questions include: ‘A food supplement is a food product’, ‘A food supplement requires marketing authorisation for sale’ and ‘A food supplement is used to treat specific diseases’. Knowledge of supplements was defined as achieving a minimum of correct answers exceeding half plus one (ie., at least 52% correct answers). The third section will be devoted to attitudes. Seventeen questions, assessed on a Likert scale (from one completely disagree to five completely agree), will cover physicians’ perception of their knowledge of supplements and their approach to them during patient history and prescription.24 25 Examples of questions include: ‘Regarding food supplements, you feel you have sufficient knowledge about dosage and administration of these products’, ‘You find it useful to periodically update your knowledge on the scientific evidence related to food supplements through informational materials provided by pharmaceutical representatives’, and ‘During medical history, you consider it appropriate to ask the patient if they are taking food supplements’. To assess the use of food supplements in clinical practice, the fourth section of the questionnaire asks 19 questions concerning the propensity to recommend food supplements, any products suggested with their category, trade name (open-ended) and manufacturer (optional), and the pathology for which they were prescribed. Information on dosage, possible benefits and adverse effects was also requested. The questions in this section are primarily on a Likert scale (1=never to 5=always), while the item concerning the specific product recommended is formulated as an open-ended question. Examples of questions include: ‘In your clinical practice, you recommend food supplements, being confident in their effectiveness’, ‘You recommend supplements for rheumatological conditions’ and ‘Regarding dosage, you recommend the one stated on the label’. Finally, section five allows for the creation of a unique alphanumeric code that enables the questionnaires to be associated after the second administration.

Phase 2

The second phase will begin after the training event for physicians and will last about a year. The participation of the patient will be voluntary and anonymous. In this phase, the physician will play an active role by providing patients with a paper copy of the questionnaire, free of identifying elements, to be completed independently while waiting for their visit. The completed questionnaires will be collected in a designated binder placed in the waiting rooms and sent periodically by the physicians to the ISS email box dedicated to the project, by scanning. To evaluate the feasibility of this operational procedure, an intermediate check will be carried out (approximately 3 months after the start of the second phase) to assess the effectiveness of the data transmission process. The Phase 2 questionnaire collects information regarding demographic factors (age, gender, educational qualification, income, health conditions, lifestyle—smoking habits, sports practice and adherence to special diets) and anamnestic factors (chronic rheumatological and non-rheumatological diseases they have and any drugs they are taking) of the patient (sections 1 and 2). Moreover, it considers the knowledge, use and perception of food supplements by the subjects surveyed (section 3). Specifically, to assess food supplement use over the past month, patients will be asked whether they use supplements, the specific products taken (including duration of use, dosage and reasons for use), who recommended the products, and whether their referring physicians (general practitioner or rheumatologist) are aware of their intake. In addition, potential seasonality in food supplement consumption will be evaluated, leveraging both the year-long duration of the survey and the questionnaire’s focus on reporting only the products used in the month prior to its completion. Finally, information on patients’ perceptions of supplements will be gathered by evaluating any benefits or adverse effects they have experienced following their use. The Phase 2 questionnaire, consisting of 35 questions in total, includes multiple-choice questions with some open-ended options.

Statistical analysis

The rheumatologists’ questionnaires will be identified by a random ID. The answers of the Phase 1 questionnaire will be downloadable in Microsoft Excel format while those of Phase 2 will be reported through data entry in a database built ad hoc. The data obtained will be managed by the researchers of the Department of Food Safety, Nutrition and Veterinary Public Health of the ISS.

Once the plausibility and correctness of the data have been checked during a validation phase of the dataset, a descriptive analysis (mean and SD, median and IQR, absolute frequencies and percentages according to the distribution of the variables) will be carried out for each questionnaire item. The nationwide representativeness of both the rheumatologist and patient populations will be assessed by evaluating their proportionality against national values reported in the ISTAT database. In addition, for Phase 1, the response rate will be calculated as the ratio between the number of completed questionnaires and the total number of eligible participants invited, in order to quantify the level of participation and assess the potential impact of non-response bias.

To evaluate the reliability of the Phase 1 questionnaire, the scores obtained in the two administrations will be correlated by calculating the Pearson correlation coefficient (stability coefficient), considering a value >0.80 as acceptable. Internal consistency will be measured with Cronbach’s alpha.

Associations and correlations between all variables will be assessed by means of the χ2 test, with Fisher’s correction when necessary, or by Pearson’s correlation. To identify possible predictive factors, a univariate logistic regression analysis will be used considering as dependent variables the tendency to recommend supplements (recommendation yes/no) for physicians and the consumption of supplements (consumption yes/no) for patients.

The combination of these factors, if significant, on the recommendation and consumption of food supplements will be assessed by means of a multivariate logistic regression analysis, which will allow the estimation of ORs with corresponding 95% CIs. A p value <0.05 (two-tailed) will be considered statistically significant. R software V.4.4.0 (https://www.r-project.org/) will be used for all statistical analyses.

Patient and public involvement

There was no patient or public involvement in the design, execution, reporting or dissemination plans of our study.

Ethics and dissemination

The study protocol has been approved by the National Ethics Committee of the Italian National Institute of Health (approval no. AOO 0032395). In both phases, the consent to participate, and to collect and process personal data, is given by filling in the form prepared by the Data Protection Officer of the Italian National Institute of Health (ISS) in accordance with the European Commission General Data Protection Regulation (679/2016). The duly completed form will then be retained by CReI to maintain the anonymity of participants. Additionally, in the first phase of the project, the platform hosting the questionnaire will include a brief information section, an invitation to complete the questionnaire and a confirmation of receipt. In the second phase, the same information will be provided in paper form. Regarding the reliability of the Phase 1 questionnaire (test-retest), the confidentiality of participants will be ensured through the creation of a unique alphanumeric code, managed independently by the physician at the end of the first administration. This code will also be used during the second administration to ensure the matching of the questionnaires, while remaining confidential and completely untraceable to the physician. No benefits will be offered for participation in the study.

Study results will be disseminated to scientists through their publication in international peer-reviewed journals and presentation at national and international conferences. The public will be reached through leaflets and articles in non-specialised journals and magazines. At last, a dedicated project page has been developed on the ISS website (www.iss.it/integrare), where the project phases, objectives and results will be published along with training materials to promote food supplement informed and safe use. This webpage will also be promoted through the website of the Italian Rheumatologists College and disseminated through the press service of the Italian National Institute of Health to reach a broader audience.

Discussion

Among the possible strategies for the management of rheumatology patients, the use of food supplements has recently emerged, being perceived by people as a natural, safe and effective way to reach the expected health effects.26 27 As a result, patients are increasingly seeking guidance from their physicians regarding food supplements to enhance their overall well-being.

However, physicians may lack adequate training on this topic, as formal medical education often does not include specific courses on food supplements. This represents an important issue which should be addressed since food supplements can cause adverse effects, including interactions with drugs, or may be contraindicated for specific conditions.28 Therefore, understanding rheumatologists’ clinical recommendations for food supplements, as well as how these recommendations are influenced by their knowledge and attitudes, is crucial.29 At the same time, exploring the patient perspective is equally important. This includes determining whether patients adhere to physicians’ advice and investigating the actual patterns of supplement use within the population. In this context, the IntegraRE project represents a groundbreaking initiative designed to address the critical gaps in understanding the use of food supplements in the management of rheumatological conditions. By evaluating both the physician’s and the patient’s perspectives, IntegraRE offers a comprehensive approach to investigating this emerging area of clinical practice.

As far as we know, no studies have been performed until now to understand rheumatologists’ knowledge, attitudes and clinical practices about food supplements. In this regard, KAP surveys offer a valuable tool to fill this gap, providing insights to guide resource allocation, planning and implementation of public health programmes.30 A national-level KAP survey could estimate the percentage of rheumatologists who recommend supplements and identify the factors influencing their recommendations. Furthermore, it could reveal the most recommended supplements in rheumatology and allow for the assessment of whether their use is supported by scientific evidence, as determined through a review of clinical studies and meta-analyses in the literature. Finally, the results of the KAP survey will help identify potential challenges or critical issues that need to be addressed to improve physician education, optimise clinical practices and ensure the safe and effective use of supplements.

Investigating patients’ perspectives also represents a key point of the study. To date, no survey on the use of supplements in rheumatology has been conducted in Italy. Furthermore, previous surveys have primarily assessed the most used categories of supplements without examining the specific composition and consumption units of individual products.31,33 A detailed description of the food supplements prescribed or consumed is therefore a particularly ambitious aspect of this project. This approach will not only enable the estimation of the percentage of rheumatology patients using supplements at the national level and the factors influencing their use but also evaluate the scientific evidence supporting the most consumed supplements. Furthermore, it will facilitate the estimation of daily nutrient and ingredient intake, thereby allowing for the monitoring of compliance with established maximum limits, where available.

Given the lack of previous surveys in the field of rheumatology, no validated questionnaires were available for use in this study, necessitating the development of ad hoc questionnaires for each phase. To ensure the reliability and internal consistency of the Phase 1 questionnaire, its validation has been planned. This will also allow us to make it suitable for future studies and adaptable to other professional categories (eg, gastroenterologists, cardiologists) with minor modifications. Since food supplements are recommended not only by rheumatologists but also by many other medical specialties, assessing their knowledge, attitudes and clinical practices is crucial to identify potential gaps and to implement national-level strategies that promote evidence-based recommendations on supplement use and, ultimately, safeguard patient health.

Furthermore, the patient questionnaire developed could be leveraged to investigate the consumption of food supplements in other target populations, offering a more comprehensive understanding of their use across diverse groups. This represents a key aspect of the project, as it will provide new tools for studying the use of food supplements among various populations, fostering future research and informed decision-making.

Despite the valuable insights provided by this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, a low response rate could affect the national representativeness of the sample, thereby impairing its reliability and generalisability. To overcome this bias, dissemination strategies have been implemented. Notably, the project was presented at the XXVII National Congress of CReI, held in Florence from 9 October 2024 to 14 October 2024. Additionally, a dedicated webpage for the project (www.iss.it/integrare) is hosted on the Italian National Institute of Health’s website and will be promoted through the website of CReI and the press service of the ISS.

Other potential limitations of the study include recall bias, as well as response and non-response bias.

Recall bias may occur if physicians or patients are unable to accurately recall the supplements recommended or used, the reasons for their recommendation or use, the categories of supplements prescribed or taken, or any adverse reactions experienced. Physicians may mitigate this limitation by consulting their medical records, whereas for patients it is generally more challenging. To reduce this bias, patients will be asked to report only the food supplements used in the past month.

Regarding response bias, this may occur when respondents provide inaccurate answers influenced by social desirability. This includes giving answers they believe are socially acceptable rather than truthful, agreeing with statements regardless of actual opinion, or favouring extreme response options (eg, ‘strongly agree’ or ‘strongly disagree’) over more moderate ones.34 To minimise these limitations, we will use anonymous questionnaires to reduce social desirability bias and carefully designed items to limit response bias.

Finally, non-response bias cannot be ruled out. Indeed, it is possible that individuals who are less interested in the topic chose not to participate, potentially skewing the results. To mitigate this risk, the survey will be disseminated broadly through multiple channels, including the Italian National Institute of Health and the CReI website, accompanied by periodic reminders. Moreover, posters will be displayed in rheumatologists’ offices with the aim of enhancing patient participation rates. In addition to these limitations, it should be noted that the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, that participation may have been influenced by self-selection, and that data on supplement use were self-reported, without objective clinical verification. Nevertheless, the study has several strengths. It represents the first nationwide investigation on food supplement use in rheumatology in Italy: the use of validated questionnaires, robust statistical methods and multiple recruitment strategies further enhance its methodological value. Ultimately, the IntegraRE project not only provides novel insights into the prescribing and consumption of supplements in rheumatology but also offers a solid evidence base for educational interventions, safer prescribing practices and improved patient–physician communication.

Overall, despite its limitations, the IntegraRE project stands out as a pioneering effort to bridge knowledge gaps in supplement use in rheumatology. Disseminating its results through international peer-reviewed journals will contribute to advancing medical education. Additionally, making the results and informational materials accessible to the public via the ISS website will enhance awareness of the proper use and potential risks of food supplements. Ultimately, the project will support evidence-based recommendations and targeted educational initiatives, fostering improved physician decision-making, safer prescribing practices and more effective patient counselling on the use of food supplements in rheumatology. Moreover, the project is expected to enhance physician–patient communication on supplement use, fostered by its dissemination, which is anticipated to stimulate patients’ curiosity. This may foster shared decision-making, improve adherence to medical advice, prevent unsupervised changes of supplement intake and ultimately support safer and more informed clinical practices.
