Imitation performance biases are moderated by perceived accuracy in golf putting
Authors: Carrie M. Peters, Romeo Chua, Sarah N. Kraeutner, Nicola J. Hodges
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Watching others act can cause unintentional biases in the observer’s next action. This “contagion” occurs due to the shared processes engaged during observation and execution, interfering with performance after observation. Biases can be imitative or compensatory, with the direction thought to be dependent on the presence of observed-induced prediction errors (PE: difference between predicted/expected and observed performance). To replicate and test the proposed PE mechanism, we compared golfers watching “on-target” and errorful putts. Twenty-three experienced golfers alternated putting a ball to a centre target on a nine-square grid (without outcome feedback) and watching videos of an actor putting to the same grid. Across four conditions, we covaried the location of the actor’s putts (centre, corner) and expectations about the actor’s aiming location, to manipulate the presence of a PE. As expected, imitative execution biases emerged after watching “correct” putts to corner squares, but not when these same corner putts were perceived as errors. Compensatory biases from PEs were also absent after watching “misses” to the centre square. These data provide evidence for different behaviours after the observation of correct versus errorful actions but raise questions about the types and/or stability of errors that underpin these effects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21919-1.

Introduction

Observing the actions of others has been shown to have profound intentional and sometimes unintentional influences on the performance and learning of motor skills (e.g., for reviews see1–4. Effects of observing others can be valuable when learning new skills through observation but potentially detrimental when the observation interferes with current performance in non-learning situations. Imitative, unintentional biases have been documented, where performance becomes more similar to an observed action, leading to errors in current performance (e.g5–8. There has also been select evidence of compensatory biases, where an observer shows subsequent performance biases in the opposite direction to observed, when what they are watching is assumed errorful6,9. These diverging imitative and compensatory biases are thought to reflect different processes occurring during observation. Here, our aim was to test for these imitative and compensatory biases following observation of golf putts and to test conditions and potential mechanisms underpinning their occurrence.

The tendency to imitate the actions of others, especially under time constraints, has been termed automatic imitation (e.g10, and is well documented across many behavioural paradigms (see11. Evidence of the unintentional nature of this effect comes from paradigms where observers make more errors or are slower to respond when shown action stimuli that are incongruent rather than congruent with a required action (e.g12–15. Additional evidence is given when behaviours are biased in the direction of observed actions (whether spatially or temporally) (e.g6,7. Such unintentional effects have not only been shown as a result of watching incongruent or different actions, but also just through seeing the consequences of those actions5. Automatic imitation is thought to occur as a result of bidirectional connections between motor and visual representations of an action (e.g10,16–18. According to ideomotor theory17,19–22, perception and action share the same “common code” at the level of the brain20,23–25. As such, the motor program used to initiate physical movement is thought to be activated when watching that same action, via an internal simulation20,26. There is considerable evidence of overlap in the brain areas engaged during the observation and execution of the same action, now commonly referred to as the action observation network (e.g27–31. The representation generated through the observation of an incongruent action is thought to compete with the representation used for execution, causing interference or what has been referred to as motor contagion32.

A second type of motor contagion, unique to seeing errorful actions, has been identified, where performance is biased away from an observed error in a compensatory fashion6,9,33,34. Diverging imitative and compensation biases were shown across two experiments6, where participants watched an actor throw a baseball to a target (Experiment 1) and reach to targets on a computer screen (Experiment 2). In both experiments, two groups watched the same videos of an actor performing with a consistent directional bias. One group was told that the actor aimed to different locations with only successful trials shown. A second group was told that the actor aimed to the centre, such that deviations would be interpreted as errors. When the actor’s performance was perceived as correct, the observer’s subsequent performance showed imitative biases. When they were perceived as errors, performance was biased away from the error in a compensatory fashion6. However, in the baseline blocks before observation, the experimental groups in both studies had performance biases that were in the same direction as those later elicited by observation.

Small compensatory biases after error observation have also been shown in an aiming task where an observer watched “puppet arms” consistently miss left or right targets9. For example, after watching consistent right-ward errors, observers showed left-ward shifts in estimations of their body’s midline, as if compensating for the observed errors9. However, these after-effects as a result of watching errors have not to date been replicatedcf., 35. Small compensatory biases have also been shown in dyadic practice of golf putting33,34. Participants showed evidence of compensatory putting in response to their partner’s errors on successive trials, even though imitative biases were on average observed after watching putts to targets of a further distance than the one being practised33,34.

One explanation for compensatory biases after watching errors is through prediction error (PE) driven updates to an internal model for the action2,6. This explanation is grounded in ideas of forward-models, where past actions are used in a Bayesian fashion to predict the sensory consequences associated with a motor command (e.g36–39. In observation, the action observation network is thought to be involved in predictions, driven by the observer’s forward model predictions of the expected effects40–42. Ikegami and colleagues2,6 recontextualized PEs in observation as the difference between what is expected and what is actually observed. In their research, a PE would be generated when participants were told that an actor was aiming to the centre of a target array, but they saw throws or reaches to non-centre areas6. This observation-induced PE is thought to drive updates to the observer’s internal model of the task, biasing subsequent performance away from the error2,6. However, in the aforementioned work, compensatory biases were present when the observer watched the actor do something different to their own action goal in addition to a prediction violation. As such, there is interference between the competing representations generated through observation and action and between expected and actual. Thus, to adequately test whether the observation-induced PE is both a necessary and sufficient component of any potential biases, there is a need to dissociate between PEs and the congruency between the actor’s performance and the participant’s goal; an approach that has not yet been taken.

Our main aim in this current study was to test for the occurrence of compensatory and imitative contagion effects (in response to the same stimuli) in a golf putting task, where it was possible to recruit “experts” and where precision in outcomes is a central facet of the skill. Despite some evidence supporting the existence of diverging performance biases, no work has directly isolated observation-induced PEs as a cause of any biases, by testing across conditions where an actor’s “miss” matches the intended target of the observer (i.e., there is still a PE, but not a discrepancy between observed and intended outcomes of the observer). In addition to questions about the mechanism underpinning compensatory biases, there is also reason to replicate and test the generalizability of such observation-driven compensation biases. Not only are there few studies showing compensatory biases from watching, all of the work is based on between participant designs where there is a lack of control for individual differences in baseline performance biases.

By adopting a repeated measures’ design, we were able to make comparisons across the same individuals for the same stimuli under different instructions to better test mechanisms underpinning contagion-type effects. Across four blocked conditions, participants alternated putting golf balls to the centre square of a target grid and watching videos of an actor putting. We varied the actor’s putting location to show putts to the corner (replication6) and centre (new) square of the target grid. We manipulated the instructions conveying information about the actor’s aiming location across the conditions to create a cognitive mismatch between expected and observed performance. The instruction manipulation was a direct replication of Ikegami and colleagues6 in order to bring about, what we term, an “observation-induced prediction error (PE)”. This PE occurs because participants are told that an actor is aiming to a different location than where their ball actually stopped (i.e., a miss). For example, observers should expect the actor to hit the centre square, but instead they see a miss to the corner. This PE condition is contrasted to a no PE condition, where the instructed and watched target are the same. This design allowed us to discern between biases solely as a result of PEs; under conditions where the actor was putting to the same or different locations to the observer. Outcome feedback was blocked on execution trials to isolate performance changes from observation. Between each condition, participants completed a “washout” block of trials with full vision to facilitate a return to baseline. Our primary measures to capture performance biases were outcome measures consisting of constant error in the y (anterior-posterior) and x (medio-lateral) axes. We also calculated a measure of variability; bivariate variable error (BVE), based on the average variable error in each direction, to provide a more general measure of performance adaptations that may not be consistent in one direction.

As shown in Fig. 1a, perceived ‘hits’ to the corner square, where there is no PE (e.g., top right), were expected to elicit imitation biases (i.e., overshoots and right-ward biases) in subsequent putting. Perceived ‘misses’ to this same corner square (i.e., PEs), were expected to elicit compensatory biases (i.e., undershoots and left-ward biases, Fig. 1b). For the “new” watch centre condition, watching correct putts to the centre was not expected to elicit any performance biases, thus serving as a control condition (Fig. 1c). However, when participants watched centre putts, but they were told they were misses (e.g., the actor should have been aiming to the top right), the presence of a PE alone could still elicit compensation in subsequent putting. We would see biases in putting towards the top-right corner (i.e., overshoots and right-ward biases, Fig. 1d). Biases in general were expected to increase with repeated observation, replicating prior work (e.g6.)

Results

Error measures were compared in 2 Prediction error (PE, no PE) x 2 watched Location (corner, centre) Linear Mixed Effect (LME) models, with blocks added as a continuous factor nested in each condition (see Methods for full details). We also included the group variable of condition-Order in select secondary analyses. Because these condition-Order analyses were not preplanned, we were not powered to test for such effects and hence provided for information only. Within-participant eta squares (η²w;43 are reported to show the proportion of variance explained by fixed effects in each LME model. Because the models were constructed with nested random effects44, which capture substantial variance associated with grouping factors, these estimates of effect size were expected to be relatively small45. Full outputs of the LME models, descriptive statistics and results from analysis of washout blocks are reported in Supplementary Materials.

Our primary error measure was constant error (CE) in the y and x axes. Because we varied the corner that participants watched, in the watch corner conditions, and the corner that the participants were told the actor was aiming to in the “watch centre, PE” condition, we flipped the direction of the CE values across conditions. Y axis CE values from bottom corners and x axis CE values from the left corners were multiplied by -1 and doing this allowed for the different corners to be analysed together (see6. Therefore, in the watch corner conditions, reported overshoot and right-ward biased errors always reflected imitative contagions and undershoot and left-ward biased errors always reflected compensatory contagions.

To confirm that the visual feedback given in the washout blocks after each condition facilitated a return to baseline performance, treatment coding was used to compare CE from each washout block to the pretest performance (all ps > 0.13; see Supplementary Tables S1 & S2). To check that participants were attending to the videos, they verbally reported the grid number of the putt outcome after each video. Accuracy was over 99% and errors were distributed approximately equally across the conditions. When an error was made, participants were reminded to pay attention to the videos.

To preface the results and provide an overall depiction of performance biases across the conditions, mean constant error (CE) in the last block of each condition is plotted over a visual representation of the target grid (Fig. 2a & b). Broadly, there was evidence of small imitative contagions in the y direction after watching corner putts that were thought to be correct (shown by the no PE grey square in Fig. 2a). When those same corner putts were presumed to be errors, there was no evidence for imitative or compensatory biases. Watching presumed misses to the centre square also did not yield performance biases (Fig. 2b).

Biases as a result of observation were expected to emerge in amplitude errors, with imitative biases evidenced as overshoot errors and compensatory biases evidenced as undershoot errors. CE in the y axis showed dependency on the presence of a PE based on the actor’s putting location and block, as shown in Fig. 3a and b (see Supplementary Tables S3 & S4). There were no main effects of watched location (β = − 0.11, 95% CI[-4.43, 4.21], p = .96, η²w <0.001), PE (β = -1.62, 95% CI[-5.85, 2.61], p = .45, η²w = 0.001), or block (β = 0.71, 95% CI[-0.07, 1.48], p = .07, η²w = 0.002). Although there was no interaction between PE and watched location (β = -1.07, 95% CI[-5.30, 3.17] p = .62, η²w < 0.001), there was a significant interaction between PE and block (β = 0.96, 95% CI[0.19, 1.74], p = .015, η²w = 0.003) and the expected three-way interaction between PE, watched location and block (β = 0.82, 95% CI[0.05, 1.60], p = .037, η²w = 0.002). Follow up comparisons of the change in error from block 1 to block 5 showed differences between PE and no PE when watching corner putts (p = .02, d = 0.71), reflecting the expected imitative overshoot biases. There were no differences in the change in error across blocks between PE and no PE conditions when watching centre putts (p = .84, d = 0.06).

Constant error (CE) x axis

Biases as a result of observation were also expected to emerge in the horizontal direction, with imitative biases evidenced as right-ward (positive) errors and compensatory biases evidenced as left-ward (negative) errors. As shown in Fig. 3c and d (see Supplementary Tables S5 & S6), CE in the x axis was moderated by the presence of a PE (β = 2.60, 95% CI[0.31, 4.88], p = .026, η²w = 0.006), but it was not moderated by the actor’s putting location as predicted (β = -1.71, 95% CI[-3.99, 0.58], p = .14, η²w = 0.003), nor did it change across blocks (β = − 0.17, 95% CI[-0.49, 0.15], p = .30, η²w < 0.001). The no PE conditions showed higher CE, reflecting right-ward imitative biases in the predicted direction relative to the PE conditions (d = 0.62). However, these right-ward biases were driven by increased error when watching centre putts (Fig. 3d), which was not expected. As shown by individual participant data in Fig. 3d, the mean error in this no PE condition was influenced by one consistently biased outlier with large right-ward biases. A re-analysis of the data with this outlier removed did not change the overall statistical pattern (see Supplementary Tables S7 & S8). Because there were no concerns in testing or data processing for this participant, we have retained their data in the final reporting. There were no significant interactions between watched location and PE (β = -1.34, 95% CI[-3.62, 0.94], p = .25, η²w = 0.002), watched location and block (β = − 0.20, 95% CI[-0.53, 0.12], p = .22, η²w < 0.001), PE and block (β = − 0.03, 95% CI[-0.36, 0.29], p = .84, η²w < 0.001), and watched location, PE and block (β = -0.04, 95% CI[-0.36, 0.29], p = .81, η²w < 0.001).

Order effects in the y axis

Because we ran a fully repeated measures’ design, we also conducted exploratory analyses to determine if the effects depended on the order in which participants completed the conditions (see Supplementary Tables S9 & S10). This analysis should be interpreted with caution due to multiple comparisons and limited power. Order effects were only analyzed for y axis CE, because it showed the strongest evidence of observation-induced performance biases. Participants were divided into either “watch correct” conditions first (so that any errors in later blocks would truly be unexpected) or “watch error” conditions first (where such errors might not be expected, despite the instructions). The data for this analysis is shown in Fig. 4a (“watch corner”) and 4b (“watch centre”).

For the watch corner conditions, there appeared to be an order effect. When watching what were assumed correct putts first, errors increased across block as predicted (i.e., no PE) and decreased across blocks as predicted in the PE condition (at block 5, there was a large difference between conditions, d = 0.78). In contrast when watching errors first (i.e., PE conditions), there were only small differences between the no PE and PE conditions (block 5, d = 0.25). Despite these descriptive trends, there was no main effect of condition-order (β = 0.38, 95% CI[-3.80, 4.57], p = .86, η²w < 0.001) and the Order interactions with Location (β = − 0.60, 95% CI[-4.78, 3.58], p = .78, η²w < 0.001) and Location and Block (β = − 0.72, 95% CI [-1.48, 0.05], p = .07, η²w = 0.001), were not statistically significant. Order effects were not shown for the watch centre conditions (Fig. 4b). At block 5, there were no difference between conditions regardless of whether participants watched ‘correct’ conditions first (d = 0.06), or watched ‘error’ conditions first (d = 0.07).

Discussion

To test if performance biases after observation are influenced by observation-induced prediction errors (PE; differences between expected and observed outcomes), experienced golfers alternated no-feedback putting of a golf ball to the centre square of a target grid and watching videos of an actor putting, with instructions informing as to the accuracy of the observed putts. Expected, yet small imitative biases were present in the y axis only after watching correct corner putts. When these same corner putts were perceived as being errors (i.e., when an observation-induced PE was present), these imitative biases were not present or were suppressed, but they were not compensatory. These results partially support earlier research, demonstrating how observation-induced performance biases are moderated by the perceived accuracy of the demonstration and have theoretical implications as we discuss below.

After seeing an actor correctly putt to a corner target, the experienced golfers showed small imitative performance biases evidenced in distance errors. Imitation effects have been shown across a range of tasks (see11 for a review), where kinematics and/or outcomes become more similar to observed (e.g5–7,15. During observation, the motor program for that observed action is thought to be activated, leading to a covert simulation of the action via the action observation network (e.g27,28,46. This activation of the action for the (different) observed action is thought to result in a conflict in representations/plans between observed and the to-be-executed action7. Connections between the motor and perceptual representations of an action develop with coupled experience (e.g10,16,47.

We introduced an observation-induced PE by informing participants that the actor was aiming to a different location to what they achieved (see6. We expected to replicate imitative and compensatory biases after watching putts to the corner that were thought to be correct or errors, respectively2,6. Observation-induced PEs, the difference between expected and observed actions, have been suggested to update the observer’s internal model and drive compensatory biases6, similar to how forward model sensory prediction errors drive updates in physical action [e.g., 36–39]. We wanted to determine if these observation-induced PEs can bring about compensation in the observer’s putting when there is only a watched PE, but no difference between the watched putting location and the observer’s target when putting. Therefore, we included a condition where participants watched what they thought were errorful putts to the centre target.

Watching perceived errors to the centre target did not elicit any directional biases. This absence of effects under PE conditions is important as it shows that observation-induced PEs are not responsible alone for driving any execution biases in the observer. Seeing a difference between one’s own target and that of the actor is a necessary condition for subsequent biases as a result of errors. Whether a PE is even a necessary condition for biases is still open for debate as seeing someone miss does not necessarily mean that the observer had to predict in advance that they would hit the target. Being aware that an upcoming action will be errorful may allow for discounting/downplaying of that trial and hence suppressing imitative biases (rather than causing compensations). Further work is necessary to better separate the PE from the outcome, potentially only telling people after a trial whether a putt/action was successful or not.

Any observation-induced errors in execution were primarily seen in the y axis, as different from previous reports of biases in both the x and y directions6. Although we did not predict that contagions would emerge primarily as amplitude biases, there is reason to think that directional biases were moderated by the characteristics of the putting task. Golfers began each trial with their feet and the ball in the same position and putted to a straight-ahead target. Because participants had vision of the ball, the target and the putter’s alignment line before hitting the ball, they would be able to calibrate the straight-ahead movement and reduce any potential lateral biases. In a study of dyadic golf putting practice in novices, contagion-type biases among pairs were also only shown in the y axis34, suggesting that this task is less sensitive to lateral biases. However, we did see evidence of small rightward biases associated with watching putts to the centre that were thought to be correct. This was the condition where no biases were expected. Mostly these biases were associated with one outlier individual who completed this control condition first and may therefore reflect some familiarization issues with the putter and the putting surface. However, removal of this individual did not impact the pattern of results (see supplementary S7 & 8) and as such we are unsure why this right-ward bias was evidenced.

There are several potential reasons why the observation of errors and the associated PEs did not reliably elicit compensatory biases. As with above, it may be that seeing a miss has little to no-effect on subsequent performance as individuals are better able to ignore or discount that trial and inhibit any action representations associated with the seen action, in comparison to seeing a correct action/outcome. However, compensation biases have been seen in other work. As such, there may be issues with the (lack of) generalizability of the “compensation” effect due to task differences between our golf putting task and the aiming and throwing tasks where error-driven compensatory biases were seen. In past work6,9, the actor’s kinematics may have divulged more information about the upcoming target error, leading to a kinematic PE when the observed kinematics were different to expected, helping to drive compensation. This may be due to either the increased forces (and hence arm swing) needed to throw a baseball and/or the direct aiming (without an implement), to the target, both different to golf putting. Actions where the body kinematics are more salient or where the kinematics more reliably predict the outcome, may be better at eliciting observation-induced performance contagions relative to actions with subtle kinematics or that have a separation between the action and outcome50.

In golf putting, the adjustments to the swing of the club between targets of different distances is subtle, therefore kinematic PEs would be suppressed. Indeed, we know that expert observers are attuned to the kinematic features of the actions of others48 and that action outcomes can be anticipated from this kinematic information across several different tasks (for reviews see48,49. There is evidence that these kinematic PEs are important in driving compensatory biases from error observation8,50,51. When kinematic PEs were suppressed in a dart-throwing task, by removing the participant’s goal belief/knowledge about the actor’s aiming location, there was no subsequent interference from observation on action51. However, because task error (i.e., success or failure) was also suppressed with this manipulation, the role of kinematic PEs in driving compensation when watching someone miss, is still unclear.

Another explanation as to why the observation of errors did not elicit compensatory biases is that the observed “errors” may have only sometimes elicited PEs, depending on the order in which participants completed the conditions. Here we used a repeated measures’ design, which is different to past work where evidence of compensatory biases have been shown through between-group comparisons (e.g6. Although we adopted this protocol to better control for individual differences, we introduced the potential for order effects to impact the potency of our conditions. One subgroup completed both “error” conditions first, therefore they always saw the actor miss the intended target, whereas the second subgroup completed both “correct” conditions first and thus always saw the actor hit the intended target. This accrued experience with the actor might have influenced expectations and hence the strength of predictions about their upcoming performance, moderating the instruction effects.

We ran analyses to test for potential order effects, even though we did not statistically power our study to detect for group-order effects (where ns = 11–12/group-order). As was illustrated in Fig. 4a, when participants first watched the actor correctly hit the target and then completed the PE (miss) conditions second, expectations for errors should be low. Indeed, under these conditions, there was descriptive evidence of compensatory biases in the y axis. In contrast, there was no evidence of compensatory biases when the “watch miss” (i.e., PE) conditions were completed first. For this group, expectations for performing correctly would be low, potentially moderating the strength of the instructional manipulation alerting to the miss. When we ran analyses on these data, there were, however, no statistically significant effects involving condition-order (only a trend for a 3-way interaction between Order, putting Location and Block, β = − 0.72, p = .07).

There is mounting evidence that contextual and probabilistic information is integrated with the incoming information to make inferences about the actions of others (e.g52–54. For example, after gaining experience watching the actions of a penalty shot taker, predictions made by handball goalkeepers were subsequently biased according to the learned tendencies of the penalty taker52. Integration of these different sources of information has been formalized through predictive processing approaches using Bayesian modeling (e.g55. Accordingly, the brain combines incoming stimuli on a given trial with prior knowledge to make predictions56,57. Descriptive evidence from our study, showing different types of errors as a result of watching correct or missed putts early or later in testing blocks, suggests that both prior knowledge about a person’s tendencies and manipulated predictions concerning the intended versus actual aiming location impacted on observation-induced biases. Further work is required to independently manipulate these sources of error, where the probability of an action outcome changes. It is also possible that prediction or expectation errors are not (solely) responsible for any observation induced biases. Reinforcement type learning principles may operate on action selection processes in the observer to avoid potential ‘failures’ 58,59, unintentionally causing biases.

As with most experimental studies, there are several methodological limitations that should be considered in its interpretation. As mentioned, task characteristics of golf putting may have reduced the salience of kinematic PEs during observation compared to actions that contain richer kinematic information, limiting the generalizability of this effect50. To test this idea, one could manipulate the salience of kinematic PEs in aiming movements by showing misses to small or large target distributions and compare the resulting performance biases. While the repeated measures’ design was informative in highlighting the trends for order effects related to the expectancy of errors, it may have limited our ability to detect compensatory errors across the entire sample.

In conclusion, we provide evidence that any interference on action from observation depends on the presumed “correctness” of the observed attempt, partially replicating prior work. However, different from previous studies, any interference effects were not a result of observation-induced PEs, as we did not see compensation biases in these PE conditions; when watching different putts to the participant (corner putts) or when watching the same putts to the participant (i.e., centre conditions). Any observation-biases were dependent on seeing differences between the observer’s and actor’s intended goals (not only a PE as intimated in prior work6). Imitative biases present when watching ‘correct’ corner putts, were absent or suppressed when those same corner putts were thought to be target misses. However, evidence for imitative biases was only seen in the y-axis and these were small effects, suggesting that any potential observation-induced ‘contagions’ were perhaps not sufficient to drive biases in the current task, where aspects of our task and design could have potentially moderated the power of detecting such potentially small effects. There was descriptive evidence that accrued experiences moderated the influence of PE manipulations on subsequent biases (as indicated by group-order effects), which requires further testing. These results inform on our understanding of action observation, by showing that performance is not merely impacted by a direct imitation of observed action, but through the integration of processes that are sensitive to the error. It is unclear whether this is done in a prospective or retrospective manner, based on expecting a success and seeing a miss, or merely seeing an error after the action has ended. Further research directed to uncovering the source of these biases in addition to their reliability and generalizability is still needed.

Methods

The study hypotheses, methods and analysis plan were preregistered and are available at https://osf.io/p629c. All study procedures were approved by the institutional behavioural research ethics’ board and all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Participants

Twenty-three experienced golfers (4 who identified as women and 19 who identified as men, ages 21–60 year) who responded to the study advert participated. All participants reported putting right-handed and had on average 10 years (SD = 6.8 year) of golf experience. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and remuneration at a rate of $15/hr was provided (the study took ~ 2 h).

We had powered the study based on a sample size estimate of n = 22 participants calculated using the G*Power 3.1.9.760 “ANOVA: Repeated measures, within factors” test. Power (1 – beta) was set at 0.80 and Type 1 error probability at 0.05. Effect size estimates of ηp2 = 0.28 and 0.16 were identified in two experiments where dissociable imitative and compensatory biases were shown6. G*Power 3.1.9.4 was used to transform the partial eta squared values to f values using the ‘as SPSS’ correction (Cohens f = .62 and .44), resulting in total sample sizes of N = 12 and N = 22. Despite using a repeated measures’ design and using linear mixed effects models to analyse our data, which are thought to be more powerful than traditional ANOVA, we opted for the more conservative estimate of N = 22 participants, given changes to the task and design and because we wanted to be sufficiently powered to make replication-based conclusions. However, we exceeded the target number by one, due to recruitment efforts where one additional expert had signed up to participate.

Task and apparatus

As illustrated in Fig. 5, participants putted a golf ball to and watched an actor putt to a nine square grid made up of 30 cm x 30 cm squares marked on the 245 cm x 549 cm putting surface. The synthetic putting surface (Camry Golf Greens) was laid flat on an indoor floor, such that it was level and consistent throughout the study. To control for roll variability due to friction, the surface was checked for debris and defects before each participant and was brushed when necessary. Participants were instructed to aim to a target that was marked at 205 cm from the golf ball start position in the centre of the middle square. A NEXIGO 1080p HD web camera was fixed to a gooseneck tripod and positioned approximately 2.5 m veridical to the target, capturing a photograph of the golf ball on the grid. A spirit level was fixed to the camera to ensure it was aimed parallel to the ground. The actor and participants used the same standard 92 cm golf putter to putt the golf balls.

In all execution blocks, participants wore visual occlusion glasses (Translucent Technologies Inc., Toronto, ON) and noise cancelling headphones. The glasses were translucent in the pre-putt period and were trigged via a custom circuit using a 12 V sound-activated relay module to occlude at the moment of putter-ball contact. The sound sensor was calibrated to trigger the relay from the sound of putter-ball contact and was placed at a standardized distance, 5 cm from the ball’s start position, to ensure consistent sound detection. While we did not directly measure the latency from sound detection to occlusion, the system is specified by the manufacturer to respond with minimal delay (timing accuracy ± 5%) and pilot testing indicated no noticeable variability or delays in the timing of the occlusion onset. Vision was manually restored by the experimenter after the putted ball was scored and removed from the grid. The glasses and noise cancelling headphones were not worn in full vision blocks of the familiarization and pretest and throughout ‘washout’ blocks between conditions.

In each of the four experimental conditions, execution blocks were interspersed with observation blocks showing videos of an experienced golfer putting to various locations on the grid, dependent on the condition (corner or centre). The videos were projected on a 220 cm by 290 cm life-size screen via a ViewSonic projector. We filmed a male actor (age = 27 year, height = 173 cm), with ~ 10 years of golf experience (and averaging ~ 5 rounds of golf /year) from above and behind, capturing the kinematics of the actor’s movement and putter and the trajectory of the ball in relation to the target grid. Each video clip lasted approximately 7 s and depicted the entire putt from backswing to the ball stopping point. The distribution of outcomes was based on methods adopted in Ikegami et al.6, whereby the majority of putts were to the corner, but with variability in order to make the outcomes more believable. The actor’s putts were divided into five groups of twenty putts, which were grouped to show putts towards each corner of the grid (top-left, top-right, bottom-left or bottom-right corners) and the centre target. In Fig. 6 we have shown putting distributions for putts to the corner (top-right corner only illustrated in Fig. 6a) and to the centre target (Fig. 6b). For the groups of videos showing putts to the corners of the grid, videos included twelve putts (60%) to the corner square, two putts (10%) each to the three adjacent squares and one putt each (5%) to the two orthogonal corners. For the group of videos showing putts to the centre square of the grid, videos included twelve putts (60%) to the centre square (square 5) and one putt each (5%) to the adjacent squares 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, and 9. Videos were presented in a random order within each group of videos.

Design and procedure

An overview of the experimental phases is shown in Fig. 7. After completing a demographics’ questionnaire, a golf experience questionnaire and informed consent, participants were familiarized to the task and apparatus and completed five “practice” putts to the centre target of the grid with and without visual feedback. Participant then completed a pre-test where the putts were scored, comprising five putts to the centre target with and without vision in a blocked design.

The four experimental conditions were completed in a blocked, repeated measures design. In each condition, five blocks of no-vision execution and four blocks of observation were repeated in an interleaved order. Execution blocks always consisted of 10 trials aiming to the centre target of the grid. Participants had full vision of the environment up until the moment of ball contact. After the stopping position of the ball was recorded and the ball removed, vision was restored. No feedback about the accuracy of the putt was provided. The observation blocks were comprised of a continuous loop of 20 putts, lasting ~ 5 min. Participants verbally reported the outcome grid number for each trial in order to keep attention focused on the task. Between each condition, participants completed a washout block of ten putts to the centre target of the grid with full visual feedback, in order to facilitate a return to baseline performance.

Conditions

In a fully orthogonal design, we systematically covaried the presence of an observation-induced PE (no PE, PE) with the putting location of the actor (centre, corner). Before watching each set of videos, participants were informed of the intended target location of the actor (centre target or a corner target). In the no PE conditions, participants were shown videos where they believed the actor was making successful putts to the instructed target. In the PE conditions, participants were shown videos where they believed the actor missed the intended target. The presence of a PE was covaried with the putting location of the actor, such that participants saw both “hits” (no PE) and “misses” (PE) to the centre and corner of the grid. The order of observation conditions was randomized across participants based on four pre-determined orders as detailed in Table 1. Each observation order had either all PE conditions first (order 1 and 2), but differed by the grid area that they saw the actor putt (centre or corner) or all no PE conditions first (order 3 and 4), again differing based on the actor’s putting location. The grid corner that the actor putted to in the corner conditions, and the informed actor’s target location in the “PE, watch centre” condition was varied across the orders.

Data processing

Putts were scored from the photographs of each trial’s outcomes using a LabVIEW (National Instruments Inc.) program62. Our primary measure was constant error (CE) in the x (medial-lateral) and y (anterior-posterior) axes, capturing bias errors in different directions. We also measured bivariate variable error (BVE) capturing two-dimensional variable error. BVE was calculated as the square root of a participant’s mean squared distance of putts in a block (k) from their mean, based on a block of 10 trials (xc, yc). The mean was defined as the positionally typical shot (average x and y position) for a participant’s block of trials:

Because we systematically varied the corner that the participants watched, we “flipped” the direction (x and y) of the CE values across conditions, such that the different corners could be analysed together. Specifically, y-axis CE values from bottom-left and bottom-right corners were multiplied by -1 and x-axis CE values from the top-left and bottom-right corners were multiplied by -1. Therefore in the watch corner conditions, overshoot and right-ward biased errors reflected “imitative contagion” and undershoot and left-ward biased errors reflected “compensatory contagion” (regardless of corner).

Analysis

Analysis was conducted in R63. Linear Mixed Effect (LME) models (lme4 package;64 were used to compare across the conditions for all analyses. LME was used instead of traditional ANOVA to better model correlations in the repeated measures design. Models were constructed with a nested random effects structure44 and likelihood ratio testing was used to evaluate for model fit using the Akaike information criterion65. Normality of residuals was checked using diagnostic plots and met before proceeding with the models. Within participant eta squares (η²w;43) values are reported to show the proportion of variance explained by main effects and interactions in the overall model. The emmeans package66 was used to follow up interactions involving more than two means and calculate Cohen’s d effect sizes. Two-tailed tests were used for all analyses and an alpha of p < .05 was used to denote statistical significance.

To confirm that the washout blocks between conditions facilitated a return to baseline from any biases in the experimental blocks, CE in the x and y axes were compared in one-way, repeated measures models across the pre-test and washout blocks. Treatment coding was used to compare each washout block to the mean of the reference block, which was set as the pre-test.

Across the experimental conditions, each of the error measures were compared in a 2 Prediction Error (PE, no PE) x 2 watched Location (watch corner, watch centre) design, with blocks added as a continuous factor nested in each condition. These two-level factors were sum contrast coded allowing for main effects to be estimated and were added as fixed effects. Model fit was tested with the addition of ‘predictor’ variables, including putting pre-test variability (i.e., BVE) and golf experience (years of regular golf involvement). These variables were only included in analysis of BVE due to improved model fit for this dependent measure only.

We also conducted an exploratory analysis to test for order effects across the subgroups. We pooled participants who completed the experiment with Order 1 and Order 2, and Order 3 and Order 4 (Table 1) to create “watch correct” first (n = 12) and “watch error” first (n = 11) subgroups, respectively. This fixed effect of condition-Order was included in subsequent LME analysis as previously detailed.
