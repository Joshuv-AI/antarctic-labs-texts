Refractory Ascites and the Alfapump
Authors: Jasleen Singh, Jayza Calderon, Sammy Saab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAscites is often the first decompensation in patients with cirrhosis and is associated with reduced survival. The formation of ascites is complex and thought to be due to an increase in hydrostatic pressure, a decrease in oncotic pressure, and increased lymph production. Standard of care for ascites includes dietary sodium restriction, use of loop diuretics and aldosterone antagonists, large-volume paracentesis (LVP), and at times transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS). Alfapump was designed in response to the need for alternative treatments for patients with refractory ascites, particularly in those who are ineligible for TIPS. The initial PIONEER trial conducted in Europe demonstrated the benefit of Alfapump with reduced need for LVP and ultimately led to the commercial launch of the pump in Europe in 2012. The MOSAIC and POSEIDON trials not only proved the efficacy of the Alfapump but also provided an alternative to surgery by performing pump implantation with interventional radiology. While the benefit with reduced need for LVP has been shown in multiple studies, the risk of renal impairment and infections such as peritonitis have also been observed. The renal injury mostly responds to albumin, which should be considered in certain cases. The infection risk is mitigated with antibiotic prophylaxis and early treatment of confirmed infections. Consensus recommendations from Europe have also helped with best practices in patients with the pump. Premarket approval was granted by the Food and Drug Administration for the use of Alfapump in patients with cirrhosis and refractory or recurrent ascites in December 2024. In the United States, Alfapump may be a good alternative for cirrhotic patients with refractory ascites who are ineligible for TIPS and potentially as a bridge to liver transplantation.Keywords: Cirrhosis, Refractory Ascites, TIPS, Alfapump, Treatment

Introduction

Ascites is the most common initial decompensation in patients with cirrhosis and portal hypertension.1 The annual risk for the development of ascites ranges from 5% to 10% and is associated with worsened survival at 5 years from 80% to 30%.1 The pathogenesis of ascites is complex and not fully understood. In the profibrotic state of cirrhosis, stasis within blood vessels leads to an increase in vasodilatory elements such as nitric oxide.2,3 In particular, splanchnic vasodilatation causes relative hypoperfusion to the kidneys, thus leading to activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and retention of sodium and water.2,3 Once activated, there is an increase in circulating blood volume causing an increase in hydrostatic pressure within the liver.2,3 This increase in pressure coupled with decreased oncotic pressure from hypoalbuminemia and vascular permeability promotes leakage of fluid into the peritoneal cavity.2,3 Typically, the lymphatic system within the liver is very efficient at returning excess fluid to the circulation.3 However, in cirrhosis, there is also thought to be an increase in lymph production, and with an increase in circulating blood volume, the lymphatic system becomes overwhelmed.3 These proposed mechanisms of ascites formation shown in Figure 1 inform the basis of treatment of ascites.

Traditional Management of Ascites

The grading system for ascites helps guide treatment and describes the degree of severity based on volume. Grade 1 ascites is considered to be mild and detected only on imaging such as ultrasound.1,4 This type of ascites is often responsive to dietary sodium restriction and diuretics.1,4 Grade 2 is moderate ascites that causes symmetric distention of the abdomen and has recurred at least 3 times in a 12-month period despite sodium restriction and diuretics.1,4 Lastly, grade 3 is large ascites associated with significant distention of the abdomen and refractory to medical therapy, thus warranting large-volume paracentesis (LVP).1,4 According to the International Club of Ascites, refractory ascites is defined as ascites that cannot be mobilized with medical therapy or recurs early after initiation of medical therapy.4 Refractory ascites can then be categorized as diuretic-resistant or diuretic-intractable. Patients with diuretic-resistant ascites are considered refractory to dietary sodium restriction and maximal diuretic use. Diuretic-intractable ascites occurs in patients who are unable to tolerate maximal doses of diuretics due to factors such as electrolyte imbalances or renal injury.4,5 Figure 2 demonstrates conventional treatments for refractory ascites.

Dietary Sodium Restriction

As described previously, sodium retention is one of the factors involved in the pathogenesis of ascites and therefore is an appropriate target for management. The goal is to decrease sodium intake and increase sodium excretion to promote a net negative balance of sodium.6 Therefore, the recommended amount of dietary sodium intake is 88 mmol per day, which is the equivalent of about 2000 mg.4,6 Dietary sodium restriction may be sufficient for those patients who have higher urinary sodium excretion, but often adjunctive therapy with diuretics is warranted.6

Diuretics

Aldosterone antagonists such as spironolactone are considered first-line diuretic therapy in ascites.1,6 By directly targeting activation of renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, aldosterone antagonists block distal tubular reabsorption.1,6,7 Studies have shown the efficacy of aldosterone antagonists alone for the treatment of ascites, but at times, combination diuretic use may be most efficacious.1,8,9 Spironolactone is initiated at a dose of 100 mg per day and can be titrated to a daily maximum of 400 mg.1 Loop diuretics such as furosemide are often added to the treatment regimen to achieve maximal diuresis and natriuresis. They block reabsorption of sodium at the loop of Henle, which allows sodium to be transported to the distal tubule. This allows aldosterone antagonists to ultimately inhibit sodium reabsorption distally.1,6, 7, 8, 9 The dose of furosemide ranges from an initial dose of 40 mg daily up to 160 mg per day.1 Adverse effects of diuretics include acute kidney injury (AKI), electrolyte abnormalities, hepatic encephalopathy (HE), gynecomastia (more commonly with spironolactone), and muscle cramps.1

Large-Volume Paracentesis

LVP is considered first-line management of refractory ascites, often with concurrent albumin administration.1 An early study established LVP as an effective therapeutic measure for ascites. A randomized controlled trial (RCT) published in 1987 divided patients into 2 groups: group 1 was treated with paracentesis and intravenous albumin, whereas group 2 was treated with diuretics or LeVeen shunt if not responsive to diuretics.10 In group 1, elimination of ascites occurred in 96.5% of patients as compared to 72.8% in group 2 (P < .05). Additionally, there was notably less HE, renal impairment, and electrolyte disturbances in group 1.10 However, one complication to monitor is the development of postparacentesis circulatory dysfunction, which results from splanchnic vasodilatation after paracentesis.11 This leads to an increase in plasma renin activity and further retention of free water and sodium, and this rapid reaccumulation of ascites can cause electrolyte disturbances, renal injury, and HE.11 Thus, in order to protect renal function and minimize rapid fluid shifts, albumin is indicated in LVP, particularly when >5 L removed.1

Transjugular Intrahepatic Portosystemic Shunt

Transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) is an endovascular shunt used to connect the portal system to the systemic circulation in an effort to treat major complications of portal hypertension such as refractory ascites and gastroesophageal varices.12 Multiple meta-analyses and reviews within the literature have demonstrated that TIPS is superior to LVPs and may even improve transplant-free survival.13, 14, 15, 16, 17 One study highlighted that patients may benefit from “early” TIPS, meaning those with a lower frequency of paracentesis pre-TIPS achieved more effective control of ascites post-TIPS.17 Although serious complications of TIPS are rare, there are certain risks that need to be considered. Procedure-related complications include intraperitoneal bleeding, arterial or biliary injury, liver infarction, puncture of liver capsule, hemobilia, in-stent thrombosis, and sepsis.12 Perhaps the most significant risk after TIPS is that of HE, with an incidence of 30%–50% of patients with cirrhosis.18 There are several risk factors for HE development, which include prior HE, advanced age, liver dysfunction, renal dysfunction, and sarcopenia.12,18 Strategies to mitigate the risk of HE include the use of smaller diameter TIPS (8 mm compared to 10 mm) and the use of rifaximin 14 days prior to TIPS placement, as demonstrated in an RCT published in 2021.19 Despite highly effective treatments such as lactulose and rifaximin for the treatment of HE, up to 8% of patients develop refractory HE post-TIPS placement, often requiring revision of the shunt.20

Considerations in Patients With Refractory Ascites

Hepatorenal syndrome, and in particular hepatorenal syndrome (HRS)-AKI, is another manifestation of portal hypertension and splanchnic vasodilation, leading to decreased renal perfusion and a precipitous decline in renal function.1 As refractory ascites and HRS-AKI have similar pathogenesis, the 2 conditions may develop concomitantly. Additionally, the presence and treatment of refractory ascites may also precipitate HRS-AKI, and thus, patients with refractory ascites should be monitored carefully for signs of renal injury.1

To add to the multi-organ complexities of portal hypertension, cirrhotic cardiomyopathy is another complication of cirrhosis and portal hypertension, whereby systolic and/or diastolic dysfunction and impaired systolic and electrophysiologic response to stressors may develop in the absence of underlying cardiac disease. The initial increase in cardiac output as a response to vasodilation may ultimately lead to cardiac dysfunction and can further contribute to refractory ascites.

Nonselective beta-blockers (NSBBs) have been studied in cirrhosis and portal hypertension and are recommended as primary prophylaxis for variceal bleeding over endoscopic band ligation and secondary prophylaxis.21 NSBBs improve survival and prevent decompensations such as ascites in patients with cirrhosis.21 However, their role in refractory ascites is more controversial. While NSBBs may prevent development of ascites, they may precipitate renal injury and HRS in patients with refractory ascites and low mean arterial pressure. Patients with cirrhotic cardiomyopathy may be more sensitive to beta-blockade, leading to arterial underfilling and organ hypoperfusion.21,22 Therefore, NSBBs should be used with caution in patients with refractory ascites and cardiorenal dysfunction and should be discontinued in the setting of AKI.21

Antibiotics have also been studied in patients with cirrhosis and ascites as a means to prevent gastrointestinal bacterial translocation and provide primary prophylaxis for infections such as spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP). One RCT in particular evaluated the effect of rifaximin vs placebo on survival in patients with cirrhosis and ascites.23 The authors found that rifaximin use did not improve survival in patients with cirrhosis and ascites, as compared to placebo. Antimicrobial resistance data are emerging and have contributed to the overall hesitation to prescribe antibiotics as primary prophylaxis, especially in the absence of data supporting this practice.

Other Interventions for Refractory Ascites

The only curative strategy for the treatment of refractory ascites is liver transplantation (LT). In cases where a patient needs a bridge to transplant or is not a candidate for LT, other treatments need to be explored. TIPS was discussed previously as a potential treatment option. Another area of research development focused on peritoneovenous shunts (PVS) such as the LeVeen shunt and, most recently, the Denver shunt.24,25 Given the access to the venous system, many complications may result after a PVS, such as infection, bleeding thrombosis, and disseminated intravascular coagulation. Ultimately, shunt patency rates are low, with one study quoting only 18.6% of shunts remained patent at 2 years.26

Peritoneal catheters in nonmalignant ascites have also been studied, but the high rates of infection are suboptimal. Peritoneal infections and catheter obstructions may be reasonable risks for patients with palliative needs but may not be acceptable in those who may be transplant candidates or with potential for improved prognosis in the future.27

The development of TIPS as discussed previously was key in the treatment of ascites without subjecting patients to as significant of risk as with PVS. However, given the contraindications and risks associated with TIPS, repeated LVP, and PVS, the need for an effective and safe device to treat refractory ascites emerged, thus leading to the production of the Alfapump.

Alfapump Design

The automated low-flow ascites pump (Alfapump) concept and prototypes were developed in the early 2000s to address the need for more effective treatments of refractory ascites.28 The Alfapump is a battery-powered pump that is implanted subcutaneously with one catheter positioned in the ascitic fluid, which then drains the fluid through a second catheter subcutaneously inserted into the bladder.29,30 The patient then excretes the ascitic fluid in the bladder through normal urination.29,30 The pump is programmed by the physician to determine how much ascitic fluid should be drained into the bladder, based on the patient’s schedule, and avoids fluid removal at nighttime.29,30 The pump does require wireless charging through the skin for about 20–30 minutes per day. Major Clinical Trials of Alfapump

PIONEER Study

Results of the PIONEER clinical study laid the groundwork for approval of Alfapump and eventually commercial launch of the device in Europe in 2012.31 The study recruited 40 patients from 4 European countries, with Alfapump implanted between February 2010 and June 2011.31 Generally speaking, the patients recruited to the study were not candidates for TIPS for various reasons. Patients who were ultimately included were ≥18 years old, had cirrhosis and refractory ascites (as defined by the recurrence of ascites with 4 weeks of LVP despite maximum doses of diuretics or diuretic intolerance), expected survival >6 months, serum creatinine <2.0 mg/dL for at least 7 days prior to study entry, and total bilirubin <5 mg/dL. Patients with active infection, malignancy, extensive loculated ascites, portal hypertensive gastrointestinal bleeding or HE in the 2 weeks prior to study inclusion, obstructive uropathy, or contraindications to anesthesia were excluded from the study. The primary outcome was safety, and secondary outcomes included the need for paracentesis, pump system function, and hemodynamic changes. Most of the patients were male: 43% with alcohol-associated cirrhosis with a median of 3.4 LVP needed monthly and a mean Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) of 12.6. All patients received general anesthesia during pump insertion and perioperative antibiotics. Additional safety recommendations were made during the study by the Data Safety Monitoring Board. Serious adverse events (AEs) could be broadly characterized as related to insertion or function of the pump, inadequate preoperative and postoperative care, and cirrhosis-related. For example, bladder catheter dislodgment was rectified by creating a new type of catheter less prone to insertion issues. Two patients developed pump pocket infections refractory to antibiotics. Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis occurred in 32.5% of patients, which was higher than expected. With the use of norfloxacin prophylaxis, the number of infections decreased substantially. Eleven patients (27.5%) experienced renal dysfunction, and this risk was mitigated with the use of albumin pre- and post-pump insertion and avoidance of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. HE developed in 32.5% of patients and responded to standard therapy. The need for LVP after pump insertion substantially decreased from a median number of 3.4 times monthly prior to 0.24 per month after pump implantation. Five patients underwent LT after pump insertion with no additional complications due to patients having received the pump. The authors noted that when strategies to reduce risks of complications were instituted, the safety profile was acceptable, as benefit was observed with a decreased need for LVP. The Alfapump was subsequently commercially launched in Europe at the European Association for the Study of the Liver in 2012.

MOSAIC Study

In North America, a feasibility and safety study of Alfapump insertion by interventional radiology (IR) was conducted, differing from prior European experience with pump insertion done by hepatobiliary surgeons under general anesthesia. The MOSAIC study recruited 30 patients from Canada and the United States who were ≥21 years old, refractory ascites with >1 LVP monthly, survival expected to be >3 months, and diagnostic paracentesis with a neutrophil count <250/μL within 24 hours of pump implantation.32 Patients were excluded if they had >2 infections in the last 6 months, loculated ascites, or current malignancy or were eligible for TIPS. The subjects were mostly male, with alcohol (30%) and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (30%) cirrhosis and a mean MELD of 11.4. All patients were managed by a hepatologist and screened for varices prior. Detailed methods of pump implantation were included in the study. The pump was implanted successfully in all 30 patients, with most (63%) requiring general anesthesia and 1 requiring open surgery. Antibiotic prophylaxis was used in all patients. Six patients (20%) developed infections, and only 1 patient was diagnosed with bacterial peritonitis and 1 with urinary tract infection. Four patients required surgical revision, and 2 explants were performed. The procedure-related complication with a total adverse event rate of 31% was to be expected, according to the authors.32 This study demonstrated the feasibility of IR implantation of the Alfapump, which was a key factor in entering the North American market.

POSEIDON Study

The most recent and major study to focus on in this review is the North American-led POSEIDON study.33 Inclusion criteria were >18 years old with a history of cirrhosis with recurrent or refractory ascites requiring ≥2 therapeutic paracentesis (TP) in the prior 30 days and had refused, were ineligible, or had contraindications to TIPS. Patients were excluded if they had loculated ascites, >1 systemic or abdominal infection in the previous 6 months, Clostridium difficile infection in the past year, history of hepatocellular carcinoma outside of Milan criteria, bladder cancer, or any nonhepatic malignancy unless it had been treated and in remission for ≥3 years. Additional exclusion criteria were body mass index >40, evidence of urinary bladder obstruction, presence of TIPS or surgical shunt, previous solid organ transplant, contraindications to general anesthesia, MELD-Na >20, and moderate renal dysfunction defined as >1.5 mg/dL. The Pivotal cohort included 71 patients who were observed for 3 months to ensure they met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Some centers did not have the experience necessary for pump implantation, so up to 3 patients per site underwent the procedure, which formed the roll-in cohort, comprising 39 patients who met inclusion and exclusion criteria but did not require the 3 months of observation. The roll-in cohort was not included in the results of this study. Eventually a total of 40 patients underwent Alfapump insertion. The co-primary effectiveness endpoints were (1) per-patient ratio of average number of TPs >1.5 L and (2) proportion of patients with ≥50% reduction in the number of TPs in the preimplantation vs postimplantation periods. The primary safety endpoint was pump-related AEs. Two quality of life (QoL) questionnaires were also administered and their data recorded including the Short Form-36 and Ascites Questionnaire. All patients received antibiotic prophylaxis, a probiotic to prevent C difficile infection, and albumin on days 14 and 28 after the procedure. The patients were mostly male, with alcohol (47.5%) and metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (37.5%) cirrhosis and a mean MELD-Na of 15.2. The patients had a mean of 3.2 TPs monthly in the previous 3 months. In terms of the endpoints, the mean per-patient ratio of preimplant to postimplant TPs was 0.18 (P < .001 vs performance goal of 0.5), and 76.8% of patients demonstrated at least 50% reduction in the number of TPs postimplant (P < .001 vs performance goal of 50%). A notable improvement in the Short Form-36 and Ascites questionnaires was observed in the cohort. The study also demonstrated a gradual improvement in liver function parameters including international normalized ratio and bilirubin. Two important AEs that were recorded included renal events and infections. Twenty-three patients experienced AKI, and most responded to albumin. While nonpump-related infections were also included, 15 infections were attributed to the pump implantation, 3 of whom were diagnosed with bacterial peritonitis. There were 5 device malfunctions and 5 events of device deficiencies such as bladder spasm. Six patients (15%) had explants of the Alfapump due to pump-related AEs. Five patients in the cohort died by the end of the 6-month period, 3 of which were related to liver disease, thus reflecting an overall survival of 87.2% at 6 months. The authors concluded that Alfapump was a safe and effective treatment in refractory ascites with manageable complications, including renal injury and infection.

The PIONEER study, MOSAIC study, and POSEIDON study demonstrated the efficacy and safety of Alfapump, ultimately leading to premarket approval (PMA) of Alfapump by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in December 2024. Table 1 summarizes the 3 major trials discussed in detail previously.

Additional Alfapump Studies

To date, only 1 RCT has been published comparing Alfapump to LVP.34 The study was designed as a prospective, multicenter, open-label trial of 7 European centers. Patients were included if ≥18 years old (men and nonpregnant women) with cirrhosis and refractory ascites requiring LVP who were ineligible for TIPS. Many of the exclusion criteria were similar to those in the PIONEER, MOSAIC, and POSEIDON studies and, generally speaking, excluded patients with recurrent infections, loculated ascites, obstructive uropathy, and contraindications to general anesthesia. The primary endpoint was time to first LVP. Secondary endpoints were overall paracentesis requirements, safety (particularly renal injury and infections), QoL questionnaire (Chronic Liver Disease Questionnaire), and survival. Patients were randomized 1:1 to Alfapump or standard of care (SoC). Ultimately, 60 patients met inclusion and exclusion criteria between July 2012 and February 2015 and were randomized: 29 to Alfapump and 31 to SoC. Ultimately 17/27 in the Alfapump group and 21/31 in the SoC group completed the 6-month study. The patients were mostly male with alcohol as the most common etiology of cirrhosis, with a mean MELD of 12.2 in the Alfapump group and 11.3 in the SoC group. All Alfapumps were implanted surgically and with general anesthesia. The time to LVP was significantly longer in the Alfapump group compared to SoC, and the median number of LVPs was also lower in the Alfapump group compared to SoC (both P < .001). Ninety percent of the total number of LVPs occurred in the SoC group. In the SoC group, 90% required LVP by the end of the study period compared to 37% in the Alfapump group. Improvements in the QoL questionnaire were noted in the abdominal symptoms and activity scores in the Alfapump group, whereas improvement was observed in the SoC patients. In terms of safety, the Alfapump group experienced a higher rate of renal and urinary disorders compared to the SoC group (P < .001), and infection rates were similar between the 2 groups including SBP and urinary tract infection (P = .883). Twelve out of 27 patients with Alfapump had at least 1 device deficiency and 3 patients required explant of the pump. This was an important RCT demonstrating the substantial benefit in reduced need for LVP and improvement in QoL as well as acceptable safety profile with Alfapump when compared to the SoC with LVP.

As all of the major trials conducted on Alfapump excluded patients who were eligible for TIPS, Will et al sought out to determine patient outcomes in those undergoing Alfapump vs TIPS. This was a single-center retrospective study that included 19 patients with TIPS and 40 patients with Alfapump between 2012 and 2018.35 Patients with Alfapump had worse liver function (mean MELD-Na 15.9 vs 12.6; P = .04) and higher incidence of HE (47.5% vs 15.8%; P = .02) than TIPS patients. Since the patients with Alfapump had worse liver function, the comparative analysis was performed between TIPS and liver-function-adjusted Alfapump patients with Child-Pugh (CP) score <10 and MELD-Na <15. Rates of HE, need for LVP, and infections were similar in the groups. More patients in the 2 Alfapump groups of CP score <10 and MELD-Na <15 had prerenal impairment, HRS, and need for reintervention than the TIPS patients. Finally, transplant-free survival at 12 months was higher in the TIPS group as compared to the Alfapump groups (TIPS 65% vs Alfapump CP score <10 26%, P = .01; TIPS 65% vs Alfapump MELD-Na <15 23%, P = .02). The authors concluded that renal dysfunction is a major complication of Alfapump usage. However, it may be a good alternative in those patients who have advanced liver disease or are not eligible for TIPS.

While no head-to-head RCTs have been conducted to date comparing Alfapump to other devices such as TIPS or peritoneal catheters (PeCas), one retrospective study did attempt to answer this question. The single-center study was conducted in Germany and included patients who underwent TIPS, PeCa, and ascites pump between 2012 and 2023.36 A total of 38 patients with an ascites pump, 187 patients with a PeCa, and 243 patients with TIPS were included in the study. The first analysis (analysis I) comparing PeCa to ascites pump demonstrated similar rates of AKI (PeCa 82% vs ascites pump 87%, P = .46) and mortality (PeCa 41% vs ascites pump 29%, P = .24) but a higher rate of peritonitis (PeCa 74% vs ascites pump 33%, P = .004) and explantation (PeCa 66% vs ascites pump 35%, P = .005) in the PeCa group. Analysis II compared TIPS to ascites pump, and no difference in mortality (TIPS 41% vs ascites pump 25%, P = .12) and peritonitis (TIPS 29% vs ascites pump 25%, P = .34) was observed between the 2 groups. However, the risk of AKI was substantially higher in the ascites pump group (TIPS 34% vs ascites pump 82%, P < .001). Finally, analysis III between the PeCa and TIPS demonstrated similar mortality rates (TIPS 38% vs PeCa 39%, P = .9) but an increased risk of AKI and peritonitis in the PeCa group (TIPS 33% vs PeCa 82%, P < .001; TIPS 33% vs PeCa 68%, P < .001, respectively). This was an important study demonstrating the risks associated with PeCas, and while no direct comparisons can be made, ascites pump may be a reasonable option in patients who may not be a candidate for TIPS.

Although the intended use according to the FDA’s PMA of Alfapump is refractory ascites in patients with cirrhosis, 1 European study did also evaluate the pump in patients with malignant ascites. A retrospective study across 6 European centers of 17 patients with Alfapump for palliative management of ascites was included in the study.37 The primary endpoint was time to the first LVP. Most of the patients were female, and a majority (35.3%) had a hepatic malignancy. Four patients experienced device-related complications and 4 patients had complications related to renal injury or infection.37 Although the primary endpoint could not be calculated due to the lack of significant LVP need after Alfapump, the median number of paracenteses was reduced from 4 pre-implant to 1 post-implant (P = .005). Only one patient experienced pump explant. This was the first real-world study to demonstrate the effectiveness of Alfapump in malignant ascites, which may ultimately be an expanded use for the device.

Lastly, as mentioned previously, the only cure for refractory ascites is LT, but this may pose surgical difficulty at the time of transplant. As such, a retrospective single-center study included 22 patients who were listed for LT and had received an Alfapump between 2012 and 2018.38 The primary outcome was feasibility of LT in patients with Alfapump. Most of the patients were male, with alcohol as the most common etiology of cirrhosis and mean MELD 15.1. Fourteen out of 22 patients underwent LT and 8 patients (all male) died prior to LT. Three of the 14 patients who were transplanted had had the pump explanted before transplant due to pump-related complications. Four (28.6%) of the 14 transplanted patients died after LT, and no deaths were related to the pump. Only 1 death prior to LT could be attributed with certainty to the pump. The authors concluded that Alfapump did not pose a significant risk or obstacle for LT and may be a reasonable option in patients awaiting LT.

Considerations for Use of Alfapump

As the approval for use of Alfapump in the United States is relatively new, the risks and benefits of Alfapump need to be weighed in potential candidates.

According to the FDA instructions for use, the intended use of Alfapump is for adult patients with refractory or recurrent ascites due to cirrhosis. It is designed for removal of ascitic fluid from the peritoneum to be transported into the bladder, where it is then eliminated with urination. Clinicians need to be trained on its use, and patients must be able to read and interpret instructions, particularly for charging and docking. Given the risks detailed in the studies discussed previously, the manufacturer states risks that prohibit use of Alfapump including peritoneal infection, coagulopathy, loculated ascites, small bladder capacity, and/or obstructive uropathy. One unfortunate hazard is that Alfapump is not considered magnetic resonance safe, nor is it safe with hyperbaric oxygen therapy. Additional details can be found in the FDA instructions for use of the Alfapump system.39

Ten years after the commercial launch of the pump, consensus guidelines for the use of Alfapump in Europe were published in 2022 based on European studies.29,31,34,40 A panel of experts including surgeons and hepatologists with Alfapump experience convened and divided recommendations into 4 distinct phases: preimplantation, implantation, postimplantation, and long-term follow-up. Table 2 is a summary based on the 4 different time periods outlined previously. In the preimplantation period, patient selection should include those patients with refractory ascites and cirrhosis who have contraindications to TIPS. The contraindications also reflect the exclusion criteria in the major studies detailed previously, which include obstructive uropathy, SBP, loculated ascites, and HRS. During the implantation period, the committee recommends antibiotic prophylaxis and paracentesis with residual ascitic fluid to test the device. Additional surgical recommendations are summarized in the table. Postimplantation recommendations target prevention of renal injury such as avoiding nephrotoxic agents and maintaining goal total daily volume (TDV) removed <1 L. Although the authors did comment on a paucity of data to recommend regular albumin infusions, they suggested use of albumin if TDV >1 L. Lastly, long-term care of the Alfapump focuses on infection prevention and treatment. As such, the committee recommends antibiotic prophylaxis in all patients until pump explant and prompt diagnosis and initiation of antibiotics for confirmed infections. Again, while no specific data are available for Alfapump, albumin infusions may need to be considered.

With the recent PMA of Alfapump by the FDA, North American centers will need guidance and best practices in patient selection and care for patients undergoing Alfapump implantation. Figure 4 represents a proposed algorithm for the management of refractory ascites. As LT is the only cure for refractory ascites in cirrhosis, this should be considered at any stage. All of the studies that have been included in this review excluded patients who were eligible for TIPS. Therefore, an important consideration for use of Alfapump is to ensure that patients are unable to receive TIPS. Additionally, the Alfapump is often surgically implanted in Europe. However, the MOSAIC and POSEIDON studies were key in demonstrating the minimally invasive approach of IR-guided pump implantation. This may potentially reduce the complications associated with major surgery and general anesthesia. The consensus recommendations also mentioned albumin when indicated. Depending on the TDV removed in patients with Alfapump, albumin infusions should be strongly considered. There is a higher incidence of renal dysfunction with Alfapump, and data suggest improvement in AKI with albumin. Lastly, antibiotic prophylaxis and prompt diagnosis of infection, especially peritonitis, is of paramount importance to ensure good patient outcomes.

Conclusion

While the Alfapump is a novel device in North America, its evidence-based use in European centers for more than a decade has provided an alternative for patients who are ineligible for TIPS. Complications such as AKI and infections need to be closely monitored and respond well to treatment such as albumin and antibiotics when indicated. With time and expertise in IR procedures, the Alfapump may pose a good option for patients with cirrhosis and refractory ascites and could serve as a bridge to LT in the United States. Additionally, the device is relatively young and thus may undergo modifications in the future that would make it an even more competitive therapeutic option for ascites. For now, the Alfapump is an alternative treatment for patients with cirrhosis and refractory ascites who are unable to undergo TIPS at a center with expertise.
