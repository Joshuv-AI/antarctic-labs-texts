Characterization of the olive fly (Bactrocera oleae) microbiome across diverse geographic regions of Morocco
Authors: Youssef El Yamlahi, Imane Remmal, Amal Maurady, Mohammed R Britel, Abdelmonaim Homrani Bakali, Naima Bel Mokhtar, Ioannis Galiatsatos, Panagiota Stathopoulou, George Tsiamis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe olive fruit fly (Bactrocera oleae) is a significant pest threatening olive production worldwide. Bactrocera oleae relies on symbiotic bacteria for nutrition, development, and adaptation to its environment. Among these, Candidatus Erwinia dacicola is the most dominant symbiont and plays a key role in the fly's physiology and ecological adaptation. Understanding the dynamics between B. oleae, Ca. E. dacicola, and other components of the B. oleae microbiome is essential for developing effective targeted area‐wide pest management strategies. This study aims to leverage full 16S rRNA gene sequencing to enhance the characterization of microbiome diversity in wild B. oleae populations from different regions in Morocco: Ouezzane, Rabat, Tanger, Errachidia, and Beni‐Mellal. The results revealed distinct microbiome compositions influenced by geographic locations, with Candidatus Erwinia dacicola as the dominant symbiont, followed by Erwinia persicina as a secondary contributor. Other bacterial taxa, including Asaia bogorensis, were also identified, highlighting the functional diversity within the olive fly microbiome. These findings provide insights into the microbial ecology of B. oleae, contributing to the development and enhancement of sustainable pest control strategies.Keywords: Candidatus Erwinia dacicola, microbial diversity, olive fruit fly, pest control, symbiotic bacteria, 16S rRNA sequencing

Introduction

The olive fruit fly, Bactrocera oleae (Rossi) (Diptera: Tephritidae), is a significant pest affecting olive cultivation worldwide (Daane & Johnson, 2010; Crisosto et al., 2011). Female flies lay eggs inside the developing olive fruits, where larvae burrow and feed on the nutrient‐rich mesocarp (Daane & Johnson, 2010; Genç, 2014). During this phase, the larvae must contend with the phytotoxins that are present in the fruit (Ghanbari et al., 2012; Pavlidi et al., 2017). To mitigate the effects of these toxic compounds and access necessary nutrients, B. oleae has formed a symbiotic relationship with a bacterium, Candidatus Erwinia dacicola (Enterobacteriaceae), which resides in the larval midgut (Capuzzo et al., 2005). While transient gut bacteria may also be present, this symbiont plays a crucial role in the larvae's development, persisting through metamorphosis and being transmitted to future generations (Sacchetti et al., 2008; Estes et al., 2009; Kounatidis et al., 2009). Studies have shown that this uncultivable bacterium contributes not only to larval development but also to the ovarian development of nutritionally stressed adult flies (Ben‐Yosef et al., 2010, 2015; Jose et al., 2019). Several studies have further investigated the bacterial communities associated with B. oleae, particularly in adult wild populations, highlighting their ecological and functional significance. Martinez‑Sañudo et al. (2024) examined the biogeographic distribution of Candidatus Erwinia dacicola, demonstrating its stable yet regionally variable presence, which is likely influenced by environmental conditions and host plant availability. Campos et al. (2022) explored microbiome shifts during metamorphosis, revealing that while Candidatus Erwinia dacicola remains dominant in adults, other bacteria such as Enterobacter and Pseudomonas persist, playing roles in digestion and detoxification. Similarly, Sacchetti et al. (2008) identified Lactobacillus and Acetobacter in wild adults, emphasizing their involvement in sugar metabolism and adaptation to nutrient‐poor environments. A broader review by Noman et al. (2020) highlighted the potential of symbiont‐based pest control, particularly by targeting Candidatus Erwinia dacicola to disrupt nitrogen recycling and amino acid biosynthesis. However, despite Morocco's importance as a leading olive‐producing country, to our knowledge no studies have yet characterized the bacterial microbiome of B. oleae populations in this region. Collectively, these studies underscore the critical role of bacterial symbionts in the olive fly's survival and development, offering potential avenues for microbiome‐targeted pest management strategies. Current pest management strategies for B. oleae include monitoring, chemical applications, the use of natural predators, mass trapping, and olive's genetic resistance varieties (Kampouraki et al., 2018; Pavlidi et al., 2018; Sikaoui et al., 2024). Traditional control methods involve the risk of resistance development and unintended effects on nontarget species and human health (Kakani et al., 2010; Koskinioti et al., 2019). As a result, there is a growing emphasis on environmentally friendly Integrated Pest Management (IPM) strategies that combine multiple control methods on an area‐wide scale (Vreysen et al., 2007; Koskinioti et al., 2019). On the other hand, Sterile Insect Technique (SIT) has proven effective in managing various insect pests by mass‐rearing and releasing sterile males to mate with wild females, leading to nonviable offspring (Knipling, 1955; Ahmad et al., 2018; Jose et al., 2019; Bigiotti et al., 2019a). However, one of the major challenges in the large‐scale application of SIT is ensuring the efficient mass‐rearing of competitive and robust flies (Economopoulos, 1972, 1977; Estes et al., 2011). Although SIT has been widely implemented for several fruit fly species, its success for B. oleae remains hindered by the difficulty of developing a suitable artificial larval diet, given the species' strict monophagous nature (Estes et al., 2011; Ras et al., 2017). While artificial diets allow larval development, they often lack the complexity of the natural microbiota found in wild flies, which plays a crucial role in digestion, nutrient assimilation, and overall fitness (Estes et al., 2011; Sohel et al., 2014; Ras et al., 2017). The microbiome, particularly Candidatus Erwinia dacicola, is essential for the development of nutritionally stressed adult flies, including their reproductive success. Understanding host‐symbiont interactions in wild populations can provide insights into optimizing rearing conditions or microbiome‐based strategies to enhance fly fitness for potential biocontrol applications (Koskinioti et al., 2019).

The aim of this study was to investigate the microbial diversity associated with wild populations of the olive fruit fly collected from five distinct geographic regions in Morocco: Ouezzane, Rabat, Tanger, Errachidia, and Beni‐Mellal. Using full‐length 16S rRNA gene sequencing, we sought to characterize the microbiome composition across these populations with particular focus on Candidatus Erwinia dacicola, the predominant symbiont, and other bacterial associates that may contribute to the fly's biology. We examined how geographic location influences the structure and diversity of bacterial communities, and assessed potential differences in microbial profiles between male and female flies. By enhancing our understanding of olive fly microbiome interactions beyond the primary symbiont, this study aims to provide insights that could inform the development of innovative, microbiome‐targeted strategies for the sustainable management of this economically significant olive pest.

Materials and methods

Samples collection

Olive fruit fly samples (n = 71) were collected from five different regions of Morocco: Ouezzane (n = 18), Rabat (n = 8), Tanger (n = 8), Errachidia (n = 20), and Beni‐Mellal (n = 17). In Rabat and Tanger, the number of collected individuals of a specific gender was limited (only 4 females in Rabat and 4 males in Tanger). To enable gender‐based comparisons, we adjusted the sample size in these two regions to include equal numbers of males and females, resulting in a smaller total sample size for these sites (Table 1 and Fig. S1). The samples were collected using 12 traps per location, each baited with a solution of NPK 15: 15: 15 [nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K)] fertilizer mixed with salt and water, following a technique commonly used by local farmers in mass trapping programs against B. oleae. This attractant was standardized across all sampling sites, and traps were adapted to allow live insect collection and were placed in olive trees. The collected olive flies were sorted by gender at each location, stored individually in absolute ethanol, and shipped to the laboratory, where they were stored at −20 °C until being processed.

DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene amplification

Before DNA extraction, the samples were rinsed with sterile water. DNA extraction was conducted using a modified Cetyl Trimethyl Ammonium Bromide (CTAB) method (Augustinos et al., 2011). To assess the quality and quantity of DNA samples, a Q5000 Micro‐volume UV‐Vis spectrophotometer (Quawell Technology, San Jose, CA, USA) was employed. Subsequently, the DNA samples were stored in Eppendorf tubes at −20 °C until use. PCR amplification of the 16S rRNA gene was performed in 25 µL reactions using universal primers: 27F (5′‐AGRGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG‐3′), 1492R (5′‐GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT‐3′) (Kuske et al., 2006), and the KAPA Taq PCR Kit (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Each reaction contained 2.5 µL of 10× KAPA Taq buffer, 0.2 µL of dNTPs (25 mmol/L), 0.2 µL of KAPA Taq DNA Polymerase (5 U/µL), 0.6 µL of each primer (25 µmol/L), 1 µL of template DNA solution, and 19.9 µL of sterile deionized water. The temperature profile of the PCR included an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 54 °C for 30 s, 2 min at 72 °C, and a final extension step at 72 °C for 5 min. The PCR products underwent electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel in TAE buffer (40 mmol/L Tris‐acetate, 1 mmol/L EDTA). The amplification product was detected using Bio‐Rad's Gel Doc XR+ system. Positive PCR products underwent purification through polyethylene glycol (20% PEG, 2.5 mol/L NaCl) (Hartley & Bowen, 2003) and were then suspended in 15 µL of sterile water. The quality and quantity of the purified PCR products were assessed using a Q5000 Microvolume UV‐Vis spectrophotometer (Quawell Technology, San Jose, CA, USA).

Indexing PCR and sequencing

The library preparation was conducted using the SQK‐NBD114‐96 kit from Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT), following the manufacturer's protocol. Initially, purified PCR products were aliquoted at 400 ng DNA per sample in a final volume of 11 µL. Subsequently, 1 µL of a diluted DNA Control Sample (DCS) and a reaction buffer‐enzyme mix were added for end‐preparation. The reaction was incubated in a thermal cycler at 20 °C for 5 min, followed by 65 °C for another 5 min. Next, 0.75 µL of the end‐prep was combined with 3 µL of sterile distilled water (SDW), 1.25 µL of a native barcode (NB01‐96), and 5 µL of Blunt/TA Ligase Master Mix. Following barcoding, a purification step was performed to remove unincorporated reagents. The resulting DNA library (12 µL) was then mixed with 37.5 µL of sequencing buffer and 25.5 µL of loading beads before being loaded onto a FLO‐MIN114 flow cell. Sequencing was performed on a MinION Mk1B device, with data acquisition managed by MINKNOW software version 23.11.5 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies). Raw 16S rRNA gene sequences generated in this study have been deposited in NCBI under BioProject number PRJNA1218261.

DNA sequence analysis

Basecalling was performed using Dorado (version 7.2.13). Demultiplexing and barcode trimming were carried‐out using Porechop (Wick et al., 2017) to separate the reads based on their barcodes, ensuring correct sample assignment. Next, the raw reads were filtered based on quality and length using NanoFilt (De Coster et al., 2018). Only reads within the size range of 1200 bp to 1800 bp were retained to match the expected size of the 16S rRNA gene region. A minimum Q‐score of 9 was also applied to filter out low‐quality reads, leaving only high‐quality sequences for further analysis. Filtered reads were then processed following NanoCLUST pipeline (Rodríguez‐Pérez et al., 2021), which clusters full‐length 16S rRNA nanopore sequencing reads and constructs polished representative sequences for taxonomic classification. Taxonomy was then assigned to representative sequences using QIIME2 against the SILVA database (version 138) (Quast et al., 2012). To evaluate microbial diversity and composition across samples, downstream analyses and visualizations, including alpha diversity, beta diversity, and differential abundance, were performed using MetaXplore (Bel Mokhtar et al., 2024). Prior to these analyses, data were normalized using the OTUs normalization method (OTUs Norm) in MetaXplore, with a minimum frequency threshold of 0.001%.

Results

Overall data analysis

After filtering, 283 960 high‐quality reads were retrieved from 71 samples, with an average of 3999 reads per sample. Among these, 277 737 reads were grouped into 38 unique clusters and assigned to four distinct phyla: Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, Desulfobacterota, and Bacteroidota (Table S1). Proteobacteria was the most abundant phylum, comprising 99.40% of the reads, followed by Firmicutes at 0.35%. Bacteroidota and Desulfobacterota were found exclusively in Ouezzane, accounting for 0.23% and 0.02% of the reads, respectively. Within Proteobacteria, the dominant classes were Alphaproteobacteria and Gammaproteobacteria, while Firmicutes were represented by the Bacilli class. At the genus level, Erwinia was the most abundant genus, representing 85.60% of the reads.

Candidatus Erwinia dacicola is the most dominant taxon

The prevalence of Candidatus Erwinia dacicola often obscures the presence of other microbial taxa, complicating the analysis of overall microbial diversity. Nevertheless, our investigation reveals significant differences in microbial profiles across various geographical regions. Alpha diversity analysis shows that flies from Ouezzane exhibit higher Shannon and Simpson indices, indicative of a rich and evenly distributed species (Fig. 1). The species richness of the microbiome remains relatively consistent across the regions, indicating a stable composition of bacterial species irrespective of geographical location (Fig. 1).

At the phylum level (Fig. 2), Proteobacteria dominated across all locations (from 97.7% ± 1.09% to 100%). Other phyla, such as Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, and Desulfobacterota, were primarily found in flies from Ouezzane, though their abundance remains relatively low (1.34% ± 0.77%, 0.89% ± 0.75%, and 0.07% ± 0.07%, respectively). A similar pattern was observed at the class level (Fig. 2), with Gammaproteobacteria being the most abundant class (from 94.2% ± 2.12% to 99.98% ± 0.01%), followed by Alphaproteobacteria and Bacilli in Ouezzane samples (3.5% ± 1.82% and 1.34% ± 0.77%, respectively). At the species level, Candidatus Erwinia dacicola was found to be highly abundant in Errachidia, Beni‐Mellal, and Tanger, with relative abundances of 94.98% ± 4.53%, 98.06% ± 0.76%, and 99.71% ± 0.05%, respectively. In comparison, its abundance was notably lower in flies from Ouazzane and Rabat, at 61.35% ± 10.71% and 74.84% ± 10.31%, respectively. Other bacterial species were found associated with flies from these regions, such as Citrobacter freundii in both Ouezzane and Rabat (13.02% ± 5.46% and 12.09% ± 7.65%, respectively), Klebsiella michiganensis in Ouezzane (12.63% ± 5.72%), and Ralstonia pickettii in Rabat (12.50% ± 7.91%) (Fig. 3). This detailed analysis underscores the dominance of Candidatus Erwinia dacicola in certain areas while also revealing distinct microbial profiles across different geographical locations.

Bacterial composition and diversity within B. oleae populations excluding Candidatus Erwinia dacicola

To assess the variation in the bacterial structure across B. oleae populations, the clusters assigned to the prevailing Candidatus Erwinia dacicola species were eliminated. A total of seven clusters were removed from the analysis, reducing the number of clusters from 38 to 31 clusters. The total number of reads significantly decreased from 277 737 to 19 242 reads. Additionally, two samples, one from Ouezzane and the other from Errachidia, were excluded from the analysis as they were found to be 100% dominated by Candidatus Erwinia dacicola, changing the total number of samples from 71 to 69.

The alpha diversity analysis excluding Candidatus Erwinia dacicola reveals that B. oleae from Ouezzane and Beni‐Mellal exhibit higher species richness compared to other regions. The bacterial community associated with flies from the Beni‐Mellal region has higher Shannon and Simpson indices, indicating a highly diverse ecosystem (Fig. 4). CAP and PCoA analyses demonstrated that samples from Ouezzane and Beni‐Mellal clustered separately from those of other locations (Figs. 5A and S2). The pairwise PERMANOVA analysis further supports this observation, indicating significant differences in microbial community composition between samples from Ouezzane and Beni‐Mellal and those from the other locations (Fig. 5B, PERMANOVA, P < 0.05), except for samples from Rabat, which exhibit a similarity in bacterial community to those from Ouezzane (Fig. 5B, PERMANOVA, P = 0.087). Conversely, no significant differences were detected in the bacterial communities of flies collected from Tanger, Rabat, and Errachidia (Fig. 5B). A PCoA analysis was performed to investigate potential differences in microbial community structure based on gender; however, no significant differences were found between males and females (PERMANOVA, P = 0.205) (Fig. S3).

The relative abundance of the bacterial community within B. oleae populations revealed that, at the phylum level, Proteobacteria dominate across all locations (from 80.07% ± 7.86% to 93.75% ± 4.06%), followed by Firmicutes (from 6.25% ± 4.06% to 18.82% ± 7.95%). A minimal presence (less than 1%) of other phyla, including Bacteroidota in flies from Ouezzane and Desulfobacterota in flies from both Ouezzane and Errachidia, was observed (Fig. 6). At the class level, members of Gammaproteobacteria were the most abundant bacterial class across all locations (from 73.22% ± 7.71% to 89.75% ± 3.32%), dominating the microbiome composition (Fig. 6). Members of Bacilli represent the second most prevalent class, with a higher abundance in flies from Ouezzane and Errachidia (18.83% ± 7.95% and 15.07% ± 6.61%, respectively), followed by Alphaproteobacteria which was present in low abundance in Rabat (0.15% ± 0.10%) compared to the other locations (from 4.14% ± 4.14% to 14.37% ± 7.98%). On the other hand, members of Bacteroidia were found in low abundance exclusively in Ouezzane (1.0% ± 0.83%), while members of Desulfovibrionia were also minimally represented, with a presence in flies from both Errachidia (0.88% ± 0.88%) and Ouezzane (0.09% ± 0.09%) (Fig. 6). The microbial composition at the species level shows significant variations between locations (Figs. 7 and S4). For instance, Erwinia persicina was highly abundant in flies from Errachidia, Tanger, and Rabat (70.0% ± 8.36%, 62.63% ± 9.21%, and 46.83% ± 10.51%, respectively), while considerably less abundant in Ouezzane and Beni‐Mellal (24.36% ± 9.43% and 23.23% ± 6.83%, respectively). Fructobacillus tropaeoli was abundant in flies from Errachidia, Rabat, Beni‐Mellal, and Tanger, with no significant variation between these regions (14.32% ± 6.65%, 7.58% ± 3.29%, 6.81% ± 2.71%, and 6.25% ± 4.06%, respectively). On the other hand, Paraburkholderia fungorum was significantly abundant in flies from Beni‐Mellal (27.45% ± 5.80%), and Ralstonia pickettii showed a marked presence in flies from Beni‐Mellal and Rabat (16.46% ± 4.14% and 12.50% ± 8.11%, respectively). Interestingly, Asaia bogorensis was mainly found in Tanger and Beni‐Mellal (14.37% ± 7.98% and 10.0% ± 5.75%, respectively). Additionally, a variety of bacterial genera were identified, including members of Lactobacillus, Citrobacter, and Klebsiella, which were mainly present in Ouezzane (Fig. 7).

Discussion

The dominance of Candidatus Erwinia dacicola in the B. oleae microbiome

The prevalence of Candidatus Erwinia dacicola as the dominant symbiont in the microbiome of B. oleae reinforces its critical role in host physiology and ecological adaptation. This bacterium is localized intracellularly within the esophageal bulb and female ovipositor diverticula and extracellularly in the foregut of adult flies (Estes et al., 2009). It plays an indispensable role in nutrient acquisition, particularly amino acid biosynthesis and nitrogen recycling, enabling the host to thrive on the nutrient‐poor and phytotoxin‐rich diet of olives (Capuzzo et al., 2005; Estes et al., 2009; Ben‐Yosef et al., 2014). Additionally, Candidatus Erwinia dacicola may aid in degrading plant compounds, further supporting the olive fly's adaptation. Though its exact pathways remain unclear, studies suggest its role in amino acid provisioning and detoxifying plant metabolites, essential for larval development and adult fitness (Capuzzo et al., 2005; Ben‐Yosef et al., 2015).

The conserved association between Candidatus Erwinia dacicola and B. oleae across diverse geographic regions highlights a stable co‐evolutionary relationship that supports the ecological success of the olive fly (Martinez‑Sañudo et al., 2024). However, variations in its relative abundance across regions may indicate differences in environmental conditions, host plant diversity, or agricultural practices, including pesticide use (Bigiotti et al., 2019b). These factors could influence the microbiome's structure and the bacterium's functional contributions to the host. However, the variations in the abundance of Candidatus Erwinia dacicola were likely not attributed to the treatment application, as olive trees in Beni‐Mellal were treated while those in Ouezzane remained untreated. Despite this, both populations of collected flies exhibited similarly low levels of Candidatus Erwinia dacicola compared to other regions. The ecological significance of Candidatus Erwinia dacicola positions it as a potential target for microbiome‐based pest management strategies (Bigiotti et al., 2019a; Malandrakis et al., 2024). Disrupting its nitrogen recycling or amino acid biosynthesis pathways could impair the olive fly's fitness, offering a sustainable alternative to traditional pest control methods. Future studies should focus on elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying this symbiosis to identify vulnerabilities that can be exploited for integrated pest management (Ben‐Yosef et al., 2014).

Emergence of Erwinia persicina as a secondary symbiont

The identification of Erwinia persicina as the second most abundant bacterial species in the olive fly microbiome underscores the complexity of its symbiotic relationships. While less studied than Candidatus Erwinia dacicola, E. persicina exhibits metabolic versatility, particularly in degrading plant‐derived compounds, which likely supports the host's ability to exploit the phytotoxin‐rich olive fruit (Zhang & Nan, 2014). Its complementary role in carbohydrate metabolism and detoxification of secondary plant compounds enhances the overall metabolic capacity of the microbiome, promoting host survival and reproductive success (Mazzon et al., 2008; Noman et al., 2020).

Regional differences in the abundance of E. persicina may be influenced by environmental factors such as agricultural practices, climate, and the availability of specific plant metabolites. These findings align with studies demonstrating the adaptability of E. persicina to diverse ecological niches, including its ability to colonize plants and insects (Noman et al., 2020). The co‐occurrence of E. persicina and Candidatus Erwinia dacicola in the insect gut may indicate a complementary division of labor, where facultative symbionts assist in digestion, nutrient acquisition, or detoxification, thereby enhancing the host's ability to exploit its challenging diet (Engel & Moran, 2013). Future research should investigate its specific symbiotic interactions to better understand its role within the olive fly microbiome and its potential as a target for innovative pest management strategies.

Presence of other bacterial taxa

The bacterial community associated with the studied B. oleae populations also includes a diverse array of bacterial taxa, such as Asaia bogorensis, Lactobacillus, Citrobacter, Klebsiella, and Paraburkholderia, which exhibit varied distributions across geographic regions.

The high abundance of Asaia bogorensis in Tanger and Beni‐Mellal is consistent with its known role as a facultative symbiont in sugar metabolism and biofilm formation (Crotti et al., 2009). Moreover, Asaia has been linked to reproductive compatibility and host physiology, further emphasizing its ecological and functional significance (Sacchetti et al., 2008). The identification of bacterial species associated with B. oleae in various studies highlights the complex and diverse microbial communities inhabiting this pest. Koskinioti et al. (2019) identified bacterial genera such as Klebsiella, Pluralibacter, Providencia, Pseudocitrobacter, Stenotrophomonas, Deinococcus, Enterococcus, and Streptococcus, which are often linked to the host's metabolic processes, nutrient acquisition, and possibly detoxification. Similarly, Bigiotti et al. (2019b) found several additional species, including Ewingella americana, Rosenbergiella collisarenosi, Erwinia aphidicola, Enterobacter muelleri, Serratia marcescens, Rahnella woolbedingensis, Morganella morganii, Cedecea lapagei, and Acinetobacter septicus, many of which are known for their roles in degrading plant‐derived compounds and contributing to nitrogen fixation, processes crucial for the olive fly's survival in nutrient‐poor environments.

In comparison to these studies, the bacterial genera identified in this research, including Lactobacillus, Citrobacter, Klebsiella, and Paraburkholderia, show some overlaps with the previous studies. These genera have been associated with nutrient acquisition and fermentation, which are vital for host adaptation to a phytotoxin‐rich diet, such as that provided by olives (Mondal et al., 2023). Additionally, the identification of Serratia marcescens and Morganella morganii in Bigiotti et al. (2019b) is consistent with the broad metabolic versatility observed in the microbiome of B. oleae, where bacteria may assist in breaking down toxic compounds present in olive fruits, enhancing the fly's reproductive success and survival in a challenging ecological niche.

Ecological and pest management implications

Our findings underscore the adaptive capacity of B. oleae microbiomes in response to environmental pressures and agricultural practices. The consistent dominance of Candidatus Erwinia dacicola, a vertically transmitted symbiont essential for nutrient provisioning and development, highlights its central role in host physiology (Estes et al., 2009; Ben‐Yosef et al., 2015). The presence of Erwinia persicina and other facultative bacteria across certain regions suggests additional microbial contributions to host adaptability, possibly linked to environmental factors or host plant variation. The distinct microbiome profiles observed between insect populations from treated and untreated areas point to the influence of agricultural inputs on microbial diversity, with implications for insect fitness and control. These insights support the potential of microbiome‐informed strategies for pest management. For instance, targeting key symbionts through disruption of transmission or metabolic interference may reduce fly survival and reproduction (Crotti et al., 2012; Douglas, 2015). Moreover, supplementing artificial diets with beneficial microbes could improve the performance of laboratory‐reared flies in SIT programs (Ras et al., 2017). As such, the stable yet variable microbiome of B. oleae represents both a vulnerability and a resource for developing sustainable, biologically based control approaches. Future research should focus on unraveling host–microbe interactions at the molecular level to optimize these applications.

Conclusions

This study highlights the dominance of Candidatus Erwinia dacicola in the microbiome of the olive fly (Bactrocera oleae) across different geographical regions, suggesting a stable association with its host. The regional variations observed in its prevalence indicate that environmental factors and agricultural practices may influence microbiome composition. The identification of Erwinia persicina as a secondary symbiont further enhances the complexity of the olive fly microbiome, with potential complementary roles in metabolism and detoxification. Additionally, the diversity of other bacterial taxa such as Asaia bogorensis and Lactobacillus, highlights the functional adaptability of the microbiome across regions. The significant differences observed between B. oleae collected from different areas suggest that the region's climate and/or the collection season may alter microbial diversity and composition. These findings point to the potential of microbiome‐based pest management strategies, where disrupting key symbiotic relationships could impair the fitness of B. oleae. Future research should focus on exploring the molecular mechanisms underlying these symbiotic interactions to develop targeted biocontrol strategies for sustainable pest management.
