Development and Evaluation of Insulation Materials for Deepwater Cementing
Authors: Shengda Shen, Yuhuan Bu, Chang Lu, Shujie Liu, Huajie Liu, Shenglai Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMarine oil and gas resources are abundant in deepwater regions, where the shallow seabed harbors natural gas hydrate layers due to the cold temperature and high-pressure environment. Hydrates are prone to thermal decomposition, which can compromise the integrity of cement sealing and even lead to accidents like blowouts. While current low-hydrated heat cement systems mitigate hydrate decomposition from cement hydration heat during the waiting period for cementing, they do not address heat transfer from deep strata to shallow hydrate layers through fluid circulation in the tubing during deep oil and gas development. Rather than utilizing costly pipe insulation technology, adjusting the thermal conductivity of well cement emerges as a cost-effective approach to prevent heat loss in the wellbore to the hydrate layer and thus inhibit hydrate decomposition. The critical aspect lies in utilizing insulation functional materials. This study delves into the functional and structural design of insulation materials suitable for cement slurry systems in well cementing, outlining the preparation methods and processes for two insulation materials (SDBW and SDBW-II) tailored for deepwater well cementing. SDBW and SDBW-II are both nuclear shell structural materials. The core is made of high-strength hollow microspheres, produced by using a reverse suspension polymerization method to form spheres followed by high-temperature sintering. The shell consists of a wear-resistant BPA epoxy resin layer, with the surface of SDBW-II also containing highly reflective glass microspheres. Incorporating 20% of material SDBW into the cement reduces the thermal conductivity of the cement stone from 0.8 to 0.31 W·(m·K)−1 while achieving a compressive strength of 6 MPa after 24 h at 20 °C. Material SDBW-II offers both thermal resistance and reflection functions, increasing reflectivity (R) from 0.3 to 0.5. By adding 20% of this material to the cement, under the same conditions, although the compressive strength decreases to 4.2 MPa, the thermal conductivity can be reduced to 0.27 W·(m·K)−1. Furthermore, there is no significant change within 180 days, demonstrating long-term thermal insulation stability. These developed insulation materials can effectively improve the thermal insulation performance of the cement sheath, thereby maintaining the stability of the upper natural gas hydrate layer in the oil and gas production process of deepwater wells, providing an innovative solution for the long-term operation of deepwater oil and gas wells.

Introduction

Deepwater marine environments, characterized by low temperatures and high pressures, often contain abundant oil and gas resources trapped beneath shallow natural gas hydrate layers.1−3 In the process of deepwater oil and gas extraction, it is crucial to cement the gas hydrate layer encountered during drilling to ensure the long-term production and safe construction of wells.4

While the gas hydrate layer is typically stable, the heat generated during cementing can trigger gas hydrate decomposition, leading to gas generation and potential damage at the cement sheath and casing bond interface.5 This may create microgaps, compromising the integrity of the cement seal and posing risks such as well blowouts.6 Consequently, professionals in the field of cementing have focused on developing various low-temperature early strength low-hydration heat cement slurry systems to mitigate gas hydrate decomposition during cementing.7−12

However, during the operation of deep oil and gas production, the heat carried by the fluids in the tubing is transferred to the gas hydrate layer via the tubing and casing, also leading to gas hydrate decomposition. In areas with shallow gas hydrate layers, operators typically choose to avoid these layers or induce gas hydrate eruption, which may disrupt the well pattern layout or result in resource wastage.13 While the use of low-temperature early strength low-hydration heat cement slurry systems can help stabilize gas hydrates during cementing, it does not fully prevent the heat transfer from oil and gas toward the gas hydrate layer during production, thus failing to address the root cause of shallow gas hydrate layer decomposition and cementation failure.

The current focus in addressing heat loss in wellbores has shifted toward utilizing the thermal conductivity of cement slurry as an alternative to costly pipe insulation technology.14,15 The insulation cement system involves incorporating low thermal conductivity materials or modifying the pore structure of the cement stone to enhance insulation.16,17 Common additives like expanded perlite and glass microspheres are employed to reduce the overall thermal conductivity by leveraging the porous nature of these materials.18 Researchers have explored improving insulation properties through alterations in pore structure, such as with foam cement systems.19 However, challenges arise when these cement systems are implemented directly in insulation operations. For systems using low thermal conductivity materials, the reduced strength of these additives may compromise the porous structure under compression, diminishing the insulation effectiveness.20 In foam cement systems, the permeability of the porous structure can lead to decreased insulation performance if reservoir fluids infiltrate the pores.21 Therefore, maintaining a stable content of low thermal conductivity materials in the cement sheath is essential for the effective development and utilization of deepwater oil and gas resources.

This paper describes the development of a thermal insulation material with a core–shell structure called a SDBW. The durable and corrosion-resistant shell material effectively protects against external fluid intrusion, maintaining the insulation properties. Additionally, the main material component provides structural support to counteract the recession in strength, often seen with the increased use of hollow materials in cement stone. Furthermore, to augment the thermal insulation properties of the material, a heat-reflective film was incorporated onto the surface of SDBW, resulting in the development of a dual-functional thermal insulation material capable of reflecting and obstructing heat, named SDBW-II. The goal is to develop insulation materials for deepwater cementing that can lower the thermal conductivity of the cement stone while preserving its necessary strength. By taking advantage of the low thermal conductivity of cementitious material, heat energy loss is reduced during the production process, ensuring that the temperature of the hydrate layer remains below the point of hydrate decomposition. This method will help maintain the stability of the hydrate layer throughout oil and gas production operations.

Design of the SDBW Material

Design Principles of the Material

In previous studies,16 the efficacy of commonly used insulation materials was evaluated by incorporating them into cement slurry. The results indicated that these materials were not suitable for direct application in deepwater cementing operations. Therefore, for the development of an insulation material (SDBW), several characteristics need to be considered, including formation characteristics, cement slurry performance, and environmental conditions.

(1)The material should possess favorable hollow characteristics. Due to the significantly lower thermal conductivity of air compared to solids, the stronger the hollow characteristics, the lower the overall thermal conductivity of the material, resulting in a higher insulation capability.20,21 Furthermore, it is crucial for this material to maintain stable hollow characteristics throughout the cement mixing, hydration, and subsequent operations.

For the design requirement that the core material be millimeter-sized, high-strength hollow microspheres, the hollow microspheres researched by the team in the previous period were used as the basis.22 The hollow microspheres were made by high-temperature sintering after ball formation by the reverse-phase suspension polymerization method and are characterized by high hardness (shown in Table 1), adjustable size, and better roundness. Selection of Shell Materials for SDBW

According to the research on encapsulation materials, it is known that synthetic polymer materials such as resins generally have better abrasion resistance and corrosion resistance, and their performance is more stable compared with natural polymer materials and inorganic materials.23 Therefore, in this paper, we chose BPA epoxy resin with good performance, stability, and durability as the raw material for the preparation of shell materials. BPA epoxy resin is generally a transparent or semitransparent liquid with high viscosity that needs to be used in conjunction with a curing agent. After curing, it has good physicochemical properties, does not react with cement and other substances, and can be stable for a long time under high salt and alkali conditions22 while also having a certain degree of toughness. The thermal conductivity of the material is 0.2 W·(m·K)−1.

Optimization of the SDBW Material

Based on the research on thermal insulation materials, it is known that SDBW mainly impedes the transfer of heat by resisting thermal conduction, utilizing its own hollow structure to reduce the material’s thermal conductivity, thereby lowering the thermal conductivity of cement. In theory, increasing the material’s cavities can meet the requirements of enhancing the material’s insulation capability. However, considering the actual application process of the material, larger particle sizes can cause difficulties in pumping, and a larger hollow structure will have a greater impact on the strength of the cement. Therefore, alternative methods are chosen to enhance the material’s insulation capability.

The heat reflection film can enable materials to reflect back a portion of the transmitted heat, reducing the level of penetrative thermal transfer. For this reason, this paper proposes the optimization and improvement of insulation materials through the combination of two effects: heat reflection and a thermal barrier. Specifically, an additional layer of reflective film is added to the surface of the insulation material (SDBW) to enable the material to reflect back a portion of the transmitted heat. The heat that is not reflected back is hindered by the internal hollow structure of the insulation material, enhanced by the addition of insulation additives, thereby improving the insulation capability of the material. The process involves replacing the hollow microspheres in 3.2.2 with an insulation material (SDBW) and replacing the dispersing effect of ultrafine calcium carbonate powder with a thermal reflective material (hollow glass microspheres). All other preparation conditions remain the same, thus forming a thermally reflective film on the surface of SDBW. The improved insulation material is named SDBW-II.

Characterization of the Insulation Material

Appearance and Form

In order to ensure that the morphological characteristics of SDBW align with the envisioned design, we employed a 3-fold approach to examine its structure. This involved utilizing mobile phone photography for quick preliminary observations, optical microscopy for a more detailed study, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) for an in-depth, high-resolution exploration. Based on these results, we can optimize the synthesis formula of SDBW to ensure the effectiveness of our design and synthesis process.

Core–Shell Structure

In order to validate the core–shell structure design of SDBW, two methods were employed: the indicator method and the resin embedding method. These procedures are outlined below:

Indicator Method

Before the encapsulation of hollow microspheres, pretreatment is performed by adding an indicator inside the microspheres. This indicator is a solid at room temperature and melts into a liquid when heated. The hollow microspheres that absorbed the indicator are encapsulated using the same method, resulting in indicator-loaded SDBW. A 10 g portion of SDBW is placed in 80 °C hot water and stirred for 30 min. After removal from the water and wiping off the surface moisture, the mass is measured. If there is no significant change in mass, this proves that this method can completely encapsulate the hollow microspheres and protect the internal cavity.

Resin Embedding Method

Randomly select a certain number of SDBW, fix them with colorless and transparent resins, and polish them from one side. The polished samples are then cleaned with acetone through ultrasonication for 20 min to remove any residues.

Strength Performance

The compressive strength of materials is used to characterize their strength performance. The prepared cement slurry is placed in the mold for curing for 1 day. After curing, the solidified standard cement blocks were removed and the compressive strength of the cement blocks was measured using a compressive strength measuring instrument.

The analysis suggests that lower rotational speeds impede the stirring blade’s ability to efficiently disperse the slurry in the reaction container, leading to the formation of larger and irregularly shaped microspheres. Moreover, an insufficient stirring speed results in incomplete suspension of the microspheres, leading to compression and surface imperfections. Conversely, higher rotational speeds facilitate the stirring blade in dispersing the slurry into smaller liquid droplets, thus decreasing the size of the microspheres. However, excessive velocity can generate friction between the microspheres and the inner surface of the reaction vessel, leading to a reduction in the particle size.

Effect of Dosage on the Strength of the Cement Stone

The compressive strength of the cement stone was tested to determine the impact of SDBW at various dosages. Cement stone samples were prepared following the formula in Table 5 and tested after 1 day of curing in a 20 °C water bath environment. The experimental results are presented in Figure 10, with the red line representing the minimum compressive strength necessary for the deepwater cement slurry (at least 3.5 MPa for 24 h cement stone), as specified in cement industry standard SY/T 6544–2017. Each set of experiments was repeated five times, and the results were averaged.

Compressive strength of the cement stone.

The data presented in Figure 10 indicate that incorporating insulation material into cement slurry tends to reduce its compressive strength. SDBW has the least detrimental effect on strength compared to other materials. Specifically, at a material dosage of 20%, cement paste with SDBW added exhibits a compressive strength of 6 MPa, whereas the strength of cement paste with SDBW-II added is 4.2 MPa. These strengths are in compliance with the strength standards for well cement slurry in deepwater environments.

The analysis indicates that two primary factors contribute to the decrease in cement strength. Initially, the presence of an insulation material, serving as an inert component in the cement mix, diminishes the overall hydration process, resulting in a slower strength development. Additionally, the hollow structure of the insulation material decreases the density of the final cement stone, subsequently impacting its strength.

The cement stone incorporating SDBW exhibits superior strength compared to other types of cement stones due to two main factors. First, the larger particle size of SDBW allows for the formation of a robust framework within the cement stone, as the large-sized admixtures interlock, creating a dense internal structure when combined with hydration products during curing. This results in a more compact arrangement, effectively delaying the decrease in compressive strength. Second, the high strength of the hollow microspheres postcalcination reinforces the support of the cement stone’s framework, contributing to its overall strength. Therefore, the combination of the solid framework formed by larger SDBW particles and the inherent strength of SDBW itself results in the exceptional compressive strength of the cement stone containing SDBW when compared to others. The cement stone incorporating SDBW-II exhibits reduced strength in comparison to that incorporating SDBW, primarily attributed to the larger average particle size of SDBW-II versus SDBW. This difference in particle size distribution leads to an overall increase in porosity within the cement stone at equivalent dosages, consequently resulting in a decline in strength.

Effect of Dosage on the Thermal Insulation Properties of the Cement Stone

The influence of insulation materials on the thermal insulation performance of cement was examined by testing the thermal conductivity of cement under various dosages. Cement samples were prepared following the formula provided in Table 5 and tested after 1 day of curing in a water bath environment at 20 °C. The findings of the experiment are presented in Figure 11. Each set of experiments was repeated five times, and the results were averaged.

Thermal conductivity of the cement stone.

The data presented in Figure 11 demonstrate a clear trend where the thermal conductivity of cement decreases as the quantity of insulation material is augmented. This can be attributed to the presence of numerous voids within the insulation material, with air having a considerably lower thermal conductivity compared to cement. As the amount of insulation material increases, the number of voids within the cement also rises, consequently elevating the volume of low thermal conductivity gas within the cement. Consequently, the thermal conductivity of the cement as a whole diminishes, thereby enhancing its insulation effectiveness.

The impact of SDBW-II on reducing the thermal conductivity of cement stands out among the various insulation materials tested. With a 20% addition of SDBW-II, the thermal conductivity of cement drops significantly from 0.8 to 0.27 W·(m·K)−1. This superiority can be attributed to two key factors. First, the larger cavity volume of SDBW-II compared to materials like glass beads results in the formation of more internal cavities within the cement matrix, thereby lowering thermal conductivity. Second, the higher structural strength of SDBW-II reduces the likelihood of rupture during mixing, enhancing its insulation properties. In contrast, materials such as expanded perlite and drift beads exhibit lower strength, leading to a notable decline in insulation effectiveness when they are utilized.

Evaluation of Long-Lasting Thermal Insulation Performance

The thermal conductivity of the cement stone was tested at various curing times to maintain the long-term effectiveness of SDBW during on-site application. The results indicate that the thermal insulation performance of SDBW and SDBW-II remains stable for up to 6 months, whereas the thermal conductivity of cement paste with additional materials shows an increase. This is due to the chemical inertness and low permeability of the SDBW and SDBW shells. The chemical inertness ensures that the shells do not react with cement materials to generate gelling substances that fill the pores, while the low permeability of the shells prevents the intrusion of external fluids, ensuring the long-term integrity of the internal cavities of the materials. During the curing process, the other three materials will react with the cement stone to generate C–S–H, filling the microcracks in the cement structure and causing an increase in the thermal conductivity of cement.

In comparison to SDBW, SDBW-II demonstrates a more significant advantage in enhancing the thermal insulation performance of cement sheaths, but this improvement is accompanied by a greater decrease in its strength. In practical applications, we can calculate the thermal conductivity of cement sheaths under stable conditions of gas hydrate layers based on the preset wellbore structure and production parameters. Depending on the on-site thermal insulation requirements, either SDBW or SDBW-II can be chosen to enhance the thermal insulation of the cement paste, ensuring effective heat loss inhibition in the hydrate layer of the wellbore during production operations. This helps maintain the stability of the hydrate layer around the well and enhances operational safety.

Conclusions and Discussion

This review focuses on the development of thermal insulation cement for deepwater well cementing by introducing two thermal insulation materials, SDBW and SDBW-II. Through experimental testing and theoretical analysis, the following key findings have been identified:

(1)The design of thermal insulation materials considers the specific requirements of deepwater well cementing, aiming to maintain “hollow” characteristics, minimal impact on cement strength, no interference with cement hydration, and no adverse effects on cement system performance. The designed thermal insulation material consists of a core–shell structure with BPA epoxy resin as the shell material and millimeter-sized hollow microspheres as the core material.

(2)The development of thermal insulation material SDBW effectively decreases the thermal conductivity of cement stone with a limited decrease in its strength. When added at 20% in cement, the thermal conductivity of cement stone reduces from 0.8 to 0.31 W·(m·K)−1 while maintaining a compressive strength of 6 MPa at 24 h.
