Facial pain as a presenting symptom of a left maxillary sinus cyst: Case report and literature review
Authors: Drissia Benfadil, Youssef Azouaghe, Achraf Amine Sbai, Azeddine Lachkar, Fahd El Ayoubi El Idrissi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Maxillary sinus retention cysts are typically benign, asymptomatic lesions that are discovered incidentally and rarely require intervention. We report a rare case of a 52-year-old male with a history of alcohol and tobacco use who presented with isolated, persistent midfacial pain without rhinologic, dental, or neurologic signs. Imaging revealed a large, expansile cystic lesion of the left maxillary sinus with extensive erosion of the sinus walls and extension into the infratemporal fossa, radiologically mimicking aggressive pathology such as mucocele, aneurysmal bone cyst, or malignancy. The patient underwent successful marsupialization via a modified Caldwell-Luc approach, with complete symptom resolution and no recurrence at 6 months. This case underscores the diagnostic challenges posed by atypical presentations of maxillary sinus retention cysts and highlights the importance of including such lesions in the differential diagnosis of destructive sinonasal masses, particularly when clinical and imaging features suggest a more aggressive process.

Introduction

Maxillary sinus cysts are common benign lesions of the paranasal sinuses, broadly categorized into mucous retention cysts and mucoceles. Retention cysts arise from the obstruction of seromucous gland ducts, leading to subepithelial accumulation of mucus. They are generally asymptomatic, discovered incidentally on imaging, and do not require treatment in most cases. In contrast, mucoceles are expansile lesions capable of eroding surrounding bone and displacing adjacent structures, with a higher prevalence in the frontal and ethmoidal sinuses than in the maxillary sinus. Differentiating these entities is essential, particularly when imaging suggests aggressive features [1].

The diagnosis of maxillary sinus retention cysts (MSRCs) is typically uncomplicated when the lesion is small, dome-shaped, and clinically silent. However, in rare instances, these cysts may become expansile and symptomatic, presenting with facial pain, swelling, or pressure. When facial pain occurs in isolation, without associated rhinologic or dental findings, diagnostic ambiguity may arise. Advanced imaging, particularly computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), is crucial in assessing the lesion’s extent, content, and impact on adjacent structures. In atypical cases, the radiologic appearance may raise concern for more aggressive or unusual entities such as odontogenic cysts, mucoceles, benign neoplasms, or even aneurysmal bone cysts (ABCs), which, although rare in the paranasal sinuses, can share overlapping imaging features due to their expansile and destructive nature.

Importantly, expansive lesions involving bony erosion must also prompt consideration of malignancies such as sinonasal carcinomas or lymphomas, particularly in middle-aged or high-risk patients [2].

The prognosis of MSRCs is excellent. Most lesions remain stable or regress spontaneously. Nonetheless, when cysts enlarge and cause bone remodeling or orbital floor displacement, they can mimic more aggressive pathology, despite their benign histology. Recognition of such atypical behavior is key to avoid misdiagnosis and overtreatment [3].

Management strategies depend on symptomatology and anatomical involvement. Asymptomatic or small cysts can be monitored with serial imaging. In contrast, symptomatic or enlarging lesions typically require surgical intervention. Endoscopic marsupialization via a middle meatal antrostomy is the standard approach for most cases. However, lesions with extensive posterior wall erosion or infratemporal extension may necessitate an external approach, such as a modified Caldwell-Luc procedure, to ensure complete drainage and access to deeper compartments [4].

Case presentation

A 52-year-old male with a significant history of chronic alcohol and tobacco use (30 pack-years) presented with a gradually progressive 3-month history of persistent left-sided facial pain. The pain was described as deep, dull, and nonpulsatile, localized to the left midface region, severely affecting both sleep and daily. The patient denied any history of similar episodes, facial trauma, dental procedures, maxillofacial surgery (including endonasal surgery), or herpes zoster infection. His past medical and surgical history was otherwise unremarkable, with no known chronic rhinosinusitis, allergic rhinitis, or autoimmune diseases. Additionally, family history revealed no neoplasms or sinus pathologies.

Physical examination revealed tenderness upon palpation of the left infraorbital rim, accompanied by a firm, nonfluctuant, and fixed swelling localized to the internal aspect of the left cheek. No cervical lymphadenopathy or cutaneous inflammatory signs (redness, warmth) were detected. Nasal endoscopy demonstrated a focal protrusion at the posterior segment of the left inferior meatus, causing mild lateral displacement of the lateral nasal wall. Cranial nerve assessment was unremarkable, with no evidence of trigeminal hypoesthesia or oculomotor deficits. Comprehensive dental evaluation excluded odontogenic infection.

Given the chronicity of symptoms, lack of neurologic or odontogenic origin, and the atypical presentation of isolated midfacial pain without neuralgia features, a focused diagnostic workup was initiated. Complete blood count (WBC 6200/mm³, Hb 14.1 g/dL, platelets 230,000/mm³), C-reactive protein (<5 mg/L), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (10 mm/h), renal and hepatic function tests, and serum electrolytes were all within normal limits.

MRI of the sinuses with gadolinium contrast demonstrated a large, well-demarcated expansile lesion occupying the entire left maxillary sinus. The mass was hypointense on T1-weighted sequences and markedly hyperintense on T2-weighted images, with thin peripheral enhancement after contrast administration. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) sequences were not available; however, the lesion exhibited no internal nodular enhancement or diffusion restriction, consistent with a benign cystic process. Based on imaging and clinical presentation, a provisional diagnosis of an expansile but benign cystic lesion was made. Differential considerations included a mucous retention cyst with secondary expansion, maxillary sinus mucocele, and ABC. Although DWI/ADC sequences were not available, the absence of internal complexity or nodular enhancement argued against malignancy.

The patient underwent surgical marsupialization via a modified Caldwell-Luc approach. This approach was chosen over endoscopic endonasal surgery due to the extensive posterior wall destruction and infratemporal fossa involvement, which limited the accessibility for endonasal techniques. Intraoperatively, a large cystic mass with thin walls was encountered, extending posteriorly into the infratemporal fossa through a complete defect in the posterior sinus wall. The cyst contents consisted of serous yellowish fluid without hemorrhagic or purulent features. Wide antrostomy and permanent drainage into the nasal cavity were achieved.

Unfortunately, histopathological examination could not be completed due to specimen loss during processing. Nevertheless, the intraoperative findings and radiologic characteristics were highly consistent with a benign MSRC exhibiting unusually aggressive expansile behavior.

Postoperatively, the patient experienced complete resolution of facial pain and buccal swelling within 2 weeks. Follow-up imaging at 3 months showed no recurrence, and the patient remained asymptomatic at 6 months. Regular follow-up is planned to monitor for potential recurrence given the lesion’s atypical, aggressive expansion.

Discussion

MSRCs are benign, epithelial-lined, mucus-filled lesions that form due to obstruction of seromucous gland ducts within the sinus mucosa. They often appear as dome-shaped, well-circumscribed opacities on imaging studies and are commonly regarded as incidental findings [5].

Retrospective CT and CBCT series report MSRC prevalence rates ranging from 6% to 13% in adult populations, with larger cysts (>20 mm) occurring in around 5%-10% of cases. There is a marginal predilection for involvement of the left maxillary sinus, although no significant sex or age differences have been demonstrated [6].

These cysts form when glandular duct outflow is obstructed, leading to mucus accumulation beneath the epithelium. Most remain contained and passive. Rarely, however, ongoing mucus production and expanding pressure effects can cause cortical bone thinning or remodeling, a phenomenon documented in case reports of enlarging MSRCs [7].

The diagnosis of MSRCs is typically straightforward when lesions are asymptomatic and incidentally discovered on imaging. However, in certain cases, particularly when they enlarge or exert pressure on adjacent structures, these cysts may present with clinical symptoms, leading to diagnostic challenges. Clinically, MSRCs may manifest as dull, nonpulsatile facial pain or localized pressure in the infraorbital region, facial swelling, or, more rarely, nasal obstruction. Pain is often nonspecific and may mimic dental, neuralgic, or sinus-related conditions. The absence of systemic symptoms, purulent rhinorrhea, cranial nerve dysfunction, or dental pathology tends to support a benign etiology. Nevertheless, red flag signs—including persistent or progressive pain, sensory disturbances, rapidly enlarging masses, or constitutional symptoms—should prompt consideration of more aggressive pathology such as mucoceles, ABCs, or malignancy [8].

Imaging plays a pivotal role in establishing a positive diagnosis and narrowing the differential. CT typically reveals MSRCs as well-circumscribed, dome-shaped soft tissue opacities, usually originating from the sinus walls and displaying homogeneous density. These cysts generally do not cause bone destruction; however, in cases of chronic expansion, smooth thinning or remodeling of the bony walls may be observed. In contrast, irregular bony erosion with ill-defined margins, especially in the posterior or superior walls, should raise suspicion for a neoplastic process. MRI further refines lesion characterization by evaluating content and enhancement patterns. MSRCs exhibit low signal intensity on T1-weighted sequences and high signal intensity on T2-weighted images, consistent with simple fluid content. After gadolinium administration, they typically show thin, regular peripheral enhancement without internal nodularity, septations, or solid components. The absence of diffusion restriction on DWI sequences supports a nonneoplastic origin. In contrast, sinonasal malignancies often display heterogeneous signal intensities, irregular contours, nodular enhancement, and restricted diffusion due to high cellularity. Mucoceles, while also benign, may show higher protein content resulting in variable T1 and T2 signals and often demonstrate remodeling or erosion of adjacent bone, particularly in the frontal or ethmoidal sinuses. ABCs, though rare in the maxillofacial region, are expansile, multiloculated lesions that typically exhibit fluid-fluid levels on both CT and MRI, with internal septations and cortical blowout features, most commonly affecting adolescents and young adults [9].

Radiologic differentiation relies on a combination of criteria: the shape and margins of the lesion, bone interface characteristics, internal architecture, and enhancement pattern. Retention cysts are usually solitary, smooth, and nonenhancing with clear fluid signal; mucoceles may be larger and more deforming, with variable signal intensities due to their proteinaceous content; ABCs are recognizable by their multiloculated appearance and fluid-fluid levels; while malignancies often exhibit irregular borders, aggressive bone erosion, heterogeneous signal, and soft tissue invasion [10].

Definitive diagnosis may be established by histopathologic analysis when feasible. Histologically, MSRCs consist of a thin fibrous capsule lined by ciliated pseudostratified columnar epithelium and filled with serous or mucinous material, without atypia, mitotic activity, or stromal invasion. In many cases, intraoperative findings such as a thin-walled cyst containing clear or yellowish serous fluid, with no evidence of hemorrhage, purulence, or tissue necrosis, may suffice to confirm the diagnosis, particularly when consistent with imaging. Nonetheless, when imaging features are atypical or there is concern for an alternative diagnosis, histologic confirmation remains essential to exclude aggressive lesions or malignancy [11].

The treatment of MSRCs is guided by symptomatology and anatomical involvement. Asymptomatic cysts discovered incidentally, particularly those smaller than 10 mm, require no intervention and can be monitored with periodic imaging. Most remain stable or regress spontaneously, and simple clinical follow-up is sufficient in the absence of suspicious features such as bone erosion or orbital displacement.

Symptomatic or expansile cysts presenting with facial pain, swelling, or nasal obstruction warrant surgical intervention. The preferred approach is endoscopic marsupialization via a middle meatal antrostomy, which allows effective drainage with minimal morbidity. This method is suitable when the lesion is accessible and confined within the sinus boundaries [12].

When the cyst extends posteriorly, erodes the posterior maxillary wall, or involves the infratemporal fossa, a modified Caldwell-Luc approach may be necessary. This transantral technique offers broader access to the maxillary sinus, particularly for deep or inaccessible lesions.

Intraoperative suspicion of malignancy suggested by thick, hemorrhagic, or friable tissue should prompt immediate biopsy and possible frozen section analysis. In such cases, wide marsupialization should be deferred until histologic confirmation is obtained. If malignancy is confirmed or suspected, surgery should be limited to diagnostic sampling, with referral for oncologic assessment and multidisciplinary management [13].

Postoperative care includes nasal irrigation and clinical follow-up. In the absence of histopathology, close imaging surveillance is recommended to exclude recurrence or misdiagnosed neoplasia.

Overall prognosis is excellent for treated MSRCs. Although minor growth can occur, recurrence after conservative surgery is rare. Literature supports sustained stability or resolution postdrainage. This favorable outcome was mirrored in our patient, affirming the benign nature of the lesion despite its aggressive presentation [14].

Conclusion

This case highlights an unusual presentation of a MSRC manifesting solely as persistent unilateral facial pain, without rhinologic or dental symptoms, and exhibiting aggressive radiologic features, including bony erosion and orbital floor displacement. Although typically indolent, MSRCs may occasionally mimic more expansive or neoplastic lesions, posing significant diagnostic challenges. Comprehensive radiologic evaluation remains critical to distinguish benign cystic entities from mucoceles, ABCs, and sinonasal malignancies. This report underscores the importance of including expansile retention cysts in the differential diagnosis of unilateral facial pain and reinforces the role of tailored surgical management, particularly when endoscopic access is limited. Awareness of such atypical presentations is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and ensure appropriate intervention.
