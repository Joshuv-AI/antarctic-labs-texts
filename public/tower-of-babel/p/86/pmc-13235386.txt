Development of a vascularized multi-organoid-on-a-chip to model the heart-islet axis in diabetic cardiomyopathy
Authors: Zhuangzhuang Yang, Lei Lin, Jinxiu Jiang, Yiran Wang, Feiyi Li, Xinyue Li, Jiali Fang, Lilin Xu, Hao Li, Liqian Du, Zhaoce Liu, Rui Liang, Shusen Wang, Xianhao Dong, Shijun Hu, Yajun Duan, Hua Qin, Deling Kong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The dynamic crosstalk between pancreatic islets and the heart plays a critical yet poorly understood role in diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM). Here, we present a vascularized multi-organoid-on-a-chip platform that enables the perfusion co-culture of human iPSC-derived vascularized cardiac organoids (VCOs) and vascularized islet organoids (VIOs). Under a gradient of hyperglycemic stress, this system recapitulated key features of DCM, including cardiomyocyte structural disassembly and vascular dysfunction. Crucially, co-culture with functional VIOs provided substantial protection against hyperglycemia, mitigating cardiac damage by restoring metabolic and contractile gene programs. Our study establishes a modular and physiologically relevant model to dissect the dose-dependent protective role of islets in diabetic heart disease and to screen for interventions targeting inter-organ communication.

Introduction

Diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM), a major driver of heart failure independent of coronary artery disease, arises from complex systemic disturbances under chronic hyperglycemia [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Beyond the direct cardiotoxic effects of high glucose, growing evidence highlights the crucial, yet underexplored, role of dynamic inter-organ communication [5,6], particularly between the pancreas and the heart. The functional decline of pancreatic islets and the progression of cardiac injury are intertwined yet modeling this reciprocal crosstalk in a human-relevant, controlled system remains a significant challenge [7]. Existing in vitro models, such as 2D cultures or static organoids, lack the vascular integration and systemic connectivity needed to simulate endocrine signaling and remote tissue responses, limiting our ability to dissect DCM pathogenesis from an “organ network” perspective [[8], [9], [10], [11]].

Recent advances in vascularized organoids and organ-on-a-chip technologies offer promising avenues. Vascular networks within organoids enhance physiological maturity and provide a conduit for biomimetic perfusion and signaling [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Interconnecting such organoids in microfluidic circuits to create “multi-organ chips” represents the next frontier for modeling systemic physiology and disease [16,17]. However, most platforms focus on tissue co-location rather than functional, dose-dependent interaction [18]. A critical unmet need is a system that can precisely control the metabolic milieu while quantitatively assessing how the functional state of one organ (e.g., islets) modulates the structural and molecular responses of another (e.g., the heart) over time.

To bridge this gap, we engineered a modular microfluidic platform for the perfusion co-culture of human iPSC-derived vascularized cardiac organoids (VCOs) and vascularized islet organoids (VIOs). The chip incorporates a hexagonal micropillar array for stable organoid retention and a biomimetic perfusion network that ensures efficient medium exchange while maintaining local tissue niches. This design enables the study of crosstalk within a human “pancreas-heart” unit.

Using this platform, we investigated the glucose-dependent protective role of functional islets in DCM. By applying a gradient of hyperglycemic stress, we modeled states from compensated insulin secretion to severe glucotoxicity. We demonstrate that VIOs confer a dose-dependent protection on cardiac tissue, substantially mitigating structural damage and pathological transcriptomic reprogramming under moderate, but not severe, hyperglycemia. This work establishes a new model to elucidate the islet-heart axis in diabetes and provides a scalable platform for probing inter-organ communication in metabolic disease.

Results

Generation of cardiovascular cells from human induced pluripotent stem cells

To construct vascularized cardiac organoids, we initiated the process by differentiating human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) into three essential cardiovascular lineages: cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, and vascular smooth muscle cells (Fig. 1A and J). Using a chemically defined induction protocol as we described previously [19,20], we first generated a heterogeneous population of vascular progenitors by day 6 of differentiation. Initial characterization revealed that approximately 24% of cells in this population expressed the endothelial marker CD31 (Fig. 1B), indicating the presence of endothelial precursors but necessitating further purification to obtain defined cellular subsets. To isolate and expand pure populations, the day-6 progenitors underwent CD34-based magnetic sorting. The CD34-positive fraction, enriched for endothelial precursors, was expanded in medium supplemented with VEGF and FGF2. These cells adopted a characteristic cobblestone-like morphology (Fig. 1C) and exhibited uniform, high-level expression of CD31, as confirmed by immunostaining (Fig. 1D). Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that the expanded cells maintained a high purity of 97% CD31-positive even after three passages (Fig. 1E), confirming the successful derivation of stable and homogeneous hiPSC-derived endothelial cells (hiPSC-ECs). The CD34-negative fraction was induced with PDGF-BB to differentiate into high-purity hiPSC-derived vascular smooth muscle cells (hiPSC-SMCs). These cells displayed a characteristic spindle-like morphology (Fig. 1F). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed co-expression of the canonical smooth muscle marker α-SMA (Fig. 1G) and the functional marker calponin (Fig. 1H). Construction and characterization of vascularized cardiac organoids

Subsequently, the three cell types were combined at an optimized ratio and seeded into AggreWell™ plates to promote three-dimensional self-assembly into uniform spheroids. These spheroids were cultured in growth-factor-free medium to support maturation and further self-organization (Fig. 1Q). After 20 days of culture, the organoids developed clear, network-like vascular structures with visible lumens (Fig. 1R), co-staining with the pericyte marker PDGFRβ revealed that the majority of vasculature in VCOs is ensheathed by PDGFRβ-positive pericytes (Fig. S1A), indicative of functional tube-like organization. The resulting organoids exhibited highly uniform sizes, with an average diameter of approximately 800 μm (Fig. 1S). A close spatial relationship was observed between cTnT+ cardiomyocytes and the CD31+ vascular network, closely mimicking the microenvironment of native cardiac tissue. Flow cytometric analysis at multiple time points revealed a stable cardiomyocyte proportion (60–70%), while the endothelial cell population increased dynamically over time, reaching about 8% by day 20. In contrast, the pericyte proportion decreased progressively from approximately 20% at day 5 to around 5% by day 20 (Fig. 1T). The fraction of ECs increases coincided with the morphological emergence of vascular networks (Fig. 1U). Together, these data confirm the progressive and coordinated self-assembly of a vascularized cardiac tissue construct, where cardiomyocytes provide structural and functional continuity and endothelial cells organize into microvascular networks. To quantitatively assess the architectural maturity of the vascular network, we randomly sampled 57 vessels from three independent organoids and analyzed their diameter distribution. Statistical analysis showed that the size distribution closely resembled that of native microvessels. The vast majority of vessels (96.5%) had diameters below 30 μm, displaying a physiological hierarchy: 26 vessels (45.6%) were <10 μm, 22 vessels (38.6%) measured 10–20 μm, and 8 vessels (14.0%) were between 20 and 30 μm. Only one vessel (1.8%) exceeded 30 μm (Fig. 1V). These data demonstrate that the vascular networks self-assemble within cardiac organoids and exhibit a physiologically relevant hierarchical organization. Furthermore, to functionally characterize the cardiac organoids, we performed real-time calcium imaging using cardiomyocytes derived from a GCaMP-expressing hESC line. The results demonstrated that vascularization markedly improved cardiomyocyte function, as evidenced by increased calcium transient amplitude (ΔF/F0) and a physiological beating rate of around 50 beats per minute (BPM) (Fig. S1B).

In addition to structural characterization, we functionally validated the vascularized cardiac organoids (VCOs) by assessing their contractile properties and pharmacological responses (Fig. 1W–Z). To evaluate whether these organoids recapitulate human cardiac tissue responses, we treated them with pharmacological agents targeting distinct signaling and metabolic pathways. Exposure to the β-adrenergic receptor antagonist propranolol at 100 μM significantly reduced the beating rate to about 15 BPM, whereas lower concentrations (1 μM and 10 μM) had no detectable effect. Treatment with the calcium channel blocker verapamil, at various concentrations and durations, nearly completely suppressed contractility, confirming the high dependence of beating on calcium flux. Furthermore, inhibition of glycolysis with 2-deoxy-D-glucose and of myosin ATPase with blebbistatin each led to marked reduction or cessation of beating, demonstrating the reliance of contraction on intact energy metabolism and actomyosin machinery.

Collectively, these results confirm that the VCOs not only resemble native cardiac tissue in structure—possessing regular contractile function, an embedded three-dimensional hierarchical microvascular network, and stable cellular composition—but also mount appropriate physiological and pharmacological responses across multiple key pathways. This functional validation supports the utility of VCOs as a reliable platform for modeling cardiac physiology, screening cardiotropic drugs, and studying metabolic regulation in vitro.

The fabrication of the vascularized islet microtissue followed a strategy analogous to our previously established method for generating vascularized liver microtissues using vessel organoids [22]. The microtissue was fabricated by embedding a mixture of hiPSC-ECs and human islets within a collagen/Matrigel composite hydrogel. Within this matrix, endothelial cells sprouted and formed an extensive, interconnected microvascular network that infiltrated and enveloped individual islets (Fig. 2B and C). High-magnification imaging confirmed that the islets were densely ensheathed by CD31-positive capillaries (Fig. 2D).

To obtain uniform, discrete organoids, the bulk microtissue was micro-dissected. Each fragment, containing a single islet along with its associated vascular network and surrounding hydrogel, was individually transferred into a round-bottom ultra-low attachment plate. Through gravitational settling and subsequent self-organization over several days, these fragments condensed into well-defined, spherical vascularized islet organoids (Fig. 2E-H) with a consistent diameter of approximately 900 μm (Fig. 2G) and a vascular content of about 13% (Fig. 2I). Morphometric analysis of the intra-organoid vasculature was performed (Fig. S2A). The diameter distribution of 57 randomly sampled vessels from three independent VIOs closely mirrored that of native micro-vessels and the vascular networks in our cardiac organoids. The vast majority (98.2%) of vessels had diameters below 30 μm, exhibiting a physiological hierarchical distribution: 33 vessels (57.9%) measured 10–20 μm, 13 vessels (22.8%) were <10 μm, and 10 vessels (17.5%) ranged from 20 to 30 μm. Only one vessel (1.8%) exceeded 30 μm (Fig. 2J). These data indicate that the vascular networks formed within islet organoids closely mirror those in cardiac organoids, exhibiting a conserved hierarchical organization based on vessel diameter. While subtle differences exist in the quantitative distribution across size categories, both systems generated microvasculature with clear physiological size gradation. This consistency suggests an evolutionarily conserved program underlying the self-assembly of endothelial networks into organized lumen structures, irrespective of the supporting parenchymal cell type (islet vs. cardiac) or the initial vascularization strategy. This finding aligns with previous work demonstrating that endothelial cells, when co-cultured with supportive cell types and stimulated with factors such as VEGF and ANGPT1/2, form vascular networks with conserved architectural principles in cardiac and liver organoids [23].

Furthermore, VIOs demonstrated enhanced glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) compared to non-vascularized controls (Fig. 2K). This functional improvement, attributable to vascular co-culture, aligns with mechanisms reported in prior studies utilizing vessel-islet assembloids [24], where endothelial support promoted long-term viability and functional preservation. The same result was also recapitulated in our experiments. Viability assays performed on day 3, 5, and 7 of in vitro culture showed that vascularization significantly reduced the apoptosis rate in islet organoids compared to non-vascularized controls. Furthermore, sustained culture of islets in conditioned medium collected from blood vessel organoids (mimicking the paracrine microenvironment provided by the vasculature in vivo) similarly lowered the rate of apoptosis, demonstrating that angiocrine signaling from the vasculature contributes significantly to the long-term survival and functional maintenance of islet tissue (Fig. S2B). The observed secretory enhancement in VIOs further mirrors our previous finding of increased albumin secretion in vascularized liver organoids [22].

Together, these results suggest that integration with vascular network may represent a general strategy to boost the functional maturation and sustain the long-term physiological performance of diverse parenchymal organoids in vitro.

Design and characterization of a modular microfluidic platform for vascularized organoid Co-culture

To enable stable 3D culture and inter-organ interaction studies of vascularized organoids within a microfluidic chip, we proposed a modular culturing strategy. This approach involves housing each type of organoid within an independent chamber, which are then fluidically connected in series to simulate systemic interactions. A core design challenge was to ensure unrestricted medium flow and sufficient perfusion for each organoid while achieving uniform, reproducible organoid distribution within the chambers and preventing their loss due to fluid flow or physical constriction.

To address this, we designed a microfluidic chip integrated with an array of micropillars. The chip core features a hexagonal topological arrangement of these micropillars (Fig. 3A). This design offers two key advantages. First, the hexagonal lattice provides excellent spatial extensibility and symmetry, ensuring uniformly distributed capture sites defined by the micropillars across the chamber. This results in highly consistent organoid positioning, significantly enhancing experimental reproducibility. Second, through precise design, the inter-pillar spacing was set at 300 μm. Regarding the fluidic path, the chip leverages the internal layout of micropillars and organoids to achieve spontaneous flow partitioning into main and branch streams. The majority of the culture medium flows rapidly through the main channel (medium rush stream), providing overall circulation and mass exchange for the system. Concurrently, part of the medium is diverted into the culture chamber containing the micropillar array, forming a perfusion stream (organoid perfusion stream), facilitated by designed fluidic resistance. This perfusion stream navigates around each micropillar, thoroughly bathing and nourishing every trapped organoid. This ensures adequate supply of nutrients and gases, along with timely removal of metabolic waste.

Therefore, chip assembly was completed through the following steps. First, the base plate and top cover were fabricated via 3D printing using acrylic resin. Subsequently, the core structure, designed to house the organoids, was produced by polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) replica molding. To prevent fluid leakage, a fluorinated ethylene propylene (FEP) film was inserted as a sealing layer between the top cover and the core. The top cover featured machined inlet and outlet channels, which were connected to external tubing via polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) connectors. Continuous medium perfusion was ultimately driven by an external peristaltic pump. The final chip was realized by aligning and assembling all components (Fig. 3B and Fig. S3A and B).

Microfluidic platform enabling efficient perfusion of vascularized organoids

To optimize the culture conditions for the serially connected chip system, we first evaluated the perfusion efficiency within the chambers under different applied flow rates. A red dye tracer dissolved in DPBS with 0.1% BSA was used to monitor medium flow and distribution in real-time. The chambers were continuously recorded during perfusion initiation (Fig. S3C and Supplementary Video 3-5). Two critical time points were recorded: 1) The moment the chamber first showed discernible coloration (tracer entry, T_start); and 2) The moment the coloration intensity within the chamber reached a plateau, indicating no further change (tracer fully filled, T_end). The time required for complete chamber perfusion was defined as T_end minus T_start (Fig. S3C).

It was indicated that perfusion kinetics were highly dependent on the applied flow rates. Under low flow rates (0.0482 mL/min), Chamber 1 required approximately 960 s for complete perfusion. Perfusion into Chamber 2 began at 540 s (marked by inflow from the upstream chamber) and completed at 1260 s. At a medium flow rates (0.1928 mL/min), the complete perfusion times for Chamber 1 and Chamber 2 shortened to 220 and 320 s, respectively, with perfusion into Chamber 2 commencing earlier at 100 s. Under a higher flow rates (0.723 mL/min), perfusion was further accelerated, with both chambers achieving complete perfusion within 120 and 160 s, respectively, and perfusion into Chamber 2 starting at 40 s. These data reveal a clear trend: as flow rates increases, the temporal overlap in the perfusion processes of the two chambers increase significantly. Furthermore, the independent perfusion durations for each chamber become more comparable. This demonstrates that hydrodynamic parameters can effectively modulate the timing and efficiency of mass exchange between serially connected chambers, providing crucial guidance for selecting flow conditions for subsequent organ co-culture experiments (Fig. S3D).

Considering that both the organoids and PDMS micropillars possess a degree of deformability, which could potentially lead to organoid loss, we assessed whether high flow rates affect organoid retention within the chip. We conducted a 2-day continuous perfusion experiment under the three different flow rate conditions. It was shown that no organoid loss from the chambers was observed over the 2-day culture period, regardless of the applied flow rate. This stability originates from our precise matching of the organoid size (approximately 800 μm in diameter) and the micropillar spacing (300 μm). This geometric relationship ensures that organoids are reliably trapped by the micropillar array while having sufficient growth space without excessive constriction, thereby maintaining reliable physical retention and stable culture positioning even under high flow rates (Fig. S3E).

To characterize the perfusion properties of the platform, we first assessed the convective delivery and uniformity of nutrient distribution within the organoids. Bovine serum albumin conjugated to Cy5 (BSA-Cy5, 5 μg/mL) was perfused through the microfluidic circuit for 2 h. Organoids located at the inlet (L1), middle (L2), and outlet (L3) regions of the chamber were then collected, fixed, and imaged by confocal fluorescence microscopy. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity showed no significant difference in BSA-Cy5 distribution between the periphery and the center of organoids across all three locations, indicating uniform convective delivery without positional bias within the microfluidic system (Fig. S3F and G).

To further evaluate the functionality of the intra-organoid vascular network, FITC-conjugated CD31 antibody (5 μg/mL) was circulated for 4 h, allowing it to bind luminal endothelial surfaces accessible to flow. After perfusion, organoids were fixed, immunostained for CD31 using a secondary antibody conjugated to AF594, and imaged. Strong co-localization of the perfused CD31-FITC signal with the total CD31-AF594 staining was observed, confirming that the vascular lumens were perfusable and functionally connected to the circulating medium. This result directly demonstrates that the vascular networks within the organoids contain patent, functional lumens, consistent with the lumen-like structures observed in prior fluorescence imaging (Fig. 3C).

In summary, this chip design resolves the conflict between uniform organoid immobilization and unrestricted growth through its hexagonal micropillar array. Furthermore, the main-perfusion stream design balances system-wide circulation with localized perfusion. Together, they provide a reliable platform for achieving long-term, stable co-culture and interaction studies of vascularized organoids.

Transcriptomic evidence of functional maturation and homeostatic regulation in normoglycemic (NG) Co-culture

To determine the impact of islet co-culture on vascularized cardiac organoids under baseline physiological conditions, we performed RNA sequencing on VCOs cultured alone (NG) and in co-culture with VIOs (NG + VIOs) under normoglycemia after 7 days (Fig. 3D). Volcano plot analysis confirmed distinct differential gene expression between the two groups (Fig. 3E). GO enrichment analysis of genes upregulated in the NG + VIOs group revealed significant enrichment in processes related to energy metabolism and cellular stability, including oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial gene expression, aerobic respiration, and protein stabilization. This indicates that co-culture enhances metabolic activity and promotes physiological stability within cardiac organoids (Fig. 3F). Using GCaMP-labeled VCOs and VIOs in co-culture system, we found that co-culture further enhanced cardiac calcium transient function but did not accelerate the beating frequency of the VCOs (Fig. S4D). Conversely, downregulated genes were primarily enriched in pathways such as the Wnt signaling pathway, response to endoplasmic reticulum stress, positive regulation of cell development, and cellular response to insulin stimulus. This pattern suggests that signals derived from islet co-culture help maintain cellular homeostasis in cardiac organoids and suppress developmental and stress-associated transcriptional programs. Transcriptomic analysis under normoglycemic conditions revealed a coordinated downregulation of the GO term "cellular response to insulin stimulus" in VCOs co-cultured with VIOs (Fig. 3G). Analysis of specific downregulated genes within this pathway provided a mechanistic clue: reduced expression of SORBS1(a gene whose downregulation is associated with increased cellular insulin sensitivity) [25] and IDE(Insulin-Degrading Enzyme; moderate downregulation of which can potentiate insulin signaling) [26] suggests that this transcriptional change may reflect an adaptive shift towards a state of enhanced insulin sensitivity rather than impaired signaling. To functionally validate this transcriptional adaptation and assess the insulin responsiveness of cardiac tissue, we quantified the expression of GLUT4, the key insulin-regulated facilitative glucose transporter, by immunofluorescence. The results showed that co-culture with VIOs promoted an overall increase in GLUT4 expression in VCOs, which may contribute to enhanced glucose sensitivity within the cardiac tissue (Fig. S4E).

KEGG pathway analysis further supported these findings. Pathways including oxidative phosphorylation and cardiac muscle contraction were upregulated in the NG + VIOs group, suggesting a potential enhancement of energy metabolism (Fig. 3H). We also observed upregulation of the tight junction pathway, which is associated with the maturation of vascular structures. However, immunofluorescence co-localization of ZO-1 and VE-cadherin revealed that vascular structures in VCOs alone already exhibited well-formed tight junctions; therefore, co-culture with VIOs did not significantly further enhance this aspect of junctional integrity (Fig. S4F).

In contrast, pathways such as Notch signaling pathway, Wnt signaling pathway, and TGF-beta signaling pathway were significantly downregulated. Given that Notch and Wnt pathways are critically involved in cardiomyocyte lineage specification, proliferation, and early heart morphogenesis [27,28], and TGF-beta signaling regulates extracellular matrix deposition and pathological remodeling [29], their downregulation aligns with a shift away from developmental/pro-fibrotic states toward maintained functional maturity (Fig. 3I). These results were consistently reproduced in an independent replicate experiment (Fig. S4A–C).

In summary, transcriptomic profiling under normoglycemic conditions demonstrates that islet co-culture actively modulates the cardiac organoid transcriptome, enhancing metabolic pathways and promoting a gene expression signature associated with functional maturity and homeostasis.

Simulation of diabetic cardiomyopathy using a hyperglycemic (HG) environment

To model diabetic cardiomyopathy in vitro, we exposed the interconnected vascularized cardiac organoids (VCOs) to a hyperglycemic environment. The basal CDM3 medium, containing a physiological glucose concentration (11.11 mM), served as the normal control (0 mM added glucose, NG). We introduced two hyperglycemic conditions: 20 mM added glucose (HG20) to mimic a state of moderate diabetes, and 70 mM added glucose (HG70) to simulate severe, acute hyperglycemia or diabetic crisis, serving as a positive control for pathological injury.

Given that diabetic cardiomyopathy in vivo is characterized by profound structural damage to cardiomyocytes, we first investigated the microstructural changes of cardiomyocytes within the VCOs under these conditions. Whole-mount immunofluorescence staining for cardiac troponin T (cTnT) and Phalloidin (indicate whole cytoskeleton) was performed, followed by high-resolution confocal imaging. The images revealed striking glucose concentration-dependent disruption of cardiomyocyte cytoarchitecture. In the NG group, cardiomyocyte myofilaments exhibited well-organized, linear sarcomere arrays with a high degree of parallel alignment (overall deviation angle <10°) (Fig. 4A–C). In contrast, the HG20 group showed significant myofilament disorganization, with approximately 70% of the cardiomyocytes displaying severe structural damage, including apparent myofilament fragmentation. The parallel alignment was markedly disturbed, with an average deviation angle increased to about 30°. The structural deterioration was most severe in the HG70 group. Here, the typically continuous myofibrillar networks were largely replaced by punctate, scattered cTnT signals. Only about 10% of cardiomyocytes maintained normal structural integrity. Furthermore, the myofilament alignment was profoundly disordered, with an average deviation angle reaching approximately 50°. This severe cytoarchitectural breakdown was further quantified by a significant reduction in sarcomere length in the HG70 group (Fig. 4D). Cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, a hallmark of diabetic cardiomyopathy characterized by increased cell area and nuclear spacing, was also faithfully recapitulated in our VCO model. Quantitative analysis revealed that the average distance between nuclei increased from approximately 2 μm in the NG group to about 4 μm under hyperglycemic stress, representing a two-fold increase (Fig. 4E). Protective role of islet organoids in diabetic cardiomyopathy progression

To evaluate the potential protective role of pancreatic islets during disease progression, we introduced vascularized islet organoids (VIOs) into the system under hyperglycemic co-culture conditions (Fig. 4A–E). Under moderate hyperglycemia, co-culture with VIOs (HG20+VIOs) significantly attenuated disease progression compared to VCOs cultured alone (HG20). In the HG20+VIOs group, only about 40% of cardiomyocytes exhibited sarcomere damage, and both myofilament misalignment and increased nuclear spacing were restored to levels comparable to the normoglycemic control (NG), indicating effective structural preservation by functional islet tissue. In stark contrast, the protective effect was insufficient against extreme glucotoxicity in the HG70+VIOs group. Although myofilament alignment and nuclear spacing were maintained near normal levels, severe sarcomere disintegration was not alleviated relative to the HG70 group without islets. Given that the sarcomere is the fundamental contractile unit, its widespread destruction would still lead to severe functional decline.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis further corroborated these structural findings (Fig. 4F–K). Sarcomere damage was severe in the HG20 and HG70 groups, consistent with immunofluorescence observations. Additionally, TEM revealed increased lipid droplet accumulation in both high-glucose groups. This accumulation was significantly reduced in the HG20+VIO group but showed no significant improvement in the HG70+VIO group compared to HG70 alone, reaffirming the dose-dependent nature of the protective effect. Mitochondrial ultrastructure was also compromised under hyperglycemia, showing matrix clearance (appearing as electron-lucent vacuoles), shortened cristae, and reduced numbers. Severely damaged mitochondria exhibited cristae disorganization and enlarged morphology, particularly in the 70 mM condition. Normal and damaged mitochondria are indicated by yellow and red arrows, respectively, in the micrographs.

These findings demonstrate that vascularized islet organoids can significantly mitigate diabetic cardiomyopathy-associated structural damage under moderate hyperglycemia, but this protective capacity is overwhelmed under conditions mimicking acute diabetic crisis. This suggests that the compensatory function of islets is a critical determinant of cardiac injury progression during diabetes. Our interactive platform thus successfully models this dynamic process of inter-organ protection and its eventual failure.

Vascular dysfunction in diabetic cardiomyopathy modeled by VCOs

Vascular injury is a major complication of diabetes. Utilizing vascularized cardiac organoids (VCOs) enables the modeling of this critical aspect within the context of diabetic cardiomyopathy [30].

A prominent observation was the differential effect on vascular networks (Fig. 4L–P). Co-culture with VIOs under hyperglycemic conditions (HG20+VIOs and HG70+VIOs) significantly promoted vascular growth, as evidenced by increased total vessel length. This is likely attributable to the high glycolytic dependence of endothelial cells for angiogenesis [31], coupled with potentially enhanced glucose uptake in the co-culture system, thereby fueling vessel expansion. In contrast, VCOs cultured alone in high glucose (HG20 and HG70) did not show a significant increase in total vessel length compared to the normoglycemic control. Notably, the HG70 group exhibited a significant reduction in vessel length compared to the HG20 group. Furthermore, the HG70+VIOs group showed a significant increase in the number of vascular junctions, reflecting enhanced network complexity and density. Although HG20 and HG70 conditions alone did not increase total vessel length, they induced significant vessel dilation (increased diameter).

Strikingly, high-resolution morphological analysis revealed severe structural damage to the vasculature under hyperglycemic stress (Fig. 4Q). Approximately 80% of vessels in the HG20 group displayed a disrupted morphology, while HG70 conditions led to near-complete vascular disintegration. This profound structural damage likely explains the lack of net growth in total vessel length despite a pro-angiogenic substrate (high glucose). Importantly, although VIO co-culture promoted vascular abundance, it did not fully prevent this high glucose-induced structural damage, indicating an intrinsic sensitivity of endothelial cells to glucotoxicity.

These vascular phenotypes mirror the trends observed in cardiomyocytes: high glucose directly causes severe structural damage, while the presence of functional islets (VIOs) provides partial protection and modulates the pathological response, particularly under moderate hyperglycemia.

To further evaluate the ability of the VCO model to mimic the pathological state of diabetes, we examined key pathological alterations induced by hyperglycemia: oxidative stress, cell death, and fibrosis. Using the DCFH-DA probe to assess reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, whole-mount fluorescence imaging revealed that the hyperglycemic environment led to a significant increase in ROS within VCOs, while islet co-culture effectively reduced ROS levels (Fig. S5A and S5B). Consistent results from TUNEL and PI staining demonstrated that high-glucose treatment significantly increased cell death in VCOs, and islet co-culture lowered the risk of cell death (Fig. S5C–F). Staining analysis of VCOs using the fibrosis marker COL1A1 showed that hyperglycemic conditions, particularly in the HG70 group, induced the formation of distinct fibrotic areas, meanwhile, islets exerted significant cardioprotective effects. (Fig. S5G and S5H).

In summary, we systematically validated the effectiveness of the constructed diabetic heart disease model across multiple dimensions using microfluidic co-culture platform, including cardiomyocyte microstructure, vascular structure, apoptosis, ROS levels, and tissue fibrosis. These findings also indicate that our platform faithfully models a key physiological characteristic of islet protection in a dose-dependent manner.

Volcano plot analysis confirmed significant differential gene expressions between the NG and HG70 groups, identifying a total of 1882 differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Among these, 1165 genes were upregulated, and 657 genes were downregulated in the HG70 group compared to the NG control (Fig. 5B). GO and KEGG analyses of HG70 VCOs revealed a coordinated downregulation of pathways essential for cardiac structure and contractility, directly correlating with the severe cytoarchitectural disruption we observed. The downregulation of cardiac muscle contraction and cGMP-PKG signaling pathway aligns with the loss of sarcomere integrity and myofilament misalignment [32]. Concurrently, the downregulation of cytoplasmic translation and aerobic respiration pathways explains the impaired synthesis of structural proteins and the energy deficit that likely precipitates this breakdown [33] (Fig. 5C and E).

Conversely, the upregulation of pathological pathways provided a mechanistic basis for the injury. The significant activation of the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications, along with NF-κB and TNF signaling pathways, delineates the core inflammatory and oxidative stress cascade triggered by high glucose [34]. This molecular state directly explains the observed cellular damage. Furthermore, the upregulation of the TGF-beta signaling pathway and Lipid and atherosclerosis pathways offers a molecular correlation for the cardiomyocyte hypertrophy (increased nuclear spacing) and the associated vascular dysfunction (vessel dilation and disruption) seen in our model [29,35] (Fig. 5D and F).

Transcriptomic analysis of the HG70+VIOs group revealed a profound rescue signature that molecularly explains the phenotypic protection. The downregulation of key injury pathways—AGE-RAGE signaling, TNF signaling, TGF-beta signaling, Insulin resistance, and Lipid and atherosclerosis—demonstrates that islet co-culture systemically dampens the glucotoxicity drivers of inflammation, fibrotic signaling, and metabolic dysfunction. This is the direct molecular counterpart to the preserved myofilament alignment and reduced structural damage in cardiomyocytes, as well as the modulated vascular response (Fig. 5I and K).

Strikingly, islet co-culture specifically reversed the metabolic paralysis induced by HG70. The upregulation of Oxidative phosphorylation, Citrate cycle (TCA cycle), mitochondrion organization, and glutathione metabolism pathways indicates a restoration of mitochondrial bioenergetics and antioxidant capacity. This metabolic rescue provides the energy and redox homeostasis necessary to support the observed maintenance of sarcomere structure and overall cellular integrity (Fig. 5J and L).

To pinpoint the processes most critically salvaged by islet co-culture, we performed a trend analysis focusing on genes with a "down-then-up" expression pattern (down in HG70 vs. NG, up in HG70+VIOs vs. HG70) (Fig. 5M). GO enrichment of this gene cluster highlighted the very processes that define functional myocardium: cardiac muscle contraction, translation, tricarboxylic acid cycle, and mitochondrion organization (Fig. 5N). This targeted analysis confirms that the protective effect of islets is not broad but is precisely focused on restoring the core transcriptional programs for contractility, protein synthesis, and energy metabolism—each of which was visibly compromised at the phenotypic level. These results were consistently reproduced in an independent replicate experiment (Fig. S6).

In summary, our transcriptomic data does not merely list pathway alterations; they provide a coherent molecular narrative that directly explains the phenotypic hallmarks of diabetic cardiomyopathy modeled in our VCOs. They demonstrate that the severe structural damage results from a synergistic collapse of metabolic, contractile, and synthetic programs, driven by defined inflammatory and stress pathways. Crucially, they reveal that islet co-culture exerts protection by specifically counteracting these driver pathways and reinstating the transcriptional foundations of cardiac metabolic and structural homeostasis.

Discussion

Organ-on-a-chip technology is fundamentally shifting the paradigm of biomedical research by leveraging micro-engineered devices to simulate complex multi-tissue interactions [[36], [37], [38]]. In this study, we successfully developed a vascularized heart-islet multi-organoid chip platform and utilized it to uncover the dose-dependent protective role of functional islets in diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM). Our work not only validates the technical feasibility of modeling long-range organ-organ crosstalk but also establishes the central cardioprotective role of islets, providing a highly scalable, human-relevant platform for investigating metabolic diseases.

The field of multi-organ chips has seen remarkable progress recently. For instance, integrated systems linking heart, liver, bone, and skin have highlighted the importance of vascular barriers in maintaining tissue phenotypes [39], while liver-islet models have focused on the pathogenesis of Type 2 diabetes [6]. However, a significant gap remains in modeling the dynamic inter-organ regulation underlying DCM—a severe systemic complication of diabetes. The primary innovation of our study lies in overcoming a critical bottleneck in DCM research: the lack of human-relevant models capable of recapitulating dynamic organ-organ interactions. Conventional single-organoid cultures or static co-culture models fail to reproduce the remote, dynamic signaling mediated by the circulatory system [40]. By integrating vascularized organoids with controlled perfusion, our microfluidic platform reconstructs a functional "islet-heart axis" in vitro. The introduction of fluid dynamics as a key variable ensures that the spatiotemporal distribution of hormones, cytokines, and metabolites closely mimics the physiological environment, providing a robust foundation for observing the dose-dependent effects of islet function.

Utilizing this platform, we obtained key insights into the pathological mechanisms of DCM, directly demonstrating the indispensable role of islets in alleviating high-glucose-induced cardiac injury. Our experiments confirmed that under moderate high-glucose stress (HG20), co-cultured islet organoids significantly mitigated sarcomere disruption, pathological hypertrophy, and microvascular dysfunction in cardiomyocytes. Conversely, under extreme high-glucose conditions (HG70), this protective effect was markedly diminished. This dose-dependent phenomenon may provide a mechanistic explanation for the clinical progression of heart failure, where cardiac function remains relatively stable during early-stage diabetes (while islets maintain compensatory capacity) but deteriorates sharply as the disease progresses toward islet exhaustion. Transcriptomic profiling further elucidated this phenotype at the molecular level: the protective effect of islets is closely associated with the restoration of cardiac energy metabolism pathways (e.g., oxidative phosphorylation, TCA cycle) and the suppression of pathological signaling pathways (e.g., AGE-RAGE, NF-κB). These findings suggest that functional islets exert their cardioprotective effects by maintaining metabolic homeostasis and inhibiting chronic inflammation and fibrosis.

While this study establishes the overall protective effect of islets, the specific molecular mediators—such as insulin, specific exosomes, or other paracrine factors—remain to be precisely identified. Future research could involve proteomic analysis of the conditioned media from the co-culture system or the use of receptor-blocking antibodies to pinpoint the key factors responsible for heart protection. Furthermore, the current model does not yet incorporate immune cells, which play a vital role in DCM pathogenesis. Given the close link between chronic inflammation and diabetic cardiac lesions, the integration of immune components, such as macrophages, into our existing platform will enable the construction of a more sophisticated "immune-metabolic-cardiac" axis, providing a more comprehensive simulation of the disease microenvironment.

In summary, this study not only introduces a powerful vascularized multi-organ chip platform but also reveals the indispensable and glucose-concentration-dependent protective function of islets in DCM. Our findings underscore the necessity of considering inter-organ communication in metabolic disease research and elevate the "islet-heart axis" from a clinical correlation to a level of causal mechanistic exploration. This work provides a robust experimental framework for the development of novel therapeutic strategies targeting diabetic cardiomyopathy.

While the present study demonstrates the feasibility of modeling inter-organ crosstalk using a microfluidic chip and vascularized organoids, it also points to a clear technological trajectory for advancing the physiological and pathological relevance of such systems. Currently, the field relies heavily on Matrigel®. While effective for organoid formation, their batch-to-batch variability, complex composition, and limited mechanical tunability constrain experimental reproducibility and the precise dissection of cell-matrix interactions. The future of organoid technology, especially for modeling complex multi-organ physiology, lies in the convergence of engineered bioactive materials, advanced 3D biomanufacturing, and integrated microfluidic systems.

Synthetic or semi-synthetic hydrogels with defined biochemical and biophysical properties represent a pivotal direction. By incorporating specific adhesive motifs (e.g., RGD peptides), protease-sensitive crosslinkers, and tunable mechanical stiffness, these designer matrices can provide a more reproducible and physiologically instructive niche. They enable the controlled presentation of growth factors and the dynamic remodeling of the extracellular environment, which is crucial for guiding organoid maturation, vascularization, and functional coupling between different tissues. Furthermore, the integration of such materials with 3D bioprinting allows for the spatially organized assembly of multiple organoid types and their supporting vasculature within a single, architecturally defined construct. This moves beyond simple co-culture towards the creation of true “body-on-a-chip” platforms with predetermined tissue interfaces and perfusion pathways.

Our modular microfluidic chip provides a foundational fluidic and interaction framework. Future iterations can directly incorporate these advanced material and fabrication strategies. For instance, 3D-bioprinted vascular networks within defined hydrogels could be precisely integrated into chip chambers, creating a more stable and scalable vascular bed. The microfluidic environment itself can then be used to deliver dynamic, organ-specific cues or to apply mechanical forces (e.g., mimicking heartbeats), further enhancing functional maturation. Ultimately, the synergy of a defined biomaterial scaffold, a biofabricated 3D tissue architecture, and a perfusion-capable microfluidic circuit will be essential to build next-generation multi-organoid systems. These systems will not only more faithfully recapitulate the pathophysiology of systemic diseases like diabetic cardiomyopathy but also provide a more robust and standardized platform for drug discovery and personalized medicine.

Methods

Human pluripotent stem cell culture

Human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs; Cellapy Biotech, China) or H9-GCaMP6f human embryonic stem cells (hESCs; WiCell Research Institute, USA) were maintained in mTeSR1 medium (StemCell Technologies, #85850) or PGM1 medium (Cellapy, #CA1001) on growth factor-reduced Matrigel®-coated plates (Corning, #356231). The medium was changed daily. Cells were passaged every 3–4 days at approximately 80% confluency using Accutase (StemCell Technologies, #07922) or DissoEasy hPSC Dissociation Reagent (Cellapy, #CA1023100). Routine mycoplasma testing was performed using a commercial detection kit (Vazyme, #PD101-01) following the manufacturer's instructions.

Differentiation of vascular lineage cells from hiPSCs

On day −3, hiPSCs were seeded into growth factor-reduced Matrigel®-coated 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well in mTeSR1 or PGM1 medium supplemented with 10 μM Y-27632 (MedChemExpress, #HY-10071). On day 0, differentiation was initiated by switching to RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, #C11875500BT) supplemented with B-27™ minus insulin (Gibco, #A18956-01) and 9–12 μM CHIR99021 (MedChemExpress, #HY-10182). On day 2, the medium was replaced with CDM3 medium (RPMI 1640 + 0.67% recombinant human albumin [Sigma, #A9731] + 0.33% L-ascorbic acid phosphate [Sigma, #A4034]) containing 100 ng/mL VEGF165 (Novoprotein, #C083) and 50 ng/mL FGF2 (Novoprotein, #C046). The medium was refreshed every 2–3 days for a total of 6 days.

Magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) and culture

On the final day of vascular progenitor differentiation, CD34+ endothelial progenitors were isolated via magnetic-activated cell sorting. Cells were dissociated using Trypsin-EDTA (0.25%, BioChannel, #BC-CE-003), centrifuged, and resuspended. The cell suspension was incubated with CD34 MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec, #130-046-702) and FcR Blocking Reagent (Miltenyi Biotec, #130-046-702) at 4 °C for 30 min. Labeled cells were passed through an MS Column (Miltenyi Biotec, #130-042-201) placed on a magnetic separator. The flow-through containing the CD34− cell fraction (hiPSC-derived smooth muscle cell progenitors, hiPSC-SMCs) was collected. After removing the column from the magnet, the retained CD34+ cell fraction (hiPSC-derived endothelial cells, hiPSC-ECs) was eluted. Both fractions were centrifuged, resuspended in CDM3 medium containing 20% FBS, and seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well. For expansion, hiPSC-ECs were maintained in CDM3 supplemented with 100 ng/mL VEGF and 50 ng/mL FGF2, while hiPSC-SMCs were cultured in CDM3 containing 50 ng/mL PDGF-BB. Medium was changed every 2–3 days. Cell identity and purity were assessed by immunofluorescence staining for CD31 (endothelial cells), α-SMA, and calponin (smooth muscle cells), and by flow cytometry for CD31+CD144+ (endothelial) and α-SMA+ (smooth muscle) populations.

Differentiation and metabolic purification of hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes

On day −3, hiPSCs or human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) were seeded into Matrigel®-coated 6-well plates at 2 × 104 cells per well in PGM1 medium with 10 μM Y-27632. Cardiomyocyte differentiation was initiated on day 0 using RPMI 1640 medium containing B-27™ minus insulin and 6 μM CHIR99021 to activate WNT signaling. On day 2, the medium was changed to CDM3 containing 3 μM IWR-1 (Sigma, #I0161) to inhibit WNT signaling and promote cardiac specification. From day 4 onward, CDM3 medium was replaced every 2–3 days. Spontaneously beating cardiomyocytes were typically observed by day 10. For metabolic purification, CDM3 was replaced on day 10 with RPMI 1640 (no glucose, Gibco, #C11879020) supplemented with 10 mM L-lactate (Sigma, #71718) to enrich for hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs). The lactate-containing medium was changed every 2–3 days. Purity was assessed by flow cytometry for cardiac troponin T (cTnT) expression at days 10 and 20.

Generation of vascularized cardiac organoids (VCOs)

HiPSC-ECs, hiPSC-SMCs, and purified hiPSC-CMs were dissociated and resuspended in CDM3 supplemented with 20% FBS and 10 μM Y-27632. To form vascularized cardiac organoids (VCOs), hiPSC-ECs (300 cells), hiPSC-SMCs (600 cells), and hiPSC-CMs (2100 cells) were combined and aggregated in AggreWell™ 800 plates (STEMCELL Technologies, #34811) according to the manufacturer's instructions. On day 2, the formed spheroids were transferred to ultra-low attachment 6-well plates and cultured in CDM3 medium, which was changed every 2–3 days. VCOs were harvested for analysis on day 20. Whole-mount immunofluorescence staining was performed using antibodies against cTnT (cardiac muscle) and CD31 (endothelium). VCO diameter, vascularization percentage, and vessel diameter distribution was quantified from confocal images using Fiji software. The cellular composition (cTnT+ and CD31+ percentages) was analyzed by flow cytometry on days 5, 10, and 20.

Isolation and culture of human pancreatic islets

Human islet isolation was conducted at the Human Islets Resource Center in Tianjin, China (No.:KY20241216-1), utilizing pancreata obtained from deceased organ donors. The isolation procedure followed a modified Ricordi protocol as we described previously [41,42], wherein pancreatic tissue underwent enzymatic digestion using either Collagenase NB1 combined with Neutral Protease NB (both from SERVA, Heidelberg, Germany) or Liberase MTF C/T (2:3) GMP Grade (Roche, Penzberg, Germany). Subsequent purification via continuous density gradient centrifugation effectively eliminated exocrine contaminants, yielding islet preparations exceeding 80% purity. Isolated islets were maintained in CMRL-1066 culture medium (Corning, Manassas, VA, USA) fortified with 10% human serum albumin (Baxter, Vienna, Austria), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and incubated at 37 °C under a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Generation of vascularized islet tissue and vascularized islet organoids (VIOs)

Human islets and hiPSC-ECs were mixed at a ratio of 1 islet to 1000 hiPSC-ECs within a hydrogel mixture consisting of Matrigel (10 mg/mL; Corning) and collagen I (3 mg/mL; Sigma) at a 1:1 vol ratio. The mixture was pipetted into a culture dish and cultured for 5 days in MCDB131 medium (Gibco, #C10372-019) supplemented with 1% GlutaMAX™, 1% B-27™ supplement, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 100 ng/mL VEGF165, and 50 ng/mL FGF2. The resulting vascularized islet tissue was fixed for whole-mount immunofluorescence staining against insulin and CD31.

To generate individual vascularized islet organoids (VIOs), the bulk tissue was micro-dissected into small fragments after 2 days. Individual fragments were transferred to an ultra-low attachment 96-well plate. Over the next 2 days, the fragments self-assembled via gravitational settling into spherical VIOs. Organoids were fixed on day 10 for whole-mount staining (insulin and CD31). VIO diameter, vascularization percentage, and vessel diameter distribution were quantified using Fiji software.

Flow cytometry

HiPSCs-ECs, hiPSCs-SMCs, hiPSCs-CMs or VCOs were washed twice with DPBS and passaged by treating with Trypsin-EDTA (0.25%, BioChannel, #BC-CE-003) at 37 °C for 1 min (VCOs 5-8 min). The digestion mixture was neutralized with an equal volume of 20% FBS in DPBS. Cells were centrifuged at 300 × g for 3 min. For hiPSCs-ECs, the sample was resuspended in FACS buffer (BD Biosciences) supplemented with fluorescently conjugated primary antibodies (CD31 and CD144). Staining was performed at 4 °C for 30 min, with the sample protected from light to prevent fluorophore quenching. For hiPSCs-SMCs and hiPSCs-CMs, the sample was fixed for 10 min and disrupted by 0.1% Triton X-100 for 20 min. The sample was resuspended in FACS buffer (BD Biosciences) supplemented with primary antibodies (hiPSCs-SMCs:α-SMA, hiPSCs-CMs:cTnT and α-actinin). After washing three times for 5 min with PBST, appropriate secondary antibodies diluted 1:200 in buffer were added for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. After staining, cells were washed twice with FACS buffer (centrifuged at 500 × g for 3 min per wash). Flow cytometry was conducted using a BD LSR Fortessa flow cytometer. For analysis, cells were first gated based on forward scatter area (FSC-A) and side scatter area (SSC-A) to exclude debris. Doublet discrimination was then performed using FSC height (FSC-H) vs. FSC width (FSC-W) and SSC height (SSC-H) vs. SSC width (SSC-W) parameters, ensuring only single cells were included in subsequent analyses. Statistical analysis and visualization were performed with FlowJo and GraphPad Prism 9.

Immunofluorescence staining

For immunostaining, the samples were incubated in blocking buffer (DPBS with 3% FBS, 1% BSA, 0.5% Triton X-100 and 0.5% Tween 20) [43] supplemented with primary antibodies at the indicated dilutions for 2 h at room temperature or overnight at 4 °C (cell staining) or for 2 days (whole mount staining) at 4 °C. After washing three times for 5 min with PBST, appropriate secondary antibodies diluted 1:200 in blocking buffer were added for 2 h at room temperature (cell staining) or 2 days (whole mount) at 4 °C in the dark. After the previous washing steps were repeated, DAPI (Sigma, D9542) was added at a final concentration of 10 μg/ml in blocking buffer for 30 min at room temperature in the dark for cell staining or whole mounts. Whole-mount samples and stained cells were ready for analysis or storage at 4 °C in PBS. The whole-mount samples can also be treated with Rapiclear (SUNJin Lab, RC149001) before imaging. The above samples were imaged for analysis via a total internal reflection fluorescence microscope TIRF&Thunder (Leica) or an LSM 800 with Airyscan (ZEISS).

Fabrication of the microfluidic chip

A master mold for the microfluidic chip was fabricated using micro-stereolithography (nanoArch® S130, BMF PRECISION TECH) with a proprietary acrylic-based photopolymer resin (HTL). The chip design was created using CAD software. The resin was loaded into the machine reservoir, and the mold was printed with a layer thickness of 15 μm, cured by a 405 nm UV laser. After printing, the mold was ultrasonically cleaned in isopropanol to remove uncured resin and then post-cured in a UV oven at 60 °C for 10 min to ensure complete cross-linking and mechanical stability.

Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS; Sylgard 184, Dow Corning) was prepared by thoroughly mixing the base and curing agent at a 10:1 (w/w) ratio for 5 min. The mixture was degassed under vacuum (−0.1 MPa) for 15 min to remove air bubbles. The degassed PDMS was then poured over the 3D-printed acrylic mold, ensuring complete coverage of the microstructures, and allowed to level on a flat surface for 30 min. The assembly was subsequently cured in an oven at 80 °C for 30 min. After curing, the solidified PDMS layer was carefully peeled off from the mold, yielding a PDMS chip with high-fidelity microstructures. All steps were performed in a clean environment to prevent contamination.

The chip fixture was fabricated from transparent polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) sheets (thickness: 4 mm) to ensure optical clarity and mechanical stability. The design for each layer was drafted using CAD software and imported into a CO2 laser cutting system. The patterns were ablated with a scan speed of 100–500 mm/s. After machining, the acrylic pieces were ultrasonically cleaned in isopropanol to remove debris and contaminants. The surfaces were treated with oxygen plasma to enhance adhesion for bonding. Multiple layers of the treated PMMA sheets were aligned and assembled. A solvent-based adhesive (chloroform) or UV-curable glue was applied for sealing. The stack was pressed in a hot press to eliminate air bubbles and ensure tight bonding between layers, followed by UV exposure for 1–10 min to complete the bonding process.

Perfusion assay of the microfluidic chip

To characterize the fluid dynamics of the chip, three flow rates corresponding to low, moderate, and high wall flow rates (0.0482, 0.1928 and 0.723 ml/min, respectively) were applied using a programmable peristaltic pump. A red dye tracer dissolved in PBS was introduced through the inlet, and the perfusion process was recorded via live brightfield imaging. The time from initial tracer entry (T_start) to full intensity equilibration (T_end) in each chamber was measured. The total perfusion time for each chamber was calculated as T_end − T_start.

To evaluate whether convective delivery was homogeneous within a single chamber, FITC-conjugated bovine serum albumin (BSA-FITC, 5 μg/mL) was perfused through the chip for 4 h. After perfusion, organoids from different locations within the same chamber were harvested separately, fixed, and imaged via confocal microscopy. Mean fluorescence intensity of BSA-FITC per organoid was quantified using ImageJ. Statistical comparison across positions confirmed no significant differences in tracer uptake, indicating uniform convective delivery throughout the chamber.

To specifically assess perfusion of the luminal vascular network, FITC-conjugated CD31 antibody (5 μg/mL) was circulated through the chip for 4 h, allowing direct labeling of endothelial luminal surfaces accessible to flow. Organoids were then fixed, permeabilized, and immunostaining with a non-conjugated CD31 primary antibody followed by an AF594-conjugated secondary antibody, thereby labeling total CD31+ vasculature. Confocal z-stack images were acquired, and the percentage of CD31-AF594+ vessels that were also positive for perfused CD31-FITC was quantified as a measure of functional intravascular perfusion efficiency.

VCOs and VIOs co-culture under different conditions

VCOs and VIOs were loaded into their respective chambers of the assembled microfluidic chip. The system was perfused with CDM3 medium containing one of the following: 0 mM added glucose (normal control), 20 mM glucose (HG20), 20 mM glucose plus VIOs in the adjacent chamber, 70 mM glucose (HG70), or 70 mM glucose plus VIOs. The medium was circulated and replaced daily for 7 days. After treatment, organoids were harvested for analysis.

Structural and functional analysis of organoids

Sarcomere organization in VCOs was assessed via immunofluorescence staining for cTnT and phalloidin. Confocal z-stacks were analyzed in ImageJ to quantify sarcomere alignment angle, sarcomere length, and the proportion of disassembled sarcomeres. Vascular networks in VIOs and VCOs were imaged by CD31 staining. Total vessel length, number of junctions, lumen diameter, and the proportion of disrupted lumens were quantified using ImageJ and ZEN software.

For ultrastructural analysis (Transmission Electron Microscopy, TEM), vascularized cardiac organoids (VCOs) were processed as follows: Samples were primarily fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) for at least 4 h at 4 °C. After three 15-min washes in the same buffer, samples were post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO4) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) for 1–2 h, followed by another three buffer washes (15 min each). Fixed samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%), spending 15–20 min at each concentration. This was followed by a 20-min incubation in absolute acetone. Dehydrated samples were infiltrated with Spurr's resin using a graded series with absolute acetone (1:1 resin/acetone for 1 h, then 3:1 resin/acetone for 3 h, both at room temperature). Samples were then transferred to pure Spurr's resin and infiltrated overnight. Finally, samples were embedded in fresh Spurr's resin in embedding molds and polymerized at 70 °C for over 9 h. Ultrathin sections (60–80 nm) were cut using an ultramicrotome (Leica EM UC7). Sections were collected on copper grids and double-stained with uranyl acetate and alkaline lead citrate, each for 5–10 min. Grids were examined using a Talos F200C transmission electron microscope operated at 80 kV.

For functional assessment, the beating rate of VCOs (beats per minute, BPM) was measured before and after treatment with pharmacological agents including propranolol (1–100 μM), verapamil (1–100 μM), 2-Deoxy-D-glucose (1–10 mM), and blebbistatin (1–10 μM). Glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) from native islets and VIOs were assessed via ELISA (#80-INSHu-E01.1) after sequential exposure to low (2.8 mM) and high (20 mM) glucose medium.

Calcium transient imaging

Human H9-GCaMP6f ESC-derived VCOs generated with the above-mentioned differentiation protocol were transferred to glass-bottom confocal culture dishes, with fresh pre-warmed CDM3 medium added prior to imaging. To capture calcium spark activity in control and experimental CCO groups, we used a Leica Thunder Imager total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) system equipped with a temperature- and gas-controlled live-cell incubation chamber, which maintained a stable physiological environment (37 °C, 5% CO2) for the entire duration of image acquisition. Fluorescence time-series were acquired at 10× magnification, with a frame sampling interval of 10 ms to ensure sufficient temporal resolution for calcium transient analysis.

Post-acquisition quantification of calcium transient parameters, including instantaneous fluorescence intensity (F), baseline fluorescence (F0), and time-to-peak of the calcium transient, was performed using the built-in analysis module of Leica Application Suite X (LAS X) software. All raw data were subjected to background subtraction and normalization, followed by statistical analysis of key functional metrics (calcium transient amplitude, beating frequency) using GraphPad Prism 9 software.

Bulk-RNA-sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted from samples of each group using TRIzol reagent. After verifying the purity and integrity of RNA, qualified samples were sequenced on an Illumina platform. Clean reads were mapped to the reference genome, and gene expression levels were normalized to FPKM. Differentially expressed genes were identified with |fold change| > 2 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed to predict the biological functions of the differentially expressed genes. Sequence data were deposited into NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under the accession number PRJNA1422459 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/?term=PRJNA1422459). All data generated or analyzed during this study are included in this published article (and its supplementary information files).

Statistics

The experimental data were quantified via Fiji. Detailed statistics and statistical significance are indicated in each figure legend. Group comparisons were performed via one-way/two-way ANOVA for experiments with one/two independent variables and ≥3 groups, followed by Student's t-test (paired/unpaired) for comparisons between two groups. The data are expressed and plotted as the mean ± SEM or mean ± SD as indicated in the figure legends. The biological replicates (independent experiments, N) and technical replicates (repeated measurements within the same experiment, n) are specified in the figure legends. Statistical analysis was performed via GraphPad Prism 9 and RStudio. The p values for significant differences are as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
