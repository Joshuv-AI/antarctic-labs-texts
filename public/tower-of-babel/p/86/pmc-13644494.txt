Healthy lifestyle attenuates the clonal hematopoiesis-associated coronary artery disease risk
Authors: Yuxin Yang, Xuxiang Shen, Keyong Huang, Hongwei Liu, Jianxin Li, Fangchao Liu, Shufeng Chen, Jie Cao, Hongfan Li, Xiaoqing Liu, Chong Shen, Ling Yu, Dongsheng Hu, Jianfeng Huang, Tong Wang, Wenjing Tian, Dongfeng Gu, Xiangfeng Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLifestyle factors and clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) both contribute to the risk of developing coronary artery disease (CAD), but whether a healthy lifestyle could attenuate CHIP-related CAD risk is unknown. A total of 6113 participants from the Prediction for Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease Risk in China project were included. Lifestyle was categorized as unfavorable, intermediate, and favorable according to the number of healthy lifestyle factors (body mass index, smoking, sleep, physical activity, and diet). CHIP mutations were detected by targeted deep sequencing (mean depth, 916 ×). After a median follow-up of 12.1 years, 760 cases of CAD were recorded. A favorable lifestyle and CHIP were associated with 53% lower (HR (hazard ratio) = 0.47; 95% CI (confidence interval): 0.36–0.61) and 41% higher (HR = 1.41; 95% CI: 1.17–1.72) CAD risk, respectively. Of note, the elevated CAD risk attributed to CHIP was found in the intermediate (HR = 1.48; 95% CI: 1.03–2.11) and unfavorable (HR = 1.57; 95% CI: 1.22–2.01) lifestyle groups, but not in the favorable (HR = 0.71; 95% CI: 0.36–1.42) group. Unfavorable lifestyle and CHIP jointly contributed to a 2.78-fold increased risk of CAD (95% CI: 1.95–3.97) compared with non-CHIP carriers with a favorable lifestyle, indicating a significant additive interaction between CHIP and lifestyle (RERI (relative excess risk due to interaction) = 1.08; 95% CI: 0.40–1.77). Specifically, the protective effect of lifestyle depends on the size of cell fractions, with an intermediate lifestyle mitigating CAD risk in small clone carriers (VAF (variant allele fraction): 0.5%-2%), and a favorable lifestyle being necessary for common clone carriers (VAF ≥ 2%). In summary, CHIP carriers exhibited a higher risk of CAD, while a favorable lifestyle could attenuate the excess risk related to CHIP. Efforts should be made to promote intensive behavioral interventions among CHIP carriers.Keywords: Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential, Lifestyle, Coronary artery disease, Small clones, Inflammatory cytokines

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD), particularly coronary artery disease (CAD), represent a significant global health challenge, accounting for over 28% of all deaths worldwide in 2021 [1]. In China alone, over 330 million individuals were affected by CVD, including 11 million with CAD, responsible for > 40% of the deaths [2,3]. As the burden of CAD continues to rise, identifying and addressing modifiable risk factors is increasingly urgent.

The acquisition of an expanded somatic blood cell clone in the absence of other hematologic abnormalities is defined as clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP), and the genes DNMT3A and TET2 are the most frequently mutated in CHIP [[4], [5], [6]]. Recent studies have shown that CHIP is closely linked to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Individuals with CHIP are at a one- to twofold increased risk of developing CAD compared with those without the condition [9]. Sequencing depth profoundly affects the accuracy of CHIP detection [[11], [12], [13]]. In most prior studies, CHIP is usually defined as a variant allele fraction (VAF) of 2% or greater [10,14,15]. However, with the increase in sequencing depth, the detection of small clones has become more precise, enabling the identification of a larger number of CHIP carriers at earlier stages.

Lifestyle behaviors are well-established risk factors for CAD [16,17]. Previous studies have confirmed that lifestyle has a significant impact on modulating the germline genetic risk of CAD [[18], [19], [20]]. However, there is limited evidence of lifestyle in the context of CHIP, with only one study from the UK biobank focusing on the effects of diet on CVD incidence among CHIP carriers [21]. Results suggest that among individuals with CHIP, those with a healthier diet had the fewest incident cardiovascular (CV) events. Still, the extent to which a healthy lifestyle can attenuate the risk of CAD among CHIP carriers remains unclear.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the association between CHIP, healthy lifestyle, and CAD to provide further insights into CHIP-related CAD in the general population.

Material and methods

Study population

The study participants were derived from three sub-cohorts of the China-PAR (Prediction for Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease Risk in China) project: the International Collaborative Study of Cardiovascular Disease in Asia (InterASIA), the China Multi-Center Collaborative Study of Cardiovascular Epidemiology (ChinaMUCA), and the Community Intervention of Metabolic Syndrome in China & Chinese Family Health Study (CIMIC). Detailed descriptions of the study designs are available elsewhere [22]. Briefly, we include two case-cohort studies derived from China MUCA and InterASIA, as well as a case-control study sampled from CIMIC [9] (Figure S1). The blood sample collection for each cohort was as follows: InterASIA (2000–2001), China MUCA and CIMIC (2007–2008). Then, InterASIA had its first follow-up in 2007–2008, and all cohorts were further followed up in 2012–2015 and 2018–2021 using a unified protocol. This study received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Fuwai Hospital in Beijing, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection (ethics approval number: 2022–1665).

A total of 6205 participants were available for CHIP detection. After excluding individuals with invalid sequencing information, or lacking lifestyle indicators and key covariates, a total of 6113 individuals were included in this main analysis. For analysis of lifestyle changes, 4126 individuals with ≥3 face-to-face visits and complete data on all five lifestyle factors at the first follow-up visit were included.

Data collection and healthy lifestyle definition

At baseline and each follow-up, study participants were interviewed and underwent physical examinations and laboratory tests. Information on demographic characteristics, lifestyle behaviors, family history, and personal medical history was collected by trained staff through standardized questionnaires. Detailed information on data collection and outcome definitions can be found in the Supplementary Methods.

A healthy lifestyle score was developed using lifestyle-related components of the American Heart Association’s Life’s Essential 8 recommendations [23], following the scoring methods described in previous studies [17,24,25]. The score includes body mass index (BMI), smoking, physical activity, diet, and sleep duration. For smoking status, never smoking was defined as healthy. Optimal BMI was defined as a range of 18.5–24.9. A healthy diet based on the Chinese Dietary Guidelines [26] was defined as having the following dietary habits ≥ 3 items (fresh vegetables and fruits ≥ 500 g/day; fish ≥ 200 g/week; soy products ≥ 125 g/day; red meat < 75 g/day; and tea ≥ 50 g/month). For physical activity, the definition followed the World Health Organization recommendations (≥ 2.5 h of moderate-intensity activity per week, ≥ 1.25 h of vigorous-intensity activity per week, or an equivalent combination of moderate and vigorous-intensity activity) [27]. Adequate sleep (7–9 h/day) was classified as healthy [[28], [29], [30]]. Participants were assigned 1 point for each healthy lifestyle behavior met, and 0 points otherwise. The sum of these points created a lifestyle score, with higher scores indicating healthier lifestyles. Then, the score was categorized as unfavorable (0–2 healthy behaviors), intermediate (3 healthy behaviors), and favorable (4–5 healthy behaviors). In addition, we created a weighted healthy lifestyle score, in which each lifestyle was weighted by the average effect size (β-coefficient) for CAD obtained from multivariate Cox proportional hazard models [[31], [32], [33]]. Lifestyle changes were assessed between baseline and the first follow-up (mean duration: 7 years) and classified into four groups. Detailed grouping methods for lifestyle change were available in Supplementary Methods.

CHIP detection

CHIP mutations were detected based on the first time of blood sample collection in each cohort. All DNA samples were extracted and sequenced concurrently using the same standardized protocols. A unified bioinformatics pipeline was applied for data preprocessing and variant calling, ensuring consistency in data quality and interpretation [9]. In brief, a custom panel including 90 leukemia-related genes was designed to detect somatic mutations [8,34]. DNA was extracted from frozen peripheral blood specimens. Participants' libraries were generated using a probe pool and capture kit, followed by sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Boke Biotech). Putative somatic mutations were called and filtered according to a standardized protocol, with candidate CHIP driver mutations identified based on widely accepted criteria [8,34]. The median depth of targeted deep sequencing was 916 ×. Based on the sequencing depth of our study, we applied a VAF cutoff of 0.5% to detect CHIP mutations with a sensitivity exceeding 84% [9]. The widely accepted definition of CHIP typically uses a VAF threshold of 2% [4]. Accordingly, we classified CHIP mutations with VAF ≥ 2% as common clones, while those with VAF below 2% were categorized as small clones.

Inflammatory cytokines

We further selected 2418 participants from InterASIA and China MUCA to test the inflammatory cytokines. Inflammatory cytokines considered to be associated with CAD or CHIP were examined (e.g., interleukin (IL) 6, IL-8, tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), C-reactive protein, and serum amyloid A). Plasma samples were analyzed using an automated enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) microfluidic system (ELLA; ProteinSimple) with specialized cartridges, following the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Statistical analysis

We present the baseline characteristics of the included participants as means with corresponding standard deviations for continuous variables and as percentages for categorical variables. We used multivariable linear regression to assess the association between CHIP and inflammatory cytokine levels. To assess the link between CHIP mutations and CAD risk, a Prentice-weighted proportional hazard regression model was applied in the two case-cohort studies [35], while binomial logistic regression was used in the case-control study. Hazard ratios (HRs) and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for the case-cohort and case-control studies, respectively. The extended correlation test based on Schoenfeld residuals [36] revealed no violations of the proportional hazards assumption (P > 0.05). In the absence of heterogeneity across cohorts, an inverse variance-weighted, fixed-effect model was employed to combine the estimates. All models were adjusted for age, gender, mean arterial pressure, type 2 diabetes, and total cholesterol value at the time of recruitment. The combined effect of CHIP and lifestyle on incident CAD was estimated, with the group of non-CHIP and a favorable lifestyle as the reference. Multiplicative interaction was evaluated by comparing models with and without interaction (cross-product) terms using the likelihood ratio test. Additive interaction was assessed based on the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and the attributable proportion due to interaction (AP), with statistical significance defined as 95% CIs that did not include zero. We conducted sensitivity analyses: 1) using alternative lifestyle grouping methods; 2) applying a weighted healthy lifestyle score; 3) further adjusting for sociodemographic factors (region, area, education, and occupation) and lipid-lowering medication; and 4) excluding current smokers.

A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Bonferroni correction was used for multiple tests when evaluating individual lifestyle factors (0.05/5). All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among the 6113 participants, the mean age was 53.8 years, and 49.9% were female. During a median follow-up of 12.1 years, 760 participants (12.4%) developed CAD. 48.8% of participants were classified as having an unfavorable lifestyle, 33.3% as having an intermediate lifestyle, and 17.9% as having a favorable lifestyle (Table 1). Individuals with an unfavorable lifestyle had higher systolic blood pressure and total cholesterol levels (Table 1). 1085 individuals (17.7%) were identified as CHIP carriers. The prevalence of CHIP mutations increased with advancing age (Figure S2). There was no significant gender-specific differences in the prevalence of CHIP (Figure S3). The most common CHIP mutations were found in DNMT3A, TET2, and ASXL1, in order (Figure S4).

Effects of lifestyle and CHIP on CAD

The risk of CAD decreases with improvements in lifestyle behaviors. Never smoking and adequate sleep duration were associated with a decreased risk of CAD, while the association did not reach statistical significance in relation to regular physical activity, healthy diet, and optimal BMI (Table S1). Compared to those with an unfavorable lifestyle, the hazard ratio (HR) of CAD for individuals with intermediate and favorable lifestyles was 0.82 (95% CI: 0.69–0.96; P = 0.01) and 0.47 (95% CI: 0.36–0.61; P = 5.23×10–9), respectively (Table S2). Similar results were observed when using the weighted healthy lifestyle score (Table S3).

The incidence of CAD was significantly higher in CHIP carriers compared to non-carriers (210 individuals (19.4%) vs. 550 individuals (10.9%); P < 0.001). After adjusting for covariates, CHIP carriers had a 41% higher risk of CAD compared to non-carriers (HR = 1.41; 9% CI: 1.17–1.72; P = 4.50 × 10–4) (Table S4).

Associations between CHIP and incident CAD by lifestyle categories

There was marked disparity in CHIP-related CAD risk across lifestyle categories. Elevated CAD risk attributed to CHIP was found in the intermediate (HR = 1.48; 95%CI: 1.03–2.11; P = 0.03) and unfavorable (HR = 1.57; 95% CI: 1.22–2.01; P = 4.64 × 10–4) lifestyle groups, but not in the favorable (HR = 0.71; 95% CI: 0.36–1.42; P = 0.34) lifestyle group (Fig. 1a). The association between CHIP and CAD under different lifestyle groups was comparable among the three cohorts. (Figure S5). When compared with non-CHIP carriers in the favorable lifestyle, participants with both CHIP exposure and unfavorable lifestyle had the highest risk of CAD, up to 2.78-fold (HR = 2.78; 95% CI: 1.95–3.97; P = 1.90 × 10–8) (Fig. 1b). We assessed the interaction between CHIP and lifestyle categories on the risk of CAD and found significant additive interactions between CHIP and both intermediate and unfavorable lifestyles (unfavorable lifestyle: RERI = 1.08, AP = 0.40; intermediate lifestyle: RERI = 0.75, AP = 0.36) (Table 2), despite the absence of significant multiplicative interaction (P = 0.15). Consistent results were observed when dissecting healthy lifestyles into five individual lifestyle factors (Fig. 2).

We further evaluated whether the protective effect of the lifestyle on CHIP-related CAD risk depended on the size of cell fractions. In the unfavorable lifestyle group, CAD risk was significantly higher for both small (HR = 1.53; 95% CI: 1.07–2.16; P = 0.02) and common clone carriers (HR = 1.58; 95% CI: 1.15–2.16; P = 4.48 × 10–3). In the intermediate lifestyle group, common clone carriers still showed a marked increase in CAD risk (HR = 1.66; 95% CI: 1.05–2.62; P = 0.03), while not significant for small clone carriers. Notably, in the favorable lifestyle group, no increased CAD risk was observed among CHIP carriers, regardless of whether they had common or small clones (Fig. 3).

We then investigated to what extent the increased risk caused by CHIP could be attenuated by changes in lifestyle. The number and proportion of participants categorized as initially favorable, improving, intermediate to intermediate/unfavorable or unfavorable to intermediate and stable unfavorable were as follows: 778 (18.86%), 436 (10.57%), 1698 (41.15%) and 1214 (29.42%). Stable unfavorable lifestyle pattern intensified the risk of CAD by CHIP (HR = 1.63; 95% CI: 1.11–2.40; P = 0.014). CHIP carriers who had an intermediate to intermediate/unfavorable or unfavorable to intermediate lifestyle also exhibited a 1.59-fold higher CAD risk (HR = 1.59; 95% CI: 1.06–2.37; P = 0.024). Notably, CHIP carriers with improving lifestyle (HR = 2.52; 95% CI: 0.86–7.36; P = 0.09) and initially favorable lifestyle (HR = 1.31; 95%CI: 0.57–3.03; P = 0.53) patterns did not show a significantly increased CAD risk (Table S5).

Inflammatory cytokines

After adjusting for covariates and applying Bonferroni correction, CHIP was associated with higher IL-6 (P = 5.35 × 10–3) levels (Table S6). When stratified by lifestyle groups, IL-6 and TNF-α was associated with CHIP in the unfavorable lifestyle group (IL-6: β = 1.70; 95% CI: 0.49–2.91; P = 6.15 × 10–3; TNF-α: β = 0.64; 95% CI: 0.17–1.10; P = 7.07 × 10–3), but this association was not statistically significant in the favorable lifestyle group (IL-6: β = 0.02; 95% CI: −1.16–1.21; P = 0.97; TNF-α: β = −4.48; 95% CI: −11.83–2.87; P = 0.23) (Table S7).

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted several sensitivity analyses to probe the robustness of our findings. The association patterns of CHIP within each lifestyle category remained consistent when using alternative lifestyle classification methods; when calculating the healthy lifestyle score using a weighted method; when further adjusting for sociodemographic factors; and when excluding current smokers (Tables S8–11).

Discussion

In this prospective, population-based, large-cohort study, a favorable lifestyle could attenuate the CAD risk associated with CHIP, and this protective effect appears to be dependent on the size of the cell fraction. Additionally, we observed consistent attenuation of CHIP-related CAD risk with long-term lifestyle improvements. The analysis of inflammatory cytokines further suggests that inflammation alleviation may serve as an important mechanism underlying the observed risk reduction. These findings have important implications for lifestyle interventions targeting CHIP to regulate associated CAD risk.

CAD is influenced by both environmental and genetic factors. Previous large-scale genomic studies have primarily focused on the interplay between behavioral lifestyle and germline genetic risk. Several studies have confirmed that maintaining a healthy lifestyle can reduce the risk of CAD, and these benefits persist even among populations with a higher genetic risk [[18], [19], [20]]. However, unlike germline mutations, the CHIP may itself be influenced by lifestyle factors [[37], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42]]. Therefore, the extent to which a lifestyle can modulate CHIP-related CAD risk may be of significant importance. A study from the UK Biobank showed that compared with participants without CHIP who had an intermediate diet, those with CHIP who had an unhealthy diet had a higher risk for a cardiovascular event [21]. Based on a large population-based cohort integrating multiple lifestyle factors, our study found that CAD risk attributed to CHIP could be offset by healthy lifestyles. Further analysis of individual lifestyle factors suggested that diet may be the most protective factor, followed by adequate sleep duration. Additionally, by examining the long-term lifestyle changes, we found that lifestyle improvements also played a significant role in reducing CHIP-related CAD risk, supporting the reliability of our results.

Leveraging high-sensitivity sequencing (mean depth: 916 ×), we found that CHIP was detectable in approximately 17% of middle-aged individuals, with small clone carriers (0.5% ≤ VAF < 2%) accounting for nearly half of all CHIP carriers. The distribution and prevalence of CHIP in our study are consistent with recent investigations employing comparable ultra-deep sequencing strategies (median depth > 1000 ×) [43,44]. Notably, our prior work demonstrated that small CHIP clones confer a 1.35-fold (95% CI, 1.12–1.63) increased risk of CAD [9]. Building upon these observations, the current investigation reveals a differential lifestyle-CAD risk interaction: individuals with small clones derive significant risk reduction from intermediate-to-favorable lifestyles, whereas those with common clones require optimal lifestyle adherence to achieve comparable risk mitigation. Given that small-clone carriers were more prevalent among adults with CHIP under 60 years of age, and were younger than those with VAF ≥ 2%, lifestyle interventions should be introduced earlier, particularly in this sub-population.

Lifestyle may modulate the association between CHIP and CAD through inflammatory reduction. We found that CHIP was associated with higher IL-6 and TNF-α levels in the unfavorable lifestyle group, while this relationship was not significant in the favorable lifestyle group. Research indicates that lifestyle interventions could significantly reduce inflammatory responses, particularly IL-6 and TNF-α levels [[45], [46], [47], [48]]. Inflammatory pathways represent a critical mechanism underlying the accelerated CHIP-related CVD risk. Previous studies have shown that CHIP carriers with disrupted IL-6 receptor signaling experience a significant reduction in cardiovascular disease incidence [49,50]. Another experimental study using wild-type (WT) mice found that TNF played an important role in driving TET2-mutant CHIP in vivo [51]. Our study provides further evidence supporting TNF inflammation as the potential mechanism from the human epidemiological perspective. Therefore, adopting a healthy lifestyle to counteract inflammation might be the priority to reduce the cardiovascular burden of CHIP.

Based on a well-established cohort with extended follow-up periods, we comprehensively explored the interplay between healthy lifestyle, CHIP, and CAD incidence for the first time. Additionally, we employed high-depth sequencing to enable the evaluation of lifestyle effects on small clone carriers in an early stage. Several limitations warrant mentioning. First, due to limited sample sizes for change analysis, cohort studies with larger samples are warranted to validate our results. Second, we employed an observational study to examine the impact of lifestyle changes, which precludes causal inferences, highlighting the need for large, well-conducted randomized controlled trials to confirm these findings. Third, the current study population is Chinese, and the applicability of our results to other ethnic groups requires further validation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this large-scale prospective cohort study suggests that CHIP may increase the risk of CAD, and that the excess risk related to CHIP could be attenuated by lifestyle. Our findings might provide new insights into the modulation of CHIP-related CAD risk, in which behavioral lifestyle changes should be encouraged for CHIP carriers.

He has led > 10 projects funded by Ministry of Science and Technology of China, National Natural Science Foundation of China, and has published > 100 articles in high impact journals, including Nature Genetics, European Heart Journal, JAMA Cardiology.
