Estimating water levels caused by a tropical storm along the Bangladesh coast: A numerical approach
Authors: Gour Chandra Paul, Irfan Raju, Md Zakir Hossain, Tauhida, Md Nuruzzaman, Mst Rupale Khatun, Md Emran Ali
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
In this study, water levels resulting from the dynamic interaction of tide and surge are estimated by solving a 2-D vertically integrated shallow water equations numerically. To solve the equations on the specific 2-D grid, the explicit Leapfrog scheme is implemented, adopting a staggered Arakawa C-grid. The domain's complex land-sea interface is approximated through the stair-step method in order to employ the finite difference technique. To incorporate the complexity of the domain with a considerably high accuracy and to reduce computational cost, one-way nested grid models are embraced. The Meghna River freshwater discharge is incorporated into the innermost child model. A stable tidal regime over the region of interest is generated by applying the four vital tidal constituents, namely M2 (principal lunar semidiurnal), S2 (principal solar semidiurnal), O1 (principal lunar diurnal) and K1 (luni-solar diurnal) in the southern open boundary of the outermost model. This previously effectuated tidal regime is used as the initial state of the sea in getting total water levels due to the dynamic interaction of tide and surge. Numerical experiments are performed with the storm AILA that hit the coast of Bangladesh on May 25, 2009. The simulated results are found to closely match observed and reported data.

Introduction

Bangladesh, with its vulnerable geography, is highly prone to various natural disasters, including tropical cyclones (TCs) and their associated surges, floods, droughts, earthquakes, river bank and coastal erosions, and salinity intrusions. These hazards predominantly affect the 710 km long coastal region of the country. The coastal area experiences an average of five to six cyclonic storms (CSs) annually, accounting for approximately 80% of global casualties [1]. Moreover, the Bay of Bengal (BOB) witnesses an average of five storm surges with heights exceeding 5 m every decade, resulting in a devastating loss of life and property [2,3]. The occurrence of devastating losses can result from different surge impulsive factors of the region, such as shallow water bathymetry, an extended continental shelf, the existence of several types of low-lying offshore islands, a complex land-sea interface, a higher tidal range (the range can be close to 6 m near Sandwip) and the world's third largest freshwater discharge of the combined flow of the Ganges-Brahmaputra-Meghna [1,3,4]. Recognizing the severe impact of TCs and TC-induced storm surges on the coastal population, it is imperative to develop an improved TC forecasting model to alleviate the suffering of the affected communities. This motivation leads us to select the region of interest for our study.

TCs and their associated storm surges have both long- and short-term effects on the lives of coastal inhabitants. The long-term effects include salinity intrusion, malnutrition, water-borne disease outbreaks, and other related issues. On the other hand, the short-term effects encompass rapid-onset events such as coastal flash floods, uprooting trees, and destruction of houses, roads, crops, etc. As it is not feasible to prevent the occurrence of these disasters, mitigation becomes the primary approach for safeguarding lives and properties from their consequences. It is worth noting that a significant amount of work has been carried out to develop an effective warning system for the region of interest [5,6]. Noteworthy studies due to Das [7], Johns and Ali [8], Murty et al. [9], Roy [10], Rao et al. [11], Paul and Ismail [12], Paul et al. [13], Paul and Ali [4], and Ali and Paul [3] have contributed to this field. The pioneering work in the storm surge simulation along the BOB region due to Das [7] was carried out by the finite difference method (FDM) approximating the complex coastal/island boundaries through the stair-step approach with a lower grid resolution (37×17) to foresee and analyze tidal surges obtained by linear superposition of tides and surges. However, we are well aware of the fact that tides are a continuous process in the sea that constantly interacts with surges nonlinearly [14]. The dynamical tide-surge interaction (TSI) as well as the high grid resolution was not taken into consideration in that investigation due to computing limitations. Therefore, improvements can be made to enhance the accuracy and comprehensiveness on that of Das [7]. Recognizing the need for improvements, subsequent studies by Johns and Ali [8], Roy [10], Paul and Ismail [5,6,12], and Paul et al. [13,14] have built upon the findings presented by Das [7]. These studies have primarily employed a semi-implicit FDM approach to discretize the model equations, benefiting from numerical stability. However, it should be mentioned here that an explicit FDM is relatively straightforward to comprehend and implement in programming and computational tasks. Additionally, an explicit FDM incurs lower computational costs compared to FDM utilizing implicit or semi-implicit schemes [15]. Taking this fact into consideration, the study conducted by Paul et al. [15] intends to use a fully explicit FDM employing the forward-in-time and centered-in-space (FTCS) scheme. However, it is important to note that these approaches are unconditionally unstable for hyperbolic problems. Since the shallow water equations (SWEs) are a set of hyperbolic partial differential equations (PDEs), this method may yield inefficient results. This explicit FTCS FDM exhibits first-order accuracy in time and second-order accuracy in space [15]. However, a major drawback of FTCS is that it requires a very high computational cost for high-grid resolutions, due to the need to ensure stability and convergence of the finite difference schemes. In the present study, our aim is to employ a technique that achieves higher-order accuracy while benefiting from numerical stability for solving the storm surge problem. To accomplish this, we have investigated the storm surge problem with the aid of the Leapfrog FDM, which takes central difference both in time and space. This approach has proven to be a valuable tool, offering good stability and second-order accuracy. Its second-order accuracy enables highly precise solutions, surpassing the accuracy of lower-order methods. By carefully selecting appropriate time step sizes and grid (2-D) spacings, adhering to the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) condition, we ensure the stability of the method throughout the simulation. This stability guarantees that the computed solution remains physically meaningful and avoids divergence over time. The simplicity and ease of understanding of the Leapfrog FDM make it an attractive choice for our study. It allows us to obtain accurate results while maintaining good stability throughout the simulation. Its higher accuracy, stemming from smaller truncation error, distinguishes it from the forward difference method. Additionally, this technique exhibits faster convergence compared to other FDMs, such as forward and backward differencing. To the best of our knowledge, the application of the Leapfrog FDM for storm surge simulation in the coast of Bangladesh (COB) has not been extensively studied. Therefore, this study brings novelty by introducing this new methodology to advance storm surge modeling in this specific area. The simplicity and ease of implementation of the Leapfrog FDM make it a practical choice for simulating storm surges, offering improved stability, accuracy, and computational efficiency compared to previous methods, thereby providing valuable insights for mitigating the impact of natural disasters on coastal populations.

The remaining part of the paper is arranged as follows: Section 2 presents the problem statements, including the model equations, wind field generation, and boundary conditions (BCs). Section 3 deals with the numerical treatments in detail. A brief discussion of input terms and the time-varying history of AILA are presented in section 4. Model experiments with AILA are illustrated in section 5. In section 6, the obtained results and model validation are discussed, and the paper concludes with the conclusions in section 7.

Numerical treatments

Nested schemes

Nested schemes are employed in coastal modeling to accurately simulate phenomena such as storm surges, particularly in areas with complex land-sea interfaces. These schemes utilize finer grids with higher resolutions near the coast to capture intricate land-sea interfaces and accurately predict localized storm surges. The higher resolution allows for a detailed representation of coastal features, including bathymetry and coastlines, resulting in more precise storm surge height predictions. Additionally, the finer grids enable the capture of local effects such as tidal amplification and channel dynamics, which have a significant impact on storm surge behavior. By focusing computational efforts on critical regions and employing coarser grids in less important areas, nested schemes optimize computational efficiency without compromising accuracy, making the simulations more realistic. The COB comprises several small and big offshore islands with complex land-sea interfaces (see Fig. 1). However, the most hazardous area is the Meghna estuary, which is emerged as the world's most vulnerable region due to its coastal complexities [12]. For accurate numerical prediction of storm surges associated with TCs, it is essential to consider the coastal complexities [12,18], which highly depend, among other factors, on grid resolution [12]. As in Paul and Ismail [12], the grid resolution can considerably be improved nearer the coast, but it is unnecessary away from the coast. It is worth mentioning that using a smaller mesh size throughout the entire computational domain would result in a significant increase in the number of grids, leading to a higher computational cost. This is because a higher grid resolution requires a smaller time step to ensure the CFL stability condition. It is important to note here that the CFL condition is related to the time step Δt with mesh size Δx or Δy, ocean depth h, and gravitational acceleration g through Δt≤Δx2gh, or Δt≤Δy2gh. To solve the problem, Paul and Ismail [12] used a doubly nested one-way scheme. Following their approach, a considerably high-resolution numerical scheme (hereafter it is referred to as the fine mesh scheme (FMS)) is nested into a scheme of relatively low resolution, named coarse mesh scheme (CMS). Except for the Meghna estuarine area, the FMS is capable of significantly incorporating the coastal complexity of the entire region (see Fig. 2(a)-(c)). In order to incorporate the complexity of the Meghna estuarine area [19], a very fine mesh scheme (VFMS) is further nested for this area into the FMS. Fig. 2(a)–(c) provides a clear illustration of how these three models handle the complexities of the coastline, highlighting that the resolution adopted for VFMS can effectively incorporate the complexities of the Meghna estuary. However, it is worth noting that with the resolution adopted in VFMS, some smaller channels may not be identified. In such cases, increasing the grid resolution is a potential solution, although it may lead to higher computational costs. In our study, we follow the same procedure as described in Ref. [12] to couple the schemes, ensuring that they have the same extent as the schemes used in Ref. [5]. Detailed information about the schemes and the coupling process can be found in a series of papers by Paul and Ismail [5,6,12] and Paul et al. [13,19,20]. However, for the sake of subsequent numerical treatments and to gain a better understanding of the subject matter, we present them here in detail. The parent model domain corresponds to the CMS, covering latitudes between 15ο−23οN and longitudes 85ο−95οE. The mesh size (distance between two consecutive grid cells on the x− axis or y− axis) in the CMS along the north-south direction (along the x-axis) is Δx=15.08 km and along the east-west direction (along the y- axis) is Δy=17.52km with 60×61 grid points in the xy-plane. Again, the FMS covers the area between 21ο15′−23οN latitudes and 89ο−92οE longitudes. This scheme contains 92×95 grid points in the xy-plane, with a mesh size of Δx=2.15 km along the x-axis and Δy=3.29 km along the y-axis. Furthermore, the extent of the VFMS is 21°46′12″–23 °N, 90.4°–92 °E with mesh sizes of Δx=0.72 km and Δy=1.14 km along the x and y axes, respectively. (1), (2), (3), but with BCs that are different from each other. The CMS operates independently, while the FMS relies on the computations performed by the CMS. Similarly, the VFMS depends on the results obtained from the FMS. To establish coupling between the CMS and FMS, the values of the parameters, u and v calculated in the CMS, are provided along the OSBs of the FMS during each time step of the solution process. To ensure compatibility between the two schemes, we employ spatial interpolation on the data obtained from the CMS, utilizing cubic spline interpolation. This interpolation generates a complete set of boundary data, which effectively represents the required BCs for the FMS. These interpolated boundary data are then provided to specify the boundary of the FMS, which integrates them forward in time. The coupling between the FMS and VFMS follows a similar approach. The FMS serves a crucial role by offering the OSBs, acquired through the CMS-FMS coupling, as invaluable input for the VFMS. The freshwater discharge from the Meghna River has a significant impact on the hydrodynamics of the coastal region. This influence becomes particularly crucial during storm surge events. When a storm surge occurs, the elevated water levels (WLs) accompanying it can create a backwater effect, which obstructs the outflow of freshwater from the river. Consequently, WLs upstream of the river mouth can rise, and the flow dynamics undergo changes. Thus, the interaction between the river discharge and the storm surge plays a crucial role, with the potential to cause increased WLs upstream and modifications in the overall flow patterns.

Grid generation and domain discretization

When solving PDEs numerically with the FDM, a grid or mesh is used to represent the physical domain. This grid or mesh acts as a geometric structure, discretizing the physical domain into a collection of discrete points. Computational grids can be categorized as structured, unstructured, or hybrid, depending on the type of discretization employed. On structured grids, the FDM is very simple and effective. Keeping the idea in mind, in the current study, we have used the FDM on the staggered Arakawa C-grid in the xy- plane shown in Fig. 3. The model equations given by Eqs. (1), (2), (3) and the BCs specified by Eqs. (6), (7), (8) are discretized explicitly using the Leapfrog FDM. The used method employs central differences in both space and time. A short description of input terms

This study requires various types of data, including meteorological, hydrological, oceanographic, and astronomical data. Among these, the meteorological inputs are particularly important. The MSWS is significant as it directly influences the generation and transmission of momentum to the ocean surface, resulting in increased stress on the water surface. This, in turn, leads to the formation of larger wind-driven waves and elevated WLs. The radius of the maximum wind (RMW) is also crucial as it determines the spatial extent of the surge and the areas most impacted by the storm. The central pressure distribution reflects the storm's intensity and affects wind strength and surge response. However, due to the presence of powerful associated winds and the resulting high values of wind stress forcing, the impact of barometric changes on surge prediction models can be neglected [1]. Finally, the storm track guides the path of the surge and identifies areas prone to coastal inundation. These meteorological inputs are essential for driving the storm surge model and accurately representing the storm's behavior. It is important to note that the MSWS data, as well as other relevant meteorological data such as RMW, central pressure distribution, and storm track, were obtained from the Bangladesh Meteorological Department (BMD) through a purchase process. This ensures that the data is accurate and relevant to our study.

Fig. 4 depicts the storm track generated using ArcGIS software, utilizing data obtained from the BMD. Along the open boundary of the northeast corner of the VFMS, the Meghna River freshwater discharge is considered, which is 5100.00 m3s−1 [21]. Water depth data are taken from the study due to Paul et al. [19], whereas Fig. 5 shows the corresponding bathymetry map. It is assumed that the bathymetry remains unchanged over time throughout our study. To ensure stable tidal conditions, we incorporate the major harmonic components of the tides, namely M2, S2, O1, and K1, based on the information provided by Paul et al. [13]. In our simulation, each constituent corresponds to a specific tidal frequency and possesses distinct physical characteristics. The M2 constituent represents the principal lunar semi-diurnal tide, primarily influenced by the gravitational pull of the moon, with a frequency of approximately two cycles per day. The S2 constituent represents the principal solar semi-diurnal tide, influenced by the gravitational pull of the sun, also occurring at a frequency of approximately two cycles per day. The O1 constituent represents the principal lunar diurnal tide, influenced by the rotation of the earth and the gravitational pull of the moon, with a frequency of approximately one cycle per day. The K1 constituent represents the luni-solar diurnal tide influenced by the combined gravitational effects of the moon and the sun, also with a frequency of approximately one cycle per day. Through the incorporation of these constituents, we achieve a stable representation of the tidal behavior in the region of our choice, accurately capturing its influence on the system under study. Also, the value of Cf, assumed to be uniformly constant throughout the computation, is set to 2.6×10−3. Such a consideration is supported by several authors (see Refs. [1,13,22,23]). The initial values of ξ, u, and v are set to zero to establish a static equilibrium state. In storm surge modeling, the static equilibrium assumption implies that the WLs, as well as the horizontal velocities, are initially at rest or in a state of balance before the storm event occurs. With the passage of time, dynamic processes driven by atmospheric forcing, particularly wind stress assume a greater role in driving storm surge generation and propagation.Fig. 4Track of CS AILA, illustrating the color-coded representation of the storm's MSWSs classified according to the Saffir-Simpson scale. The time history of the cyclonic storm AILA

AILA, a Category 1 cyclone (with sustained wind speeds of 65–75 mph), formed over the BOB and made a fierce landfall in the south-western coastal region of Bangladesh and the eastern part of the West Bengal province in India on May 25, 2009, during the full moon [28]. Based on the reports from the Joint Typhoon Warning Center on the late evening of May 21, 2009, a tropical disturbance was detected around 950 km south of Kolkata, India. The disturbance had originated within the Southwest Monsoon and displayed early indications of growth. Initially, the disturbance manifested as a broad and disorganized region of intense thunderstorm activity. However, within the previous 12 h, a consolidated low-level circulation center had formed within the disturbance. It subsequently moved northward and intensified into a well-marked low over the southwest Bay and neighboring west-central Bay at 0300 Universal Time Coordinated (UTC) on May 23, with a wind speed of 25 kt. At 0600 UTC on May 23, the system reached the depression (D) stage and was centered near 16.5°N,88.0°E, approximately 600 km south of Sagar Island. Continuing its northerly movement, the depression intensified into a deep depression (DD) at 0300 UTC on May 24, when it was near 18.0°N,88.5°E. Later that day, at 0900 UTC, the system continued moving northward. After 2100 UTC, it further intensified into a DD and continued its trajectory in a north-northward direction. At 12 UTC on May 24, the system had intensified into a CS and was named AILA and was centered near 18.5°N,88.5°E, with maximum wind speed exceeding 33 kt. AILA continued its northerly movement and intensified into a severe cyclonic storm (SCS) at 0600 UTC on May 25. By then it was centered over the northwest BOB near 21.5°N,88.0°E, in proximity to Sagar Island. The system crossed West Bengal of India and Khulna of Bangladesh coast close to the east of Sagar Island between 0800 and 0900 UTC as a severe CS, with wind speeds of 55–60 kt. The lowest estimated central pressure was about 968 hPa at the time of landfall [29]. After landfall, the system continued its northerly movement, gradually weakening until it transitioned into a CS, and at 1500 UTC on 25 May, it was centered over Gangetic West Bengal, close to Kolkata. While it continued moving northward, it further weakened into a DD and at 0300 UTC on May 26, it was over Sub-Himalayan West Bengal, close to Malda. At 0600 UTC on May 26, it weakened into a depression, close to Bagdogra. It weakened further and became less marked by 27 May (Fig. 4). Model experimentation

Initially, the values of ξ, u, and v are assumed to be zero for representing a cold start and from that initial condition, a stable tidal regime is generated. The tide generation process in our study is similar to that of Paul et al. [13]. In order to generate a stable tidal regime within our designated area of interest, we have incorporated four major tidal constituents, namely M2, S2, O1, and K1 at the southern OSB of the outermost scheme. The constants related to the constituents are taken from the study of Paul et al. [13], which are also available in parts II, III, V, and IV, respectively, of Schwiderski [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. Then from the cold start in the nonappearance of the atmospheric pressure gradient force and wind stress, a stable tidal condition is achieved after four tidal cycles of integration. For attaining WLs due to the nonlinear TSI, the generated tidal condition provided the initial condition of the sea at the model time t=0.

Discussion of the obtained results and model validation

Results associated with the storm AILA are calculated at all the grid points representing the water mentioned above and the results attained at some grids representing coastal stations (see Fig. 1) are displayed. In Bangladesh, BIWTA (Bangladesh Inland Water Transport Authority) collects hourly WL data at some of the stations displayed in Fig. 1, but record shows that during a stormy period, WL data are rarely available at those stations [12]. As a result, there is a paucity of observed data and, therefore, the comparison of our model simulated results with observation is limited. The results are calculated for 59 h and are offered for the last 48 h from 2000 UTC 23 May to 2000 UTC May 25, 2009.

Interaction effect

The dynamic TSI plays a crucial role in storm surge modeling for the BOB region. This interaction effect, as observed in various studies [3,8,17,20], can vary from −1 m to 1 m with respect to the MSL, potentially affecting total WLs by up to 2 m. Following the notation used by Antony et al. [2], we represent the WLs caused by tide, surge, and TSI as ξT, ξS, ξT+S+I, respectively. Then the TSI effect can be defined as ξI = ξT+S+I − (ξT+ξS), superposition of tide and surge by ξT+S=ξT+ξS, and non-tidal residual (NTR) as ξS+I=ξT+S+I−ξT. The NTR, also known as practical surge level, represents the sea level variability with meteorological contributions in the absence of astronomical tide [3]. Fig. 10 (a)-(c) portrays our computed WLs due to tide (ξT), surge (ξS), dynamic TSI (ξT+S+I), superposition of tide and surge (ξT+S), interaction effect (ξI), and practical surge height (ξS+I) at Hiron Point, Char Changa, and Chittagong. It can be observed from Fig. 10 (a)–(c) that the interaction curve (ξI) exhibits both positive and negative values with respect to the MSL. The maximum variation in ξI occurs when the cyclone makes landfall (see Fig. 10 (a)–(c)). Furthermore, the interaction effect responds to tidal variations, showing its peak during low tide and the opposite characteristic during high tide (see Fig. 10 (a)–(c)). This finding is consistent with the results obtained in the studies by Gayathri et al. [30], Rahman et al. [31], and Antony et al. [2]. For the sake of brevity, such results for other stations are avoided. Similarly, the NTR is found to be higher than the surge level during low tide and lower during high tide. This finding is supported by the studies conducted by Antony et al. [2] and Rahman et al. [31]. Computational efficiency, uncertainties, and model limitations

In order to demonstrate the superiority of the method adopted in the study over FDM explicit [15] and semi-implicit methods [13], a comparative testing approach is typically employed. The performance and outcomes of the different methods are compared under specific conditions, enabling the evaluation of the advantages and improvements provided by the chosen method in comparison to the FDM explicit and semi-implicit methods. Notably, the Leapfrog method has been found to demonstrate favorable characteristics in terms of accuracy, as illustrated in Table 4.

In this study, the computational results were presented using a time step of 60 s. Additional simulations were conducted for different time steps, including 10 s, 20 s, 30 s, 90 s, and 120 s, and it was found that the results for different time steps were similar to those obtained with a time step of 60 s. However, both the FDM explicit and semi-implicit schemes failed to achieve stable results for all the specified time steps.

Uncertainties can be present in our simulated results, originating from various factors including the position of the cyclone, MSWS, and the RMW. Madsen and Jakobsen [32] and Lewis et al. [33] have identified these parameters as significant sources of uncertainty. To evaluate these uncertainties, each parameter was individually modified while keeping the other parameters constant, utilizing freely available data sources. The analysis revealed an uncertainty in WL magnitude of approximately 0.5 m, which aligns with the expected accuracy of WL forecasts (see Table 4). However, it is important to acknowledge that uncertainties associated with freely available data sources can be substantial, as emphasized by Lewis et al. [34].

A limitation of our study is the availability of comprehensive and authentic observed WL data, which hinders a thorough comparison between the simulated and observed results. The BIWTA collects WL data at coastal and island stations using staff and automatic gauges; however, these measurements are typically referenced to the local chart datum (CD). Information regarding the relationship between CD and the public works datum (PWD) is often limited, except for a few stations [31]. Additionally, adverse weather conditions during severe cyclonic periods frequently result in the unavailability of WL data. Consequently, the limited availability of authentic time series of observed data restricts our ability to conduct a comprehensive comparison of WLs between the simulated and observed results.

Conclusion

In this research, vertically integrated SWEs in Cartesian coordinates are solved using the Leapfrog scheme on an Arakawa C-grid and applied to simulate WLs due to the dynamical interaction of tide and surge associated with the tropical storm AILA, 2009 along the coastal areas of Bangladesh. To achieve better accuracy and numerical stability, we employed the Leapfrog FDM. The model simulated WLs due to tide and surge and their nonlinear interaction are found to be in good agreement with the results obtained in other studies conducted with different approaches. The simulated WLs resulting from the dynamic TSI at some stations where observed data are available are found to compare reasonably well with the observed data obtained from BIWTA. Based on the RMSE values resulting from the simulated results of this model, it can be concluded that the model is highly efficient and can serve as a valuable tool for foreseeing WLs associated with a storm along the COB. The model can incorporate the complexities with a good accuracy. In our investigation, the contributions of storm surge influencing factors like inverse barometer, Coriolis force, bathymetry effect etc. are not taken into account. Since the goal of this study is to assess the effectiveness of the utilized scheme in producing results, and as such, the effects of the factors mentioned above are not addressed in this paper. Nonetheless, a comprehensive discussion on those factors can be found in our earlier publications [12,13]. The accurate simulation of WLs during storm events using the model has significant practical implications. It improves our understanding of coastal vulnerability, enhances disaster preparedness and response, guides planning and infrastructure development, supports risk assessment and insurance processes, and aids climate change adaptation planning. By accurately capturing the dynamics of storm surge and its impact on WLs, the model contributes to effective coastal management, minimizes loss of life and property damage, informs infrastructure design, facilitates risk assessment and insurance decisions, and helps develop resilience measures for future climate challenges. These practical applications highlight the relevance and importance of the study's findings for coastal communities and decision-makers.
