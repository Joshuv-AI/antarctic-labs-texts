Team-based learning in thoracic surgery nursing education: a mixed-methods study of effectiveness and learning experiences
Authors: Danyu Xiang, Sufen Yu, Jieping Zhang, Tianying Lv, Yufang Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTeam-based learning (TBL) is increasingly used as an active learning strategy in nursing education. However, evidence regarding its application in continuing education for in-service nurses in high-acuity specialty settings remains limited. This study evaluated educational outcomes and explored nurses’ learning experiences within a TBL-based educational program in a thoracic surgery department.MethodsA convergent parallel mixed-methods design incorporating a two-group randomized educational intervention was used. Eighty-eight clinical nurses who participated in the departmental continuing nursing education program were included in the quantitative component. Participants were randomly assigned to a TBL-based teaching group (n = 44) or a lecture-based teaching group (n = 44). The TBL-based teaching format included pre-class preparation, readiness testing, team discussion, evidence-based case learning, and scenario-based skills application. Educational outcomes included theoretical knowledge, practical skills, critical thinking disposition measured using the Chinese version of the Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory (CTDI-CV), and teaching feedback. Between-group comparisons were conducted using appropriate non-parametric tests, and CTDI-CV changes were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models. For the qualitative component, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 15 nurses from the TBL group, and data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s seven-step method.ResultsNurses in the TBL group achieved higher theoretical knowledge scores than those in the lecture-based teaching group [median 93.00 (IQR 92.00–94.00) vs. 83.00 (IQR 81.75–88.00), P < 0.001, r = 0.74; Hodges-Lehmann difference 9.00 points, 95% CI 6.00 to 11.00]. Practical skills scores were also higher in the TBL group [median 95.00 (IQR 93.00–97.00) vs. 89.00 (IQR 85.75–91.25), P < 0.001, r = 0.68; Hodges-Lehmann difference 6.00 points, 95% CI 5.00 to 8.00]. A significant group-by-time interaction was observed for the CTDI-CV total score (F = 8.96, P = 0.003), indicating a greater increase over time in overall critical thinking disposition in the TBL group. Significant group-by-time interactions were also found for open-mindedness, analyticity, inquisitiveness, and cognitive maturity. Teaching feedback scores were higher in the TBL group across all six items (all P < 0.001), with large rank-based effect sizes (r = 0.65–0.91). Qualitative analysis generated four themes: transition from passive attendance to active participation, connecting clinical cases with evidence-based learning, team collaboration and shared learning, and challenges in adapting to TBL implementation.ConclusionsTBL may be a feasible and acceptable approach for continuing education among thoracic surgery nurses. It was associated with higher theoretical knowledge and practical skills scores, more positive learning experiences, and greater increases over time in overall and selected aspects of critical thinking disposition. Introduction

Continuing professional education is important for maintaining and updating nurses’ clinical competence, particularly in specialty areas where patient conditions are complex and rapidly evolving [1, 2]. In addition to technical skills, clinical nurses are increasingly expected to demonstrate critical thinking, effective communication, teamwork, evidence-based decision-making, and self-directed learning [2]. These competencies are particularly relevant to thoracic surgery nursing, where nurses are required to integrate perioperative knowledge, clinical observation, emergency response, and multidisciplinary collaboration in daily practice [1, 3].

Thoracic surgery involves complex perioperative management across open and minimally invasive procedures [4]. Patients undergoing general thoracic surgery or esophagectomy may experience postoperative complications that require timely assessment and intervention [5, 6]. Postoperative pain may further increase the complexity of perioperative care [7]. These situations require nurses to recognize changes in patient status, make appropriate clinical judgments, and coordinate care with the healthcare team [3]. Therefore, thoracic surgery nursing education should extend beyond the delivery of theoretical knowledge and technical skills to foster clinical reasoning, adaptability, and collaborative problem-solving abilities [1, 3].

Traditional lecture-based teaching remains widely used in continuing nursing education because it allows standardized delivery of knowledge within a limited time. However, this approach may not fully meet the learning needs of in-service nurses, who often have different levels of clinical experience, shift-work constraints, and varied experience with evidence-based learning [2]. Educational approaches that explicitly promote self-regulated learning may help learners take a more active role in assessment, reflection, and knowledge application [8]. In high-acuity specialty settings, nurses also need opportunities to connect theoretical knowledge with real clinical cases, discuss complex problems with peers, and apply learning to practice-oriented situations [1, 2].

Team-based learning (TBL) is a structured active learning approach that combines pre-class preparation, readiness assurance, team discussion, and application-oriented problem solving [9, 10]. By requiring learners to prepare before class, discuss problems in teams, and apply knowledge to clinical scenarios, TBL encourages active participation and shared responsibility for learning [9, 11]. Previous reviews have suggested that TBL may enhance knowledge acquisition and learner engagement in health professions and nursing education [12]. It may also support teamwork and collaborative learning [13, 14]. Other evidence indicates potential benefits for problem-solving, clinical reasoning, and critical thinking [15, 16].

Although TBL has been widely used in nursing education, its implementation requires attention to the clinical context, learners’ prior experience, workload, and available teaching resources [17, 18]. Thoracic surgery nursing provides an appropriate context for examining TBL because it involves complex perioperative care, frequent clinical decision-making, and close team collaboration [3, 19]. Understanding both educational outcomes and nurses’ learning experiences may help educators refine TBL-based training and adapt it to specialty nursing education [20, 21].

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate educational outcomes associated with a TBL-based continuing education program and explore nurses’ learning experiences in a thoracic surgery department using a mixed-methods design. The findings may inform the design and implementation of learner-centered continuing education programs for specialty nurses.

Theoretical framework

This study was informed by Malcolm Knowles’ adult learning theory. Adult learning theory emphasizes that adult learners bring prior professional experiences to the learning process, prefer self-directed and problem-oriented learning, and are more engaged when learning activities are relevant to their practical roles [22]. These assumptions are particularly applicable to in-service clinical nurses, whose learning is closely connected with clinical responsibilities, previous experiences, and the need to address real-world patient care challenges.

In this study, the TBL-based teaching format was designed to reflect these principles by encouraging nurses to draw on their previous thoracic surgery experience, complete pre-class preparation, participate actively in team discussions, and apply knowledge to clinical cases and scenario-based activities. Through this process, learners were expected to transition from passive reception of information toward active engagement, collaborative reasoning, and practice-oriented knowledge application.

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a convergent parallel mixed-methods design incorporating a two-group randomized educational intervention within a departmental continuing nursing education program. The quantitative component evaluated educational outcomes associated with two teaching formats (TBL-based teaching and lecture-based teaching), while the qualitative component explored nurses’ learning experiences and perceptions of the TBL program through semi-structured interviews. Quantitative educational assessment data and qualitative interview data were analyzed separately and integrated during interpretation to provide a contextualized understanding of the educational outcomes and implementation experiences of TBL in thoracic surgery nursing education.

Setting and participants

Quantitative participants

From October to December 2021, clinical nurses from the Department of Thoracic Surgery at a tertiary hospital in Zhejiang Province, China, participated in the departmental continuing nursing education program. Eligible participants were registered nurses working in the thoracic surgery department who attended the educational program during the study period. Nurses in internship or visiting training programs were excluded.

A total of 88 nurses participated in the program and were randomly assigned to either the TBL group or the lecture-based teaching group, with 44 nurses in each group. All participants were female and held bachelor’s degrees in nursing. Participants represented different professional nursing levels, reflecting varying levels of clinical experience and professional background. Baseline characteristics, including age and professional nursing level, were compared between the two teaching groups.

Qualitative participants

For the qualitative component, participants were purposively selected from the TBL group. This sampling strategy was used because the qualitative strand aimed to gain an in-depth understanding of nurses’ experiences with the TBL-based teaching format rather than to compare experiences between the two teaching groups. Maximum variation sampling was used to include nurses with different professional levels, years of nursing experience, years of thoracic surgery experience, and levels of participation in the TBL activities.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) completion of at least three TBL sessions; and (2) willingness and ability to describe personal learning experiences. Nurses in internship or visiting training programs were excluded. Recruitment continued until data saturation was achieved, when no additional themes emerged from subsequent interviews.

A total of 15 female nurses from the TBL group participated in the interviews. Their ages ranged from 20 to 44 years. Participants represented different professional levels, including N1 nurses (n = 7), N2 nurses (n = 3), and N3 nurses or nurse educators (n = 5). Their total nursing experience ranged from 1 to over 20 years, and their thoracic surgery experience ranged from 1 to more than 10 years. Exact age and experience data were grouped to protect anonymity. Detailed characteristics of the interview participants are provided in Supplementary File 2.

Educational program and teaching formats

Both teaching groups received the same teaching content, instructors, training duration, and core learning materials. The educational program followed the department’s standardized quarterly nursing education plan and consisted of six sessions. The content covered perioperative nursing care for thoracic surgery patients, emergency management, and evidence-based nursing practice. The main difference between the two groups was the teaching format.

Lecture-based teaching group

Nurses in the lecture-based teaching group received traditional lecture-based instruction combined with demonstration-based skills training. Teaching was delivered by the same teaching team. Instructors explained key theoretical content, demonstrated clinical skills, and answered questions after the teaching sessions.

TBL group

Nurses in the TBL group received the same educational content, teaching team, and training duration as the lecture-based teaching group; however, the course was delivered using a structured TBL format [9, 10]. The 44 nurses in the TBL group were divided into four learning teams, with 11 nurses in each team. Each team included nurses with different professional levels to promote peer learning and balanced participation among nurses with varying clinical experience. Each TBL session was facilitated by two nursing education mentors and two senior clinical nurses with teaching responsibilities, who organized the sessions, guided team discussions, facilitated case analysis, and provided summary feedback. Two designated team members prepared case-related materials and selected relevant Chinese and English evidence-based literature for the mini-lecture component.

The TBL process included three stages [23]. First, during pre-class preparation, learning materials were distributed three days before class through the institutional online learning platform. These materials included clinical case information, relevant diagnostic and treatment guidelines, perioperative nursing materials based on enhanced recovery after surgery principles [24], and videos related to emergency response. Nurses completed independent preparation before class.

Second, during discussion-based learning, participants completed an individual readiness test for approximately 10 min. The test focused on etiology, clinical manifestations, pathophysiology, treatment, and nursing care. This was followed by team-based testing and discussion. Each team discussed a clinical case-related question for approximately 10 min. Team representatives then presented their conclusions, and other members contributed additional perspectives. When differences in opinion arose, inter-team discussion and debate were encouraged based on pre-class preparation and literature evidence. Nursing education mentors and senior clinical nurses summarized and evaluated the discussions. In addition, two team members delivered brief mini-lectures based on selected Chinese and English evidence-based literature related to the clinical cases, such as guidelines for enhanced recovery after lung surgery [24].

Third, during skills application, participants practiced responses to emergency scenarios using role-play and scenario-based drills, such as emergency management of postoperative thoracic bleeding [4]. Team members learned through discussion and feedback, with activities emphasizing clinical reasoning and problem-solving. The overall process of the TBL-based teaching format is shown in Fig. 1.

Data collection and instruments

Quantitative data collection and instruments

Quantitative data included demographic characteristics (age and professional nursing level), theoretical knowledge scores, practical skills assessment scores, critical thinking disposition, and teaching feedback.

Theoretical knowledge and practical skills were assessed after completion of the educational program as part of the routine educational assessment. The theoretical knowledge test was administered online and developed according to the training objectives and teaching content.

Practical skills were evaluated after completion of the educational program using hospital-approved assessment checklists routinely used for thoracic surgery nursing competency evaluation. These checklists covered holistic nursing assessment, case reporting, identification of nursing problems, nursing interventions, health education, and specialty-related questioning. Scores ranged from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better practical performance. The practical skills assessment checklist is provided in Supplementary File 1.

Critical thinking disposition was measured before and after the educational program using the Chinese version of the Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory (CTDI-CV) [25]. The CTDI-CV consists of 70 items across seven dimensions and is rated on a 6-point Likert scale. The total score ranges from 70 to 420, with higher scores indicating stronger critical thinking disposition. A total score above 280 indicates a positive critical thinking disposition.

A self-developed teaching feedback questionnaire was used after the educational program to assess participants’ perceptions of the teaching format. The questionnaire was developed based on the objectives of the educational program and relevant nursing education literature. It included items related to perceived learning outcomes, learning motivation, information searching, clinical problem-solving, clinical reasoning, and teamwork. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The content validity of the questionnaire was reviewed by two experts in nursing education. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.83 in the current study.

Qualitative data collection

Qualitative data were collected through one-on-one semi-structured interviews with purposively selected nurses from the TBL group. The interview guide was developed based on the study aims, the TBL-based teaching format, relevant literature, and expert consultation with nursing education specialists. Two pilot interviews were conducted to refine the wording and sequence of questions. The interview guide and characteristics of interview participants are provided in Supplementary File 2.

The guiding questions included: (1) Could you describe your experience participating in the TBL sessions? (2) How did the TBL-based teaching format influence your learning or clinical thinking, if at all? (3) How did team discussion and case-based learning influence your participation? (4) What difficulties did you encounter during the learning process? (5) What suggestions do you have for improving the TBL-based educational program?

Before each interview, the interviewer explained the study purpose, the voluntary nature of participation, and confidentiality protections. Written informed consent was obtained before the interviews. Interviews were conducted in a quiet and private meeting room, audio-recorded with permission, and lasted approximately 30 to 50 min. Participants were identified using alphabetical codes. To reduce social desirability bias, participants were informed that their responses would not affect performance evaluation, professional appraisal, or future training opportunities.

Data analysis

Quantitative data analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25.0. Continuous variables were first assessed for normality. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed variables were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages.

Baseline characteristics between the TBL group and the lecture-based teaching group were compared using independent-samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables, and chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, as appropriate.

For outcomes analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test, the rank-based effect size was calculated as r = |Z|/√N, where Z was the standardized test statistic and N was the total sample size. Values of approximately 0.10, 0.30, and 0.50 were interpreted as small, moderate, and large effects, respectively. Hodges–Lehmann estimates and their 95% confidence intervals were reported for theoretical knowledge and practical skills scores. N

For CTDI-CV scores measured before and after the educational program, linear mixed-effects models were used to examine the fixed effects of time, group, and the group-by-time interaction. Participant ID was included as a random intercept to account for within-participant correlation. This approach allowed all available valid observations to be included despite incomplete repeated measurements.

Qualitative data analysis and rigor

Qualitative data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s seven-step phenomenological method [26]. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim within 24 h after each interview. Two researchers independently read the transcripts several times to become familiar with the data, extracted significant statements, formulated meanings, and generated initial codes. Similar codes were then grouped into meaning units and themes. Disagreements in coding or theme development were discussed within the research team until consensus was reached.

Several strategies were used to enhance the rigor of the qualitative findings. Maximum variation sampling was used to include nurses with different professional levels and clinical experience. Member checking was conducted by returning summarized findings to participants for confirmation. The research team maintained analytic notes to document coding decisions and theme development. In addition, both positive and challenging learning experiences were considered during analysis to reduce researcher bias. The potential influence of the researchers’ involvement in the educational setting was also considered when interpreting the interview data.

Mixed-methods integration

Quantitative and qualitative data were analyzed separately and integrated during the interpretation stage. Integration was conducted by comparing quantitative findings on educational outcomes with qualitative themes related to learning experiences, perceived benefits, and implementation challenges. A joint display was developed to present areas of convergence and complementarity between the two strands. This integration aimed to provide a contextualized understanding of educational outcomes associated with the TBL-based teaching format and nurses’ experiences during its implementation in thoracic surgery nursing education.

Ethical considerations

The departmental continuing nursing education program was conducted from October to December 2021 as part of routine educational management in the thoracic surgery department. The TBL sessions, lecture-based teaching, routine examinations, practical skills assessments, CTDI-CV assessments, and teaching feedback were all components of this continuing education program. The program did not involve patient data, alter routine clinical care, or expose participating nurses to additional clinical risks.

In 2022, the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine reviewed and approved the use of these educational data for systematic research analysis and scientific publication, as well as the qualitative interview component (Approval No. 2022 − 427). Written informed consent was obtained from participants before the qualitative interviews. Educational assessment data used in the quantitative analysis were anonymized before analysis. Participants were informed about the study purpose, procedures, voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality protections, and their right to withdraw without consequences for their employment, performance appraisal, or future training opportunities. Permission for audio recording was obtained before each interview. All data were analyzed in anonymized form and used only for research purposes.

Results

Quantitative results

Participant characteristics

A total of 88 clinical nurses participated in the quantitative component, including 44 nurses in the TBL group and 44 nurses in the lecture-based teaching group. All participants completed the educational program and were included in the analyses of post-program theoretical knowledge, practical skills, and teaching feedback. For the CTDI-CV analysis, all available valid pre- and post-program records were included in the linear mixed-effects models because the number of valid observations differed between time points.

The mean age was 30.90 ± 7.05 years in the TBL group and 31.02 ± 6.71 years in the lecture-based teaching group. The distribution of professional nursing levels was also similar between the groups. No statistically significant between-group differences were observed in age or professional nursing level at baseline.

Theoretical knowledge and practical skills

Because the theoretical knowledge and practical skills scores were not normally distributed, between-group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test.

Nurses in the TBL group achieved higher theoretical knowledge scores than those in the lecture-based teaching group [median 93.00 (IQR 92.00–94.00) vs. 83.00 (IQR 81.75-88.00); U = 133.00, Z = − 6.983, P < 0.001, r = 0.74]. The Hodges-Lehmann estimated between-group difference was 9.00 points (95% CI 6.00 to 11.00), indicating a large effect.

Practical skills scores were also higher in the TBL group than in the lecture-based teaching group [median 95.00 (IQR 93.00–97.00) vs. 89.00 (IQR 85.75–91.25); U = 205.00, Z = -6.381, P < 0.001, r = 0.68]. The Hodges-Lehmann estimated between-group difference was 6.00 points (95% CI 5.00 to 8.00), also indicating a large effect.

Significant time effects were observed for the CTDI-CV total score and all seven subdimensions, indicating that CTDI-CV scores generally increased after the educational program. For the CTDI-CV total score, the mean score increased from 234.10 ± 22.72 before the program to 319.55 ± 22.71 after the program in the TBL group and from 234.35 ± 29.61 to 290.00 ± 28.86 in the lecture-based teaching group. The group-by-time interaction for the total score was statistically significant (F = 8.96, P = 0.003), indicating a greater increase over time in overall critical thinking disposition in the TBL group.

These findings indicate that the TBL group showed greater increases over time in overall critical thinking disposition and selected dimensions than the lecture-based teaching group.

Qualitative results

A total of 15 nurses from the TBL group participated in the semi-structured interviews. The qualitative analysis generated four themes: (1) transition from passive attendance to active participation; (2) connecting clinical cases with evidence-based learning; (3) team collaboration and shared learning; and (4) challenges in adapting to TBL implementation.

Theme 1: Transition from passive attendance to active participation

Participants described TBL as a more active and engaging learning approach than traditional nursing rounds or lecture-based teaching. They reported that pre-class preparation, readiness testing, and group discussion encouraged them to take more responsibility for learning and to think more actively during the sessions.

One participant stated, “In the past, I mainly listened during nursing rounds and did not think much. With TBL, because there are tests before discussion, I have to think more by myself” (P8). Another nurse said, “This model changed me from passively listening to actively learning and searching for relevant information” (P12).

Theme 2: Connecting clinical cases with evidence-based learning

Participants felt that TBL helped them connect theoretical knowledge with real clinical situations. Starting from difficult cases allowed nurses to analyze disease progression, nursing problems, and clinical responses in a more concrete way. Literature sharing also broadened their understanding of updated evidence and clinical guidelines.

One participant explained, “It begins with a real case and then extends to the disease, nursing problems, and new clinical research, so we can learn more than what we usually see in clinical work” (P1). Another nurse noted, “Through Chinese and English literature sharing, we improved our ability to search literature and learned about frontier knowledge and developments” (P15).

Theme 3: Team collaboration and shared learning

Teamwork was frequently described as a central feature of TBL. Participants reported that team members divided tasks, discussed questions, integrated different viewpoints, and presented conclusions together. This process promoted peer learning and allowed nurses with different levels of clinical experience to learn from one another.

One participant stated, “TBL mainly tests teamwork. Each person first completes their own task, and then the team integrates everything together” (P4). Another nurse commented, “During discussion, one person gives an opinion and another gives a different opinion, which makes the atmosphere more active and stimulates thinking” (P11).

Theme 4: Challenges in adapting to TBL implementation

Although participants generally viewed TBL positively, they also identified several challenges. The most frequently mentioned difficulties were searching and reading English-language literature, designing clinically meaningful discussion questions, and managing the time required for preparation.

One participant said, “English literature is difficult to read, especially because I am not familiar with some medical English terms” (P6). Another nurse noted, “A good TBL session requires a lot of time and energy from the presenting team” (P13). Some participants also suggested that future TBL sessions should provide clearer guidance on literature searching and discussion question design.

Joint display of quantitative and qualitative findings

The qualitative findings helped explain the processes underlying the observed educational outcomes. Participants described that pre-class preparation, readiness testing, team discussion, literature searching, and scenario-based activities encouraged active engagement and connection between theoretical knowledge and clinical practice. These findings converged with the quantitative results showing higher theoretical knowledge and practical skills scores in the TBL group.

The qualitative data also complemented the CTDI-CV findings. Nurses described that case-based discussion, evidence-based literature sharing, and peer debate encouraged consideration of clinical problems from multiple perspectives. These experiences may help explain the greater increases over time observed in the TBL group for the CTDI-CV total score and selected subdimensions, including open-mindedness, analyticity, inquisitiveness, and cognitive maturity.

At the same time, qualitative findings highlighted factors that may influence implementation. Participants reported preparation burden, difficulty reading English-language literature, and challenges in designing high-quality discussion questions. These findings suggest that implementation of the TBL-based educational program requires structured guidance, facilitator support, and accessible learning resources.

Discussion

This mixed-methods study examined educational outcomes and learning experiences associated with a TBL-based continuing education program for thoracic surgery nurses. Compared with lecture-based teaching, the TBL group had higher theoretical knowledge and practical skills scores, more favorable teaching feedback, and greater increases in overall and selected dimensions of critical thinking disposition. The qualitative findings added context by showing how nurses experienced the program, including both its perceived benefits and the practical demands of participation. Overall, the findings support the use of TBL as a potentially useful approach in specialty nursing education, while also indicating that outcomes may reflect the combined influence of several active learning components rather than TBL alone [11, 13].

The differences in theoretical knowledge and practical skills may be related to the way the program linked preparation, discussion, and application. Nurses were expected to review materials before class, respond to readiness questions, work through clinical cases with peers, and apply their conclusions in scenario-based activities. This sequence gave participants repeated opportunities to retrieve knowledge, compare clinical reasoning, and use what they had learned in situations resembling practice. A systematic review of online TBL reported educational outcomes related to student learning and experience [27]. Practice-oriented applications have also been described in emergency surgery nursing education [28]. Studies of blended TBL and interactive TBL formats have reported favorable learning outcomes and clinical reasoning [29, 30]. Similar practice-oriented benefits have been observed in ICU teaching and simulation-based nursing education [31, 32]. In thoracic surgery nursing, where patient assessment and response decisions often need to be made quickly, this close connection between learning content and clinical situations may be particularly relevant.

The CTDI-CV results suggest that the program was associated with changes in selected aspects of critical thinking disposition rather than across all dimensions. Both groups improved over time, but the TBL group showed greater increases in the total score, open-mindedness, analyticity, inquisitiveness, and cognitive maturity. These dimensions are closely related to considering alternative views, examining clinical information, seeking further evidence, and making judgments under uncertainty. The combination of independent preparation, peer discussion, literature searching, and case analysis may have supported these changes, which is consistent with previous evidence on critical thinking and clinical reasoning in TBL [15, 30]. By contrast, truth-seeking, systematicity, and critical thinking self-confidence did not show significant differential change. These dispositions may require longer exposure, repeated opportunities for reflection, or more explicit instructional support than was available during the six-session program.

Teaching feedback and interview findings together suggest that the TBL format was acceptable to most participants. Nurses described moving from passive attendance toward more active involvement, and they valued the opportunity to exchange views with colleagues who had different levels of experience. These accounts are consistent with the higher ratings for learning motivation, information searching, problem-solving, clinical thinking, and teamwork. The findings also align with adult learning theory, which emphasizes professional relevance, self-direction, and the use of prior experience in learning [22]. In this setting, clinical cases and peer discussion allowed nurses to relate new information to situations they had encountered in thoracic surgery practice.

The qualitative data also identified limits that were not apparent from the outcome scores. Some participants found English-language literature difficult to interpret, while others struggled to formulate questions that were both clinically meaningful and sufficiently challenging. Preparation also required more time than conventional teaching, which may be difficult for nurses working rotating shifts. These concerns indicate that implementation depends on the surrounding educational support, not only on the teaching format. Guidance based on established evidence-based practice processes may help learners move more systematically from clinical questions to evidence acquisition and appraisal [33]. Practical tools such as the PubMed PICO search function may also support literature searching [34]. Question-design templates and skilled facilitation may further improve the quality of team discussion [23].

The mixed-methods design was useful because the two strands addressed different parts of the evaluation. Quantitative analyses identified differences in educational outcomes, whereas the interviews helped explain how participants engaged with the learning process and why some elements were difficult to sustain. The convergence between higher outcome scores and accounts of active, case-based learning strengthens the interpretation of the findings. At the same time, the reported workload and resource demands provide an important counterbalance to the generally positive quantitative results. This combination suggests that TBL may be feasible in continuing nursing education when the program is adapted to staff workload and supported by appropriate learning resources [27, 29].

For nursing education practice, the findings support a gradual and context-sensitive use of TBL rather than simple replacement of all lecture-based teaching. TBL may be most valuable for topics that require integration of assessment, evidence, communication, and clinical decision-making. Blended and dual-track teaching models also suggest that TBL can be combined with complementary methods rather than used as a stand-alone replacement for lectures [35, 36]. Short preparatory tasks, carefully selected cases, and protected discussion time may make participation more manageable for in-service nurses. Educators should also monitor participation within teams so that experienced and less-experienced nurses both contribute to the learning process.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single thoracic surgery department at one tertiary hospital, which may limit the transferability of the findings to other clinical settings or specialties. Second, although participants were randomly assigned to the two teaching formats, allocation concealment and blinding were not feasible because the intervention was delivered within a real departmental education setting. Communication between participants and contextual factors such as facilitator involvement, peer interaction, and differences in learning engagement may therefore have influenced the observed outcomes. Third, the teaching feedback questionnaire was self-developed and primarily reflected participants’ perceived learning experiences. Although content validity was reviewed by nursing education experts and internal consistency was acceptable in this study, further validation of this questionnaire is needed. Fourth, the CTDI-CV dataset contained incomplete repeated measurements, with fewer valid records at some time points. Although linear mixed-effects models were used to include available cleaned data and account for within-participant correlation, findings related to changes in critical thinking disposition should be interpreted cautiously and confirmed in future studies with more complete longitudinal data. Finally, qualitative interviews were conducted only among nurses in the TBL group; therefore, the qualitative findings mainly reflected experiences of participating in TBL rather than direct comparisons of experiences between different teaching formats.

Conclusion

This mixed-methods study suggests that TBL may be a feasible and acceptable approach for continuing education among thoracic surgery nurses. Compared with lecture-based teaching, TBL was associated with higher theoretical knowledge and practical skills scores, more positive teaching feedback, and greater increases over time in overall critical thinking disposition. Qualitative findings indicated that TBL was perceived to support active preparation, case-based reasoning, evidence-based learning, and team collaboration, while also requiring more preparation time and support for literature searching and question design. Future studies with larger samples, more complete longitudinal data, and multi-center designs are needed to further examine the applicability and feasibility of TBL in specialty nursing education.
