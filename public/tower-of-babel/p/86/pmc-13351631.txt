Exploring oxidative and antioxidant biomarkers in temporomandibular joint disorders: An exploratory systematic review across multiple biological fluids
Authors: Yukta Saxena, Piyush G Limdiwala, JS Shah, SJ Parikh, Mruga Joshi, Purv S Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo determine if oxidative stress markers are consistently altered in TMD patients compared to healthy controls, by synthesizing evidence from blood, saliva, and synovial fluid analyses and assessing study quality.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, Medline/PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar, Cochrane and Embase were systematically searched for English articles up to September 2025 using MeSH and free keywords. Human observational and clinical studies comparing biomarkers in TMD patients and healthy controls in blood, saliva, and synovial fluid were included. Risk of bias was assessed by using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS)ResultsFourteen studies were included in the final analysis. Methodological quality was moderate to high (average NOS score 7.07 ± 1.07), with low risk-of-bias studies. Serum-based analyses most consistently showed higher oxidant markers (e.g., MDA) and reduced antioxidants (e.g., SOD) in TMD patients. Saliva results were the most varied.ConclusionThis ‘compensatory-to-depletion’ transition may represent a key biochemical indicator for disease staging. Evidence consistently links TMDs with oxidative stress imbalance across sample types, characterized by elevated oxidants (e.g., MDA) correlating with pain severity. Introduction

Temporomandibular disorders (TMDs) encompass a group of musculoskeletal and neuromuscular conditions involving the Temporomandibular Joint (TMJ), the masticatory muscles, and related craniofacial structures. These conditions often present with chronic orofacial pain, joint noises such as clicking or crepitus, limitations in mandibular range of motion, and functional impairment. TMDs are among the most frequently reported non-dental sources of pain in the facial region, with a global prevalence estimated to range between 5% and 12% [1]. Despite the high prevalence and clinical burden of TMDs, their precise etiology and underlying mechanisms remains a subject of on-going investigation. Contributing factors include anatomical abnormalities, occlusal discrepancies, trauma, bruxism, psychological stress, and central sensitization [2]. Recent research has shifted attention toward the molecular and biochemical mechanisms underlying chronic TMDs, with particular focus on inflammatory processes, neurogenic components, and oxidative stress pathways [3], [4].

Oxidative stress has emerged as a potential factor in the pathogenesis of chronic pain and degenerative joint conditions, including those affecting the TMJ. Oxidative stress is defined as an imbalance between the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the body's capacity to neutralize them through enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems. Under physiological conditions, ROS play essential roles in cell signalling and homeostasis. However, when produced in excess, these reactive species can damage proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and DNA ultimately compromising cellular integrity and tissue function [5].

In the context of TMDs, oxidative stress may act as a mediator of inflammation, cartilage degradation, muscle fatigue, and nociceptive sensitization. ROS can activate various pro-inflammatory transcription factors, such as NF-κB, and promote the synthesis and release of cytokines like interleukin−1β (IL−1β), tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and prostaglandins. These mediators contribute to local joint inflammation, muscle dysfunction, and peripheral sensitization, all of which are hallmarks of TMD-related pain. Moreover, oxidative damage to synovial tissues and cartilage may exacerbate degenerative changes within the joint, especially in individuals with chronic or arthrogenic forms of TMD [5], [6].

A variety of biochemical markers have been used in clinical and experimental studies to assess oxidative stress in TMD patients. These include markers of lipid peroxidation such as Malondialdehyde (MDA) and 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), enzymatic antioxidants like superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), and catalase, as well as non-enzymatic antioxidants such as glutathione (GSH), uric acid, and vitamin E. While some studies have consistently reported increased oxidative markers and reduced antioxidant activity in TMD patients, others have yielded inconclusive or conflicting results [3], [4], [7]

In recent years, there has been an increase in primary studies evaluating oxidative and antioxidant biomarkers in TMD patients. However, these studies often differ in design, populations studied, and biomarkers assessed leading to conflicting and sometimes inconclusive findings. Therefore, it remains uncertain whether oxidative stress is a cause or a consequence of Temporomandibular Disorders (TMDs), and there is no consensus currently exists on which oxidative markers are the most reliable for diagnosis, prognosis, or clinical application.

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Eslami et al. confirmed the association between oxidative stress and TMDs using serum and salivary markers. However, their analysis explicitly excluded studies on synovial fluid, which represents the most proximal biological sample to the site of joint pathology [8].

The primary objective of this systematic review was to address a crucial research question: Is oxidative stress significantly elevated in patients with Temporomandibular Disorders (TMDs) compared to healthy individuals, indicating a possible role in the pathogenesis of TMDs?

This systematic review aims to determine whether oxidative stress markers are consistently altered in patients with Temporomandibular Disorders (TMDs) across various biological samples such as serum (systemic), saliva (local/glandular), and synovial fluid (intra-articular) to provide a more thorough understanding of its systemic and local roles. It also seeks to identify which specific biomarkers such as Malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and glutathione (GSH) demonstrate the most reliable associations with TMDs which can be a potential diagnostic or prognostic indicator and to examine whether oxidative stress acts as a downstream consequence of chronic pain and inflammation or if it plays a more direct, causative role in TMD pathophysiology. Finally, the review will assess the methodological quality of existing studies, aiming to provide evidence-based recommendations for standardizing oxidative stress biomarker assessment in future research.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines, and the study was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251186118).

The following PICO question was addressed: whether oxidative stress is significantly altered in individuals diagnosed with Temporomandibular Disorders (TMDs) compared with healthy individuals without TMDs. These principles were applied across observational human studies as follows:

PopulationThe population comprised patients clinically diagnosed with Temporomandibular Disorders.InclusionThe exposure of interest was the presence of oxidative stress, assessed through biochemical biomarkers such as Malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione (GSH), catalase, and related oxidative and antioxidant markers measured in serum, saliva, and synovial fluid.ComparisonThe comparison group consisted of healthy individuals without clinical evidence of TMDs.OutcomesThe primary outcomes were differences in oxidative stress status between TMD patients and controls, including elevated oxidative damage markers and reduced antioxidant capacity, as well as comparative evaluation of serum, salivary, and synovial fluid samples to determine their relative utility in reflecting oxidative stress associated with TMD pathophysiology.

Eligibility criteria

This review included a broad range of clinical study designs across human participant. The following inclusion criteria were used for the various study designs: Eligible studies comprised randomized controlled clinical trials, prospective and retrospective controlled and uncontrolled clinical trials, cohort studies, cross-sectional studies, case–control studies, and case series with a minimum sample size of five participants. Studies published up to September 2025 were considered for inclusion. Only articles written in the English language were included in the review (Table 1).

While the search protocol was designed to include randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and cohort studies, the final inclusion consisted exclusively of observational case-control and cross-sectional studies, reflecting the current state of biomarker research in TMD.

Exclusion criteria

Animal studies were excluded from this review. Studies involving participants aged 18 years or younger were not eligible for inclusion. Articles of poor methodological quality, studies lacking an appropriate control or comparison group, and non–peer-reviewed reports were excluded. In addition, studies published in languages other than English were not considered.

The electronic databases Medline/PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar, Cochrane and Embase were searched. The search strategy included MeSH and free keywords for both Temporomandibular Disorders and oxidative stress, and the search strategy was adapted for each database (Table 2).

Study selection

The study selection is outlined in Fig. 1 (PRISMA study selection flowchart). Two reviewers (YS, PL) independently reviewed all papers in two steps; initially using titles and abstracts, and then studies selected from the first step were retrieved in full-text and screened for eligibility. If the titles and abstracts were unclear or did not include sufficient information about the methodology or the results, they were recruited for full text screening to further analyse the articles. In case of conflict, a third reviewer (MJ) resolved the disagreement.

The PRISMA flowchart of the selection process of the study.

Data extraction

Studies were arranged in a custom excel spread sheet for systematic data extraction and synthesis. Data from the selected articles were extracted using a standardised form which included Author & Year, Place, Study Design, Key Outcomes, Sample Size, Diagnostic Tools, and Biomarkers Evaluated. Excluded studies and the reasons for exclusion were also recorded.

Risk of bias assessment

Two independent reviewers (YS and PL) used the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) to measure risk of bias, which evaluates studies based on three domains: selection of study groups, comparability of groups, and ascertainment of exposure or outcome. In the case of a disagreement on scores between two reviewers, a final mutual decision was made. Articles were assessed regarding sample selection, sample size, study context, outcome measurement quality, statistical analysis, and confounding factors.

The risk of bias for the included studies was assessed using NOS Scale. Studies were awarded a maximum of nine stars according to the NOS criteria. The overall quality of each study was classified as low risk of bias when it achieved a score of 7–9 stars, moderate risk when it achieved 4–6 stars, and high risk when it achieved ≤ 3 stars.

Inter-reviewer agreement for study quality assessment was high, with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.766, indicating 'substantial' reliability in the risk of bias evaluation.

Data synthesis

Data regarding the country of the studies, type of TMD, characteristics of the control group, sample size, age of the participants, and the source of the samples were extracted from the studies. The outcome was oxidative markers which were extracted for TMD and controls. Studies investigating the difference between oxidative stress marker levels in TMD patients and the control group in serum, saliva and synovial fluid were analysed.

From the included studies, a brief description of the study design and sample is provided, following by identifying the oxidative stress markers measured in saliva, blood, and/or synovial fluid and their relation with Temporomandibular Disorder pain. Then the main findings and the associated conclusions are reported.

A narrative, systematic synthesis was planned a priori for this review. A quantitative meta-analysis was deemed inappropriate from the outset due to the anticipated high methodological and clinical heterogeneity including variations in biomarkers measured, assay techniques, and TMD diagnostic criteria.

In the studies provided, antioxidant capacity was measured using both Total Antioxidant Capacity (TAC) and Total Antioxidant Status (TAS) tests. While these terms are occasionally used interchangeably in scholarly work, they denote somewhat distinct methodological frameworks based on the assay technique utilized. TAC usually denotes the total ability of antioxidants in a biological sample to counteract reactive oxygen species, while TAS generally indicates the overall antioxidant level assessed through specific colorimetric or spectrophotometric tests. In this review, TAC and TAS were viewed as interconnected yet separate measures of overall antioxidant activity.

Results

Literature search

The process of study selection is outlined as PRISMA flowchart. (Fig. 1) The initial search across the databases yielded 159 records. After removing 25 duplicates, 134 records were screened for eligibility, after removing duplicate studies and irrelevant studies, 20 studies remained, two were meta-analysis, one was a narrative review, one was comprehensive review, one was mini review, and one was proposed hypothesis which were all excluded. Finally, fourteen studies fulfilled the criteria for the study. Studies which had inadequate methodological quality, significant risk of bias in aspects including improper sampling, absence of explicit diagnostic criteria, unreliable or non-standard laboratory assays, lack of statistical analysis, ambiguous data reporting, or unaccounted confounders were omitted to preserve the integrity of the evidence.

A sensitivity analysis excluding studies with very small control groups (n < 10) was performed to evaluate potential small-study effects. The exclusion of the study with a control group < 10 participants (Cai et al., 2006) did not alter the primary conclusion regarding elevated serum MDA levels in TMD patients in the quantitative synthesis, confirming the robustness of the findings across the included observational studies. Findings from salivary samples

Based on the eight studies evaluating salivary biomarkers, the results were highly heterogeneous. The findings for “Total Antioxidant Capacity (TAC) and Total Antioxidant Status (TAS), two related measures of overall antioxidant activity.” were in direct conflict. For example, studies found that TAC, TAS levels were significantly lower in TMD patients compared to controls [10], [11], [12]. In direct contrast, certain research reported that TAC was significantly higher in the TMD group [13], [14]. To complicate matters further, studies by Omidpanah et al. and Lawaf et al. found no significant difference in salivary TAC between patients and healthy individuals [15], [16].

Similar contradictions were evident in the measurement of oxidant markers. Investigations for MDA and for MDA and 8-OHdG reported that salivary oxidants were significantly higher in TMD patients [10], [15]. However, Total Oxidant Status (TOS) levels were lower in their TMD group [13]. Finally, several other analyses, for MDA and 8-OHdG [17] and for TOS [11], [12] concluded there was no significant difference in the levels of these oxidants when comparing TMD patients with controls. The studies suggest these discrepancies may be due to the specific TMD subtypes (e.g., myogenous vs. arthrogenous), the chronicity and intensity of the pain, and the different laboratory methods used for measurement.

Findings from synovial fluid samples

Only four studies investigated synovial fluid. Superoxide Dismutase (SOD) activity "progressively decreased as the stage of the disease increased" suggesting an "insufficient scavenging capacity" [18]. Supporting this trend of antioxidant depletion, the overall antioxidant capacity was "significantly decreased" in patients diagnosed with closed lock compared to those without [19]. However, in direct opposition, it was found that SOD activity was "significantly higher" in TMD patients than in healthy controls [20]. Yet, examining a different oxidative stress marker (F2-isoprostane), found "no significant differences" in its SF levels between painful TMD subjects and pain-free controls [21].

Evaluation of different fluids like saliva, serum, and synovial fluid for oxidative stress biomarkers in all of the included studies, with special emphasis on Glutathione (GSH), Superoxide dismutase (SOD), Malondialdehyde (MDA), and Total antioxidant capacity (TAC) was done. The results of studies which include saliva as diagnostic fluid showed significant variation. As compared to healthy controls, a number of studies found that patients with TMDs had significantly higher salivary MDA levels and lower levels of catalase and TAC. These findings suggest that the oral environment has reduced antioxidant defence systems and increased lipid peroxidation. In contrast, some research found compensatory increases in TAC in people with myogenous or chronic TMDs, which may indicate increased antioxidant activity in reaction to long-term oxidative stress.

The evidence was more consistent in serum-based analyses, where the majority of studies reported reduced antioxidant enzyme activity (SOD, GSH, and Catalase) and significantly higher serum concentrations of MDA, Total oxidant status (TOS), and Oxidative stress index (OSI) [5], [6], [7], [22]. This pattern leads to the idea that oxidative stress linked to TMD affects the systemic circulation in addition to local tissue involvement.

Synovial fluid evaluations offered the significant clear results of localized oxidative processes in the Temporomandibular Joint. Patients with internal disc derangement and advanced Wilkes stages had increased levels of lipid peroxidation products and lower total antioxidant capacity (TAC), suggesting an association between oxidative stress and articular cartilage degradation and the chronic inflammatory response [18].

All together, these findings suggest an association between oxidative stress imbalance and TMDs characterized by elevated oxidant markers (MDA, TOS, and 8-OHdG) and reduced antioxidant defences (TAC, SOD, GSH). However, the degree of oxidative alteration varied according to the biomarker analysed and the biological fluid examined.

A comprehensive quantitative meta-analysis was not feasible due to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity among the included studies. This heterogeneity arose from differences in biomarker measurement techniques (e.g., spectrophotometric, ELISA, and chemiluminescent assays), variability in TMD diagnostic criteria (Wilkes classification, RDC/TMD, and DC/TMD), differences in biological sample types and processing protocols, and heterogeneity in patient populations, including variations in age ranges (18–65 years) and TMD subtypes. In addition, several studies did not report the required variance measures necessary for quantitative synthesis. Although several studies evaluated Serum Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in TMD patients, quantitative pooling was not feasible due to methodological heterogeneity and inconsistent statistical reporting. Nevertheless, the majority of studies demonstrated higher serum MDA levels in TMD patients compared to controls.

Discussion

Mechanical stress and pressure on TMJ, masticatory muscles and degenerative disc disorders leads to free oxygen radicals release through different mechanisms, which cause oxidative stress [23]. Many studies have suggested that an imbalance between oxidant and antioxidant factors is associated with the pathogenesis of TMD and other diseases [8].

This systematic review demonstrates a significant association between Temporomandibular Disorders (TMDs) and oxidative stress imbalance in saliva, serum and synovial fluid. The principal finding across the included literature is that patients diagnosed with TMDs consistently exhibit altered levels of oxidative and antioxidant biomarkers when compared to healthy individuals [13], [15], [18]. This imbalance is typically marked by increased oxidative stress markers, including Malondialdehyde (MDA), lipid peroxides (LPO), and Total Oxidant Status (TOS), along with changes in antioxidant defences. These results strongly support the hypothesis that oxidative stress is a key biochemical factor in TMD pathophysiology [9], [14], [18].

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review to comprehensively synthesize and contrast the findings on oxidative stress in TMD across three distinct biological samples, saliva, serum and synovial fluid. This unique analysis reveals that while synovial fluid provides the most direct evidence of local joint pathology, serum offers a consistent reflection of systemic imbalance, and saliva, though promising and non-invasive, yields the most varied results. The apparent contradiction between Guven et al. (antioxidant depletion) and Cai et al. (antioxidant increase) may be explained by the Wilkes staging of the participants. While Cai et al. observed elevated SOD in earlier inflammatory stages likely a protective response to acute ROS. Guven et al. identified a failure of this system in advanced internal derangement, suggesting that synovial fluid markers are highly stage-dependent [10], [18], [20], [21].

A second novel contribution of this work is that it sorts and explains the confusing and sometimes contradictory results i.e. how “Compensatory-to-Depletion" response pattern could account for the inconsistent antioxidant results shown in several investigations as because the initial compensatory increase in antioxidants may occur in response to chronic pain or stress [13], [14], which is then followed by a depletion of these defences as the disease becomes more severe or functionally limiting in more advanced stages of TMD [18], [19]. However, because the included studies are cross-sectional, it is unable to demonstrate causative mechanisms or temporal correlations, hence this interpretation should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Furthermore, this review is the first to contextualize findings from emerging biomarkers; for example, it highlights the significant elevation of serum prolidase in patients with internal derangement, suggesting that altered collagen metabolism is a key component of oxidative stress in TMD pathophysiology [19].

This review also highlights the complex and dynamic antioxidant response in TMD patients. A significant amount of evidence indicates a reduction in antioxidant capacity, implying that the defence system is under strain. Studies consistently reported significantly lower levels of Total Antioxidant Status (TAS) or Capacity (TAC), superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione (GSH) in the serum, saliva, and synovial fluid of TMD patients. These significantly lower levels were often linked to disease severity; SOD activity progressively decreased with advancing Wilkes stages of internal derangement [18] and Ishimaru et al. noted significantly lower antioxidant capacity in patients with a “closed lock” diagnosis [19]. Conversely, and highlighting the conflicting nature of the existing literature, several studies reported an increase in antioxidant levels. The SOD activity was significantly higher in the synovial fluid of TMD patients alongside elevated oxidants [20]. Similarly, significant higher salivary TAC was noted in chronic TMD patients, particularly those with high pain intensity or psychological stress [13], [14]. This increase may represent a “compensatory mechanism”, an “adaptation to the disease” (hormesis) due to “chronicity”, or an “overgeneration” of enzymes in response to high oxidant levels. Additionally, some studies found no significant overall group differences in the studied markers, underscoring the heterogeneity in the field [17].

The type of biological sample, local or systemic greatly influenced the findings and their interpretation. Although only four studies [18], [19], [20], [21] in this review evaluated synovial fluid biomarkers, but offered the most direct evidence of oxidative processes at the pathological site, linking biomarkers explicitly to joint mechanics and severity. Synovial fluid is the biological fluid most closely linked to processes inside the joint, such as cartilage metabolism, synovial inflammation, and disc degeneration. As a result, finding oxidative stress markers in synovial fluid gives direct evidence of redox imbalance at the site of joint problems. For instance, lower levels of antioxidants like superoxide dismutase and total antioxidant capacity in synovial fluid were linked to more advanced internal derangement, which points to ongoing oxidative damage in the joint [18]. This supports the idea that reactive oxygen species play a role in cartilage breakdown, synovial inflammation, and structural changes in TMD.

Importantly, when interpreted alongside serum findings, these synovial fluid studies help clarify the potential local-systemic relationship in TMD pathogenesis. While synovial fluid reflects localized oxidative processes occurring within the joint [18], [19], serum biomarkers demonstrate that these local pathological changes may also be accompanied by systemic oxidative imbalance [9], [17]. This combined evidence suggests that oxidative stress in TMD may originate locally within the joint and subsequently contribute to broader systemic biochemical alterations associated with chronic inflammation and pain.

In contrast, serum studies have consistently revealed that the local disorder has a measurable systemic influence, demonstrating increased oxidants and lower antioxidants in the bloodstream. This indicates that serum-based markers may offer potential for diagnostic use. Saliva, as a non-invasive medium, yielded the most varied findings. While some studies confirmed a clear oxidative imbalance, others reported a compensatory increase in antioxidants or no significant difference at all. This variability was reported as significant changes in serum TAC but not in salivary TAC, suggests that while saliva is a convenient diagnostic fluid; its biomarker levels may be more prone to confounding variables than serum [16].

Temporomandibular disorders are a heterogeneous group of myogenous (muscular) and arthrogenous (joint-related) diseases; either in acute or chronic stages and at different levels of internal derangement. Diagnostic frameworks varied across studies included in this review, including the Research Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (RDC/TMD), the updated Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD) and Wilkes classification of internal derangement. Such diagnostic differences result in clinical heterogeneity that may at least partially account for variability in oxidative stress biomarkers between studies [10], [13], [18].

RDC/TMD or DC/TMD criteria were used in many studies that tended to have broader definitions of TMD, and often included both myogenous and arthrogenous conditions. In contrast, studies using the Wilkes classification mainly concentrated on structural joint pathology like disc displacement and internal derangement [18]. Thus, patterns of oxidative stress seen in synovial fluid studies may reflect later stages of intra-articular degeneration while serum and salivary studies represent systemic or muscle-interrelated inflammatory processes.

Furthermore, several studies did not differentiate between acute and chronic TMD, which could affect profiles of oxidative stress. Chronic TMD has been associated with prolonged inflammatory activity and compensatory antioxidant responses, but acute inflammatory stages may show different redox dynamics. These variations highlight how important it is to consider diagnostic criteria and clinical subtype when assessing oxidative stress findings in TMD research.

As a result, while evaluating the review's findings, this clinical diversity should be taken into account. Future studies should employ subtype-specific analysis and standardized diagnostic techniques like DC/TMD to further understand the relationship between oxidative stress and different TMD symptoms.

A significant relationship between oxidative stress indicators and clinical parameters, especially pain, was clearly demonstrated in the literature. Rodríguez de Sotillo et al. indicated that increased levels of both 8-OHdG and MDA were directly linked to higher pain intensity scores [10]. Furthermore, in the investigation by Kazan et al. although no significant group difference was reported, a strong positive correlation was noted between pain levels and serum concentrations of 8-OHdG and the inflammatory marker IL−6. In addition, psychosocial factors were implicated, with studies reporting associations between altered oxidative status and heightened levels of salivary cortisol or perceived psychological stress, thus reinforcing the biopsychosocial model of TMDs [17].

Increase in the MDA and 8-OHdG levels in serum and saliva were positively correlated with higher visual analogue scale (VAS) pain scores and longer disease duration [9], [15], [17]. Conversely, reduced TAC and SOD activity were associated with increased pain chronicity, suggesting exhaustion of antioxidant reserves in prolonged inflammatory states [12].

Additionally, synovial oxidative parameters showed a direct relationship with joint degeneration severity as classified by the Wilkes system [24]. These observations suggest that oxidative stress is not merely a secondary outcome of tissue damage but may actively mediate inflammation, nociception, and degenerative remodelling within the Temporomandibular Joint.

A post-hoc assessment revealed that 8/14 studies had sample sizes below 40 participants (20 per group), which may be underpowered to detect moderate effect sizes (Cohen's d = 0.5) with 80% power. This likely contributes to the variability in findings, particularly in salivary biomarker studies.

The main strength of this review is its systematic methodology in synthesizing a body of literature characterized by conflicting outcomes. Nevertheless, the review faces several limitations, with the most significant being the considerable heterogeneity present among the studies included. This variability in patient demographics, diagnostic criteria (such as Wilkes classification, RDC/TMD, DC/TMD), evaluated biomarkers, and laboratory techniques hindered the possibility of conducting a quantitative meta-analysis. Additionally, the small sample sizes in numerous included studies restrict the applicability of their results. Lastly, by limiting the search to articles published in English, there is a risk of introducing a language bias.

The results of this review carry significant implications for both clinical practice and research. The established link between systemic oxidative stress markers and TMD indicates that these markers may function as objective biomarkers, assisting in diagnosis and monitoring disease progression. Additionally, their relationship with pain suggests they could be valuable in evaluating the severity of symptoms. For future investigations, it is crucial that research efforts focus on standardizing methodologies. This should encompass the consistent application of validated diagnostic criteria, such as the RDC/TMD, the identification of a core set of the most dependable biomarkers (for instance, serum MDA and TAS show a high degree of consistency), and the implementation of longitudinal study designs. Such prospective research is vital to ultimately resolve the pivotal question of causality whether oxidative stress acts as a triggering factor in TMD or is merely a subsequent effect of chronic inflammation and pain.

Conclusion

This systematic review, based on the evidence synthesized, concludes that while the cross-sectional nature of the available literature and the significant heterogeneity in methodologies prevent a definitive determination of causality, oxidative stress appears to be associated with the clinical manifestations and subgrouping of TMD. This relationship is consistently found across studies that analyse blood, saliva, and synovial fluid. The imbalance is characterized by an increase in oxidative damage by-products, such as Malondialdehyde and total oxidant status, along with a complex alteration in antioxidant defences, which seem to be diminished in severe disease states but may show a compensatory increase in chronic conditions. Importantly, these biochemical changes are not merely laboratory results; they are significantly correlated with key clinical parameters, including pain intensity and functional limitations, highlighting their importance in the pathophysiology of TMDs.

While the cross-sectional nature of the available literature and the significant heterogeneity in methodologies prevent a definitive determination of causality, oxidative stress is clearly implicated in the TMD disease subgrouping process. There was also a lot of variability in the analysis, with different studies using different methods to measure oxidative stress markers, such as spectrophotometric, ELISA-based, or chemiluminescent assays. Differences in sample type, processing protocols, fasting status, and time of day also added to the differences between studies. This heterogeneity constrains the viability of meta-analysis and underscores the necessity for standardized diagnostic and laboratory protocols in forthcoming TMD oxidative stress research.

The findings suggest that biomarkers of oxidative stress could serve as future tools for diagnosis and monitoring disease activity. However, further research with standardized methodologies and longitudinal designs is essential to clarify the precise role of oxidative stress and to explore the therapeutic potential of antioxidant-based strategies in managing Temporomandibular Disorders.
