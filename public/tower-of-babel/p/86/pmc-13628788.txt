A multicenter machine learning model for predicting ICU mortality in mechanically ventilated patients: development and external validation
Authors: Yi Zhang, Xu Chen, Xinghan Tian, Hui Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate early prediction of mortality in mechanically ventilated intensive care unit (ICU) patients remains challenging due to disease heterogeneity and dynamic clinical trajectories. Traditional severity scoring systems have limited flexibility and generalizability. This study aimed to develop and externally validate a machine learning–based model using routinely available clinical variables to predict ICU mortality in mechanically ventilated patients.MethodsA retrospective multicenter cohort study was conducted using the MIMIC database as the development cohort, with external validation performed in the eICU Collaborative Research Database and an independent institutional cohort. Adult ICU patients receiving invasive mechanical ventilation were included. A two-step feature selection strategy combining least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and recursive feature elimination was applied to identify robust predictors. Six machine learning algorithms were compared, and a random forest model was selected. Model performance was evaluated using discrimination, calibration, decision curve analysis, time-dependent area under the curve (AUC), and concordance index (C-index). Model interpretability was assessed using SHAP analysis.ResultsA total of 21 predictors were selected for model development. The random forest model demonstrated superior overall performance compared with alternative algorithms. In external validation, the model showed favorable early discrimination, with near-perfect AUC values during the first 48 hours in the local cohort and consistently acceptable performance in the eICU cohort. Predictive performance attenuated over time, particularly in the local cohort, whereas temporal stability was observed in the multicenter dataset. Calibration and decision curve analyses supported the reliability and clinical utility of the model. Key predictors included age, markers of respiratory dysfunction, metabolic stress, organ failure, and treatment intensity.ConclusionsThe proposed random forest model provides accurate and interpretable admission-time risk stratification for ICU mortality in mechanically ventilated patients. We recommend its use as an admission triage and screening tool for identifying high-risk patients within the first 48 hours of ICU admission. While predictive performance declined over time, the model demonstrated robust early risk stratification and generalizability across independent cohorts, highlighting its potential value for early clinical decision support in critical care settings.Clinical trial numberNot applicable. This study was an observational retrospective cohort study and was not registered as a clinical trial.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-026-03590-2.

Introduction

Mechanical ventilation (MV) is a cornerstone of life-support therapy for critically ill patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) [1, 2]. Despite advances in ventilatory strategies and critical care management, mechanically ventilated patients continue to experience high short-term and long-term mortality [1, 3–6], reflecting the complexity and heterogeneity of underlying disease severity, organ dysfunction, and treatment response. Accurate early risk stratification after ICU admission remains a major clinical challenge but is essential for guiding individualized treatment strategies, resource allocation, and shared decision-making [4, 7].

Traditional prognostic assessment in mechanically ventilated ICU patients has largely relied on severity-of-illness scoring systems [6, 8], such as the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), Acute Physiology Score III (APS III), Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), and other composite indices. Although these scores are widely used, they have several limitations. First, they are often designed for population-level risk adjustment rather than individualized prediction. Second, they require manual calculation and incorporate predefined linear assumptions that may fail to capture complex, nonlinear interactions among clinical variables. Third, their predictive performance may degrade when applied across different institutions, patient populations, or time horizons [9–13].

In recent years, machine learning (ML) techniques have emerged as powerful tools for clinical risk prediction by leveraging high-dimensional data and modeling nonlinear relationships without strong parametric assumptions [14–16]. Several studies have explored ML-based prediction models in ICU populations; however, most have focused on single-center cohorts, short-term outcomes, or relied heavily on composite scoring systems as predictors. Moreover, external validation across independent databases—an essential step for assessing model generalizability—has often been insufficient or absent [8, 17–19].

Therefore, the objective of this study was to develop and externally validate robust machine learning models for predicting short-term (7-day), medium-term (14-day), and long-term (28-day) ICU mortality among mechanically ventilated patients. Using a retrospective multicenter design, we integrated data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC), the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD), and an independent institutional cohort. Importantly, to enhance clinical interpretability and generalizability, we deliberately excluded traditional severity scoring systems and constructed models based solely on routinely available demographic characteristics, comorbidities, vital signs, laboratory results, ventilatory parameters, and treatment-related variables. We further performed comprehensive internal validation, external validation, calibration analysis, decision curve analysis, time-dependent discrimination assessment, and explainability analysis using SHAP values.

Methods

Study design and data sources

This retrospective multicenter cohort study was conducted using three independent critical care databases:

MIMIC-IV Database (development cohort, version 3.1): A large, publicly available database containing de-identified health-related data from ICU admissions at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, the data coverage years (2008–2022). [20, 21].

eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD) (external validation cohort): A multicenter database comprising ICU admissions from multiple hospitals across the United States [22].

Local ICU Database (Yantai Yuhuangding Hospital, external validation cohort): A real-world cohort derived from a tertiary-care hospital.

The study protocol complied with the ethical standards of the respective data providers. All data were fully de-identified, and informed consent was waived in accordance with institutional and database governance requirements.

Study population

Adult patients (≥18 years) admitted to the ICU who received invasive mechanical ventilation were eligible for inclusion. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: Missing outcome data for ICU mortality at predefined time points; ICU length of stay ≤ 1 days; Key clinical variables unavailable for model construction; For patients with multiple ICU admissions, only the first ICU stay was included to avoid within-subject correlation (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Flow diagram of patient selection and cohort construction

Flow diagram of patient selection and cohort construction

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were ICU mortality at three predefined time horizons: Short-term mortality: Death within 3 or 7 days after ICU admission, Medium-term mortality: Death within 14 days after ICU admission, Long-term mortality: Death within 28 days after ICU admission. Additionally, ICU length of stay was used as the time-to-event variable for survival-based analyses.

Candidate predictors

Candidate predictors were selected based on clinical relevance, prior literature, and availability across all datasets. To improve model generalizability and avoid circularity, all composite severity scoring systems (e.g., SOFA, APACHE, APS III, SAPS II, OASIS, SIRS, Charlson Comorbidity Index, and Glasgow Coma Scale) were explicitly excluded.

Predictors were grouped into the following categories: Demographics: Age, sex, body mass index, weight, height; Comorbidities: Hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease, hepatitis; Vital signs: Heart rate(HR), mean arterial pressure(MAP), respiratory rate(RR), oxygen saturation(SpO2), body temperature; Laboratory parameters: Hematologic indices, electrolytes, renal and liver function tests, acid–base parameters, lactate; Ventilatory parameters: Positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2), ROX index (calculated as the ratio of peripheral oxygen saturation to the fraction of inspired oxygen, divided by respiratory rate); Therapeutic interventions: Vasopressor and inotrope use, continuous renal replacement therapy, fluid input/output and balance

Data cleaning and missing data handling

A structured, multi-step data preprocessing pipeline was applied uniformly across datasets. outcome completeness: Records with missing mortality outcomes were excluded. Missing data assessment: Variable-wise missingness was quantified. Variable exclusion: Demographic or comorbidity variables with ≥5% missingness were excluded. Laboratory and physiologic variables with >20% missingness were excluded. Imputation: Remaining missing values in continuous clinical variables were imputed using multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE) with predictive mean matching. Variable transformation: Categorical variables were encoded as factors, and continuous variables were standardized when required for model training [23–27]. The percentage of missingness for all final predictors in the development cohort is reported in Table S1.

Feature selection

After splitting the MIMIC development cohort into training (70%) and internal testing (30%) sets, feature selection was performed exclusively on the training set. To reduce dimensionality and prevent overfitting, a two-stage feature selection strategy was employed in the development cohort:

Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) Cox regression, optimized using 10-fold cross-validation, to identify predictors associated with time-to-event outcomes.

Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) based on random forest classifiers to rank predictors by importance in mortality classification.

Features selected by both methods were considered robust predictors and carried forward into model development [28–31].

Machine learning model development

Using the selected predictors, multiple machine learning models were trained to predict ICU mortality, including: Logistic regression(LR), Random forest(RF), Support vector machine (radial kernel, SVM), Extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), Naïve Bayes(NB), Elastic net regression [32–37].

The development cohort was randomly split into training (70%) and internal testing (30%) sets. Model training was conducted using repeated 10-fold cross-validation with area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) as the primary optimization metric [38–40].

Model evaluation and validation

Model performance was assessed using. Discrimination: AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1 score; Calibration: Calibration curves with bootstrap-based 95% confidence intervals; Clinical utility: Decision curve analysis (DCA); Time-dependent performance: Time-dependent AUC and C-index; Explainability: SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis. The final model was externally validated in the eICU and local hospital cohorts using identical preprocessing, scaling, and evaluation procedures [8, 16, 17, 41–47]. The optimal probability threshold for binary classification was determined in the internal test set using the Youden index. This threshold was then fixed and applied to all external validation cohorts without re-optimization, to ensure unbiased assessment of generalizability.

Development of the bedside nomogram

To enable rapid, point-of-care risk stratification, a nomogram was constructed based on the previously established random forest prediction model. The variables which identified by both LASSO and recursive feature elimination were retained, and fluid input/output were rescaled to litres (÷1000) for visual clarity. The nomogram was generated to estimate the probability of 3-day ICU mortality.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1). Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant where applicable.

Results

Baseline characteristics

After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 14,503 mechanically ventilated ICU patients from the MIMIC database were included as the development cohort, among whom 4,132 (28.5%) died within 28 days in the ICU and were classified as the non-survivor group (ICU mortality), while 10,371 (71.5%) survived and were classified as the survivor group. For external validation, 14,606 patients from the eICU Collaborative Research Database and 11,693 patients from the local hospital cohort were included. In the eICU cohort, 3,794 (26.0%) patients died in the ICU, whereas 3,831 (32.8%) patients in the local cohort experienced ICU mortality within 28 days.

Laboratory findings demonstrated a consistent pattern of increased systemic inflammation, metabolic stress, and multi-organ dysfunction among non-survivors. Specifically, non-survivors showed higher levels of white blood cell count, lactate, glucose, prothrombin time, bilirubin, transaminases (ALT and AST), blood urea nitrogen, and creatinine, along with lower hemoglobin, platelet count, and albumin. Acid–base disturbances were also more pronounced in non-survivors, reflected by lower pH and oxygenation indices in most cohorts.

In terms of fluid balance and organ support, non-survivors generally exhibited lower urine output and a more positive fluid balance, particularly in the MIMIC and eICU cohorts. Indicators of disease severity, including SOFA score in MIMIC and APACHE score (Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation) in eICU, were substantially higher among non-survivors, although these scores were not used for model development. Moreover, non-survivors more frequently received vasopressors and renal replacement therapy in the public databases, reflecting greater circulatory and renal dysfunction. While minor differences in comorbidity prevalence and treatment patterns were observed across datasets, the overall baseline profiles were remarkably consistent, supporting the robustness and generalizability of subsequent model development and validation. Taken together, despite differences in case-mix and practice patterns, all three cohorts demonstrated highly concordant baseline characteristics, with ICU mortality being strongly associated with advanced age, deranged vital signs, elevated lactate, impaired renal and hepatic function, and greater physiological instability at ICU admission.

Feature selection

To reduce dimensionality and enhance model robustness, a two-step feature selection strategy was applied in the development cohort, combining LASSO regression and recursive feature elimination (RFE).

Using LASSO Cox regression, coefficient paths demonstrated progressive shrinkage of predictors with increasing penalization, resulting in a parsimonious subset of variables with non-zero coefficients at the optimal penalty parameter (Fig. 2A). Ten-fold cross-validation identified the optimal value of λ based on the minimum partial likelihood deviance and the one–standard-error rule (Fig. 2B), yielding a stable set of candidate predictors while minimizing overfitting. In parallel, RFE was performed using repeated cross-validation to iteratively rank predictors according to their contribution to model performance. The RFE procedure demonstrated that model accuracy and Cohen’s kappa increased with the number of features and plateaued at approximately 30 variables, indicating diminishing returns beyond this point (Fig. 2C). The top-ranked features identified by RFE predominantly reflected age, respiratory status, renal function, metabolic derangement, fluid balance, and vasopressor use (Fig. 2D). To enhance robustness and reduce method-specific bias, features selected by both LASSO and RFE were retained for subsequent model development. A Venn diagram illustrated the overlap between the two approaches, resulting in 21 shared predictors (Fig. 2E). These variables encompassed key domains of patient severity, including demographics, vital signs, laboratory markers of inflammation and organ dysfunction, respiratory indices, fluid management, and circulatory support. Discussion

In this multicenter retrospective study, we developed and externally validated a machine learning–based prediction model for ICU mortality among mechanically ventilated patients using routinely available clinical variables. By integrating penalized regression and wrapper-based feature selection methods, we identified a compact and clinically interpretable set of predictors and demonstrated that a random forest model achieved robust early discrimination, satisfactory calibration, and meaningful clinical utility across heterogeneous ICU populations.

Model construction and selection strategy

A key strength of the present study lies in the methodological rigor of model development. Rather than relying on a single feature selection or modeling approach, we employed a two-step feature selection strategy combining LASSO regression and recursive feature elimination, thereby reducing dimensionality while minimizing method-specific bias. This approach allowed us to retain predictors that were consistently informative across both statistical and machine learning paradigms.

Among the six evaluated algorithms, the random forest model demonstrated the most favorable balance between discrimination and classification performance. Although other models, particularly XGBoost, achieved comparable AUC values, the random forest model showed superior stability across multiple evaluation metrics, including F1 score and specificity, which are particularly relevant in the context of ICU mortality prediction. Importantly, the choice of random forest was not based solely on discrimination but on overall robustness, interpretability, and suitability for modeling nonlinear interactions, all of which are essential for clinical implementation.

Interpretation of important predictors

The top-ranked predictors identified by the random forest model predominantly reflected age, respiratory status, metabolic stress, organ dysfunction, and treatment intensity, underscoring the multifactorial nature of ICU mortality in mechanically ventilated patients. Age consistently emerged as one of the most influential predictors, aligning with extensive literature demonstrating its strong association with critical illness outcomes [48–50].

Markers of perfusion and metabolic derangement, particularly lactate and glucose, ranked highly among important variables. Elevated lactate reflects impaired tissue oxygenation and circulatory failure, while dysglycemia is a well-established indicator of stress response and disease severity in critically ill patients. Similarly, renal and hepatic function markers, including creatinine, urea nitrogen, bilirubin, and transaminases, contributed substantially to mortality risk, highlighting the prognostic significance of early multi-organ dysfunction [51–53]. Respiratory-related variables, such as respiratory rate, oxygenation indices, and acid–base parameters, further emphasized the central role of respiratory failure severity in this population. Notably, variables reflecting circulatory support and fluid balance, including vasopressor use and urine output, were also among the most influential predictors, suggesting that both physiological derangement and therapeutic intensity are integral to early mortality risk stratification [54–57].

SHAP-based explainability analyses reinforced the clinical plausibility of these findings by demonstrating consistent and interpretable directional effects of individual predictors on model outputs. Together, these results indicate that the random forest model captured meaningful pathophysiological patterns rather than spurious statistical associations [58–60].

External validation and model performance

External validation across two independent cohorts-one large multicenter database (eICU) and one real-world institutional cohort—provided important insights into the generalizability and temporal behavior of the model. In both cohorts, the random forest model exhibited excellent early discrimination, with particularly strong performance during the first 48 hours after ICU admission. In the local hospital cohort, early AUC values approached 1.0, indicating outstanding early risk stratification capability.

However, the model’s predictive performance attenuated over time, with a more pronounced decline observed in the local cohort compared with the eICU cohort. While the eICU dataset maintained AUC values consistently above 0.7 throughout follow-up, performance in the local cohort declined to approximately 0.5–0.6 at later time points. This divergence highlights the importance of temporal validation and underscores that early prediction performance does not necessarily extrapolate uniformly to longer-term horizons.

Several factors may contribute to this temporal attenuation. First, the model was constructed using variables collected at or near ICU admission, which are most informative for early mortality risk. As ICU stay progresses, subsequent clinical events, treatment responses, complications, and evolving organ dysfunction may exert increasing influence on outcomes, thereby reducing the predictive value of baseline features alone. Second, differences in clinical practice patterns, resource availability, and case-mix between the local hospital cohort and the multicenter eICU dataset may further modulate long-term outcome trajectories [16, 61–63]. Overall, the external validation results indicate that the random forest model provides excellent admission‑time risk stratification for ICU mortality, with performance optimized for the first 48 hours after ICU admission. The model should be understood as an admission triage and screening tool rather than a dynamic prognostic instrument.

Differences between the local and eICU cohorts

The observed discrepancy in long-term predictive performance between the local and eICU cohorts warrants careful consideration. The greater temporal stability observed in the eICU cohort may reflect its larger sample size, broader institutional diversity, and more heterogeneous patient population, which could enhance model generalizability. In contrast, the local cohort likely reflects institution-specific admission practices, treatment protocols, and discharge policies, which may introduce additional variability over time.

Beyond these factors, we recognize that differences in treatment protocols and end-of-life care practices between Western (US-based) and Chinese ICUs may substantially influence the “non-survivor” profiles. Specifically, in many Western ICUs (including those in MIMIC and eICU), withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy is a common and often early event for patients with poor prognosis, whereas in China, family-driven decision-making frequently results in prolonged aggressive support, with withdrawal occurring later or less frequently [64–66]. Consequently, a patient with similar baseline severity may experience a later death in a Chinese ICU due to continued life support, shifting the mortality risk distribution toward later time points. This temporal shift means that static baseline variables—which are highly informative for early death—become less predictive of later death, as the latter is increasingly influenced by ongoing treatment intensity, family decisions, and complication management, all of which are not captured by admission data. Additionally, differences in protocol thresholds for interventions such as renal replacement therapy, or vasopressor titration may further modulate long-term trajectories. These practice variations are not encoded in our baseline feature set, and their effects may accumulate over time, contributing to the sharper performance decline in the local cohort.

The near-perfect early AUC in the local cohort reflects the model’s ability to capture acute physiological derangements that drive early mortality, rather than merely identifying patients already in a moribund state. The subsequent decline in performance beyond 48 hours is expected for a static baseline model and is further amplified in the local cohort by population-specific factors, including differences in genetic background, treatment practices, and end-of-life care. This pattern does not invalidate the model’s utility; rather, it reinforces its intended role as an early warning tool for risk stratification in the initial phase of ICU admission, when actionable decisions are most critical.

Importantly, these differences should not be interpreted as model failure but rather as evidence that baseline risk prediction is inherently context-dependent, particularly beyond the early phase of critical illness. The strong early performance across all cohorts suggests that the model is well suited for early risk stratification, while later performance may benefit from dynamic updating with time-varying clinical data.

Clinical implications

From a clinical perspective, the proposed random forest model offers several advantages. It relies exclusively on routinely available bedside variables, avoids dependence on composite severity scores, and provides interpretable risk estimates through SHAP analysis. The excellent early predictive performance—particularly within the first 48 hours after ICU admission, during which the model achieved near-perfect discrimination in the local cohort and consistently high AUC values in the eICU cohort—supports its utility as an early warning tool for identifying high-risk mechanically ventilated patients shortly after admission. Such early risk stratification can facilitate closer monitoring, timely escalation of care, and informed shared decision-making. However, given the observed temporal attenuation of performance, including near-random AUC values after 48 hours in the local cohort, we explicitly do not recommend using this model for continuous or long-term prognostication beyond the early phase. The model’s reliance on static baseline variables inherently limits its accuracy over extended time horizons, as later outcomes are increasingly influenced by dynamic clinical events, treatment responses, and complications. Accordingly, we advocate its use as a decision-support tool for early risk assessment rather than a standalone prognostic instrument for later time points. Future iterations should incorporate time-varying covariates to enable dynamic risk updating. Furthermore, the marked performance discrepancy between the eICU cohort (stable AUC > 0.7) and the local cohort (sharp decline after 48 hours) may partly reflect racial/ethnic differences in disease trajectories and treatment responses, underscoring the need for population-specific validation when applying prediction models across diverse ancestries. Decision curve analyses across all cohorts confirmed a net clinical benefit within the early period, supporting potential integration into clinical workflows.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design introduces the possibility of residual confounding despite rigorous preprocessing and validation. Second, the model relies solely on static baseline variables collected at or near ICU admission. It does not incorporate dynamic trends such as lactate clearance, changes in PEEP requirements, or evolving organ dysfunction over time—factors that may be more prognostic for later mortality. Consequently, the model is best characterized as an admission triage and screening tool rather than a dynamic prognostic instrument. Future studies should explore the integration of time-varying covariates to enable continuous risk updating. Third, although external validation was performed in two independent cohorts, further validation in prospective and international settings is warranted. Fourth, although key ABG parameters (PaO2, pH, lactate) were retained in the final model, PaCO2 was not selected by the feature selection process. This may be due to its collinearity with pH in the setting of acute respiratory failure, as hypercapnia primarily manifests as respiratory acidosis. The combination of PaO2, pH, lactate, and respiratory rate in our model likely captures the multidimensional nature of respiratory dysfunction. However, we acknowledge that in specific populations—such as patients with chronic hypercapnia (e.g., severe COPD)—PaCO2 might carry independent prognostic value. Future studies with larger, more diverse cohorts may investigate whether inclusion of PaCO2 or other ABG parameters further improves predictive accuracy. Finally, the observed overfitting tendency in the training set underscores the importance of cautious interpretation and external validation. The nomogram derived from our model should be used with an understanding of its developmental population (predominantly Western). Local validation or recalibration is recommended when applied to populations with different baseline risks or practice patterns, particularly for longer-term predictions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed and externally validated a machine learning–based random forest model for admission-time risk stratification in mechanically ventilated patients. We recommend its use as an admission triage and screening tool for identifying high-risk patients within the first 48 hours of ICU admission. Although predictive performance attenuated beyond this window, the model consistently provided strong early risk stratification. These findings highlight the value of static baseline models for early clinical decision support and the need for dynamic updating for longer-term prediction.
