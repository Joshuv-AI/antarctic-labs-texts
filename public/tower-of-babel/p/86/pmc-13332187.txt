Ancillary and Advanced Techniques for the Postmortem Examination
Authors: Halit Pinar, Füsun Gündoğan, Linda M Ernst, Beverly B Rogers, Don B Singer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose:This article describes how techniques such as advanced imaging, nucleic acid evaluation, integrated approaches, and data collection systems can facilitate the examination of a stillbirth or newborn, particularly when an autopsy is not possible.Content:The traditional autopsy remains the gold standard for investigating stillbirth and neonatal death. However, declining rates due to parental refusal, financial constraints, and cultural barriers highlight the need to widely adopt modern alternatives. This review examines emerging ancillary and advanced diagnostic techniques necessary to maximize diagnostic yield and ensure equitable access to comprehensive postmortem evaluations. Since imaging alone often misses critical microscopic pathologies, hybrid methods like Minimally Invasive Autopsy with Laparoscopically Assisted Sampling (MinImAL) are essential. MinImAL combines Postmortem Magnetic Resonance Imaging (PMMR) with image-guided tissue sampling to acquire specimens for histological and microbiological analysis. Complementary molecular techniques aid in identifying underlying genetic causes and informing recurrence risk. The success of these tests relies on optimized tissue selection. Systemic improvements include adopting standardized protocols, establishing Stillbirth Centers of Excellence, and using Artificial Intelligence (AI) for risk prediction. Importantly, integrating these advanced, less invasive methods is vital for addressing financial and racial health disparities, especially given the lack of Medicaid coverage for autopsy, thereby ensuring all bereaved families receive accurate and necessary diagnostic information.Keywords: fetus, autopsy, virtopsy, radiology, DNA, artificial intelligence, review

Introduction

The conventional autopsy remains the gold standard of postmortem examination in the investigation and study of stillbirth, providing the most comprehensive pathological assessment of the fetus and associated structures. Through meticulous external inspection, organ examination, histopathological analysis, and ancillary testing, an autopsy establishes definitive causes of death, determines genetic and heritable conditions, and provides invaluable information for genetic counseling and family management. Despite its proven diagnostic accuracy and importance in improving mortality statistics and public health surveillance, autopsy rates have declined dramatically over recent decades. Hospital autopsy rates in the United States decreased from approximately 50% in the 1950s to 7.4% by 2020, and in the United Kingdom and Wales, rates now fall below 1%.1-3 For stillbirths specifically, this decline has been attributed to parental refusal due to emotional, cultural, or religious concerns; physician reluctance to pursue autopsy; perceived medicolegal consequences; financial constraints; and reimbursement issues. 4 5

Emerging Technologies Transforming Stillbirth Pathology

Virtopsy: Virtual Autopsy

Virtopsy, developed at the University of Bern’s Institute of Forensic Medicine, is an innovative imaging-based method that combines advanced cross-sectional imaging with 3-dimensional visualization technology.6,7 The term “virtopsy” comes from “virtual autopsy” and describes the integration of postmortem multi-slice computed tomography (MSCT) and/or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with sophisticated postprocessing software to produce detailed, high-resolution 3D reconstructions of internal and external anatomy.

Instead of performing invasive dissection, virtopsy uses MSCT to capture volumetric imaging data from the entire body to generate up to 1200 customizable axial cross-sections with variable slice thickness and reconstruction intervals—typically completed in about ten minutes. The main benefits of virtopsy include its noninvasive nature, objective and investigator-independent documentation, preservation of body integrity without disfigurement, and the ability to create reproducible digital records that can be reviewed remotely by multiple experts and used as forensic evidence in legal cases.

Multi-slice CT remains the primary modality for clinical in vivo imaging in humans, with well-established uses in cardiac imaging, coronary artery assessment, trauma evaluation, oncological staging, and comprehensive body imaging across diverse patient groups. This technology allows for quick screening and thorough evaluation of living patients. Recently, Microfocus Computed Tomography (Micro-CT) has become the preferred computed tomography method for Postmortem Computed Tomography (PMCT) in virtual autopsies. In perinatal postmortem investigations, virtopsy remains mainly an emerging technology, as standard imaging protocols for fetal and neonatal death investigations favor PMMR over PMCT due to its superior soft tissue contrast and the difficulty of imaging small bodies.8,9

Postmortem Magnetic Resonance Imaging (PMMR)

PMMR functions best as a guided or supplementary tool rather than a full replacement for autopsy. 10 Diagnostic accuracy depends on fetal body weight, with less precise imaging in very small fetuses under 20 weeks’ gestation.11,12 PMMR offers detailed anatomical images with excellent soft-tissue contrast, especially without motion artifacts that often impair clinical MRI images. This technique is particularly useful in perinatal pathology for determining causes of fetal death and pregnancy termination, achieving about 89% diagnostic agreement with traditional autopsy when used alongside additional tests like placental exams and serological analysis. 13

PMMR demonstrates notable strengths in identifying cerebral malformations (sensitivity 88.4% and specificity 95.2%), intracranial hemorrhage (sensitivity 100% and specificity 99.1%), and cardiac structural anomalies. 13 In cases with significant maceration and autolysis, where traditional neuropathological exams are limited, PMMR provided clinically relevant diagnostic information in 53% of cases. It is especially better than obstetrical ultrasound at characterizing major central nervous system and fetal body malformations, particularly during the second trimester.14,15

Microfocus Computed Tomography (Micro-CT)

Microfocus CT is limited to small specimens and tissue samples, with typical maximum sample sizes of 9 to 50 mm in diameter, depending on the desired voxel size. 8 The maximum object size for single scans is usually 27 mm in diameter, with offset scanning techniques capable of extending the field of view up to approximately 4 cm.8,9 This makes micro-CT ideally suited for postmortem imaging of fetuses ≤20 weeks gestational age. 8 Microfocus CT achieves near-histological levels of detail. At 3 to 15 µm resolution, micro-CT can visualize cellular architecture, preserve tissue-paraffin interfaces, and provide 3-dimensional rendering of microscopic structures. When combined with appropriate staining protocols (such as eosin-based or iodine staining for soft tissue), micro-CT demonstrates architectural details comparable to conventional histopathological sections while maintaining 3-dimensional information.

Pathologies Frequently Missed by Postmortem Imaging

The main limitation of postmortem imaging, especially non-contrast-enhanced PMCT and PMMR, is its restricted ability to detect subtle soft tissue changes, micro-level pathology, and chemical or biochemical causes of death. Cardiovascular events, such as ischemic myocardial lesions, minor vascular lesions, cardiomyopathies, and fibrosis, are often overlooked or misdiagnosed on unenhanced PMCT and PMMR, which cannot visualize microvascular details. Systemic processes that depend on microscopic and chemical assessments pose significant challenges for imaging alone. While severe lung diseases may be visible, conditions like intrauterine pneumonia and interstitial lung disease are difficult to distinguish from postmortem changes. The systemic, microcirculatory, and cellular effects of sepsis and other forms of multiple organ dysfunction are not detectable through imaging. Conditions requiring histopathology, such as inflammation, early ischemic changes, or specific viral inclusions, are inherently missed by radiological methods. External signs like bruising and petechial hemorrhages are often overlooked or misinterpreted on imaging. Multiple studies, including systematic reviews, have shown a substantial discrepancy rate—up to 40% in some cases—between the cause of death determined solely by imaging and that found through conventional autopsy, with imaging frequently underperforming.16-18

The current consensus in forensic and clinical pathology is that virtual autopsy techniques are a valuable adjunct and often a necessary tool for targeted investigation (e.g., directing a pathologist to a specific injury) or as an alternative when autopsy is culturally or legally restricted. Still, they cannot fully replace the diagnostic depth and confirmation provided by traditional dissection and histology.11,19,20

Advanced Testing and Integrated Approaches

Multi-Modal Evaluation

Approaches to the perinatal autopsy may employ complementary techniques rather than a single methodology. For example, combining non-invasive imaging with targeted tissue sampling offers the benefits of comprehensive anatomical assessment while providing tissue for histological and genetic analysis. Image-guided needle tissue biopsy coupled with postmortem imaging can overcome the limitation of a lack of histology in purely radiological approaches. 21

Minimally Invasive Autopsy With Laparoscopically Assisted Sampling (MinImAL)

The MinImAL procedure represents an innovative compromise between non-invasive imaging and conventional autopsy. MinImAL procedure combines postmortem imaging—typically whole-body magnetic resonance imaging—with endoscopic visualization and targeted tissue sampling.22,23 Using principles adapted from laparoscopic surgery, the method allows examination of abdominal and thoracic cavities through small incisions, enabling direct visualization of organs while obtaining adequate tissue specimens for histopathological examination. 22 The technique also permits the collection of samples for toxicological testing and genetic analyses.

In a study of 103 cases, the MinImAL approach demonstrated excellent sampling adequacy for the most significant organs: heart (100%), lungs (100%), kidneys (100%), liver (96.7%), and spleen (94.5%). 22 This approach maintains a procedure duration comparable to a standard autopsy while significantly improving parental acceptance. 22 Significant limitations include the inability to assess brain pathology and skull contents with current techniques, the inability to weigh organs, and the potential to miss incidental microscopic findings not visible on imaging or targeted for sampling. The thymus showed lower adequate sampling rates (56%), suggesting this tissue may be more challenging to obtain. Clear procedural protocols regarding trocar placement, sequence of organ examination, and exclusion criteria remain areas requiring further standardization.

Assessment of Infection

The role of infection in stillbirth may be greatly underestimated due to inconsistent testing. 24 A recent study found that fetal bacterial cultures were obtained in only 18.2% of stillbirth cases, yet potential pathogens were identified in 28.8% of those tested. 25 This crucial step cannot be performed with only radiological techniques. Universal placental pathology and fetal autopsy would allow for bacterial culture and/or viral PCR in all cases where pathological features suggest infection.

Comprehensive Genetic Testing

Genetic testing with an external examination has been promoted as a quicker and more acceptable alternative to traditional stillbirth postmortem examinations. However, this method can only be performed when a tissue sample is available. The success rate of genetic testing varies considerably depending on the type of tissue and the testing method used. 26 Recent studies show that prepartum amniotic fluid has a 100% test completion rate, compared to 84.0% for placental tissue and 54.5% for fetal-only tissue. 27 Chromosomal microarray analysis achieves very high success rates (96.7%) compared to traditional karyotyping (74.1%). 28 For tissue sampling, skin (83.3% success in cases with minimal maceration) and kidney (75.0%) yield better results than liver (25.0%). 27 Recognizing these differences helps optimize tissue selection protocols to maximize diagnostic yield.

Exome sequencing (ES) has become an increasingly useful tool in the pathological evaluation of stillbirth or newborn. When included as part of a molecular autopsy, trio exome sequencing—which analyzes the stillborn and both parents—can identify underlying genetic causes in fetuses with structural abnormalities that might otherwise go unexplained. 27 These molecular findings offer parents vital information about developmental issues, clarify recurrence risks, and help manage future pregnancies.

Addressing Health Disparities

Health disparities in the use of autopsies for stillbirths represent a critical and severely under-addressed dimension of perinatal health inequity. These inequities manifest through a complex interplay of systemic barriers—racial and ethnic differences in stillbirth risk, profound socioeconomic obstacles, insurance coverage gaps, cultural hurdles, and provider biases—that disproportionately affect marginalized populations, perpetuating a cycle of incomplete diagnosis and inadequate prevention.

Racial Disparities in Stillbirth Risk

The extent of racial disparity in stillbirths is concerning, with Black women facing an overall 2.2 times higher risk than non-Hispanic White women. 29 Importantly, this disparity remains even among the most socioeconomically advantaged Black women, suggesting that socioeconomic factors alone do not explain the inequity. 29 Additionally, the rate of stillbirths with no known cause was significantly higher in Black populations compared to White populations, highlighting the urgent need for comprehensive autopsy investigations in communities that face the greatest barriers to accessing this diagnostic service.

Financial Barriers to Fetal Autopsy Access

Despite the critical importance of autopsies in determining the cause of death and providing diagnostic guidance for future pregnancies, current utilization rates are alarmingly low. While the medical recommendation for autopsy utilization is 75%, actual rates range between only 22% and 34%. 23 This gap is most pronounced among non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic women, who demonstrate notably lower uptake due to significant financial obstacles. 23 The primary deterrent for many families is the high out-of-pocket cost, which typically ranges from $1500 to $5000 per procedure. These costs are often prohibitive for low-income families, particularly because no U.S. insurance provider currently covers autopsy services. Historically, hospitals were incentivized to maintain autopsy programs to qualify for Medicare reimbursement. However, systemic access has declined following specific regulatory changes: (a) In 2019, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) removed regulation 482.22(d), which eliminated the requirement for hospitals to have an autopsy program. (b) Institutional Impact: This policy change likely led many hospitals—particularly those serving high-density Medicaid populations—to discontinue offering autopsy services entirely. Since Black mothers disproportionately rely on public insurance (66.05% public vs 27.5% private) compared to White mothers (29.66% public vs 63.37% private), this policy effectively creates a 2-tiered system where the highest-risk populations are financially excluded from crucial diagnostic services.25,30

Cultural and Religious Barriers

Cultural and religious beliefs also create significant barriers, with nearly 45% of respondents agreeing that “the body must not be tampered with.” These concerns, especially related to bodily mutilation and the need for prompt burial, are particularly strong in Muslim and Jewish communities, leading to a disparate racial and ethnic impact because healthcare providers often poorly understand and address these barriers.31-33

However, emerging evidence suggests that less invasive imaging techniques like virtopsy and MinImAL procedures have gained acceptability among Muslim and Jewish religious authorities34,35 These methods preserve body integrity and reduce the need for extensive dissection, which aligns with religious observances. Implementation of culturally sensitive autopsy counseling and awareness of religiously permissible alternatives could significantly increase acceptance rates.

Systemic Barriers and Provider Bias

Additionally, healthcare provider behavior indicates systematic discouragement of autopsies due to concerns about cost, inconclusive results, or potential disfigurement. Implicit bias may further reinforce this discouragement, resulting in a lower standard of care and less informed consent for marginalized patients.

These compounding factors create a vicious cycle: high stillbirth risk in marginalized populations is met with systematic barriers to autopsy, leading to incomplete diagnostic information. This results in inaccurate mortality surveillance data because autopsy results are rarely included in fetal death certificates. Consequently, prevention efforts are misdirected, failing to address the true underlying causes of stillbirth in high-risk populations and allowing unacceptable racial disparities to persist.36,37

Addressing the Equity Gap

Addressing this failure of reproductive justice and medical ethics requires urgent, multifaceted action. Policy reform must support Medicaid and insurance coverage for autopsy as an essential part of maternal care. Furthermore, healthcare systems should implement less invasive autopsy alternatives (like virtopsy), which many Muslim and Jewish authorities have deemed religiously permissible and that can significantly increase acceptance rates (from 60% to 78.6%).34,35 Finally, providers need training in cultural competence and implicit bias reduction to ensure equitable and sensitive autopsy counseling, thereby breaking the cycle of inequity and giving all bereaved families the answers crucial for preventing future losses.

Policy Recommendations and Systemic Improvements

Standardized Protocols and Training

A key part of improving the perinatal postmortem workup involves developing standardized training and resources to ensure that comprehensive evaluations (autopsy, placental pathology, and genetic studies) become more common and consistent. This standardization would enhance data collection quality and reliability while increasing diagnostic accuracy. The creation of Stillbirth Centers of Excellence has been suggested to offer specialized expertise and resources for thorough investigations.20,38-41

Enhanced Data Collection and Integration

Enhanced data collection systems that standardize reporting across jurisdictions will support future efforts in stillbirth investigation. Recommendations include methods to improve the consistency and quality of data collection, standardize case definitions, and improve processes for updating vital records as new information becomes available. The establishment of a maternal-child health data ecosystem has been proposed to serve as a comprehensive resource for research on stillbirth and other adverse pregnancy outcomes.20,38

Artificial Intelligence and Advanced Analytics

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is revolutionizing stillbirth analysis by transitioning care from reactive diagnosis to proactive prediction. Machine learning algorithms—such as XGBoost and neural networks—analyze vast datasets comprising maternal health records, fetal growth metrics, and cardiotocography (CTG) traces to calculate individual risk scores with high accuracy.42-45

Crucially, AI excels at identifying complex, non-linear risk patterns often invisible to human clinicians, such as specific interactions between maternal diabetes and fetal sex. In pathology, AI-driven image analysis automates the examination of placental tissue, precisely segmenting vascular structures and identifying lesions correlated with fetal mortality. While these tools promise to reduce preventable deaths through personalized intervention and earlier detection, widespread adoption faces challenges regarding data bias, clinical validation, and the ethical transparency of algorithmic decision-making. 46

The Shifting Cost Landscape: Analyzing the Real Expenses of Virtual Autopsy

The adoption of virtual autopsy methods brings a fundamental change in cost structure compared to traditional, labor-intensive dissection. While supporters often emphasize efficiency, the actual costs of a virtual autopsy are often higher than those of a traditional autopsy when only the initial procedural expense is considered. The financial burden shifts from labor to high capital and operational expenditures.

High Upfront and Capital Investment

The biggest financial barrier to adopting virtual autopsy is the initial investment in equipment and specialized infrastructure. Imaging tools like Multidetector CT scanners involve substantial capital costs, often ranging from hundreds of thousands to millions of dollars. MRI scanners (PMMR), which offer superior soft tissue contrast, are even more expensive to purchase, set up, and maintain. Along with the equipment, specialized facilities are required, such as shielding for MRI systems, dedicated power sources, and cooling systems. Additionally, these advanced CT and MRI machines necessitate costly maintenance agreements and routine technical services to keep them operational. 12

Operational Costs and Specialized Expertise

Once the initial capital expense is paid, the ongoing operational costs per case remain high. Virtual autopsy requires highly trained staff who earn higher wages, including forensic radiologists or technicians who operate scanners and produce high-quality images, and forensic pathologists who interpret postmortem radiological findings. Although non-invasive, more advanced virtual techniques also need consumables. Another major and ongoing operational expense is data storage and archiving, because virtual autopsy generates large amounts of digital data (DICOM files) that require significant investment in secure, long-term digital storage systems. 17

The Complementary Cost Factor

A key aspect of the “real cost” is that the virtual autopsy is frequently used as an adjunct rather than a complete replacement for the traditional autopsy. When radiological methods serve as a screening tool before a traditional autopsy, the overall cost of the investigation is higher because it includes both the imaging procedure and the subsequent dissection and histology. Since imaging alone cannot reliably identify microscopic pathologies, such as early infections, or chemical causes of death, the virtual autopsy often needs to be supplemented with minimally invasive tissue sampling or toxicological analysis, which incurs additional costs not covered by the imaging itself.

Potential for Long-Term Efficiency

Despite the high initial investment and per-case expense, supporters argue that virtual autopsy offers long-term benefits, mostly in non-monetary value. In high-volume facilities, the speed of PMCT scans (often minutes per scan) can allow for quicker processing. Furthermore, the non-invasive nature of the scan lowers the risk of infection for staff handling infectious diseases, cutting costs related to PPE and biohazard precautions. Crucially, the permanent, objective digital data set can reduce the need for repeated tests and facilitate easy second opinions years later, potentially saving money on legal issues and additional investigations.

Making stillbirth workups more affordable and accessible remains a crucial challenge. Recommendations include improving access to autopsy, genetic testing, and placental pathology by addressing financial barriers. This may involve changes to insurance coverage, developing more cost-effective testing methods, or creating subsidized testing programs for families experiencing stillbirth. In summary, the high cost of virtual autopsy results from specialized equipment and personnel. Although it may provide non-monetary benefits like safety and efficiency, it often incurs additional expenses when used alongside traditional methods to ensure a thorough diagnosis.

Summary

The conventional autopsy remains the gold standard for stillbirth investigation, but sharply declining rates—driven by parental refusal, financial barriers, and cultural concerns—necessitate adopting advanced diagnostic methods. This manuscript reviews the shift toward non-invasive and minimally invasive techniques to maximize diagnostic yield and address health disparities. The key innovation is virtopsy, which uses PMMR and is the preferred alternative for fetuses beyond 20 weeks, showing high diagnostic accuracy (up to 94%) and nearly 100% parental acceptance, as it preserves body integrity. Recognizing that imaging alone can miss critical findings—such as microscopic pathology, sepsis, or subtle cardiac lesions—hybrid methods like Minimally Invasive Autopsy (MinImAL) combine PMMR/microCT with image-guided tissue sampling to ensure histological and microbiological assessment.

Complementary molecular testing, especially Exome Sequencing (ES) and chromosomal microarray analysis, is essential for identifying underlying genetic causes and informing recurrence risk. The success of this testing depends on optimized tissue protocols, focusing on amniotic fluid, skin, or kidney. Additionally, establishing standardized protocols, Stillbirth Centers of Excellence, and utilizing Artificial Intelligence (AI) for risk prediction are crucial to improving data quality and enhancing prevention efforts.

Critically, the implementation of these advanced techniques must prioritize addressing racial and health disparities in autopsy utilization. By reducing financial barriers through Medicaid and insurance coverage, offering religiously permissible alternatives, and providing cultural competency training to healthcare providers, the field can ensure that all bereaved families—regardless of race, ethnicity, or socioeconomic status—receive comprehensive diagnostic evaluations that honor their preferences and provide the answers necessary to prevent future losses.
