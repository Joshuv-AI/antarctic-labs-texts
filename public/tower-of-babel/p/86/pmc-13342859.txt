Evaluation of a proposed link between the SARS-CoV-2 furin cleavage site and mouse-adapted MERS-coronavirus MA30
Authors: Sarah M. Metzger, Terry C. Jones, Jenny I. J. Meier, Anja Richter, Mark-Christian Klassen, Ruth Olmer, Nicolai Diegmüller, Kim C. Heimsch, Christian Drosten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThis study formally evaluates a hypothesis that has been advanced by some scientists and public commentators in support of a nonnatural origin of SARS-CoV-2. The hypothesis proposes that the unique polybasic furin cleavage motif of SARS-CoV-2 may be technically or evolutionarily derived from a mouse-adapted laboratory strain of MERS-coronavirus (MA30). While the World Health Organization’s Scientific Advisory Group on the Origins of Novel Pathogens (SAGO) concluded that the available evidence was insufficient to support the proposed link, the underlying scientific rationale for this conclusion has not been published. We systematically assessed the evidence from genomic surveillance and conducted additional experimental studies. Together, these data do not support an evolutionary or genetic relationship.

Understanding the origin and functional relevance of the SARS-CoV-2 furin cleavage site (FCS) remains central to ongoing debates regarding the origin of COVID-19. The RRAR amino acid motif at the S1/S2 junction is unique among known sarbecoviruses and contributes to enhanced cell entry, pathogenicity, and host range (1, 2). Although several studies have documented naturally occurring furin-like sites in other betacoronaviruses, including MERS-CoV and HKU1 (3, 4), no close analogue to the SARS-CoV-2 sequence has been found in bat sarbecoviruses. This gap has led to hypotheses of nonnatural origins. One such hypothesis, published by Lisewski in 2024, argues that the artificial mouse-adapted MERS-CoV strain, MERS-MA30, described by Li et al. (5) contains an amino acid sequence (RRVR) closely resembling the SARS-CoV-2 S1/S2 motif (6). The hypothesis suggests that RRVR might have been artificially introduced into a SARS-CoV-2 precursor virus, later mutating to the extant RRAR motif (6). The hypothesis has been used to trigger public debate (7) and was also presented to the WHO Scientific Advisory group on the origins of pandemics (SAGO), as mentioned in their 2025 independent assessment of SARS-CoV-2 origins (8). SAGO concludes that RRVR-containing viruses occurred sporadically during the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic in humans but have no link to early SARS-CoV-2 strains. Here we document and expand the studies behind the SAGO assessment. We examined epidemiological sequence data and studied experimental virus variants. Based on GISAID (9) data, transitory forms from the MERS-MA30 S1/S2-encoding nucleotide (nt) sequence intermediate with that of SARS-CoV-2 were not found in early circulating SARS-CoV-2. RRVR appeared and disappeared sporadically over three years from the pandemic onset, and was always spatiotemporally circumscribed. Using reverse genetics, we generated RRVR-containing SARS-CoV-2, and found that RRVR consistently reduced entry efficiency in both D614G and the early 614D backgrounds and had inferior replication fitness. RRVR viruses did not revert toward RRAR but instead accumulated alternative substitutions, such as RRGR. Collectively, these findings do not support any evolutionary links between MERS-MA30 and SARS-CoV-2.

Results

FCS Nucleotide Composition Early in the Pandemic.

To assess potential similarities between MERS-MA30 and SARS-CoV-2 sequences during the pandemic, we examined 17,231,704 genomes downloaded from the GISAID database on September 28, 2025. After filtering (Materials and Methods) we were left with 16,532,772 human sequences for FCS analysis. All sequence counts mentioned below are followed by a percentage in parentheses, which refers to this filtered total. The filtered sequences were individually aligned to the Wuhan-Hu-1 (GenBank ID NC_045512.2) SARS-CoV-2 reference genome.

To investigate the occurrence of S:684V at the beginning of the pandemic at the nucleotide level, we first considered the 12 nt sequence (genome sites 23,606 through 23,617) coding for the four spike AAs (sites 682-685) of the FCS. In 16,510,617 (99.87%) cases the 12 nt sequence could be translated using the standard IUPAC genetic code. The MERS-MA30 nt sequence for RRVR (CGCCGTGTGCGC) and the reference Wuhan nt sequence for RRAR (CGGCGGGCACGT) differ in five nucleotide positions (here referred to as their distance) as shown in Fig. 1A. Fig. 1B shows the distances from each sequence in our dataset to the MERS-MA30 nt sequence (left plot) and to the reference Wuhan nt sequence (right plot) for all sequences through March 15, 2020. Of the 18,703 translatable genomes in this period, all but 28 were identical to the Wuhan sequence, and all of these were distance one from the Wuhan sequence. Of these, four were distance four from MERS-MA30, all others were distance five or six. Two of the distance four sequences had a valine in the third amino acid (AA) position, in both cases involving a GCA→GTA mutation, as seen in later-circulating sporadic mutants. Seventeen of the sequences that were distance 1 from the Wuhan reference were also further (distance 6) from the MERS-MA30 sequence. Because this analysis considered distances between the full 12 nt FCS sequences, it was repeated, focusing on just the third AA of the FCS. There were three cases where the codon differed from the Wuhan GCA: the two GCA→GTA instances just described, and a synonymous GCA→GCG mutation, preserving the alanine. As an additional check on the nt composition of the FCS third AA throughout the pandemic, we considered whether there might be a tendency toward a mixed S:684 valine/alanine population in infections, as may be evidenced by ambiguous nucleotide codes in called genome consensus sequences. The clear conclusion (Materials and Methods) was that there were virtually no SARS-CoV-2 consensus sequences in our dataset with ambiguous FCS 3rd AA nucleotides consistent with a predominantly valine/alanine mixture.

FCS Amino Acid Composition Throughout the Pandemic.

Using the data downloaded from GISAID (as above), we next examined the relative frequencies throughout the pandemic of a six-AA region containing the FCS. Fig. 1C shows a histogram of the prevalence of the high-frequency (at least 2,000 occurrences) six-AA sequences per month over time, against a background indicating the prevailing SARS-CoV-2 variant. AA sequences that occurred at lower frequencies comprised a total of 18,411 (0.11%) sequences. A clear pattern of variant-driven transitions between the prevailing six-AA sequence is apparent. There is an extreme preponderance of alanine at S:684, with the three most-frequent AA sequences (HRRARS, RRRARS, and PRRARS) comprising a total of 16,442,594 (99.45%) sequences. Overall, S:684V was present in 9,474 (0.06%) of the unambiguous 18 nt sequences, in nine distinct 6-AA compositions. Fig. 1D shows the global frequency of the nine 6-AA S:684V sequences over time as a percentage of all sequences observed per month.

Regular Occurrence But Nonfixation of S:684V During the Pandemic.

To examine the occurrence of S:684V in more detail, we downloaded a SARS-CoV-2 phylogenetic tree from GISAID containing 12,674,144 tips (and their associated metadata) on September 19, 2025. Containing ~72% of the 17.23 M GISAID sequences, this sample can be expected to give as accurate an impression of S:684V occurrence as it is possible to obtain, given the degree of global undersampling of SARS-CoV-2 sequences. The GISAID tree contains 156 different human six-AA sequences from 303 geographic regions (typically recorded at the country level). Of the 9,502 S:684V sequences with S:684V, 2,651 (27.90%) were not present in the tree, as would be expected given that the tree contains 72% of the GISAID sequences.

We located the tree tip nodes corresponding to S:684V sequences and then ascended from each tip to successive internal tree nodes to identify S:684V-rich phylogenetic clades. The tree ascent stopped the first time an internal node’s parent had a proportion of descendant tips with S:684V below 5% (i.e., at the last node in the tree ascent from an S:684V tip that still had at least 5% S:684V among its descendants). Ascending from the 6,851 tree tips with S:684V in this manner resulted in 1,374 unique internal tree nodes. The mean timespan among all samples sequenced in the descendant tips of these 1,374 S:684V subtrees is 14.61 d (SD 29.25 d), with a median period of 1 d, with S:684V sequences found in 94 geographic regions (typically countries). The mean number of countries involved in these S:684V periods was 1.18 (median 1).

To get a visual overview of the periods of highest S:684V prevalence, we filtered for subtrees in which at least 3% of their tips have S:684V and with at least five S:684V occurrences. There were 228/1374 (16.59%) such clades. The periods covered by the range of samples in these clades are plotted in Fig. 1E. Each blue horizontal line shows the timespan of a selected clade. The orange horizontal region within each timespan indicates the period during which S:684V sequences were observed during the timespan of their overall clade. To highlight periods of higher S:684V prevalence, the darker reddish horizontal lines indicate S:684V periods of at least two weeks, during which at least 50 S:684V sequences were detected. There were 16/1374 (1.16%) such clades.

The S:684 Codon Has Been Under Positive Selection Pressure.

To investigate whether selection pressure has acted on the sites of the FCS, a Fitch algorithm analysis was performed to produce a dN/dS ratio, relating the relative nonsynonymous nucleotide substitution rate to that of synonymous changes. The S:684 codon has a dN/dS value of 9.35, indicating strong positive selection. Purifying (negative) selection is strongly indicated at sites S:682 and S:683 and suggested at the fourth FCS position, S:685 (SI Appendix, Table S1).

We examined the inferred presence of nonsynonymous S:684 changes through the phylogeny. There are chains of internal nodes with an inferred substituted amino acid of length up to 73 internal nodes, with up to 407 tips included in their associated clades. A summary of the occurrence counts of inferred alternate AAs for chains of at least one internal node is V (677), S (142), T (120), P (9), and E (2).

The selection analyses and the nonfixation of S:684V (or any other substitution) suggests that S:684 has been under transient positive selection. However, all observed alternate amino acids were apparently either deleterious or provided a fitness increment that failed to spread due to stochastic reasons.

S:684V Results in Reduced Fitness In Vitro.

To evaluate whether the nonfixation of S:684V may be due to observable fitness differences, we engineered the mutation into an infectious clone of SARS-CoV-2 lineage B1 using a TAR-cloning based reverse genetics system. We compared replication kinetics of the original virus (rB1) with the S:684V variant (rB1 S:684V) in Calu-3, Vero E6, and A549AT cells (A549 overexpressing ACE2 and TMPRSS2) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.01. We sampled supernatants over three days and determined infectious titers through plaque assay. We found overall highly similar replication kinetics for both viruses, with slightly lower peak titers for rB1 S:684V in A549AT (Fig. 2A).

To clarify a potential fitness difference in Calu-3 cells at 48 h post infection (hpi), we infected Calu-3 cells with both rB1 and rB1 S:684V at an MOI of 0.01 with starting ratios of 1:1, 1:10, and 10:1, and competed the two viruses over five passages. We used Sanger sequencing, with results analyzed by a custom Python script (13), to genotype the resulting populations after each passaging step. We found that rB1 outcompeted rB1 S:684V, independently of the starting ratio (Fig. 2B), corroborating the fitness advantage of rB1.

S:684V Leads to Reduced Entry in Cell Lines With High and Low TMPRSS Expression.

Considering the function of the FCS, we hypothesized that fitness differences may be determined by entry efficiency. We therefore compared entry efficiency at a high MOI. We adsorbed virus at MOI 1 to Calu-3, Vero E6, and A549AT cells at 4 °C and later moved cells to 37 °C for synchronized entry. Cells were lysed at four (Calu-3) or six (A549AT, Vero E6) hpi, and sgRNA N transcript was quantified by qRT-PCR. We found that entry of rB1 S:684V was reduced in all cell lines (Fig. 2C). Because Vero E6 do not express TMPRSS2, we assume that S:684V confers a negative fitness effect on cell entry, but not necessarily on TMPRSS2-specific mechanisms.

Instability of S:684V Upon Passaging in a Human Cell Line With High TMPRSS2 Expression.

Although S:684V has not been observed in any phylogenetically basal position, the increased fitness associated with the prevalent S:684A opens the possibility that a clonal precursor bearing S:684V might have fixed a V684A mutation before the onset of SARS-CoV-2 surveillance and sequencing. To test this scenario, we passaged freshly rescued rB1 and rB1 S:684V through Calu-3 cells. We used Sanger sequencing, as described above, to determine the identity of the second nucleotide in the S:684 triplet. We found a relative instability of this nucleotide, with nonalanine minorities arising spontaneously in all five passages in Calu-3 (Fig. 2D). For rB1, the percentage of nonalanine amino acids rose to ~12% by passage five. For rB1 S:684V, the nonalanine percentage was ~20% by passage five, with glycine by far the most abundant minority.

Reversion of S:D614G Has a Mild Mitigating Effect on S:A684V Fitness Consequences.

The D614G substitution in the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2 arose in early 2020 and has been conserved throughout the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic. It confers altered receptor binding domain (RBD) disposition and more effective cleavage by host proteases (14). Had an amino acid exchange in S:684 occurred prior to the D614G transition, it would have arisen against an S:614D background. We therefore constructed rB1 S:G614D (614D) and rB1 S:G614D+A684V (614D+A684V), and competed them over five passages in Calu-3 cells, as described above. 614D outcompeted 614D+A684V at 1:1 and 10:1 starting ratios, however we did not observe a strong effect for the 1:10 starting ratio (Fig. 2E). Replication kinetics in A549AT, Calu-3, and Vero E6 were similar between viruses. Only in A549AT, titers of 614D+A684V were slightly lower at the later timepoints (Fig. 2F). Synchronized entry assays revealed an overall advantage for 614D in the tested cell lines. Differences between the viruses were smaller compared to their D614G counterparts, and virtually absent in Vero E6, a cell line with extremely low TMPRSS2 expression (Fig. 2G). Thus, A684V overall maintains an adverse effect on viral fitness that is, however, less pronounced after reversion of D614G.

Relative Stability of S:684 After Reversion of S:D614G.

To assess whether and which amino acids would spontaneously arise after clonal rescue in an S:684V background, we repeated the passaging experiment in Calu-3 cells, as described above. While there were very small minorities of glutamic acid and glycine for both viruses, we did not observe any increase of other amino acids in S:684 across five passages, respectively. In particular we did not observe V684A (Fig. 2H).

While the employed cell lines are widely used in laboratories, any hypothetical adaptive changes might also have occurred in human-to-human transmission. To test this scenario, we passaged 614D+A684V in primary human bronchial epithelial cells (hBECs) cultured at the air–liquid interface (ALI). ALI cultures were infected with freshly rescued 614D+A684V, and virus was collected and passaged onward every 48 h. Three independent experiments were performed in parallel over five passages. One lineage (lineage B) went extinct after passage four. Genotyping of supernatants was done after every passage. We found relative stability of S:A684V across passages (Fig. 2I). However, in two of the three lineages, we intermittently observed glycine minorities. These were not observed for longer than one passage, indicating that only a fraction of the transferred virus populations initiated subsequent infection cycles. Alanine did not arise in any of the passage series.

Discussion

Here we challenged the hypothesis that the polybasic FCS in SARS-CoV-2 is genetically related to a similar sequence in MERS-MA30.

MERS-MA30 was generated through 30 passages in human DPP4 knock-in mice and acquired multiple adaptive mutations, including changes at its S1/S2 junction (5). Importantly, the MERS-MA30 RRVR motif is embedded within a merbecovirus-specific structural and functional context and is not known in any natural sarbecovirus lineage. The MERS-MA30-related hypothesis requires two key features: i) that RRVR existed as an early or basal variant within SARS-CoV-2 genomic diversity, and ii) that RRVR-containing SARS-CoV-2 viruses evolved toward the nucleotide sequences of SARS-CoV-2 viruses with RRAR. In case a SARS-CoV-2 precursor had been generated in the laboratory based on MERS MA-30, the subsequent nucleotide sequence evolution would have to have affected more than the A- to -V change, as the nucleotide code in the FCS region has five additional nt differences between SARS-CoV-2 and MERS-CoV MA-30.

For evaluation of circulating viruses, we analyzed over three years of GISAID data and confirmed that RRVR variants appeared only sporadically, were never phylogenetically basal in known viruses, and did not expand their geographic range or persist to any relevant degree.

The bioinformatic analysis showed that just 0.06% of the 17.23 M sequences in the GISAID database contained S:684V. The substitution was present at a global frequency of typically around 0.06% throughout the pandemic. The metadata from S:684V containing clades of the 12 M tip phylogenetic tree revealed 1,374 groups of closely related sequences (i.e., phylogenetic subtrees) when S:684V sequences were circulating. The mean length of these periods was 14.61 d (median 1 d), with a mean of 1.18 (median 1) countries involved. These numbers clearly show that although the S:684V substitution has occurred regularly, it has tended to then quickly disappear and has rarely been transmitted between regions.

For experimental assessment we engineered SARS-CoV-2 RRVR viruses with a SARS-CoV-2-typical codon utilization, ignoring coding discrepancies and enabling adaptive evolution with minimal sequence change. This minimal change scenario (GUA→GCA for V684A) was chosen because we found no sequences with amino acid coding as in the respective MERS-MA30 sequence. For V684A, a change from the MERS-MA30 codon would have required two mutational steps (GUG→GUA→GCA or GUG→GCG→GCA), which is less likely to occur.

In our experiments, RRVR virus showed reduced entry competence in both 614D (early Wuhan-like) and D614G (pandemic) backgrounds, consistent with RRAR being functionally superior in human cells. Critically, the effect of V684A is not limited to cells expressing TMPRRS2, diminishing the possibility that our cell models may insufficiently represent the proteolytic environment of respiratory epithelium. Entry phenotypes are more pronounced in the later D614G background than the original 614D type in which early changes would be projected. Selective pressures reflected by competition experiments would thus act against 684 V, even if pressure is somewhat limited in the 614D background. Whereas this supports the idea that initial RRVR would have been selected against, this selection requires a competing variant with higher fitness. Our passaging experiments with clonal virus suggest a preference of alternative changes such as RRGR instead of RRAR in all models tested, including differentiated human respiratory epithelia.

Our study has several limitations. The bioinformatic analysis is necessarily limited by the number of available sequences. Worldwide, approximately 778 M COVID-19 cases had been laboratory confirmed as of October 12, 2025 (15). A seroprevalence survey in April 2021 estimated that 25% of the worldwide population had SARS-CoV-2 specific antibodies (16). A conservative estimate of the actual number of infections through the end of 2025, might thus be four billion. The GISAID collection of 17.23 M SARS-CoV-2 sequences therefore likely represents less than 0.5% of all SARS-CoV-2 (consensus) genomes that have existed. Despite the very low overall percentage of worldwide infections for which a consensus genome is available, sequencing was performed on a geographically and temporally broad range of cases, making it unlikely that a significant sustained but unobserved period of S:684V circulation occurred. Due to a general lack of sequence data from the pre-epidemic period, it is moreover impossible to formally exclude the existence of any hypothetical sequences before emergence of the extant virus. Experimental studies were done to investigate hypothetical early evolutionary steps but in general cannot reflect the complexities in natural populations.

In conclusion, our results do not support early circulation of RRVR or MERS-MA30-derived nucleotide sequences in the FCS-encoding region of SARS-CoV-2. They also refute direct evolution of RRAR from RRVR in SARS-CoV-2

Materials and Methods

Bioinformatic Analysis.

17,231,704 SARS-CoV-2 sequences were downloaded from the GISAID (9) database on September 28, 2025. These were individually aligned to the Wuhan-Hu-1 (GenBank ID NC_045512.2) SARS-CoV-2 reference sequence using the gb2seq package (17) interfacing to edlib (18) for the alignment and the dark-matter package (19) to canonicalize edlib alignment gaps. Processing used the vsplit package (20) to virtually split the sequences file for parallel processing on a SLURM cluster. A region of 18 nucleotides (sites 23,603 through 23,620) from the spike protein was extracted from each sequence and, when possible, translated to an amino acid sequence using the standard IUPAC genetic code, as provided by the BioPython (21) package.

Sequences unsuitable for our analysis were filtered out as follows (with parenthesized percentages based on the 17.23 M download): 24,780 (0.14%) samples where the date was only given as a year; 20,082 (0.12%) nonhuman sequences; 5,968 (0.03%) sequences where the genome alignment failed; 261,969 (1.52%) sequences where the 18 nt region of interest consisted only of gaps or, in 71,126 (0.41%) cases, only the fully ambiguous N nucleotide code, or in 3 (0.00%) cases contained a stop codon. Of the sequences with nonhuman origin, only two had the S:684V substitution; dog and deer samples collected September 14, 2020, and December 2, 2023, respectively.

The above filtering resulted in 16,532,772 human sequences with a third FCS codon that was unambiguous. The filtered data contain 356,480 (2.13%) sample collection dates that were incomplete but completed based on the GISAID submission date, as follows. If the collection date was either a year and a month or just a year and it matched the GISAID submission date, the submission date was used. In cases where the collection date was just a year and this did not match the submission year, the record was filtered out. The records in this latter category had collection year counts as follows: 2020: 4,959; 2021: 17,715; 2022: 1,351; 2023: 492; and 2024: 263.

We examined the nt composition of the FCS third AA throughout the pandemic, to consider whether there might be a tendency toward a mixed S:684 valine/alanine population in infections. This could be evidenced by ambiguous nucleotide codes in called genome consensus sequences. To this end, we extracted the three nt of all ambiguous 3rd codon sites and examined the set of AA consistent with these sequences. In all but two cases, the set of compatible AA contained both valine and alanine. In only 303 (0.00%) cases was there an ambiguous codon compatible with only those two. The most frequent ambiguous 3 nt sequences and their compatible AAs were as follows: NNN: 285,797 (1.73%) occurrences, compatible with all AA; GNN: 6,821 (0.04%) occurrences, compatible with ADEGV; NNA: 2,963 (0.02%) occurrences, compatible with *AEGIKLPQRSTV; GNA: 796 (0.00%) occurrences, compatible with AEGV; GYA: 303 (0.00%) occurrences, compatible with AV; KNN: 6 (0.00%) occurrences, compatible with *ACDEFGLSVWY (where * represents any stop codon). Thus, there was no sign of a mixed S:684 population composed of just alanine and valine.

For selection pressure analysis, the size of the phylogeny (~12.6 M tips, ~37 M internal branches) made the use of sophisticated analysis tools infeasible as these are typically designed to handle trees with only thousands of tips. The analysis was therefore performed using custom Python scripts to directly calculate dN/dS ratios. The approach used the Fitch algorithm (22) to (bottom–up) assign inferred sequences to internal phylogenetic tree nodes, followed by the (top–down) tabulation of genome changes according to Nei-Gojobori (23) considering only internal branches, and applying a Jukes–Cantor adjustment. A Chi-squared test using the Python scipy library (version 1.16.3) (24) was used to check for significance of deviation of nonsynonymous substitutions from the rate that would be expected under neutral evolution. The code for calculating dN/dS was written using Claude Code (25). To ensure accuracy, two isolated and independent implementations were requested (in neither case giving an indication of how the ratio should be derived and also giving the opportunity for third-party packages to be used). Each Claude Code instance was then asked to critique the script written by the other, and improvement suggestions were applied to both. The implementations both used the Fitch algorithm, but with minor differences in approach. The dN/dS ratios from both approaches agree exactly.

Recombinant Viruses.

All recombinant viruses in this study were generated using a TAR cloning based reverse genetics system as described in (26). Briefly, fragments of the SARS-CoV-2 genome were amplified from RNA by RT-PCR. The desired mutations were introduced through site-directed mutagenesis (Q5 site-directed mutagenesis kit, NEB #E0554) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fragments were then transformed into highly transformable yeast strain VL6-48N for assembly of YACs.

YACs were isolated from individual yeast colonies and propagated in electrocompetent E.cloni (LGC #60052-1). After miniprep, plasmids were subjected to Oxford Nanopore sequencing to confirm sequence integrity.

In vitro-transcription and rescue of rSARS-CoV-2 followed the strategy described in (27). Briefly, plasmids were preamplified with EquiPhi29™ DNA-Polymerase (Thermo Fisher #A39391) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Plasmids were then linearized and purified through Phenol-Chloroform extraction followed by NaAc precipitation. Purified templates were in vitro-transcribed using the HiScribe® T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (NEB #E2040S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions but with an incubation time of 24 h at 25 °C. Anti-reverse cap analog (ARCA, NEB #S1411) was used for cotranscriptional capping. Transcripts were purified using the Monarch® Spin RNA Cleanup Kit (NEB #T2040S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

5 µg of purified transcript were electroporated into BHK-21 cells with five pulses of 860 V, 99 µs as described in (27), and cells were plated and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2. After 24 h, 5 mL of supernatant were transferred to freshly seeded Vero E6 TMPRSS2 cells, and cells were monitored daily for CPE.

Cell Culture.

VeroE6, A549AT, Calu-3, BHK-21 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cells were subcultured at 70-90% confluency.

Human Bronchial Epithelial Cells (hBEC) p1+ were isolated from explanted lungs, obtained from the Hannover Lung Transplant Program after patients informed consent, ethical vote 2923 to 201, and deidentified prior to use in this study. Cells were differentiated as described previously (28). Briefly, hBECs were expanded in PneumaCult™Ex-Plus Medium (StemCell Technologies), until suitable confluency was reached at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cells were resuspended in PneumaCult™-Ex Plus complete medium and seeded on 6.5 mm Greiner ThinCert cell culture inserts according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were airlifted three days after seeding and maintained with PneumaCult™-ALI complete medium according to manufacturers’ instructions. Beating of ciliated cells were observed after three weeks and air–liquid interface (ALI) cell cultures were used for experiments 21-35 d post airlift.

Competition Assay and Passaging of Virus On Calu-3 Cells.

Calu-3 cells were seeded in 24-well plates one day prior to the experiment. Virus was diluted at the desired ratios in OptiPro™ serum free medium. Cells were infected at a total MOI of 0.01 and incubated for one hour at 37 °C, 5%CO2. Inocula were removed and fresh growth medium (DMEM + 2%FBS) was supplied. Supernatant was sampled every 48 h and subsequently passaged on to fresh cells at a 1:500 dilution.

RNA was extracted from samples with a MagNAPure 96 (Roche) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After reverse transcription of the target region, samples were subjected to Sanger sequencing (Microsynth Seqlab GmbH, 9436 Balgach, Switzerland). Six samples did not produce reliable Sanger reads after repeated attempts and were excluded from further analysis.

Growth Kinetics.

Vero E6, Calu-3, and A549AT cells were seeded in a 24-well plate one day prior to experiment. Cells were washed 1× and infected with the respective virus diluted in OptiPro™ serum free medium at an MOI of 0.01. After incubating for one hour at 37 °C, 5% CO2, inocula were removed and fresh growth medium (DMEM + 10%FBS) was supplied. Samples were taken at the indicated time points and titers were determined through plaque assay.

Entry Assay.

VeroE6, Calu-3, and A549AT cells were seeded in a 24-well plate one day prior to experiment. Virus dilutions were prepared in cold OptiPro™ serum free medium (4 °C), and cells were washed 1x in cold PBS prior to infection. Cells were infected at an MOI of 1 and were kept at 4 °C for 1 h. Inocula were then removed, cells were washed rigorously with cold PBS, and t0-samples were taken through cell lysis. Cells were incubated at 37 °C, 5%CO2, until final sampling (4/6hpi).

Passaging on ALI Cultures.

Prior to infection, ALI cultures were washed twice with PBS. Per wash, 500 µL of PBS were added to the apical side of the cultures, plates were incubated at 37 °C for 15 min, and liquid was aspirated from the apical side. Virus dilutions were then added to the apical side of the cultures and incubated at 37 °C, 5%CO2, for 1 h. Inocula were removed and cultures were washed once. At the indicated sampling time points, 200 µL of PBS were added apically to the cultures, and plates were incubated for 15 min at 37 °C. Apical liquid was collected, and fresh cultures were infected at a 1:2 dilution as described above. 50 µL of the remaining sample was inactivated and RNA was extracted using the MagNAPure 96 as described above.

Data Analysis and Presentation.

Data analysis and plotting was done in R (v4.3.1) and Rstudio (v2023.06.1) unless otherwise specified. All experiments were done in triplicates unless otherwise indicated.
