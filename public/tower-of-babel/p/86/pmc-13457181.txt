Dilating the aging clock with light
Authors: Jessica Jing Yi Chang, Ivan Yee Man Ho, Clarissa Bernice Quah, Yu Yin Bai, Jiahe Ren, Aaron Song Chuan Foo, Daniel Boon Loong Teh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrecise and rapid modeling of aging remains a significant challenge. This review explores the potential of emerging light-based technologies—including direct light exposure, photodynamic therapy (PDT), and optogenetics—as accelerated, mechanistically targeted models for interrogating aging processes. Unlike conventional methods that rely on chronologically aged organisms or non-specific stressors, these light-driven approaches can induce specific hallmark aging phenotypes within days. This offers unprecedented temporal and spatial control, allowing researchers to precisely trigger and define molecular pathways, such as localized oxidative bursts or programmed protein interactions. Such capabilities provide a powerful platform for testing causal hypotheses about aging, especially organ-specific aging. The review also outlines a framework connecting each light modality to aging, including cellular senescence, telomere attrition, proteostasis loss, and epigenetic drift. It discusses a mechanistic matrix linking each light modality to specific molecular targets and age-associated outcomes. Light-based platforms, when combined with genetic and pharmacological tools, are poised to accelerate discovery in aging by enabling high-throughput and organelle-specific perturbation of aging biology. These approaches could ultimately redefine how we experimentally dissect and potentially modulate the aging process either by rapidly recapitulating aging features for study or by revealing leverage points to slow aging.Keywords: photoaging, natural aging, UV light, photodynamic, optogenetic, aging model

Introduction

Aging is the principal risk factor underlying a spectrum of chronic diseases, including cardiovascular dysfunction, neurodegeneration, cancer, and ocular pathologies (Figure 1A) such as age-related macular degeneration (AMD),1,2,3 neurodegenerative diseases,4 and metabolic dysfunction.5 At the cellular level, aging is characterized by hallmarks such as genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alterations, loss of proteostasis, and the accumulation of senescent cells.6 Nonetheless, research elucidating the onset of aging and developing effective interventions is significantly hampered by two major hurdles: the immense financial and temporal burden of prolonged aging studies in young mice7 and the complexities of reliably reversing the biological clock in induced pluripotent cells8 (Figure 1B). Moreover, the challenges are exacerbated by the unique, non-linear aging phases that occur in different organs over time, a pattern observed in both preclinical animal models9,10 and humans11,12; likewise, species-level differences in telomere shortening also track with lifespan (Figure 1C). While intrinsic aging unfolds over time via genetically programmed and metabolically regulated pathways, increasing evidence shows that chronic environmental exposures can accelerate aging extrinsically. In particular, ultraviolet (UV) and high-energy visible (HEV) light are potent external aging stimuli, giving rise to the phenomenon of photoaging classically observed in sun-exposed skin and eyes.13,14,15 This offers a rapid, economical, and spatiotemporally adaptable approach to model aging (Figure 1D).

The photobiology of aging

Biological molecules inherently absorb light across the electromagnetic spectrum (UV to visible and beyond), with varying sensitivities. Upon excitation, photon energy transfers to biological tissue, underpinning numerous biological processes including vision, photodynamic cancer therapy,17 cellular or medical imaging,18 energy production,19 and vitamin D synthesis.20,21 Photon energy is inversely proportional to wavelength. This is especially so in the visible spectrum, where shorter-wavelength photons carry higher energy and therefore impart a stronger effect on cellular tissue with high absorption coefficients.22 Conversely, longer wavelengths tend to penetrate deeper into the tissue.23 However, this pattern is not always linear. Hence, the susceptibility of premature photoaging due to the deterioration of biological tissues resulting from prolonged cumulative exposure to radiation is most prominently observed in regions exposed to the sun, such as skin and the eyes.14,24,25 It is mediated by complex cascades, including reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, DNA damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, and the induction of cellular senescence.14,24,26,27,28 Unlike intrinsic aging, which occurs gradually over decades, photoaging can be rapidly induced and modulated, providing a unique opportunity to interrogate aging mechanisms within accelerated experimental time frames. Indeed, recent advances have transformed light from merely an environmental stressor into a powerful experimental modality. For instance, photodynamic therapy (PDT) employs light-activated compounds (photosensitizers) to generate bursts of ROSs in specific subcellular compartments, while optogenetics enables light-triggered modulation of cellular activities via genetically encoded photo-switchable proteins. Both approaches have been successfully used to induce aging-relevant phenotypes in a controlled manner, engaging key features such as oxidative stress, lysosomal dysfunction, mitochondrial damage, and pro-inflammatory signaling.29,30,31,32,33,34,35

We posit that targeted light-based interventions can compress or expand the timescale of aging processes in experimental systems, effectively “dilating” the aging clock. In other words, we define “dilating the aging clock” as the ability to artificially modulate the pace at which aging hallmarks develop, by either potentially accelerating aging features or decelerating them for therapeutic exploration. Figure 1 provides a conceptual overview linking existing light-based techniques to the hallmarks of aging they most directly influence. However, it must be noted that, while light-based models can trigger specific aging-related changes, they do not capture the full systemic, multifactorial physiology of organismal aging.

Comparison of light-based aging models

Light-based approaches that accelerate aging fall into three categories: direct light exposure, PDT, and optogenetics. These modalities differ in their mechanism of action, precision, and controllability. Direct UV/blue light induces diffuse, non-specific damage with minimal control (all exposed cells accumulate DNA damage and oxidative stress).14,26,28,36,37 In contrast, PDT uses targeted photosensitizers to generate ROSs in specific locations, allowing increased precision (targeted organelle or tissue with oxidative damage).30,38,39,40 Optogenetics provide the highest precision and amenability in which light-responsive proteins modulate signaling pathways or organelles with pinpoint accuracy, and some effects are often reversible by removing the light source.33,41,42 Table 1 presents a mechanistic summary of the aforementioned three modalities. The hierarchy (optogenetic > PDT > direct light) reflects increasing ability to control where, when, and which aging features are induced. Each modality also carries different practical considerations for experimental use, which we discuss in later sections.

UV-light-induced photoaging

The deleterious effects of high-energy light, encompassing both natural sunlight and synthetic illumination, are well documented in the acceleration of cellular and tissue aging. In particular, UV is a well-established extrinsic aging factor. Chronic UV exposure leads to cumulative DNA mutations, telomere shortening, extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation in skin, and a pro-inflammatory senescent cell phenotype in exposed areas44 (Figure 2A). These changes underlie the familiar clinical signs of photoaged skin (wrinkles, laxity, pigmented lesions) and contribute to age-related ocular conditions such as cataracts and retinal damage. Experiments have shown that UV-treated human skin cells exhibit classic markers of cellular aging—for instance, γH2AX foci indicating DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), which correlate with the onset of senescence.45 In particular, when such DSB foci are present at chromosomal ends (co-localizing with telomeric DNA), they form telomere dysfunction-induced foci (TIFs), which trigger a permanent growth arrest via the ATM/p53 pathway—a mechanism underlying replicative senescence.27 In prolonged sun-exposed human skin, dermal fibroblasts have been shown to harbor significantly shorter telomeres than in protected skin, linking UV to telomere attrition in vivo.46 The hypersensitivity of human telomeres to UV light stems from their repetitive 5′TTAGGG/CCCTAA3′ sequences.47 These sequences are particularly susceptible to formation of photo-byproducts such as cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers (CPDs) and pyrimidine(6–4) pyrimidone photoproducts (6–4PP),48,49 which in turn impair the crucial role of telomeres in maintaining chromosomal stability. The consequences of this damage can be localized to tissue dysfunction or extended globally to affect the organism’s overall lifespan.16 However, the direct impact of UV on telomeres in the context of accelerated aging may not be straightforward and is potentially compounded by factors such as ROS or genomic methylation drift. Therefore, more precise interventions such as PDT could facilitate this. Additionally, UV instigates long-lasting epigenetic changes, with even acute exposure possessing capabilities of remodeling the epigenome, causing widespread histone modification shifts and aberrant DNA methylation that mirror those seen in intrinsically aged cells.50 Genome-wide analyses of chronically sun-exposed skin have revealed large domains of DNA hypomethylation overlapping with age-associated epigenetic drift and cancer-associated regions.24 These findings suggest that direct UV light inflicts immediate DNA damage and perturbs epigenetic regulation.

Another key feature of UV photoaging is ECM degradation in the skin through matrix-metalloproteinase (MMP) pathways. Kim et al. showed that UVB-mediated DNA damage activates an AhR-ATM-p38/JNK-SP1 axis, leading to transcriptional induction of MMP2, MMP11, and MMP9 in keratinocytes and human dermal fibroblasts. Genetic or pharmacological disruption of AhR, ATM, or p38/JNK blocks MMP induction and attenuates UVB-induced wrinkle formation in mice. The study established a direct link between UVB-triggered DNA damage, MMP upregulation, and photoaging.14 Budden et al. further demonstrated that chronic UV radiation increases MMP-1 expression, which cleaves type I collagen, resulting in progressive collagen fragmentation, reduced dermal collagen density, and structural remodeling of the ECM scaffold.28,44 Moreover, the effects of ECM damage following chronic UV exposure are well illustrated by a truck driver who had developed unilateral dermatoheliosis, whereby one side of his face presented with sun-induced hyperkeratosis, while the other side remained unaffected36 (Figure 2B). The application of a sunscreen with a long UVA filter prevents such alterations, directly linking UVA to metabolic and cellular stress pathways that drive tissue aging and dermal matrix degradation.36,51

In summary, UV light can rapidly induce multiple aging hallmarks in cells and tissues. Unlike the gradual damage in intrinsic aging, UV-driven aging changes can manifest after merely weeks of repeated exposure in laboratory settings, thus making it a convenient tool for accelerating aging in experimental models. However, UV exposure is indiscriminate, in which multicellular components are damaged simultaneously, thus requiring careful dosing in UV-based models to avoid acute cell death.

Visible light (blue) and accelerated aging

Beyond UV, the visible spectrum—especially blue light (400–500 nm), has emerged as another contributor to accelerated aging across diverse biological systems via the induction of oxidative stress and metabolic dysfunction. Song et al. demonstrated that blue-light exposure dramatically shortened lifespan and aggravated neurodegeneration in Drosophila melanogaster, particularly in aged flies.52 These effects, which extend beyond the absence of functional retinal photoreceptors, are attributed to decreased succinate dehydrogenase (mitochondrial respiratory complex II) activity and ATP production, resulting in energy deficiency and heightened susceptibility to environmental stress.53 Through untargeted metabolomics profiling, Yang et al. also revealed that chronic blue-light exposure disrupts neurotransmitter homeostasis in D. melanogaster.43 Coupled with succinate accumulation, observed decreases in glutamate, gamma-aminobutyric acid, and riboflavin all indicate widespread metabolic distress and redox imbalance. These studies have shown that blue-light exposure induces disruption in cellular redox balance and energy metabolism, driving progressive functional decline similar to that seen with biological aging.6

The aforementioned findings are corroborated by human and other mammalian studies. In murine B16F1 melanoma cells, exposure to blue light at physiologically relevant intensities induces ROS production, mitochondrial membrane depolarization, DNA strand breaks, and cell-cycle arrest. Correspondingly, the upregulation of pro-apoptotic genes such as Bax and p21 and downregulation of anti-apoptotic genes such as Bcl-2 and survivin were observed, signaling activation of mitochondrial and DNA-damage pathways.54 Upon p53 inhibition in ex vivo porcine retinal cultures, mitochondria swelling, membrane disruption, ATP depletion, and apoptosis associated with blue-light-triggered oxidative stress were mitigated.55 Together, these data underscore the mechanistic impact of blue light on aging phenotypes, particularly when combined with age-related pigments or stressors.

Serving as the body’s primary barrier, the skin is the most light-exposed organ and is thus exceptionally susceptible to blue-light-driven dermatoheliosis. Nakashima et al. utilized in vivo redox-sensitive GFP imaging to show that blue light, relative to lights of other wavelengths, has a higher capability to inflict mitochondrial oxidative stress in mouse and human skin.26 Notably, flavin, functioning as an endogenous photosensitizer, was implicated in preferential mitochondrial ROS generation. A study with human fibroblasts conducted by Mann et al. also demonstrated that blue-light exposure at doses equivalent to 1 h of summer sun leads to ROS accumulation and a reduction of key cutaneous antioxidants in vivo.37 Compared to traditional UV filters, supplementation with Nrf2-inducing antioxidants was found to confer greater protection against blue-light-induced ROS accumulation. At the molecular level, blue-light irradiation downregulates type I collagen expression by ablating TGF-β/SMAD and TGFBR2 signaling, with MMP1 expression concurrently upregulated via the JNK/AP-1 and EGFR pathways. These transcriptional changes, along with inhibited proliferation of human dermal fibroblasts, are not completely reversible by ROS scavenging, hence revealing an ROS-independent mechanism of photoaging.56 The extensive implications of light exposure on aging are further reinforced by investigations into circadian biology and systemic physiological disruptions. The retina and brain’s circadian centers respond to blue-light signals, and abnormal light exposure cycles can desynchronize internal clocks and lead to metabolic and inflammatory disturbances.57 In mice, chronic disruption of circadian rhythms caused by altered light cycles results in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, fibrosis, and spontaneous hepatocarcinogenesis. These outcomes are driven by persistent dysregulation of metabolic pathways controlled by nuclear receptors, as well as disruptions in bile acid levels and sympathetic signaling.58,59 Despite a focus on circadian aspects of light, they highlight how light exposure beyond the UV spectrum significantly alters metabolic, redox, and tissue homeostasis, ultimately contributing to aging and its related diseases.

Collectively, these studies outline a range of direct and indirect mechanisms by which blue and HEV light drive premature aging at cellular, tissue, and organismal levels. These span from acute or repetitive mitochondrial impairment, ROS production and energy depletion, to downstream initiations of apoptosis and loss of DNA and lysosomal integrity, eventually culminating in tissue deterioration. In addition, the phenotypes are specifically potentiated by age, metabolic susceptibility, or pre-existing pigmentary or mitochondrial disorders.

In summary, visible blue light, like UV, is an exogenous stress that can accelerate certain aging phenotypes. Its effects on skin and retinal cells share common threads with UV-induced aging but also involve unique aspects (e.g., circadian disruption, different depths of tissue penetration). Blue-light-induced aging models offer another tool to induce aging hallmarks with the advantage of being less genotoxic than UV but still capable of generating oxidative and proteostatic stress. Nonetheless, it requires carefully calibrated intensity and duration to mimic chronic low-grade stress as opposed to acute phototoxicity. While studies have yet to clearly differentiate these two modes of aging, doing so remains a critical objective for the field.

Photodynamic aging

An established clinical modality for treating cancers and skin pathologies, PDT induces ROS-mediated cellular damage via light-activated photosensitizers. This mechanism has also been productively repurposed as a research tool to model aging-related cellular damage with high specificity.60,61,62 PDT involves three components: a photosensitizer molecule, a specific light wavelength, and molecular oxygen.38 Upon light activation, the photosensitizer enters an excited state and initiates photochemical reactions that induce ROS-mediated cytotoxicity via two main mechanisms: the first involves electrons or hydrogen atoms being transferred from the light-activated photosensitizers to surrounding molecules, forming highly reactive unstable ROS species such as superoxide anions (O2−⋅), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and hydroxyl radicals (⋅OH), which propagate and cause oxidative damage; the second entails energy being transferred directly from excited triplet-state photosensitizers to molecular oxygen, yielding singlet oxygen (1O2)—a more stable but longer-lived ROS species that effects cellular damage over a more protracted duration63,64,65 (Figure 3A). By choosing photosensitizers that localize to specific organelles, researchers can induce spatiotemporally controlled oxidative stress in a targeted subcellular compartment such as mitochondria or lysosomes,29,32 thereby engaging hallmark pathways implicated in aging-related damage.6 Unlike the gradual and diffuse ROS accumulation seen in natural aging, PDT enables acute, localized oxidative insults based on photosensitizer distribution and light fluence.63,64 This exclusive capability allows PDT to be used as a powerful on-demand tool to preclinically model organelle-specific dysfunction in aging while offering a more refined approach to dissect the underlying cellular events governing this phenotype.

PDT-induced mitochondrial dysfunction to accelerate aging

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a well-recognized hallmark of aging.6,66 As cells age, mitochondria deteriorate—losing membrane potential, accumulating mtDNA mutations, and exhibiting imbalanced fission-fusion dynamics.6 Oxidative stress is a major contributor to mitochondrial impairment, and PDT has demonstrated an ability to recreate the key aspects of these age-related mitochondrial defects.39,40 Studies have shown that an acute burst of mitochondrial ROSs can mimic the functional decline of old mitochondria seen in senescent or energy-deprived cells, albeit induced on an accelerated timescale. Owari et al. demonstrated that, in prostate cancer cells, the photosensitizer, 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) is taken up by mitochondria and metabolized to protoporphyrin IX (PpIX), which, upon activation by 630-nm light, drives mitochondrial ROS production even under hypoxic conditions.32,39 The photosensitizers can also be engineered to target mitochondria67,68,69 (Figure 3B). The acute oxidative formation rapidly collapses the mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), leading to ATP depletion and intrinsic apoptosis. Complementary findings by Zhu et al. showed that PDT in THP-1 macrophages triggers a rapid ROS burst, opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), and mitochondrial depolarization, all of which contribute to activation of the mitochondrial pro-apoptotic cascade with intra-mitochondrial translocation of Bax, release of cytochrome c, and cleavage of pro-apoptotic caspases.40 PDT also encourages mitochondrial fragmentation by upregulating Drp1 and Fis1 expression and downregulating Mitofusin-1/2 expression (Figure 3C), which promotes fission and inhibits fusion, respectively. This phenomenon is also observed in senescence, albeit at a much slower rate in the process of natural aging.70 An important compounding factor is the tumor suppressor p53. Oxidative PDT stress can activate a p53-Drp1 feedback loop: Drp1 has been shown to physically interact with p53 and is required for translocation of p53 to mitochondria under oxidative stress, the latter of which acts on-site as a pro-apoptotic factor. This interaction destabilizes the mitochondrial outer membrane, thereby amplifying mitochondrial membrane permeabilization and dysfunction.71 As already demonstrated in inflammatory stress models, this pathway provides a general ROS-responsive mechanism by which acute oxidative bursts, such as those induced by PDT, may similarly reinforce mitochondrial damage.

Mechanistic cascade: From PDT-induced ROSs to senescence via mitophagy failure

Interestingly, using a sub-lethal PDT dose that targets mitochondria can shift mitochondrial physiology toward a state of sustained dysfunction rather than immediate death.72 Such cells enter a growth-arrested state and begin to secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines—a phenotype resembling the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) seen in aging. Mechanistically, chronic oxidative damage from repeated or mild PDT can impair the quality-control systems of the organelle. Mitophagy decline is one of such outcomes: sustained ROS stress is known to dampen PINK1/Parkin signaling and reduce Parkin recruitment, impairing mitochondrial autophagy, causing defective mitochondria to accumulate73,74 (Figure 4A). In parallel, reduced mitophagy can amplify inflammatory signaling as mitophagy normally restrains inflammasome activation and cytokine production.75 Consistently, in normal aging and in senescence, researchers have observed a shutdown of PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, leading to a buildup of swollen, inefficient mitochondria that produce excess ROSs.73 Consistently, persistent mitochondrial ROSs from successive PDT treatments would be expected to exacerbate this effect as more damaged mitochondria linger, feeding into a cycle of oxidative stress and cellular senescence (Figure 4B). Alongside mitophagy impairment, repeated photo-oxidative stress can also disrupt the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt), reducing the ability of the cell to cope with misfolded proteins in mitochondria (Figure 4C). This ultimately results in a senescent cell phenotype with a pro-inflammatory secretome. In support of this cascade, studies have shown that eliminating dysfunctional mitochondria from senescent cells (e.g., via forced Parkin overexpression) significantly blunts the markers of senescence and the production of SASP inflammatory factors70 (Figure 4D).

PDT-induced lysosomal dysfunction to accelerate aging

Apart from introducing mitochondrial dysfunction, PDT-induced ROSs also recapitulate the aging process by disrupting the lysosome-autophagosome degradation system. During natural aging, lysosomes exhibit decreased acidification, reduced hydrolase activity, and impaired autophagosome-lysosome fusion. One mechanism through which PDT induces lysosomal dysfunction is via ROS-mediated structural damage to the lysosomal membranes by destabilizing lysosomal membranes and impairing catabolic efficiency, contributing to lysosomal dysfunction and autophagic flux failure. Even at sub-lethal ROS levels, the oxidation of lipids and proteins impairs lysosomal hydrolase activity and promotes the accumulation of non-degradable aggregates such as lipofuscin—a histopathological hallmark of aging.76 Furthermore, Xu et al. showed that, in RPE cells, the age-associated fluorophore A2E synergizes with blue light to induce lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP)30 (Figure 4E). LMP triggers leakage of protons and cathepsins—particularly cathepsins B and D—into the cytosol, activating downstream apoptotic and senescence pathways.32,62,76 Cathepsins B and D released from permeabilized lysosomes can interact with mitochondria by cleaving full-length BH3-interacting domain death agonist (BID) to its active truncated form (tBID), leading to cytochrome c release and caspase activation.77 Additionally, JNK1 (MAPK8) is activated, which modulates Bcl-2-Bax complex and can influence lysosomal stability, thereby promoting LMP-associated cell death.78,79 An overall reduction in cathepsin activity is hence reminiscent of natural aging as autophagy-delivered substrates undergo incomplete degradation and accumulate within the autolysosome, forming dense, compacted lipofuscin that crosslinks with ROSs.76

Beyond inflicting structural damage, PDT-induced ROSs also impair lysosomal function by repressing autophagy gene expression and inhibiting lysosomal biogenesis. The transcription factors TFEB and TFE are master regulators of autophagy and lysosomal biogenesis, and their cytosol-nuclear translocation is in turn modulated by the central regulator of mammalian metabolism mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) complex I.80 In tandem with aging, TFEB/TFE3 activity diminishes in response to increasing mTORC1-dependent phosphorylation, which limits their nuclear residence and promotes their cytosolic retention.81,82 Autophagic processes are integral for maintenance of cellular homeostasis and have a cytoprotective role, especially in the setting of any concomitant mitochondrial insults.83 Kessel et al. have reported that this protective function is abolished when lysosomes are the primary PDT target, suggesting a block in autophagic flux due to compromised degradative capacity.83 In support of this finding, a more recent lysosome-specific PDT platform designed to enhance ferroptosis-mediated immunotherapy demonstrated inhibition of protective autophagy upon light activation,84 further reinforcing the concept that lysosomal ROS stress can mimic key hallmarks of lysosomal aging, including impaired autophagic degradation, defective lysosome regeneration, and the collapse of nutrient-sensing pathways. Studies further show that endosome-lysosome fusion machinery, including RAB7, SNAREs, and ESCRT proteins (e.g., CHMP2B in FTD), is implicated in lysosomal aging and neurodegenerative pathology.76

Some PDT models could rely on the administration of exogenous photosensitizers, which exert light-independent metabolic or cytotoxic effects even in the absence of irradiation. These photosensitizers have been shown to exhibit selective dark cytotoxicity through mechanisms such as mitochondrial or lysosomal perturbation, independent of photochemical activation.85 This highlights that the possibility of photosensitizer exposure alone may alter baseline cellular physiology.

Optogenetics-driven premature aging

Optogenetics is a biological toolbox that utilizes light-sensitive proteins (opsins) to control cellular function or signaling pathways (Figure 5A). While optogenetics for rapid aging is relatively unexplored, the method has been used for cell-specific, precise generation of ROSs, control of cell death effectors, lysosome functions, mitochondria fission, and protein shuttling—all of which are associated with aging. Unlike direct light or PDT, which rely on broad photochemical effects, optogenetics allows fusion of these light-responsive domains to proteins of interest or signaling molecules, thereby allowing scientists to turn pathways on or off at will with high spatiotemporal resolution. Optogenetics has been revolutionary in neuroscience and biology86,87,88,89; in aging research, its power lies in dissecting causal sequences and timing of aging processes.

One application of optogenetics is to induce localized ROS production inside cells, similar to PDT but genetically encoded. For instance, Formella et al. induced telomeric DNA damage and toxicity by light activation of an ROS-producing gene, KillerRed, in zebrafish spinal motor neurons. ROS levels in KillerRed-expressing spinal motor neurons increased after 2 h of green-light illumination (535–575 nm, 80 mW/cm2) on a fluorescence microscope setup.33 The ROSs generated by KillerRed caused axonal blebbing at 1.25 h, cell shrinkage at 5 h, and complete cell loss at 8 h post illumination. Interestingly, when tagged with telomere repeat binding factor 1 (TRF1), KillerRed activation-induced damage was found to be limited to telomeric DNA, leading to telomere shortening without widespread genomic damage.90 As KillerRed activity depends on green-light intensity and duration, the amount of ROSs generated, and their subsequent cytotoxicity can be finely manipulated to induce aging phenotype by adjusting illumination parameters.

Optogenetic-induced lysosomal dysfunction to accelerate aging

Elsewhere, the effects of light-sensitive channel proteins (e.g., NpHR3.0, ArchT, and ChR2) on lysosomal function were investigated. Whole-lysosome patch clamp recording revealed that brief light stimulation can acutely alter lysosomal membrane potential—illumination with 589-nm light depolarized lyso-NpHR3.0 or lyso-ArchT, while 470-nm light hyperpolarized lyso-ChR2.34 Further investigation revealed that the activation of lyso-NpHR3.0 increases lysosomal pH and reduces degradation of a fluorescent autophagy substrate (DQ-Red BSA), indicating a reduction in lysosomal degradative activity. In a separate study, Perny et al. introduced a D156A point mutation of ChR2 and found that prolonged blue-light activation of this mutant channel protein led to increased apoptosis in human melanoma cells91 (Figure 5B). The authors postulated that the apoptosis was due to cytosolic Ca2+ overabundance, causing Ca2+ dysregulation in both the mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum (ER). This is consistent with natural aging in which mitochondrial Ca2+ overload was observed in senescent cells.92

Furthermore, it has also been shown that another important lysosome-mitochondria interaction—mitochondrial fission—can be enhanced using optogenetics-controlled protein-protein interactions. Lysosomes expressing LAMP-mCherry-CRY2 and mitochondria expressing TOM20-CIB1-GFP were engineered in cells to dimerize upon blue-light stimulation (488–500 nm, 300 μW/cm2). Qiu et al. demonstrated that, upon illumination, the frequency of mitochondria-lysosome contacts (MLCs) increased from 26.46% in the dark to 70.83% under blue light, and this surge in contacts precipitated mitochondrial fission events41 (Figure 5C). Excessive fission without balanced fusion, as can be optically induced, recapitulates the fragmented mitochondrial network characteristic of aged or senescent cells. The resultant mitochondria are smaller and often less efficient, paralleling the mitochondrial morphology and functional decline seen in cellular aging. Collectively, these examples illustrate the feasibility of using precise, organelle-specific optogenetic interventions to recapitulate aging phenotypes. Together, these systems enable precise and reversible control of mitochondrial redox balance, apoptosis, and organelle interactions, offering experimental models of aging phenotypes.

Optogenetic-induced dysregulation of cells

Optogenetics also excels at controlling signaling pathways and cell fates relevant to aging. Aging often dysregulates cell death: insufficient clearance of damaged or dysfunctional cells can lead to senescent cell accumulation, while excessive cell death can cause tissue atrophy.93 To probe these processes with precision, optogenetic systems have been created to trigger specific forms of programmed cell death. He et al. found that blue-light illumination of mCh-CRY2-RIPK1 fusion protein leads to the oligomerization of RIPK1, which initiates a downstream reaction that leads to necroptosis.94 The same authors subsequently developed photo-tunable systems LiPOP1 and LiPOP2, which are capable of inducing necroptosis and pyroptosis, respectively. LiPOP1 utilizes Ca2+ influx to induce necroptosis and cell death, while LiPOP2 harnesses a light-activated gasdermin D (GSDMD) “uncaging” mechanism to conditionally release the 30-kDa N-terminal fragment (GSDMD-NT, also known as p30) that leads to pyroptosis. When illuminated with blue light, the percentage of cells double marked by SYTOX Blue and Annexin V increased dramatically, demonstrating a rapid rise in cell death.

Another class of optogenetics tools that regulate cell death effectors is known as optoCDEs, which have been designed to induce light-activated apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis accordingly.95 One of these tools—optoCaspase-1—can elicit graded degrees of cellular pyroptosis when stimulated with incremental doses of light. In human macrophage-like cell lines, a pulsed illumination with low-intensity 488-nm light induced sub-lethal activation of optoCaspase-1, with approximately 50% of cells undergoing pyroptosis, as indicated by nuclear DRAQ7 staining. A second light treatment increased the fraction of pyroptotic cells to 75%.95 Although light activation of optoCaspase-1 triggers pyroptotic cell death, the process could be partially reversed within 30–40 min post illumination in a fraction of cells, highlighting the reversible and flexible nature of this system. By exploiting this adjustability, optogenetic control of cell-death processes can be precisely regulated to either accelerate aging phenotypes, through increased cell loss and inflammatory cell death, or mitigate aspects of aging in experimental models.

Optogenetics has also been used to regulate the localization and activity of nuclear proteins relevant to aging. Niopek et al. developed a light-inducible nuclear export system (LEXY), which makes use of a genetically encoded protein tag to optically inhibit the nuclear export receptor CRM1.42 Using LEXY, Niopek et al. established precise optical control of p53 localization and transcriptional activity in p53-deficient H1299 human lung carcinoma cells.42 Light activation of LEXY effectively inhibited CRM1, increasing nuclear retention of p53 and thereby enhancing transcriptional activity of p53. This was evidenced by a 3-fold decrease in p21 expression, a downstream consequence of heightened p53 activity.42 The advent of this technique suggests a myriad of possible applications, and such an effective system can definitely be exploited for the regulation of aging-related nuclear factors or proteins that can artificially quicken or slow down the process in experimental models. However, because optogenetic models require genetic expression of exogenous ion channels, opsin expression and activation may perturb the baseline physiological states. Octeau et al. evidence that optogenetic excitation can induce transient yet biological changes in extracellular potassium levels, highlighting that optogenetic tools can alter local tissue physiology beyond the intended effects.96

Biological interpretation of light-induced aging models

A key conceptual distinction is that light-induced cellular remodeling originates from an exogenous photochemical insult, whereas natural organismal aging reflects lifelong, systemic, and multifactorial decline. Nevertheless, when light exposure is delivered at sub-lethal intensities and repeated over short intervals, all three light modalities can engage downstream biological processes that mimic the aging hallmarks. Light does not “replace” natural aging; instead, it serves as a precision accelerator of specific hallmarks within defined cellular contexts.

It is critical to distinguish aging-specific mechanisms from the general phototoxic stress response. High-dose single–pulse illumination can cause acute injury or apoptosis that mimics tissue thinning, but this is more traumatic than aging.40 In contrast, aging-relevant modeling can be achieved with repeated sub-lethal or low-dose exposure that induces chronic ROS leakage, persistent DNA-damage checkpoint activation, mitochondrial stress, and stable entry into senescence while maintaining overall tissue viability.97 These conditions allow cellular damage to accumulate over time and better mimic the slow degradation observed in natural aging. Furthermore, epigenetic age measurements provide the most direct discriminator between acute phototoxic stress and aging-like state. While phototoxic injury can transiently activate stress pathways and DNA-damage signaling, an aging-like model is expected to produce durable remodeling of the epigenome. DNA methylation-based clocks quantify biological aging in ways that extend beyond chronological time and are linked to functional decline and risk, including morbidity and mortality prediction.98,99 Acute phototoxic stress responses alone are not sufficient evidence of accelerated aging, because biological age signatures can shift dynamically. Systemic stressors have been shown to increase biological age and subsequently return toward baseline upon recovery,100 emphasizing the importance of persistent rather than short-lived stress activation. In addition to DNA methylation clocks, transcriptomic aging clock— often referred to as 'RNA age'—provides a complementary measure of biological aging by quantifying age-associated changes in gene expression.101,102 Single-cell transcriptomic aging clocks trained on RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) profiles have been shown to accurately predict chronological or biological age in mouse.102

Together, applying multiple clocks in light-induced aging models provides stringent discrimination to validate persistent aging-like molecular conditions while remaining compatible with traditional hallmark biomarkers and functional assays.

Critical differences between natural aging

While light-induced aging reproduces several cell-intrinsic molecular features of aging, it does not fully recapitulate the immune surveillance, senescent cell clearance dynamics, or systemic inflammatory characteristics of natural aging.6 Many PDT- and optogenetic-based models are conducted in vitro or in immune-deficient systems,39,103 where interaction with innate and adaptive immune systems is absent. Although light-induced senescence activates SASP regulators and induces pro-inflammatory cytokines, the composition, persistence, and immune engagement of the SASP may differ from that observed in natural aging. Light-induced senescent cells exhibit a paracrine SASP capable of propagating tissue dysfunction locally, including telomere damage in neighboring cells,97 while, in natural aging, chronic immune surveillance and clearance dynamics play a central role in determining senescent cell persistence, with impaired cytotoxic immune function leading to accumulation of senescent cells and sustained inflammatory signaling at the organismal level.104 Thus, light-based models are best interpreted as tools to induce and study cell-intrinsic senescence programs and localized SASP signaling. Experimental applications and practical constraints of light-based aging models

Compared to other aging models, such as genetic, pharmacological, or chronological aging models, light-based aging models are uniquely suited for interrogating temporal causality, spatial specificity, and threshold effects of aging hallmarks. Genetic models enable sustained perturbation of defined pathways across the lifespan but offer limited temporal control and are often confounded by compensatory transcriptions triggered by mutations.105 Pharmacological approaches provide partial reversibility and variation of the dose but typically lack cell-type or subcellular precision and can produce off-target pathway effects.106,107 Chronological aging models are the most faithful representation of organismal complexity and integrative physiology yet are inherently slow (requiring years), variable, and poorly suited for mechanistic dissection of early or transient aging events.107,108

In contrast, light-based modalities allow researchers to precisely control when, where, and to what extent aging-related stressors are applied. They can allow rapid induction of defined hallmarks without the need for lifelong perturbation. This is valuable for testing causality, dissecting early molecular events, and probing dose-response relationships that distinguish adaptive stress responses from pathological aging. Collectively, light-based modalities are best viewed as precision accelerators of selected hallmarks and mechanistic insight, which can be validated in genetic or organismal aging models.

However, these advantages are accompanied by important practical constraints that limit interpretability and reproducibility. A major challenge is the lack of standardization in light parameters across studies. Different experiments employ widely varying wavelengths, intensities, exposure durations, and treatment schedules, making it difficult to compare results or replicate experiments in another lab setting. For example, blue light can range from 400 to 470 nm in various setups,109,110 and dose reporting is inconsistent with J/cm2 and mW/cm2 for a given time.111,112 Establishing standard protocols for photoaging studies, including the common dose metrics, calibration methods, and reporting criteria, would greatly enhance reproducibility and the translational relevance of results. Similarly, tissue optical properties differ; calibration of in vitro light doses to physiologically relevant in vivo exposure levels is needed.

Another limitation is that light alone may not fully replicate multifactorial aging processes. Future aging models should apply hybrid approaches that combine light with other cellular stressors, which could replicate multifaceted aging cascades more accurately. For instance, integrating PDT with proteotoxic agents, such as misfolded proteins or proteasome inhibitors, could effectively mimic the progressive protein aggregation and impaired proteostasis observed during aging and neurodegenerative diseases.113,114

As with all light-based modalities, effective tissue penetration is limited by absorption and scattering, although the skin and eyes are more accessible targets.115 Effective light-based modeling of systemic aging, particularly within internal organs, consequently demands either invasive light-delivery techniques (such as fiber optics), the utilization of less scattered near-infrared (NIR) spectra,116 or the deployment of implantable wireless light delivery systems, whether electronic117,118,119 or non-electronic in nature.23,40,120,121,122 Despite these limitations, direct light remains a fast, low-cost aging model, ideal for high-throughput screening.

Future directions

Advancements in future light-induced aging models should prioritize enhancing tissue-penetration capabilities and minimizing device-related invasiveness. One promising strategy is the development of NIR-based photoaging. Upconversion nanoparticles (UCNPs) absorb low-energy NIR light and emit higher-energy photons to activate conventional photosensitizers.120 This allows light stimulation at greater depths while concomitantly limiting photodamage to overlying tissues.121,123 Similarly, NIR-II (1,000–1,700 nm) excitation fluorophores may reduce light scattering and further enhance tissue penetration, but current applications are primarily limited to short-term or proof-of-concept studies.124 Next-generation implantable devices, such as wireless micro-LEDs and biodegradable optical waveguides, are also being developed to improve biocompatibility and device-related morbidity in optogenetic applications.122,123 These platforms enable long-term and precise illumination of deep tissues such as the brain or retina, thus expanding light-induced aging models beyond superficial layers. Key challenges include limited long-term stability, immune and fibrotic responses to implanted materials, and power-delivery constraints.125 Scalability is also a concern, as many devices tailored for small animal models may not readily translate to larger organisms or lifespan studies. For a larger illumination area, large-area NIR-responsive implants have been synthesized into silicone implants,126 although the biocompatibility of the implant itself, when used for long-term in vivo lifespan for years, may interfere with the interpretation.127 Another promising direction involves decoupling ROS production from oxygen dependency. The development of type I photosensitizers, which generate ROSs via electron transfer rather than singlet oxygen, facilitates reliable induction of oxidative stress even in hypoxic environments. Furthermore, the development of genetically encoded photosensitizers—such as engineered fluorescent proteins (e.g., KillerRed, TagRFP) and flavin-binding proteins (e.g., miniSOG, Pp2FbFPL30M)—offers an appealing avenue for subcellular targeting of ROS generation.128 These constructs can be delivered via gene-therapy techniques and equipped with targeting motifs to particular organelles or cell types, providing the possibility of combining PDT with genetic specificity.129

Conclusion

In summary, light-based modalities—direct light exposure, PDT, and optogenetics—constitute a powerful technology trio for accelerating and dissecting the specific hallmarks of aging with unmatched spatial-temporal and mechanistic precision. By enabling sub-lethal, localized, and repeatable perturbation of defined cellular pathways, light-based models allow the dissection of early aging events, organelle dysfunction, and senescence events that are difficult to isolate using chronological, genetic, or pharmacological approaches alone.
