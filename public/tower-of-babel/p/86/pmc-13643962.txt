Developing cultural competence among health professions educators through an international faculty development program: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Sisi Li, Xunchen Liao, Xi Chen, Chunhui Zhang, Zebin Qiu, Qin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCultural competence is important for health professions educators working in diverse educational and healthcare contexts. However, faculty development programs often focus more on instructional techniques than on educators’ reflective, communicative, and adaptive capacities for culturally diverse learning environments. This study examined participants’ self-perceived development of cultural competence and learning experiences within a two-year international faculty development program.MethodsA convergent mixed-methods study was conducted with 52 health professions educators from 30 universities across three countries. The quantitative component used a single-time-point retrospective pre–post self-assessment survey to examine participants’ perceived changes in cultural competence. Paired-sample t-tests and Cohen’s dz were used to assess statistical significance and effect sizes. The qualitative component included semi-structured interviews with 13 participants, analyzed using thematic analysis. Quantitative and qualitative findings were integrated to examine convergence, complementarity, and discrepancy.ResultsParticipants reported statistically significant self-perceived gains in 8 of the 13 measured items. The largest effect was observed for adjustment of cultural understanding, t(51) = − 5.578, p < .001, Cohen’s dz = 0.77. A moderate effect was also observed for adjustment of communication strategies, t(51) = − 4.071, p < .001, dz = 0.56. Small-to-moderate effects were observed for cultural self-awareness, knowledge adjustment, social confidence, persistence, and understanding of cultural value frameworks. No statistically significant perceived changes were observed in stress coping, p = .142; immediate cultural sensing, p = .302; or changing behavior to suit different cultural situations, p = .192. Qualitative findings suggested that participants’ learning was supported by three interrelated processes: Contextualized Construction, Collaborative Learning, and Dynamic Co-Creation. These processes informed the CoLearn Framework and a developmental pathway from Awareness to Understanding and, for some participants, toward aspects of Adaptation.ConclusionsThe program was associated with participants’ self-perceived gains in selected dimensions of cultural competence, particularly cultural understanding and communication strategy adjustment. The CoLearn Framework offers an explanatory model for designing reflective, relational, and situated faculty development. More complex dimensions, including stress coping and stable behavioral adaptation, may require immersive practice, institutional support, and longer-term follow-up.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09905-1.Keywords: Cultural competence, Faculty development, International faculty development, Health professions education, Reflective practice, Mixed methods

Introduction

The growing diversity of patient populations and the globalization of healthcare pose new challenges for health professions educators. Cultural competence (CC)—understood as the ongoing integration of cultural humility, responsiveness, and advocacy into professional practice—is widely recognized as a core capability for fostering inclusive learning environments and addressing health inequities [1, 2]. As a multidimensional construct, CC encompasses the awareness, knowledge, skills, and behaviors that enable individuals to engage effectively across cultural boundaries and promote equitable healthcare delivery [3, 4]. In this study, we use “cultural competence” as the overarching term, while recognizing that it overlaps with related concepts such as intercultural competence and cross-cultural competence. The focus is on educators’ reflective, communicative, and adaptive capacity to work across culturally diverse educational contexts.

Historically, the concept of CC emerged to address racial disparities in health outcomes. Cross et al. (1989) defined it as “a set of congruent behaviors, attitudes, and policies that enable professionals to work effectively in cross-cultural situations.” [5] The term combines the adjective “cultural,” which situates clinical skills within patients’ social and contextual realities, with “competence,” which emphasizes the ability to provide effective care across those contexts [6]. Over time, CC has evolved from a behavioral framework to an umbrella concept integrating cultural humility, critical consciousness, and responsiveness to structural determinants of health. Consequently, CC training should not be confined to knowledge acquisition but should cultivate reflective practice, intuitive and critical reasoning, and advocacy for equity. In this sense, CC represents an ongoing developmental process—one grounded in self-reflection, learning from patients and communities, and enacting systemic change to reduce health disparities [1, 3, 6].

Despite increased awareness of its importance, significant gaps persist in developing CC among health professions educators. Studies suggest that while many educators possess basic cultural awareness, they often lack the ability to address structural inequities or adapt teaching to meet the needs of diverse learners [7, 8]. In addition, standardized frameworks for CC training remain limited, leading to fragmented and inconsistent educational practices [9]. When poorly integrated into curricula, CC is sometimes perceived by learners as a form of political correctness rather than a professional and clinical necessity [10, 11].

Conceptual frameworks are essential for guiding the intentional development of intercultural capabilities. Bennett’s (1993) Developmental Model of Intercultural Sensitivity (DMIS) offers a valuable framework for understanding the progression of intercultural competence. The DMIS outlines six stages, transitioning from ethnocentrism (denial, defense, minimization) to ethnorelativism (acceptance, adaptation, integration), and parallels the growth of cultural intelligence from basic awareness to strategic adaptability [12]. The DMIS provides a developmental lens for understanding how cultural sensitivity evolves and underscores the need for reflective and adaptive learning processes. Importantly, it aligns with the principles of cultural humility, emphasizing lifelong learning and critical self-reflection in recognizing and navigating power differentials.

Faculty Development Programs (FDPs) have been identified as strategic interventions to bridge competency gaps in health professions education. However, many existing FDPs prioritize instructional techniques and research productivity over intercultural understanding and behavioral adaptability [13, 14]. There is growing consensus that effective FDPs should integrate experiential learning, reflection, and transformation to foster intercultural competence in teaching and communication [7, 15].

This study addresses these needs by examining a two-year International Faculty Development Program (IFDP) affiliated with the Foundation for Advancement of International Medical Education and Research (FAIMER) and hosted by a regional institute in China [16]. The program was informed by developmental, experiential, and reflective approaches to cultural competence development, including Bennett’s Developmental Model of Intercultural Sensitivity. Rather than treating cultural competence as a fixed set of knowledge or skills, the program emphasized reflection, dialogue, contextualized learning, and collaborative engagement across cultural and institutional boundaries. This study examined participants’ self-perceived development of cultural competence and their learning experiences within the IFDP. In addition to evaluating perceived changes in selected dimensions of cultural competence, the study sought to generate an explanatory framework for understanding how cultural competence may be fostered through international faculty development.

Methods

Study design

This study used a convergent parallel mixed-methods design [17] to examine participants’ perceived development of cultural competence and their learning experiences in an international faculty development program. The quantitative component used a single-time-point retrospective pre–post self-assessment survey. At the end of the program, participants rated each cultural competence item twice: first by retrospectively assessing their perceived competence before participation in the program (“Before FAIMER”), and then by assessing their current perceived competence at the end of the program (“Today”). The qualitative component consisted of semi-structured interviews conducted after program completion.

The retrospective pre–post approach was selected because participants’ understanding of cultural competence was expected to evolve during the program, creating the possibility of response-shift bias. In educational program evaluation, this design has been used when learners’ internal frame of reference may change as a result of the intervention [18]. In this study, the retrospective design allowed participants to reassess their prior competence using their post-program understanding of cultural competence. However, because both ratings were collected at a single time point, the quantitative findings were interpreted as self-perceived changes in competence rather than objective measures of competence development.

Quantitative and qualitative data were analyzed separately and then integrated during interpretation to compare patterns of convergence, complementarity, and discrepancy between survey results and interview findings.

Context and setting

The study was conducted within a FAIMER-affiliated IFDP hosted by a regional institute in China. Although most participants were based in China, the program followed an international faculty development model and included participants from East and Southeast Asia. English was used as the primary language of instruction to facilitate cross-cultural communication and participation within an international learning community.

The IFDP aimed to strengthen participants’ competencies in medical education, educational research, leadership, project management, and cultural competence. Cultural competence was addressed both as an explicit learning focus and as an embedded component of collaborative learning activities, group projects, and intercultural communication within the program.

Participants and recruitment

A total of 52 participants, including clinicians, educators, and academic staff, were enrolled in the program. Participants came from 30 universities across three countries. Convenience sampling was used for the quantitative component: all program participants were invited to complete the post-program retrospective pre–post self-assessment survey.

For the qualitative component, interviewees were recruited through voluntary sign-up, supplemented by purposive sampling to ensure variation in gender, geographic background, professional role, institutional context, and experience with international education. Thirteen participants were interviewed. The interview sample included clinical teachers, basic science faculty members, and educational administrators.

Intervention: Two-year international faculty development program

The intervention was a two-year international faculty development program designed to support reflective, intercultural, and collaborative learning among health professions educators. The program combined theoretical instruction, experiential learning, structured peer collaboration, and project-based learning. English was used as the main language of instruction and communication. Participants worked in mixed groups throughout the program, allowing them to engage with peers from different cultural, disciplinary, and institutional backgrounds.

The program included three key components.

First, theoretical modules introduced participants to foundational concepts related to cultural competence, implicit bias, intercultural communication, inclusive education, and the role of context in health professions education. These sessions provided conceptual grounding for understanding cultural difference and applying culturally responsive approaches in teaching, assessment, feedback, and professional communication.

Second, practical workshops used small-group, case-based discussions to simulate cross-cultural educational and clinical situations. Groups typically included six to eight participants. These workshops encouraged participants to apply theoretical concepts, examine assumptions, exchange perspectives, practice adaptive communication strategies, and consider how cultural values may shape educational and clinical decision-making.

Third, the program fosustained peer learning and collaborative project work. Participants from different cultural and institutional backgrounds worked together over time, engaged in structured reflection, and co-developed educational products or project outputs. These activities were intended to promote intercultural dialogue, mutual learning, and iterative adaptation of educational practices across contexts.

Quantitative component

Measurement instrument

Participants’ self-perceived cultural competence was assessed using the Metacognitive-Centered Multicultural Competence Scale (MCMCS), a study-specific adapted instrument developed for this evaluation. The MCMCS was designed to capture selected dimensions of cultural competence relevant to international faculty development, including metacognitive awareness, cognitive knowledge, motivational confidence, and behavioral adaptability.

Items were adapted from several established instruments and frameworks related to cultural intelligence, intercultural communication, and cross-cultural adaptability. These included the Cultural Intelligence Scale and its extended version, the Short Form Cultural Intelligence Scale, the Cross-Cultural Adaptability Inventory, the Behavioral Assessment Scale for Intercultural Communication, and related cultural competence and cultural intelligence measures [19–26]. Items were reviewed and adapted by the research team to fit the context of health professions faculty development and the learning objectives of the IFDP.

The final MCMCS contained 13 items. Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree. Participants rated each item twice in the same post-program survey: once retrospectively for their perceived competence before the program and once for their current perceived competence at the end of the program.

The MCMCS should be regarded as a study-specific adapted instrument rather than a fully validated scale. Evidence for content validity was supported by the systematic adaptation of items from established instruments and by review within the research team to ensure alignment with the cultural competence construct and the IFDP context. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha for the retrospective “Before FAIMER” ratings and the “Today” ratings. No exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis was conducted because of the small sample size; therefore, structural validity and the four-domain structure of the instrument could not be formally established in this study.

Quantitative data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and item-level responses. Means, standard deviations, medians, minimum values, and maximum values were calculated for each item at both retrospective pre-program and post-program time points.

Paired-sample t-tests were used to compare participants’ retrospective “Before FAIMER” ratings with their “Today” ratings for each MCMCS item. Because the survey used paired ratings from the same participants, Cohen’s dz was calculated as an effect size for each paired comparison. Effect sizes were interpreted to distinguish statistically significant but modest perceived changes from changes that may be more educationally meaningful. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Version 27.0.

Given the retrospective self-assessment design, all quantitative findings were interpreted as changes in self-reported or self-perceived competence rather than objective changes in cultural competence or observed behavior.

Qualitative component

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted 2 to 4 weeks after program completion. The interview guide explored participants’ experiences of cultural competence development during the program, critical incidents that shaped intercultural learning, perceived challenges in cross-cultural interaction, and strategies used to address cultural differences or conflicts.

Interviews were conducted via video conferencing, lasted approximately 40 to 70 minutes, and were audio-recorded with participants’ informed consent. All interviews were transcribed verbatim. Identifying information was removed from the transcripts before analysis.

Qualitative data analysis

Interview data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach: familiarization with the data, generation of initial codes, development of candidate themes, review of themes, definition and naming of themes, and production of the final analytic account. NVivo Version 12 was used to support data management, coding, and organization of themes.

Two researchers independently read the transcripts and generated initial codes. Coding and theme development were then discussed within the research team to refine interpretations, examine alternative explanations, and enhance reflexivity. The analysis focused on identifying patterns in how participants described their intercultural learning, reflection, adaptation, and engagement in collaborative program activities.

Data collection and analysis continued until the research team judged that the interview data provided sufficient information power to address the study aims, considering the specificity of the study aim, the relevance and specificity of participants’ experiences, the use of established theory, the quality and depth of interview dialogue, and the recurrence of key patterns across interviews [27].

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings

The quantitative and qualitative findings were integrated during interpretation. Quantitative survey results were used to identify patterns of self-perceived change across different dimensions of cultural competence. Qualitative interview findings were used to explain how participants experienced these changes, what program processes supported their learning, and where perceived development appeared more limited.

Integration focused on areas of convergence, complementarity, and discrepancy. For example, qualitative narratives were compared with quantitative findings to examine whether participants’ accounts of reflection, communication, and adaptation aligned with item-level changes in self-reported competence. Discrepancies between the two strands, such as strong qualitative accounts of adaptation alongside non-significant quantitative changes in immediate cultural sensing or stress coping, were treated as important findings rather than as inconsistencies to be resolved.

Research team characteristics, reflexivity, and trustworthiness

The research team included health professions educators and researchers with backgrounds in medical education, educational research, faculty development, and qualitative inquiry. Team members had varying levels of involvement in the IFDP, which created both contextual familiarity and a need for reflexive attention to potential assumptions.

To enhance trustworthiness, the research team used several strategies. First, two researchers independently engaged with the transcripts and discussed coding differences to refine interpretations and enhance reflexivity. Second, regular team debriefing meetings were held to examine emerging interpretations and consider alternative explanations. Third, reflexive memoing was used to document analytic decisions and to consider how researchers’ professional roles, cultural backgrounds, and prior experiences with faculty development may have shaped interpretation. Fourth, verbatim quotations were used to preserve participants’ voices and support transparency in the presentation of qualitative findings.

Qualitative interview results

Thematic analysis of the 13 interviews identified two interrelated analytic outputs. First, participants described three core learning processes that supported their cultural competence development: Contextualized Construction, Collaborative Learning, and Dynamic Co-Creation. These processes formed the basis of the CoLearn Framework. Second, participants’ narratives suggested a developmental pathway from Awareness to Understanding and, for some participants, toward aspects of Adaptation.

The CoLearn Framework and the developmental pathway are presented together in Table 4. The framework captures the pedagogical processes through which the program supported intercultural learning, while the developmental pathway describes how participants made sense of their own learning trajectories. The stages should not be interpreted as fixed, linear, or fully achieved outcomes; rather, they represent patterns in participants’ accounts of how their cultural awareness, interpretive understanding, and adaptive engagement changed over time.

The CoLearn framework for cross-cultural faculty development

Contextualized construction

Participants described the cultural diversity of the cohort as an important pedagogical resource. Ten of the 13 interviewees referred to learning from peers with different national, institutional, or disciplinary backgrounds. These interactions helped participants move beyond abstract concepts of cultural competence and apply them to concrete teaching and professional situations. One participant reflected:

“Having Malaysian, Nepalese, and Chinese faculty in my group forced me to articulate assumptions I’d never questioned. Our debates about medical ethics revealed how culture shapes professional values” (Participant 6).

Participants also emphasized the role of scenario-based learning in connecting cultural theory with practice. For example, one participant described the program’s curriculum as a scaffolded process:

"The spiral curriculum design gradually exposed us to Western educational philosophies while adapting to Southeast Asian learners’ needs – it’s like climbing a ladder where each step builds cultural awareness" (Participant 2)

These accounts suggest that contextualized learning activities supported participants’ recognition of how cultural assumptions operate in educational and clinical contexts.

Collaborative learning

Participants reported that structured group work created opportunities for peer learning, negotiation, and reflection. Rather than treating cultural diversity as background information, the program used mixed-group activities to make different perspectives visible. One participant described how a group-based exercise changed their understanding of collaboration:

“The river-crossing game wasn’t about individual brilliance; it taught us to value diverse problem-solving approaches. Our Malaysian colleague’s input on collective responsibility fundamentally changed how I design group projects.” (Participant 8).

International and Chinese participants also described co-creating educational products as a process that revealed the cultural assumptions embedded in teaching and assessment. One participant explained:

“Designing assessment rubrics with Chinese peers revealed how evaluation criteria themselves carry cultural assumptions. Our hybrid model now respects both Confucian diligence and Western critical thinking.” (Participant 13).

These narratives indicate that collaborative learning provided a setting in which participants could examine cultural assumptions and negotiate shared educational practices.

Dynamic co-creation

Participants described cultural competence development as an iterative process shaped by feedback, dialogue, and revision. Several interviewees noted that collaborative project work required them to test, revise, and adapt educational ideas in response to peers’ perspectives.

One participant explained:

“Every module prototype underwent ‘culture stress-testing’; our Nepalese peer’s insight about indirect communication styles transformed how we present negative feedback.” (Participant 5).

Another participant described the model as “alive and responsive to real classroom tensions” (Participant 7). These accounts suggest that the program supported a dynamic form of learning in which participants did not simply acquire cultural knowledge but used intercultural dialogue to reshape educational practices.

The mixed-group project structure also made cultural assumptions more visible at the operational level. One Chinese participant reflected:

“Working with Nepalese colleagues revealed divergent priorities: they emphasized direct task execution, while we valued relationship-building first. The structured reflection exercises helped us synthesize these approaches.” (Participant 10).

Developmental pathway of cultural competence

Awareness stage

In the awareness stage, participants described recognizing cultural differences and becoming more conscious of their own assumptions. However, this awareness was often tentative. Some participants initially understood respect as avoiding sensitive topics or minimizing disagreement. One participant explained:

“Discussing ethical dilemmas in mixed groups exposed blind spots in my own cultural assumptions. It wasn’t about right answers but understanding why others prioritized different values” (Participant 10).

This stage was characterized by emerging cultural self-awareness, but not necessarily by confident or consistent behavioral adaptation.

Understanding stage

In the understanding stage, participants described moving from passive recognition toward more intentional engagement with cultural differences. They began to interpret cultural differences more carefully and to use communication strategies such as simplified English, visual aids, and culturally familiar examples.

One participant reflected:

“I avoided discussing politics with international peers, fearing offense. Training taught me to acknowledge differences proactively rather than tiptoe around them” (Participant 8).

These accounts suggest that participants developed greater interpretive flexibility and were more willing to address cultural differences directly, although often with caution.

Adaptation stage

In the adaptation stage, some participants described a shift from avoiding cultural difference to using cultural difference as a resource for educational design. One participant explained:

“Initially I focused on avoiding offense. Through structured interactions, I learned cultural differences aren’t obstacles but design parameters. Now I actively seek cultural friction points as innovation opportunities.” (Participant 9).

Twelve of the 13 interviewees provided narrative evidence consistent with some features of adaptation, such as reflective reframing, strategic communication, or willingness to redesign educational practices. However, these accounts should not be interpreted as evidence that participants had fully achieved stable behavioral adaptation across all intercultural situations. This cautious interpretation is important because the quantitative findings showed non-significant perceived changes in immediate cultural sensing and changing behavior to suit different cultural situations.

Mixed-methods integration

Integration of the quantitative and qualitative findings provided a more nuanced understanding of how participants perceived and experienced cultural competence development during the program. The survey results indicated statistically significant self-perceived gains in selected dimensions of cultural competence, especially adjustment of cultural understanding, communication strategy adaptation, cultural self-awareness, and understanding of cultural value frameworks. The interview findings helped explain these perceived gains by showing how participants engaged in reflection, dialogue, peer negotiation, and collaborative project work.

Convergence between self-perceived gains and reflective practice

The strongest quantitative finding was the perceived gain in participants’ ability to adjust their cultural understanding during intercultural interaction. This aligned with interview narratives in which participants described becoming more aware of cultural assumptions and more willing to reinterpret educational or professional situations from multiple perspectives. For example, participants’ reflections on ethical dilemmas, assessment rubrics, and feedback practices showed how cultural assumptions became more visible through structured interaction.

Similarly, the significant perceived gain in adjusting communication strategies was supported by qualitative accounts of participants using simplified English, visual aids, culturally familiar examples, and more explicit negotiation of meaning. These findings suggest convergence between self-reported gains in metacognitive and communication-related items and participants’ descriptions of reflective practice.

Complementarity between collaborative learning and perceived competence

The qualitative theme of Collaborative Learning complemented the quantitative findings by explaining how perceived competence gains may have developed. Participants described mixed-group tasks, peer feedback, and co-created educational products as opportunities to practice perspective-taking and negotiate differences. These experiences appeared to provide a practical setting for applying cultural knowledge and communication strategies.

The qualitative data therefore extended the survey findings by identifying relational and pedagogical mechanisms that may have supported participants’ perceived development. In particular, collaborative project work functioned as a learning space in which participants could make cultural assumptions explicit and test culturally responsive teaching strategies.

Discrepancies between adaptation narratives and quantitative results

The integration also revealed important discrepancies. While several participants described experiences consistent with aspects of adaptation, the quantitative results did not show statistically significant perceived changes in stress coping, immediate cultural sensing, or changing behavior to suit different cultural situations. These findings suggest that reflective understanding and strategic communication may be more readily influenced by the program than affective resilience or immediate behavioral adaptation.

Rather than treating this discrepancy as a contradiction, it indicates that different dimensions of cultural competence may develop at different rates. The program appeared to support perceived gains in metacognitive awareness and communication strategy, while more embodied, emotionally demanding, and context-dependent forms of adaptation may require additional real-world practice, institutional support, and longitudinal reinforcement.

Developmental progression and program impact

Taken together, the integrated findings suggest that the IFDP supported participants’ perceived movement from cultural awareness toward deeper understanding and, for some participants, toward aspects of adaptive engagement. However, the evidence should be interpreted cautiously. The quantitative data reflect retrospective self-assessment rather than objective behavioral change, and the qualitative data provide narrative evidence of developmental processes rather than direct observation of practice.

The CoLearn Framework therefore offers a useful explanatory model for how cultural competence may be fostered through contextualized construction, collaborative learning, and dynamic co-creation. At the same time, the mixed-methods findings indicate that future program evaluation should include longer-term follow-up and more direct evidence of behavioral change, such as peer observation, teaching portfolio analysis, or feedback from students and colleagues.

Discussion

Principal findings

This mixed-methods study examined how participants perceived and experienced the development of cultural competence during a two-year international faculty development program. The retrospective self-assessment survey indicated statistically significant self-perceived gains in 8 of the 13 measured items, with the largest effects observed in adjustment of cultural understanding and adaptation of communication strategies. Qualitative findings suggested that participants’ learning was supported by three interrelated processes: Contextualized Construction, Collaborative Learning, and Dynamic Co-Creation. These processes informed the CoLearn Framework and were associated with a developmental pathway from Awareness to Understanding and, for some participants, toward aspects of Adaptation.

These findings should be interpreted within the methodological boundaries of the study. Because the quantitative data were collected using a single-time-point retrospective pre–post self-assessment, they do not provide objective evidence of behavioral change. Rather, they indicate participants’ perceived changes after completing the program. Retrospective pre–post designs can be useful in educational program evaluation when learners’ understanding of a construct change during an intervention, but they remain dependent on self-report and are vulnerable to recall and social desirability bias [18]. The qualitative findings add explanatory depth, but they should also be understood as narrative evidence rather than direct observation of teaching or intercultural behavior.

Interpreting self-perceived gains in cultural competence

The strongest quantitative finding was the perceived gain in participants’ ability to adjust their cultural understanding during intercultural interaction. This finding is consistent with the program’s emphasis on reflection, dialogue, and repeated exposure to culturally diverse perspectives. Participants’ interview accounts suggested that case discussions, mixed-group projects, and peer feedback helped them recognize how cultural assumptions shape educational values, communication patterns, and teaching practices. These findings align with developmental views of cultural competence that emphasize self-awareness, reflective practice, cultural humility, and ongoing adaptation rather than mastery of fixed cultural knowledge [1, 3, 6].

A moderate perceived gain was also observed in adjustment of communication strategies, an immediately applicable dimension of cultural competence in faculty development. Participants described using simplified English, visual aids, culturally familiar examples, and explicit negotiation of meaning when interacting with colleagues or students from different cultural backgrounds. However, several statistically significant findings had only small-to-moderate effect sizes. Because statistical significance should not be equated with educational significance, the effect size pattern suggests that the program was more strongly associated with perceived changes in reflective understanding and communication strategy than with deeper affective or behavioral dimensions of cultural adaptation [28].

Stress coping and immediate adaptation

The non-significant findings for stress coping, immediate cultural sensing, and changing behavior to suit different cultural situations are important. They suggest that certain dimensions of cultural competence may be more difficult to develop through faculty development alone, even when the program is longitudinal and extends over two years.

Stress coping may depend on repeated real-world immersion rather than structured educational activities alone. Although the IFDP provided intercultural interaction, group projects, and scenario-based learning, participants may not have experienced sustained exposure to high-stakes or emotionally demanding intercultural situations. Stress coping involves affective regulation, tolerance of ambiguity, and resilience under uncertainty, which may require longitudinal mentorship, real-time feedback, and repeated practice in authentic intercultural settings [4, 29].

Similarly, immediate cultural sensing is a situated and embodied capacity. The ability to recognize whether an intercultural interaction is going well or wrong may depend on contextual cues, language fluency, prior experience, and tacit knowledge accumulated over time. Classroom discussions and simulations can support awareness and interpretation, but they may not reproduce the complexity and emotional pressure of real intercultural encounters [12]. Behavioral adaptation may also require institutional permission and structural support, as faculty members’ ability to enact culturally responsive practices can be constrained by curriculum requirements, assessment systems, workload, and local expectations of teacher authority [4, 13, 29].

The CoLearn framework as an explanatory model

The CoLearn Framework—Contextualized Construction, Collaborative Learning, and Dynamic Co-Creation—offers an explanatory model for how cultural competence may be developed within international faculty development. The framework treats cultural competence not as a fixed endpoint or checklist of skills, but as an ongoing, reflective, and context-responsive capacity constructed through interaction, negotiation, and co-creation [1, 6, 7, 30].

Contextualized Construction highlights the importance of situating cultural learning within real educational and professional dilemmas. Participants reported that case discussions and scenario-based learning helped them connect abstract concepts to concrete issues in teaching, feedback, assessment, and professional communication. Collaborative Learning emphasizes the relational nature of cultural competence development: mixed-group activities allowed participants to encounter different assumptions about authority, responsibility, communication, and assessment. Dynamic Co-Creation represents the most practice-oriented component of the framework, as participants revised educational products, adapted feedback practices, and tested ideas through peer dialogue. In this sense, co-creation may help move cultural competence from awareness toward practice [7, 15, 29].

Rethinking the developmental pathway

The qualitative findings suggested a developmental pathway from Awareness to Understanding and, for some participants, toward aspects of Adaptation. This pathway resonates with Bennett’s Developmental Model of Intercultural Sensitivity, but progression should not be interpreted as linear, complete, or uniform across participants [12].

In the Awareness stage, participants became more conscious of cultural difference and of their own assumptions, although this awareness was sometimes expressed as avoidance of sensitive topics. In the Understanding stage, participants began to interpret cultural difference more deliberately and described using more flexible communication strategies. The Adaptation stage requires more cautious interpretation. Several participants described experiences consistent with aspects of adaptation, such as reframing cultural difference as a design resource or co-creating culturally responsive educational products. However, the quantitative findings did not show significant perceived changes in immediate cultural sensing or general behavioral adaptation. It would therefore be premature to claim that participants had fully reached a stable adaptation stage.

Power dynamics and contextual influences

Several power dynamics may have shaped participants’ engagement and learning experiences. Although the program was international in orientation, it was hosted in China and included a predominantly Chinese cohort. This context may have influenced which assumptions, examples, and interactional norms were treated as familiar or normative during group discussions. Participants from other countries may have had different levels of comfort in challenging dominant perspectives or introducing alternative educational assumptions [4].

The use of English as the primary language of instruction also created linguistic power dynamics. Participants with stronger English skills may have been better positioned to participate actively, express disagreement, or shape group outputs, whereas those with lower English confidence may have faced constraints in articulating complex cultural or pedagogical ideas [4, 8]. Institutional and geopolitical dynamics may also have influenced the program [31, 32]. The IFDP drew on globally circulating frameworks in medical education, which provided legitimacy and a shared pedagogical language but may also have positioned some forms of knowledge as more authoritative than local or regional practices [4, 29]. These considerations are consistent with broader discussions of cultural competence education, which emphasize the influence ofstructural factors, institutional contexts, and intersectionality on learning and practice [16, 33]

Implications for international faculty development

These findings suggest that international faculty development programs should move beyond knowledge transmission and include structured opportunities for reflection, intercultural dialogue, and co-creation of educational practices. Program evaluation should also combine self-report measures with more direct evidence of practice, such as observation, peer feedback, student feedback, teaching portfolios, and follow-up interviews after participants return to their institutions [4, 13, 29, 34]. Sustained change may require post-program mentorship, communities of practice, institutional action plans, and mechanisms for local adaptation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample was relatively small and self-selected, and all participants came from a single international faculty development program. Although participants represented different professional roles and institutional backgrounds, the predominance of Chinese participants limits transferability to other international or multicultural contexts.

Second, the quantitative component used a single-time-point retrospective pre–post self-assessment. This design was appropriate for exploring perceived change and addressing possible response-shift bias, but it cannot establish objective improvement in cultural competence [18]. The results may also be influenced by recall bias, social desirability bias, and participants’ positive feelings toward the program.

Third, the MCMCS was a study-specific adapted instrument rather than a fully validated scale. Although items were adapted from established instruments and internal consistency was acceptable, no exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis was conducted because of the small sample size. Therefore, the structural validity and four-domain structure of the instrument could not be established in this study.

Fourth, the qualitative findings were based on participants’ retrospective accounts. The study did not include direct observation of teaching practice, student perspectives, or institutional outcome data. As a result, claims about behavioral adaptation and downstream educational impact should remain cautious [35]. Finally, the program context itself may have shaped the findings, as the program was hosted in China, conducted in English, and affiliated with an international faculty development model [4, 8, 29].

Conclusions

This study suggests that a two-year international faculty development program was associated with participants’ self-perceived gains in selected dimensions of cultural competence, particularly cultural understanding, communication strategy adjustment, cultural self-awareness, and understanding of cultural value frameworks. Qualitative findings further suggest that these perceived gains were supported by contextualized construction, collaborative learning, and dynamic co-creation. The proposed CoLearn Framework offers a useful model for designing faculty development that treats cultural competence as relational, reflective, and situated rather than as a static checklist of knowledge or skills [1, 6, 7]. At the same time, more complex dimensions, such as stress coping, immediate cultural sensing, and stable behavioral adaptation, may require more immersive practice, institutional support, and longitudinal follow-up. Future research should examine whether perceived gains are sustained over time and translated into observable teaching practices, curriculum design, and institutional change.
