Pulmonary Functional Impact of Resecting Additional Ipsilateral Ground‐Glass Opacities: A Retrospective Propensity Score–Matched Study
Authors: Jia Han, Guofei Zhang, Yaojuan Jin, Junqiang Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe 1‐year pulmonary functional impact of resecting additional ipsilateral ground‐glass opacities (GGOs) during unilateral surgery remains uncertain. We evaluated whether resection of multiple ipsilateral GGOs is associated with less favorable postoperative pulmonary function preservation than resection of a single lesion.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed patients with at least two ipsilateral GGOs who underwent pulmonary surgery between January 2020 and December 2024. Patients were grouped according to whether 1 lesion or ≥ 2 lesions were resected during the index operation. The primary outcomes were 1‐year retention of FVC, FEV1, and DLCOc‐SB.ResultsA total of 291 patients were included; 146 underwent resection of 1 lesion and 145 underwent resection of ≥ 2 lesions. After propensity score matching, 232 patients remained (116 per group). In the matched cohort, absolute postoperative FVC, FEV1, and DLCOc‐SB at 1 year were similar between groups. However, resection of ≥ 2 lesions was associated with greater declines and lower retention of FVC (90.41% vs. 94.55%; p < 0.001), FEV1 (87.10% vs. 93.73%; p < 0.001), and DLCOc‐SB (91.04% vs. 93.70%; p = 0.016). In multivariable analyses, resection of ≥ 2 lesions remained independently associated with lower FEV1, FVC, and DLCOc‐SB retention. Operative time was longer in the ≥ 2‐lesion group, whereas other perioperative outcomes were similar.ConclusionsAmong surgically fit patients with multiple ipsilateral GGOs, resection of ≥ 2 lesions was associated with less favorable pulmonary function preservation than resection of a single lesion, suggesting a functional trade‐off when considering resection of additional ipsilateral lesions.Keywords: ground‐glass opacity, ipsilateral, lung resection, multiple pulmonary nodules, propensity score matching, pulmonary function

Introduction

The increasing use of high‐resolution computed tomography and lung cancer screening has made multiple pulmonary ground‐glass opacities (GGOs) a common finding in thoracic practice [1, 2]. Many of these lesions correspond to early‐stage adenocarcinoma‐spectrum disease, including adenocarcinoma in situ and minimally invasive adenocarcinoma, and are associated with favorable long‐term outcomes after resection [3, 4]. In this setting, treatment planning should address not only oncologic control but also preservation of postoperative pulmonary function, particularly in patients expected to have long survival.

A common dilemma arises when two or more GGOs are identified within the same lung. The surgeon may either remove the dominant lesion alone and leave the remaining nodules under radiologic surveillance, or extend the procedure to include additional ipsilateral lesions when technically feasible [5, 6]. The latter strategy offers more complete pathologic assessment and may reduce the number of residual nodules, but it also increases the extent of parenchymal sacrifice. For patients with generally indolent lesions and long expected survival, the functional cost of removing additional nodules may be clinically important.

Previous studies on multiple GGOs have mainly focused on radiologic‐pathologic correlation, the natural history of residual nodules, and oncologic outcomes [3, 7, 8, 9]. To our knowledge, evidence specifically comparing postoperative pulmonary function preservation between single‐ and multiple‐lesion resection strategies in patients with multiple ipsilateral GGOs remain limited.

To address this question, we conducted a retrospective propensity score‐matched study of patients with at least two ipsilateral GGOs who underwent unilateral pulmonary resection. We compared patients who underwent resection of 1 lesion with those who underwent resection of ≥ 2 lesions during the index operation. The primary objective was to evaluate pulmonary function preservation at 1 year after surgery, with particular attention to pulmonary function retention. Supportive analyses included absolute change from baseline and absolute postoperative pulmonary function at 1 year. Secondary objectives included perioperative outcomes and the pathological findings of the resected lesions.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patients

We assembled a retrospective follow‐up cohort of surgically treated patients with available baseline and 1‐year pulmonary function tests. Patients were included if they were aged ≥ 18 years, had at least two ipsilateral ground‐glass opacities (GGOs) on preoperative thin‐section computed tomography, underwent unilateral pulmonary resection, and had available preoperative pulmonary function tests (PFTs) as well as postoperative 1‐year PFTs obtained at 11–13 months after surgery. Patients with prior lung resection, distant metastasis, severe preoperative airflow limitation (FEV1 < 50% predicted), incomplete surgical records, or incomplete pulmonary function data were excluded. Patients who underwent repeat lung surgery or other thoracic interventions within 1 year after the index operation were also excluded.

The electronically screened cohort was restricted to patients with available pulmonary function follow‐up and complete records. Within this screened cohort, the analytic sample was defined according to the nodule‐ and surgery‐specific criteria shown in Figure 1. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our hospital (Approval No. 2026–0498). Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Flow chart of patient selection. The initial screening cohort consisted of electronically identified adult patients with complete clinical and surgical data and available preoperative and 1‐year postoperative pulmonary function tests.

The exposure of interest was the number of lesions resected during the index operation, categorized as resection of 1 lesion versus resection of ≥ 2 lesions. We selected this variable because it was consistently documented in operative and pathological records and reflects a common clinical decision in patients with multiple ipsilateral GGOs: whether to remove only the dominant or target lesion or to simultaneously resect additional ipsilateral lesions. We reviewed the available operative and pathological records for more direct measures of cumulative resection burden, such as resected lung volume and specimen weight. However, these data were not routinely recorded and could not be reliably reconstructed retrospectively. Therefore, lesion number was used as a pragmatic exposure variable rather than a direct quantitative measure of resected parenchymal volume. This approach may introduce exposure misclassification because the number of resected lesions does not necessarily correspond to the actual amount of lung parenchyma removed.

Data Collection and Definitions

Clinical, radiologic, surgical, pathologic, and pulmonary function data were retrospectively retrieved from the institutional medical record system. Preoperative thin‐section computed tomography was used to assess the number, size, and radiologic characteristics of ipsilateral GGOs. The consolidation‐to‐tumor ratio (CTR) was calculated as the maximum diameter of the solid component divided by the maximum tumor diameter and multiplied by 100%. Radiologic assessments were performed by two thoracic surgeons, with discrepancies resolved by consensus. The dominant lesion was defined according to a prespecified hierarchical rule, prioritizing radiologic suspicion for malignancy and using lesion size when the degree of suspicion was comparable.

Baseline variables included age, sex, body mass index, smoking history, cardiopulmonary comorbidity, preoperative pulmonary function, number of ipsilateral nodules, dominant lesion size, dominant lesion CTR, multilobar distribution, dominant lesion location, and resection type of the dominant lesion. Multilobar distribution was defined as GGOs distributed across different ipsilateral lobes. Patients were categorized according to the number of lesions resected during the index operation as resection of 1 lesion or resection of ≥ 2 lesions. The resection type of the dominant lesion was recorded as wedge resection, segmentectomy, or lobectomy.

Pulmonary function tests were performed according to ATS/ERS standards at baseline and 11–13 months after surgery. The variables of interest were forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), and hemoglobin‐corrected single‐breath diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCOc‐SB). Pulmonary function retention was calculated as postoperative value divided by preoperative value ×100, and absolute change from baseline was calculated as postoperative value minus preoperative value. The primary outcomes were 1‐year retention of FVC, FEV1, and DLCOc‐SB. Supportive outcomes were absolute change from baseline and absolute postoperative pulmonary function values at 1 year. Secondary outcomes included perioperative outcomes and pathologic findings. Perioperative complications were defined as events recorded during the index postoperative hospitalization, including prolonged air leak, pneumonia, fever (temperature ≥ 38.5°C), and atrial fibrillation. Drainage duration and postoperative length of stay were recorded in days.

Propensity Score Matching and Statistical Analysis

There were no missing data for variables included in the propensity score model in the analytic cohort. To reduce baseline imbalance between groups, propensity score matching was performed using a logistic regression model including age, sex, body mass index, smoking history, cardiopulmonary comorbidity, preoperative pulmonary function, number of ipsilateral nodules, dominant lesion size, dominant lesion CTR, multilobar distribution, dominant lesion location, and resection type of the dominant lesion. One‐to‐one nearest‐neighbor matching without replacement was performed with a caliper of 0.2 SD of the logit of the propensity score. Covariate balance before and after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an absolute SMD < 0.10 considered indicative of adequate balance.

Continuous variables are presented as mean (standard deviation) or median [interquartile range], as appropriate, and categorical variables are presented as number (percentage). Group comparisons were performed using the Student's t‐test or Wilcoxon rank‐sum test for continuous variables and the chi‐square test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

In the matched cohort, multivariable linear regression analyses were performed to evaluate the association between resection group and pulmonary function outcomes at 1 year. To account for any residual imbalance after matching and to improve precision, the post‐matching regression models were additionally adjusted for prespecified covariates, including age, sex, body mass index, smoking history, cardiopulmonary comorbidity, dominant lesion size, dominant lesion CTR, multilobar distribution, and resection type of the dominant lesion. Models for absolute postoperative pulmonary function were additionally adjusted for the corresponding baseline pulmonary function value. To assess whether minor residual imbalance in preoperative pulmonary function influenced retention‐based comparisons, a sensitivity analysis was performed in which the retention models were additionally adjusted for the corresponding baseline pulmonary function value. Model assumptions were assessed by residual diagnostics, and multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors. Cluster‐robust standard errors were applied according to matched pairs. Sensitivity analyses included analyses excluding patients who underwent dominant‐lesion lobectomy. Prespecified subgroup analyses were performed according to multilobar distribution. To assess whether the main findings were driven by more extensive resection of additional lesions, a sensitivity analysis was performed after restricting the ≥ 2‐lesion group to patients whose additional resected lesions were all treated by wedge resection, followed by repeated propensity score matching using the same matching strategy.

All statistical tests were two‐sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2.

Pulmonary Function at 1 Year

Pulmonary function outcomes in the matched cohort are shown in Table 3. Preoperative FVC, FEV1, and DLCOc‐SB were similar between groups. In unadjusted comparisons, absolute postoperative FVC, FEV1, and DLCOc‐SB at 1 year did not differ significantly between groups. In contrast, resection of ≥ 2 lesions was associated with less favorable pulmonary function preservation. Compared with resection of 1 lesion, resection of ≥ 2 lesions was associated with greater declines in FVC (ΔFVC: −0.30 [−0.58, −0.10] vs. −0.16 [−0.33, 0.05] L; p < 0.001), FEV1 (ΔFEV1: −0.28 [−0.48, −0.14] vs. −0.16 [−0.32, −0.03] L; p < 0.001), and DLCOc‐SB (ΔDLCOc‐SB: −0.59 [−1.13, −0.10] vs. −0.42 [−0.86, 0.07] mmol/min/kPa; p = 0.041). Retention was also lower in the resection of ≥ 2 lesions group for FVC (90.41 [81.18, 97.08] vs. 94.55 [87.52, 101.69]%; p < 0.001), FEV1 (87.10 [81.19, 93.15] vs. 93.73 [86.80, 98.52]%; p < 0.001), and DLCOc‐SB (91.04 [83.17, 98.09] vs. 93.70 [88.19, 101.00]%; p = 0.016). These distributions are shown in Figure 3.

Perioperative Outcomes and Characteristics of Dominant and Additional Lesions

Perioperative outcomes and dominant lesion pathology are summarized in Table 4. Resection of ≥ 2 lesions was associated with longer operative time than resection of 1 lesion (82.5 [65.0, 105.0] vs. 60.0 [45.0, 80.0] min, p < 0.001). Drainage duration (2.44 ± 1.20 vs. 2.47 ± 1.32 days, p = 0.835) and postoperative length of stay (2.52 ± 1.39 vs. 2.62 ± 1.58 days, p = 0.597) were similar between groups. Overall postoperative complications were comparable between groups (12.1% vs. 8.6%, p = 0.389). The distribution of dominant lesion pathology was also similar (p = 0.875), with MIA as the most frequent diagnosis in both groups.

Among the 145 patients who underwent resection of ≥ 2 lesions, 84 (57.9%) had at least one additional lesion in a different lobe from the dominant lesion, whereas in 61 patients (42.1%) the additional lesion(s) were confined to the same lobe. Among these 61 patients, 19 (13.1% of the 145 patients) had an additional lesion in the same segment as the dominant lesion and 42 (28.9%) had an additional lesion in a different segment within the same lobe (Table S1, Panel A). With respect to the second resected lesion, wedge resection was performed in 100 patients (69.0%), segmentectomy in 31 (21.4%), and lobectomy in 14 (9.7%) (Table S1, Panel B). Pathologic examination of all 178 additional resected lesions showed benign disease in 12 lesions (6.7%), AAH or AIS in 111 (62.4%), MIA in 51 (28.7%), and IAC in 4 (2.2%) (Table S1, Panel C).

Association Between Resection Group and Pulmonary Function

Multivariable analyses in the matched cohort are presented in Table 5 and Figure 4. In the primary analysis, resection of ≥ 2 lesions was associated with lower 1‐year pulmonary function retention: FVC retention (β −5.07, 95% CI: −8.12 to −2.02, p = 0.001), FEV1 retention (β −5.78, 95% CI: −8.60 to −2.96, p < 0.001), and DLCOc‐SB retention (β −4.28, 95% CI: −7.95 to −0.60, p = 0.024).

Supportive analyses were consistent with the primary findings. After additional adjustment for the corresponding baseline pulmonary function value, resection of ≥ 2 lesions remained associated with lower retention of FVC (β −4.72, 95% CI: −7.70 to −1.74, p = 0.002), FEV1 (β −5.56, 95% CI: −8.31 to −2.81, p < 0.001), and DLCOc‐SB (β −4.13, 95% CI: −7.60 to −0.65, p = 0.021). In models additionally adjusted for baseline pulmonary function, resection of ≥ 2 lesions was associated with lower postoperative FVC (β −0.164 L, 95% CI: −0.251 to −0.077, p < 0.001), FEV1 (β −0.142 L, 95% CI: −0.204 to −0.079, p < 0.001), and DLCOc‐SB (β −0.275 mmol/min/kPa, 95% CI: −0.492 to −0.058, p = 0.014). In analyses using absolute change from baseline, resection of ≥ 2 lesions was also associated with greater declines in ΔFVC (β −0.151 L, 95% CI: −0.219 to −0.083, p < 0.001), ΔFEV1 (β −0.178 L, 95% CI: −0.271 to −0.086, p < 0.001), and ΔDLCOc‐SB (β −0.288 mmol/min/kPa, 95% CI: −0.531 to −0.044, p = 0.021).

Sensitivity and Subgroup Analyses

In the sensitivity analysis excluding patients who underwent dominant‐lesion lobectomy (Table S2), the association between resection of ≥ 2 lesions and lower pulmonary function retention remained significant for FVC retention (β −3.62, 95% CI: −7.03 to −0.22, p = 0.039) and FEV1 retention (β −4.23, 95% CI: −7.21 to −1.25, p = 0.006). The estimate for DLCOc‐SB retention was similar in direction but did not reach statistical significance (β −3.73, 95% CI: −7.57 to 0.11, p = 0.059).

Subgroup analyses by multilobar distribution are shown in Table S3. The associations with lower FVC retention and FEV1 retention were similar in the single‐lobe and different‐lobes subgroups. For DLCOc‐SB retention, the association was not significant in the single‐lobe subgroup (β −1.16, 95% CI: −6.82 to 4.49, p = 0.687) but was significant in the different‐lobes subgroup (β −6.74, 95% CI: −11.14 to −2.34, p = 0.003). There was no evidence of interaction between resection group and multilobar distribution for FVC retention (p for interaction = 0.905), FEV1 retention (0.591), or DLCOc‐SB retention (0.135).

To address whether the observed pulmonary function differences were driven by segmentectomy or lobectomy for additional lesions, we performed a repeated propensity score matching analysis restricted to patients in whom all additional resected lesions were treated by wedge resection. This analysis yielded 85 matched pairs with comparable baseline pulmonary function. In this wedge‐only additional resection cohort, resection of ≥ 2 lesions remained associated with greater declines in FVC (−0.27 vs. −0.18 L; p = 0.010) and FEV1 (−0.28 vs. −0.18 L; p = 0.001). The difference in ΔDLCOc‐SB was directionally similar but did not reach statistical significance. Retention was lower in the ≥ 2‐lesion group for FVC (91.08% vs. 93.93%; p = 0.015), FEV1 (88.19% vs. 93.45%; p < 0.001), and DLCOc‐SB (91.95% vs. 93.46%; p = 0.029) at 1 year (Table S4).

Discussion

In this propensity score–matched analysis, resection of ≥ 2 lesions was associated with less favorable preservation of pulmonary function at 1 year than resection of 1 lesion. Although unadjusted postoperative FVC, FEV1, and DLCOc‐SB values were similar between groups, patients who underwent resection of ≥ 2 lesions had lower retention and greater declines from baseline across all three measures. This apparent discrepancy likely reflects the different information captured by absolute and relative metrics. Absolute postoperative values are strongly influenced by preoperative pulmonary reserve, whereas retention and change‐from‐baseline measures quantify the degree of functional preservation within each patient. After accounting for baseline pulmonary function, resection of ≥ 2 lesions remained associated with lower postoperative FVC, FEV1, and DLCOc‐SB, consistent with the retention and change‐from‐baseline analyses. Thus, the findings are best interpreted as indicating less favorable pulmonary function preservation after multiple‐lesion resection, rather than uniformly worse absolute pulmonary function at 1 year.

An important issue in interpreting these findings is the potential dissociation between the number of resected lesions and the actual extent of lung resection. Lesion number indicates how many radiologic targets were removed, but does not directly quantify the amount or functional importance of parenchyma sacrificed. A single‐lesion lobectomy may impose a greater functional burden than two limited wedge resections, whereas multiple wedge resections may still impair function if they are deep, numerous, or distributed across lobes. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted at the level of surgical strategy rather than as evidence that lesion count itself directly determines postoperative functional loss. Although matching and adjustment for dominant‐lesion resection type reduced confounding from the major resection component, the cumulative burden of all additional resections could not be fully captured. To address whether the findings were driven by segmentectomy or lobectomy for additional lesions, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to patients in the ≥ 2‐lesion group whose additional lesions were all treated by wedge resection. After repeated propensity score matching between this subgroup and the 1‐lesion group, the ≥ 2‐lesion group still had greater declines in FVC and FEV1 and lower retention of FVC, FEV1, and DLCOc‐SB at 1 year. This finding suggests that the between‐group differences in pulmonary function were not explained solely by the inclusion of patients who underwent segmentectomy or lobectomy for additional lesions. However, wedge resections may still vary in number, depth, location, and cumulative parenchymal volume removed, and the choice of resection strategy for additional lesions was not randomized. Therefore, this sensitivity analysis should be interpreted as supportive rather than definitive.

The clinical relevance of these differences should be interpreted cautiously, as no universally accepted MCID has been established for postoperative pulmonary function decline or retention after lung resection in patients with multifocal GGOs. Therefore, absolute decline and retention rate should be considered together. At 1 year, the ≥ 2‐lesion group showed greater median losses in FVC, FEV1, and DLCOc‐SB than the 1‐lesion group (−0.30 vs. −0.16 L, −0.28 vs. −0.16 L, and −0.59 vs. −0.42 mmol/min/kPa, respectively), with consistently lower retention rates (FVC: 90.41% vs. 94.55%; FEV1: 87.10% vs. 93.73%; DLCOc‐SB: 91.04% vs. 93.70%). Although MCID thresholds derived from COPD, IPF, or other chronic respiratory disease populations cannot be directly applied to this surgical multifocal GGO cohort, they provide useful reference values for interpretation. Previous studies have proposed clinically important FEV1 changes of approximately 100–140 mL or 5%–10% from baseline [10], while changes of 2%–6% in predicted FVC have been suggested to be potentially meaningful in IPF [11]. Viewed against these reference ranges, the observed FEV1 and FVC differences appear potentially clinically meaningful, whereas the smaller DLCOc‐SB difference should be interpreted more conservatively.

The present findings also help define a concrete clinical trade‐off in the management of multiple ipsilateral GGOs. In this study, the key decision was not whether the dominant lesion warranted resection, but whether additional ipsilateral nodules should be removed during the same operation. Among the additionally resected lesions, 62.4% were AAH or AIS, 28.7% were MIA, and only 2.2% were invasive adenocarcinoma. Thus, the pathological yield of additional resection was predominantly preinvasive or minimally invasive disease, whereas the functional trade‐off was less favorable preservation of pulmonary function at 1 year, with approximately 5%–6% lower adjusted retention of FEV1 and FVC. Although this decrement may be modest at the population level, it may still be clinically relevant in patients with limited pulmonary reserve. These data do not suggest that simultaneous resection of additional lesions is inappropriate; rather, they indicate that its expected benefit often lies in reducing residual nodule burden, obtaining pathological clarification, and potentially avoiding future ipsilateral intervention, rather than primarily in the immediate removal of invasive carcinoma. Whether this trade‐off is clinically acceptable depends on patient context. In patients with preserved pulmonary reserve, technically favorable peripheral nodules, substantial surveillance burden, or interval nodule progression, accepting a modest reduction in pulmonary function preservation may be reasonable. In contrast, in patients with marginal pulmonary reserve, advanced age, major comorbidity, or a high likelihood of requiring future pulmonary treatment, the predominance of AAH/AIS or MIA and the low frequency of invasive adenocarcinoma among additional lesions may support a more selective resection strategy.

Several mechanisms may explain these findings. Greater cumulative parenchymal resection may reduce ventilated lung volume, contributing to lower FVC and a more restrictive postoperative pattern [12, 13]. The decline in FEV1 may reflect both volume loss and altered postoperative mechanics after lung resection [14, 15]. The smaller reduction in DLCOc‐SB retention, which was significant in the primary analysis, is compatible with a decrease in effective alveolar‐capillary gas‐transfer surface area [16, 17]. However, the present study was not designed to determine the precise physiologic mechanisms underlying these differences, and factors such as lesion location, resection depth, lobe‐specific functional contribution, and postoperative mechanical remodeling may also contribute.

These findings should be interpreted within the broader context of parenchyma‐sparing surgery. Recent evidence has shown that sublobar resection can provide acceptable oncologic outcomes for selected patients with small peripheral NSCLC [18], and prospective data support intentional limited resection for carefully selected GGO‐dominant peripheral lung cancers [19]. However, in patients with multifocal GGOs, the clinical question often extends beyond the optimal extent of resection for a single lesion to whether additional ipsilateral nodules should be removed during the same operation [20]. Prior functional studies have shown that postoperative pulmonary function depends not only on the nominal extent of resection but also on the amount and functional contribution of preserved parenchyma [13, 14, 15]. The present results suggest that even within a generally lung‐sparing treatment framework, simultaneous resection of additional lesions may be associated with measurable loss of pulmonary function preservation.

The consistency of the findings across sensitivity analyses supports the robustness of the observed association. The direction of effect was similar across retention, absolute change from baseline, and baseline‐adjusted postoperative pulmonary function models. Additional adjustment for the corresponding baseline pulmonary function value did not materially alter the retention‐based associations, suggesting that minor residual differences in preoperative pulmonary function after matching were unlikely to explain the lower retention observed in the ≥ 2‐lesion group. Exclusion of patients who underwent dominant‐lesion lobectomy also did not materially alter the associations for FVC and FEV1 retention. In addition, subgroup analyses according to multilobar distribution showed no statistically significant interaction. Nevertheless, these analyses should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited sample size within subgroups.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective single‐center study, and residual confounding cannot be excluded despite propensity score matching [21]. Because treatment allocation was not randomized, the decision to perform simultaneous resection of additional lesions was subject to indication bias and surgeon selection. Some determinants of surgical decision‐making, including technical complexity, detailed lesion depth, anticipated functional loss, and surgeon preference, were not fully captured in the present dataset. Second, the analysis was restricted to patients with available preoperative and 1‐year postoperative pulmonary function data, which may have introduced follow‐up selection bias. Patients who were frailer, had marginal pulmonary reserve, experienced postoperative complications, or were unable to return for regular follow‐up may have been underrepresented. Therefore, the findings are most directly applicable to surgically fit patients with relatively preserved preoperative pulmonary function who can complete regular postoperative pulmonary function follow‐up. Third, pulmonary function was assessed only at 1 year. The lack of serial long‐term data precluded assessment of whether the observed between‐group differences would persist, diminish, or increase over 2, 3, or 5 years. Fourth, the number of resected lesions is an imperfect surrogate for resection burden and should not be interpreted as a direct measure of resected lung volume. Resected lung volume and specimen weight were not routinely recorded and could not be reliably reconstructed retrospectively, which may have introduced exposure misclassification. Postoperative functional loss is also influenced by resection type, lesion location, lobe‐specific functional contribution, and cumulative parenchymal volume removed [13, 14, 15, 22, 23]. Although dominant‐lesion resection type was balanced and adjusted for, the cumulative burden of additional resections and exact resected volume were unavailable. Fifth, residual unresected lesions and their radiologic evolution during follow‐up were not fully characterized, which may have influenced subsequent surveillance intensity and longer‐term management. Finally, because this was a single‐center cohort from an Asian institution, the findings may not be fully generalizable to other populations or practice settings. Future multicenter studies incorporating detailed quantification of total resection burden, lobe‐specific function, and longer‐term pulmonary function follow‐up are needed to better define which patients are most likely to benefit from simultaneous resection of additional ipsilateral lesions.

Despite these limitations, the present study addresses a clinically relevant but insufficiently studied question in the management of multiple ipsilateral GGOs. Overall, when additional ipsilateral lesions are considered for simultaneous resection, the potential reduction in residual nodule burden should be balanced against the possibility of less favorable pulmonary function preservation. Surgical decisions should be individualized with careful consideration of baseline pulmonary reserve, competing risks, oncologic necessity, and the likelihood of requiring future pulmonary interventions.

Conclusion

In this propensity score‐matched cohort of surgically fit patients with multiple ipsilateral GGOs who had relatively preserved pulmonary function and completed 1‐year follow‐up, resection of ≥ 2 lesions was associated with greater decline from baseline and lower 1‐year retention of FVC, FEV1, and DLCOc‐SB than resection of a single lesion, although absolute postoperative pulmonary function values were similar between groups. These findings suggest a potential functional trade‐off when considering simultaneous resection of additional ipsilateral lesions and support individualized surgical planning that balances more extensive lesion management against preservation of lung function.
