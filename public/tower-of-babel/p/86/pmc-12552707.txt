Intelligent traffic light management using predictive and dynamic traffic flow analysis
Authors: Kuldeep Nautiyal, Durgaprasad Gangodkar, Manoj Diwakar, Prabhishek Singh, Anchit Bijalwan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Traffic congestion in urban areas is a major challenge that leads to real-time problems such as elevated pollution level, greater fuel consumption, higher chances of vehicle collisions and stressed drivers. Therefore, Intelligent Transport Systems (ITS) are needed to improve the efficiency of transportation networks, for smoother and faster travel while reducing strain on existing infrastructure. Smart Traffic Light Systems (TLS) are a critical component of ITS, helping to achieve these objectives. Adaptive Traffic Light Systems (ATLS) employ an approach that dynamically adjusts signal timings based on real-time traffic demand. Compared to traditional TLS with fixed timing schemes, ATLS is capable of meeting the dynamic requirements of ITS. This paper proposes an Adaptive Traffic Light System that predicts traffic volume for the current hour and day using machine learning. It integrates this prediction with a pressure-based method that dynamically controls traffic light phases based on present traffic conditions. A simulation environment was developed using the Simulator of Urban Mobility (SUMO) to evaluate and validate the proposed approach at an isolated intersection with traffic demand and patterns varying by hour and day. To select the most suitable traffic prediction method, a comparative study was conducted among Random Forest, K-Nearest Neighbors, Decision Tree, Gradient Boosting, and XGBoost algorithms. The proposed system was compared with recent methods with similar objectives across 12 different scenarios. Results showed an average reduction of 26.3% in average waiting time, 22.4% in average time loss, 19.4% in total time loss, 23.8% in average CO emission, and 17.4% in average CO2 emission.

Introduction

Ongoing technological advancements have driven significant urbanization and transformed modern life. For this reason, people are migrating to cities, leading to rapid urban population growth. Improved living standards have enabled more individuals to afford private vehicles, increasing traffic demand. However, present transportation infrastructure is not fully capable of handling this population boom and increased traffic volume. Therefore, traffic management and improvement of transportation network are areas that requires attention. Smart cities employ advanced technologies to improve residents’ quality of life by addressing their needs. Establishing a uniform technological base for smart cities and measuring their effectiveness in achieving their objectives pose significant challenges. Rising urban population densities have led to increased traffic congestion, causing delays, inconvenience, stress on individuals and city infrastructure, elevated air pollution levels and greater fuel consumption, prompting concerns about the need for efficient traffic management and control1. Traffic management should be responsive to real-time circumstances and should aim to prevent bottlenecks at critical junctures along transportation routes. Inadequate timing of traffic signals is a major factor in causing traffic jams and delays. Traditional signal systems operate on fixed, daily schedules for signal timing. In contrast, adaptive signal control technology dynamically adjusts the timing of red, yellow, and green lights to adapt to changing traffic patterns and alleviate traffic congestion. This paper aims to contribute to the development of a more efficient transportation system by enhancing traffic management and control at urban intersections. The proposed approach involves the combination of traffic prediction and traffic volume based TSC. Additionally, TL timings are also extended based on the predicted traffic flow. A simulation-based study was performed to evaluate the effectiveness of proposed approach in different scenarios with varying traffic demands.

The major contributions of this paper are as follows: (i) Simulation of various traffic scenarios to analyze time-varying traffic volume and patterns using the SUMO simulator. (ii) A machine learning-based prediction model that uses hour and day as inputs to estimate traffic counts for each direction which is based on historical data. (iii) A traffic control method that considers both current traffic conditions and predicted traffic flow for adaptive traffic light phase selection. (iv) An adaptive strategy that extends the duration of the current traffic light phase in response to congestion.

This paper is organized as follows: Sect. Related works reviews recent related work on adaptive traffic light systems (ATLS). Section Methodology describes the methodology adopted in this study. Section Results and discussion presents the results obtained from comparing the proposed method with existing approaches. Section Conclusion concludes the paper.

Related works

Gandhi et al.1 proposed a system that uses live images from traffic junction cameras, applying image processing and AI techniques to estimate traffic density and adaptively adjust signal timings in real time to reduce congestion and improve transit. The system achieved a traffic detection accuracy of 75–80%2. considered the dynamic states of vehicles and used a receding horizon control framework for optimizing traffic signals. A microscopic traffic simulation was used to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed scheme under various scenarios. Results indicated improved traffic flow, with minimized total crossing time resulting in smoother and more efficient movement. Additionally, the method involves the elimination of stop delays for automated vehicles in high traffic volumes3. includes Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) for improving traffic signal control. Authors have extended DRL to a multi-agent scenario, where multiple agents collaborate in controlling traffic signals in a complex road network. The results demonstrated the potential effectiveness of DRL for traffic signal optimization. The challenges of scaling DRL from a single intersection to a multi-agent scenario in large and complex road networks are also discussed. In4, the authors proposed a Linear-Quadratic Regulator (LQR) designed to adaptively reduce traffic delay and fuel consumption. The model accounts for interactions between intersections and updates adaptively, demonstrating reductions in vehicle travel delay across the network5. introduced the Urban Traffic Control (UTC) system, which is designed to manage real-time dynamic traffic flow and enhance the management of road traffic networks. UTC methodologies consider the entire traffic network, rather than just individual intersections. These methodologies include vehicle counting, traffic flow control, and lane status evaluation. The UTC system integrates with existing traffic lights, providing each lane with assurance to move while considering no-interference lane movement. This allows for the efficient management of traffic without significant changes to existing infrastructure6. proposed a method based on prediction that includes OWENN algorithm and is based on IoT. The proposed system also involves an Intel 80,286 microprocessor for traffic signal control7. leverages high-dimensional sensory information from connected vehicles, to inform the decision-making process. Deep neural networks process high-dimensional sensory inputs from connected vehicles, which are then used by a reinforcement learning agent to learn optimal control policies without manual feature extraction8. proposed a novel context-aware multiagent RL approach for mixed pedestrian and vehicles. For making decisions, authors used a flat network structure. This approach also considers the situations of pedestrians waiting at the intersection. Additionally, a context-aware mechanism comes into play, utilizing data from neighbouring agents and potential state information collected by a LSTM Network9. introduced a Quantized Max-Pressure (MP) Controller, which takes real-time vehicular dynamics as input. It is SDN-enabled and uses this information to dynamically calculate the duration of traffic light phases, utilizing dynamic time intervals to accommodate the heterogeneity of traffic conditions. In generating traffic light control signals compatible with SDN, parameters like vehicle speed and position are incorporated10. combines a linear model-based control scheme with a neural network-based approach. This combination aims to accurately capture the diverse dynamics of traffic. Additionally, a multiple-model Neural Network scheme is formulated to deal with conditions wherein uncertainties exist in both the parameters and structures of the Neural Networks. To exhibit the effectiveness of this technique, a simulation study was conducted11. proposed a traffic signal control system based on prediction of traffic flow. The objective was to ensure smooth flow of traffic at intersections by reducing the number of blocked vehicles. This system combined traffic flow prediction with traffic light scheduling12. combined traffic flow prediction and optimization of traffic signal by using a combination of BPNN and SVM for prediction and fuzzy control for optimization. Collectively, these works contribute to the development of intelligent transportation systems (ITS) by proposing adaptive traffic light strategies, including dynamic phase selection and new timing schemes tailored to varying network conditions. However, most previous methods do not account for recurring temporal variations in traffic patterns (e.g., daily, hourly, weekly) or for different flows observed at various times of day (e.g., morning, evening). The approach proposed in this paper addresses these limitations by explicitly modelling and adapting to time-varying traffic patterns and flows encountered in real-world scenarios. Additionally, a combination of pressure based-approach and traffic flow prediction is utilized for improved decision-making.

Traffic flow modelling

Traffic simulation serves as a platform for modelling real-world traffic scenarios. Simulation tools capture the complexity of dynamic traffic conditions and allows the modelling of various transportation components, including roads, intersections, corridors, optimization of signal timings and transport hubs. By creating hypothetical scenarios, simulation tools facilitate scenario testing, capacity analysis, and performance evaluation, allowing transportation engineers and urban planners to assess the impacts of traffic measures and make informed, sustainable decisions prior to real-world implementation Additionally, these tools can consider environmental factors, integrate public transit operations and contribute to safety assessments by identifying potential risk points and evaluating safety measures.

Simulation of Urban Mobility (SUMO), an open-source traffic simulation software, was employed in this study to analyze traffic management strategies and assess the impact of infrastructure changes. The study focuses on an isolated, signal-controlled 4-way junction, as shown in Fig. 2. Various traffic flow scenarios were developed by defining vehicle routes through the junction using connected network edges. Simulations were conducted hourly over a period of four weeks, producing a database of average hourly vehicle counts for all incoming and outgoing roads. The directions surrounding the junction correspond to distinct land uses: east leads to recreational areas, west to residential zones, north to markets and commercial districts, and south to schools and offices. Lane Area Detectors (LADs) are used for traffic monitoring. They observe vehicle presence within specified lane segments, functioning similarly to vehicle tracking cameras. Unlike ground-embedded induction loops, LADs cover a defined lane length configurable by parameters such as position and length. Additionally, E2 Detectors were utilized to monitor queues of stationary or congested vehicles, providing specialized outputs and maintaining records of all vehicles within their coverage areas. Figure 3 illustrates the process of scenario generation and database creation. TraCI (Traffic Control Interface), a component of the SUMO framework, enables standardized, real-time communication between SUMO and external applications, such as traffic control algorithms or management systems. This interface facilitates dynamic control and manipulation of simulations. The setup process for establishing a TraCI connection to SUMO is depicted in Fig. 413. This stated that peak traffic congestion occurs in mornings and evenings. Figure 5 shows the different scenarios that were created for the study and comparison. Predictive modelling

Various methods were utilized for traffic signal control based on predictions made by various models. These models are trained on traffic dataset which is then used for prediction or short-term forecasting of traffic demand. The objectives for such approaches include reducing congestion, improving traffic flow, reducing environmental impact and so on12. combined SVM and BPNN for the purpose of prediction of traffic14. used a LSTM model for making predictions and compared to ARIMA and BPNN. Results showed that the mean RMSEs were 61.16, 26.87 and 14.44, respectively. This comparative study also highlighted ARIMA’s weakness when large fluctuations were present. Short-term predictions for traffic flow were made based on LSTM-BiLSTM model15. Ningbo dataset was used in this work. Four different models were compared and proposed method outperformed others with RMSE value of 16.72 and MAE value of 12.63. However, this study only considered weekday traffic under normal traffic condition16. developed a future demand-based traffic signal control that was adaptive in nature. This divided oversaturation into Queue Formation (QF) and Queue Discharge (QD). This paper focused on oversaturated intersections. Comparative study revealed that the vehicle delay was reduced by 63%, 55%, and 40% when compared with other traditional methods. An adaptive TLS was proposed in17 that focused on prediction of traffic flow based on machine learning models. Five models were considered and compared in this paper that included Linear Regressor, Random Forest (RF) Regressor, Decision Tree (DT) Regressor, Gradient Boosting (GB) Regressor and K- nearest neighbour (KNN) regressor. MSE and MAE were used for comparison. Random Forest performed best with MSE value of 111.84 and MAE value of 54.8318. included a Traffic flow prediction model that applied collective learning method. Proposed approach was divided into three parts namely data collection, prediction and evaluation. Results showed that ensemble ML based method performed the best when compared to other ML methods. Authors aim to combine ML and DL based methods in the future.

Dataset generated by the simulator contains hourly traffic count for a total of four weeks, totaling 672 records. Currently, dataset only includes day and hour as features. Traffic vehicles were homogenous in nature. Traffic counts for each hour were returned by the simulator. These counts were then aggregated based on the hour of the day. In case of missing values, the average value for the same hour and day from historical data was used. To predict traffic counts, five different ML models were trained on our dataset and the one with the best performance was selected. An LSTM model was also evaluated, but it performed worse than the other five models, possibly due to the low dimensionality and limited volume of the data. Its best results were an MSE of 124.95 and a test RMSE of 11.17 during the testing phase. For finding the suitable value of parameters for models, Grid Search method was used. Adaptive control

Adaptive control refers to the ability of a system to assess present traffic condition and take decisions accordingly to ensure optimal traffic flow and reduction in traffic congestion. This plays a crucial role in the development of an ATLS. The proposed method aimed to consider not only present conditions but also prediction of traffic count made by ML model for all directions.

Liu et al.19 proposed a novel MP (Max Pressure) algorithm which was developed by the consideration of traffic delay in the transport network. It outperformed other MP based Variants in simulation tests. Total travel delay obtained in the previous time step was used for calculation of movement weights20. introduced a new traffic flow prediction model and combined it with MP algorithm for traffic signal control. Evaluation was done on an isolated intersection by using simulation. SUMO simulator was chosen for simulation and evaluation. Compared to MP algorithm, PB-ELM showed improvements of 23.26% less Avg. Fuel Consumption and 23.40% less Avg. CO2 Emission. Authors intend to extend their work in a larger transportation network. A Maximum Weighted Traffic flow approach was used in21 for ATSC. This involves first estimating traffic flow at given lane in intersection and then formation of weighted traffic flow estimate. Five different algorithms were used for comparative study. Results showed that the proposed method managed to outperform others in three different scenarios. Zoabi & Haddad22 presented a MP based approach that considers delay caused in travel times. Limitations of previous pressure-based approaches is also mentioned in this study. SUMO was used to compare the proposed approach with other MP based methods. Results showed 20% reduction as compared to other methods in terms of travel time delay. The method proposed by Agafonov et al.21 gave motivation to use a pressure-based method that not only accounts for pressure caused by vehicles present at the intersection but also the waiting time of vehicles present at intersection for pressure based adaptive control. Thus, a pressure-based adaptive control algorithm was developed, incorporating both predicted demand and current vehicle presence at the intersection. RF Regressor is selected for the purpose of making predictions. These predictions are done periodically after time interval T. Based on the combined values of prediction for traffic volume and current traffic volume, phases or state of traffic light is selected. For making the selection of phases fair, during the ongoing phase, the calculations are made for the remaining phases. So, if there are four phases P1, P2, P3 and P4 and if current phase is P1, then predictions will be made for remaining phases.Algorithm for Adaptive Control

Algorithm for Adaptive Control

Traffic counts are observed during the green and yellow times of the current phase. Based on these values, the next phase is selected, and the process repeats. The duration of each phase is based on current condition of the intersection. The timings are reactive to currently observed state. Based on predictions, the maximum and minimum allowed Green times are increased and decreased for the phases with highest and lowest predicted vehicle counts, respectively. To ensure that a condition of starvation does not form, the calculation includes the phases excluding the current phase. Ablation study

The proposed approach uses a prediction model that takes the current hour and day as input to estimate traffic counts in all directions. To select a prediction model, five ML models were considered. These models involved an RF regressor, DT regressor, KNN regressor, GB regressor and XGBoost Regressor. Grid Search was used to identify the optimal hyperparameters for each model by comparing performance across a specified parameter grid. RF is an ensemble of decision trees that averages results to reduce variance, enhancing stability and robustness. Overfitting risk can be tackled by tuning the hyperparameters to limit the trees depth. RF can effectively capture complex nonlinear relationships and interactions between features. XGBoost has built-in regularization that reduces the risk of overfitting. It is highly scalable. GB Regressor is suitable for datasets with non-linear relationships. It faces a risk of overfitting and thus requires proper tuning. DT regressor is more prone to overfitting as compared to others. Pruning is needed to reduce overfitting risk. It works good on smaller datasets with simple relationships. KNN regressor heavily depends on the choice of k. It is unsuitable for large datasets and risks overfitting with very low k or underfitting with very high k. Cross-validation is a strategy used in ML to test how a model generalizes to unseen data. In the proposed approach, K-Fold Cross Validation (KCV) was applied for assessing the models on their performance. I n KCV, the dataset is split into K folds, with each fold serving as the test set once while the remaining folds are used for training. To determine the optimal value for K in KCV, various values of K were tested. The performance of the model was then evaluated using Mean Squared Error (MSE) and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE). Comparison of models for different values of K is graphically represented in Fig. 6 (a) for average MSE and in Fig. 6 (b) for average RMSE. Table 3 (a) and (b) shows corresponding values for average MSE and RMSE for different values of K. For K = 2, the performance was worst indicating that there was need to increase the number of folds. For K = 5, performance improved a lot. For K = 7, there was not much improvement when compared to K = 5. For K = 10, best performance was obtained, and all models performed good. After selecting K = 10, MSE and RMSE values were recorded for each iteration, and the model with the best performance was chosen for further development.

Results and discussion

To evaluate the performance of the proposed approach, various experiments were conducted. The setup included the use of SUMO simulator version 1.19.0 which was used to simulate traffic scenarios and was interacted using TraCi version 1.19.0 with Python version 3.19.8. All the methods were tested in a similar simulation environment in different scenarios. These scenarios varied from normal to peak traffic flows. Twelve different scenarios were used for evaluating proposed approach for ATSC. The proposed approach involves several steps. First one is to estimate or predict the traffic flow or volume in near future for specific period of time. For the task of predicting or estimating the traffic count, five prediction models were initially considered. For the purpose of hyperparameter tuning, grid search method was used. K-Fold cross validation was applied where number of folds were determined by comparing different values of K. From comparisons based on MSE and RMSE values, the value of K was selected as 10. Next involved the usage of predictive model along-with the observation of current condition of traffic present at the intersection. Whenever a phase was allotted green time, observations were made on other phases. The traffic volume and time lost due to being halted were calculated. A minimum phase duration was set after which if there was absence of traffic the phase would be changed. The proposed approach also included implementation of a time-shift in conjunction with predictive and adaptive TSC. This time-shift was introduced for extension of allotted green time for current phase if traffic was still present after the allotted time. This was done to clear out traffic which exceeded a threshold value for occupancy of vehicles present currently at the intersection. The number of times for these extensions could be used were also bound by an upper limit to mitigate the issue of fairness for other phases. This ensures that all phases have equal chance of getting selected. Each simulation was run for 30,000 simulation steps for every scenario. Comparisons were made between Fixed TSC (FTSC)21,24–26, and proposed approach.

Conclusion

This study was conducted with the aim of developing and evaluating an ATLS. Simulation of Urban Mobility (SUMO) was used to generate traffic data to create a database containing vehicle counts for different hours of the day for each phase. This database was used for predicting the traffic at a particular hour of the day. Five ML models were initially chosen for the prediction task. Grid search was used to find optimal values for hyperparameters of models. KCV was used for cross-validation. Comparisons were made for K values of 2,5,7 and 10. After selecting number of folds or K, models were compared on basis of their MSE and RMSE values. Minimum and maximum phase durations were determined for phases that were predicted to have least and most traffic, respectively. A pressure-based approach was implemented that not only considered the QL but also waiting time or delay. This was used in conjunction with predictions made by prediction model. Based on the occupancy at intersection extensions were provided to current phase. A comparative analysis was done between existing methods and the proposed approach on 12 different traffic scenarios. Results showed an average reduction of 26.3% in average waiting time, 22.4% in average time loss, 19.4% in total time loss, 23.8% in average CO emission, and 17.4% in average CO2 emission.

Despite these results, there are several limitations in the current approach. First, the simulation scenarios were limited to an isolated intersection, which does not capture interactions between the intersection and a larger network. Second, the prediction models were trained on a dataset generated by a simulator, and therefore lack real-world data. Additionally, the dataset does not include features such as weather and seasonality, which are important for understanding traffic conditions over extended periods. Third, the evaluation was conducted solely in a simulated environment without accounting for special events such as festivals, roadworks, or emergencies. Finally, the model does not include cyclists and pedestrians who are also part of the transportation system. For future work, a network of intersections and multiple agents would be considered. EVs and heterogeneity of vehicles can also be considered. Inclusion of pedestrians and priority to emergency vehicles can also be considered.
