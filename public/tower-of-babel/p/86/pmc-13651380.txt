Background

Aneurysmal bone cysts (ABCs) are locally destructive, blood-filled benign bone lesions traditionally regarded as reactive rather than true neoplastic lesions [1]. ABCs most frequently involve the proximal humerus, distal femur, proximal tibia, and spine [1]. Calcaneal involvement is uncommon, accounting for approximately 1.6% of cases [2–6]. A PubMed search identified nine publications describing 22 cases of calcaneal aneurysmal bone cysts with detailed clinical courses (Table 1) [2–10]. Here, we report a rare case of ABC arising in the calcaneus and review the relevant literature.

Case presentation

We report a case of a 17-year-old Japanese male presenting with left calcaneal pain and gait disturbance. The patient’s medical history was unremarkable. He initially consulted another clinician for these symptoms, was diagnosed with a bone tumor, and was subsequently referred to our department. Examination of the foot showed no visible or palpable swelling, normal local temperature, and tenderness on deep palpation of the dorsal aspect of the ankle. X-ray examination demonstrated a radiolucent lesion in the left calcaneus. Computed tomography (CT) showed a radiolucent mass localized to the posteromedial calcaneus containing internal calcification (Fig. 1). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated that the lesion was faintly hyperintense on T1-weighted images and showed multiple areas of hyperintense, mildly hyperintense, and hypointense signal intensity on T2-weighted images. Areas of faint hyperintensity and hypointensity on T2-weighted images were enhanced following gadolinium contrast. Fat-suppressed T2-weighted images showed fluid–fluid levels, representing the interface between sedimented blood products and liquid components, which are characteristic imaging findings of ABC (Fig. 2). However, fluid–fluid levels are not specific to ABC and may also be observed in telangiectatic osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, and giant cell tumor. Therefore, histopathological confirmation remains essential for definitive diagnosis.

During the initial surgery, intraoperative pathological assessment was conducted via a medial approach. ABC was diagnosed, and the patient underwent curettage with phenol–ethanol ablation followed by artificial bone packing (β-tricalcium phosphate [β-TCP], AFFINOS®, KURARAY Co., Tokyo, Japan). Pathological evaluation revealed a thick cyst wall without endothelial lining, fibroblast proliferation, multinucleated giant cells, and osteoid formation, consistent with ABC (Fig. 3). Postoperative X-ray examination confirmed disappearance of the radiolucent lesion with satisfactory filling of the artificial bone. No local tumor recurrence was observed at the 36-month follow-up (Fig. 4). The patient was free of pain, and ankle range of motion was unrestricted.

Discussion

ABCs are benign osteolytic lesions characterized by blood-filled cystic spaces that predominantly occur in the metaphysis of long bones and the spine [1]. Calcaneal involvement is uncommon [2]. A search of PubMed identified nine publications describing 22 cases with detailed clinical courses of ABC occurring in the calcaneus [2–10].

Conventional treatment for ABC typically involves surgical excision of the entire lesion or as extensive a resection as possible [11]. Vergel et al. [12] reported that recurrence is most common (90%) during the first two years. To reduce the recurrence rate following curettage, which is approximately 10–20% [1], various adjuvant therapies have been developed, including the use of cement, a high-speed burr, argon beam coagulation, phenol, and cryotherapy [13].

Bone cement may act as a recurrence-reducing adjuvant due to the exothermic effect during hardening [13]. Phenol is used to sterilize lesions and eradicate residual lesional cells following curettage [13]. In a retrospective case series, Capanna et al. [14] reported a 7% recurrence rate after curettage with phenol compared with 41% after curettage alone. Bitzan et al. [15] demonstrated that curettage and phenol therapy in nine patients resulted in no recurrence. Takagi et al. [16] also reported that resection of a recurrent ABC of the talus combined with phenol–ethanol ablation prevented recurrence.

Among the 22 detailed cases of calcaneal ABCs reported in the literature [2–10] (Table 1), adjuvant therapy in addition to curettage or resection included bone cement in one case and phenol-based treatment in four cases [3, 5, 6]. Phenol-based adjuvant therapy was the most commonly used adjunctive treatment, and there were no reports of argon beam therapy or cryotherapy; therefore, phenol–ethanol ablation was used in the present case.

Among the 22 detailed cases of calcaneal ABCs reported in the literature, local recurrence was documented in only one case treated with curettage alone. No local recurrence was observed in the patients who underwent adjuvant therapy. The reported recurrence rate of calcaneal ABCs appears to be lower than that reported for ABCs arising in long bones (10–20%) [1, 12, 13]; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because of the small number of reported cases, potential publication bias, and relatively short follow-up periods in several reports [2–10]. Furthermore, most previously reported calcaneal ABC cases had postoperative follow-up periods of less than two years [2–10], which may have underestimated the true recurrence rate.

Although the available literature suggests a relatively low recurrence rate after surgical treatment of calcaneal ABCs, the number of reported cases remains limited. Calcaneal ABCs may differ biologically and clinically from ABCs arising in long bones; however, further studies with larger numbers of patients and longer follow-up are needed to determine whether the apparent lower recurrence rate reflects true biological behavior or reporting bias.

Conclusions

We report a rare case of calcaneal aneurysmal bone cyst in a 17-year-old Japanese male. No local recurrence was observed during the 36-month follow-up period. At the latest follow-up, the patient had returned to normal athletic activities without functional limitation. Although phenol–ethanol ablation was successfully used in the present case, the necessity of adjuvant therapy for calcaneal ABC remains unclear because of the limited number of reported cases. Further studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up periods are required.
