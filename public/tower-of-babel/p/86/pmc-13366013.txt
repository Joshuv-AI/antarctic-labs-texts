Telehealth Vs. Face‐to‐Face Treatment for Children and Adolescents With Obesity: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Lior Friedman, Tzach Itzhak Gil, Amit Yaniv, Eylam Ziv‐Av, Alon Manhart, Yftach Gepner, Gal Dubnov‐Raz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTelemedicine (TELE) may improve accessibility to paediatric obesity treatment, yet its effectiveness relative to face‐to‐face (FTF) care remains uncertain.ObjectiveTo compare the effects of a high‐intensity, multidisciplinary TELE intervention with standard FTF care in children and adolescents with obesity.MethodsParticipants aged 10–18 years with obesity [body mass index (BMI) ≥ 2 standard deviations] were randomized to a 6‐month TELE or FTF program in a tertiary‐care centre paediatric obesity clinic. The TELE group received 30 weekly remote multidisciplinary consultations and a gamified step‐monitoring application, along with three in‐person visits. The FTF group attended six monthly in‐clinic visits. The primary outcome was BMI z‐score changes. Secondary outcomes included body composition, weight‐related‐quality‐of‐life (QOL), adherence, and satisfaction.ResultsOne hundred participants were randomized 1:1 to TELE or FTF, of whom 65 completed the intervention (TELE n = 32; FTF n = 33). Baseline BMI z‐scores were similar between groups (2.11 ± 0.32 vs. 2.11 ± 0.29). The reduction in BMI z‐score was significantly greater in FTF than TELE (−0.16 ± 0.14 vs. −0.05 ± 0.15; p = 0.002), and any reduction was observed among 85% of FTF participants compared with 56% in TELE (p = 0.015). Body composition and QOL improved similarly in both groups, whereas satisfaction was higher in FTF.ConclusionsTELE‐based care was less effective than FTF in reducing BMI z‐score in children with obesity. Hybrid models integrating TELE with FTF visits warrant further investigation. Trial Registration: ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05700409Keywords: BMI, lifestyle intervention, paediatric obesity, telemedicine

Introduction

Paediatric obesity represents one of the most pressing public health challenges of the 21st century, with a marked increase in prevalence among children and adolescents worldwide [1, 2]. It is a strong predictor of obesity and cardiometabolic morbidity in adulthood, and is associated with reduced quality of life (QOL), emotional distress, and substantial long‐term healthcare burden [3]. Despite the magnitude of the problem, the effectiveness of existing paediatric obesity treatment programs that focus exclusively on behavioural changes remains limited.

Comprehensive lifestyle‐based interventions are considered the cornerstone of paediatric obesity treatment [3, 4]; however, systematic reviews and meta‐analyses demonstrated that average reductions in body mass index (BMI) are modest and often fall below clinically meaningful thresholds [5, 6, 7, 8]. Evidence indicates that successful weight outcomes typically require high‐intensity, long‐term interventions [3, 9]. Maintaining such intensity in routine clinical practice is often challenging. The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force reported that more than 26 contact hours are required to achieve improvements in weight among children with obesity [10], raising challenges regarding the feasibility and scalability of traditional clinic‐based care.

Engagement in face‐to‐face (FTF) obesity treatment is often constrained by logistical barriers such as school and work absences and travel to specialized centres. The COVID‐19 pandemic further intensified these challenges, contributing to adverse lifestyle behaviours among children [11, 12, 13, 14, 15], and increasing rates of obesity [15, 16]. At the same time, it accelerated the adoption of telemedicine (TELE), underscoring its potential role when traditional FTF care is difficult to deliver.

TELE‐based interventions offer several advantages for paediatric obesity treatment, as remote consultations reduce travel‐related barriers and improve accessibility. Importantly, children and adolescents are highly receptive to digital technologies, creating opportunities to integrate TELE with mobile health tools and gamified behavioural reinforcement [17]. During the COVID‐19 pandemic, we developed a mostly remote multidisciplinary treatment program for children with obesity that achieved high success rates and was not inferior to historical, pre‐pandemic FTF care [18]. Growing evidence supports the feasibility of such programs, with systematic reviews and clinical trials reporting modest improvements in weight‐related outcomes alongside enhanced engagement [19, 20]. However, while some studies demonstrated beneficial effects, others reported minimal or non‐significant impact. Moreover, many prior trials were limited by short follow‐up, small sample sizes, heterogeneous intervention intensity, retrospective design, or lack of a direct comparison with FTF care. These limitations underscore the need for rigorously designed controlled trials that compare TELE with FTF care.

The present study aimed to directly compare our high‐intensity, multidisciplinary TELE‐based treatment program developed during the COVID‐19 pandemic [18] with standard FTF care among children and adolescents with obesity. Based on the potential for increased engagement and reduced barriers to care, our previous positive experience with the program, and the absence of pandemic‐related limitations, we hypothesized that the high‐intensity TELE intervention would yield greater reductions in BMI z‐score compared with standard FTF care.

Methods

Study Design

This study was a randomized, parallel‐group controlled trial comparing a TELE‐based multidisciplinary obesity treatment program with standard FTF clinic‐based care among children and adolescents with obesity (Figure 1). The intervention period lasted 6 months, and patient recruitment lasted from February 2023 to July 2024. Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of participants and care providers was not feasible. Recruitment was performed by an advertisement brochure posted on the website of our medical centre and circulated to physician groups, with the clinic phone number for receiving initial details and booking the first clinic visit.

Study design and methods. Randomized parallel‐group trial in 100 children and adolescents (10–18 years) with obesity (BMI ≥ 2 SD, WHO). Participants were randomized 1:1 to telemedicine‐based (TELE) or face‐to‐face (FTF) multidisciplinary care for 6 months. The primary outcome was the change in BMI z‐score; secondary outcomes included body composition, quality of life, and visit adherence.

The study was conducted at a tertiary paediatric lifestyle and obesity clinic at Sheba Medical Center in Israel. This clinic utilizes a sensitive, multidisciplinary lifestyle intervention that prioritizes the child's overall well‐being and health habits rather than focusing on body weight. The standard clinic care is based on updated clinical guidelines and integrates personalized nutritional, medical, and physical activity guidance, tailored to each patient's individual preferences and physical capabilities.

The trial protocol was approved by the Sheba Medical Center ethics committee (SMC‐21‐8876), and written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians, with assent from all participating children. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Safety and adverse events were monitored throughout the intervention period. Adverse events were defined as any unintended medical occurrence that required clinical evaluation or resulted in discontinuation of the intervention.

Participants

Children and adolescents aged 10–18 years with obesity were eligible for inclusion. Obesity was defined as a BMI at or above 2 standard deviations according to the World Health Organization (WHO) age‐ and sex‐specific growth reference [21]. Additional inclusion criteria included ownership of a smartphone compatible with the intervention platform and the willingness of both the child and at least one parent to participate in the program and its follow‐up visits.

Exclusion criteria were intentionally limited to enhance generalizability and included only conditions that posed significant physical or cognitive barriers to participation in lifestyle modification or physical activity, as determined by the principal investigator. Comorbid conditions such as attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder, emotional difficulties, or mild orthopaedic complaints were not exclusionary.

Randomization and Allocation

Eligible participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to either the TELE group or the FTF group using a manual randomization procedure that included drawing a folded note from one of four plastic bags, marked either male/female with a designation of age group (10–14 or ≥ 15), prepared by a secretary who had no patient contact. The investigators were not blinded to the study arms, given the study design.

Interventions

TELE Intervention

Participants randomized to the TELE group received a high‐intensity, multidisciplinary intervention consisting primarily of remote consultations delivered via secure video conferencing, as developed in our clinic during the COVID‐19 pandemic [18]. Over the 6‐month intervention period, participants were scheduled for a total of 30 consultations, including physician visits, dietary counselling by a paediatric dietitian, exercise consultations with an exercise physiologist, and psychological support focused on goal setting and overall well‐being. Each consultation lasted 30 min, amounting to a goal of 15 contact hours dispersed along the study duration. Three in‐person clinic visits were conducted at baseline, 3 months, and 6 months for physician assessment, anthropometric and body composition measurements, and technical support. All other encounters were conducted remotely.

As part of the TELE intervention, participants were provided access to a customized smartphone application incorporating step monitoring, physical activity content, educational materials, and a gamified reward system (https://yuvital.com/solution/health/). For the purpose of this study, this freely available application was adapted for paediatric use, with the removal of calorie counting and food‐based rewards. The application tracked daily step counts and engagement metrics, awarding virtual tokens that could be redeemed for health‐related gifts. The types of gifts included skip ropes, elastic training bands, water bottles, earphones, and movie tickets. A closed community feature allowed the study participants to view anonymized peer rankings, fostering engagement and motivation.

FTF Intervention

Participants randomized to the FTF group received standard clinic‐based care, delivered through six once‐monthly in‐person visits over the study period. Each visit included dietary consultations by a paediatric dietician and exercise counselling by the physician or exercise physiologist. The visit lasted 1 h, amounting to a goal of 6 contact hours dispersed along the study duration.

Intervention Strategy

The same clinic staff provided the consultations to the FTF and TELE groups, focusing on lifestyle modification through dietary changes, increased physical activity, and behavioural goal setting. Interventions were guided by motivational interviewing principles and tailored to the preferences and readiness of the child and family. Dietary counselling emphasized healthy eating patterns and achievable behavioural changes, such as reducing sugar‐sweetened beverage intake, increasing fruit and vegetable consumption, modifying snacking habits, limiting portion sizes, and raising awareness of hunger/satiety cues. Physical activity counselling aimed to support the accumulation of an average of at least 60 min of daily moderate‐to‐vigorous activity, with flexibility to accommodate individual interests and circumstances. Psychological support focused on goal setting, adherence, self‐efficacy, and family‐related factors influencing behaviour change.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the change in BMI z‐score from baseline to 6 months. BMI z‐scores were calculated using WHO age‐ and sex‐specific growth reference standards.

Secondary outcomes included changes in body composition, daily step counts, weight‐related quality of life, treatment adherence, visit attendance, and satisfaction with the allocated treatment.

Data Collection and Measurements

All measurements were performed at the clinic by trained staff according to standardized protocols at baseline, 3, and 6 months. Participants were measured wearing light clothing and no shoes.

Anthropometric Measurements

Height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a wall‐mounted stadiometer (Seca 206, Seca GmbH, Hamburg, Germany), with participants standing upright and head positioned in the Frankfort horizontal plane. Weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using a calibrated digital scale. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared and converted to age‐ and sex‐specific z‐scores using the WHO growth reference standards.

Body Composition

Body composition was assessed using a multi‐frequency bioelectrical impedance analyser (Tanita BC‐418 MA, Tanita Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). This specific model has a very high agreement with dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry in body fat percent measurements and previously served to create body fat reference curves for children and adolescents [22]. Measurements included total body fat mass and body fat percentage, recorded to the nearest 0.1%. Participants were instructed to avoid vigorous physical activity and to fast for at least 2 h before measurements.

Physical Activity and App Use

Daily step counts were obtained for the TELE group using participants' smartphone pedometer functions or wearable activity trackers when available. Participants were instructed to activate step‐counting features at the baseline assessment for the study duration. Monthly average step counts were used for longitudinal analyses. Gifts redeemed through the app were also counted for each participant.

Quality of Life

Weight‐related QOL was assessed using the validated Hebrew version of the Impact of Weight on Quality of Life–Kids (IWQOL‐Kids) questionnaire [23]. Both child‐reported and parent‐reported versions were administered at baseline and at the end of the intervention. This questionnaire evaluates four domains: physical comfort, body esteem, social life and family relations, and generates domain‐specific and total scores ranging from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better QOL.

Visit Adherence

Visit adherence was calculated as the proportion of attended scheduled visits out of the total number of planned visits for each participant. Visit cancellations and missed appointments were recorded prospectively throughout the study period.

Patient‐Reported Experience Measure (PREM)

Participant satisfaction from the study was assessed using a modified Patient‐Reported Experience Measure (PREM) that is commonly used in our medical centre. The questionnaire included items regarding the overall quality of care, the effectiveness of consultations, and whether sufficient time was provided to discuss the child's specific needs on a 1–6 scale. Additionally, it gathered feedback on administrative and technical aspects such as ease of scheduling, the usability of the study's mobile application, and how effectively video calls served as a substitute for in‐person visits.

Statistical Analysis

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size was based on detecting a clinically meaningful between‐group difference of 0.15 units in BMI z‐score over 6 months. Using data from our previous telehealth cohort [18], we assumed a pooled standard deviation of 0.25. With a two‐sided α = 0.05 and 80% power, 44 participants per group were required. To account for an anticipated dropout, the target sample was inflated to 50 participants per group, yielding a planned total enrolment of 100 participants.

Analyses

The primary analyses of longitudinal changes were performed using repeated‐measures models. In addition, intention‐to‐treat analyses (ITT) using linear mixed‐effects models were conducted as sensitivity analyses. Results were consistent between approaches. Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed continuous variables, and as median with interquartile range for non‐normally distributed variables. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Baseline characteristics of participants who completed the intervention were compared between groups using independent‐samples t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables, and chi‐square tests or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables, as appropriate. Between‐group differences in changes from baseline to 6 months were assessed using Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA), adjusting for baseline values. Within‐group changes from baseline to follow‐up were examined using paired t‐tests. Repeated‐measures ANOVA was used to assess overall time and time × group effects. Clinically meaningful weight loss was defined a priori as a reduction of at least 0.25 units in BMI z‐score. The proportions of participants who achieved any reduction in BMI z‐score, those who achieved a reduction of 0.05 units in BMI z‐score, and those who achieved clinically meaningful reductions were compared descriptively between groups. Associations between visit adherence and changes in anthropometric outcomes and QOL measures were evaluated using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients, depending on data distribution. Similar correlation analyses were conducted to assess relationships between app engagement metrics (including compliance scores and gifts obtained) and changes in BMI z‐score and QOL outcomes within the TELE group. QOL outcomes were analyzed using total and domain‐specific scores from the IWQOL‐Kids questionnaire. Internal consistency of the questionnaire was assessed by examining correlations between total scores and domain scores. Correlation analyses between visit adherence and anthropometric outcomes, and analyses of associations between app engagement metrics and outcomes within the TELE group, were exploratory and conducted post hoc. Between‐group differences in changes in QOL outcomes were further evaluated using multivariable linear regression models adjusting for age, sex, and baseline BMI z‐score. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 100 children and adolescents were randomized equally to TELE or FTF intervention groups (n = 50 per group). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were similar between groups in age, sex distribution, anthropometric measures, and body composition (Table 1). Mean baseline BMI z‐scores were identical in the two groups. Sixty‐five participants completed the 6‐month intervention (TELE: n = 32; FTF: n = 33; Figure 2). Completers and non‐completers did not differ significantly on any baseline characteristic, including age, sex, height, weight, BMI, BMI z‐score, or body fat percentage (all p > 0.05), in either the overall sample or within each treatment arm (Supporting Information S1).

Reduction in BMI z‐score was significantly greater in the FTF group compared to the TELE group (−0.16 ± 0.14 units vs. −0.05 ± 0.15 units; p = 0.002) (Figure 3A). Similar findings were observed in ITT sensitivity analyses, the mean change in BMI z‐score was −0.14 ± 0.14 in the FTF group and −0.04 ± 0.14 in the TELE group (p = 0.002, Cohen's d = 0.73). Any reduction in BMI z‐score was observed in 85% of participants in the FTF group compared with 56% in the TELE group (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.015). A reduction of 0.05 units in BMI z‐score was seen in 50.0% of participants in the FTF group compared with 44.4% in the TELE group (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.743). At study end, 63.6% of FTF participants and 46.9% of TELE participants were below the obesity threshold (χ 2 = 1.84, p = 0.17). Repeated‐measures ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of time, indicating an overall reduction in BMI z‐score across the study period (p < 0.001). A significant time × group interaction was observed (p = 0.004), demonstrating a differential pattern of change between the TELE and FTF groups (Figure 4). Clinically meaningful weight loss was achieved by 18% of participants in the FTF group and 9% of participants in the TELE group (Fisher's exact test, p = 0.29). Mean body weight increased in the TELE group (1.98 ± 3.82 kg), whereas it decreased in the FTF group (−0.47 ± 4.42 kg, p = 0.02) (Figure 3B). In parallel, mean BMI increased in the TELE group (0.85 ± 1.41 kg/m2) and decreased in the FTF group (−1.09 ± 1.45 kg/m2), with a significant between‐group difference (p = 0.001, Figure 3C). Reductions in body fat percentage (Figure 3D) and its z‐score did not differ significantly between groups (Table 2).

Visits adherence was high in both groups, with TELE participants attending 89% of scheduled visits and FTF attending 90% (p = 0.76 between groups). Higher visit adherence was associated with greater reductions in fat mass z‐score at 6 months (r = −0.26, p = 0.04). In subgroup analyses, visit adherence in the FTF group was significantly associated with reductions in BMI z‐score (r = −0.36, p = 0.04) and fat mass z‐score (r = −0.47, p = 0.01). No significant associations were observed between visit adherence and anthropometric outcomes within the TELE group. No serious adverse events related to the intervention were observed in either group throughout the study period.

App engagement metrics within the TELE group were strongly interrelated, with higher use associated with greater reward accumulation (r = 0.55, p < 0.01). No significant associations were observed between app engagement measures or rewards and changes in BMI z‐score, body composition, or QOL outcomes during the study.

Improvements in weight‐related QOL were observed in both groups over the intervention period (Supporting Information S2). Parent‐reported total scores increased by 6.81 ± 10.7 in the TELE group and 4.76 ± 10.5 in the FTF group, while child‐reported scores increased by 3.89 ± 9.28 and 2.01 ± 9.02, respectively. No significant between‐group differences were observed (all p > 0.05), including in multivariable analyses adjusting for age, sex, and baseline BMI z‐score (p = 0.14). Domain‐level analyses showed numerically greater improvements in self‐esteem and social functioning in the TELE group, whereas family‐related QOL improved more in the FTF group. Changes in child‐reported and parent‐reported QOL scores were not significantly correlated.

PREM scores were high in both groups and were higher in the FTF group for several items, including overall satisfaction with the treatment at the clinic, the extent to which the consultation sessions were of benefit, and the clarity of the clinical recommendations (Supporting Information S3).

Discussion

In this randomized controlled trial that compared a high‐intensity TELE‐based multidisciplinary intervention with standard FTF care for children with obesity, FTF care resulted in significantly greater reductions in BMI z‐score and a higher proportion of participants achieving BMI z‐score reduction. Although TELE care was associated with comparable improvements in weight‐related QOL, it was less effective in producing anthropometric changes. Patient satisfaction was also higher in the FTF group.

The greater reduction in BMI z‐score and the numerically larger reduction in fat mass observed in the FTF group are consistent with prior evidence supporting structured, clinic‐based paediatric obesity programs [5, 6, 7]. A landmark overview of Cochrane reviews reported a mean baseline BMI z‐score across studies of 2.2, with an average zBMI change of −0.18; these figures align well with our data [5]. In‐person encounters may strengthen therapeutic alliance, enhance accountability, and facilitate real‐time behavioural reinforcement. Notably, these differences emerged despite the higher frequency of contact in the TELE arm, suggesting that mode of delivery may be as important as intervention intensity. Even modest reductions in BMI z‐score have been associated with improvements in cardiometabolic risk factors in youth [8], underscoring the clinical relevance of these findings. Although TELE was inferior to FTF for weight outcomes, more than half of TELE participants achieved reductions in BMI z‐score, and meaningful improvements were observed in psychosocial domains. Improvements in child‐ and parent‐reported QOL were comparable between groups. These findings align with evidence that paediatric obesity interventions may confer psychological benefits independent of weight change [24]. Interestingly, the proportion of children with zBMI reductions in TELE was comparable to that seen in our initial study of the program [18] (56% vs. 71%, Fisher's exact test p = 0.24). This finding suggests that the effectiveness of TELE remained similar even after the COVID pandemic restrictions were lifted.

Our findings contribute to the evolving literature on telehealth interventions in paediatric obesity. Systematic reviews and recent trials report modest but significant improvements in weight‐related outcomes with telehealth and wearable‐based approaches [25, 26]. However, telehealth encompasses heterogeneous formats that vary in intensity, multidisciplinary involvement, and parental engagement. The present study evaluated a comprehensive, high‐intensity TELE model with weekly multidisciplinary input, yet FTF care remained superior for the primary anthropometric outcomes. This highlights the potential importance of direct human interaction in facilitating sustained behavioural change in paediatric populations.

The absence of association between application engagement metrics and changes in BMI z‐score or QOL suggests that gamification and digital rewards alone may be insufficient to drive clinically meaningful weight reduction. Digital tools should currently be considered supportive components within clinician‐guided multidisciplinary care, rather than independent therapeutic drivers. The lack of correlation between child‐ and parent‐reported QOL changes is consistent with previous findings indicating that parent‐proxy reports may not fully reflect children's subjective experience [27], emphasizing the importance of incorporating both perspectives when evaluating treatment outcomes.

Finally, the finding from the PREM questionnaires that FTF consultations produced higher satisfaction, benefit, and clearer recommendations is consistent with prior research. One qualitative study reported that remote communication reduced the quality, clarity, and richness of information transfer in clinical settings [28]. One possible explanation for these findings is that FTF encounters may facilitate richer communication, stronger therapeutic alliance, and improved understanding of recommendations compared with video‐based interactions [29]. This mechanism is a possible explanation for the higher PREM scores seen in FTF. Further, we believe that this could be the main reason for the superiority of the in‐person visits seen in the study.

We acknowledge that our study has limitations. While intensive health behaviour and lifestyle treatments are foundational in paediatric obesity treatment, guidelines emphasize that their isolated impact is often modest and non‐durable [3]. A limitation of this study is the lack of long‐term follow‐up to assess the durability of these outcomes after program end, or over a longer treatment duration. Furthermore, data on individuals screened but deemed ineligible during recruitment were not systematically recorded, which precludes a complete CONSORT‐compliant screening log and limits full assessment of the sample's external validity. Although 100 participants were randomized, only 65 completed the intervention, which may have reduced statistical power for secondary outcomes and may limit the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the study was conducted at a single large tertiary centre, which may limit its generalizability to other healthcare settings.

The study also has several strengths, namely the randomized controlled design that allowed a direct “head‐to‐head” comparison of two clinically relevant care models that were delivered within the same healthcare setting, high adherence rates of the study completers, and the comprehensive assessment of anthropometric and psychosocial outcomes.

In conclusion, while telemedicine enhances accessibility and supports psychosocial well‐being, it was less effective than in‐person care for reducing BMI z‐score in children and adolescents with obesity in our study. These findings support consideration of hybrid care models, which integrate in‐person encounters with online care, to optimize both clinical effectiveness and patient experience.
