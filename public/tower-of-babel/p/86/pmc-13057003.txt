Predicting drainage capillary pressure curves in natural porous media by NMR-T2 relaxometry: implications for CO2 storage
Authors: Strahinja Markovic, Aleksandr Kurochkin, Mohamed Efara, Shehzad Ahmed, Emad W. Al-Shalabi, Waleed AlAmeri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mercury injection capillary pressure (MICP) tests are used to characterize capillary profiles of geological formations. For CO2 geo-storage, sealing and trapping capacity is often assessed by converting air–mercury curves into brine–CO2 curves. These tests, however, are typically performed only at sparse intervals along the well. To extend coverage, many studies have attempted to derive continuous pseudo-capillary pressure curves from NMR T2​ measurements. Conventional approaches match T2​ and MICP curves to estimate surface relaxivity as a conversion factor, but this assumes uniform relaxivity and simple pore geometry, conditions rarely valid for complex lithologies and requiring calibration by rock type. Because conversion of cumulative T2​ response into capillary curve depends on lithology, we introduce J parameter based on sample gas porosity and permeability (J​), bimodality index (BI) for quantifying the degree of peak separation in T2 spectrum, and the bin-weighted T2​ logarithmic mean (T2lmA​) to allow autonomous rock typing by the model. Using these features, a CatBoost model was trained on 30 core samples (carbonates, sandstones, tight mudstones) spanning permeabilities from 0.001 to 11,050 mD. For the training set, the MICP and NMR T2​ distributions of 30 rock cores were subsampled resulting in 3112 ​​ datapoints. Blind test subset comprised 6 additional cores kept outside the training loop. In the blind test phase, the model reproduced MICP curves across 10− 1–105 psi (7 × 10− 4–7 × 102 MPa) pressure range with mean R2 = 0.94, and SHg saturation mean absolute error of 3.6% indicating reliable estimation of air–mercury and by conversion to brine–CO2 curves.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-36861-z.

Introduction

Carbon dioxide (CO2) storage in underground geological formations has been intensively studied as a viable strategy for reducing atmospheric CO2 in recent years. Saline aquifers, depleted oil and gas reservoirs, coalbed methane formations, and low-permeable formations such as shales have been studied for their storage potential, each with distinct geochemistry, permeability, structural heterogeneity, and pressure-temperature conditions, all of which are integral to identifying trapping mechanisms governing CO2 containment1. These include structural and stratigraphic trapping, capillary trapping, adsorption trapping in shales, solubility trapping through CO2 dissolution in fluids, and mineral trapping via long-term chemical reactions with reservoir rocks2–4.

Capillary trapping is defined as the immobilization of the non-wetting phase by capillary forces5. The capacity of a formation to store CO2 depends on the magnitude and distribution of these forces occurring as a function of pore geometry, solid-fluid phase wetting, interfacial dynamics, and the presence of a pressure gradient2,6. Once the CO2 is introduced into the underground formation, its density contrast with the residing wetting phase initiates a CO2 rise due to buoyancy. At the same time, a certain fraction remains immobilized by the snap-off effect, particularly in places with a higher pore-to-throat ratio5,7. To prevent buoyant CO2 from escaping into the atmosphere, CO2 is often injected into geological structures comprising an impermeable top (caprock). In the context of structural CO2 trapping, the capillary entry pressure of the caprock strongly predicts its sealing capacity to the CO2 phase.

Much recent research relies on MICP capillary pressure data analysis for underground CO2 storage5,8–10. In these studies, pore-throat geometry and capillary force balance are often derived from mercury injection capillary pressure (MICP) tests, also known as mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP), and high-pressure mercury injection (HPMI). These measurements are used in saturation-height modeling to estimate residual CO2-water saturations, which are default porous medium descriptors in numerical modeling for gas storage potential in targeted formations and structural sealing potential of respective cap rocks.

For instance, at the Ketzin CO2 storage site in Germany, a recent study8 characterized the Upper Triassic Weser Formation caprock to evaluate its suitability for underground hydrogen storage. The researchers obtained seal caprock pore throat size distribution and capillary curves by HPMI tests on core samples for evaluation of capillary entry pressures for CO2 and H2 gases. Capillary data analysis suggested a caprock capacity to support CO2 column height of about 450–1100 m, and H2 column height between 800 m and 1300 m.

In another study9, authors quantified the effectiveness of CO2 as cushion gas for large-scale underground hydrogen storage (UHS) in the Yakela gas reservoir by linking pore-scale physics to field-scale performance. Capillary-pressure curves derived with a pore-network simulation show that the entry pressure drops by ~ 64% for both H2 and CO2, and the peak capillary pressure falls by ~ 63%. These simulated capillary curves demonstrate that CO2 invades pore throats earlier, creating a low-capillary-force front that suppresses water fingering and shields the H2 zone. Upscaled simulations confirm that this barrier lowers interfacial tension from 54 to 27 mN m⁻¹, cuts capillary force in half, and reduces water cut from 28% to 12% in the Yakela reservoir.

Lohr et al.10 applied MICP pressure–saturation analysis to evaluate the caprock’s ability to seal CO2 in the Tuscaloosa Shales. The MICP-derived capillary pressure-saturation profile was used to estimate the maximum CO2 column height the shale can sustain before capillary breakthrough. The Hg-air capillary pressures were converted to equivalent brine–CO2 conditions by adjusting for interfacial tension and contact angle differences. Using this conversion method, the results indicated that water-saturated shales could support CO2 columns on the order of tens to a few hundred meters (approximately 23–255 m) before leakage would occur, indicating a functional capillary sealing.

Campos et al.11 quantified porosity and permeability alteration pre- and post-supercritical CO2 injection into reservoir sandstone samples by mercury intrusion-extrusion tests. As MICP tests resolve micro- and mesoporosity reliably, the authors identified a drastic reduction in mesopore volume after CO2 injection. The subsequent pore network modeling allowed the authors to simulate Hg intrusion-extrusion and to compare structural parameters before and after CO2 treatment. Findings showed that CO2 exposure led to pore clogging or mineral precipitation, selectively targeting mesopores and significantly decreasing sandstone sample total porosity.

Tanino et al.5 combined MICP-derived pore throat-size distributions (TSD) with nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) T2 pore size distributions (PSD) analysis and X-ray micro-CT tomography to study residual CO2 trapping capacity of water-wet limestones and sandstones. A comparative analysis was performed to examine the functional dependence of pore-to-throat aspect ratio between samples using a pore network modeling approach. The findings indicated decreased CO2 capillary trapping by the snap-off mechanism at lower aspect ratios, and vice versa. Moreover, regarding the pore geometry differences of the sandstone and limestone lithotypes, their study has not captured significant dependence on residual CO2 saturation, and there is an absence of dependence in the rock’s microporosity domain.

Although MICP tests are relatively fast and inexpensive, they have numerous limitations. Due to the small size of the rock samples, a large volume of these tests is required to capture the studied formation’s capillary heterogeneity adequately. In addition, the accuracy of capillary pressure curves depends heavily on precise measurements of sample pore and bulk volumes, rendering saturation estimates prone to significant error12. Further challenges arise from conformance issues caused by logging data before the mercury-rock interface is fully established at the start of the test13. These errors are often subjective and difficult to quantify. MICP tests are also destructive, meaning samples cannot be reused for further analysis. Moreover, this type of porosimetry is limited to the use of non-representative formation fluids, which is why conversion of pressure-saturation data to the formation conditions relies on empirical correlations that introduce additional uncertainties14,15. The toxicity of mercury is an additional constraint laboratories must adhere to strict handling and disposal regulations, which increases the cost and limits the practicality of MICP testing16.

The low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) measurements are a non-invasive, fast approach to estimating pore-size distributions of natural porous media. In contrast to MICP experiments, LF-NMR is used both as a benchtop laboratory device and as a wireline well-logging device. This enables lab-developed methods and models to be used in-situ for continuous aquifer and reservoir profiling of various petrophysical properties, including capillary pressure-saturation estimates, reducing MICP-associated costs17–24. However, converting transverse relaxation time (T2) distributions from NMR tests into pressure-saturation curves is challenging in heterogeneous rocks containing intra-aggregate and multimodal porous systems such as carbonates3,14. In such systems, the correlation of T2 relaxation to capillary pressure requires extensive calibration using MICP data. This is further complicated by heterogeneities in pore surface relaxivity, non-uniform pore geometry, and the presence of clays25.

To this end, recent works have proposed data-driven approaches as an alternative to the frequent calibration and segmentation required by the T2-Pc mapping approach. The rock and pore geometry classification constraints are thus incorporated into the workflow, as are empirically determined typing parameters, attempting to derive physically informed models. For instance, Wu et al.20 propose a piecewise multi-parameter power function to map T2 time to a predefined pressure value obtained from the MICP test. Similarly as in26, the approach requires core sample classification based on rock quality index and subsequent segmentation of T2 spectrum into small and large pores. Novel proposition involves optimizing T2-Pc mapping using simulated annealing and genetic algorithm. In their following work27, authors extract multiple parameters from T2 echo-decay curves to minimize the effect of regularization loss due to inversion of echo data. Although both approaches performed well for the suite of 19 tight sandstones used for calibration, the authors point out that re-training is required for applications to different lithologies. Similarly, in28, the authors demonstrated the application of NMR echo decay parameters obtained by Laplace transform pairs (model inputs) to derive an artificial neural network-based (ANN) model using 18 tight sandstone samples. Since selection of ANNs initial weights and thresholds is non-trivial, authors use quantum genetic algorithm to improve the ANN convergence to optimal hyperparameters. Subsequently, the network is trained and tasked with capillary pressure prediction. Although the reported results indicate that the models generalize well on the test samples, they remain to be validated on complex lithologies outside of the train-test dataset. Another potential limitation of these approaches is using statistical moments derived from inverted T2 relaxation distributions or T2 decay curves, which can cause a loss of the direct correlation of discrete T2 responses with MICP pressure points, thereby reducing the interpretability of the model.

Capillary pressure prediction by NMR and digital rock simulation has been also attempted recently. In their study29, the authors used digital core samples obtained from CT scans to generate a synthetic NMR T2 distribution dataset using deep generative adversarial networks, subsequently correlating it to MICP capillary curves from different sandstones. The neural network regression model is subsequently trained on synthetic data and later tasked with prediction of capillary pressure. Still, due to the CT imaging resolution constraints, the model works reliably only for pore radii over three µm, corresponding to conventional high permeability sandstones (> 30 mD)29.

Another recent study30 uses stacked ensemble learning models to derive capillary pressure curves (water retention curves) in gas hydrate formations, by T2 measurements. The curves are indirectly inferred from Schlumberger-Doll-Research T2 permeability model which allows determination of surface relaxivity (ρ2), thereby allowing conversion of T2 spectrum into pore size distribution by Washburn equation. Resulting curves were fit with van Genuchten model for comparison and validated against grain-size based entry pressure estimates and prior numerical pore-network simulations, albeit further validation is required on experimentally obtained capillary pressure curves.

This work proposes an LF-NMR T2-based boosted ensemble model for predicting the primary drainage air-Hg capillary curve. The model is formulated to predict saturation as a function of an instance of a T2 time and corresponding amplitude, conditioned on a pressure step of interest. Assumptions of uniformity of pore-to-throat ratios, geometry, pore surface relaxivity, or diffusion coupling are redundant. Lastly, Purcell’s method converted the actual and NMR-generated air-mercury pressure-saturation curves to equivalent brine-CO2 systems31, commonly employed in CO2 storage studies to estimate CO2 capillary threshold, column height, and by extension, the sealing capacities of a caprock8,10,32.

Theory: NMR T2 spin-spin relaxation and capillary pressure

Mercury injection experiments correspond to the air-Hg system’s primary drainage process by the non-wetting phase (Hg), where the pressure-saturation curve (Pc-Snw) is obtained as an output. The relation of the capillary pressure with throat radii is established through the Washburn expression (Eq. 1)33:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{c}=\frac{2\sigma\:\mathrm{cos}\left(\theta\:\right)}{r}$$\end{document}

where r is the pore throat radius, σ is the interfacial tension between the non-wetting and wetting phase, and θ is the contact angle of the non-wetting phase with the pore wall in the presence of the wetting phase. Typically, σ and θ are well documented for various rock-fluid systems under various temperature-pressure conditions, while r is anticipated as a distribution of values5. In MICP tests, as mercury is injected into the rock by a predetermined number of logarithmically spaced pressure steps, mercury pore entry pressure is a function of r, where, for low r, higher pressure is required for the mercury to enter the pore. This allows the transformation of pressure–saturation data into the pore throat distributions.

Conversely, LF-NMR tools measure signal from H+ containing fluids in saturated porous media, where the signal amplitude is proportional to its content. More importantly, the amplitude of the NMR signal varies with the pore size, that is, with the pore surface-to-volume ratio and surface relaxivity of the pore walls. The transverse T2 relaxation time in such a system is dominated by surface relaxation of excited H+ protons34:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{1}{{T}_{2}}={\rho\:}_{2}\frac{S}{V}$$\end{document}

where ρ2 is the pore wall surface relaxivity and S/V is the surface-to-volume ratio of the pore. Pore space S/V quantifies internal surface area of pores to their volume, thereby influencing fluid-rock interactions and mass transfer. While homogenous porous systems like clean water-wet sandstones have uniform S/V and ρ2, systems with mixed composition, such as carbonates, are heterogenous in these terms. Hence, by Eq. 2, the T2 distribution of the sample is proportional to S/V, that is, the proxy for the system’s pore size distribution (PSD)34.

Further, primary drainage capillary curves quantify the rate of increase of non-wetting fluid relative to applied pressure rate, and for the constant contact angle (θ) and phase interfacial tension (σ), the primary factor affecting the rate of their change is throat radius (r), simplifying Eq. 1:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{c}\sim\:\frac{1}{r}$$\end{document}

In this sense, a cumulative saturation curve corresponding to a range of pressure points will be roughly proportional to the distribution of pore throat sizes35.

If NMR T2 distribution depends on pore size distribution and MICP test resolves throat size distribution, the T2 mapping to capillary pressure can be converted by the conversion coefficient (Fig. 1). According to Eq. 2, attenuation of the T2 signal is inversely proportional to S/V. Since S/V depends on pore geometry and size, in practice, idealized shapes are used, most often cylindrical and spherical21.4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Cylindrical:\:\frac{S}{V}=\:\frac{{F}_{s}}{r}=\frac{2}{r}$$\end{document}5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Spherical:\:\frac{S}{V}=\:\frac{{F}_{s}}{r}=\frac{3}{r}$$\end{document}

where Fs is the pore geometry factor. Therefore, considering Eqs. 2–5 the T2 relaxation time can be rewritten as:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{1}{{T}_{2}}={\rho\:}_{2}\frac{S}{V}={\rho\:}_{2}\frac{{F}_{s}}{r}$$\end{document}

Rearranging Eq. 6 to solve for pore throat radius yields:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:r={F}_{s}{\rho\:}_{2}{T}_{2}$$\end{document}

Combining Eqs. 1 and 7 yields:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{c}=\frac{2\sigma\:\mathrm{cos}\left(\theta\:\right)}{{F}_{s}{\rho\:}_{2}{T}_{2}}$$\end{document}

Assuming uniform wettability, pore geometry and surface relaxivity (e.g., clean water-wet sandstones), Eq. 8 is further reduced as follows:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{c}=\frac{C}{{T}_{2}}=C{T}_{2}^{-1}$$\end{document}

where C is a scaling constant or conversion coefficient. In literature, the scaling constant C is typically determined by transforming the cumulative T2 distribution into capillary pressure space by Eq. 9 and finding the value of C that minimizes the saturation mismatch, typically via mean square error, between the transformed T2 and the MICP curves, averaged over a group of samples of similar lithology35,36. Geometry factor (Fs) and surface relaxivity (ρ2) for natural rocks are anticipated to have a distribution of values for complex lithologies such as carbonates due to mineralogical and diagenetic heterogeneity, rendering direct T2 relaxation time mapping to capillary pressure unreliable (Fig. 1).

In their work26 further refined this approach by introducing classified piecewise power function (CPPF) method. Authors propose Eq. 9 oversimplifies pore-to-throat relationship, especially in bimodal porous systems, leading to mismatches at high Pc (small pores) in particular tight sandstones, and they demonstrate that the Pc–T2 mapping is better described as a segmented power-law relationship, instead of a single linear function. They showed that the cross-plots of Pc-1/T2 or Rc-T2 form two distinct trends corresponding to large and small pore-throat systems, separated at the valley point of the T2 distribution (coinciding with the MICP inflection). LF-NMR and MICP experiments

Of the 36 core plug samples analyzed, nine Indiana limestones, one Savonnieres limestone, and three sandstones were measured specifically for this study, whereas the NMR and MICP data for the remaining 23 samples were sourced from open-access repositories. The NMR T2 relaxation distributions of brine-saturated carbonate and sandstone core plugs were acquired using a 2 MHz Magritek Rock Core Analyzer, by Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (CPMG) pulse sequence. Numerical inversion of echo decay data was performed in Prospa 4.26, using the Lawson-Hansen non-negative least squares solver, which provided regularized solutions. The smoothing parameter was selected using the L-curve criterion39. A T2 spectrum of the plastic wrapper used to wrap the core samples was measured separately and subtracted from the T2 spectra of the core plugs. This ensured that no additional signal from the wrapper was present in the final spectrum. The scanning was performed until a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of 50 was achieved. The T2 spectrum binning was set to 120 relaxation steps. The CPMG acquisition parameters for all samples in this study are presented in Fig. 2.

MICP for nine in-house samples was performed on 1 × 1-inch core trims using an AutoPore IV 9500 system. The system recorded the volume of mercury (Hg) intruded at each logarithmically spaced 120 pressure steps (0.59-54,571 psi) and generated a capillary pressure–saturation curve. Using the Washburn model with pre-measured interfacial tension (σ = 485 mN/m) and contact angle (θ = 140˚) of the Hg-air-rock system, the corresponding throat radii were calculated (Eq. 1).

Data provision

Figure 3 illustrates the model development workflow. A database of 36 core samples was utilized in this study, obtained from routine core analysis (RCA) data, pressure-saturation curves from MICP tests, and normalized T2 spectra from NMR tests. Six samples representing each lithology were set aside for blind testing of the model (holdout set). Data provision involved establishing a function for mapping T2 time to capillary pressure Pc (T2→Pc), and subsequent derivation of model inputs from RCA, NMR, and MICP data.

Mapping T2→Pc is based on the approach presented in various literature reports24,35,36,40 by Eq. 9 (Pci=C⋅T2i − 1). In these works, scaling constant C is determined by shifting the inverse-cumulative NMR T2​ curve to obtain the best match with cumulative MICP saturation curve (Fig. 1). In this work however, Eq. 9 was used to map the typical span of T2​ relaxation distributions (0.01-10,000 ms) with a typical span of capillary‐pressure steps used in the MICP tests (from 0.59 to about 60,000 psi). As our MICP measurement comprised 120 log-spaced pressure points, the NMR echo decay was inverted into exactly 120 log-spaced T2​ time bins; then each T2 time column was sorted in descending order and aligned with Pc pressures in ascending order. A parity plot was constructed with scaled T2 time presented on log-scaled X-axis, and Pc pressure on log scaled Y-axis. A power law regression fitting was performed in form of Eq. 9, yielding C = 7.34 psi·s, and ensured a one‐to‐one mapping across the entire scaled T2​ and Pc​ ranges without relying on cumulative‐curve matching. For a step-by-step procedure the reader is referred to Appendix A.

Since the objective was to establish a single global T2→Pc ​mapping via Eq. 9, applied uniformly to all samples. In principle, any constant C would technically serve that purpose. Once selected, this value is used throughout the study, independent of sample type. Therefore, scaled T2 time values should not be interpreted as true T2 times, and similarly a global scaling constant C should not be interpreted as the average C or surface relaxivity (ρ2).

As described in Sect. 3.3, scaled T2 relaxation times (sT2i) and normalized cumulative amplitudes (Aqi) for fully brine-saturated rock samples were obtained following the procedure disclosed in earlier works24,35,36,40. By rearranging Eq. 9, we can solve for scaled T2 times (sT2).14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{sT}_{2i}=C\cdot\:\:{P}_{ci}^{-1},\:for\:i=1,\dots\:,\:m$$\end{document}15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}_{qi}=\:\frac{{A}_{i}}{A},\:for\:i=1,\dots\:,m$$\end{document}

where Ai is the cumulative amplitude at i-th instance, and A is total amplitude. The Aqi for each sT2 time is obtained by evaluating cubic spline fit from cumulative unscaled T2 distribution (i.e., subsampling).

For the proposed model to work on various types of rock, it is necessary to define a parameter that relates rock porosity, permeability and wettability. Heterogeneity of the rock can be described by relating these parameters using Leverett J-function which is used in reservoir modelling practice to average Pc(Sw) curves for a group of core samples by calculating and plotting J-curves against the measured wetting phase saturation (Sw)12,33. In this work, J​ feature is not used to construct J(Sw) curves, but to scale individual pressure instances with rock-fluid properties in physically and dimensionally consistent way12. To enable our model to work across rock types, we calculate J feature as (Eq. 16):16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{J}_{i}=\frac{{P}_{ci}}{\sigma\:\mathrm{cos}\theta\:}\sqrt{\frac{k}{\phi\:}},\:for\:i=1,\dots\:,\:m$$\end{document}

where Pci​ denotes the i-th user-defined target capillary pressure value for which model is tasked to predict the saturation. Here, we use J as an input feature evaluated at user-selected Pc​ values and do not construct J(Sw) curves. The factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sqrt{k/\phi\:}$$\end{document} captures pore-scale length and is proportional to the Reservoir Quality Index (RQI) used in rock typing and flow-unit analysis. When working with brine–CO2 or other fluid systems, and with capillary pressure test data that allow direct use of CO2 (e.g., centrifuge, porous-plate), including σ and θ allows adjustment of interfacial tension and contact angle to target conditions.

However, rock typing by J cannot be sufficient in scenarios where rocks of substantially different pore size distribution have similar ratio between permeability and porosity. In these situations, information about distribution modality and proportions of micro and macro porosity can be used to improve rock typing further. NMR T2 distributions approximate pore-size distributions and can contain lithology-specific information, such as pore system modality (single vs. dual porosity) and the log-mean T2 (T2lm). To capture the contribution of individual bin i to the overall sample T2 distribution, we introduced bin-weighted log-mean shift \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{T}}_{2\mathrm{l}\mathrm{m}}^{\left(\mathrm{A}\mathrm{i}\right)}$$\end{document} as the Ai scaled by the distribution log-mean:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{T}_{2lm}^{\left(Ai\right)}={T}_{2lm}\left(1-{A}_{i}\right)$$\end{document}18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{T}_{2lm}=exp\left[\sum\:\frac{{A}_{i}}{A}\cdot\:{ln}\left({T}_{2i}\right)\right]$$\end{document}

As a pore size distribution (PSD) typing criterion, it informs the model on the contribution of the measured amplitude to the logarithmic mean and implicitly describes distribution skewness which may be informative of lithology, thereby providing the model with more information for predicting saturation for different rock types.

Lastly, to quantify pore size distribution modality, we calculated a Bimodality Index (BI) to distinguish between unimodal and multimodal rock samples. BI is regularly used in ranking bimodal signatures in genetic studies41. Since T2 spectra are logarithmically spaced, we fit two-component Gaussian mixture to log-transformed T2 time values, to estimate each peak mean (µ1, µ2), spread (σ1, σ2) and mixing weights (w1, w2). Then, we computed Ashman’s separation index D and BI as:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D = \left| \mu_1 - \mu_2 \right| \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^{2} + \sigma_2^{2}}{2}}$$\end{document}20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:BI=D\sqrt{{w}_{1}{w}_{2}}$$\end{document}

where D is a measure of peak separation, while BI is scaled D by mixture weights w1 and w2. Therefore, if the spectrum has a strong overlap of the peaks, or their amplitudes are disproportionate, the BI value is anticipated to reduce. Figure 4 illustrates an example of T2 spectra with strong bimodality (BI = 1.69, Savonnieres limestone), and low bimodality (BI = 0.53, Pavo sandstone).

After obtaining model inputs, the resulting training dataset comprised 3112 observations and five model input features, and a target SHg [3112 × 6] obtained from 30 rock samples. The training set was standardized by Z-score. The means and standard deviation obtained from the training set standardization were subsequently applied to hold-out samples (blind set).

Model training and testing

The primary model was developed with CatBoost, a gradient boosting algorithm adapted for regression tasks. A detailed description of the algorithm is provided by Prokhorenkova et al.44. In basic terms, function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}^{t\left(x\right)}=\:{F}^{\left\{t-1\right\}\left(x\right)}+\:\alpha\:{h}_{t\left(x\right)}\:$$\end{document}builds the model incrementally such that each new base learner ht is fitted a negative gradient of the loss function L.22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{h}_{t}=\mathrm{arg}{min}E\left[{\left(-{g}_{t}\left(x,{S}_{Hg}\right)-h\left(x\right)\right)}^{2}\right],\:{g}_{t}\left(x,{S}_{Hg}\right)={\left.\frac{\partial\:L\left({S}_{Hg},{\widehat{S}}_{Hg}\right)}{\partial\:{\widehat{S}}_{Hg}}\right|}_{{\widehat{S}}_{Hg}={F}^{\left(t-1\right)}\left(x\right)}$$\end{document}

In the case of mercury saturation (SHg) for the given input x (e.g., Aqi, Ji), the model iteratively improves the predicted mercury saturation (ŜHg) by adding a new base learner (ht) that reduces the difference between true and predicted SHg. To enhance this process, the negative gradient (-gt) updates the model about the magnitude and direction of the SHg prediction error, allowing a more accurate estimate of ht in the subsequent iteration.

Another important aspect is model optimization. We used Bayesian optimization framework Optuna, by the leave-one-sample-out cross-validation (LOSO CV) approach45.

The NMR data and MICP targets for 30 samples are partitioned separately. At each iteration the model is fit on all but one sample (29 training samples) and evaluate it on the entire held-out sheet, so that performance reflects how well the model generalizes to completely unseen groups rather than to new points randomly drawn from the same group (Fig. 3).

To mitigate the risk of overfitting, which is particularly pronounced with small datasets, we constrained model complexity by limiting tree depth, applying strong L2 regularization and used a conservatively low learning rate. These hyperparameters collectively condition the model toward smoother fits rather than overfitting of training data. The optimal hyperparameters are listed in Appendix A, Table A3.

The trained model was then evaluated on the blind sample subset. This step is essential for predicting a whole Pc-SHg curve for a user-specified number of Pc pressure points. Error metrics used include the coefficient of determination (R²), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), and mean absolute error (MAE).

23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \bar{y})^2}$$\end{document} 24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{MAPE} = \frac{100\%}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left| \frac{y_i - \hat{y}_i}{y_i} \right|$$\end{document} 25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{MAE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left| y_i - \hat{y}_i \right|$$\end{document}

where yi, ŷi and n are reference SHg, predicted SHg, and number of data points, respectively.

Interaction of model inputs and sensitivity analysis were performed by SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)46. The algorithm assigns each model input a contribution weight as a function of the average magnitude of the prediction residuals, where weights reflect the average effect of each feature. Feature importance metrics in tree-based additive models are typically derived from reductions in node split impurity. This approach can bias the importance toward features with high cardinality or those that are highly correlated with other inputs, potentially masking the contribution of other informative variables and underestimating their relevance. In the SHAP method, each feature’s marginal distribution is averaged over feature subsets, where the contribution is weighted between all features regardless of the split score46.

Lastly, using Eq. 13, the predicted SHg was converted into a brine-CO2 capillary pressure curve and compared with converted pressure reference data obtained from the MICP experiment. The interfacial tension and contact angle values were selected, assuming a reservoir depth of 2000 m. Parameters listed in Table 6 were obtained from literature and used to simulate storage conditions for the brine-CO2-rock system.

The J feature is the pressure at which saturation is predicted, scaled by the sample \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sqrt{\mathrm{k}/{\upvarphi\:}}$$\end{document}. As seen at Fig. 8, high J values predict higher SHg. This is logical since as the pressure increases, mercury penetrates smaller pores increasing saturation, which is why it has the strongest influence on predicted SHg. Additionally, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sqrt{\mathrm{k}/{\upvarphi\:}}$$\end{document} scaling, informs the model about pressure ranges at which mercury starts penetrating the largest pores and the range at which full saturation (SHg=1) is achieved. For instance, Idaho sandstone with permeability of 11,046 mD, mercury pore entry pressure is at 2 psi, while for mudstone samples with permeability below 0.1 mD, entry pressure is typically over 25 psi.

Scaled T2 times (sT2) and their cumulative amplitudes (Aq) naturally have the same orientation. From Fig. 8, sT2 and J are clearly asymmetric. In MICP, the derivative of pore-throat radius with respect to saturation gives the throat-size distribution, where high capillary pressure corresponds to small pores. By contrast, in T2 spectra, large T2 values correspond to the large pores. Thus, sT2 and pressure are inversely related, so for high values of sT2 and Aq the model predicts low SHg.

For BI, SHAP magnitudes are confined to roughly − 1 to + 1, which is much smaller than for (J) and (sT2), and predictions are distributed on both sides of zero. Higher BI values nudge predictions slightly upward. For \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{T}}_{2\mathrm{l}\mathrm{m}}^{\left(\mathrm{A}\mathrm{i}\right)}$$\end{document} SHAP is nearly symmetric around zero, with low values (blue) associated with decrease in SHg and higher values (green to red) clustering near zero or slightly increasing SHg. This is expected, because BI and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{T}}_{2\mathrm{l}\mathrm{m}}^{\left(\mathrm{A}\mathrm{i}\right)}$$\end{document} are descriptors of pore size distribution modality and skewness. Therefore, they have higher order effects, that is, rather than influencing the rate of SHg growth, as is the case with pressure-dependent features, their values affect the overall shape of the predicted Pc curve, that is, the number of inflection points and the skewness of the predicted (Pc) curve overall.

Model limitations

Model limitations stem from assumptions required for mercury injection tests, which assume water-wet, fully saturated systems (primary drainage). Therefore, the model-generated Pc-Sw curves for the brine-CO2 system corresponds to scenario of CO2 injection into confined aquifers. It is worth noting that the model requires calculation of the J feature, which in principle requires independent measurements of interfacial tension and contact angles for the rock–air–Hg system, even though, in laboratory practice, empirically measured values are widely accepted. However, for conversion of air-Hg capillary curves into another system (e.g., brine–CO2), it is strongly recommended to determine both contact angles and interfacial tensions independently for the targeted reservoir rock material and its specific pressure, temperature, and salinity conditions.

Another consideration is dataset size. Subsampling core plug NMR and MICP distributions is an effective way to increase sample size, reduce the prediction variance, and smooth conditional distributions of features. However, this approach cannot guarantee that between-plug heterogeneity is fully generalized. The 36 samples used in this study were obtained from open-source repositories and originate from diverse reservoirs, which is favourable for model generalization. Nonetheless, further diversification of the dataset would be beneficial. Therefore, future works will comprise an expansion of the database to validate the model generalization over more unique lithologies. Furthermore, a case study with NMR well-log measurements for continuous capillary pressure prediction and evaluation of variable signal-to-noise ratio effects on the model will be conducted.

An additional source of error in using NMR T2 directly for pore-throat or capillary pressure inference is the presence of paramagnetic minerals (e.g., Fe-oxides, certain clays) which can enhance T2 relaxation further. Such species enhance surface relaxivity, shortening T2, and thereby bias the pore-size distributions derived under the assumption of uniform or low relaxivity.

Still, average MAE below 4% and R2 > 0.94 for both train LOSO and blind sets (Table 7) indicate that the prediction of capillary-saturation curves based on LF-NMR T2 distribution can prove to be a viable alternative to extensive MICP testing. Moreover, combined with NMR logging data, the proposed model may be extended to in-situ applications for rapid saturation-height function modeling, cap rock sealing characterization, post-injection PSD alterations, and other studies involving CO2 injection into natural porous media.

Conclusions

In this study, we presented a machine learning model for the prediction of primary drainage capillary curves for the air-Hg-rock systems using NMR T2 distributions. Predicted curves were converted to brine-CO2-rock capillary curves for three lithological groups, including carbonates, sandstones, and tight mudstones.
