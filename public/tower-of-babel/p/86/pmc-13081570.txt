Effect of TyG index on endovascular therapy in large vessel occlusion stroke with different etiologies
Authors: Wei Deng, Xiaojun Zhu, Peiyang Zhou, Jun Han, Yanli Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aims to evaluate the association between the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and outcomes in large vessel occlusion (LVO) patients undergoing endovascular therapy (EVT) with different etiologies.MethodsA total of 702 patients with acute anterior-circulation LVO due to large artery atherosclerosis (LAA) and cardioembolism (CE), who underwent EVT, were retrospectively included in this study between September 2018 and January 2024. Patients were stratified into three groups based on tertiles of the TyG index. The primary outcome was poor outcome, defined as 3-month modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score ≥ 3. Secondary outcomes included 3-month mRS, symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH), and 3-month mortality.ResultsAmong EVT patients (median age 69 years, 55.1% male), the median TyG index was 9.02 (8.53–9.59) for LAA and 8.65 (8.24–9.30) for CE (p < 0.001). In LAA patients, an elevated TyG index was associated with poor outcome (adjusted odds ratio [OR] = 1.49; 95% CI = 1.04–2.15; p = 0.031), 3-month mRS (OR = 1.59; 95% CI = 1.09–2.35; p = 0.019), and mortality (OR = 1.67; 95% CI = 1.05–2.70; p = 0.032), but not with sICH. Similar association was also oberved in CE or all patients. However, there was no significant CE/LAA and TyG index interaction on any outcome.ConclusionThis study found that while the TyG index differed between LAA and CE patients with LVO undergoing EVT, an elevated TyG index was associated with worse clinical outcomes in both groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04787-8.Keywords: Acute ischemic stroke, Endovascular therapy, Triglyceride-glucose index, Large artery atherosclerosis, Cardioembolism

Introduction

The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, calculated from the combination of fasting glucose and triglycerides, has been recognized as a novel biomarker of insulin resistance (IR) [1], with the advantage of being simple and readily accessible in clinical practice. Numerous studies have demonstrated that IR exacerbates systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and dysregulated nitric oxide production, thereby increasing endothelial apoptosis and worsening prognosis [2–4]. Thus, IR could adversely affect outcomes in large vessel occlusion (LVO) patients undergoing endovascular therapy (EVT). It has been associated with poor functional outcomes, early neurological deterioration (END), and increased mortality post-EVT, although its relationship with symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH) remains inconsistent [5–7].

Of note, the TyG index may differ in LVO strokes with varying etiologies. A higher TyG index has been observed in LVO strokes caused by large artery atherosclerosis (LAA) compared to cardioembolism (CE), reflecting differences in the pathogenesis of ischemic stroke subtypes [8, 9]. This is likely related to the higher prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and obesity in LAA patients. Additionally, Huo et al. [10] demonstrated that the TyG index is significantly associated with unstable carotid plaques, particularly in patients with diabetes mellitus (DM). The TyG index is also associated with cerebral artery stenosis in stroke patients [11–12]. Furthermore, Daoussi et al. [9] suggested that the TyG index could serve as a valuable biomarker for differentiating CE from non-cardioembolic (NCE) strokes. However, it remains unclear whether the associations between TyG index and outcomes vary by stroke etiology in LVO patients undergoing EVT, such as LAA and CE.

Therefore, we conducted this study to to examine the TyG index in LVO patients undergoing EVT due to LAA and CE, and to evaluate the association between TyG index and post-EVT outcomes in these two patient groups.

Methods

Study population

This study is a retrospective cohort study involving LVO patients who received EVT at six stroke centers between September 2018 and January 2024. The Xiangyang No.1 People’s Hospital Human Research Ethics Committee approved this study (No. 2025KY058), waiving the requirement for written informed consent. This work was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The inclusion criteria specified that patients were ≥ 18 years old, diagnosed with anterior-circulation LVO strokes due to LAA or CE, and received EVT. The study excluded patients with a prestroke modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score ≥ 2, missing follow-up data at 90 days or baseline critical information, and/or those with a serious, advanced, or terminal illness unrelated to acute ischemic stroke. Further details on the interventions are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Data collection and definitions

Clinical data of LVO patients were collected by consulting electronic medical records. These included age, sex, stroke risk factors, systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) at admission. Venous blood samples were collected by trained medical staff in the early morning of the day following admission, after a minimum 8-hour fast. The samples were analyzed by fully automated hematology analyzers to obtain measurements for fasting plasma glucose (FPG), glycosylated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), urea nitrogen (Urea), and creatinine (Crea). The TyG index was calculated using the following formula: TyG index = Ln [TG (mg/dL)×FPG (mg/dL) / 2] [1]. The stroke etiology was classified as LAA, CE, other/undetermined etiology by the TOAST classification [13] by local investigators, based on medical history, clinical and imaging characteristics of the patients.

Outcomes

Trained research coordinators, who were masked to the clinical information, collected 3-month follow-up outcomes through in-clinic interviews or telephone surveys. The primary outcome was poor outcome, defined as 3-month mRS score ≥ 3. Secondary outcomes included 3-month mRS [14], symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH), and 3-month mortality. Successful reperfusion was defined as the restoration of blood flow to greater than 50% (2b to 3) of the involved territory, as assessed with the use of the expanded TICI scale [eTICI, scores range from 0 (no flow) to 3 (normal flow)] [15]. sICH was defined according to the Heidelberg Bleeding Classification [16], occurring within 48 h.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Missing baseline data were minimal and imputed using multiple imputation methods, as detailed in the Supplementary Material. Continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while those with a non-normal distribution are reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. Univariate comparisons were made using the Kruskal–Wallis test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the chi-squared (χ²) test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

Patients were classified into three groups based on TyG index tertiles: T1 ≤ 8.51, 8.51 < T2 ≤ 9.18, and T3 > 9.18. TyG index was also analyzed as a continuous variable to enhance the robustness of the findings. The association between TyG index and binary outcomes was assessed using binary logistic regression models. Ordinal logistic regression was used to calculate the common odds ratio for a 1-point worsening shift on the mRS. These analyses were conducted for all patients, and separately for those with LAA and CE strokes. Three distinct models were developed to facilitate a comprehensive analysis: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 included adjustments for age, sex; and Model 3 contained additional adjustments for atrial fibrillation, hypertension, smoking, Urea, intravenous thrombolysis (IVT), National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) [17], Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography Score (ASPECT) [18], onset to puncture time, anesthesia, successful recanalization, and occlusion location. The confounders adjusted for in this analysis were selected based on their clinical relevance. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with values below 5 deemed acceptable. Additionally, the interaction between the continuous TyG index and stroke etiology (LAA vs. CE) on each outcome was analyzed using binary or ordinal logistic regression in all patients, based on Model 3. Sensitivity analyses were conducted in patients with successful recanalization with similar statistical methods as above.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among a total of 1475 patients who underwent EVT, 702 eligible patients (median age 69 years, 55.1% males, median baseline NIHSS 16, median onset to puncture time 329 min) were analyzed, including 305 LAA and 397 CE stroke patients (flowchart in Supplemental Figure S1). Compared to CE stroke patients, LAA stroke patients were younger, more likely to be male, smokers, and have hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, and a longer onset to puncture/recanalization time (p < 0.05). However, they had a lower median baseline NIHSS (p < 0.001; Table 1).

The proportions of patients across TyG index tertiles differed between those with LAA and CE strokes. Higher TyG index tertiles were associated with a greater proportion of patients with LAA strokes, while the opposite was observed for CE strokes (T3: LAA 39.9% vs. CE 28.6%; p < 0.05; Table S1 and Supplemental Figure S2). Additionally, among all patients, those with an elevated TyG index were more likely to have hypertension and diabetes but less likely to have atrial fibrillation (p < 0.05).

In LAA stroke patients, those with an elevated TyG index had higher laboratory values (TG, FPG, HbA1c, TC, LDL-C; p < 0.05) and a higher prevalence of hyperlipidemia and diabetes (p < 0.001). However, no significant differences were observed in other baseline characteristics among the three subgroups (Supplemental Table S2). Similarly, in CE stroke patients, those with an elevated TyG index had higher laboratory values (TG, FPG, HbA1c, TC, LDL-C; p < 0.05) and a higher prevalence of hyperlipidemia and diabetes (p < 0.001), but no significant differences in other baseline characteristics (Supplemental Table S3).

Associations of the TyG index with outcomes

In all patients, an elevated TyG index was significantly and independently associated with poor outcome (Model 3: adjusted OR = 1.64; 95% CI = 1.30–2.10; p < 0.001), 3-month mRS shift (OR = 1.71; 95% CI = 1.34–2.21; p < 0.001), and mortality (OR = 1.56; 95% CI = 1.20–2.03; p = 0.001). However, no significant associations were observed between the TyG index and sICH in any multivariate regression model (Supplemental Tables S4). To further explore the relationship between TyG tertiles and outcomes after EVT, binary or ordinal logistic regression analyses were performed using the lowest tertile (T1) as the reference group. After full adjustment (Model 3), patients in the T3 tertile had a significantly higher risk of poor outcomes, while no significant difference was found between T2 and T1.

In LAA stroke patients, an elevated TyG index was significantly and independently associated with poor outcome (Model 3: adjusted OR = 1.49; 95% CI = 1.04–2.15; p = 0.031), 3-month mRS shift (OR = 1.59; 95% CI = 1.09–2.35; p = 0.019), and mortality (OR = 1.67; 95% CI = 1.05–2.70; p = 0.032). TyG tertiles were strongly associated with the 3-month mRS distribution (Model 3: T3 vs. T1 OR = 3.21; 95% CI = 1.18–9.59; p = 0.028; Fig. 1). However, no significant association was found between TyG index and sICH in any multivariate regression model (Table 2 and Fig. 2). In CE stroke patients, an elevated TyG index was associated with similar results in both primary and secondary outcomes as observed in LAA patients (Fig. 2). When the TyG index was treated as a categorical variable, the association with poor functional outcomes was stronger in CE patients. Specifically, compared to T1, T3 was independently associated with an increased risk of poor outcome, higher 3-month mRS, and mortality across all multivariate regression models (all p < 0.05). In contrast, in LAA stroke patients, T3 was significantly associated with higher 3-month mRS and mortality only in Models 2 and 3 (Table 2).

Moreover, the overall interaction between the three TyG tertiles and stroke etiology (LAA vs. CE) on poor outcome in LVO patients undergoing EVT was not statistically significant (p = 0.91). Additionally, the interaction between the continuous TyG index and stroke etiology on outcomes after EVT did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 2).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were performed on 644 of 702 (91.7%) total patients, 272 of 305 (89.2%) LAA patients, and 372 of 397 (93.7%) CE patients with successful recanalization. The results were consistent, showing that an elevated TyG index was associated with increased risks of poor outcome, 3-month mRS shift, and mortality in both LAA and CE stroke patients, except for sICH. Similar to the overall analyses, the interaction between the continuous TyG index and stroke etiology (LAA vs. CE) on outcomes did not reach statistical significance in the sensitivity analyses (Supplemental Figure S3).

Discussion

In a multicenter, retrospective cohort study, we found that the TyG index differed in LVO patients undergoing EVT due to different etiologies. Although the TyG index was higher in LAA stroke patients than in CE patients, an elevated TyG index was associated with increased risks of poor outcome, 3-month mRS shift, and mortality, both in all patients and within the LAA and CE subgroups. However, no significant association was found with sICH. Importantly, no statistically significant interaction was observed between TyG index and stroke etiology (LAA vs. CE) regarding the outcomes.

Previous studies have demonstrated that diabetes and admission hyperglycemia are associated with poor clinical outcomes in patients undergoing EVT [19–21]. Additionally, admission hyperglycemia has been linked to increased infarct growth [22] and lower rates of successful recanalization [23]. However, in the late time window, Kersten et al. [24] found no evidence to suggest that admission glucose levels or hyperglycemia influence EVT outcomes in LVO patients undergoing EVT. In recent years, the TyG index, a composite marker derived from FBG and TG, has emerged as a key indicator of metabolic syndrome, encompassing conditions such as diabetes, obesity, hypertension, and dyslipidemia [25]. The TyG index has been strongly associated with carotid atherosclerosis [10] and cerebral artery stenosis in stroke patients [11, 12]. In patients treated with IVT, several studies have reported that an elevated TyG index is linked to poor outcomes and END [26–27]. However, studies examining the relationship between the TyG index and outcomes in patients undergoing EVT are limited and present inconsistent findings [6–7]. This may be due to the more severe condition of LVO patients and the complex factors influencing outcomes, highlighting the need for subgroup-specific studies. Previous research has shown that an elevated TyG index is strongly associated with an increased risk of LAA stroke, helping differentiate it from CE stroke [9, 28]. Therefore, exploring the TyG index and its impact on EVT outcomes across different LVO stroke subtypes is important for better patient selection and prognostication, as well as guiding periprocedural therapy.

In this study, we found that the TyG index differed in LVO patients undergoing EVT due to different etiologies, consistent with previous findings [9, 28]. More importantly, an elevated TyG index was associated with increased risks of poor outcome, 3-month mRS shift, and mortality, both in all patients and within the LAA and CE subgroups. To further validate these findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses in patients with successful recanalization, which yielded consistent results. The findings were consistent with a previous small-scale study, where an elevated TyG index was associated with poor outcome at 90 days (OR 1.931, 95% CI 1.204–3.097) and mortality at 90 days (OR 2.384, 95% CI 1.093–5.199) in LVO patients undergoing EVT [6]. And Minwoo Lee ea al. [5] discovered that an elevated TyG index was associated with poor outcomes in patients who received reperfusion therapies. Notably, this study found no association between the TyG index and sICH in patients undergoing EVT for LAA and CE, consistent with the findings of Huang et al. [7] and two other independent studies in AIS patients treated with IVT [26, 29], but inconsistent with the results of Sun et al. [6]. This discrepancy may be attributed to underlying mechanisms and the potential confounding effect of other risk factors, which may obscure the relationship between the TyG index and outcomes. In LAA and CE stroke patients, despite the higher comorbidity of conventional vascular risk factors (hypertension, obesity, diabetes mellitus) in LAA patients, the conclusions from both groups were consistent.

While the underlying mechanism remains incompletely understood, several possible explanations exist for these findings. IR contributes to systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and dysregulated nitric oxide production, all of which can deteriorate vascular endothelial function and impair collateral vessel remodeling [2–4]. Previous studies have indicated that inflammatory cytokines partially mediated the association between IR and the prognosis of ischemic stroke [7, 30, 31]. Moreover, the dyslipidemia components reflected by the TyG index, including elevated triglycerides, exacerbate vascular injury and atherosclerotic burden, which contribute to arterial stenosis or occlusion [32]. Additionally, IR can lead to hemodynamic disturbances, particularly in the context of hypotension or cerebral hypoperfusion, and disrupt brain metabolism through inflammatory and oxidative stress mechanisms, thereby worsening ischemic stroke injury [33]. Consequently, an elevated TyG index likely exacerbates poor stroke outcomes through insulin resistance-induced endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and dyslipidemia.

However, this study has several limitations. First, as a multi-center, retrospective, observational study, it can only suggest associations, not causality. Second, while the TyG index is a practical tool, it remains an indirect measure of IR and would benefit from validation through direct assessments in future research. Third, due to its retrospective design, some data were missing, such as thrombectomy type, thrombus escape, stent implantation, and procedure-related complications, which warrants cautious interpretation of the findings. Fourth, the study was conducted in Chinese patients with anterior-circulation LVO, and the results may not be generalizable to other populations or posterior-circulation LVO. Future research involving more diverse populations is needed to enhance generalizability.

In conclusion, we found that the TyG index, as a marker of IR, was significantly associated with increased risks of poor outcome, 3-month mRS shift, and mortality in LVO patients undergoing EVT. These findings were consistent in both LAA and CE stroke subgroups, highlighting the TyG index as a modifiable factor that warrants further investigation to improve post-EVT stroke outcomes.
