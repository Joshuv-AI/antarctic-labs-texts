Application of the Medical Research Council guidance for complex interventions in the development of VIeSA, an intervention to support healthy ageing among community-dwelling older adults
Authors: Eleni-Marina Ashikali, Catherine Ludwig, Christophe Graf, Irina Ionita, Catherine Busnel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe ageing population, coupled with the desire to age-in-place, highlight the need for programs that target health promotion as a means of maintaining functional ability, autonomy, and independence among community-dwelling older adults. This paper describes the development of the VIeSA intervention, which aimed to model a healthy ageing trajectory, including the identification of the necessary tools and methods, that would allow people older people, in partnership with health and social care professionals, to define personal health-related goals and the actions to achieve them. A key element of the intervention development was the creation of a support tool intended to assist this process.MethodsThe UK Medical Research Council (MRC) guidance on developing and evaluating complex interventions was applied in the development of the intervention and of the support tool. A participatory approach was selected, with stakeholders engaged on different occasions to allow the refinement of the intervention and of the support tool. Following the steps and suggested actions in the MRC development phase, the development process was conducted by identifying (1) the evidence base and (2) the theoretical framework and (3) by modelling the process and outcomes of the intervention.ResultsFollowing a literature review on effective interventions for functional ability, draft 1 of the support tool was designed. Focus groups with stakeholders provided feedback on this draft allowing for its refinement in terms of content, language use, and structure (draft 2). A review of the approaches for health promotion delivery led to further additions to the tool (draft 3) and informed the content of the training of health and social care professionals. After their training, professionals provided feedback on the acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility of different elements of the intervention. Results suggested that no further major refinement to the intervention or support tool was necessary.ConclusionsThe design and development of the VIeSA intervention using the MRC guidance allowed for a clarity of direction, an optimised content in terms of usefulness and accessibility for all concerned stakeholders, and greater opportunities for its implementation and uptake.

Background

The world demographic shift includes an ageing population, with people aged 65 and over expected to be more than double the number of children under the age of five by 2050 [1]. The heterogeneity of the older population, characterised by different life trajectories and experiences [2] suggests that when it comes to health care, “one size does not fit all”. This calls for a shift from largely disease-centred approaches and hospital-centric intervention models [3] to more preventative and personalised approaches, which focus on individuals’ needs, objectives, capacities, and resources [4]. In order to allow older adults to maximise their years and to remain at home in the best possible conditions, there is therefore a need for programs which promote healthy ageing in a personalised manner.

Healthy ageing: definition and international initiatives

In 2015, the World Health Organization (WHO) defined healthy ageing as “the process of developing and maintaining functional ability that enables well-being in older age” [5, p. 28]. Functional ability refers to the elements which enable individuals to live meaningfully: meeting basic needs; learning and making decisions; being mobile; building and maintaining relationships; contributing to society [6]. It results from the interaction between intrinsic capacity, an individual’s mental and physical abilities, and the environment (e.g. home, community, broader society) [5, 6]. As such, this perspective of healthy ageing moves the focus away from disease and is consistent with WHO’s 1948 definition of health “as a state of complete physical, mental, and social wellbeing”, rather than merely an “absence of disease or infirmity [7, p. 16].

More recently, the United Nations (UN) member states and WHO adopted a 10-year plan of action, formally known as the UN Decade of Healthy Ageing (2021–2030). This worldwide collaboration initiative aims to ensure that all older people are able to enjoy healthy longer lives [8] through four main action areas: combatting ageism, creating age-friendly environments, providing integrated care, and ensuring access to long-term care.

Four “enablers”, key in addressing the action areas, were also identified, consisting of strengthening research and innovation, building leadership and capacity for integrated care, providing a voice and meaningful engagement with older people, family members, caregivers, communities, and connecting stakeholders around the world to share experiences. As such, two of these enablers place focus on the importance of including older persons and wider stakeholders in healthy ageing initiatives. “A pathway [bringing them] together to accelerate implementation and optimise functional ability” [6, p. 21, 9] has been specified, offering a concrete operationalisation for healthy ageing initiatives. The pathway proposes 6 points: clarify what aspects of functional ability will be prioritised; identify evidence on interventions addressing intrinsic capacity and functional ability; design programs or interventions and assess them with older people and their families; evaluate implementation and effectiveness; reach potentially marginalised individuals; accelerate impact.

The Decade of Healthy Ageing is in line with the necessary shift from disease-focused health models, to care that is able to respond to older people’s individual needs and objectives [6, 10] and which can support them to identify and develop their own strengths and capacities [11].

Interventions in health: models and principles

Interventions in health, refer to any action or strategy implemented to bring about a change in health status, behaviour, or health-related outcome among individuals or entire populations. Such interventions can be simple, with few interacting components and a linear causality, or complex, such as multifaceted programs or strategies the encompass several interacting components [12, 13]. With a range of people likely to be concerned by a given intervention- from those it is aimed at, to the professionals delivering it, or the community policy makers who may choose to adopt it,- stakeholder involvement becomes a primary objective in the development of health interventions [14–20].

Different degrees of involvement have been identified [15, 21], ranging from information, whereby stakeholders receive information, but do not play a contributing role, to co-decision, where they are considered as equal members in the development of interventions and in decision-making [21, 22]. Co-design and co-creation are learning processes in which different partners pool their respective skills to create new approaches [23] to the ‘initiation and/or design process.’ Co-creation in health promotion is a complex intervention [24] it must integrate various stakeholders and also consider an environment conducive to aging in place by promoting autonomy and engagement among older adults [25].

The Montreal Model [20] puts forward the concept of partnership between “patients” (i.e. the person concerned by care or services, and his/her family/caregivers) and health and social care professionals (HSCPs). As such, it considers the patient as a full actor and partner, at an equal level as HSCPs. Offering an interesting perspective on participation, it defines the continuum of involvement, from information to partnership, in five different domains relevant to healthcare [20]. For the domain of research, involvement ranges from the provision of information about research initiatives, to full involvement of patients in research, including the definition of research questions to the diffusion of results [20]. Patient and public involvement (PPI) [26] is increasingly considered as an integral element to research [27] defined “as research being carried out ‘with’ or ‘by’ members of the public, rather than ‘to’, ‘about’, or ‘for’ them” [26].

Involving relevant stakeholders, the end-users of interventions, in the development of interventions may shed light on the different needs of these groups, allowing for the refinement of interventions in such a way that they may be more relevant, useful, and beneficial to everyone involved [28].

Interventions rooted in health promotion require to question their delivery methods by HSCPs, the principal operators in the process, and their reception by the people concerned. The role of HSCPs in disease-centred health care models is to treat or cure, and health promotion imposes a shift in this role, raising the issues of HSCPs’ competencies, training needs, and roles in such efforts [29–31].

The present article

Using the guidance for the development and evaluation of complex interventions put forward by the UK Medical Research Council (MRC) [12, 13], the present article presents the development of an intervention, which sought to enable the creation of personalised health trajectories among older persons, from the definition of personal health-related objectives, to the identification of actions to address these. More specifically, through two key stages, the VIeSA (VIeillissement en SAnté, i.e. “healthy ageing”) intervention [32, 33] aimed to:

Create an evidence-based and informative support tool for use by older people and HSCPs. The support tool aimed to provide scientific findings for healthy ageing and to contextualise these locally by providing information on local organisms and activities geared at older people.Define a healthy ageing “pathway”, that is a set of methods and tools to bring together members of a multidisciplinary and interprofessional team in the health and social care fields (also including the individual, and family members) to agree on the tasks to be accomplished for healthy ageing. It is the concrete expression of a program designed to optimise aging by guaranteeing high-quality, efficient preventive services [34].

Methods

Design

A complex intervention design was chosen due to the interacting components of the intervention in terms of the stakeholders, the nature of the intervention (individualised), its variable setting (home visits), its delivery by different HSCPs with diverse professional backgrounds and experiences, and its reception by a heterogeneous population with different needs and pre-existing conditions.

The MRC framework is composed of four distinct phases: development, feasibility, evaluation, and implementation [13]. Several updates to the framework have been made, with specific and more detailed guidelines being presented for distinct phases [36, 37]. The present article reports on the development phase of the VIeSA intervention through the three key steps put forward by the MRC: (1) identifying the evidence base, (2) identifying theoretical framework, and (3) modelling process and outcomes [12]. Highlighted in this reporting is the engagement in the suggested actions [36] for the development phase, which include involving stakeholders, bringing together a team, reviewing evidence and drawing on existing theories, articulating program theory, undertaking primary data collection, understating the context of the intervention, refining the intervention, and considering future implementation.

In addition to the complex design, an iterative PPI methodology was also selected in order to maximise the acceptability, appropriateness and feasibility of the intervention and its material among concerned stakeholders [28] “and to enhance prospects of achieving change in policy or practice” [13, p. 5].

Setting

The intervention was developed in the canton of Geneva, Switzerland, through the collaboration of four major institutions: the Geneva Institution for Home Care, the Geneva University Hospital, the Geneva School of Health Sciences of the University of Applied Sciences and Arts Western Switzerland (HES-SO), and the Plateforme du réseau seniors Genève, an institution which brings together member associations and public bodies committed to the quality of life of older people. These institutions, which are major local “players” in health and social care for older people, were represented in the development of the VIeSA intervention through the participation of one member from each institution in the steering committee. The areas of expertise characterising the steering committee were therefore in domiciliary care, geriatrics, gerontology, and research methodologies, and in local social organisations/associations for older people. The steering committee (N = 4) was complemented by a research team (N = 4) composed of a social psychologist and researcher, a doctor (geriatrician with specialisation in psychiatry), a clinical nurse, and a sociologist with expertise in the local context.

Stakeholder partners

Older persons, community representatives, and HSCPs were invited to contribute to the development of the intervention on several occasions, by sharing their views and expressing their needs in focus groups (stakeholder N = 36), and through questionnaires (stakeholder N = 11) examining the acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility of different elements of the intervention. Details of their characteristics are provided in the relevant parts of the Results section.

MRC development phase step 1: identifying the evidence base

To develop a first draft of the evidence-based intervention material, a literature review on interventions for healthy ageing in community-dwelling older people was carried out [40]. Once draft 1 of the support tool was designed, focus groups were organised with stakeholders to obtain their feedback and suggestions, as well as to explore their potential needs from such a document.

Literature review

Following WHO’s definition of healthy ageing as developing and maintaining functional ability, a targeted literature review of recommendations and effective interventions for the reinforcement or maintenance of intrinsic capacities and functional ability in community-dwelling people aged 50 and over was carried out. The intention of this literature review was to subsequently design complex intervention support material that relies on scientific evidence.

Literature review on effective interventions for healthy ageing among seniors.

Identification of delivery approaches in PCC

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses were extracted from a recent Evidence Gap Map (EGM) on interventions to improve functional ability among community-dwelling older people [41]. EGMs are digital interactive resources which map existing evidence and identify research gaps on a specific topic. Developed by WHO, EGMs are intended to render research of practical use to stakeholders by allowing them to identify “which interventions are supported by research, which are not, and which need more research” [42]. Evidence-based guidelines were identified from the databases of three key institutions in healthcare: WHO, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), and the United States Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) using the search terms “older”, “older people”, and “seniors”. The outcomes of interest were related to: (a) intrinsic capacity (sensory, locomotor, cognitive, vitality, and psychological); (b) functional ability (social relationships, performance of activities of daily living and instrumental activities of daily living); (c) physiological system health; and (d) caregiver support.

Findings

Twenty meta-analyses and/or systematic reviews, and 18 guidelines were included in the review. The identified interventions (N = 54) were mapped onto the relevant health domains and classified as promising or effective, according to the strength and certainty of the evidence. Full details on the methodology and findings of this review can be found in a separate article [43].

Development of the support tool (draft 1)

Draft 1 of the support tool summarised the findings from the literature review in the form of fact sheets which presented effective and promising interventions, as well as international recommendations for each health domain. To link this scientific knowledge to the local context, local organisms (associations, clubs, institutions) geared at older people were identified according to predefined inclusion criteria agreed upon by the research team and steering committee (e.g. organisms specific to persons aged 50 and over, offering individual or collective activities which are regular, and which address the healthy ageing domains researched in the literature review [43] and outlined by WHO [44]). Information on the activities these that organisms offered was gathered and presented in the support tool in a detailed format listing including, to the extent possible, the location, timings, and cost for each. In designing the support tool, the research team took into consideration the local context and the intended audience by applying strategies for enhancing cultural appropriateness [40, 45]. These included evidential and linguistic strategies, such that the support tool was designed in the local language (French) to present scientific evidence in a manner that is comprehensible by all concerned stakeholders (i.e. older persons, HSCPs, caregivers), and to consider different levels of health literacy, “the degree to which individuals have the ability to…understand, and use information…to inform health-related decisions and actions” [46].

Stakeholder focus groups

Following the creation of the support tool (draft 1), we turned to stakeholders (N = 36) to collect primary data on their views on it, to understand their needs, and to refine the material in terms of presentation and content. We identified stakeholders as the persons concerned by the intervention (seniors), those delivering it (HSCPs), and the service providers (local associations offering activities and communities). Seniors (aged 65 and over; N = 4), health care professionals (e.g. nutritionists; N = 13), social care professionals (e.g. association representatives; social workers; N = 9), and community/canton representatives (N = 10) participated in five focus groups carried out between May and September 2022. Stakeholders were recruited through flyers distributed in the community, institutional social media posts and newsletters, direct emails to local associations, and local communities. Participants called or emailed the research team in order to express their interest in the study, ask any questions about the procedures, and sign up to participate.

The first focus group was composed of a range of stakeholders, the remaining four were with each stakeholder group separately (i.e. seniors; communities; health care professionals; social care professionals). We chose to hold sessions with stakeholder groups separately to allow a focus on the reactions and needs of each group and to ensure that participants felt confident to express their thoughts among peers. Semi-structured interviews focused on the content of the prototype (clarity, missing information), as well as its relevance, usefulness, and usability. The sessions were held at a local association, easily accessible via public transport. They were conducted by two members of the research team, following an identical procedure beginning with a brief presentation of the intervention project, dedicated time for participants to look at the support tool, and ending with a discussion around the interview questions. Sessions were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim.

A deductive thematic analysis [47] was performed on each session individually by one member of the research team, with two sessions coded by a second, and team meetings organised to obtain consensus on the final themes [48].

Findings

Table 2 provides an overview of the main themes and subthemes, illustrated with quotes from different stakeholders. The semi-structured interview questions provided the main two themes in the analysis: content and applicability. Two sub-themes were identified in each: (1) expressed needs and clarity in terms of the content, and (2) relevance and usefulness of the support tool within the local context. Overall, stakeholders expressed an interest in draft 1 of the support tool, considering the initiative to present scientific evidence in a synthesised manner that is accessible to all interested parties useful. They highlighted the need to simplify the support tool, both in terms of the type and the amount of information provided, as well as in the language used. This complicated nature of the support tool impacted views on its utility, with some stating they wouldn’t use it in its current state, and others considering it as a useful tool for their profession. There was a consensus between stakeholders that it would be difficult for users to have a meaningful engagement with the support tool without being accompanied. In cases where stakeholder groups expressed contradictory needs or concerns regarding the support material, team discussions permitted to select the option which was practically most feasible, whilst alternative solutions to differing needs were considered. For example, whereas seniors mentioned the usefulness of having detailed information on local activities (e.g. location, schedule), canton and social association representatives highlighted that this would be an extremely difficult endeavour, given the number of service providers and the evolving nature of the activities (Table 2, expressed needs). These differing points-of-view were addressed with an alternative solution, as outlined below.

Modifications and refinements to the support tool (draft 2)

The findings from the focus groups led to several modifications and refinements to the support tool, and the development of a second draft. Draft 2 of the support tool contained two distinct chapters presenting: (1) the health domains (in the form of fact sheets), (2) the local context (local organisms for older people). The idea of providing a detailed listing of activities offered by these organisms was discarded due to the rapid obsolescence of this information. Instead, maps with information on each local community and a directory of local organisms were created in order to orient older persons and HSCPs in the community. The home environment was added as a health domain, following emphasis placed on this by stakeholders as a key element to consider within healthy ageing, and the language was revised and simplified to increase comprehensibility. MRC development phase step 2: identifying theory

The overarching approach adopted in the VIeSA intervention was the Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) approach by WHO [44], which offers a particularly interesting theoretical and operational framework, given that it was developed to consider the older population specifically, and to directly address the aims of the UN Decade of Healthy Ageing. The ICOPE adopts the theoretical framework of healthy ageing put forward by WHO [5], and proposes evidence-based recommendations for the reinforcement of each of the health domains [44]. It offers an operational framework by defining five steps in the provision of integrated care: screening, person-centred assessment in primary care, developing personalised care plans, ensuring referral point and plan monitoring, and engaging communities and carers [49].

MRC development phase step 3: modelling process and outcome

Review of health promotion delivery

To identify consultation methods and professional tools for health promotion which are line with personalised and individualised care, a basic narrative review was carried out. The intention was to use the findings to inform the procedures of the intervention, the development of the support tool, and the training of HSCPs who would be recruited to deliver the intervention. Given the choice to adopt the ICOPE approach as the theoretical and operational framework for the VIeSA intervention, the guidance handbook was used as a core document. National initiatives were targeted through Promotion Santé Suisse (PSS), which is a foundation legally mandated by the Federal Office of Public Health to initiate, coordinate, and evaluate measures to promote health and prevent disease. Guidelines were identified by searching the PSS website (www.promotionsante.ch) for “health promotion” and “older people” (in French).

Findings

Guidelines for the implementation of health promotion consultations in older people were identified from the PSS website [50]. Considering the notion that the shift in the model of care (from curative to preventive and personalised) implies a shift in approaches to care by HSCPs [51], the PSS guidelines [50] outlined a series of roles that HSCPs can adopt. Seven different roles were formulated, such that the preferences of the older person in how he/she wants to be accompanied by a professional can be taken into account. The roles range in the degree to which they are directive, from observer (least directive) to leader (most directive), with different relational approaches to adopt in each (Fig. 4).

Closely linked to the efficient delivery of personalised care are the goals and aspirations of the older person. These objectives should be developed according to SMART criteria, such that they are Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Realistic, and Time-bound [50]. SMART objectives allow for the creation of specific goals with clearly defined actions and criteria to attain them and to measure their success. They consider where the person is currently, where he/she wants to be, and how he/she will get there. The definition of such goals can be facilitated through a collaboration between the seniors and HSCPs, by, for example, prioritising the goals and the actions to attain them, or even breaking down more ambitious goals into smaller to match the SMART criteria. The ICOPE approach [44] offers guidance on how to undertake person-centred goal setting, beginning with the identification of goals, both general and health-related, with the older person and their family/caregivers. This is followed by setting the goals more specifically and within a given time-frame, prioritising them according to the person’s needs and willingness to work on them [49] in a manner that fosters shared decision-making.

Refinements to the support tool (draft 3) and HSCP training

To align with the ICOPE steps for the provision of integrated care, and guidance on goal setting, two additions were made to the support tool, designing a draft 3. The first was a self-evaluation of the health domains, based on the screening questions used in ICOPE. The second the formulation of six steps in the definition and setting of personalised goals, and the identification of local activities that older people could engage in to address their goal (Fig. 5).

The training of HSCPs was also developed at this stage, and a reference manual was created to provide them with guidance on the study procedures, suggestions on the approaches and behaviours relevant to each of the seven roles identified, and assistance with responses to potential problematic situations (e.g. senior participant unwell) with Two training sessions were planned: a 4-hour group session, and a 2-hour individual session. These were delivered by two members of the research team, led by the clinical nurse. The group session was guided by an interactive presentation covering the concepts and theoretical implementation principles for fostering healthy ageing, with a focus on principles, such as the ICOPE model [49] partnership [20], shared decision-making, and personalised delivery methods (e.g. the seven roles to be mobilised for individualised support [50]). The individual session took place seven days after the group session, making it possible to specify elements of content or practice to be addressed, according to the needs of the HSCPs. Requests by HSCPs included answering or clarifying specific questions related to the study procedures or material, developing hypothetical scenarios, and engaging in role play.

HSCP perceived feasibility

The acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility of four different elements of the intervention was assessed to allow for the potential further refinement of the intervention. The elements concerned the support tool: (1) the fact sheets of the health domains, (2) the six steps for the definition of a health objective, (3) the maps containing local information, and the (4) methods and procedures put in place for the partnership between HSCPs and older people.

The recruited HSCPs (N = 11; age range = 27–48, age mean 37.5) were 8 women and 3 men who were nurses (N = 4), occupational therapists (N = 2), physical therapists (N = 2), nutritionists (N = 2), and a social worker.

A validated questionnaire [52] measuring the acceptability, relevance, and feasibility of interventions was back translated [53] from English to French by four Authors (CB, CL, EA, II). Each subscale contained four items, including “[intervention system element] is appealing to me” (acceptability), “[intervention system element] seems applicable” (appropriateness), and “[intervention system element] seems implementable” (feasibility). Two items in the feasibility subscale (…seems doable / …seems possible) were replaced with items such that more focus was placed on the ease of use “[intervention system element] seems easy to understand”, and on whether professionals would recommend to their peers “I would recommend [intervention system element] to my colleagues”. Responses ranged from “strongly disagree” [1] to “strongly agree” [5]. HSCPs responded to the questionnaire immediately after the group and individual training they received prior to the implementation of the intervention.

Findings

Table 3 presents the responses of HSCPs after both training sessions. After the first training session (4 h group training), responses were relatively neutral, with HSCPs noting that there were not yet familiar enough with the material and procedures. However, there did seem to be interest in the fact sheets, with the majority of HSCPs considering them as acceptable, appropriate, and feasible. Looking at HSCPs individual responses, the only intervention element which received negative responses (disagree) was the tool intended to outline the steps in the definition of a health objective and the actions to achieve it. Although some negative responses were noted, the majority of HSCPs was undecided on the tool’s appropriateness. Following the individual training, which was entirely personalised to HSCPs’ needs, HSCPs perceptions of the acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility increased for all intervention elements. Whilst minor errors in the support tool (e.g. orthographical) were pointed out and noted for correction in a subsequent phase, HSCPs did not report any concerns or major flaws in the intervention material or procedures. It was therefore not necessary to perform any further significant modifications or refinements at this stage.

Whilst this general outline was provided as guidance for HSCPs, it is essential for the process to unfold according to the unique needs and situation of the older person. The role of HSCPs would be to accompany older persons in this process according to the roles selected by the older person (i.e. observer, moderator, etc) [50], whether by discussing the health domains, assisting in goal setting (e.g. breaking down of “big” goals into smaller parts), or in the actions to take to achieve the goals (e.g. using the support tool to identify suitable activities or to contact different associations with the older person). HSCPs would be able to consult or seek advice with their peers or the research team, encouraging collaboration between the different stakeholders. The intervention followed two steps of ICOPE: screening (self-evaluation in the support tool) and developing personalised care plans [49] and the first three points (clarify, identify, design) of the pathway outlined in the Decade of Healthy Ageing [9].Overall, the VIeSA intervention proposed a health promotion model whereby the older person was the main “driver” in the creation of his/her healthy ageing trajectory.

Discussion

The aim of this article was to describe the use of the MRC framework [12, 13, 36] in the development of a complex intervention for healthy ageing among community-dwelling older people. The step-by-step approach to intervention development put forward by the MRC, as well as the additional resources for this phase, provided guidance in developing the VIeSA intervention and allowed for a clearer vision of the intervention as a whole. Our ability to take inspiration from and model the presentation of our findings on previous studies using the MRC [38–40], points to the clarity of the framework and to the consequent transparency it permits. The flexible and iterative nature of intervention development permitted the constant refinement of the intervention material, so that it may be more useful and practical to the end-users. Finally, the choice of solid theoretical and operational frameworks, nestled in worldwide health initiatives, allowed for the development of a structured, coherent, and evidence-based intervention. This is reflected in the recognition of the VIeSA intervention as contributing to the UN’s Decade of Healthy Ageing [33].

The principle of stakeholder involvement was applied throughout the development of the VIeSA intervention, as well as in its subsequent pilot testing [35] through a PPI methodology. In addition to their active participation in the development of the intervention, stakeholders were consistently engaged through the dissemination of the progress and findings of the research, scientific and professional publications, conference presentations, and a stakeholder meeting at the end of the project to discuss the findings and future directions. The experiential and professional expertise of these stakeholders enriched the content of the intervention, and although engaging all stakeholders can be methodologically complicated or time-consuming, the benefits it can bring are invaluable. Future initiatives could endeavour to involve stakeholders at a more elevated degree than that applied in the present research. In the case of this project for example, the participation of stakeholders before the development of the support material may have facilitated the creation of its consolidated version. Participatory approaches [20] are in line with recent local public policy objectives both on a cantonal [54] and national level [55] which consider that “patients must be able to play a fully-fledged, self-determined role in their relationship with healthcare professionals” [56, p. 3].

Multidisciplinarity and inter-institutional collaboration were other elements that characterised the development of the intervention, and which are in line with holistic approaches to healthy ageing combining social, health, and care aspects. The alignment of the philosophy, objectives, and actions of four major institutions in the development of the intervention enabled us to reach a consensus, to have a vision that was not siloed or separate, but complementary.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of the VIeSA intervention is the creation of the evidence-based support tool which was contextualised locally. The revisions and refinements outlined in the present paper constitute the first phase of construction. Refinements in the next phase of the intervention (feasibility) allowed for its further improvement, notably the consideration of its format (paper vs. electronic).

The sustainability of the support tool must also be considered. The scientific information presented is based on evidence from meta-analyses and systematic reviews, covering consistent findings over the last few decades. It is therefore likely to only require periodical updates. However, the same does not apply for the information provided on the local context. Despite our efforts to address this by guiding users to the main local resource points, the constantly-evolving nature of social associations and activities on offer for older people suggests a rapid obsolescence of information in the absence of regular updates. A multidisciplinary dedicated team would be needed to perform such updates and suggests that an electronic format of the material would be most sustainable. This implies a certain level of technological literacy and access to online services in users, which needs to be considered and addressed. This could be done by organising workshops and individualised support through partnerships with social associations and communities, for example.

In terms of the HSCPs, the training provided in the context of the present study was short, albeit personalised with the individual training session. For a standardised and sustainable implementation of the VIeSA intervention, it would be appropriate to consider offering more in-depth education, specific to healthy ageing and to the shift in HSCP stance of accompanying or providing guidance, rather than treating or curing [51]. This would require significantly more time and resources, but may assist the process subsequently.

Finally, it is important to address the question of the duration of the study. In the context of this specific project, composed of its development and feasibility phases, we decided to offer a short program (3 to 4 months) with close follow-ups, allowing for up to six possible meetings between seniors and HSCPs (in-person or by phone). This frequency of short-term follow-ups is considered high [50], and meets the aim of assessing the feasibility of the intervention, rather than measuring behavioural changes in participants. The flexibility of the intervention in the sense of advancing according to the rhythm of each individual, would allow for a shorter or longer time-frame for the meetings between HSCPs and seniors, in the context of a long-term implementation.

Future directions and conclusion

After testing and confirming the feasibility of the VIeSA intervention [57] the next steps were to make the material accessible and to disseminate the findings of the project, which requires the support of public authorities. The refined draft of the support tool was made available in both digital and paper formats, and in three different versions: short [58], detailed [59] and a third specifically for HSCPs [60]. Distributing these within local health, community, or social club networks would also contribute to their accessibility and visibility. Moreover, the dissemination of the findings of the project to health policy bodies will allow for better optimisation of content in terms of usefulness and accessibility for the concerned stakeholders, and for greater implementation opportunities.

Following the MRC, the next phase, which will require resources (human, financial), that go beyond the scope of the present study, is the evaluation phase- the testing of the effectiveness of the VIeSA intervention by means of a new study [12, 13]. Key outcome variables would include the continuity of the pursued goals, engagement in the activities selected, and self-perceived psychological and physical health. This would allow for further insights into the potential benefits of such an intervention and its impact on long-term behavioural change.

The VIeSA intervention proposed a new model for health promotion in older people living in the canton of Geneva. To our knowledge, the support tool produced for use by older people and HSCPs and based on the principles of ICOPE, is the first of its kind. The applicability of these principles to all countries across the world, suggests that healthy ageing initiatives elsewhere could consider similar means to inform and guide concerned stakeholders, whether seniors, professionals, or service providers. Within the local Swiss context, it would be entirely feasible to adapt the support material content and the process to match the local context of different regions (e.g. language and service providers). Another avenue for the adaptation of the VIeSA intervention would be to target a different population, notably just before the age of retirement. A transitional period, retirement can, in some people, bring about uncertainty in which the lack of routine may benefit from the uptake of activities. The possibility of a personalised plan and information on the local services on offer may therefore be of particular interest during this period.

Taken together, our experience suggested that the design and development of complex interventions using structured frameworks has several benefits, including a clarity of direction, an optimised content in terms of usefulness and accessibility for all concerned stakeholders, and greater opportunities for implementation.
