Mitochondrial PDHA1 acetylation orchestrates lactate-dependent epigenetic reprogramming to promote fibrosis via NUAK2
Authors: Yujie Wang, Guanglian Du, Jinjin Zhang, Nailiang Zhai, Weili Liu, Guohong Cao, Hongbo Li, Bo Liu, Huiling Yang, Changjun Lv, Xiaodong Song, Songzi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIdiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a progressive and fatal lung disease with few effective treatment options. While metabolic reprogramming has been associated with IPF, the precise mechanisms connecting mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction to epigenetically driven fibrogenesis remain unclear. In this study, we identify a pathogenic pathway focused on the mitochondrial enzyme pyruvate dehydrogenase E1 alpha subunit (PDHA1). In fibrosis model, downregulation of the deacetylase sirtuin3 (SIRT3) leads to hyperacetylation of PDHA1 at Lys-83. This modification inhibits pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) activity, causing a metabolic shift toward glycolysis and increased lactate production. Lactate, in turn, serves as a precursor for the lactylation of histone H4 at K12 (H4K12la), which activates super-enhancer (SE) at the NUAK2 gene locus, significantly enhancing NUAK2 expression. Both genetic and pharmacological inhibition of NUAK2 confirm its role in driving myofibroblast activation and fibrotic progression. Critically, the pro-fibrotic effects of PDHA1 K83 acetylation (K83ac) were reversed by NUAK2 knockdown. Our findings uncover a novel PDHA1 K83ac-H4K12la-NUAK2 pathway that integrates metabolic and epigenetic signals to promote fibrosis, suggesting that targeting PDHA1 deacetylation and inhibiting NUAK2 could be promising therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06166-5.Keywords: Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, Acetylation, PDHA1, Lactylation, Super-enhancer, NUAK2

Introduction

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a progressive and fatal lung disease, characterized by the thickening and scarring of lung tissue [1–3]. As condition advances, the extracellular matrix accumulates and lung structure undergoes significant changes, impairing its ability to exchange gases effectively. This leads to respiratory failure, which is the primary cause of death in IPF patients [4–6]. The median survival time for those diagnosed with IPF is typically only 3 to 5 years, presenting a major clinical challenge [7, 8]. Current treatments, such as antifibrotic drugs like nintedanib and pirfenidone, can slow the decline in lung function, but they do not offer a cure [9, 10]. This emphasizes the urgent need for a better understanding of the disease mechanisms to help develop more effective therapies [11].

Metabolic reprogramming, particularly a shift toward glycolysis similar to the Warburg effect, is increasingly recognized as a critical factor driving fibrogenesis in IPF [12–14]. This metabolic shift not only provides energy and biosynthetic materials for cells but also results in lactate accumulation, which was once considered a mere waste product but is now understood to possess significant signaling functions [15–17]. A significant portion of cellular metabolic sensing and signaling is orchestrated through post-translational modifications (PTMs), which act as rapid and reversible molecular switches to regulate protein function, localization, and stability [18, 19]. A considerable fraction of cellular acetylation, a major PTM, occurs within mitochondria, creating a direct mechanistic link between metabolic state and enzymatic activity [20–22]. One important candidate connecting metabolism to fibrosis through acetylation is the mitochondrial pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) complex, which regulated the flow of carbon into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. The acetylation of pyruvate dehydrogenase E1 alpha subunit (PDHA1) tightly regulates this process [23, 24]. This regulatory mechanism is counterbalanced by SIRT3, the primary mitochondrial NAD⁺-dependent deacetylase [25, 26], which maintains PDHA1 in a deacetylated, active state, thereby promoting oxidative phosphorylation [27, 28]. When SIRT3 expression is impaired, PDHA1 becomes hyperacetylated, leading to metabolic dysfunction, particularly in conditions such as sepsis-induced kidney injury and renal fibrosis [29, 30]. However, the role of the SIRT3-PDHA1 axis in metabolic dysregulation and lactate-induced fibrogenesis in IPF is still largely unexplored.

Recent studies have also shed light on lactate’s role as a signaling molecule, particularly through histone lysine lactylation, which serves as an epigenetic marker of active gene transcription [31–33]. For example, in niraparib-resistant ovarian cancer, lactate-induced H4K12 lactylation activates a super-enhancer that drives the expression of RAD23A [34]. Super-enhancers are expansive regulatory regions that control the expression of genes associated with cell identity and disease states [35, 36]. Their roles have been extensively investigated in cancers, such as hematologic malignancies [37] and gastric cancer [38]. Importantly, their function in IPF is highlighted by their regulation of pro-fibrotic genes during fibroblast activation [39]. Based on this, we hypothesize that lactate-driven histone lactylation, particularly at H4K12, may enhance pro-fibrotic gene expression by activating super-enhancers. To test this hypothesis and identify key effectors, we employed an integrated multi-omics approach, combining genome-wide H4K12la profiling with super-enhancer mapping. This integrated approach revealed NUAK2 as a potential candidate. NUAK2 is an AMPK-related kinase well-established as a fine-tuning regulator of stress, metabolic, and TGF-β signaling pathways [40, 41]. Notably, its oncogenic role in promoting proliferation, migration, and fibrosis-associated processes like EMT in various cancers aligns closely with the pathogenic phenotype of activated myofibroblasts in IPF [42, 43]. Nevertheless, whether NUAK2 is regulated by a lactate-H4K12la-super-enhancer axis in IPF remains unknown.

In this study, we propose that the dysregulation of the SIRT3–PDHA1 axis contributes to a lactate-rich metabolic environment in pulmonary fibrosis. This, in turn, facilitates histone lactylation and drives pro-fibrotic gene expression through super-enhancers. Using acetylproteomics, we identified hyperacetylation of PDHA1 K83 as a hallmark of fibrotic lungs. Our findings suggest that the downregulation of SIRT3 leads to increased PDHA1 K83 acetylation, suppressing PDH activity and shifting metabolism toward lactate production. This lactate, in turn, promotes H4K12 lactylation, activating super-enhancer at the NUAK2 locus. This process enhances its transcription and contributes to fibrogenesis. Our research outlines a novel SIRT3–PDHA1 K83ac–lactate–H4K12la–NUAK2 axis that connects mitochondrial metabolic defects with nuclear epigenetic reprogramming, offering new insights into both the mechanisms of pulmonary fibrosis and potential therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

The human fetal lung fibroblast cell line MRC-5 was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection. Cells were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% GlutaMax, 1% non-essential amino acids, 1% sodium pyruvate, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Cells were passaged routinely with trypsin when they reached 80% confluence. For experimental treatments, cells were stimulated with 5 ng/mL TGF-β1 for specific time periods. Prior to stimulation, cells were pretreated for 6 h with siRNA at 10 µg/mL or overexpression vectors at 1 ng/mL, followed by incubation in serum-free or TGF-β1-containing medium for an additional 72 h. For NUAK2 modulation, cells were transfected with NUAK2-targeting siRNA or NUAK2 overexpression plasmid using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In inhibitor experiments, cells were pretreated with WZ4003 for 2 h before TGF-β1 stimulation.

Western blot

MRC-5 cells were lysed on ice for 10 min using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Tissue samples were minced and homogenized in lysis buffer. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay. Protein samples were denatured with 4× SDS loading buffer at 95 °C for 10 min, separated by SDS-PAGE at voltages ranging from 80 to 120 V, and transferred to methanol-activated PVDF membranes via wet transfer at 100 V for 90 min. Membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk in TBST for 2 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies diluted in 5% BSA/TBST at 4 °C for 12 to 16 h. After washing, membranes were probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein signals were detected using ECL reagent from Spark Jade (China) and visualized by chemiluminescence.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cellular or tissue samples using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Reverse transcription of circRNAs and mRNAs was carried out using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara), following the manufacturer’s recommended setup and incubation conditions. qRT-PCR was performed on a Rotor-Gene 3000 system (Corbett Research) using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Takara) in a 20 µL total reaction volume. The thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 10 min, with a final step at 95 °C for 15 s. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Real-time cellular proliferation and migration analyses

For proliferation assays, cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells per milliliter in E-Plates from Agilent. For migration assays, CIM plates from Corning were used, with 30 µL of serum-free medium placed in the upper chamber and 165 µL of medium containing serum in the lower chamber. The RTCA DPlus system from ACEA Biosciences was calibrated prior to use with optimized settings for automatic and continuous recording of real-time proliferation or migration curves. Data were analyzed using the RTCA software from ACEA Biosciences. Additionally, scratch wound assays were performed by creating a uniform scratch in confluent cell monolayers. Wound closure was monitored dynamically using the IncuCyte S3 Live-Cell Analysis System (Essen BioScience), and images were scanned and analyzed with IncuCyte 2021 A software.

Animal model and ethics statement

The experimental protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Binzhou Medical University (Approval No. 2022 0006). Male C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old, weighing 20 ± 5 g) were obtained from Jinan Pengyue Laboratory Animal Co. After a 1-week acclimation period under specific pathogen-free conditions, mice were randomly assigned to five experimental groups, with ten mice per group. These groups included sham-operated, BLM-induced fibrosis model, BLM plus negative control virus, BLM plus PDHA1 WT intervention, and BLM plus PDHA1 K83R mutant intervention. For viral pretreatment, mice in the PDHA1 intervention groups received 1.0 × 10¹² vg/kg of recombinant adeno-associated virus carrying either WT or K83R mutant PDHA1, delivered via tracheal nebulization using a Penn-Century MicroSprayer. The negative control group received an equivalent dose of empty vector. Sham and BLM-only groups were given an equal volume of saline. Seven days after viral delivery, BLM was administered at 5 mg/kg via tracheal spray to induce fibrosis, although sham group animals received saline. On day 28 post-induction, mice underwent in vivo micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) imaging using a Bruker Skyscan 1276 system and pulmonary function tests with the AniRes2005 Animal Lung Function Analysis System. Lung tissues were collected and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for molecular analyses. In a separate pharmacological study, mice were divided into sham, BLM + DMSO, and BLM + WZ4003 treatment groups, receiving 10 mg/kg or 20 mg/kg of the inhibitor. WZ4003 was administered via intraperitoneal injection after BLM modeling. Lung function was assessed prior to tissue collection on day 28.

Micro-computed tomography (MicroCT)

Mice were anesthetized with intraperitoneal injection and placed in a supine position on the scanning stage of a PerkinElmer MicroCT system. Image acquisition was performed using the following parameters: X-ray voltage 90 kV, current 88 µA, field of view 36 mm, and exposure time 4 min. Two-dimensional cross-sectional images were obtained using SimpleViewer software, and three-dimensional reconstructions were generated using Analyze 11.0 from AnalyzeDirect. Fibrosis severity was quantified by calculating the mean CT values to assess changes in lung tissue density.

CRISPR-dCas9-KRAB-mediated epigenetic repression

Lentiviral vectors expressing dCas9-KRAB and specific guide RNAs (gRNAs) targeting the H4K12la-enriched region of the NUAK2 super-enhancer were constructed, with a non-targeting gRNA used as a control. MRC-5 cells were co-transfected with dCas9-KRAB and plasmid vectors encoding five individual gRNAs targeting distinct sites using Lipofectamine 3000. Seventy-two hours after transfection, cells were harvested for RNA extraction, and NUAK2 mRNA levels were assessed by qRT-PCR.

Acetylproteomic analysis by LC-MS/MS

Lung tissues from BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis mice and sham groups were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer. Proteins were extracted, digested with trypsin, and enriched using an anti-acetyl-lysine antibody. The enriched acetylated peptides were then analyzed by LC-MS/MS using an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). Raw data were processed with MaxQuant software and aligned with the UniProt mouse database for identification.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) Assay

MRC-5 cells were washed with pre-chilled PBS and placed on ice. Cells were lysed in lysis buffer containing 1% PMSF and scraped from the culture dish. The lysates were sonicated and centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, with a small aliquot stored at − 20 °C as a positive control. The remaining lysate was used for immunoprecipitation. To remove non-specific binding proteins, 5 µL of Protein A and 5 µL of Protein G (Absin, China) were added to the lysate and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h with gentle rotation. After centrifugation, the supernatant was incubated overnight at 4 °C with either normal rabbit IgG or the primary antibody against the target protein. The next day, an additional 5 µL of Protein A and 5 µL of Protein G were added and incubated for 3 h at 4 °C with rotation to capture the immune complexes. Precipitates were collected by centrifugation, washed three times with wash buffer to remove non-specific proteins, and resuspended in SDS sample buffer. The samples were vortexed, centrifuged, and boiled. The supernatant was analyzed by SDS–PAGE followed by Western blot.

Immunofluorescence staining

MRC-5 cells were seeded onto confocal dishes (Biosharp, China) and cultured to about 60% confluence. Cells were then transfected or stimulated with TGF-β1, followed by serum-free culture for 1–2 days. After treatment, cells were washed with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, China) at 4 °C for 1 h. Following fixation, cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 3 min, washed, and blocked with goat serum for 30 min. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against α-SMA (Bioswamp, China), PDHA1 (Abcam, UK), and H4K12ac (Jingjie, China). The following day, cells were incubated with fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies (Affinity, China) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Abbkine, USA) and mounted with anti-fade reagent. Fluorescent images were captured using a laser-scanning confocal microscope (LEICA Stellaris 5, Germany).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq)

Chromatin immunoprecipitation was carried out using the SimpleChIP® Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, #9002) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, approximately 1 × 10⁷ MRC-5 cells, either untreated or treated with TGF-β1, were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde (Macklin, F809702) at room temperature for 10 min. The cross-linking was stopped by adding 2.5 M glycine (Meilunbio, MB4166) for 5 min. Cells were lysed, and chromatin was digested to generate DNA fragments (150–900 bp). Immunoprecipitation was performed overnight using H4K12la (Jingjie, #PTM-1411RM), or IgG (CST, 2729P). The antibody-antigen complexes were captured with Protein G Agarose Beads (Cell Signaling Technology, #9007) and incubated at 4 °C for 2 h. After washing, the complexes were eluted, and cross-links were reversed by incubating with 0.2 M NaCl at 65 °C overnight, followed by proteinase K treatment. The immunoprecipitated DNA was purified and analyzed.

Super-enhancer identification

Super‑enhancers were identified using the ROSE algorithm (Rank Ordering of Super‑Enhancers) according to the method as previously described [44]. Briefly, enhancers were first identified using ROSE as described previously. Peaks located within 12.5 kb were subsequently merged to form candidate enhancer regions. These merged regions were ranked according to their total H3K27ac signal intensity. The threshold separating super-enhancers from typical enhancers was defined as the inflection point in the rank-ordered plot, where the slope of the signal-versus-rank curve equals one.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR)

ChIP was performed as described for ChIP-seq. Immunoprecipitated DNA was analyzed by qPCR using SYBR Green Master Mix. Primers were designed to amplify the SE and promoter regions of the NUAK2 gene. Enrichment was calculated as a percentage of the input DNA. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Measurement of PDH activity and lactate production

PDH enzyme activity was measured using species-specific ELISA kits. The mouse PDH ELISA Kit (KYY-1086M1) was used for mouse lung tissue homogenates, Although the human PDH ELISA Kit (KYY-13434H1) was used for human cell lysates, following the manufacturer’s protocols. Lactate concentrations were quantified using the mouse Lactate ELISA Kit (KYY-0766M1) for mouse samples and the human Lactate ELISA Kit (KYY-12907H1) for human cell lysates, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Histopathological staining (H&E and masson’s trichrome)

Mouse lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin overnight. The tissues were sectioned into 4 μm slices using a Leica RM2255 microtome. Sections were stained with H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining kit (Solarbio, China). After staining, sections were dehydrated in absolute ethanol, cleared with xylene, and mounted with neutral gum before being sealed with cover glass. Lung tissue sections from each group were observed using a light microscope.

Lung function measurement

Pulmonary function, including FVC and lung compliance, was measured using the AniRes2005 Animal Lung Function Analysis System. Mice were anesthetized with 2.5% Avertin solution (0.1 mL/10 g body weight, intraperitoneally). After sufficient anesthesia, the trachea was exposed through a midline cervical incision and intubated, with the tube secured by a suture. Mice were placed in a plethysmograph chamber with an initial negative pressure set to 25 cmH₂O, a breathing frequency of 60 breaths per minute, and an inspiration-to-expiration ratio of 20:10. The “Start” mode was activated, and passive inhalation was automatically initiated until the preset pressure was reached, followed by exhalation.

Statistical analysis

All in vitro experiments were performed with three independent replicates. In vivo studies used six mice per group, and representative experiments are shown. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism, employing Student’s t-test for pairwise comparisons and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for multiple comparisons. Results are presented as means ± SD. *, p < 0.05; **, p < 0.01; NS, not significant (p > 0.05).

Results

Acetylproteomic profiling reveals PDHA1 K83 hyperacetylation in pulmonary fibrosis

To investigate changes of the acetylome during fibrosis progression, we conducted an acetylproteomic analysis using lung tissues from bleomycin (BLM)-treated mice and sham group. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) was employed for this purpose (Fig. 1A). The distinct acetylation profiles between sham and fibrotic lungs were clearly visible in the heatmap (Fig. 1B). Quantitative analysis revealed 87 acetylated sites on 82 proteins that were significantly upregulated, and 467 acetylated sites on 331 proteins that were significantly downregulated in fibrotic tissue (Fig. 1C). A volcano plot highlighted the acetylation of PDHA1 as one of the most significantly upregulated acetylation modifications associated with fibrosis (Fig. 1D). Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses showed that pyruvate metabolism and the TCA cycle were among the most enriched pathways (Fig. 1E, F). A detailed site-specific analysis of PDHA1 acetylation, based on acetyl-lysine immunoprecipitation sequencing data, identified four acetylated lysine residues (K77, K83, K313, K321). Notably, only the acetylation at K83 showed a significant increase in fibrotic lungs compared to controls, with a 7.281-fold increase, while acetylation at K77, K313, and K321 did not change significantly (Fig. 1G). The presence of PDHA1 K83ac was independently verified by extracting its representative MS/MS spectrum from the LC-MS/MS data, which displayed clear fragment ions confirming the modification (Fig. 1H). Furthermore, this lysine residue was found to be evolutionarily conserved across multiple species (Fig. 1I). These findings establish PDHA1 K83 as a specifically hyperacetylated and conserved site in pulmonary fibrosis.

SIRT3 deficiency mediates PDHA1 hyperacetylation in pulmonary fibrosis

To validate the functional implications of these findings, we focused on PDHA1, examining its protein expression and acetylation levels. Western blot analysis showed that PDHA1 total protein was significantly downregulated in both BLM-induced fibrotic mouse lungs tissue and TGF-β1-stimulated human lung fibroblasts (MRC-5 cells) (Fig. 2A). Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed the downregulation of PDHA1 and an increase in the fibrotic marker α-SMA, supporting the validity of the fibrotic model (Fig. 2B). We then validated PDHA1 hyperacetylation under fibrotic conditions in TGF-β1-stimulated fibroblasts. Pan-acetyl-lysine immunoprecipitation followed by PDHA1 immunoblotting revealed enhanced acetylation of PDHA1 in these cells (Fig. 2C). Next, we explored the upstream mechanism responsible for PDHA1 hyperacetylation. Since SIRT3 is the primary mitochondrial NAD⁺-dependent deacetylase known to target PDHA1 and maintain its activity in other pathological contexts [25, 27], we hypothesized that SIRT3 deficiency might be responsible for the observed PDHA1 K83 hyperacetylation in pulmonary fibrosis. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays using an anti-PDHA1 antibody in TGF-β1-stimulated MRC-5 fibroblasts revealed that SIRT3 protein expression was significantly downregulated, and its interaction with PDHA1 was markedly weakened under fibrotic conditions (Fig. 2D). Reciprocal Co-IP experiments using an anti-SIRT3 antibody further confirmed the disrupted interaction between SIRT3 and PDHA1, as well as the downregulation of SIRT3 at the protein level (Fig. 2E). To determine whether SIRT3 deficiency directly drives PDHA1 hyperacetylation, we performed functional studies. Knockdown of SIRT3 significantly increased the acetylation level of PDHA1, as detected by immunoprecipitation with a pan-acetylation antibody followed by immunoblotting with anti-PDHA1 antibody (Fig. 2F). Conversely, overexpression of SIRT3 effectively suppressed PDHA1 hyperacetylation under the same experimental conditions (Fig. 2G). Collectively, these results demonstrate that PDHA1 is downregulated and hyperacetylated which is driven by loss of SIRT3 in fibrosis.

PDHA1 K83ac drives fibrotic phenotypes via metabolic reprogramming

We next investigated the functional role of PDHA1 K83ac in pulmonary fibrosis. To confirm the specificity of the acetylation site, two PDHA1 mutants were generated: an acetylation-mimic mutant (Lys-83 to Gln, K83Q) and an acetylation-deficient mutant (Lys-83 to Arg, K83R). Overexpression of the acetylation-mimic mutant PDHA1 K83Q, which simulates constant acetylation at lysine 83, significantly enhanced TGF-β1-induced fibroblast migration and proliferation. In contrast, the expression of WT PDHA1 or the acetylation-deficient K83R mutant attenuated these responses (Fig. 3A, B). Western blot analysis revealed that both WT and K83R PDHA1 reduced the TGF-β1-induced expression of fibrotic markers such as vimentin, α-SMA, and collagen I, although K83Q increased their production (Fig. 3C). Additionally, knocking down endogenous PDHA1 promoted fibroblast migration and proliferation. However, rescue experiments showed that re-expression of WT or K83R PDHA1 rescued this phenotype, while K83Q further enhanced it (Fig. 3D, E). Similarly, PDHA1 knockdown increased fibrotic marker expression, which was reversed by WT or K83R PDHA1 but exacerbated by K83Q (Fig. 3F). The functional data highlight PDHA1 K83 as an essential regulatory site. The observation that overexpressing wild-type PDHA1 provides a protective effect, even in a fibrotic environment that promotes its acetylation, suggests that enhancing the overall cellular levels of the enzyme can offset its specific inactivation at this site.

To explore the underlying metabolic mechanisms, we examined key metabolic parameters. Both cell and mice models of pulmonary fibrosis exhibited reduced PDH activity (Fig. 3G) and increased lactate secretion (Fig. 3H). In TGF-β1-stimulated fibroblasts, overexpression of WT or K83R PDHA1 resulted in increased PDH activity and decreased lactate production. In contrast, the acetylation-mimic K83Q mutant further suppressed PDH activity and enhanced lactate production (Fig. 3I). Furthermore, PDHA1 knockdown led to reduced PDH activity and increased lactate accumulation, effects that were reversed by WT or K83R but not by K83Q rescue (Fig. 3J). These findings collectively highlight the significance of PDHA1 K83ac as a crucial molecular switch in the development of fibrosis. Furthermore, our results demonstrate that increasing the overall levels of PDHA1 can effectively counteract its pathogenic inactivation.

Evaluation of the potential of PDHA1 as a therapeutic target in mice

Having established that increasing functional PDHA1 can counteract fibrotic phenotypes in vitro, we aimed to explore whether this strategy could have therapeutic potential in vivo. To test this, we employed a BLM-treated mouse model of pulmonary fibrosis. Mice were administered adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9) carrying either an empty vector (NC), WT PDHA1, or PDHA1-K83R via tracheal nebulization (Fig. 4A). Sham-operated mice exhibited the mildest pathological features. Both the PDHA1 and PDHA1-K83R overexpression groups showed significant attenuation of weight loss compared to the BLM and BLM + NC groups, which exhibited the most severe symptoms (Fig. 4B). Micro-CT imaging illustrated notable amelioration of pulmonary structural abnormalities in mice overexpressing PDHA1 or PDHA1-K83R, in contrast to the severe damage observed in the BLM and BLM + NC groups (Fig. 4C). Pulmonary function tests demonstrated that BLM-induced impairment in forced vital capacity (FVC) was significantly improved in mice overexpressing PDHA1 or PDHA1-K83R compared to the BLM and BLM + NC groups (Fig. 4D). Additionally, dynamic lung compliance (Cdyn) measurements revealed that BLM-induced reduction in Cdyn was significantly improved in both the PDHA1 and PDHA1-K83R groups (Fig. 4E). Western blot analysis indicated that overexpression of either PDHA1 or PDHA1-K83R effectively suppressed the upregulation of fibrotic markers, including collagen I, α-SMA, and vimentin (Fig. 4F). Histopathological evaluation confirmed that architectural damage, inflammatory infiltration, and collagen deposition were minimal in sham group, moderately alleviated in the PDHA1 and PDHA1-K83R groups, and most severe in the BLM and BLM + NC groups (Fig. 4G). These results suggest that both WT PDHA1 and PDHA1-K83R overexpression offer partial, yet similar, protective effects against BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis.

PDHA1 K83ac-mediated lactate production promotes histone H4K12 lactylation

As shown in Fig. 3I and J, PDHA1 K83ac drives lactate accumulation. To investigate whether this metabolic shift contributes to epigenetic regulation in fibrosis, we investigated the role of lactate-derived lactylation. A significant increase in global histone lactylation was observed in both BLM-induced fibrotic lungs and TGF-β1-stimulated fibroblasts (Fig. 5A, B). Among the several histone lactylation sites we examined, H4K12la emerged as the most consistently and markedly upregulated site in our fibrotic models (Fig. 5C). Given its prominent upregulation, we next examined whether its levels were directly regulated by PDHA1 K83ac. Both WT PDHA1 and acetylation-deficient mutant K83R suppressed TGF-β1-induced H4K12la, whereas the acetylation-mimic mutant K83Q enhanced it (Fig. 5D). Additionally, knocking down PDHA1 resulted in an increase in H4K12la, which could be reversed by reintroducing WT or K83R PDHA1, but not by K83Q (Fig. 5E). Immunofluorescence studies confirmed that overexpression of either WT PDHA1 or K83R mutant reduced TGF-β1-induced nuclear H4K12la, while K83Q enhanced it (Fig. 5F). Similarly, in PDHA1-knockdown cells, re-expression of WT or K83R PDHA1 attenuated the elevation of nuclear H4K12la, although K83Q failed to reverse this effect and further increased H4K12la levels (Fig. 5G). These results highlight PDHA1 K83ac as a crucial factor driving H4K12 lactylation during fibrosis progression.

Integrated multi-omics profiling identifies NUAK2 as a super-enhancer-driven target of H4K12la in pulmonary fibrosis

To understand how lactate-driven H4K12la contributes to fibrotic transcriptional reprogramming, we performed comprehensive genome-wide profiling of H4K12la with ChIP-seq in both control and TGF-β1-induced MRC-5 cells. The analysis revealed a widespread gain of H4K12la signals in fibrotic cells (Fig. 6A). These H4K12la peaks were primarily enriched near promoter regions and within introns (Fig. 6B). GO analysis highlighted a strong association between genes near these gained H4K12la peaks and fibrotic pathways (Fig. 6C). Given the enrichment of H4K12la in regulatory regions, we hypothesized that H4K12la might activate fibrotic genes by facilitating the activity of SE, which are well-known drivers of disease-specific gene expression. To investigate this, we conducted CUT&Tag of histone H3K27 acetylation (H3K27ac), as H3K27ac is a reliable marker for active SEs. Using the ROSE algorithm, we identified SE-associated genes, revealing 205 genes in control cells and 889 genes in fibrotic cells (Fig. 6D, E). Among the 51 high-confidence candidate genes with regulatory regions that co-localized H4K12la and SE markers, we selected NUAK2 for further validation due to its biological relevance and peak overlap. IGV visualization confirmed a fibrosis-specific SE at the NUAK2 locus (chr1:205,284,224–205,319,633; Fig. 6F). Notably, a 1.9-fold increase in H4K12la signal was observed within this SE region (chr1:205,308,448–205,309,028) in fibrotic cells compared to controls (Fig. 6G), indicating that H4K12la is specifically enriched at this SE. This integrated profiling highlights NUAK2 as a key candidate, suggesting that its transcriptional upregulation in fibrosis may be influenced by a mechanism driven by lactate, H4K12la and SE.

Lactate-dependent H4K12la licenses a SE to amplify NUAK2-driven fibrogenesis

We next aimed to validate the role of the lactate–H4K12la–SE axis in regulating NUAK2 expression. Quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) revealed that NUAK2 mRNA was significantly upregulated, approximately 2.2-fold, in TGF-β1-induced MRC-5 cells compared to controls (Fig. 7A). To assess whether this upregulation depends on SE activity, we treated the cells with BET inhibitor JQ1 (20 µM) which disrupts SE function by inhibiting BRD4 binding [34, 39]. Treatment with JQ1 effectively reduced NUAK2 mRNA expression (Fig. 7B). Additionally, immunoblot analysis revealed a dose-dependent decrease in NUAK2 protein levels in TGF-β1-stimulated cells after JQ1 treatment (Fig. 7C), confirming that NUAK2 is regulated by SE activity. Building on our previous findings, which showed that PDHA1 K83ac promotes lactate accumulation, we explored whether lactate directly influences NUAK2 expression. Supplementing with lactate at 15 mM to mimic elevated lactylation increased NUAK2 mRNA expression (Fig. 7D). Furthermore, lactate treatment resulted in a dose-dependent increase in NUAK2 protein levels, as well as in the fibrotic marker α-SMA and epigenetic modifications H4K12la (Fig. 7E). Conversely, inhibiting glycolysis with 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) at 15 mM, which reduces lactate production, suppressed NUAK2 mRNA expression (Fig. 7F). 2-DG treatment also led to a dose-dependent decrease in NUAK2, α-SMA and H4K12la protein levels (Fig. 7G), highlighting the metabolic dependency of this regulatory pathway.

ChIP-qPCR analyses were performed to directly investigate the chromatin status at the NUAK2 locus. A strong enrichment of H3K27ac was observed at the NUAK2 SE in fibrotic cells compared to controls (Fig. 7H). In parallel, H4K12la showed significant enrichment at the NUAK2 SE in fibrotic cells (Fig. 7I). A detailed examination of the promoter region using five primer sets confirmed a significant increase in H4K12la signals (Fig. 7J). To establish a direct causal link between the H4K12la-marked SE and NUAK2 transcription, CRISPR-mediated epigenetic repression was employed. Targeted repression of the H4K12la peaks using dCas9-KRAB fused with five specific gRNAs resulted in a significant reduction in NUAK2 mRNA expression (Fig. 7K). This finding demonstrates that the H4K12la-enriched SE is functionally required to sustain high NUAK2 transcription in fibrosis.

Next, we evaluated the direct impact of lactate flux on these epigenetic modifications. Lactate treatment dramatically enhanced the enrichment of H4K12la at the NUAK2 SE, increasing it over 500-fold compared to IgG, a significant rise from the ~ 100-fold enrichment observed under control conditions (Fig. 7L). Simultaneously, lactate enhanced H3K27ac occupancy at the same site to over 60-fold versus IgG, up from a baseline of around 30-fold enrichment (Fig. 7M). Conversely, treatment with 2-DG, which reduces lactate levels, led to about a 50% decrease in the enrichment of both H4K12la and H3K27ac at the NUAK2 super-enhancer (Fig. 7N), establishing a direct link between lactate availability and the maintenance of these activating marks. Overall, these findings indicate that lactate-derived H4K12la activates the SE at the NUAK2 locus, promoting H3K27ac deposition to drive NUAK2 expression and contribute to fibrogenesis.

NUAK2 functions as a critical downstream effector of the PDHA1–lactylation axis and its inhibition attenuates fibrosis

To better understand the role of NUAK2 as a downstream effector of the metabolic-epigenetic axis, we conducted gain- and loss-of-function studies in MRC-5 cells. Western blot analysis revealed that TGF-β1 stimulation induced a time-dependent increase in NUAK2 expression, along with the fibrotic markers’ collagen I, α-SMA, and vimentin (Fig. 8A). Consistent with these findings, NUAK2 knockdown suppressed the TGF-β1-induced expression of α-SMA, vimentin, and collagen I (Fig. 8B), although NUAK2 overexpression significantly enhanced the production of these fibrotic markers (Fig. 8C). Next, we assessed the functional consequences of modulating NUAK2 expression. Real-time cellular proliferation assays showed that NUAK2 knockdown reduced fibroblast proliferation and overexpression of NUAK2 significantly enhanced proliferative capacity (Fig. 8D, E). Scratch wound assays further demonstrated that NUAK2 knockdown impaired cell migration, while NUAK2 overexpression substantially increased the migration rate of MRC-5 cells (Fig. 8F). Consistent with in vitro findings, NUAK2 was also markedly upregulated in lung tissues from BLM-treated mice (Supplemental Fig. S1A, B), emphasizing its relevance in fibrotic progression in vivo.

To explore the therapeutic potential of targeting NUAK2, we used the selective NUAK2 inhibitor WZ4003 in a BLM-treated mouse model of pulmonary fibrosis (Fig. 8G). BLM challenge induced significant body weight loss compared to sham controls. Notably, WZ4003 treatment attenuated this weight loss in a dose-dependent manner, with the 20 mg/kg dose providing a significantly greater protective effect than the 10 mg/kg dose (Fig. 8H). Lung function assessment revealed that BLM-induced impairments in dynamic lung compliance and FVC were markedly improved by WZ4003 treatment (Fig. 8I, J). Consistent with these functional improvements, western blot analysis of lung tissues showed that the BLM-induced upregulation of fibrotic markers, including collagen I, α-SMA, and vimentin, was significantly suppressed by WZ4003 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 8K). Histopathological evaluation using H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining confirmed that the severe architectural distortion, inflammatory cell infiltration, and collagen deposition characteristic of BLM-induced fibrosis were substantially alleviated by WZ4003 treatment (Fig. 8L). To definitively establish NUAK2 as a downstream effector of the PDHA1 K83ac–lactate–H4K12la axis, we performed critical rescue experiments. Genetic rescue using NUAK2 knockdown showed that the pro-fibrotic effect of PDHA1 K83Q overexpression was significantly reversed by concurrent NUAK2 silencing (Fig. 8M). Pharmacological rescue with WZ4003 further confirmed this relationship, as the enhanced expression of fibrotic markers induced by PDHA1 K83Q was suppressed in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 8N). These results collectively establish NUAK2 as an essential downstream effector through which the PDHA1 acetylation–lactylation pathway promotes fibrosis. Therefore, NUAK2 functions as a critical downstream component of the PDHA1–lactylation axis and represents a druggable target, as its pharmacological inhibition effectively attenuates experimental pulmonary fibrosis.

Discussion

Our study reveals a critical metabolic-epigenetic circuit involved in the development of pulmonary fibrosis. We propose that, upon exposure to pro-fibrotic factors like TGF-β1, the expression of the mitochondrial deacetylase SIRT3 is reduced. This loss of SIRT3 disrupts its interaction with PDHA1, resulting in hyperacetylation at the K83 site. This modification impairs PDH activity, shifting cellular metabolism toward aerobic glycolysis and leading to lactate accumulation. The excess lactate then enters the nucleus, where it serves as a substrate for histone lactylation, particularly at the H4K12 site. The lactylation of H4K12, known as H4K12la, promotes and stabilizes a SE at the NUAK2 gene locus, enhancing the transcription of this pro-fibrotic gene. Ultimately, the upregulation of NUAK2 drives myofibroblast activation, proliferation, and migration which is critical for fibrosis development (Fig. 9). This pathway connects mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction with nuclear epigenetic changes, revealing a novel regulatory mechanism in the pathogenesis of IPF.

Our data reveal a dual role for PDHA1 in pulmonary fibrosis, with its function being influenced by its acetylation status. The results confirm that PDHA1 acts as a key metabolic checkpoint against fibrosis, consistent with its established role in mitochondrial pyruvate oxidation [45]. The protective function of PDHA1 is influenced by the environment. In a fibrotic model, the endogenous PDHA1 becomes dysfunctional due to decreased expression and hyperacetylation at the K83 site. This creates a paradox that while the environment is prone to hyperacetylation, overexpressing wild-type PDHA1 still offers protection. This paradox can be explained by a model suggesting that providing a significant excess of the enzyme boosts the overall amount of active protein available, thereby compensating for the inactivation caused by the pathogenic conditions. The positive impact of PDHA1 is directly associated with increased PDH activity, which helps correct pathological metabolic reprogramming by channeling pyruvate into the TCA cycle, thus reducing lactate production [46, 47]. However, we discovered that PDHA1’s protective function is compromised in a fibrotic environment. Specifically, pathogenic hyperacetylation at K83, caused by SIRT3 downregulation, converts PDHA1 from a fibrosis suppressor into a pro-fibrotic agent [29, 30]. This shift in PDHA1’s function due to SIRT3 loss is not unique to pulmonary fibrosis. A recent study showed that in hypertensive perivascular adipose tissue, myeloid-specific SIRT3 deficiency also resulted in PDHA1 inactivation, leading to a metabolic shift toward glycolysis, lactate accumulation, and subsequent activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome [48].

The pathogenic metabolic-epigenetic circuit identified in this study may extend beyond pulmonary fibrosis. Our findings are consistent with and supported by emerging evidence implicating mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction and lactate-driven signaling in other fibroproliferative disorders. In liver fibrosis, metabolic reprogramming toward glycolysis is a recognized driver of stellate cell activation and extracellular matrix deposition [12]. In renal fibrosis, SIRT3 deficiency and consequent PDHA1 hyperacetylation have been directly linked to lactate overproduction and disease progression [30]. Notably, in cardiac fibrosis, cardiac-specific deletion of PDHA1 has been shown to disrupt glucose oxidation, promote a metabolic shift toward glycolysis, and exacerbate ischemia-induced fibrosis and dysfunction. PDH activation attenuates these pathological changes [49]. This convergence highlights the potential to target this core metabolic-epigenetic module as a unifying therapeutic strategy for multiple fibrotic diseases.

Our findings demonstrate that H4K12la levels are increased in fibrotic models and are directly influenced by PDHA1 acetylation status, linking a specific mitochondrial metabolic defect to nuclear epigenomic changes. In selecting histone lactylation sites for mechanistic investigation, we were guided by comprehensive screening and mechanistic precedence. Our initial profiling detected upregulation of several lactylation marks on both H3 and H4 in fibrosis. Among these, H4K12la exhibited the most marked and consistent upregulation (Fig. 5C). We selected H4K12la not only due to its extent of change, but also because of its established functional link to super-enhancer biology; prior work has shown H4K12la specifically facilitates super-enhancer activity to drive pathogenic gene programs [34]. Given the central role of super-enhancer remodeling in fibrosis [39], H4K12la represents a mechanistically coherent connection between lactate accumulation and SE-driven transcription. This focused approach enabled us to elucidate a clear the PDHA1–lactate–H4K12la–SE–NUAK2 axis. We acknowledge that other lactylation sites (including on H3) likely contribute to fibrotic reprogramming and plan to investigate their roles in future studies.

Through an integrated multi-omics approach, we found that H4K12la is significantly enriched at super-enhancers that undergo remodeling during fibrosis, with the NUAK2 locus identified as a key target. Super-enhancers are well-known regulators of genes that define cell identity and disease states [50, 51], and their role in fibrosis is increasingly acknowledged [39, 52]. Our findings indicate that lactate-derived H4K12la acts as an initial signal for the activation of super-enhancers, which in turn promotes the recruitment of H3K27ac, leading to an increase in NUAK2 transcription. This model is supported by our epigenetic editing experiments, which show that altering H4K12la levels directly affects H3K27ac enrichment, a marker of active enhancers, which in turn influences NUAK2 expression. This creates a positive feedback loop, converting a temporary metabolic signal into a sustained pro-fibrotic transcriptional state.

The critical role of NUAK2 as a downstream effector is well-established in our study, demonstrating that NUAK2 is not only necessary but also sufficient to drive the activation, proliferation, and migration of myofibroblasts. Genetic rescue experiments revealed that NUAK2 knockdown completely reversed the pro-fibrotic effects of PDHA1 K83Q overexpression, reinforcing NUAK2’s role in the PDHA1-lactylation pathway. This finding aligns with the emerging role of NUAK kinases in integrating stress and metabolic signals to modulate cellular responses [40, 41]. Furthermore, our study provides strong pharmacological evidence supporting the targeting of this pathway. The selective NUAK2 inhibitor WZ4003 demonstrated a dose-dependent reduction in pulmonary fibrosis in the BLM model, highlighting the therapeutic potential of disrupting this pathway. These results position NUAK2 as a promising druggable target for IPF and underscore the clinical relevance of the entire SIRT3-PDHA1-K83ac-lactate-H4K12la-NUAK2 axis.

Several important questions remain regarding the mechanisms behind SIRT3 downregulation in pulmonary fibrosis. Further investigation is needed to clarify various aspects, such as transcriptional regulation, protein stability, or NAD+ availability. Additionally, it is important to explore whether other histone lactylation sites or the lactylation of non-histone proteins contribute to the expression of fibrotic genes. Moreover, the clinical applicability of targeting PDHA1 acetylation or NUAK2 activity in IPF warrants careful evaluation.

In conclusion, our research reveals a previously unrecognized pathogenic circuit in pulmonary fibrosis. Specifically, SIRT3 downregulation leads to PDHA1 K83 hyperacetylation, impairing oxidative metabolism and promoting lactate accumulation. This accumulation triggers H4K12la-mediated epigenetic reprogramming, activating the NUAK2 SE and driving fibrogenesis. Our study advances the understanding of how a mitochondrial metabolic defect is converted into a specific epigenetic and transcriptional signal in the nucleus, maintaining a pro-fibrotic state. These findings suggest that targeting PDHA1 K83 deacetylation and inhibiting its downstream effector, NUAK2, may offer promising therapeutic strategies for IPF.
