Optimization of energy consumption in the gas refinery sweetening unit using RSM, ANN, and Aspen HYSYS
Authors: Erfan Gholamzadeh, Abolfazl Shokri, Ahad Ghaemi, Bahman Heydari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This research focuses on introducing the gas sweetening unit and identifying its optimal operating conditions using advanced modeling techniques. By analyzing experimental data collected over 1227 days from a gas refinery’s sweetening unit, the study investigates factors influencing energy consumption. Two methods, response surface methodology (RSM) and artificial neural networks (ANNs), were employed to model the process, with the ANNs utilizing a multilayer perceptron (MLP) and a radial basis function (RBF). The R² value for RSM was 0.930, whereas the ANN models achieved higher accuracy, with R² values of 0.981 for RBF and 0.986 for MLP. Performance metrics also favored MLP, which recorded a lower error value of 0.002 compared to RBF (0.0051), making MLP the preferred method. Using the optimized MLP model, it was predicted that with an input feed of 8.3 MMSCM, 30.3% DEA amine, and 12.7% methyl diethanol amine (MDEA), fuel consumption could be reduced to 17380.2 SCM - a saving of 12710 SCM. Finally, the accuracy of the MLP model’s predictions was validated through simulations in Aspen HYSYS, further confirming its effectiveness in optimizing energy consumption.

Natural gas sweetening unit

The gas sweetening unit in gas refining facilities is one of the most crucial units in the process of gas purification and is known for its power consumption. This unit separates harmful and acidic gases, including CO2 and H2S, from the gas stream using amine solutions. As shown in Fig. 1, the sour gas (stream 2) enters the bottom part of the absorption tower and moves upwards through the amine solution. During this process, the amine absorbs the acidic gases, and the treated, sweet gas is let out from the top of the tower (stream 3). To regenerate the amine solution, which becomes saturated with acidic compounds (stream 6), the pressure of the amine stream is reduced in a flash evaporation process16. By reducing the pressure of the amine stream, a portion of the acid gases dissolved in the amine is released (stream 8) and exits the system. In the subsequent stage, the amine stream undergoes preheating in a heat exchanger before entering the disposal tower. In the disposal tower, the amine is injected from the top, while steam is introduced from the bottom. As the amine and steam flows are heated, all the acidic compounds are removed from the amine. Finally, the regenerated amine stream, now free of acidic gases, is pre-cooled in the heat exchanger and directed into the air coolers to lower the temperature (stream 20) further. In the next phase, a pump increases the pressure of the amine stream, allowing the gas sweetening cycle to repeat, with the fresh amine flow (stream 5) entering the absorption tower17.

The schematic of gas sweetening unit.

To analyze the energy performance of the gas sweetening unit, all factors influencing its energy consumption are identified. These factors are categorized into two types: static factors and relevant variables. Static factors are those that affect energy consumption but remain constant over time, while relevant variables are those that continuously change and have a measurable impact on energy consumption. These variables must be quantifiable and capable of being monitored. The process of monitoring and measuring involves tracking key parameters that influence energy performance. To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the data, it is crucial that the energy consumption and other defined influencing factors are correctly measured. For this purpose, the key operational characteristics affecting energy performance are systematically monitored and recorded at predefined intervals. The data utilized in this study were collected experimentally, with a total of 18,405 data points recorded over a period of 1,227 days.

Key parameters in the gas sweetening unit include the flow rate and percentage composition of circulating amine, the amount of steam consumed, and the energy consumption by pumps and fans. Among these, the circulating amine flow rate is particularly crucial in the gas sweetening process, as it directly influences other measured parameters18. If the composition, pressure, flow rate, and temperature of the sour gas entering the sweetening unit remain constant, the key parameters in the process can be adjusted to minimize energy consumption. At the same time, these adjustments must ensure that the sweet gas exiting the process meets the refinery’s quality standards19. This process is referred to as energy optimization. The H2S concentration is measured daily using an HPLC device in the refinery laboratory. The feed flow rate and steam flow rate are measured and recorded by flow meters installed in the main inlet path20. Moreover, to examine multicollinearity among input variables and prevent its adverse effects on the accuracy of machine learning models, the Pearson correlation matrix was utilized. Table 2 presents the linear correlation coefficients among 14 independent variables. This analysis is a crucial step in data preprocessing since high multicollinearity can lead to model coefficient instability, reduced generalizability, and increased prediction error. The results indicate that most absolute correlation coefficients are below 0.7, suggesting no severe multicollinearity among the majority of variables. However, some significant and notable relationships are observed; for instance, a strong correlation of 0.85 between the sour gas feed flow rate (feed MMSCM) and the produced acid gas volume (acid gas MSCMD), which aligns with process logic and confirms the direct impact of feed quantity on the amount of separated acid gas. Among the amine composition variables, a moderately strong inverse correlation of −0.58 is seen between the percentages of MDEA and DEA, indicating that an increase in one naturally results in a decrease in the other within the amine mixture. Additionally, the MDEA percentage shows negative correlations with other variables such as rich loading and fuel specific gravity, which may reflect indirect effects of amine structure on operational process characteristics. Furthermore, the steam consumption in the reduction unit (steam Ton/hr) positively correlates with MDEA concentration of 0.34 and amine rate of 0.38, implying that increases in amine flow rate and concentration likely lead to higher steam demand for regenerating the rich solution in the distillation column. A negative correlation of −0.52 between the H₂S outlet concentration and fuel specific gravity is also observed, possibly related to the type of fuel used and its effects on process temperature and separation efficiency. Considering these analyses, no variables were excluded from the dataset, as the interdependencies among independent parameters did not exceed a correlation coefficient of 0.7.

Response surface methodology

RSM is an effective tool for evaluating, modeling, and analyzing the effects of various operational variables and their interactions. The datasets used for fitting, based on experimental tests, were derived from empirical models, with the resulting mathematical relationships and statistical methods incorporated into the RSM. In recent years, RSM has garnered significant attention from researchers due to its ability to optimize complex processes21. Although many input variables influence process performance and response characteristics, RSM employs a set of mathematical and statistical techniques to analyze the interactions between these operating variables. This method is capable of considering system conditions and optimizing the response. To comprehensively describe the process and potential variations in operating conditions, it is essential to model its performance using at least one quadratic model and one higher-order quadratic or nonlinear model. MLP results

The first layer of MLP is the structure of the input layer that allows data (such as flow, temperature, pressure, etc.) to enter the network; the last layer is the output layer, which provides the target data for this network, which is energy consumption. To improve the model training process, the data were randomly divided into training, validation, and testing sets in a 70%, 15%, and 15% ratio, respectively. The network consisted of 14 inputs and one output. Two hidden layers were employed to improve model accuracy, though this increased the complexity and potential for error. The optimal configuration, which provided the best accuracy, included 20 neurons in the first hidden layer and 25 neurons in the second layer30. In Fig. 7, the R2 values are 0.993, 0.96214, and 0.956 for the training Fig. 7a, validation Fig. 7b, and testing Fig. 7c datasets, respectively, and 0.98648 for the overall prediction set Fig. 7d. One of the main advantages of the MLP model is its ability to identify nonlinear patterns and complex relationships among parameters in energy consumption. This model can simultaneously consider the interactions of multiple variables and capture subtle and sudden fluctuations in energy consumption ranges with high accuracy. In practice, this feature allows the MLP to better predict and analyze nonlinear behaviors of real systems, especially under conditions where parameter changes are interdependent or the effect of a variable on energy consumption is nonlinear. In contrast, the RSM method, primarily designed for modeling simple or nearly linear relationships, has limitations in capturing complex details and nonlinear fluctuations and cannot reflect the precise interactions of variables in real conditions to the same extent as the MLP model.

The RBF model also has the capability to capture nonlinear relationships and often performs well; however, its structure, based on radial basis functions, is highly sensitive to the selection of radius parameters and the number of centers. Therefore, the performance difference of MLP compared to RBF arises from its superior ability to learn and model extensive nonlinear relationships, its greater stability with respect to network parameters, and its higher accuracy in capturing subtle system changes.

HYSYS simulates the studied process based on its internal thermodynamic and mass transfer equations and provides key process parameter values when operational conditions are input. However, the default HYSYS results do not necessarily represent the process’s optimal points. Determining these optimal points requires using the software’s optimization module, which relies on precisely defined process equations. In this study, access to the complete process equations was not available, making direct optimization in HYSYS impossible.

To overcome this limitation, the MLP model was first used to determine the optimal operational points, which were then input into HYSYS to verify whether the software could successfully run the simulations and reproduce the process behavior with these values. Successful simulation indicates that the operational points suggested by the MLP model are logically consistent with the process equations and can be considered valid for industrial application. In this validation, both proposed operational points were successfully simulated in HYSYS, and the key parameters, including steam consumption and circulating amine flow, were accurately reproduced, confirming the reliability and capability of the MLP model to provide meaningful operational points consistent with the real process.

Conclusion

In this research, an attempt was made to model the collected data from the sweetening unit of a gas refinery in Iran using various methods. The goal was to identify the technique with the highest accuracy and, subsequently, use the selected method to determine the optimal operating conditions for the sweetening unit. By controlling these parameters, the operating conditions can be managed to achieve the desired outcome, which is a reduction in energy consumption. Given that the collected data is real-world data and may contain errors (due to factors such as equipment calibration issues or human error during data collection), the operating conditions presented are based on a combination of the authors’ expertise and the predicted outcomes from the MLP network. The results show that optimizing the percentage of available amines can lead to significant energy savings. For instance, in the actual data, the input feed to the sweetening unit is 8.3 million standard cubic meters, with 29.5% MDE amine and 7.6% MDE amine, resulting in a fuel consumption of 30,120 SCM. However, the MLP network predicts that with the same input feed and adjustments to 30.3% MDE amine and 12.7% MDE amine, the fuel consumption would decrease to 17,380 SCM, representing a savings of 12,710 SCM. Considering an average fuel price of 0.3 USD per standard cubic meter, this reduction corresponds to an annual economic saving of approximately 3,813 USD. This saving not only reduces direct fuel costs but also decreases maintenance expenses and environmental emissions, contributing significantly to the operational sustainability of the sweetening unit. Based on the MLP network predictions and the authors’ experience, for sweetening 8.3 million standard cubic meters of gas with 1,776 mg/m³ H2S at a temperature range of 40–50 °C, a flow rate of 280 cubic meters per hour of amine, with 30.3% MDE amine and 12.7% MDE amine, energy consumption reduction is predicted.
