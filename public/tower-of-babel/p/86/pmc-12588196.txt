Licoricidin Enhances Paclitaxel-induced Apoptosis Through Endoplasmic Reticulum Stress in Human Cervical Cancer Cells
Authors: CHING-TING TAI, YI-CHANG CHEN, YI-HSIEN HSIEH, CHIA-LIANG LIN, TSUNG-HO YING, JIN-CHERNG CHEN
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background/AimThe adverse side effects associated with chemotherapeutic agents have prompted the exploration of natural compounds as adjuvants to chemotherapy, offering more effective therapeutic alternatives. Licoricidin, a bioactive constituent of licorice, possesses diverse pharmacological properties. However, the antitumor mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of Licoricidin combined with paclitaxel in cervical cancer remain unclear and further investigations are warranted.Materials and MethodsCell growth and apoptosis were assessed using the MTT assay and Annexin V/PI staining. Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress was evaluated using the ER-ID assay. The expression of apoptosis- and ER stress-related proteins in response to the combined treatment was analyzed using western blotting.ResultsCombined treatment with licoricidin and paclitaxel effectively inhibited cell growth and induced apoptosis in human HeLa and C-33A cells. We further revealed that this combined treatment increased the expression of apoptotic proteins (c-PARP, Bax, and DR5) while decreasing the expression of pro-caspase proteins (pro-caspase-3 and pro-caspase-9) in HeLa cells. In addition, the combined treatment induced ER stress, as evidenced by decreased expression levels of ERp44, ERp57, and ERp72, whereas the expression of GRP78 was increased in HeLa cells.ConclusionThe combination of licoricidin and paclitaxel induced ER stress-mediated apoptosis and may serve as a potential antitumor therapeutic strategy against human cervical cancer cells.

Introduction

Currently, the incidence and mortality of cervical cancer continue to rise (1). According to the 2022 global cancer statistics, the newly diagnosed cases of cervical cancer and deaths are increasing worldwide, indicating that cervical cancer remains a major threat to women’s health. In current clinical practice, conventional radiotherapy and chemotherapy are frequently limited by therapeutic resistance and severe adverse effects (2,3). Although cellular immunotherapy demonstrates potential, its clinical efficacy remains restricted due to the presence of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, lengthy preparation processes, and pronounced physiological adverse reactions (4). Similarly, targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors are primarily applicable to patients with specific target-positive tumors, thereby limiting their broader clinical utility (5). Consequently, in-depth investigation into novel combination therapeutic strategies for cervical cancer is urgently required.

Paclitaxel is a clinically approved chemotherapeutic agent for cervical cancer and is commonly employed as a neoadjuvant therapy (6). However, some patients exhibit limited response to paclitaxel, and clinical adjuvant chemotherapy may fail to completely suppress tumor progression, leading to suboptimal therapeutic outcomes and potential delays in administering more effective treatments (7). These limitations are largely attributed to the multiple adverse effects associated with paclitaxel, which can result in unnecessary systemic damage (8). Therefore, identifying natural compounds that can be combined with paclitaxel to suppress cervical cancer and enhance therapeutic efficacy is of critical importance.

Licoricidin, an isoflavone isolated from the root of Glycyrrhiza glabra, was initially reported for its therapeutic effect against gastric ulcers (9). Some studies have demonstrated that licoricidin possesses a lot of pharmacological activities, with particular attention to its remarkable anticancer effects on several cancers including breast cancer (10), gastric cancer (11), colorectal cancer (12), and prostate cancer (13), and has also been reported to induce DNA damage (14). More importantly, recent evidence has suggested that licoricidin induces immunogenic cell death in cervical cancer cells through endoplasmic reticulum stress and HMGB1 protein expression (15). Despite these findings, studies investigating the combination of licoricidin with clinically used chemotherapeutic agents remain limited. To date, only one report has described a synergistic effect of licoricidin and gemcitabine in osteosarcoma cells, where the combination promoted apoptosis by inhibiting the Akt and NF-κB signaling pathways (16). However, the antitumor effect of licoricidin in combination with paclitaxel in cervical cancer remains unclear. Therefore, the aim of this study was to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying this potential therapeutic strategy.

Materials and Methods

Cell culture and combined drug assay. Human cervical adenocarcinoma cells (HeLa; BCRC 60005) and cervical carcinoma cells (C-33A; BCRC 60554) were purchased from Bioresource Collection and Research Center (BCRC, Hsinchu, Taiwan, ROC), and cervical squamous carcinoma cells (SiHa; ATCC HTB-35) was purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were cultured in RPMI-1640, DMEM, or MEM medium (Gibco; Invitrogen Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA), respectively, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) and 1×penicillin-streptomycin (P/S) solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). All cells were maintained at 37˚C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Licoricidin (ChemFaces, Wuhan, Hubei, PR China) was administered at a final concentration of 20 µM, and paclitaxel (ChemFaces) at 10 µM, either alone or in combination for 24 h.

Cell cytotoxicity assay. Three cervical cancer cells (HeLa, SiHa and C-33A) were treated with Licoricidin (20 µM), paclitaxel (10 µM), or their combination for 24 h. After the incubation for 24 h, MTT solution (0.5 mg/ml) was added to each well for 2 h. The crystal violet formazan was dissolved with isopropanol for 5 min, following measuring the absorbance at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Labsystems, Helsinki, Finland) to calculate cell viability (%).

Cell apoptosis assay with Annexin V/PI staining. Muse® Annexin V & Dead Cell Assay Kit was used to detect cell apoptosis. HeLa and C-33A cells were treated with licoricidin (20 µM), paclitaxel (10 µM), or their combination for 24 h. Cells were harvested and stained with Muse™ Annexin V & Dead Cell Reagent for 10 min. Apoptosis was monitored using a Guava Muse Cell Analyzer flow cytometer and Muse® Cell Analyzer Assays (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany).

ER stress detection. ER-ID® Green detection kit (Enzo Life Sciences, Farmingdale, NY, USA) was used as an ER fluorescent probe to detect ER stress. Briefly, HeLa and C-33A cells (8×103 cells/well) were incubated with ER-ID® Green detection reagent in serum free medium at 37˚C for 15 min, counterstained with Hoechst 33342, and observed under an ImageXpress® Pico Automated Cell Imaging System (Molecular Devices, San Francisco, CA, USA).

Western blotting. Total proteins were isolated from human cervical cancer cells and separated on 8-10% SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4˚C with the following primary antibodies: anti anti-cleaved-PARP (#5625; Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-caspase-9 (9508S; Cell Signaling), anti-DR5 (sc-166624; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-Bax (sc-6236; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), ER protein folding antibody sampler kit (#4759; Cell signaling,), and anti-β-actin (sc-47778; Santa Cruz Biotechnology). After washing for twice, the PVDF membranes were incubated with the HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL), and band intensity was quantified with ImageJ software, with β-actin used as a loading control for normalization.

Statistical analysis. The results are expressed as the means±SEMs. One-way ANOVA test was used to determine the statistical significance of differences between groups. Data were considered statistically significant if p<0.05 or p<0.01. All the statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad software version 6.0.

Results

Combination of licoricidin and paclitaxel inhibits the growth of human cervical cancer cells. The chemical structures of licoricidin and paclitaxel are shown in Figure 1. To evaluate the effects of their combination on cell growth and cytotoxicity, C-33A, HeLa, and SiHa cells were treated with licoricidin, paclitaxel or their combination and cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay. The results demonstrated that the combination of licoricidin (20 µM) and paclitaxel (10 µM) significantly reduced growth of C-33A and HeLa cells, compared with treatment with either licoricidin or paclitaxel alone (Figure 2A and B). In contrast, the same combination did not have a significant inhibitory effect on SiHa cells (Figure 2C). These findings suggest that the combination of licoricidin and paclitaxel effectively inhibits the growth of C-33A and HeLa cells.

(A) HeLa, (B) C-33A, and (C) SiHa cells were treated with licoricidin (20 μM), paclitaxel (10 μM), or their combination for 24 h. Cell growth was assessed using the MTT assay. Data are presented as the mean±SD of three independent experiments. **p<0.01 compared with untreated cells; #p <0.05 compared with licoricidin or paclitaxel treatment.

Combination of licoricidin and paclitaxel induces apoptosis of C-33A and HeLa cells. The results shown in Figure 2 suggested that the combination of licoricidin and paclitaxel inhibits growth of cervical cancer cells. We next investigated whether this combination could also induce apoptosis in C-33A and HeLa cells. Annexin V/PI staining followed by flow cytometry analysis revealed that licoricidin treatment increased the proportion of apoptotic cells in C-33A (35.1%) and SiHa (29.1%) cells, while paclitaxel increased apoptosis in C-33A (40.8%) and SiHa (46.5%) cells. Notably, the combination treatment further enhanced apoptosis, with apoptotic rates of 57.1% in C-33A cells and 73.4% in SiHa cells (Figure 3). These findings demonstrated that the combined treatment with licoricidin and paclitaxel effectively promotes apoptosis in cervical cancer cells.

Data are presented as the mean±SD of three independent experiments. **p<0.01 compared with untreated cells; ##p<0.01 compared with licoricidin or paclitaxel treatment.

Combination of licoricidin and paclitaxel triggers intrinsic and extrinsic apoptotic pathways in C-33A and HeLa cells. Apoptosis is a type of programmed cell death that involves both the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways (17). To investigate whether the combination of licoricidin and paclitaxel activates these apoptotic pathways in C-33A and HeLa cells, western blot analysis was performed. Treatment with licoricidin (20 µM) and paclitaxel (10 µM) in combination significantly increased the extrinsic pathway-related proteins cleaved-PARP and Bax, decreased the levels of pro-caspase-3 and pro-caspase-9, and enhanced the expression of the intrinsic pathway-related protein DR5 in HeLa cells, compared with treatment with drug either alone (Figure 4). These findings indicate that the combination of licoricidin and paclitaxel induces apoptosis through both the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways.

The expression of apoptosis-related proteins was analyzed using western blotting. β-actin was used as a loading control. Data are presented the mean±SD of three independent experiments. *p<0.05, **p<0.01 compared with untreated cells; #p<0.05 compared with licoricidin or paclitaxel treatment.

Combination of licoricidin and paclitaxel triggers endoplasmic reticulum stress in HeLa cells. Studies have shown that the induction of tumor cell apoptosis by natural compounds is closely associated with endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress (18). The ER-ID assay was used to examine whether licoricidin and paclitaxel trigger ER stress in HeLa cells. Treatment with licoricidin or paclitaxel alone increased the fluorescence intensity of ER-Tracker Green, while the combination treatment further significantly enhanced the fluorescent intensity in HeLa cells (Figure 5). We next examined the expression of ER stress-related proteins (ERp44, ERp57, ERp72, and GRP78) in response to the combination treatment. Western blot analysis revealed that the combination of licoricidin (20 µM) and paclitaxel (10 µM) significantly decreased the expression of ERp44, ERp57, and ERp72, while increasing the expression of GRP78 in HeLa cells, compared with either treatment alone (Figure 6). These findings indicate that the combination of licoricidin and paclitaxel induces ER stress in HeLa.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated for the first time that combination treatment with licoricidin and paclitaxel induces ER stress and apoptosis by increasing ER stress-related proteins and activating both intrinsic and extrinsic apoptotic pathways in human cervical cancer cells. In our previous study we reported that licoricidin exerts antitumor effects against human cervical cancer cells through the induction of apoptosis and immunogenic cell death (15). Paclitaxel is widely recognized as a first-line chemotherapeutic agent for cervical cancer (19); however, its clinical efficacy is limited by severe side effects and toxicity, which compromise treatment outcomes (20). Increasing evidence also suggests that licoricidin exhibits antitumor activity in various cancer cell types (11,13). To address the toxicity concerns associated with paclitaxel, we examined the effect of combination treatment with licoricidin and paclitaxel and found that it effectively suppresses cervical cancer cell growth and promotes apoptosis in vitro (Figure 7).

Proposed mechanism of ER stress and apoptosis induction by combination treatment with licoricidin and paclitaxel in human cervical cancer cells.

ER stress refers to a cellular state caused by the excessive accumulation of unfolded or misfolded proteins in the ER. Under excessive or prolonged ER stress cells undergo apoptosis. Upon accumulation of unfolded proteins in cells, the ER-resident chaperone GRP78 is highly upregulated, thereby activating the unfolded protein response (UPR) (21). Subsequently, GRP78 binds strongly to misfolded proteins to reduce aggregation and promote correct folding. During ER stress, these ER-associated proteins are activated to mediate protein folding and promote cellular homeostasis (22). Our results indicated that combination treatment with licoricidin and paclitaxel triggers cell apoptosis by inducing ER stress in human cervical cancer cells (Figure 5). Rellmann et al. demonstrated that 4-Phenylbutyric Acid (4-PBA) restores the expression of ERp57 which is sufficient to reduces ER stress in chondrocytes (23). Similarly, inhibition of ERp44 was shown to trigger apoptosis via ER stress-mediated upregulation of CHOP and GRP78 in HeLa cells (24). Wang et al. reported that a novel platinum compound, E-platinum, increased the expression of ER stress-associated proteins such as GRP78 (25). Moreover, tunicamycin-induced apoptosis was shown to increase expression of ER stress marker proteins BiP and CHOP in chondrocytes (26). Consistent with these findings, we observed that licoricidin combined with paclitaxel induced ER stress in cervical cancer cells. Interestingly, this treatment decreased the expression levels of ER-associated proteins (ERp57, ERp44, and ERp72) while increasing GRP78 expression in HeLa cells. However, further studies are needed to clarify whether ER stress is directly associated with apoptosis in response to the combination treatment with licoricidin and paclitaxel.

Apoptosis can be initiated through the extrinsic, death receptor-mediated pathway (27). Briefly, death ligands bind to their corresponding death receptors 5 (DR5) on the cell surface. Dearth receptor 5 (DR5) is an important TRAIL receptor that triggers the extrinsic pathway of apoptosis through the FADD/caspase-8 pathway, and it can amplify the signal by cross-talking with the mitochondrial pathway (28). Paclitaxel has been reported to upregulate DR5 expression, thereby activating the caspase-8/-3 cascade and promoting mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis (29). Similarly, luteolin in combination with paclitaxel was shown to synergistically induce apoptosis in ESCC cells (30). In addition, Dewangan et al. demonstrated that paclitaxel and kaempferol encapsulated in solid lipid nanoparticles represent a novel targeted therapy against breast cancer (31). Our study is the first to show that the combination of licoricidin and paclitaxel induces DR5-mediated apoptosis in human cervical cancer cells. In future studies, additional evidence is needed to clarify the molecular mechanisms by which the combination of licoricidin and paclitaxel targets DR5 expression and whether this affects the downstream activation of caspase-8/-3 signaling pathways.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that the combination of licoricidin and paclitaxel exerts a synergistic antitumor effect by inducing apoptosis through activation of ER stress mediated by ER-associated proteins (ERp57, ERp44, ERp72, and GRP78) in human cervical cancer cells. Further investigations are needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms, and in vivo animal studies are warranted to evaluate the antitumor efficacy of this combination therapy.
