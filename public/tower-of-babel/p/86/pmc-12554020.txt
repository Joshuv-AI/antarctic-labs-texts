Genome-wide identification and expression analyses of HSP90 genes in cassava (Manihot esculenta)
Authors: Hao Wan, Xutang Tu, Xiaohua Duan, Jun Luo, Zhengrong Zou, Shangguang Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeat shock proteins (HSPs) play a crucial role in plant thermotolerance responses. While the regulatory mechanisms of HSPs in Manihot esculenta have been extensively studied, current understanding of the HSP90 gene family, which serves as key regulators in high-temperature adaptation, remains limited in M. esculenta research. HSP90 not only influences M. esculenta growth and development but also significantly affects starch content, ultimately impacting yield. In this study, we identified and characterized 11 HSP90 genes in the M. esculenta genome, which are randomly distributed across seven chromosomes. These genes contain 10 conserved motifs and exhibit variable intron numbers (1–19), suggesting their broad involvement in regulatory networks. Phylogenetic analysis revealed high homology between M. esculenta HSP90 genes and those in Solanum tuberosum. Quantitative real-time PCR demonstrated distinct expression patterns of HSP90 genes under 43 °C heat stress, with significant tissue-specific variations. These findings address a critical knowledge gap by elucidating the cascade effects of HSP90 within the HSP family. Importantly, our results not only clarify the unique regulatory role of HSP90 in M. esculenta ’s heat stress response but also reveal its molecular mechanism in high temperature adaptation through starch biosynthesis regulation, providing valuable molecular targets and theoretical foundations for developing heat-tolerant M. esculenta cultivars.Keywords: Manihot esculenta, HSP90, Phylogenetic analysis, Heat stress

Introduction

Manihot esculenta, a dicotyledonous plant from the Euphorbiaceae family and native to South America, ranks as the sixth largest food crop globally due to its remarkable drought tolerance and adaptability to nutrient deficient soils.1 This crop is crucial for global food security. However, M. esculenta yields in tropical regions have declined by 10 % over the past two decades, largely due to increasing temperatures driven by global warming.2 This decline highlights the urgent need to explore strategies to mitigate the adverse effects of high temperatures on M. esculenta production.3 While improving cultivation conditions can help alleviate some of these negative impacts, using molecular breeding techniques to develop heat resistant M. esculenta varieties represents a more advanced and promising approach.

Heat shock proteins (HSPs) play a critical role in plant physiology, primarily assisting plants in coping with and adapting to high temperatures.4 As molecular chaperones, HSPs prevent protein aggregation and denaturation, thereby protecting cells from heat stress-induced damage.5 Wang et al. found that exposure to high temperatures significantly increases the expression of HSPs genes, which helps plant cells maintain proper protein folding and function. HSPs proteins are classified into six subfamilies: HSP100, HSP90, HSP70, HSP60, HSP40, and HSP20,6 HSP90 is a highly conserved molecular chaperone essential for the proper folding among them, stability, and functionality of various intracellular proteins. It is critical for the normal operation of key proteins, including kinases and transcription factors. HSP90 plays an essential role in managing thermal stress by regulating the activity of these associated proteins, thereby promoting cell proliferation and differentiation under high temperature conditions, processes critical for the development of roots, stems, leaves, and other organs. Additionally, HSP90 enhances starch accumulation in M. esculenta under high temperature stress by protecting key starch biosynthesis enzymes (AGPase, GBSSI) from thermal denaturation and activating starch-related genes through abscisic acid (ABA) signaling and SnRK2 kinase-mediated pathways. Notably, HSP90 plays a crucial role in the ABA signaling cascade by interacting with receptor kinases and transcription factors to mediate ABA responses7. The endogenous ABA level serves as a critical indicator of plant thermotolerance, with recent studies demonstrating that HSP90 significantly improves drought stress resistance through its involvement in ABA-dependent signaling pathways.8

To date, six HSP90 genes have been identified in Arabidopsis thaliana,9 7 in Oryza sativa,10 8 in Solanum tuberosum11 and 10 Triticum aestivum.12 11 HSP90 genes have been identified in M. esculenta13. At the molecular level, HSP90 aids in the proper folding of other proteins, thereby maintaining the stability of intracellular proteins14. It also activates the antioxidant enzyme system, which reduces the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and protects cells from damage caused by free radicals.15 Additionally, HSP90 regulates the expression of HSPs genes, promoting the synthesis of various members of the HSPs genes. At the physiological level, HSP90 contributes to water retention in M. esculenta by regulating membrane permeability and promoting wax layer formation.16 Furthermore, it maintains cellular integrity by reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, thereby optimizing the starch biosynthesis microenvironment and supporting plant growth and development. Collectively, through its molecular chaperone activity, HSP90 significantly mitigates the detrimental effects of heat stress on M. esculenta. It coordinately regulates heat shock responses, antioxidant mechanisms, and cellular stability, ultimately enhancing plant growth and ensuring yield stability under thermal stress conditions.

In recent years, although the functions of HSP90 have been extensively characterized in model plants such as Oryza sativa, Arabidopsis thaliana, and Triticum aestivum, its regulatory mechanisms in M. esculenta, particularly under heat stress conditions, remain poorly understood. To address this knowledge gap, this study identified 11 key HSP90 isoforms potentially involved in thermotolerance regulation through bioinformatic analysis, systematically characterized the HSP90 gene family in the M. esculenta genome, and investigated the spatiotemporal expression patterns of HSP90 genes under high temperature stress using Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). Our results revealed that these 11 HSP90 genes exhibited distinct differential expression profiles in roots, stems, and leaves under 43 °C heat stress at various time points (0, 6, 12, and 24 h). These findings provide fundamental insights into HSP90 mediated heat shock response mechanisms in M. esculenta and establish a critical foundation for developing molecular breeding strategies and transgenic approaches to enhance heat tolerance in this economically important crop.

Materials and methods

Identification of HSP90 family genes in M. esculenta

The complete genome sequence and gene structure annotation data (GFF3) for M. esculenta were retrieved from the EnsemblPlants database (https://plants.ensembl.org/index.html). The amino acid sequence of HSP90 from A. thaliana was obtained from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). A bidirectional blast analysis was subsequently conducted to identify candidate genes in the M. esculenta genome that share high sequence similarity with A. thaliana HSP90. The physicochemical properties of the M. esculenta HSP90 protein, including molecular weight, total amino acid count, and isoelectric point, were analyzed using the ExPASy online tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/).17 To determine the subcellular localization of M. esculenta HSP90 genes, we utilized Wolf Psort (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/).18 Additionally, we predicted their transmembrane localization with TMHMM 2.0 (https://www.Cbs.Dtu.dk/services/TMHMM-2.0/)19 and assess the presence of signal peptides using SignalP-5.0 (https://www.cbs.dtu.dk/cgi-bin).20

Phylogenetic analysis of M. esculenta HSP90 genes

Amino acid sequences for A. thaliana, O. sativa, S. tuberosum, and M. esculenta a were retrieved from the EnsemblPlants database. The phylogenetic tree was initially constructed using MEGA 7.0 (Stat-Ease Inc, Minneapolis, MN, USA),21 applying the neighbor-joining (NJ) method with a bootstrap value of 1000 to ensure robustness in model selection. Following this analysis, the resulting data were fitted and visualized using EvolView (https://www.evolgenius.info/evolview/).22

Conserved motif, structural characterization, and chromosome localization

The complete genome sequence and GFF3 file for M. esculenta were obtained from the EnsemblPlants database. To identify conserved motifs within the HSP90 protein sequences of M. esculenta, the MEME Suite (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme) was employed, and the results were saved in mastxml format. The genome annotation file and MEME output were then imported into TBtools (https://github.com/CJ-Chen/TBtools)23 for visualization of the coding sequence (CDS) structure and motif arrangement. Using the GFF3 sequence extractor tool in TBtools, sequences located 2000 bp upstream of all gene coding sequences were extracted. The fasta extractor tool was subsequently utilized to retrieve the promoter regions of HSP90 family members, which were then submitted to the PlantCARE database (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/btml/)24 for the prediction of cis regulatory elements. The predicted data were organized, summarized, and visualized using the simple biosequence viewer in TBtools. Finally, the genomic annotation information for the M. esculenta HSP90 genes was mapped onto the chromosomes, providing a visual representation of gene localization.

Examination of the key features of HSP90 family members in M. esculenta

To investigate the cis-regulatory elements in the promoter region of the HSP90 genes, we analyzed the sequences located within 5 kb upstream of the transcription start site using the PlantCARE database. The resulting data on cis-acting elements were then imported into TBtools to generate an overview map of protein motifs and cis-regulatory elements.

Examination of duplication events and collinearity of HSP90

Following the methodology outlined by Yao et al.,25 we not only evaluated the gene duplication events of M. esculenta HSP90 but also conducted collinearity analysis with three other species, the results were visualized using TBtools.

Computational modeling of the tertiary structure of HSP90

To predict the three-dimensional structures of the 11 HSP90 isoforms, we analyzed the protein sequences obtained from previous steps, we utilized the Swiss-Model Interactive Workshop (https://www.swissmodel.expasy.org/interactive)26 to evaluate the tertiary structure of HSP90 proteins.

Development of protein interaction network

The HSP90 protein sequences were analyzed using the String database (https://string-db.org/) to predict potential protein interactions. The resulting regulatory networks, which include HSP90 and its interacting partners, were then visualized using Cytoscape (https://www.cytoscape.org/).27 To assess the physicochemical properties of M. esculenta HSP90 proteins, such as molecular weight, amino acid residue count, and isoelectric point, we utilized the ExPASy database. Additionally, the subcellular localization of M. esculenta HSP90 family members was predicted using Wolf Psort. Signal peptides were detected with SignalP-5.0, and transmembrane localization was predicted using TMHMM 2.0. These analyses provide a comprehensive understanding of the functional characteristics and interactions of the M. esculenta HSP90 proteins.

M. esculenta growth, high temperature stress treatment

M. esculenta seeds were surface sterilized by soaking them in a 5 % sodium hypochlorite solution for 15 min. After this treatment, the seeds were transferred to seedling trays and watered according to the moisture levels of the soil. The trays were then placed in a climate-controlled chamber set to 28 °C, with a light intensity of 20,000 lx, relative humidity maintained at 60 %, and a photoperiod of 16 h of light followed by 8 h of darkness, for a total of 30 d. Following this initial growth period, M. esculenta plants of appropriate size were selected and transferred to a separate climate controlled chamber for high temperature (43 °C) treatment. The exposure durations for the high temperature treatment were 0.5, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h, with untreated plants serving as the control group. Leaves were uniformly selected as the detection sample for expression levels at different time points. M. esculenta plant adults (90 d) treated with high temperature for 24 h were selected as samples for expression level analysis of different partsEach experimental condition was replicated with three biological replicates. Upon treatment completion, plant samples were rapidly rinsed with distilled water, immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C to ensure accurate assessment of cassava's physiological responses to heat stress in subsequent experiments. For tissue-specific expression analysis, roots, stems, and leaves were separated during sampling to prevent RNA degradation caused by mechanical damage.

qRT-PCR experiments

In this study, RNA was isolated from the treated samples using the Trizol (Thermo Fisher Scientific) method, and complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). To quantify the expression levels of HSP90 in each sample, qRT-PCR was performed with a dye-based fluorescent qPCR premix kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific).28 The qRT-PCR reaction mixture consisted of 10 μL of fluorescent qPCR premix, 2 μL of cDNA, and 0.4 μL of both upstream and downstream primers. Ultrapure water was added to adjust the final volume to 20 μL. The thermal cycling conditions included an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 10 s, followed by annealing at 60 °C for 20 s and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, repeated for a total of 40 cycles. Specific primers were designed using the online tool Primer 5 (Table 1). Due to its stable expression and high accuracy, the Actin gene was chosen as the internal control for all experiments. This approach ensured reliable and accurate quantification of HSP90 expression in the samples.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were visualized using GraphPad Software (GraphPad Software Inc, San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical significance was evaluated at the 0.05 and 0.01 probability levels through analysis of variance (ANOVA) conducted in SPSS Statistics (IBM Corp, Armonk, NYC, USA). This rigorous statistical approach ensured a comprehensive assessment of the results obtained from the experiments, facilitating reliable interpretations of the data.

Results

Detection of HSP90 genes in M. esculenta

A total of 11 candidate members of the HSP90 gene family were identified in M. esculenta, namely MeHSP90.1, MeHSP90.2, MeHSP90.3, MeHSP90.4, MeHSP90.5, MeHSP90.6, MeHSP90.7, MeHSP90.8, MeHSP90.9, MeHSP90.10, and MeHSP90.11 (Table 2). Analysis revealed that these M. esculenta HSP90 proteins exhibit considerable variation in their amino acid composition, with lengths ranging from 593 to 813 residues. Specifically, MeHSP90.9 is the shortest, containing 593 amino acids, while MeHSP90.10 is the longest, with 813 amino acids. The molecular weights of the proteins also vary, ranging from 68.52 kDa for MeHSP90.9–93.19 kDa for MeHSP90.10. The isoelectric points (pI) of these proteins range from 4.83 to 5.32, classifying them all as acidic proteins.29 Predictions of subcellular localization suggest that all the identified proteins are localized in the cytoplasm and exhibit a transmembrane prediction score of 1. Notably, signal peptide analysis indicates that only MeHSP90.10 contains a signal peptide, whereas the other members lack this feature (Table 2).

Phylogenetic analyses of HSP90 genes

We identified 26, 25, and 8 similar members of the HSP90 gene family from A. thaliana, O. sativa, and S. tuberosum, respectively. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using a total of 70 genes, comprising 11 from M. esculenta, 26 from A. thaliana, 25 from O. sativa, and 8 from S. tuberosum. These 70 genes can be classified into two distinct groups, each containing 35 members. Group A consists of 14 HSP90 genes from A. thaliana, 7 from M. esculenta, 9 from O. sativa, and 5 from S. tuberosum. In contrast, Group B includes 12 HSP90 genes from A. thaliana, 4 from M. esculenta, 16 from O. sativa, and 3 from S. tuberosum (Fig. 1).30 Notably, the M. esculenta HSP90 family members in Group B display a smaller number of amino acid residues and lower relative molecular weights compared to those in Group A. The amino acid lengths in Group B range from 593 to 705 residues, corresponding to molecular weights between 68.52 and 81.07 kDa. Within Group B, we identified one pair of orthologous genes (MeHSP90.4) along with 9 pairs of paralogous genes: AtHSP90.11 and AtHSP90.13, AtHSP90.17 and AtHSP90.19, AtHSP90.21 and AtHSP90.23, MeHSP90.7 and MeHSP90.8, StHSP90.2 and StHSP90.4, StHSP90.5 and StHSP90.7, MeHSP90.10 and MeHSP90.11, AtHSP90.22 and AtHSP90.26, OrHSP90.20 and OrHSP90.16. In Group A, there are 2 pairs of orthologous genes (StHSP90.1 and MeHSP90.3, OrHSP90.8 and StHSP90.3) as well as 8 pairs of paralogous genes: OrHSP90.3 and OrHSP90.1, OrHSP90.2 and OrHSP90.5, AtHSP90. 1 and AtHSP90.3, AtHSP90.2 and OrHSP90.7, MeHSP90.5 and MeHSP90.6, AtHSP90.10 and AtHSP90.7, OrHSP90. 12 and OrHSP90.13, OrHSP90.10 and OrHSP90.11. This analysis also indicates a significant level of homology between the HSP90 genes in M. esculenta and those found in S. tuberosum.31

Chromosomal location of HSP90 in M. esculenta genome

The 11 identified members are distributed across seven chromosomes, specifically chromosomes 1, 2, 5, 6, 12, 14, and 18. The distribution of these genes is uneven, and no tandem duplication events were observed (Fig. 2). Notably, three genes—MeHSP90.4, MeHSP90.5, and MeHSP90.6—are clustered on chromosome 6. MeHSP90.4 is located in the 20–25 MB region of chromosome 6, while MeHSP90.5 and MeHSP90.6 are situated in the adjacent 25–30 MB range. Additionally, two pairs of genes are also clustered on chromosomes 14 and 18. Specifically, MeHSP90.8 is located in the 0–5 MB region of chromosome 14, while MeHSP90.9 is positioned in the 5–10 MB region of the same linkage group. Genes MeHSP90.10 and MeHSP90.11 are located in the 0–5 MB region of chromosome 18. In contrast, genes MeHSP90.1 and MeHSP90.2 are located in the 0–5 MB regions of chromosomes 1 and 2, respectively. The remaining chromosomes, 5 and 12, each contain a single HSP90 gene: MeHSP90.3 on chromosome 5 and MeHSP90.7 on chromosome 12. More specifically, MeHSP90.3 is located in the terminal region of chromosome 5, while MeHSP90.7 is situated in the 5–10 MB region of chromosome 12 (Fig. 2).

Gene architecture and motif composition of HSP90 in M. esculenta

A phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the amino acid sequences of 11 M. esculenta HSP90 proteins. The proteins MeHSP90.1, MeHSP90.2, MeHSP90.3, MeHSP90.4, MeHSP90.5, MeHSP90.7, MeHSP90.8, MeHSP90.9, MeHSP90.10, and MeHSP90.11 all contain 10 conserved motifs, with their arrangement order being highly consistent (Fig. 3A). Notably, MeHSP90.7 harbors two copies of Motif 1, whereas MeHSP90.10 completely lacks this motif. Furthermore, MeHSP90.6 is missing both Motif 8 and Motif 9. The analysis also revealed significant variability in the number of introns among the members of the M. esculenta HSP90 family (Fig. 3B).32 To further investigate the biological functions associated with these protein domains, we performed a motif analysis of the M. esculenta HSP90 proteins, identifying a total of 15 conserved motifs across the sequences (Fig. 3C). This analysis also included an examination of the exon–intron structures of the 11 HSP90 genes (Fig. 3B). The results demonstrated that 8 HSP90 genes exhibit conserved exon–intron arrangements, while the remaining genes show a similar distribution pattern in their exon–intron structures.33

Analysis of HSP90 promoter

The promoter regions of M. esculenta HSP90 genes primarily contain cis-acting elements associated with high-temperature stress, hormonal regulation, and photosensitivity. Notably, all 11 members of the M. esculenta HSP90 gene family harbor elements linked to light responsiveness, anaerobic induction, cell cycle regulation, and methyl jasmonate (MeJA) response. Specifically, the promoters of all 11 genes include cis-acting elements for light response and anaerobic induction. 6 promoters—MeHSP90.1, MeHSP90.5, MeHSP90.6, MeHSP90.8, MeHSP90.10, and MeHSP90.11—contain elements involved in cell cycle regulation. Additionally, 8 promoters—MeHSP90.1, MeHSP90.2, MeHSP90.4, MeHSP90.5, MeHSP90.8, MeHSP90.9, MeHSP90.10, and MeHSP90.11—feature cis-acting elements responsive to MeJA (Fig. 4).34

Analysis of gene duplication and synteny within the HSP90 genes

To explore the homologous relationships within the HSP90 gene family in M. esculenta, we conducted a synteny analysis both within the species and across different species (Fig. 5). For interspecies comparisons, we selected A. thaliana, O. sativa, and S. tuberosum as model plants. The intraspecies analysis revealed that all 11 HSP90 genes in M. esculenta exhibit homology with each other (Fig. 5A). In the interspecies analysis, we identified 6 orthologous relationships between the 6 HSP90 family members in O. sativa and the HSP90 genes in M. esculenta. Similarly, 5 orthologous relationships were established between the HSP90 genes in A. thaliana and those in M. esculenta, as well as between the HSP90 members in S. tuberosum and the HSP90 genes in M. esculenta (Fig. 5B).

Tertiary configuration of HSP90 proteins in M. esculenta

The tertiary structure analysis of M. esculenta HSP90 proteins revealed that these proteins are primarily composed of α-helices, β-sheets, and random coils (Fig. 6). Notably, the proteins MeHSP90.1, MeHSP90.2, MeHSP90.3, MeHSP90.4, MeHSP90.5, and MeHSP90.10 exhibited similar structural characteristics, suggesting they share analogous functions. These proteins are rich in α-helices and perform binding functions as molecular chaperones.35 Additionally, we identified structural similarities among the proteins MeHSP90.6, MeHSP90.7, MeHSP90.8, MeHSP90.9, and MeHSP90.11, all of which have larger molecular weights and form an α-β sandwich pocket structure. This structural feature enables them to interact with signaling proteins such as protein kinases and steroid hormone receptors.36

Analysis of HSP90 genes expression across stems, leaves and roots

Investigating tissue-specific expression patterns is a fundamental approach to understanding gene functions.37 In this study, we examined the tissue-specific expression of HSP90 genes in M. esculenta. Our results revealed significant differences in the expression levels of 11 HSP90 genes across roots, stems, and leaves (Fig. 7). Notably, most HSP90 genes were expressed in all analyzed tissues. Among these, MeHSP90.1 exhibited the highest expression levels in both stems and leaves, while MeHSP90.4 was predominantly expressed in the leaves compared to other tissues. In contrast, MeHSP90.2, MeHSP90.6, MeHSP90.11, and MeHSP90.10 showed relatively low expression levels across all tissue types.

Quantitative analysis of the expression levels of 11 HSP90 genes in different tissues of M. esculenta. R denotes root, S denotes stem, and L denotes leaf (α = 0.05, Duncan test).

Expression of HSP90 genes under high temperatures

To investigate their potential roles under heat stress, we selected HSP90 genes from 11 subfamilies for further analysis. In contrast to the control group, M. esculenta leaves subjected to high temperature treatments for 0.5, 1, 3, and 6 h did not exhibit significant morphological changes (Fig. 8A). However, noticeable leaf curling became evident after 12 and 24 h of heat exposure. The expression profiles of these genes were assessed using qRT-PCR, which revealed a significant upregulation of HSP90 transcript levels, with expression peaking at 24 h. Notably, all gene expression levels increased compared to their pre-treatment values (Fig. 8B). Among these genes, the expression levels of MeHSP90.1, MeHSP90.3, MeHSP90.5, MeHSP90.6, MeHSP90.8, MeHSP90.9, and MeHSP90.11 showed progressive increases over time, with MeHSP90.1 exhibiting the most pronounced upregulation. In contrast, the expression of MeHSP90.2, MeHSP90.4, and MeHSP90.10 remained relatively stable throughout the treatment period, while MeHSP90.7 and MeHSP90.2 displayed gradual downward trends (Fig. 9). This comprehensive analysis reveals the dynamic response patterns of HSP90 genes to heat stress in cassava leaves, providing critical insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying plant stress responses.

Forecasting and correlation analysis of proteins interacting with HSP90

Building protein interaction networks is crucial for understanding gene interactions and regulatory relationships. In this study, we constructed an interaction network of genes associated with heat stress alleviation in M. esculenta to identify proteins that interact with heat shock proteins (HSPs) in M. esculenta. Our findings demonstrate that HSP90 can interact with HSP20 and HSP70 proteins and is closely associated with flowering-related proteins (CO, LFY, and FT) during cassava growth and development (Fig. 10). Furthermore, proteins associated with the HSP90 network are also involved in responses to various abiotic stresses, including salt, drought, cold, and heat stress. Additionally, the HSP90 proteins interacts with genes that control the development of shelling and floral organs.

Discussion

HSP90 is a critical chaperone protein that plays a vital role in plant growth and development. In M. esculenta, the HSPs consists of 225 members, including HSP20, HSP40, HSP60, HSP90, and HSP100.36 These proteins are crucial for regulating cassava's response to high temperatures stress. Research by Wang et al. has demonstrated that these genes are consistently expressed in plant cells under normal conditions, with their expression levels significantly increasing under extreme environmental stress. This underscores their essential role in plant stress adaptation.38 In this study, we performed a comprehensive genome wide identification of the HSP90 family in M. esculenta, identifying a total of 11 members (Table 2). The distribution of these M. esculenta HSP90 genes is primarily located at the ends of chromosomes, a pattern similar to that observed in S. tuberosum HSP90 genes.9 Furthermore, M. esculenta HSP90 proteins are classified as acidic proteins, consistent with findings from studies on other plant species, including A. thaliana, O. sativa, Phyllostachys edulis, and Solanum lycopersicum,39 this suggests that the physicochemical properties of plant HSP90 proteins are relatively conserved across species.

The abundance of HSP90 genes varies across different plant species. For example, M. esculenta possesses more HSP90 genes than A. thaliana,9 indicating that M. esculenta has an expanded gene family that may enhance its ability to finely regulate responses to various stressors. In contrast, wheat contains an even greater number of HSP90 genes compared to M. esculenta,40 suggesting that polyploid organisms, which have multiple sets of chromosomes, may harbor additional HSP90 genes due to gene duplication. Phylogenetic tree analysis is an effective method for revealing the evolutionary relationships between homologous and paralogous genes across species.41 We included HSP90 genes from four model plants, including M. esculenta. The results showed significant homology between M. esculenta and S. tuberosum (Fig. 1), offering valuable insights into the functional conservation of these genes across species and providing a foundation for predicting the potential functions of M. esculenta’s HSP90 family members. Based on previous studies on the functions of HSP90 in other model plants, such as Capsicum annuum and T. aestivum,42 we can infer that these homologous genes are likely to perform similar functions in M. esculenta as well.

Studies have shown a strong correlation between the number of introns in a gene and its sensitivity to external stress. Generally, genes with fewer introns exhibit greater flexibility in adapting to environmental pressures.43 In our study, 11 intronic sequences of HSP90 genes were identified, revealing a relatively low intron count (Fig. 3). This suggests that the HSP90 gene family possesses high adaptability to environmental stress, which may contribute to cassava’s drought tolerance.44

The number of members in the HSP90 gene family is influenced by factors such as genome size, duplication events, and ploidy levels. In general, higher plants tend to possess more HSP90 genes than lower plants.45 Gene duplication events, including segmental duplications and tandem repeats, are critical for the expansion of M. esculenta’s HSP90 gene family. Typically, as environmental pressures increase during evolution, gene duplication events become more prevalent.46 Consequently, the repeated sequences within the HSP90 gene family likely result from multiple selective pressures, a phenomenon observed in various other species (Fig. 4). Homology analysis further revealed shared HSP90 gene members across species, including A. thaliana, Populus przewalskii, and Hevea brasiliensis.47 Notably, M. esculenta shares the highest number of homologous HSP90 genes with Hevea brasiliensis.48

Cis-acting regulatory elements (CAREs) are essential molecular switches that regulate the transcriptional activity of complex gene networks.49 In our study, we identified several stress responsive elements associated with the HSP90 genes in M. esculenta (Fig. 4). Similar cis-regulatory elements have also been observed in Fagopyrum esculentum and Glycine max.50 The presence of these CAREs suggests their role in regulating various physiological processes and plant growth responses to environmental stimuli. Previous studies on A. thaliana have demonstrated that ABA can upregulate the expression of HSP90 proteins, enhancing thermotolerance.51 Similar findings have been reported in Piper nigrum, Malus pumila, and Brassica oleracea,52 reinforcing the idea that the biological functions of HSP90 are generally conserved across plant species and contribute to their responses to high temperatures stress. This conservation underscores the importance of HSP90 proteins in helping plants adapt to fluctuating environmental conditions.

Wang et al. analyzed the temporal expression patterns of M. esculenta HSPs under 42 °C conditions, finding that the expression trend of the HSP90 gene family differed significantly from that of other HSPs genes, providing valuable research insights. However, the expression patterns of HSP90 at different time points and in different tissues have not been studied independently. Our results show that the expression level of HSP90 gradually increases during the 0–12h period (Fig. 8). In contrast to the findings of Wang et al., the expression pattern from 12 to 24 h differs, indicating that HSP90 proteins play different roles in the alleviation of heat stress.53 This upregulation likely facilitates root storage organ specialization and counters starch loss induced by heat stress during cultivation.54 The stem, primarily responsible for nutrient transport, also displayed changes in local gene expression, regulated largely by external factors. High temperatures increased transcript levels in the stem, promoting cellular stability and ensuring proper nutrient circulation.55 Leaves, being the primary sites for photosynthesis, are highly sensitive to environmental stress. We observed that when exposed to extreme temperatures, the expression of HSP90 genes in leaves significantly increased. This suggests that maintaining an effective heat stress response mechanism is essential (Fig. 7). These differential expression patterns align with findings in other important species, such as Nicotiana tabacum and Antirrhinum majus,56 demonstrating that these principles are broadly applicable to the expression patterns of M. esculenta.

The increase in carbon emissions has resulted in more frequent high temperatures climate events, which have negatively impacted crop yields over the past decade and led to a decline in the environmental carrying capacity. Among the affected crops, M. esculenta cultivation faces significant challenges. To cope with these conditions, M. esculenta relies on mechanisms mediated by HSP90 proteins, which play a crucial role in enhancing its resistance to various environmental stresses (Fig. 10). HSP90 proteins interact with several signaling pathways, especially interacting with transcription factors related to growth, development, and flowering in the CO and LFY families. These interactions help stabilize and activate these transcription factors, thereby improving the plant's tolerance to heat stress.57 Future research should focus on elucidating the mechanisms underlying HSP90 function and enhancing crop resistance to climate change challenges. In the long term, this will be crucial for optimizing sustainable yield potential while ensuring global food security.

Conclusion

In this study, we identified 11 members of the HSP90 genes in M. esculenta and characterized their gene structures. A phylogenetic analysis was performed to compare the M. esculenta HSP90 genes with their homologs in O. sativa, A. thaliana, and S. tuberosum, revealing a high degree of homology among these species. Additionally, a protein interaction network was constructed, which suggested that several HSP90 proteins are involved in regulating the expression of genes related to high temperature stress. Furthermore, qRT-PCR experiment revealed distinct spatiotemporal expression patterns of M. esculenta HSP90 genes under heat stress conditions, addressing a critical gap in current research. This study deepens our understanding of the role of M. esculenta HSP90 in heat stress regulation and provides valuable insights that could aid in improving yield and preserving breeding resources.
