Effect of ultrasonic treatment on structure and physicochemical properties of starch: An updated review
Authors: Ning Bei, Zihao Song, Yuqi Shen, Guanhua Liu, Fang Dong, Xia Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Starch is a naturally abundant, low-cost, and renewable high-molecular-weight polysaccharide. As the main energy reserve of many plants, it is widely distributed in cereals, legumes, and tuber crops. It also serves as a foundation of dietary energy for billions of people worldwide and accounts for approximately two-thirds of daily caloric intake. Apart from being a basic nutrient, starch is widely used in the food industry as a thickener, stabilizer and gelling agent and non-food industry such as pharmaceuticals, papermaking and biodegradable materials due to its unique physicochemical properties [1]. The physicochemical properties of starch including thermal stability, pasting viscosity, solubility, swelling capacity and rheological properties are fundamentally regulated by its multi-scale microstructure, such as the crystalline and granule structure, which in turn determine the final behavior of starch during processing and use [2], [3], [4]. However, the inherent molecular and granular structure of native starch lead to a series of application limitations, such as low aqueous solubility at room temperature and a pronounced tendency toward retrogradation, which restrict its application in high-value-added products and modern food processing [5].

To overcome the intrinsic shortcomings of native starch and broaden its application, starch was modified extensively by the physical, chemical and enzymatic methods. Although traditional chemical modification significantly improves the starch properties by effectively introducing functional groups, it may also pose the risk of residual chemical reagents that affect food safety and cause environmental pollution. Enzymatic modification with strong specificity and pure products has a relatively high cost and strict reaction conditions (such as temperature and pH), which limits its large-scale industrial applications. In comparison, physical modification shows greater potential for sustainable development thanks to no exogenous chemical reagents, clean process and easy operation. Ultrasound treatment, due to its unique cavitation effect, mechanical shearing and thermal effect, has marked advantages among various physical modification techniques [6], [7]. High-frequency vibration energy, micro-streams and intense cavitation effects generated during the propagation of ultrasonic waves in a liquid medium can impose strong shear forces on starch granules and their molecular chains. These forces are sufficient to damage the surface structure of granules, crystalline and amorphous regions, and cleave starch molecular chains to remodel the starch structure and consequently improve the processability and nutritional value [8].

In summary, it is of theoretical significance for the precise application of starch after ultrasonic treatment by systematically elucidating how ultrasound influences the multi-scale structure of starch and its subsequent physicochemical properties. Although this topic has been reviewed ten years ago, substantial advances have been made in recent years [9]. Therefore, this article aims to conduct a comprehensive update and expansion of the study in this field, not only covering the latest studies on the single ultrasonic treatment, but also the synergistic effects of ultrasound combined with other modification technologies. Moreover, the influence of ultrasonic treatment parameters (power, time, and temperature, etc.) on the structure and physicochemical properties of starch was discussed in detail, with the intention of providing a more comprehensive and forward-looking review.

Structure and functional properties of starch

Starch is composed of two glucose polymers: amylose (AM) and amylopectin (AP). AM with the limited branching is a predominantly linear polysaccharide composed of D-glucopyranose residues linked through α-(1,4) glycosidic bonds. Its degree of polymerization (DP) typically ranges from 500 to 6,000 glucose units, with a molecular weight (Mw) of about 10⁶ Da. Differently, AP is a highly branched macromolecule composed of D-glucopyranose units linked by α-(1,4) glycosidic bonds and α-(1,6) glycosidic bonds at branch points. The DP of AP ranges from 3 × 105 to 3 × 10⁶, with the Mw of approximately 108 Da [10], [11]. The Mw, DP, chain length distribution (CLD) and branching degree (BD) of AM and AP together constitute the fine structure of starch. The branched structure of AP facilitates the formation of double helices between adjacent starch chains, which subsequently stack into crystalline lamellae. Based on the arrangement of these double helices, starch crystals are classified into three types: A-, B- and C-type. A-type crystals exhibit a monoclinic unit cell, whereas B-type crystals possess a hexagonal unit cell [12]. C-type crystals represent a hybrid structure composed of both A-type and B-type characteristics [13]. Cereals starches typically display A-type crystalline, roots and tubers starches predominantly exhibit B-type crystalline and the C-type crystals are primarily found in legume starches [14].

The long-range molecular order of the starch crystalline structure is typically characterized by the X-ray diffraction (XRD). Starches with different crystalline types exhibit diffraction peaks at different positions of the XRD pattern, and the relative crystallinity (RC) of the starch can be measured by calculating the area of the diffraction peaks [15]. A-type crystals present diffraction peaks at 15°, 17°, and 23° (2θ) and the B-type crystals show diffraction peaks at approximately 5.5°, 17°, 22° and 24° (2θ). The semi-crystalline regions formed together by the alternating crystalline and amorphous lamellae are interspersed with amorphous regions, which can be observed under microscopy and named as growth rings [13], [16]. The ratio of the crystalline to amorphous regions in starch can be characterized using the Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). A higher absorbance ratio at 1047 cm-1 to 1022 cm-1 (R1047/1022) indicates a greater degree of short-range molecular order [17]. Multiple growth rings are arranged in a concentric, layer-by-layer manner, with the diameter progressively increasing from the center toward the periphery of the starch granule, resulting in a well-defined lamellar structure of starch granules [18]. The morphology and size of starch granules vary considerably depending to the plant source. For example, potato starch granules are typically spherical or oval, ranging from 10 to 100 μm in diameter, whereas rice starch granules exhibit a polygonal shape with sizes between 3 and 8 μm [19], [20].

The functionalities of starch are primarily determined by its structure, which directly influence its applications in both food and non-food industries [21], [22], [23]. Starch granule can absorb water and its water absorption capacity is closely associated with the structural integrity. A more intact granule structure can increase the water absorption rate, thereby improving the resistance of starch granule to mechanical or physical disruption [24]. When the starch solution is heated in the presence of heat or shear force, the absorption of water will result in the filtration of AM, the dissolution of AP and the disruption of double helical and crystalline structure, which referred to as starch gelatinization. The degree of gelatinization increases with the increasing temperature, water content and shear force [25], [26], [27]. The increasing viscosity and digestibility were showed for the gelatinized starch [21]. During cooling, gelatinized starch can reassociate to form dense and ordered crystalline structure through hydrogen bonding, which can decrease starch digestibility. The process is known as starch retrogradation, which can be classified into short-term retrogradation of AM and long-term retrogradation of AP [28], [29]. The modification of starch structure can optimize its functional properties and expand the potential applications of starch in food and non-food industries.

Mechanisms of ultrasonic treatment

Ultrasound is a form of mechanical vibrational energy whose frequency is typically higher than 20 kHz and can reach 100 MHz or even higher [30]. As an efficient and green non-thermal physical processing technology, ultrasonic treatment has been widely applied in the fields of food, materials and chemicals [31]. It is commonly categorized into contact and non-contact modes, in which the former transfers acoustic energy via a probe or a liquid coupling medium, whereas the latter uses air as the coupling medium to deliver the acoustic stream to the target [32], [33]. The contact mode is predominantly utilized for the ultrasonic treatment of the starch, the action mechanism constitutes a complex physical process featuring multi-effect synergy including mainly the mechanical, cavitation and thermal effect [34]. The mechanical effect is the most direct manifestation of ultrasound and arises from the periodic vibration of particles induced as the sound wave propagates through a liquid medium [35]. The strong alternating acoustic pressure generated by the high-frequency vibration can impose sustained shear and agitation on starch granule to cause the surface erosion and fragmentation of starch granule, which increases specific surface area of starch granule and affects the structure and physicochemical properties of starch [36]. The cavitation effect is the most decisive mechanism during the ultrasonic treatment. A large number of microbubbles were formed in the liquid when the ultrasonic intensity is sufficient, these bubbles rapidly expand during the rarefaction phase and collapse violently during the compression phase of the sound wave, which is known as the acoustic cavitation. At the instant of bubble collapse, extreme temperatures (>5000 K), high pressures (approximately 50 MPa) and high-velocity microjets (>400 km/h) can be generated locally, which cause intense impacts on starch granule surfaces and create pores or cracks. In addition, highly reactive radicals (·H, ·OH) generated by the decomposition of water during cavitation can attack the glycosidic bonds of starch molecular chains, causing chain scission and the decrease of Mw, this is referred to as the radical effect. Accordingly, cavitation effect integrates both mechanical disruption and chemical degradation to modify starch [37], [38]. The thermal effect is a secondary mechanism. As ultrasound propagates through a medium, part of the acoustic energy is absorbed and converted into heat to rise temperature. Although ultrasound is classified as a “non-thermal technology”, the accumulated heat can promote the water absorption and swelling of starch granule, which working together with cavitation and mechanical effects to accelerate structure disassembly and molecule rearrangements of starch [39], [40]. The effect of ultrasonic treatment is influenced by both the ultrasonic parameters (such as power, and time) and the inherent characteristics of the starch (such as the starch source) (Fig. 1) [22], [40]. Effects of ultrasound on the starch structure

Effects of ultrasound on the fine structure of starch

The content of AM and AP can be determined by measuring the absorption spectrum of the starch-iodine complex using spectrophotometry based on the principle that the blue and red appears when iodine molecules combine with AM and AP, respectively [41]. Most studies showed that the amylose content (AC) increased after ultrasonic treatment [8], [41], [42], [43], [44], [45], [46], [47], and it generally increased with increasing ultrasonic power [8], [42], [45], [47], [48], [49], [50], [51], [52] and time [46], [47], [53], which can be attributed to the combination of iodine with long branches generated from the cleavage of the α‑1,6 glycosidic bonds at the branching points of AP by ultrasound [53], [54]. The AC first increased then slightly decreased as increasing treatment time from 15 to 35 min, indicating that there are optimal conditions for the release of AM [55]. The combining of ultrasonication with pullulanase synergistically promoted the cleavage of AP and increased AC, which is because pullulanase, as a debranching enzyme, can cleave the α-1,6 glycosidic bonds of AP [56], [57]. In addition, dual modification of ultrasonication with annealing [58], electric field [42], heat-moisture [48] increased AC more markedly than ultrasonication alone. There are also studies showed that the AC decreased after ultrasonic treatment [54], [59], [60], [61], [62], [63], which may be due to the rearrangement of dispersed starch chains or the degradation of AM after treatment [54]. The decreasing of AC was more pronounced after the dual modification with ultrasonic and glucoamylase [64], dual-enzyme (α-amylase and cellulose) [61] treatment due to the hydrolysis of AM. Ultrasonication combined with hydrothermal treatment can also significantly decrease AC, which was mainly due to the interaction or degradation of starch chains, as well as the interaction between AM and lipids [8], [45].

The ratio of AM to AP directly determines its structural properties of starch. According to the DP, the CLD of starch can be classified as follows: A chains (DP = 6–12), B1 chains (DP = 13–24), B2 chains (DP = 25–36), and B3 chains (DP ≥ 37) [50]. Ultrasonication can markedly alter the CLD and branching characteristics of starch and most studies showed that appropriate ultrasonic treatment degraded long chains and increased the proportion of short chains [45]. The A chains proportion of waxy corn starch increased, while the proportions of B1, B2 and B3 chains decreased as the ultrasonic power increased from 100 W to 400 W due to the degradation of B chains located in the amorphous regions [65]. Rice starch exhibited similar changes under ultrasonic powers of 150–300 W, however, the proportion of A chains decreased and the relative proportions of B2 and B3 chains increased when the power was further increased, because the higher power further disrupted the crystalline regions and unwound the double helix of starch [66]. The effects of ultrasound time on the CLD of starch are similar that the short-time treatment (10 min) increased the A and B1 chains and reduced B2 and B3 chains, whereas the long-term ultrasonication (50 min) decreased the A chains [67]. The ultrasonication mode is also an important factor influencing the CLD. The double-frequency ultrasound treatment resulted in a higher proportion of A chain and lower B3 chain compared to the single-frequency ultrasound [50]. Simultaneous treatment of vacuum and ultrasonication reduced the percentages of B2 and B3 chains, whereas the ultrasonication prior to vacuum treatment decreased the proportions of A and B1 chains but increased those of B2 and B3 chains [68]. Compared with the single ultrasound treatment, the combined treatment of ultrasound and hydrothermal decreased the proportion of A chains while increasing the proportion of B1 chains of Agriophyllum squarrosum seed starch [59]. Ultrasonication-assisted glucoamylase treatment significantly reduced the proportion of short AP compared with ultrasonication alone due to the enzymatic hydrolysis of the outer short chains by glucoamylase [64].

1H NMR can be used to determine the BD in starch by characterizing the α‑1,4 (around 5.11 ppm) and α‑1,6 (around 4.75 ppm) glycosidic linkages [65], [69]. The BD of starch decreased after ultrasonic treatment [65], [70], [71], and it decreased further with increasing ultrasonic power [50], which is attributed to shear forces of ultrasonication causing the cleavage of AP side chains [67].

Ultrasonication also affects the Mw of starch and many studies have found that Mw decreased after treatment, indicating that ultrasonication can depolymerize of macromolecules into smaller molecules [45], [47], [59], [65], [72], [73]. And the Mw decreased as increasing ultrasonic time [64], [67] and power [64], [65], [72]. This may be because low‑energy ultrasound generated by short times and low power primarily disrupts noncovalent bonds to dissociate starch polymer clusters, whereas high‑energy ultrasound generated at long times and high power breaks covalent glycosidic bonds of starch to cleave the chains [72]. Studies showed that the Mw of AP decreased but which of AM increased due to the degradation of AP and the generation of AM after ultrasonic treatment [74], [75]. The decrease in Mw was more pronounced when ultrasonication was combined with other techniques, such as glucoamylase and hydrothermal treatment, which can be attributed to that the loosen structure after ultrasonication facilitating the enzymatic action of glucoamylase [73], and hydrothermal treatment can intensify the damage to the linear AP in the amorphous region caused by ultrasonic pre-treatment [59].

Effects of ultrasound on short-range molecular order of starch

Short-range molecular order of starch is an important indicator describing the starch chain arrangement and can be characterized by the FTIR, the Laser confocal microRaman spectroscopy (LCM-Raman) and the high-resolution solid-state 13C cross-polarization/magic angle spinning (CP/MAS) NMR spectra [76]. The lower the full width at half maximum of the band at 480 cm-1 (FWHM480) of Raman and the higher R1047/1022 mentioned above indicates the better the short-range order of starch [66], [77]. Other characteristic absorption signals of starch were also exhibited in FTIR spectra. For example, a broad band at 3700–3200 cm-1 represents the stretching vibration of –OH groups of AM and AP [69]. Most studies showed the typical FTIR absorption peaks of starch have neither disappeared nor new peaks have appeared after ultrasonic treatment, indicating that no new chemical bonds or functional groups were formed or lost [8], [36], [56], [58], [69], [72], [76], [78], [79], [80], [81], [82], [83]. However, the decreasing [60], [84], [85], [86], [87], [88] or increasing [36], [55], [86] band intensity representing –OH vibrations at 3400–3600 cm-1 were found after ultrasonication, suggesting that ultrasonication has changed the hydration capacity of starch. Similarly, studies have found that the intensities of Raman peaks representing different functional groups decreased after ultrasonic treatment [89].

The relative proportions of double helices, single helices and amorphous regions of starch can also be calculated based on the NMR spectrum. It has been reported that the proportion of double helices decreased but the proportions of single helices and amorphous regions increased after ultrasonic treatment [65], and this tendency became more pronounced with increasing ultrasonic power and time [65], [90]. This because the shear forces, thermal and mechanical effects, and the generation of free radicals during ultrasonic treatment disrupt the crystalline regions of starch to unwind the double helices into short single helices or amorphous structure [65].

Most studies have reported that the ultrasonication reduced the short-range molecular order of starch due to the looseness of helical structure or unwinding of starch chains [45], [46], [59], [60], [85], [91], [92], [93], [94], [95], [96], [97]. The influence of ultrasonication on the short-range molecular order of starch varied with treatment conditions, and the short-range order of starch decreased with increasing ultrasonic duration [46], [48], [55], [78], [90], [98] and power [78]. In contrast, the increasing short-range order of starch after ultrasonication can be attributed to several causes: (1) the unwound double helices may recrystallize into new ordered structures [71]; (2) ultrasound may preferentially disrupt amorphous regions rather than the more stable crystalline domains [99]; (3) the enhanced hydrogen bond interactions induced by ultrasound may stabilize the double helical structure [77].

The combined treatment of ultrasonication with other modification methods can further regulate the short-range order of starch. For instance, the ultrasonication and freezing treatment can disrupt internal chemical bonds of starch and markedly decrease the peak intensities in FTIR spectrum [82]. Combined treatments of ultrasonication with ozone [41], ball milling [100], dual-enzyme (α-amylase and cellulose) [61], heat moisture [48] tartaric acid [101] and microwave irradiation [102] can reduce the short-range molecular order of starch. In contrast, ultrasonication combined with plasma treatment [67], enzymatic hydrolysis [56], konjac glucomannan [103], pH–shift [75], freeze–thaw cycling [76] and soaking [83] can enhance the short-range ordered structure of starch, which could be due to the formation of relatively ordered structure after the synergistic action of ultrasound with these techniques [56], [83].

Effects of ultrasound on long-range order of starch

As described above, the long-range crystalline structure of starch is commonly characterized using the XRD and the RC of the starch can be calculated. The RC is related to the content and packing form of AM and AP [82]. In general, the crystalline type of starch remained unchanged but the RC was affected after ultrasonic treatment [91], [93], [104], [105], [106], [107], [108]. The RC increased or decreased depending on treatment conditions, botanical origin and the stacking of crystalline and amorphous regions within granules [8], [45], [109]. Ultrasonication can disrupt the crystalline structure of starch, with a decrease in RC and the characteristic peaks intensity of starch crystalline [60], [66], [71], [72], [102], [109], [110], [111], [112], [113], which was due to the disruption of lamellar structure and binding forces between double helices unwinding double helices and loosening the lattice of crystalline regions [46], [65], [85]. The RC of starch decreased with increasing ultrasonic power [61], [66], [78], [79], [98], [114], treatment duration [55], [61], [78], [90], [95], [107], ultrasonic frequency [54], [55] and probe diameter [115]. It has been reported that the RC of oat starch remained unchanged after treatment at 39 W for 10 or 20 min, but decreased when treated at 48 W for 20 min or at 63 W for 10 and 20 min [108]. When the ultrasonic treatment lasted for a longer time, such as 90 min, the starch crystalline structure was almost completely destroyed, and the reduction of RC was greater for waxy corn starch with higher AP content than for normal corn starch [89]. In contrast, the disruption of crystalline structure in wheat starch with higher AM content was more pronounced than cassava starch, which is attributed to that amorphous regions are more susceptible to ultrasonic disruption during shorter treatment times (within 15 min) [115]. Ultrasonication can also increase the RC of starch [36], [42], [80], [94], [111], [116], [117], [118], [119], [120], which may be attributed to the formation of submicrocrystal, the rearrangement of the original crystalline structure into new crystalline domains under high ultrasonic power and cavitation effects [121] and the preferential destruction of amorphous regions [80], [94], [117]. The RC of Indian wheat starch increased after ultrasonication for both 15 and 30 min, with a more pronounced increase after 15 min, which was explained as being due to the preferential disruption of amorphous regions in starch during short duration treatment [43]. Some studies have also observed that the RC first decreased and then increased with the increasing the ultrasonic treatment time and power. The RC of retrograded starch (RS3) decreased from 32.4 % to 12.49 as the ultrasonic power increases from 100 W to 300 due to the disruption of AP double helices. The RC of RS3 increased to 28.28 % when the ultrasonic power was further increased to 600 W, which is attributed to the formation of more V-type crystalline structures under higher ultrasonic power [51]. The crystalline regions of the quinoa starch were degraded and the RC decreased after 2 h of ultrasonication, whereas extending the treatment time to 4 h led to an increase in RC due to the annealing effect [53].

The small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) can also be used to characterize the lamellar structure of starch based on the different electron cloud density of crystalline and amorphous lamellae [68], [89], and several structural parameters can be obtained to analyze the lamellar organization of starch by fitting the data of electron density distribution variations. The characteristic scattering peak at approximately q ≈ 0.6 nm-1 exhibited a shift accompanied by reductions in peak area and intensity after ultrasound [68], [90]. The peak intensity and area, and the dC value (representing crystalline lamellar thickness) and the dL value (representing semicrystalline lamellar thickness) decreased with increasing ultrasonication time, indicating the lamellar order being decreased and the starch structure being loosed [90]. The Bragg parameter (d = 2π/q) represents the thickness of the semicrystalline layers in starch and a larger d value indicates a greater proportion of amorphous regions. Studies have shown that the d value increased after ultrasonication [71], [77]. Dm represents the mass fractal dimension of starch and studies have found that the Dm value decreased after ultrasonication, indicating that the starch structure became progressively looser. This phenomenon was attributed to the disruption of the semicrystalline growth rings by ultrasound [77]. The fractal dimension (α) can be used to describe the surface and mass fractal structure of starch. The α value of potato starch decreased from 3.0 to 2.77 after ultrasonication, indicating that the starch has transformed into a mass fractal model with reduced structure density [71].

The combination of ultrasonication with other technologies may alter the crystalline type of starch. For example, the crystalline type of pea starch changed from C-type to A-type after the dual treatment of ultrasound and heat-moisture process [45]. Similarly, A-type oat starch became B + C type after combined ultrasonic and enzymatic treatment [56]. The greater damage to the crystalline regions was found after the combination of ultrasonication with other techniques, such as heat-moisture process [48], [49], [122], acid hydrolysis [80], [101], [121], octenyl succinic anhydride [123], plasma treatment [67], freeze–thaw cycling [76], ball milling [100], calcium chloride treatment [124], enzyme (α- amylase, glucoamylase and proteolytic enzyme) [125], [126], [127], pH-shift [75] and high pressure homogenization [128], which could be due to the synergistic destruction of the starch crystal structure by these treatments. Differently, ultrasound-assisted pullulanase treatment provided with a more stable crystalline structure by promoting the rearrangement of AM into tightly packed crystals [57], [129]. Comparing with ultrasound treatment, the dual treatment of ultrasound and microwave can increase the RC of pinto bean and maize starch, which was explained as due to the formation of AM-lipid complexes or the preferential destruction of the amorphous regions after microwave treatment [130], [131]. In addition, the starch treated by the combination of ultrasound with konjac glucomannan [103] and annealing treatment [119] presented higher RC than the native and ultrasound treatment starch, which is attributed to konjac glucomannan and annealing treatment promoting the rearrangement of double helix structures.

Effect of ultrasonic treatment on the granule morphology of starch

Ultrasonic treatment can significantly affect the granule morphology of starch. The different degrees of holes and cracks on the surface of starch granules appeared and the particle size changed after ultrasonic treatment, which was mainly related to the starch type, particle size, ultrasonic power, processing duration, and whether ultrasound was combined with other technologies.

Unlike untreated starch, which typically exhibited a compact and smooth spherical or polygonal appearance [8], [69], [72], [98], [125], starch granule subjected to ultrasonic treatment displayed rough surfaces with cracks of different sizes, circular holes of varying depths, and pitted or eroded features [42], [54], [85], [88], [92], [95], [98], [109], [110], [132], [133], [134], [135]. Larger cracks can lead to a loose starch structure [136] or the peeling of the outer growth ring [65], [66], [102]. The primary mechanism behind these changes involves the rapid expansion, contraction and breakage of bubbles within the liquid due to the pressure fluctuations and cavitation when the high energy vibrations of ultrasound passing through the liquid medium. The high pressure and shear forces caused by shock wave, microjet and turbulent flow in this process can damage the granule morphology and integrity [68]. But in some cases, the surface damage of starch granules after ultrasonic treatment was not obvious or difficult to observe, which can primarily be attributed to the protection of the starch granules by other structures or substances. For example, no surface cracks or pores were observed in rice starch after ultrasonic treatment due to the protection of proteins and lipids in rice flour [109]. Similarly, the starch wrapped in the endosperm of corn kernels can also be protected from the cavitation effect due to the protection of the endosperm with the compact structure [137].

The frequency, power and duration of ultrasonic treatment are critical factors affecting the surface morphology of starch granule. As the ultrasonic power, frequency and time increased, the surface damage degree increased, such as the increasing number of depressions, cracks and pores on starch granules surface [36], [46], [48], [54], [65], [77], [79], [84], [86], [90], [98], [106], [107], [114], [117], [133], [138], [139]. Fig. 2 shows a typical example of the increasing damage degree of sorghum starch with the increasing ultrasonic time and power. Additionally, the AC of starch plays a significant role in determining the extent of surface damage. The number of pores and porosity increased and the pore size decreased with increasing the AC [140]. For instance, wheat starch, which has a higher AC, exhibited greater sensitivity to the ultrasonic treatment compared to cassava starch under the same treatment conditions [115]. The effect of ultrasound on the morphology of starch granule is influenced by particle size. Smaller particles tended to escape the ultrasound action more readily compared to larger granules [43], [53], [98], [107]. The size of starch granules generally reduced due to the destructive effect of shear force produced by ultrasonic treatment [80], [96], [108], [109], [114], [116], [141], and the broken micrometer starch particles and smaller nano starch particles could be observed [112]. Extended ultrasonic time [111] and elevated temperature [62] facilitated the formation of starch particles with smaller size. However, the starch particle size will not be significantly affected if the duration of ultrasonic treatment is insufficient or the energy level is too low. For instance, no significant reduction in particle size of corn and cassava starch was observed after treating at 40 kHz for 10–20 min [85]. It has also been observed that granules with larger than average size appeared after ultrasonic treatment [53], [71], [79], [106], which is attributed to the reaggregation of starch granules promoted by ultrasound [51], [134]. Additionally, it has been noted that the granule size of waxy corn starch initially decreased and subsequently increased during ultrasonic treatment [117], because ultrasonic treatment firstly disrupt starch particles into smaller particles through shear forces, and subsequently, smaller particles can recombine into new aggregates or stick to the surface of the large particles to promote the agglomeration of particles to form larger particles due to the enhanced the surface activity of damaged starch by the continuous increase of ultrasonic energy [142]. The distribution of starch granule sizes became broader after ultrasonic treatment [56], [71], [87], with more pronounced as the treatment time increased [107]. Inversely, a significant decrease in granule sizes distribution with increasing ultrasound time and frequency was explained as being due to the formation of finer starch particles after the ultrasonic treatment [55].

Numerous studies have demonstrated that the effects of the combined treatment of ultrasound with other technologies on granule morphology of starch are more pronounced compared to single treatments. For instance, the damage of starch surface was aggravated by the combined treatment of ultrasonic and microwave [131]. The starch surface exhibited greater damage such as the appearance of starch rupture when lotus root starch was subjected to combined treatments of the heat-moisture and ultrasound, with the sequence of heat-moisture treatment followed by ultrasound showing significant effect [8]. Moreover, synergistic effects on starch granule morphology were also observed when ultrasound was combined with electric field treatments [42], vacuum treatments [68], enzymatic or/and oxidative treatment [127] and freeze–thaw cycles [143]. Additionally, ultrasound-assisted acid hydrolysis [121] and heat-moisture treatment [48] resulted in smaller starch particle sizes compared to the corresponding single processing method.

Effects of ultrasound on physicochemical properties of starch

Hydration properties

The hydration properties of starch include the water absorption capacity (WAC), solubility (S) and swelling power (SP). In most cases, the ultrasonic treatment can increase the WAC of starch [36], [93], [111], [141], which has been attributed to several causes: (1) the depressions, fissures, and pores on the granule surface after ultrasonic treatment as described in Section 4.1.4. facilitating more water to enter the interior of starch granules [95], [114]. In addition, the decreasing granule size and increasing the specific surface area can also enhance the WAC of starch [95]; (2) the exposure of hydrophilic groups such as free hydroxyls [44]; (3) the starch was degraded into more hydrophilic molecules such as dextrins, maltose and glucose [102]; (4) the breakage of C-C bonds between AM and AP chains resulting in a more compact structure to increase the affinity for water [95]. The WAC of starch generally increased with increasing ultrasonic power [36], [44], [114] and time [44], [95], [106], [114]. Combined ultrasonic and citric acid treatment significantly increased the WAC of elephant foot yam starch compared with ultrasonication alone, explained as being due to the disruption of starch structure and exposure of hydrogen bonds after the citric acid treatment [120].

The SP reflects the ability of starch granules to absorb water and expand, which is closely related to the interactions between starch molecular chains and dependent on the hydration degree of the amorphous region. Ultrasound treatment significantly affected the swelling properties of starch granule by disrupting the crystalline and amorphous regions of the starch [52], [144]. The S of starch reflects the release capacity of free AM during the swelling of starch granules [59]. The SP and S of starch are influenced by factors such as temperature and starch type [115], [145], [146], and the S and SP increased with increasing the test temperature [45], [56], [67], [108], [147], which was attributed to the weakened hydrogen bonds of the crystalline structure caused by starch gelatinization at higher temperatures facilitated water penetration [45], [108]. Compared with wheat, barley and corn starches, native rice starch exhibited the highest SP value, which may be due to the relatively lower content of phosphate groups on the AP chains of the former three starches [148]. The SP and S values of wheat starch were lower than those of cassava starch, which can be attributed to the higher AM and lipid contents in wheat starch [115].

The effects of ultrasonic treatment on the SP and S of starch are similar and most studies have shown that ultrasonic treatment can increase both SP and S of starch [46], [53], [75], [91], [92], [100], [103], [108], [109], [110], [111], [115], [130], [148], [149]. Furthermore, these parameters increased with increasing ultrasonic power [36], [114], treatment duration [46], [55], [78], [95], [109], and ultrasonic frequency [145]. The increases in SP and S is caused by the combined effects of physical damage to starch granules (Section 2.1.4), disruption of the crystalline structure (Section 2.1.3), as well as the dissociation of short-range ordered structures (Section 2.1.2) and ordered molecular structures (Section 2.1.1), which allow the combine of water and free hydroxyl groups of starch chains by hydrogen bonds to enhance the ability of water to penetrate both amorphous and crystalline regions of starch granules [46], [109], [148]. The removal of the protein layer on the surface of starch granules facilitated the release of AM, which might be the reason for the increase in SP [43]. However, some studies have found that the SP of starch decreased after ultrasonic treatment [51], [59], [101], [145], [147], which may be attributed to the rearrangement of disrupted starch chains into more ordered structures during ultrasonication can inhibit the swelling of starch granules [51].

Compared with starch subjected to ultrasonication alone, sequential treatment with an electric field followed by ultrasonication increased both the SP and S of starch due to the increased exposure of AM induced by the dual treatment [42]. The combine treatment of ultrasonication and alkaline conditions (pH > 11) significantly increased SP than ultrasonication alone, which was attributed to the repulsive forces generated by the ionization of hydroxyl groups causing further disruption of the granule structure and thereby water penetration [110]. Ultrasonication combined with other treatments such as ozone treatment [105], pullulanase hydrolysis [56],α-amylase and cellulose treatment [61] can also improve the S of starch. Inversely the combined ultrasonic-microwave treatment caused more severe disruption of starch structure at high temperatures than the single modification, which limited the swelling of starch granule and decreased the SP [57], [102]. Comparing with the enzyme treatment alone, the S increased and the SP decreased when ultrasound combined compound enzyme (α-amylase and glucoamylase) or dextranase treatments [93], [125].

Thermodynamic properties

The thermodynamic properties of starch are typically determined by the differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). Thermodynamic parameters including onset temperature (To), peak temperature (Tp), conclusion temperature (Tc), temperature range (ΔT, Tc- To) and enthalpy change (ΔH) can be obtained by analyzing the thermodynamic thermograms. Specifically, To represents the gelatinization of relatively disordered structures in starch [108]. Tp is the temperature at which most of the crystal structures in the starch have been melted [146]. Tc represents complete melting of the starch crystal structure [96]. ΔH reflects the loss of ordered structure during starch gelatinization [9], [42]. ΔT reflects the homogeneity of the crystalline structure, a smaller ΔT value indicates a more uniform starch crystal structure [56], [72], [96].

Most studies showed that the To, Tp, and Tc of starch increased [89], [106], [108], [110], [114], [141] and ΔT decreased [72], [73], [91], [109] after ultrasonic treatment. The increase in gelatinization temperature was attributed to the disruption of weakly ordered structures of starch and the remaining more ordered structures requiring higher temperatures to be disrupted [110]. The decrease in ΔT is due to the remaining more ordered crystal structures having relatively uniform stability [91], [109]. Conversely, the decreasing To, Tp, and Tc of starch after ultrasonic treatment has also been found [53], [71], [102], [122], which was likely to be due to the granule damage facilitating water penetration into the starch granules and reducing the structural order of starch [71], [122]. These different results may be related to factors such as the starch type and ultrasonic treatment time. Ultrasound also affects the ΔH of starch and some studies found that ultrasonic treatment decreased ΔH, which was attributed to the disruption of the double-helical structure of starch [72], [97], [102], [107], [138]. Other studies showed that ultrasonic treatment increased ΔH due to the mere disruption of the amorphous regions in the starch structure [99], [145]. Another explanations for the increasing ΔH as being due to the fact that the protective layer formed by the leakage of AM from within the starch and its gelation on the surface of the starch granules can prevent the contact between the starch interior and water and thus the starch gelatinization [67], [109]. It has been seen that the effect of ultrasound on the ΔH of starch mainly depended on whether it affects the crystalline region of the starch structure. The ΔH tended to increase when ultrasound acted solely on amorphous regions, however, ΔH typically decreased once crystalline domains were disrupted [88], [99].

The ultrasonic treatment time and power can affect the ΔH and gelatinization temperature of starch [66], [67], [77], [83], [85], [99], [102], [108], [114], [150], but no universal conclusion has been obtained from current relevant studies (Table 3), which may be related to the different ultrasonic power, treatment time and starch type. For example, the gelatinization temperature increased and the ΔH of arrowhead starch decreased with increasing the treatment time from 15 min to 30 min when processing at a power of 300 W. However, the opposite results occurred when the power was 600 and 900 W [114]. The ultrasonic treatment temperature also can affect the thermal properties of starch and it is usually interfered by the annealing effect [145]. For instance, the Tc of tef flour decreased as the ultrasonic treatment temperature increased, which was attributed to the increased starch chain mobility by annealing at higher temperatures further enhancing the disruption of ordered double helices and crystal structures by cavitation effect. However, ΔH first decreased and then increased with increasing ultrasonic treatment temperature, the increase in ΔH at higher temperatures was due to the annealing promoting the rearrangement of starch helical structures [96]. The ultrasonic probe configuration is another factor that can influence the thermal properties of starch. Under the same conditions, increasing the probe diameter (from 20 mm to 100 mm) caused a significant increase in To, Tp, Tc and a decrease in ΔH of cassava starch [115], which was attributed to the stronger mechanical action of the 100 mm probe that was more conducive to the entry of water molecules into the interior of the granule. Thermal property changes of starch induced by ultrasonication.

In recent years, the ultrasound combined with other physical, chemical and enzymatic treatments for starch modification has become an important research direction. The sequence of different physical treatments can influence the gelatinization temperature and ΔH of starch [74], [131]. For example, ultrasound followed by microwave treatment decreased ΔH of native potato starch, whereas the ΔH value increased after the reversing treatment order. This result can be attributed to a protective layer formed by the microwave treatment increasing ΔH, but the more disordered structure after ultrasound treatment decreasing ΔH [131]. The application of the vacuum treatment prior to ultrasonication yielded lower Tc and ΔH than the opposite sequence due to the vacuum environment effectively reducing the loss of ultrasonic energy [68]. Ultrasound combined with freeze–thaw treatment increased the disruption of crystalline and double helical structures in starch, leading to a further decrease in ΔH [76], [143]. When ultrasound was combined with the heat-moisture treatment and annealing, the gelatinization temperature and ΔH value increased due to the increasing crystalline stability after rearranging the molecular chains of starch [58], [59], [70], [82], [119], [122]. When the ultrasound combined with chemical treatments, compared to citric acid treatment alone, dual treatment of ultrasound and citric acid further decreased the To, Tp and Tc of starch [120], which was likely to be due to the prolonged chain length of AP by ultrasonic treatment. The ultrasound and CaCl2 treatment can synergistically disrupt starch granules to reduce starch thermal stability and ΔH [124]. When combined with cellulase or pullulanase treatment, the size of starch granules decreased and became more uniform, and new formed double helical structures making the crystal structure denser, which were all the reasons for the increase in the thermal stability and ΔH of starch [56], [146]. While the decreasing ΔH after the combined treatment of ultrasound and dual-enzyme (α-amylase and glucoamylase or cellulose) were due to the disruption of the ordered structure [61], [125].

Pasting properties

The pasting properties of starch can be characterized by the rapid visco analyzer (RVA), and parameters including pasting temperature (PT), peak viscosity (PV), trough viscosity (TV), final viscosity (FV), breakdown viscosity (BV), and setback viscosity (SV) can be quantified by analyzing the pasting curve of starch. A higher PT indicates a greater resistance to swelling and gelatinization due to the more compact granule structure [65], [113]. PV reflects the maximum swelling capacity of starch and a higher PV corresponds to greater granule swelling and WAC [45], [68]. TV reflects the shear resistance of starch, with a higher TV indicating greater shear stability [45]. FV reflects the gel strength and stability after cooling, a higher FV suggests a greater tendency for starch retrogradation [119], [143]. BV, calculated as PV minus TV, which is an important indicator of the hot paste stability of starch. A larger BV indicates that the starch paste is more prone to breakdown under high temperature and shear [91], [119]. SV (FV minus TV) reflects the rearrangement of AM and the ability of form a gel network during cooling. A higher SV indicates the faster starch retrogradation [67], [109].

Most studies showed that the PT, PV, TV, FV, BV and SV decreased after ultrasonic treatment [72], [88], [101], [102], [106], [128], [151]. The decrease in PT, PV and TV was primarily attributed to the disruption of the starch surface structure by ultrasound facilitating water penetration and the degradation of AP, which make starch granules more susceptible to gelatinization and reduce paste viscosity [46], [66], [72], [95]. However, the increasing PT, PV and TV after ultrasonication have also been observed [42], [45], [55], [67], [81], [86], [97], [103], [119], which was likely to be due to the preferential disruption of less ordered structures, while the remaining more stable residual crystallites enhancing resistance to swelling and increasing paste viscosity. The decrease in BV was mainly due to the degradation and rearrangement of starch chains resulting in a more homogeneous and stable structure to improve hot paste stability [113], [145]. Conversely, the increasing BV possibly resulting from the increasing breakdown under high temperature and shear due to the extensive disruption of the crystalline structure [8], [45], [66], [77]. The reduction in FV and SV was ascribed to the degradation of AM and structural changes of AP with long chain, which decreasing leaching and Mw of AM and thereby inhibiting retrogradation [65], [72], [88], [91], [102], [128]. Nevertheless, the increases in FV and SV potentially was due to the depolymerization of long-chain AP increasing the AC, which promoting rearrangement of starch during cooling and thus retrogradation [45], [46], [77]. It was hypothesized that these different results may be due to differences in the starch source and concentration, as well as variations in the ultrasonic treatment time and frequency [83], [97], [98], [109], [150], [151].

The ultrasonic treatment time and power are two major factors that affect the pasting properties of starch [46], [66], [88], [98], [109], [147], [150], [152]. The starch paste viscosity increased after the short-duration ultrasonic treatment, whereas prolonged treatment caused the starch paste viscosity to decrease [53], [78], [81], [88], [98], [111], [147], [150]. This result was likely to be due to the disruption of the surface and less ordered structures after the short-duration ultrasonic treatment, and the remaining stable crystalline structure can increase paste viscosity. With extended treatment time, the further disruption of the crystalline structure facilitating starch gelatinization and reducing paste viscosity. Similarly, the increasing starch paste viscosity after low-power processing was due to that ultrasonic treatment promotes the exudation of AM and the degradation of long AP, resulting in an increase in the AC and facilitating the rearrangement of starch molecules. While the intense mechanical and cavitation effects were generated under the condition of high-power ultrasound, which severely damaged both AM and AP, reduced its Mw, inhibited the rearrangement of starch molecules, and consequently caused a decrease in paste viscosity [66], [77], [81], [98], [152]. Furthermore, the ultrasonic probe size and treatment temperature also can affect starch paste viscosity [115], [145]. As the probe diameter increased from 20 mm to 100 mm, the PT, FV and SV decreased but PV and BV increased for cassava starch, and the PT and BV decreased but PV, FV and SV increased for wheat starch, which was attributed to the fact that higher ultrasonic intensity generated by the larger probe can effectively disrupt the crystalline structure and promote AP degradation [115]. The decreased PV and BV of potato and wheat starch with increasing the ultrasonic temperature from 25 to 60 °C was explained as being due to the swelling of starch granule at higher temperature making the internal structure more susceptible to ultrasonic disruption [145].

When ultrasound is combined with other techniques such as microwave [74], [102], [130], plasma [67], [153], vacuum [68], heat-moisture treatment [70], [98], [122], acid [120], [151], ozone [105] and enzymatic treatments [146], it generally led to a further reduction in starch paste viscosity due to collaboratively disruption of the multi-scale structure. Conversely, the combination of ultrasound with electric field [42] and annealing treatment [91], [119], [122] have been reported to inhibit the decrease in paste viscosity, which can be attributed to the fact that the electric field inhibits the damage to the starch structure caused by ultrasound and the annealing promotes the rearrangement of starch molecules. Therefore, different technologies can be selected to combine with ultrasonic treatment to regulate the starch paste viscosity based on different requirements. However, it also indicated that the current study on the combination of ultrasound with other technologies is limited. In the future, the research scope should be further expanded in order to provide more comprehensive and precise theoretical references for starch applications.

Rheological properties

The rheological properties of starch are often examined using a rheometer. The viscosity of starch pastes can also be obtained by analyzing the steady-state rheological behavior, which typically decreased with increasing shear rate, indicating a shear-thinning pattern. And starch still exhibited the shear-thinning behavior after ultrasonic treatment [81], [107], [113], [114], [124], [154]. In addition, the relationship between paste viscosity and shear rate can be fitted using the power-law model (τ = K·γ̇n), where τ represents shear stress and γ̇ denotes shear rate. The flow behavior index (n) reflects the shear-thinning intensity of starch, with lower n values indicating more pronounced shear thinning. A higher n value suggests that the starch granule was disrupted by ultrasonication. The consistency coefficient (K) increased as intermolecular interactions and chain entanglements become stronger, corresponding to a higher apparent viscosity of the starch paste [155]. The viscoelastic behavior of starch can also be evaluated through oscillatory rheological measurements, which provide the parameters including the storage modulus (G’), loss modulus (G’’) and loss tangent (tan δ = G’’/G’). G’ reflects the elastic behavior of starch, with higher values indicating a more stable structural network. G’’ represents the viscous component, and higher G’’ value corresponds to greater viscous behavior. Both tan δ and n value less than 1 indicated that starch pastes exhibited the characteristics of a pseudoplastic fluid dominated by elastic rather than viscous responses [148].

Ultrasonication can affect the rheological properties of starch including viscosity [71], [77], [81], [100], [107], [109], [114], [126], [130], [146], G’, G’’ and tan δ [54], [71], [77], [81], [86], [127], [146], [151], as well as n and K value [54], [77], [81]. However, no consistent results and conclusions were reached likely due to differences of starch sources and ultrasonic treatment conditions. For example, the G’ and G’’ value of lotus seed starch increased with increasing ultrasonic power from 90 W to 270 W, but it decreased when the power exceeded 360 W due to the disruption of hydrogen bond by the intense cavitation. The n value and K values decreased and increased respectively with increasing ultrasonic power from 90 W to 450 W [77]. As the ultrasonic amplitude increased from 30 % to 100 %, the G’ value of sorghum starch firstly increased and then decreased, whereas G’’ tan δ and firstly decreased and then increased. A higher n value was exhibited for starch after ultrasonic treatment. The K value increased under relatively mild ultrasonication conditions (e.g., 10 % amplitude for 30 min or 20 % amplitude for 60 min), indicating stronger intermolecular interaction within the starch [81]. Effect of ultrasonic treatment on the rheological properties of starch.

To further improve the rheological properties of starch, the combined treatment of ultrasonication with other modification techniques were studied. Such combined treatments often exhibit either synergistic or antagonistic effects depending on the modification method. The decreasing viscosity and increasing elasticity of starch paste were observed when ultrasonication combined with certain chemical or physical modifications. For instance, compared with ultrasonic treatment alone, the combination of ultrasonication and acid hydrolysis significantly decreased starch viscosity, which was mainly attributed to the fact that ultrasonic treatment promoted the degradation of glycosidic bonds by acid [139]. In addition, the greater increases in G’ and G’’ and a reduction in tan δ under the condition of acetylation followed by ultrasonication was due to the removal of acetyl groups from starch chains during ultrasonication [151]. Moreover, the increasing G’ and decreasing G’’ and tan δ after the combination of ultrasonication with annealing or heat-moisture treatment indicating the formation of a stronger elastic network due to the stabilization of starch structure induced by the dual treatments [8], [91]. However, the combination of ultrasonication and other modification techniques also can increase viscosity and decrease elasticity [71], [124], [146]. For example, synergistic treatment of ultrasonication and enzymatic modification resulted in the decrease of G’ and G’’ and the increase of tan δ in rice flour, which may be related to starch chain breakage and structure loosening caused by enzymatic degradation [146].

Digestibility

Based on the digestion rate and extent of starch in the small intestine, starch can be classified into three categories: rapidly digestible starch (RDS), which is digested within 20 min; slowly digestible starch (SDS), which is digested between 20 and 120 min; and resistant starch (RS), which is digested after 120 min [156]. The equilibrium concentration (C∞) and the digestion rate constant (k) can be obtained by fitting the hydrolysis curve of starch during digestion. A higher C∞ indicates a greater final extent of starch hydrolysis, whereas a larger k value reflects a faster digestion rate [71]. In general, starches with more intact granule surfaces, higher AC, and a higher degree of structural order exhibit stronger resistance to enzymatic hydrolysis [56].

Studies have shown that the ultrasonic treatment can increase the starch digestibility, as evidenced by an increase in RDS, k value and digestion extent [44], [70], [71], [88], [95], [147], [149] and a decrease in RS [70], [71], [103], [130], [157], which was attributed to the disruption of granule surfaces and crystalline regions facilitating enzyme penetration into the interior of the starch granules and promoting starch hydrolysis [71], [95]. The increasing enzyme activity after the appropriate ultrasonication was also an important factor that increased starch digestibility [57]. In contrast, other studies have reported that ultrasonication decreased the starch digestibility, leading to higher contents of SDS and RS while reducing the proportion of RDS [57], [75], [79], [83], [98], [144], [146], [151], which was explained as being due to the reassembly of disrupted starch chains into ordered helical structure [83], [98], [151]. The discrepancies among studies may be due to differences of starch sources and reaction temperature. For example, starch with higher AC was more prone to forming resistant starch after ultrasonic treatment [158].

Starch digestibility is affected by ultrasonic time and studies have shown that the digestibility of quinoa flour, sorghum starch and millet starch increased with increasing treatment time [95], [147], which is attributable to the increasing cumulative energy of ultrasound acting on starch causing more double helices to be disrupted and an increase in short-chain starch [95]. In another study, the digestibility of corn starch increased after ultrasonic treatment for 5 min due to the disruption of starch structure. However, when the treatment time was extended to 30 min, the formation of the ordered structure by the rearrangement of starch chains led to the decreasing of starch digestibility [157]. The RS content of indica rice starch increased from 20.07 (100/g) to 23.23 (100/g) with increasing the treatment time from 15 to 60 min at an ultrasonic amplitude of 30 %. However, when the amplitude was excessively high (60–100 %), the RS content showed an initial increase followed by a decrease with increasing ultrasonic time [83]. These results indicated that the effect of ultrasonic time on starch digestibility was strongly influenced by ultrasonic power. Generally, moderate increases in ultrasonic intensity and power can enhance digestibility, whereas the digestibility decreased after the ultrasonic treatment with the excessive intensity due to the abnormal expansion of cavitation bubbles weakening the cavitation effect [51], [54], [73]. The slightly decreasing RS content of the retrograded starch was observed when the power increased from 100 W to 200, but the rearrangement of starch chains resulted in a highly ordered structure when the ultrasonic power was further increased to 600 W, which increased the formation of RS [51]. Other studies have found that the RS content of potato and corn starch firstly increased and then decreased, while C∞ and k values firstly decreased and then increased as ultrasonic power increased, explained as being due to the moderate ultrasonication favoring the rearrangement of starch chain but excessive power leading to severe structure destruction [157]. The different results may be related to the difference of ultrasonic time and starch source. The ultrasonic treatment temperature is another factor affecting the starch digestibility. The ultrasonic treatment at relatively low temperatures (around 55 °C) promoted enzymatic hydrolysis by increasing mass transfer, but the starch digestibility decreased at higher temperatures (>70 °C) due to the reduction of enzyme thermal stability and the inhibition of cavitation effect [73].

The combination of ultrasound with other methods can significantly decrease the starch digestibility and prepare RS [159], [160]. The combination of ultrasound and pullulanase treatment produced starch with a rougher surface morphology, higher crystallinity, more ordered molecular structure and larger granule size, leading to stronger resistance to enzymatic hydrolysis than enzyme treatment alone [56]. The combined treatments of the ultrasonication with the chemical crosslinking of starch with phosphates at alkaline conditions (pH ≥ 10.5) enhanced RS content more effectively than conventional crosslinking, which can be attributed to the fact that the penetration of crosslinking agents with ultrasound to introduce new covalent bonds into AM and AP [110]. In addition, compared with ultrasonication alone, the combined treatments of ultrasound with enzymes [146], microwave [130], and microwave combined enzyme processing [57], flavonoids [71] and hydrothermal treatment [45], [59] can also enhance starch resistance to digestion. Overall, the combination of ultrasound with other modification techniques provides a promising strategy for starch with improved resistance to digestion. Future research should prioritize the optimization of treatment parameters and sequences, as well as a deeper elucidation of the interaction mechanisms among different modification methods to more precisely control the starch digestibility.

Innovation and Perspectives

Although significant progress has been achieved in recent years regarding the ultrasonic modification of starch, in-depth comparison among existing studies remains challenging due to the large variability in ultrasonic parameters including equipment type, probe diameter, sample volume, duty cycle and reactor geometry. Therefore, an equipment-independent concept termed Equivalent Cavitation Dose (ECD) was proposed as a conceptual framework for future investigations aimed at facilitating the standardization and comparison of ultrasonic treatment conditions, while its quantitative application in experimental studies remains to be further explored. The ECD can be comprehensively defined as the product of effective acoustic power, duty cycle, treatment duration and a geometric correction factor (probe diameter, sample volume and probe immersion depth), which established a unified description of ultrasonic energy input for different experimental setups. Based on this parameter, ultrasonic dose–response curves for various starch types can be constructed to quantitatively elucidate the relationships between ultrasonic energy input and starch structural disruption. Furthermore, this approach facilitates the exploration of potential starch damage thresholds or saturation regimes under ultrasonic treatment, thereby providing a theoretical basis for improving comparability and parameter transferability in future studies. The calculation formula for ECD is given as follows:ECD=Peff×DC×t×ΦPeff (W): effective acoustic power, defined as the acoustic power that is actually transmitted into the sample system and actively involved in cavitation phenomena, rather than the nominal power specified by the ultrasonic device; DC (−): duty cycle, representing the ratio of the ultrasonic on-time to the total treatment duration; t (s): ultrasonic treatment time; Φ (−): geometric correction factor, characterizing the geometric matching between the ultrasonic probe and the sample system.Φ=f(dp,V,h)dp: the diameter of the ultrasonic probe; V: the sample volume; h: the probe immersion depth.

The key of the ECD lies in the use of the acoustic energy that actually absorbed by the sample. Unlike the nominal power specified by ultrasonic equipment, the effective acoustic power must be determined through experimental calibration.Peff=mcΔTΔtM (g): sample mass; c (J/g·oC): specific heat capacity; ΔTΔt (oC/s): temperature rise rate (slope of the temperature–time curve).

This calibration procedure needs to be performed only once, after which the obtained Peff value can be applied to all subsequent experiments conducted under constant-temperature conditions. Given the sample mass (m), specific heat capacity (c) and the temperature rise rate of the sample during ultrasonic treatment, the acoustic energy actually absorbed by the sample can be estimated based on the principle of energy conservation. This calorimetric approach has been well established and widely applied in the standard determination of ultrasonic power [161]. The nominal ultrasonic power may be used as an approximation when the temperature rise cannot be directly measured, provided that the associated methodological limitations are clearly stated in the discussion. For example, the heat generated by ultrasonic input is rapidly removed by the cooling system under constant-temperature conditions such as a thermostatic water bath, resulting in negligible temperature increase of the sample. Under such circumstances, the Peff cannot be directly determined using a temperature-rise-based calorimetric method. The most reliable solution remains to determine Peff through a one-time calibration by temporarily disabling the temperature control system. This Peff value can be consistently applied to all subsequent experiments conducted under identical equipment and operating conditions. In cases where the starch system exhibits high viscosity, calibration may alternatively be performed using water or a dilute suspension. The resulting Peff can then be employed as an approximate value for subsequent calculations.

This formulation provides a more realistic representation of cavitation intensity by comprehensively accounting for effective acoustic power, treatment duration, geometric characteristics and material properties. Accordingly, it is recommended as a preferred dose description for future studies. It should be noted that the primary purpose of proposing the ECD in this review is not to retrospectively reanalyze all existing studies, but to establish a conceptual and methodological framework for unifying ultrasonic parameters. Owing to the incomplete and heterogeneous reporting of ultrasonic conditions in current literature, systematic application of this formulation remains limited at present. Nevertheless, this limitation itself highlights the necessity of such a unified descriptor. By explicitly defining the essential parameters required to characterize cavitation intensity, the ECD framework provides clear guidance for future experimental design, parameter reporting, and cross-study comparison, thereby facilitating improved reproducibility and transferability in ultrasonic modification research of starch.

Conclusion and future directions

As a green and controlled physical modification technique, ultrasound modification can reconstruct the multi-scale structure of starch including molecular structure, crystalline structure and granule morphology. Appropriate ultrasonic cavitation and shear effects can alter the AM-to-AP ratio and the CLD of starch, disrupt or rearrange the short-range ordered structures and crystalline regions and change granule surface morphology. These structural modifications led to changes in physicochemical properties, manifested as variations in starch hydration and swelling behavior, thermodynamic properties, gelatinization and pasting characteristics, rheological behavior and in vitro digestibility. Such effects provide new technological opportunities for the targeted development of functional starches with the enhanced swelling capacity and increased resistance to enzymatic digestion. In addition, the combined application of ultrasound with physical, enzymatic and chemical modifications further enriched the regulatory methods of starch and starch-based food by producing synergistic or antagonistic effects. To address the critical limitations of substantial heterogeneity in the definition and setting of ultrasonic parameters, energy utilization efficiency and acoustic field distribution, this review innovatively proposed the concept of ECD based on a systematic analysis of recent advances of starch ultrasonic modification and established a corresponding quantitative characterization model with detailed calculation formulas, which mapped complex and variable processing conditions into a comparable indicators. The introduction of ECD and its formula has made it feasible to conduct rigorous cross-study analyses to get quantitative and generalizable conclusions. More importantly, the ECD concept lays a theoretical foundation for establishing quantitative relationships among ultrasonic cavitation dose, starch multi-scale structure and physicochemical properties, and offering practical guidance for the precise design of starch ultrasonic modification. It is necessary to systematically validate the ECD model and its parameter sensitivity in the systems with different starch sources, structure characteristics and ultrasonic conditions in the future. The intrinsic influence mechanism of ultrasonic cavitation on starch structure can be further deeply revealed by combining it with in-situ characterization techniques. Based on this, further exploration should be conducted on the cavitation dose distribution in processes of ultrasound-assisted physical, chemical, and enzymatic modification. The ultrasonic modification of starch will gradually shift from being driven by experience to quantitative design and intelligent optimization as the concept and model of ECD being improved continuously.
