Protocol for the generation of human medullary spinal trigeminal nucleus-like organoids and trigeminothalamic assembloids
Authors: Wei Pang, Jinkui Zhu, Siyuan Chu, Yangfei Xiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryNucleus-specific brain organoids enable precise human brain research, yet refined hindbrain models are rare. Here, we present a guided differentiation protocol for generating human medullary spinal trigeminal nucleus-like organoids (hmSpVOs). We describe steps for constructing spinal trigeminal nucleus (SpV)-thalamus assembloids. Moreover, we outline the assays to characterize functional connections within these assembloids.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Pang et al.1

Before you begin

In recent years, region-specific brain organoids2,3,4,5 derived from human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) have significantly advanced our understanding of the developmental processes and pathological mechanisms in different regions of the human brain. However, current models lack precision in recapitulating brain nucleus-specific architectures, including the hindbrain region. Nucleus-specific brain organoids enable the investigation of the human brain at higher resolution, which will further deepen our understanding of its development and related disorders.

The spinal trigeminal nucleus (SpV), the largest cranial nerve nucleus spanning the brainstem, plays a vital role in relaying nociception and thermal sensations to the thalamus, which are crucial for maintaining normal physiological functions of the body.6 Despite its importance, models for human SpV are currently lacking.

Here, we present a detailed protocol for generating hPSCs derived human medullary SpV-like organoids (hmSpVOs).1Alternatives: Here, we describe the generation of hmSpVO from hESCs (H9 ESCs and H1 ESCs). We have also had success with two hiPSCs (RC01001A and RC01001B).

Alternatives: Here, we describe the generation of hmSpVO from hESCs (H9 ESCs and H1 ESCs). We have also had success with two hiPSCs (RC01001A and RC01001B).

To generate hmSpVO, we employed a previously established static-to-spinning 3D culture strategy.7,8,9,10 Dual SMAD inhibition was applied to induce the neuroectoderm fate. Retinoic acid (RA)11 and Wnt are caudalization signals essential for the development of the hindbrain, while Wnt signaling also induces dorsal patterning.12,13,14 We employed the combined modulation of the Wnt and RA signaling pathways to induce confined differentiation of the dorsal-caudal hindbrain. Long-time Wnt activation will promote neuroepithelial expansion. To facilitate the folding of neuroepithelial tissue, dissolved Matrigel (1% v/v) was supplemented in hmSpVO culture. We also applied sliced organoid culture at an air-liquid interface15 for the long-term development of hmSpVOs. These organoids can recapitulate the lineage specification, structural and functional maturation of the SpV.

Region-specific brain organoids have enabled the modeling of crosstalk between different regions of the nervous system.8,9,16,17,18,19 Next, we constructed a fused human SpV-thalamus organoid (hSTO) model by combining hmSpVOs with thalamic organoids (hThOs) under air-liquid interface condition to recapitulate the trigeminothalamic projections between the human SpV and thalamus.

Innovation

Recent advances in brain organoid technology have enabled the generation of organoids with certain region- or even nucleus-specific features. For example, models that recapitulate the arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus20 or the thalamic reticular nucleus7 have been developed, and nucleus-specific neuronal populations21 can also be generated using two-dimensional (2D) differentiation protocols. However, most existing organoid approaches focus on broader brain regions or employ caudalization signals to induce hindbrain-like identities, without achieving fine subregional or nucleus-level specificity, especially for the human hindbrain.

Existing hindbrain organoid protocols21,22,23,24,25 have enabled the generation of organoids with diverse neuronal populations and improved maturation; however, they typically lack detailed nucleus-specific architectures and functional circuit connectivity. In contrast, the protocol described here enables the reproducible generation of hmSpVOs, which closely resemble the SpV in transcriptional, cellular, and functional properties. In addition, by integrating hmSpVOs with hThOs, our protocol supports reconstruction of trigeminothalamic circuits, providing a novel and advanced platform for investigating neural connectivity, disease mechanisms, and therapeutic strategies relevant to human hindbrain nuclei.

Materials and equipment

Equipment: This protocol requires common tissue culture and electrophysiology supplies and equipment, as listed above.Note: Filter all tissue culture media through a 0.22-μm membrane. Expected outcomes

This protocol aims to generate human medullary SpV-like organoids that recapitulate the development of the SpV derived from the dorsal medulla and can be cultured long term to achieve functional maturation of inhibitory and excitatory neuronal populations. This method is convenient to implement across various hPSC lines. Starting hPSCs must possess optimal morphology, which includes smooth borders on a dense colony of stem cells (Figure 2A). Typically, on the second day of organoid formation, organoids should be relatively uniform and round, and it is normal to have a small number of cells on the periphery of the organoids (Figure 2B). Neuroepithelium began to expand rapidly after day 12, necessitating the addition of dissolved Matrigel during days 12 through 16 to aid in folding of the neuroepithelium (Figure 2C). Without the addition of dissolved Matrigel, the neuroepithelium fails to fold, resulting in the formation of sheet-shaped structures (Figure 2D). Once organoids are patterned after day 16, they should be mostly homogeneous in their size and circularity (Figures 2C and S1).

Failures in organoid differentiation may also occur. EB formation was usually observed after one day of cell plating. However, when the EBs were formed without sufficient Rock inhibitor Y-27632, or when the centrifugation time was too long and the rotation speed was too high, the cells were unable to aggregate and form EBs (Figure 2E, left panel). When medium changes are performed improperly (e.g., if the pipette tip touches the organoid or the medium is added forcefully), organoids may develop irregular shapes and uneven surfaces (Figure 2E, middle panel). Furthermore, if stem cells have undergone partial differentiation, the organoids may develop nonspecific structures (indicated by the black arrow; Figure 2E, right panel).

On day 16 of differentiation, immunostaining can be used to confirm the generation of dorsal hindbrain progenitor cells marked by PAX7 and SOX2 (Figures 3A and 3B). At this stage, PAX2+ inhibitory neurons begin to appear (Figure 3A). At this stage, there is no production of SOX10+ neural crest cells, PHOX2B+ or NKX2-2+ ventrally-derived cells in the organoids (Figures 3B and 3C). With prolonged culture (day 30), the organoids further develop, giving rise to large populations of LMX1B+ and PAX2+ excitatory and inhibitory neurons (Figures 3D and 3E). Meanwhile, the LBX1+ cells, which derive from the dorsal medullary dB1 domain, are broadly produced in hmSpVO, whereas OLIG3+ medullary dorsal class A neuronal lineages are not detected (Figure 3F). These validation analyses are essential for determining whether the organoids possess the identity of medullary SpV. In addition, samples can be collected at day 30 for qPCR analysis of HOX gene expression to confirm whether the organoids correspond to the hindbrain region.1 We have demonstrated the ability to reproducibly generate hmSpVOs using various cell lines, including four human PSC lines (H9 hESCs, H1 hESCs, hiPSC lines RC01001A and RC01001B) (Figure S1).1

Furthermore, it is helpful to compare single-cell sequencing data from the organoids with datasets from the human fetal brain atlas to further verify their medullary regional identity. Comparison with annotated human hindbrain nuclei datasets can help assess the similarity between the differentiated organoids and human medullary nuclei.1

To benefit the long-term development of hmSpVOs, particularly to facilitate the growth of projecting axons, we prepared hmSpVO sections (Figures 4A–4C) and cultured them at an air-liquid interface (Figures 4D–4F). Whole-mount staining can reveal abundant NF+ axons and MAP2+ dendrites within hmSpVOs. After long-term culture (over 120 days), NF+ axons exhibited patterned organization adjacent to organoid edges, eventually forming a coalesced NF+ axonal pathway devoid of MAP2+ dendritic processes (Figures 4G and 4H).

Cryosection and immunostaining of hmSpVOs cultured at the air-liquid interface for 1 month revealed the presence of LMX1B+/vGlut2+ excitatory neurons and PAX2+/GABA+ inhibitory neurons, indicating that the development of excitatory and inhibitory lineages (Figures 5A and 5B). Only a small number of GFAP-positive astrocytes were observed in two-month-old hmSpVOs (Figure 5C), which is consistent with results from transcriptomic analysis.1 After long-term culture, abundant GFAP+ astrocytes were produced in hmSpVOs (Figure 5D). Functional characterization of organoids can be detected by a multi-electrode array (MEA) system to evaluate the electrophysiological activity of hmSpVOs.1 When hmSpVOs were stimulated with calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP),29 a key neuropeptide involved in sensory processing including nociception, MEA recordings showed that CGRP treatment could increase spike events in hmSpVOs. (Figures 5E and 5F).

hmSpVO and hThO sections were fused at an air-liquid interface to produce hSTOs (Figures 6A–6C). Following fusion, axons projecting from the SPVO into the thalamic organoid can be observed. Extended co-culture periods result in more extensive axonal innervation (Figure 6D). Axonal projections from GFP+ hmSpVO into the thalamic organoid (hThO) were typically more robust than those from mCherry+ hThO into the hmSpVO (Figure 6E). Immunostaining of the fused organoids confirmed the maintenance of regional identity: the thalamic compartment was enriched for TCF7L2+ neurons, while the hmSpVO compartment was enriched for PAX2+ neurons (Figure 6E).

To further examine the connections between SpV and thalamic cells in hSTOs, retrograde tracing with the rAAV2-retro variant30 was performed (Figure 7). The key steps of the virus injection procedure are shown in Figures 7A–7F. Injection of the rAAV2-retro-hSyn-EGFP virus into the hThO region (Figure 7G, left panel) or the hmSpVO region (Figure 7G, right panel) of hSTOs revealed that retrograde tracing from hThO to hmSpVO resulted in a higher number of GFP+ cells compared to tracing from hmSpVO to hThO, further validating that the axon projection tract was biased toward trigeminothalamic rather than thalamotrigeminal connectivity. The retrogradely labeled GFP+ hmSpVO cells predominantly expressed the neuronal marker MAP2, but not the astrocytic marker GFAP (Figure 7J). The majority of neurons projecting to the hThO were vGLUT2+ excitatory neurons, rather than PAX2+ inhibitory neurons (Figure 7J). These findings are consistent with in vivo observations that trigeminothalamic projections originate primarily from vGLUT2+ neurons.31 Be cautious, as excessive injection may lead to viral leakage. For instance, when hSyn-EGFP virus (a non-tracing virus) was injected into the hThO region, excessive injection volume (more than 0.5 μL) and insufficient distance between the injection site and fusion border resulted in GFP+ cells appearing in the hmSpVO region near the fusion border (Figure 7H), indicating possible viral leakage. Unlike retrograde tracing, viral labeling caused by leakage mostly appears near the fusion border, whereas retrogradely traced cells can be more widely distributed within the organoids—for example, at the far edges of the traced organoids (Figures 7G and 7H).

The fusion-after-viral-infection strategy can help avoid potential effects caused by viral leakage; however, the number of retrogradely labeled GFP+ cells detected in the hmSpVO region of hSTOs was lower than that observed with direct injection (Figures 7I and G). Therefore, both injection and pre-fusion infection strategies have distinct advantages and disadvantages. When using the injection strategy, it is important to carefully control the volume of virus injected and to ensure that the injection site is as far as possible from the hmSpVO region.

To further investigate whether neurons within hmSpVOs and hThOs establish connections, AAV1-hSyn-ChrimsonR-tdTomato can be microinjected into the SpV region of hSTOs (Figure 8A). Optogenetic stimulation of hmSpVO axons on the thalamic side of the hSTOs then can be performed (Figure 8B). Illumination with 561 nm light evoked calcium responses in thalamic cells within the hThO region (Figure 8C). Spike events were observed following stimulation; application of the glutamate receptor antagonists CNQX (20 μM) and AP5 (50 μM) abolished these responses, indicating that glutamatergic transmission occurs from hmSpVOs to hThOs (Figure 8D). Furthermore, using a high-density 3D MEA (HD-MEA) system, we applied electrical stimulation to the SpV region of hSTOs via micrometer-scale electrodes. Analysis of the thalamic side showed that electrical stimulation of SpV cells elicited spike events in the thalamic region, further demonstrating the existence of connections between SpV and thalamic neurons (Figures 8E and 8F). These analyses demonstrate that functional connectivity is established between neurons in hmSpVOs and hThOs within hSTOs.

Limitations

During long-term culture, organoids may develop central necrosis due to insufficient oxygen and nutrient supply, a condition that is further exacerbated by prolonged activation of Wnt signaling. To mitigate this issue, we sectioned the organoids and employed air–liquid interface culture techniques. Although these methods can alleviate core necrosis, they do not fully eliminate it during extended culture periods.

We identified the presence of both PAX2+ and LMX1B+ inhibitory and excitatory neurons within hmSpVOs, with PAX2+ inhibitory neurons exhibiting a relatively higher proportion. Currently, the ratio of excitatory versus inhibitory neurons in the human SpV region is unclear, and there is no available data to investigate. Future analysis of the human SpV at the single-cell level will provide deeper insights into its molecular and cellular characteristics, guiding the generation of brain organoids in vitro. Future studies may be required to explore whether and how cell type ratio can be more authentically recapitulated in brain organoids.

Problem 2

Stem cells did not survive passage.

Potential solution

Matrigel coating of the plates was not performed properly may cause this problem. Store the Matrigel properly and keep it on ice while working with it to make sure it does not solidify.

Problem 3

Failure to aggregate into a single embryoid body (EB).

Potential solution

This problem may be caused by lack of Y-27632 during EBs formation. Remember to add Y-27632(50 μM) during EB formation.

Problem 4

Significant cell death surrounding the central aggregate.

Potential solution

Make sure fresh media are used and include all necessary components.

Pay attention to sterile technique during experiments.
