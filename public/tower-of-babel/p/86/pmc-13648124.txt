Swiss-Webster and C57BL/6 mice are differentially sensitive to the stimulant effects of methamphetamine
Authors: Bo Jarrett Wood, Nicole M Hall, M Frances Vest, Papori Sharma, Kevin S Murnane
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMethamphetamine is a highly addictive psychostimulant with significant neurobiological consequences, yet strain-dependent differences in its effects remain poorly understood. This study investigated behavioral and molecular differences in Swiss-Webster and C57BL/6 mice following methamphetamine exposure. Swiss-Webster mice exhibited greater behavioral sensitivity to methamphetamine compared to C57BL/6 mice, as demonstrated by lower peak doses required to elicit locomotor stimulation and conditioned place preference. Gene expression analyses revealed significantly higher striatal dopamine D2 receptor mRNA levels in Swiss-Webster mice, alongside a main effect of strain across multiple dopaminergic receptors, suggesting broader transcriptional differences may contribute to their heightened behavioral response. However, no strain differences were observed in dopamine transporter or dopamine D1 receptor mRNA expression. Following a neurotoxic binge-dosing regimen, both strains showed significant dopamine depletion, but no differences in the dopamine metabolite DOPAC were observed between strains in the striatum. Given the role of dopaminergic signaling in stimulant sensitivity and reward, these findings suggest that strain-dependent differences in methamphetamine response may reflect broader alterations in dopamine system function between these strains. This is the first study to directly compare Swiss-Webster and C57BL/6 mice in methamphetamine-induced CPP and provides novel insight into strain-specific drug reward mechanisms.Keywords: Swiss-Webster, C57BL/6, dopamine receptor, methamphetamine, addiction, strain-differences

Introduction

Methamphetamine has increasingly become a public health issue in the United States. According to the National Survey on Drug Use and Health, over 2.5 million people in the United States aged 12 or older reported using methamphetamine in the year 2020, with 1.5 million meeting criteria for methamphetamine use disorder (Han et al., 2021). Given the growing impact of methamphetamine misuse, understanding its pharmacological profile in preclinical models is critical for identifying biological mechanisms that may underlie its effects across different populations.

Methamphetamine rapidly crosses the blood-brain barrier due to its small size and lipophilic nature, where it acts as an indirect agonist of monoamines, primarily dopamine and norepinephrine (Park and Haning, 2016), by promoting their release centrally and peripherally (Shin et al., 2017). Methamphetamine facilitates dopamine release from by reversing dopamine transporter (DAT) and vesicular monoamine transporter 2 function, driving excess dopamine into the synapse. This mechanism contributes to methamphetamine's behavioral and reinforcing effects and is implicated in its neurotoxic profile (Park and Haning, 2016; Yang et al., 2018). Chronic use may lead to dopamine system dysregulation, resulting in lasting physiological and psychological impairments (Yang et al., 2018; Wise and Robble, 2020; Wood et al., 2025).

While no single mouse model can fully recapitulate the complexity of neuropsychiatric disorders, including substance use disorders, these models remain essential for studying the multifaceted effects of drugs of misuse. Studies of behavioral and neurotoxic effects of methamphetamine have been frequently carried out using mouse models (Ray et al., 2019a; Miyamoto et al., 2014). Two of the most widely used strains in drug addiction research are the C57BL/6 strain and the Swiss-Webster strain. The C57BL/6 inbred strain, derived from the original C57BL lineage, is commonly used as background strain for genetic manipulation studies (Mekada et al., 2009). In contrast, Swiss-Webster, a robust outbred strain derived from nine mice imported to the United States from Switzerland in 1962, are frequently used as a general-purpose strain for pharmacological studies, including the effects of other stimulants (Ray et al., 2019b; Goeders et al., 2009; Riday et al., 2012; Achat-Mendes et al., 2003), the effects of phytocannabinoids on alcohol drinking (Oppong-Damoah et al., 2019a), age-related neurocognitive decline (Goeders et al., 2009), and the neuropharmacology of thermogenesis (Murphy and Murnane, 2019).

While both C57BL/6 and Swiss-Webster mice are commonly used in addiction-related research, accumulating evidence suggests that they differ markedly in their behavioral and physiological responses to environmental and pharmacological stimuli. For example, C57BL/6 mice display heightened autonomic responses to stress, whereas Swiss-Webster mice show minimal autonomic activation (van Bogaert et al., 2006). Swiss-Webster mice have also demonstrated greater mobility and increased glial reactivity in models of spinal cord injury compared to C57BL/6 mice (Noristani et al., 2018), suggesting broader differences in central nervous system responsivity.

Building on this, both strains have been widely used in preclinical studies of the behavioral and neuropharmacological effects of misused drugs, including methamphetamine (Ray et al., 2019a; Murnane et al., 2012; Hutchinson et al., 2014). However, few studies have directly compared Swiss-Webster and C57BL/6 mice in the context of methamphetamine exposure. Extending this research to understanding strain-specific differences in the behavioral and neurotoxic effects of methamphetamine will advance knowledge in this area, allowing the research community to better contextualize findings between studies, and provide a more solid foundation for the use of these strains in the development of pharmaceutical treatments for methamphetamine.

In the present study, we directly compared male Swiss-Webster and C57BL/6 mice in their behavioral and neurochemical responses to methamphetamine. To assess strain differences in the stimulant and rewarding properties of methamphetamine, we evaluated dose-dependent locomotor activity in an open field and conditioned place preference (CPP). To explore potential molecular mechanisms underlying behavioral differences, we measured striatal gene expression of dopamine-related targets using qRT-PCR in drug-naïve animals. Finally, we assessed dopamine and DOPAC levels in key brain regions following a neurotoxic methamphetamine dosing regimen to examine whether strain differences extended to dopaminergic depletion. These complementary measures were selected to provide a comprehensive evaluation of strain-dependent sensitivity to methamphetamine across behavioral, molecular, and neurochemical domains.

Materials and methods

Drugs

Methamphetamine was obtained from the National Institute of Drug Abuse (Research Triangle Park, NC, USA) and dissolved in 0.9 % NaCL. All doses are reported as the salt form. All injections were administrated intraperitoneally at a volume of 0.01 mL physiological saline (0.9 %) or drug solution per gram body weight of each mouse.

Animals

Male Swiss-Webster and C57BL/6 mice (Charles River Laboratories, Wilmington, MA), weighing 25–50 g and aged 3–6 months, were used in this study. The use of adult males was consistent with prior work in our lab (Ray et al., 2019a; Oppong-Damoah et al., 2019a; Oppong-Damoah et al., 2019b) and reflects largely the stimulant and CPP literature, where male cohorts are often used to establish baseline effects. However, we acknowledge that this limits generalizability, and future studies should include females to assess potential sex-dependent differences. Mice were housed five per cage in a temperature-controlled room on a 12-h light/dark cycle, with all experiments conducted during the light phase (7:00 a.m.–7:00 p.m.) at ambient temperatures (21–22 °C). Food and water were available ad libitum. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Louisiana State University Health Sciences Center – Shreveport.

Open field - locomotor activity

Open field is a behavioral assay to assess locomotor activity, using transparent square acrylic chambers (26 × 26 × 39 cm) with infrared photobeam detection coupled with an automated activity monitor (Coulbourn Instruments; Whitehall, PA). This system monitors both horizontal (XY-move distance) and vertical activity (rearing). Methamphetamine doses of 1, 3.2, 5.6, and 10 mg/kg were selected based on prior studies assessing stimulant-induced locomotor activity in mice, which demonstrated dose-dependent increases in activity within a similar range (Ortman et al., 2021; Good and Radcliffe, 2011; Hall et al., 2008). These prior studies informed the starting range, which we expanded into a full dose–response curve to evaluate both strain-dependent potency and efficacy. Mice were acclimated for 30 min in their home cage within the experimental room, but not in the open-field chambers. This period allowed animals to habituate to the environment prior to drug administration and testing.

This system monitors both horizontal (XY-move distance) and vertical activity (rearing). Methamphetamine doses of 1, 3.2, 5.6, and 10 mg/kg were tested in both strains. This period allowed animals to habituate to the environment prior to drug administration and testing. Locomotor activity during acclimation was not recorded, and strain differences during this period were not assessed. Each dose group consisted of separate, drug-naïve animals. No mice were re-used or re-tested across doses to avoid potential carry-over effects or sensitization. The total ambulatory distance traveled (in centimeters) over the 90-min recording was evaluated.

Conditioned Place Preference (CPP)

CPP is a behavioral assay used to assess the positive appetitive conditioned effects of a drug. In this study, we used an expedited CPP process where mice undergo two conditioning sessions each day for four days as previously described (Oppong-Damoah et al., 2019a; Oppong-Damoah et al., 2019b; Calcagnetti and Schechter, 1992). In this experiment, CPP was performed using a two-compartment chamber (Opto-VariMex, Columbus Instruments; Columbus, OH). One side of the chamber was composed of black walls and a smooth floor while the other side had white walls, and a plastic mesh floor insert.

Mice were first habituated to the CPP apparatus over two days, with 30-min sessions each day during which they had free access to both compartments. To account for individual baseline preferences, a biased CPP protocol was used, in which the drug was paired with the animal's initially less-preferred side of the chamber (as determined on Day 3 during a 30-min pre-test with free access to both sides) (Oppong-Damoah et al., 2019a; Oppong-Damoah et al., 2019b).

Methamphetamine doses of 0.56, 1, 1.8 and 3 mg/kg were selected based on previous studies using doses within this range (Ikekubo et al., 2020; Ren et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). These doses allowed for construction of a dose-response curve capable of capturing, threshold, and peak reward effects. Following this baseline assessment, mice underwent conditioning sessions twice daily for four consecutive days. During this time, the animals received a saline injection in the morning (11:00 h) while being confined to the preferred compartment for 30 min. In the afternoon (14:00 h), each mouse received an injection of methamphetamine and was confined to the less preferred compartment for 30 min. In between each conditioning, mice were placed back in their home cage. On Day 5, a 30-min post-test was conducted with free access to both compartments. Conditioned place preference was assessed as the change in time spent in the drug-paired compartment: (CPP score = (post−prepre) × 100), where post and pre represent time (in seconds) spent in the initially non-preferred compartment during the post-test and pre-test, respectively. To evaluate dose-dependent conditioned place preference across strains, each dose group consisted of independent, drug-naïve animals. No animals were re-used across doses, minimizing potential carry-over effects.

Neurotoxic dosing regimen

Mice were exposed to either saline or methamphetamine using a neurotoxic binge-like dosing regimen, in which a unit dose of 5 mg/kg methamphetamine or vehicle was administered 4 times with 2 h separating each administration (QID, q2h). All injections were administered intraperitoneally with methamphetamine solution dissolved in vehicle per gram body weight of each mouse. Stimulants such as amphetamine derivatives (Murnane et al., 2012; Chitre et al., 2020) and certain cathinone stimulants (Ray et al., 2019b) are known to induce persistent neurochemical depletions (Murnane et al., 2012).

Neurochemical Measurements and Brain Dissection

On the fifth day following the neurotoxic dosing regimen, mice were euthanized via rapid cervical dislocation followed by decapitation. Brains were rapidly extracted on ice, flash-frozen with isopentane, and stored at −80 °C until processing. On the day of analysis, brains were thawed at 4 °C and placed in a cold brain matrix. Coronal slices approximately 1 mm thick were prepared using a razor blade and placed on a frozen metal plate. For both strains, animals subjected to the neurotoxic binge-dosing regimen or saline binge control had their brains sectioned. Key regions of interest, including the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and striatum (STR), were manually dissected from the slices for neurochemical analysis as previously described (Ray et al., 2019b; Murnane et al., 2012; Chitre et al., 2020). Reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with a Waters Spherisorb ODS 5 μm 4.6x250mm analytical column operated at 28.1 °C (Eppendorf CH-30) was used to measure levels of dopamine and its metabolite, 3.4-dihy-droxyphenylacetic acid (DOPAC), in the STR, PFC, and hippocampus. The tissue was homogenized and sent to HPLC for analysis using methods adapted from Salvatore et al. (2012) In brief, autosampler injections (120uL for PFC & HC and 50uL for STR) were made using a Waters 717 plus autoinjector, set to house samples at 10 °C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1 M sodium phosphate with 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.3 mM 1-octane sulfonic acid, and 3.75 % acetonitrile. It was pH adjusted to 3.05 with O-phosphoric acid and pumped at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min, with a 45 min runtime per sample. Detection was accomplished with an amperometric BAS detector, equipped with a glassy carbon working electrode, set to 780 mV. Chromatogram processing was performed by Waters Empower software (ver. 2).

Quantitative PCR

RNA was extracted from the STR in naïve mice of both strains using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596026) after homogenizing the tissue using 1 mm zirconia/silica beads(11079110z) in a tissue homogenizer. The aqueous phase was collected using phenol:chloroform extraction procedure and then RNA was isolated using RNeasy Mini kit (QIAGEN, 74106) according to manufacturer's instructions. The concentration and purity were evaluated with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). Single-strand cDNA was synthesized using iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), from 1 μg of total RNA in a final volume of 20 μL cDNA was stored at −20 °C for future use. Quantitative PCR reactions were performed using the universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) on a CFX96 thermal cycler with Bio-Rad CFX Manager software (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). 50 ng of cDNA were used for each reaction. To assess PCR efficiency, a standard curve was generated by performing qPCR on a series of five 10-fold serial dilutions of pooled cDNA template (1, 1:10, 1:100, 1:1,000, 1:10,000). The mean threshold cycle (Ct) values for each dilution were plotted against the logarithm of the cDNA dilution factor. Primers for D1 dopamine receptor (D1DR: F5’-CAC GGC ATC CAT CCT TAA CCT-3′,R5’-TGC CTT CGG AGT CAT CTT CCT-3′), D2 dopamine receptor (D2DR: F5’-ACC TGT CCT GGT ACG ATG ATG-3′, R5’-GCA TGG CAT AGT AGT TGT AGT GG-3′), dopamine transporter (DAT: F5’-AAA TGC TCC GTG GGA CCA ATG-3′, R5’-GTC TCC CGC TCT TGA ACC TC-3′) and GAPDH (housekeeping gene) (GAPDH: F5’- AAGGTCATCCCAGAGCTGAA-3′, R5’- CTGCTTCACCACCTTCTTGA-3′) were used in this study. All samples were run in triplicates, results were normalized to housekeeping genes (GAPDH) and expressed as fold change using the ΔΔ-Ct threshold cycle method of normalization (Rao et al., 2013).

Data analysis

All graphical data were generated using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA) and are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Acute locomotor activity and CPP were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Sidak's post hoc test for multiple group comparisons. Gene expression data from qPCR were analyzed using two-way ANOVA to assess main effects of strain and gene target for dopamine receptors, with Sidak's post hoc test where appropriate. DAT expression was analyzed separately using an unpaired two-tailed t-test. Neurochemical measurements of dopamine and DOPAC were evaluated using two-way ANOVA with Sidak's post hoc test for group and strain comparisons.

Results

Acute methamphetamine induced locomotor activity

Open field testing was used to assess the acute locomotor effects of methamphetamine in Swiss-Webster and C57BL/6 mice. A two-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of dose (F(4,69) = 44.61, p < 0.0001), a main effect of strain (F(1,69) = 9.08, p = 0.0036), and a significant strain × dose interaction (F(4,69) = 3.56, p = 0.0106), indicating that the locomotor response to methamphetamine differed across strains and doses. The dose-response curve revealed a rightward shift in peak locomotor activity in C57BL/6 mice compared to Swiss-Webster mice, suggesting a strain-dependent difference in potency (Fig. 1A). Sidak's post hoc comparisons confirmed that Swiss-Webster mice exhibited significantly higher locomotor activity at 3.2 mg/kg compared to C57BL/6 mice at the same dose (p = 0.0007), and greater activity than C57BL/6 mice at 5.6 mg/kg (p = 0.0148), which was the peak dose in C57BL/6 mice (Fig. 1B). Within-strain analyses showed that both strains had significantly greater locomotor activity at their respective peak methamphetamine doses relative to their saline controls (Swiss-Webster - 3.2 mg/kg vs. saline: p < 0.0001; C57BL/6–5.6 mg/kg vs. saline: p < 0.0001). These findings demonstrate that Swiss-Webster mice are more sensitive to the locomotor stimulant effects of methamphetamine compared to C57BL/6 mice. To determine whether anxiety-related behavior may have contributed to the observed strain differences in activity at the peak dose, we analyzed percent time spent in the center of the open field for each strain. No significant difference was observed (data not shown), suggesting that anxiety-like behavior is unlikely to account for differences in locomotor response we are seeing at the peak dose.

Conditioned place preference

To assess the rewarding effects of methamphetamine across strains, we conducted a CPP dose-response curve in Swiss-Webster (0.56, 1.0, 1.8 mg/kg) and C57BL/6 (1.0, 1.8, 3.0 mg/kg) mice (Fig. 2A). Doses were selected to capture a potential rightward shift in sensitivity, as observed in locomotor activity. Consistent with this, peak preference occurred at a lower dose in Swiss-Webster mice (1.0 mg/kg) compared to C57BL/6 mice (1.8 mg/kg). To directly compare strains, a two-way ANOVA was conducted using the overlapping doses (1.0 and 1.8 mg/kg) and saline controls (Fig. 2B). This analysis revealed a significant interaction between strain and dose (F(2,38) = 5.9, p = 0.0055) and a main effect of dose (F(2,38) = 12.0, p < 0.0001), indicating that the effects of methamphetamine varied by dose and strain. Although the main effect of strain was not significant (p = 0.3965), Sidak's post hoc comparisons revealed significant CPP at 1.0 mg/kg in Swiss-Webster mice (vs. saline, p = 0.0002) and at 1.8 mg/kg in C57BL/6 mice (vs. saline, p = 0.0084) (Fig. 2B). Additionally, place preference at 1.0 mg/kg was significantly greater in Swiss-Webster than in C57BL/6 mice (p = 0.0119), consistent with the shift in the dose–response curve and supporting enhanced methamphetamine sensitivity in Swiss-Webster mice.

Quantitative real-time PCR

To evaluate potential strain differences in baseline gene expression related to dopamine signaling, we measured D1DR, D2DR, and DAT mRNA levels in the striatum of naïve Swiss-Webster and C57BL/6 mice using qRT-PCR. A two-way ANOVA conducted on D1DR and D2DR expression (excluding DAT due to its functional distinction as a transporter) revealed a significant main effect of strain (F(1,70) = 8.2, p = 0.0055), indicating overall higher receptor expression in Swiss-Webster mice. No significant main effect of receptor (p = 0.8844) or strain × gene interaction (p = 0.4432) was detected. Sidak's Post hoc comparisons showed significantly greater D2DR expression in Swiss-Webster compared to C57BL/6 mice (p = 0.0244), while D1DR expression differences did not reach significance (p = 0.2663). For DAT, a separate unpaired t-test revealed no significant strain difference (p = 0.2131, Fig. 3C). These findings suggest that enhanced striatal dopamine receptor expression, particularly D2DR, may contribute to strain differences in methamphetamine-related behaviors, while DAT levels do not appear to differ significantly.

Effects on neurochemistry

The effects of strain on cortical and striatal dopamine levels and its major metabolite, DOPAC, were evaluated in both saline-exposed mice and those exposed to a neurotoxic methamphetamine regimen. In the PFC, neither strain (F(1,19) = 0.0011, p = 0.9161) nor methamphetamine exposure (F(1,19) = 0.4364, p = 0.5168) significantly affected dopamine levels. While strain did not significantly influence DOPAC levels (F(1,19) = 1.987, p = 0.1748), methamphetamine exposure did (F (1,19) = 5.667, p = 0.0279). However, Sidak's post-hoc testing did not reveal significant pairwise differences between groups (p > 0.05 for all comparisons) (Fig. 4A). In contrast, in the STR, there was no significant main effect of strain on dopamine levels (F(1,19) = 0.2222, p = 0.6428), but methamphetamine exposure led to a significant reduction (F(1,19) = 21.42, p = 0.0002). Sidak's post-hoc test confirmed significant differences between saline- and methamphetamine-exposed groups in both Swiss-Webster (p = 0.0052) and C57BL/6 mice (p = 0.0492) (Fig. 4B). For DOPAC, there was no main effect of strain (F(1,19) = 1.351, p = 0.2596), but methamphetamine exposure significantly reduced levels in the STR (F(1,19) = 18.68, p = 0.0004).

Discussion

In the present study, two strains of mice, Swiss-Webster and C57BL/6 mice, were evaluated to establish whether there are strain specific differences in the behavioral and/or neurochemical response to methamphetamine. Our findings suggest that there are significant strain-dependent differences in behavioral responses to methamphetamine, highlighting key distinctions in sensitivity and potency. In open field and CPP assays, Swiss-Webster mice exhibit greater behavioral effects at lower doses of methamphetamine when compared to C57BL/6 mice. The acute locomotor effects of methamphetamine revealed a significant efficacy difference between strains, with Swiss-Webster mice displaying more ambulatory movement at their peak dose (3.2 mg/kg) vs C57BL/6 mice's peak dose (5.6 mg/kg). While ambulatory distance is a widely used measure of psychostimulant-induced locomotor activity, it does not fully capture the spectrum of behaviors elicited by high doses of methamphetamine. The dose-response curve observed here followed an inverted-U pattern, with locomotor activity increasing at moderate doses and declining at higher doses. The reduction in activity at the 10 mg/kg dose in both strains likely reflects the emergence of stereotyped behaviors, such as head bobbing or sniffing, which do not register as ambulatory movement. These non-ambulatory, repetitive movements may have differentially affected how locomotor efficacy was represented across strains, particularly if one strain expressed more stereotypy than the other. In addition to locomotion, open field tests can offer insight into emotional and cognitive domains, including anxiety-like behavior (Kraeuter et al., 2019), impulsivity (Loos et al., 2009), and habituation (Daenen et al., 2001). Although this study was not designed to assess these endpoints in detail, we conducted an analysis of center time at each strain's respective peak locomotor dose, a common index of anxiety-like behavior, and found no significant differences. Still, previous studies report mixed findings on anxiety-like traits between C57BL/6 and Swiss-Webster mice, and the potential influence of strain differences in emotionality or stereotypy on locomotor outcomes should not be discounted. Future studies using video-based scoring, latency-to-center metrics, or time-bin analyses could help disentangle these overlapping behavioral domains.

In CPP, we observed a rightward shift in the dose-effect curve in C57BL/6 mice compared to Swiss-Webster mice. Specifically, methamphetamine produced peak place preference at a lower dose (1.0 mg/kg) in Swiss-Webster mice, whereas C57BL/6 mice required a higher dose (1.8 mg/kg) to achieve a comparable effect. This dose-specific difference, supported by a significant strain × dose interaction, suggests a strain difference in sensitivity (potency). However, the magnitude of preference at each strain's peak dose did not differ significantly, indicating no strain difference in efficacy under these conditions.

The heightened sensitivity of Swiss-Webster mice observed in the current study aligns with findings from a previous study, underscoring strain-specific differences in response to amphetamines. Weaver et al. found that Swiss-Webster mice displayed an 8-fold increase in amphetamine lethality under group-housed conditions compared to isolated conditions, whereas C57BL/6 mice showed a smaller, less-than 2-fold difference in lethality under the same conditions (Weaver and Kerley, 1962). This aligns with the current findings where Swiss-Webster mice showed an increased behavioral response to methamphetamine compared to C57BL/6 mice.

To build on these observed strain differences in behavioral and physiological response, we can look at studies that have compared C57BL/6 mice to other strains in the context of stimulant effects. While direct comparisons between C57BL/6 and Swiss-Webster mice in stimulant studies are limited, research on C57BL/6 mice has been extensive, particularly in evaluating their sensitivity to methamphetamine. For example, C57BL/6 mice show relatively low sensitivity to methamphetamine-induced locomotor activity when compared to several other mouse strains, including dd, ICR, BALB/c, C3H/He, and DBA/2 (Kuribara and Tadokoro, 1989; Kuribara and Tadokoro, 1987). Notably, BALB/c mice exhibit a similar insensitivity to methamphetamine's locomotor-stimulating effects, highlighting strain-specific variations in response to stimulant exposure (Kuribara and Tadokoro, 1989; Kuribara and Tadokoro, 1987; Kitahama and Valatx, 1979). These findings suggest that while C57BL/6 mice have been widely studied for their stimulant response, further exploration of how Swiss-Webster mice compare can provide valuable insights into the neurobiological mechanisms underlying these behavioral differences. Strain differences may also extend beyond locomotion to include emotionality-like traits such as anxiety or impulsivity. Previous studies have reported mixed results regarding anxiety-like behavior in C57BL/6 versus Swiss-Webster mice, with some suggesting heightened anxiety-like traits in Swiss-Websters (van Gaalen and Steckler, 2000), while others report the opposite (Morgan and Pfaff, 2002). In the present study, we found no strain differences in time spent in the center of the open field at the peak dose of methamphetamine, suggesting comparable anxiety-like behavior under these conditions. However, because stereotyped movements were not directly quantified, it remains possible that high-dose methamphetamine induced more stereotypy in C57BL/6 mice, which could have masked or suppressed their locomotor output. Future studies using video-based behavioral analysis or scoring of stereotypy behavior could help disentangle these overlapping effects.

Although locomotor activity is commonly used to assess stimulant sensitivity across mouse strains, it does not fully capture the positive, appetitive properties of drugs properties that are central to assessing misuse liability. While both Swiss-Webster and C57BL/6 mice have been widely used in CPP studies across various drug classes, direct comparisons of their methamphetamine-induced CPP responses have not been reported. Both strains have reliably shown CPP for alcohol (Oppong-Damoah et al., 2019a; Risinger and Oakes, 1996), stimulants such as methamphetamine and cocaine (Orsini et al., 2005; Shetty et al., 2017), opioids (Orsini et al., 2005; Cook and Beardsley, 2003), and nicotine (Kutlu et al., 2015) (Risinger and Oakes, 1995). Notably, strain differences in CPP sensitivity have been documented for other drugs of misuse. For instance, C57BL/6 mice, but not DBA/1 J mice, developed a nicotine place preference, while the latter displayed conditioned aversion (Kutlu et al., 2015). Likewise, C57BL/6 mice were more sensitive than DBA/2 J mice to the rewarding effects of both cocaine and morphine (Orsini et al., 2005). While no prior studies have directly compared Swiss-Webster to other strains using CPP, one study using a methamphetamine dose-response curve in Swiss-Webster mice found that 0.5 mg/kg produced the strongest place preference, with no significant difference between 0.5 and 1.0 mg/kg. The descending limb of their dose-response emerged at 2.0–4.0 mg/kg (Shetty et al., 2017), which is like the descending pattern we observed at 1.8 mg/kg in our Swiss-Webster cohort. Taken together, our findings address a key gap in the literature by directly comparing methamphetamine-induced CPP across mouse strains. We demonstrate that Swiss-Webster mice are more sensitive to the rewarding effects of methamphetamine than C57BL/6 mice, providing novel insight into strain-dependent differences in drug reward.

To investigate the factors contributing to the observed differences in response to methamphetamine, we analyzed mRNA levels of dopamine receptors and transporters in the STR using samples from naive animals. Additionally, we conducted neurochemical analyses using HPLC to measure levels of dopamine and its metabolite, DOPAC, in animals that underwent a neurotoxic binge-dose regimen of methamphetamine and compared them to saline-treated controls. In drug-naïve animals, we observed a significant main effect of strain on dopamine receptor expression (D1DR, D2DR), with Swiss-Webster mice exhibiting a significant difference in D2DR receptor expression relative to C57BL/6 mice. Although difference in DAT expression did not reach significance, there was a trend towards elevated DAT in Swiss-Webster mice. These results suggest that enhanced dopaminergic signaling, potentially via D2DR, may contribute to the observed behavioral differences, although D1DR and DAT may also play a role. Neurochemical analyses via HPLC in animals treated with a neurotoxic binge-dose regimen of methamphetamine revealed alterations in dopamine and DOPAC compared to saline-treated controls in both strains. There was no significant difference between strains in dopamine or DOPAC levels in the neurotoxic binge-dosing group and their respective saline-treated controls. These findings together suggest that Swiss-Webster are more sensitive to the behavioral effects of methamphetamine due to increased D2DR expression.

To contextualize these findings, it is important to examine previous research on dopaminergic signaling and stimulant sensitivity. Although there are no prior studies directly comparing dopamine-related gene expression between C57BL/6 and Swiss-Webster mice, several lines of evidence suggest that differences in D2DR, D1DR, and DAT expression may underlie behavioral variability across strains. Mice lacking D2 receptors exhibit reduced sensitivity to the locomotor-stimulant effects of methamphetamine and show blunted responses to sensitization protocols (Kelly et al., 2008). Similarly, D2 receptor knockout mice display attenuated cocaine-induced locomotion in a genotype-dependent manner, with wild-type > heterozygous > knockout (Chausmer et al., 2002). Moreover, studies in Swiss-Webster mice have demonstrated that D2 antagonists like haloperidol and raclopride reduce cocaine-induced locomotion (Chausmer and Katz, 2001). These findings collectively suggest that higher D2 receptor expression, as seen in Swiss-Webster mice, may contribute to their enhanced stimulant sensitivity. Beyond locomotor effects, D2 signaling also influences reward-related behaviors. In rats, D2 receptor antagonism attenuated the acquisition of amphetamine-induced CPP (Sevak et al., 2007). Similarly, another study showed that D2DR antagonism reduced the acquisition, but not the expression, of amphetamine-induced CPP, whereas D1 receptor antagonism with SCH23390 blocked both the acquisition and expression of CPP (Hiroi and White, 1991). However, a contrasting study found that D2 receptor antagonism did not block the acquisition of methylphenidate-induced CPP, while D1 antagonism was effective (Cummins et al., 2014). These studies highlight that the complex interplay among dopamine receptors becomes more apparent in studies involving D1 signaling.

Expanding on this, D1 knockout mice exhibit reduced locomotor activity and impaired intracranial self-stimulation (Tran et al., 2005), a commonly used assay to index drug reward. By contrast, D2 knockout mice show reduced locomotion but intact reward sensitivity in the same task, suggesting more selective involvement of D1 receptors in reward-related behaviors. Furthermore, in D2 knockout mice, pretreatment with the D1 antagonist SCH23390 produced more robust reductions in methamphetamine-induced locomotion than in wild-type mice, emphasizing how loss of D2 receptor function increases reliance on D1 signaling. In wild-type animals, D1 receptor antagonism alone reduced stimulant-induced activity, though to a lesser extent, highlighting both additive and interactive contributions of these receptor subtypes. Other studies in rats have similarly shown that blockade of D1 receptors with SCH23390 reduces locomotor activity in a dose-dependent fashion (Hoffman and Beninger, 1985). Moreover, the D1 antagonist SCH23390 blocks both the acquisition and expression of morphine-induced CPP in rats (Rezaei et al., 2022), again implicating D1 signaling in both motor and reward functions.

Finally, DAT expression also plays a critical role in modulating stimulant sensitivity. A study comparing DBA/2 J and C57BL/6 J mice found that DBA/2 J mice had higher striatal DAT density and exhibited greater heroin- and methamphetamine-induced locomotor activity (Kuribara and Tadokoro, 1989; Kuribara and Tadokoro, 1987; Bailey et al., 2010), a pattern that mirrors the heightened methamphetamine sensitivity we observed in Swiss-Webster mice. These results underscore the importance of DAT in governing extracellular dopamine availability and stimulant response. Although our findings revealed a main effect of strain across dopaminergic receptors (D1, D2) with a similar trend seen in DAT, the converging evidence suggests that Swiss-Webster mice may exhibit a broadly upregulated dopamine system, which in turn could account for their enhanced locomotor and reward-related sensitivity to methamphetamine.

Together, our findings demonstrate that Swiss-Webster mice exhibit heightened sensitivity to the behavioral effects of methamphetamine compared to C57BL/6 mice. To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly evaluate methamphetamine's strain-specific effects between Swiss-Webster and C57BL/6 mice using the CPP paradigm, providing new insights into strain-dependent differences in drug reward. Although no significant differences were observed in DRD1 or DAT expression in naïve mice or in neurochemical outcomes following a binge-dosing regimen, Swiss-Webster mice showed elevated DRD2 expression, which may partially contribute to their enhanced behavioral sensitivity. However, given the main effect of strain observed across all dopamine receptors tested, it is likely that broader differences in dopamine system function, not solely DRD2, underlie the behavioral phenotypes observed. These results highlight the importance of considering genetic background when interpreting preclinical addiction models and suggest that strain-dependent neurobiological differences can significantly influence outcomes in stimulant-related research.

While our study provides valuable insights into strain-specific effects of methamphetamine in Swiss-Webster and C57BL/6 mice, it is not without limitations. First, we did not evaluate sex-specific differences, as the study was conducted exclusively in male mice. This limits generalizability, particularly given the influence of sex hormones on dopaminergic signaling and drug-related behaviors (Bendis et al., 2024; Sinclair et al., 2014). Future studies should incorporate both sexes to assess potential sex-based differences in methamphetamine sensitivity and underlying neurobiological mechanisms. Second, our cohort included only young adult mice (3–6 months), which may not reflect developmental or age-related changes in dopaminergic systems or behavioral responses to psychostimulants (Good and Radcliffe, 2011). Third, although we observed strain differences in DRD2 expression and behavioral sensitivity to methamphetamine, we did not assess other potentially relevant molecular pathways such as serotonergic signaling (Alex and Pehek, 2007), which may contribute to strain differences. Moreover, our analyses were limited to gene expression; future work should assess whether transcriptional changes translate to functional protein-level differences using techniques like western blotting or receptor binding assays. Additionally, while ambulatory distance was our primary behavioral endpoint, it does not capture the full behavioral repertoire associated with stimulant exposure. High doses of methamphetamine can elicit stereotyped behaviors (e.g., sniffing, head bobbing) that may reduce measurable locomotor output, potentially confounding interpretation of efficacy differences. Since stereotypy was not directly quantified, future studies employing video-based scoring or behavioral ethograms could better distinguish between hyperactivity and repetitive, non-ambulatory behavior. Finally, as discussed above, the open field assay can provide insights into emotionality-related behaviors such as anxiety and impulsivity, which may vary by strain. Although we conducted an analysis of center time at each strain's peak locomotor dose and found no significant strain differences, we did not comprehensively assess anxiety-like or cognitive behaviors, which methamphetamine can modulate. Given mixed findings in the literature regarding anxiety traits in these strains, future studies should consider additional metrics, such as elevated plus or zero maze, light-dark box and marble burying. Likewise, while the CPP model offers valuable information on reward, it does not capture other addiction-relevant behaviors like compulsive drug-seeking or relapse. Incorporating models such as operant self-administration or reinstatement would offer a more comprehensive understanding of strain-dependent differences in methamphetamine use.

This study revealed significant strain-dependent differences in methamphetamine sensitivity, with Swiss-Webster mice exhibiting a greater behavioral response at lower doses than C57BL/6 mice, an effect potentially linked to differences in dopamine receptors. These findings highlight the need to consider genetic background when interpreting preclinical results and selecting animal models for addiction research. While the study provides valuable insight into dopaminergic contributions to strain differences, it is limited by its exclusive focus on male mice and a narrow age range, as well as by the exclusion of other neurochemical pathways such as glutamatergic and serotonergic systems. Future studies should broaden the scope to include sex and developmental stage comparisons, investigate additional molecular mediators, and incorporate behavioral models like operant self-administration to better capture addiction-relevant phenotypes. Ultimately, contextualizing these strain differences will help bridge inconsistencies across studies and refine translational strategies for developing pharmacological interventions for methamphetamine use disorder.
