Benchmarking large language models for cell typing in single-cell RNA-Seq
Authors: Tianxiang Xiao, Dezhi Hua, Yanan Wang, Xuemei Lu, Chao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLarge language models (LLMs) show significant potential for cell type annotation in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), but a systematic framework for their application and a comprehensive performance comparison are lacking. Here, we systematically benchmarked seven leading LLM models and three traditional bioinformatics tools across 34 diverse human and mouse datasets. We establish that using marker genes selected by statistical significance and ranked by log₂ fold change optimizes annotation accuracy. Our benchmark reveals that LLMs profoundly outperform traditional methods, particularly in resolving fine-grained cell subtypes. A top tier of models, including Kimi-k2, GPT-5, Claude-4.1, and Grok-4, consistently delivered the highest accuracy. To harness their collective strength, we developed an elite ensemble strategy that achieves state-of-the-art performance. We encapsulated these findings into DeepCellSeek, an open-source R package and interactive web platform, to provide a validated, high-performance solution. This work provides a practical roadmap for leveraging LLMs in single-cell research and paves the way for their evolution into powerful discovery engines.

Introduction

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has made groundbreaking advances in the past decade, enabling researchers to reveal cellular heterogeneity within tissues at unprecedented resolution [1]. This technology has become indispensable for dissecting complex biological systems across diverse fields, including developmental biology, cancer research, aging studies, and immunology [2–4]. However, accurate cell type and subtype identification in scRNA-seq data remains a significant challenge. Current approaches heavily depend on manual annotation by domain experts—a process that is not only time consuming and labor intensive but also suffers from lack of standardized criteria [5].

Traditional computational methods for single-cell annotation heavily rely on predefined databases of cell-type-specific marker genes, as exemplified by widely adopted tools including SingleR [6], Cellmarker2.0 [7], and ScType [8]. In recent years, the rapid development of large language models (LLMs) has opened new pathways for analyzing complex data. Our study evaluates the most recent, state-of-the-art models from prominent families including DeepSeek [9], Claude [10], GPT [11], Gemini [12], Grok [13], Doubao [14], and Kimi [15], whose release timeline underscores their novelty (Fig. 1a). Generally based on the Transformer architecture, they are pretrained on massive text and code data, gaining powerful knowledge integration, contextual understanding, and reasoning capabilities [16]. This equips them with the potential to integrate biomedical knowledge dispersed across vast literature with single-cell gene expression patterns, providing new possibilities for analyzing complex biological data. Their successes in fields ranging from protein structure prediction to medical diagnosis highlight their potential to synthesize dispersed biomedical insights with complex single-cell gene expression patterns [17, 18].

A systematic benchmarking framework for LLM-based cell type annotation. (a) Timeline of the large language models (LLMs) evaluated in this study, indicating their version and release date. (b) A flowchart illustrating the overall study design. Initially, public scRNA-seq datasets are collected and processed using the standard Seurat pipeline. The processed data are then subjected to three parallel annotation pathways: (i) manual annotation by experts, serving as the gold standard; (ii) annotation by a suite of LLMs (DeepSeek, Doubao, Claude, GPT, Kimi, Grok, and Gemini); and (iii) annotation using conventional bioinformatics tools (SingleR, ScType, and Cellmarker2.0). Finally, the results from LLMs and conventional tools are benchmarked against expert annotations to evaluate their accuracy in both cell type and subtype recognition. This comprehensive evaluation informs the development of DeepCellSeek, an integrated R package and web platform for LLM-based cell annotation.

The application of LLMs to scRNA-seq cell type annotation is a rapidly advancing frontier, building upon pioneering efforts such as GPTCelltype that first demonstrated the significant potential of individual LLMs in this domain [19]. However, a critical gap remains in establishing a systematic framework for their application. Key questions regarding how upstream analytical choices, such as marker gene selection strategies, impact LLM performance have been largely unaddressed. Furthermore, the robustness of these models against noisy or incomplete inputs has not been systematically quantified, hindering their reliable deployment in real-world research.

To address these gaps, we conduct a comprehensive benchmark to establish a robust and optimized framework for LLM-based single-cell annotation (Fig. 1b). We first perform a multifaceted dissection of upstream analytical strategies, systematically evaluating how LLM temperature, marker identification tests, gene ranking criteria, input gene quantity, and the inclusion of species context collectively influence annotation performance. We then quantify model robustness through rigorous gene substitution, gene addition, and reproducibility experiments.

Building on this optimized framework, we benchmark seven leading LLM models and three traditional bioinformatics tools across 34 diverse datasets. Our results not only demonstrate the profound superiority of LLMs but also reveal task-dependent performance hierarchies. Crucially, we develop an elite ensemble strategy that integrates the top-performing models, achieving state-of-the-art accuracy and stability. Furthermore, we extend our benchmark to a targeted analysis of model performance on challenging, disease-associated cell types, drawing upon four specialized datasets. Finally, all these findings are encapsulated in DeepCellSeek (https://deepcellseek.kiz.ac.cn), an open-source R package and an interactive web platform designed to provide the research community with a validated, high-performance solution for single-cell annotation.

Materials and methods

Dataset collection

To systematically evaluate the performance of LLMs across diverse biological contexts, we curated a comprehensive collection of publicly available scRNA-seq datasets (Supplementary Table S1). We included mouse datasets such as brain [20], multiple colon samples [21, 22], cerebrospinal fluid [23], abdomen [24], aorta [25], heart [26], spleen [27], thymus [27], mammary [27], marrow [27], lung [27], liver [27], kidney [27, 28], and neocortical tissue [29]. We also included human datasets such as colon cancer [30], colorectal cancer [30], breast [31], liver [32], venous blood [33], pancreas [34], lung (Azimuth) [35] and bone marrow (Azimuth) [36], cord blood [37], spleen [37], endothelium [38], germline [38], lymphoid cell [38], and myeloid cell [38]. To specifically assess performance on pathological cell types, we incorporated datasets of breast cancer [39], hepatocellular carcinoma [40], lung tissue from COVID-19 patients [41], and middle temporal gyrus from Alzheimer’s disease patients [42]. These datasets covered multiple sequencing platforms (e.g. 10X Genomics, SMART-Seq) and sources, including Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) [43], National Genomics Data Center (NGDC) [44], Single Cell Portal (SCP) [20], Tabula Muris [27], Human Cell Landscape (HCL) [37], Tabula Sapiens [38], and Azimuth reference atlas (https://azimuth.hubmapconsortium.org/). All selected datasets contained expert-provided annotations at the cell type level, and some also included finer-grained cell subtype annotations, allowing us to comprehensively assess model performance across different annotation granularities.

Large-scale benchmark for cell subtype annotation

To complement the broad cell type analysis, we established a comprehensive, large-scale benchmark focused specifically on the more challenging task of fine-grained cell subtype annotation.

The selection of tasks for this benchmark was guided by a key criterion to ensure a rigorous comparison: we required that the chosen subtypes be well characterized, with clear, fine-grained annotations in the source data to serve as a reliable ground truth for assessment.

Applying this criterion, we curated a benchmark of 13 diverse and challenging subtype annotation tasks. This benchmark includes the annotation of (1) T-cell subtypes in venous blood, (2) progenitor cell subtypes in bone marrow, (3) myeloid cell subtypes from a pan-immune context, (4) lymphoid cell subtypes from a pan-immune context, (5) germline cell subtypes, (6) endothelial cell subtypes, (7) secretory cell subtypes in the pancreas, (8) T-cell functional states in colorectal cancer, (9) neuron subtypes in the neocortex, (10) renal tubular epithelial cell subtypes, (11) lung monocyte subtypes, (12) heart cardiomyocyte subtypes, and (13) abdomen dendritic cell subtypes. This collection represents a rigorous and multifaceted testbed for evaluating the models’ capabilities on fine-grained cell identity characterization across a wide range of healthy and disease tissues.

Data preprocessing and marker gene identification

For the collected datasets, we used the standard Seurat (v5.2.1) workflow with appropriate adjustments tailored to the study’s goals [45]. Since the datasets were pre-filtered and quality controlled by original authors, we directly utilized the curated data and expert annotations from the publications.

We first created Seurat objects using expression matrices as counts data, along with metadata containing cell type annotations. The data were normalized using the NormalizeData function by scaling total counts per cell to 10 000 followed by log1p transformation. Highly variable genes were identified using FindVariableFeatures with the “vst” method, selecting the top 2000 genes for downstream analysis. The ScaleData function was then used to scale the data and mitigate sequencing depth differences between cells.

For marker gene identification, each pre-annotated cell type provided in the original datasets was treated as a distinct cluster. We then employed the FindAllMarkers function to perform differential expression analysis by comparing each cluster against all others using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. To ensure the identification of robust markers, we set the following key parameters: genes were required to be upregulated in the target cluster (only.pos = TRUE), to be expressed in a minimum of 25% of cells in either of the two compared populations (min.pct = 0.25), and to exhibit an average log₂ fold change of at least 0.25 (logfc.threshold = 0.25).

Data visualization

Dimensionality reduction was performed using the standard Seurat workflow, with default parameters for all functions unless otherwise specified. First, principal component analysis (PCA) was computed on the 2000 highly variable genes using the RunPCA function. Subsequently, the top 30 principal components were used to construct a nearest-neighbor graph, where each node represents an individual cell. This graph then served as the basis for generating a 2D embedding by applying the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) algorithm via the RunUMAP function.

Benchmark design for LLM input generation

To systematically evaluate how input gene lists affect LLM performance, we benchmarked several key parameters for their generation:

Statistical Test: We compared marker genes identified using two different statistical tests: the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (the default in our workflow) and the t-test.

Gene Ranking Method: We assessed three distinct methods for ranking the identified marker genes:

(1) P-value only: Genes were ranked by their ascending adjusted P-value (p_val_adj), corresponding to the default output of the FindAllMarkers function.

(1) P-value only: Genes were ranked by their ascending adjusted P-value (p_val_adj), corresponding to the default output of the FindAllMarkers function.

(2) log₂FC + P-value: Genes were first filtered for high statistical significance (p_val_adj < 2.2e-16) and then ranked by their descending log₂ fold change (avg_log2FC).

(2) log₂FC + P-value: Genes were first filtered for high statistical significance (p_val_adj < 2.2e-16) and then ranked by their descending log₂ fold change (avg_log2FC).

(3) Δpercentage + P-value: Genes were first filtered for high significance (p_val_adj < 2.2e-16) and subsequently ranked by the descending difference in expression percentage (pct.1 – pct.2), where pct.1 is the proportion of cells expressing the gene in the target cluster and pct.2 is the proportion in all other clusters.

(3) Δpercentage + P-value: Genes were first filtered for high significance (p_val_adj < 2.2e-16) and subsequently ranked by the descending difference in expression percentage (pct.1 – pct.2), where pct.1 is the proportion of cells expressing the gene in the target cluster and pct.2 is the proportion in all other clusters.

The strict P-value threshold of 2.2e-16 represents the minimum value for double-precision numbers in R.

Number of Genes: For each ranking method, we tested the impact of providing different numbers of top genes as input to the LLMs, including lists of 10, 20, and 30 genes.

Evaluated large language models

This study evaluated a total of 11 specific model endpoints from seven prominent large language model families, as of 11 August 2025. The evaluated models were as follows: OpenAI’s GPT-5 [11] and GPT-4o [46]; Anthropic’s Claude Opus 4.1 (claude-opus-4-1-20250805) [10] and Claude 3.7 Sonnet (claude-3-7-sonnet-20250219) [47]; Google’s Gemini 2.5 Flash [12] and Gemini 2.0 Flash [48]; xAI’s Grok-4 (grok-4-0709) [13] and Grok-3 [49]; as well as DeepSeek-Reasoner [9], Doubao (doubao-seed-1-6-250615) [14], and Kimi (kimi-k2-turbo-preview) [15].

L‌LM API calls and parameter selection

All interactions with the LLMs were conducted via their official APIs. A preliminary evaluation was conducted across five temperature settings (0.0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, and 1.0) to assess the impact on annotation accuracy. As no significant difference in accuracy was observed across these settings, we prioritized output stability and reproducibility for the main benchmark analyses (Supplementary Fig. S1a). It is well established that lower temperature settings reduce output randomness and improve deterministic behavior, with a temperature of 0.0 producing the most consistent results [50]. Therefore, a fixed temperature of 0.0 was selected for all models that allowed this parameter to be set. A notable exception was OpenAI’s GPT-5, which did not allow temperature modification and operated at its default setting.

Regarding prompt strategies, prior studies have shown no significant difference in annotation accuracy among basic prompting, chain-of-thought, and repeated prompting [19]. Thus, to simplify the workflow, reduce computational cost, and ensure a fair comparison across different models, we adopted the basic prompt strategy, inspired by prior work suggesting its sufficiency for this task. This strategy involves supplying the model with the necessary background information and marker gene lists in a single request, without additional reasoning steps or multi-turn interactions.

We designed two unified prompt templates for broad cell type and cell subtype identification. The final design of these templates was guided by a series of systematic evaluations. Key principles of the final design include (1) Adherence to established nomenclature, which was enforced by explicitly instructing the models to use standardized names from the Cell Ontology (CL) [51]. (2) Inclusion of essential biological context. This includes the tissue of origin (e.g. brain) to ensure that retrieved cell types are relevant to the specified organ, and, for subtype annotation tasks, the parent cell type to guide fine-grained classification. (3) Inclusion of species context (e.g. human) was incorporated into the final prompt design. (4) Use of the top 30 upregulated marker genes per cluster, identified via Wilcoxon rank-sum tests and ranked by log₂ fold change, as input.

The template for broad cell type identification is structured as follows:

Identify cell types of [species] [tissue_name] cells using the following markers separately for each row. You MUST use standardized cell type names from the Cell Ontology (CL). If no exact CL term exists, use the most specific CL term available and add descriptive modifiers. Only provide the cell type name. Do not include any numbers or extra annotations before the name. Do not include any explanatory text, introductory phrases, or descriptions. IMPORTANT: Return exactly [num_clusters] lines, one for each row. Some can be a mixture of multiple cell types.

[marker_data].

In this template, the placeholders “species,” “tissue_name,” and “num_clusters” are dynamically replaced with the organism’s name, tissue of origin, and the number of clusters, respectively. The “marker_data” placeholder is populated with the top 30 marker genes for each cluster.

For cell subtype annotation, a similar template is employed. It introduces an additional placeholder, “celltype_name,” which specifies the parent cell type to guide the model toward a more fine-grained classification while strictly adhering to the CL. The template for this task is as follows:

Identify detailed cell subtypes for [celltype_name] in [species] [tissue_name] cells using the following markers, provided separately for each row. You MUST use standardized cell subtype names from the Cell Ontology (CL). If no exact CL term exists, use the most specific CL term available and add descriptive modifiers. Only output the cell subtype name. Do not include any numbers or extra annotations before the name. Do not include any explanatory text, introductory phrases, or descriptions. IMPORTANT: Return exactly [num_clusters] lines, one for each row. Note: Some rows may represent a mixture of multiple subtypes.

[marker_data].

Computationally intensive analyses on a dataset subset

To ensure computational feasibility for a series of computationally intensive analyses—specifically, assessing model robustness, stability, and practical utility (time and cost)—we randomly selected a subset of datasets. For the broad cell type evaluation, this subset included the Mouse Brain, Mouse Kidney, and Human Colorectal Cancer datasets. For the subtype evaluation, we focused on Neocortical neuron cells, Renal tubular epithelial cells, and Colorectal Cancer T cells.

Gene substitution test

To systematically evaluate model robustness against noisy or incomplete gene sets, we designed a gene substitution experiment. For each cluster’s top-30 marker gene list, we specifically tested the impact of losing the least informative markers. To do this, we progressively replaced the lowest-ranked 6, 12, 18, and 24 genes from the tail of the list, corresponding to substitution rates of 20%, 40%, 60%, and 80%, respectively. This substitution procedure was performed using two distinct pools of replacement genes. First, replacement genes were randomly sampled from the set of non–highly variable genes (non-HVGs). Second, a parallel set of lists was created using replacement genes randomly sampled from the pool of Highly Variable Genes (HVGs). For this HVG-based substitution, the sampling pool for each cluster was curated to exclude all genes identified as markers for that specific cluster by the differential expression analysis. To ensure a fair comparison, the exact same sets of substituted gene lists were used as input for all evaluated models. (Supplementary Table S2).

Gene addition test

To complement the substitution analysis, we designed a gene addition experiment to test model robustness against irrelevant information. Starting with the original top-30 marker gene lists, we created augmented lists by appending 3, 9, and 15 additional genes to the end of each list, corresponding to 10%, 30%, and 50% noise addition, respectively. These appended genes were sampled from two distinct pools. First, appended genes were randomly sampled from the non-HVG pool. Second, a parallel set of lists was created using appended genes randomly sampled from the HVG pool. As with the substitution test, the HVG sampling pool for each cluster was curated to exclude all genes identified as markers for that specific cluster. To ensure a fair comparison, the exact same sets of modified gene lists were used as input for all evaluated models (Supplementary Table S2).

Reproducibility and stability analysis

To verify the deterministic behavior and output consistency of the LLMs at a fixed temperature of 0.0, we conducted a stability analysis. Each input gene list was submitted to each LLM 5 consecutive times. We then evaluated the stability of the outputs by examining the consistency of the resulting annotation scores across the five trials for each input.

Computational time and financial cost analysis

To evaluate the practical utility of each LLM, we benchmarked their computational time and financial cost. For each API call, we measured the wall-clock time from the moment the request was sent to the moment a complete response was received to capture the end-to-end latency, and we programmatically tracked the number of input and output tokens to calculate the financial cost. To specifically assess how these metrics scale with input size, we systematically analyzed the relationship between the number of input marker genes (10, 20, and 30) and the resulting response time and financial cost. The financial cost was calculated based on the official pricing models for each API endpoint as of August 2025 (Supplementary Table S3). For models priced in Chinese yuan (CNY), all costs were standardized to US dollars (USD) using an exchange rate of 1 CNY to 0.14 USD.

Application of traditional computational tools

SingleR (v2.8.0) annotation was performed using default parameters [6]. Depending on the sample species, its built-in Human Primary Cell Atlas or MouseRNAseqData reference datasets were utilized [52, 53]. While SingleR provides annotations at single-cell resolution, a final cluster-level annotation was determined through a majority voting scheme. For each cluster, the cell type label most frequently assigned by SingleR to the cells within that cluster was adopted as the definitive annotation for the entire cluster.

Cellmarker2.0 annotation was performed by submitting the same marker gene lists used for the LLMs to its web service [7]. From the results, the highest-scoring predicted cell type for each cluster was selected and subsequently mapped to a unified nomenclature for comparison.

ScType (v1.0) annotation was performed using its tissue-specific marker gene database with default parameters [8]. Expert-annotated cluster labels served as input, yielding cluster-level annotations.

Evaluation metrics and statistical analysis

To ensure an objective and robust evaluation while addressing the critical challenge of nomenclature inconsistencies (Supplementary Table S4), we developed an ontology-aware scoring system based on the CL [51].

We constructed a comprehensive cell type normalization mapping table to standardize all ground-truth and predicted cell type names. Using the Ontology Lookup Service (OLS) R package, each unique cell type term was automatically queried against the CL database to retrieve the best-matching canonical CL term along with its standardized name and OBO identifier. The automatically generated mapping table then underwent rigorous manual review and correction by domain experts to ensure accuracy, particularly for handling ambiguous terms and mixed annotations. For each successfully mapped cell type, we extracted the complete hierarchical path from the cell ontology root to the specific term using the ontologyIndex R package, with paths truncated before overly general terms (e.g. “eukaryotic cell”) to maintain meaningful specificity (Supplementary Table S5).

A hierarchical scoring logic was then applied to evaluate the relationship between the standardized prediction and the ground-truth label:

Perfect Match (Score = 1.0): Awarded when the predicted cell type was identical to, or a more specific subtype of, the ground-truth cell type within the ontology hierarchy.

Partial Match (Score = 0.5): Awarded in three distinct scenarios: (1) the prediction is an ancestor (a broader parent category) of the ground-truth term in the ontology hierarchy; (2) the prediction and ground truth share a common ancestor that is not on a predefined list of overly generic terms (e.g. “cell,” “neural cell”), thereby establishing a meaningful sibling or cousin relationship; (3) the prediction is a mixed annotation (e.g. “T cell | B cell”) where at least one of the constituent terms has a valid relationship (perfect or partial) with the ground truth.

No Match (Score = 0.0): Assigned if no hierarchical relationship was found.

Ensemble annotation strategy for performance enhancement

To improve annotation accuracy and robustness beyond the capabilities of any single model, we developed an ensemble strategy. This method integrated the annotations from the four LLMs identified in our benchmark as having the best overall performance: Kimi-k2, GPT-5, Claude-4.1, and Grok-4.

The ensemble process was actualized via a final, integrative LLM call, for which the Kimi-k2 model was designated as the integrator. For each cell cluster, the individual annotations from the four selected models were compiled and provided as input. The integrator model was guided by a prompt structured around a multi-step reasoning framework: first, the model was prompted to perform a biological plausibility check, effectively steering it to disregard any prediction whose Cell Ontology ID is not a descendant of a declared lineage root for the given species and tissue. Following this prompt-guided validation, the model was instructed to consolidate the remaining synonymous predictions and then determine the final annotation by majority vote. To ensure reproducibility, the prompt also included a deterministic tie-breaking rule for cases where the vote resulted in a tie. This rule instructed the model to select the prediction from the first model in a predefined sequence: Kimi-k2, GPT-5, Claude-4.1, and Grok-4.

A single, unified prompt template was used for this integration step. The prompt was structured as follows, with placeholders populated dynamically for each query:

Integrate multiple AI model predictions to determine the final cell type for [species] [tissue_name] cells. Predictions are provided separately for each cell type. Do not consolidate predictions that have a hierarchical relationship (e.g., parent–child classes in Cell Ontology) into a single entity. Before voting, filter out any prediction whose Cell Ontology ID is not a descendant of the declared lineage root for [species] [tissue_name]. Determine the final annotation by majority vote. If this vote results in a tie, select the prediction from the first model listed. You MUST return a standardized cell type name from the Cell Ontology (CL). Consolidate synonymous predictions into a single entity before voting. Only output the final cell type name. Do not include any numbers or extra annotations before the name. Do not include any explanatory text, introductory phrases, or descriptions. IMPORTANT: Return exactly [num_clusters] lines, one for each row.

[prediction_data].

In this template, “prediction_data” was populated with the outputs of the four elite models, labeled generically to avoid biasing the integrator model.

Analysis of disease-associated cell types

To evaluate model performance on clinically relevant and complex cell populations, we performed a targeted analysis on four disease-specific datasets. These datasets included Hepatocellular Carcinoma, middle temporal gyrus from Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) patients, Breast Cancer, and lung tissue from COVID-19 patients. For each dataset, we identified the target cell population by directly using the expert annotations provided in the accompanying cell metadata from the original studies: Malignant cells, Disease-Associated Microglia (DAM), Cancer-Associated Fibroblasts (CAFs), and Pathological Fibroblasts, respectively. Consistent with the optimal strategy identified in our upstream benchmarks, the top 30 marker genes ranked by descending log₂ fold change for each disease population were used as input for the LLMs and the ensemble model. For this specific analysis, we employed two prompt variations to assess the impact of nomenclature constraints. The first was identical to the standard subtype prompt, enforcing strict adherence to CL terms. The second was a relaxed version from which the instruction mandating CL-standardized names was removed. The performance on this task was assessed using a specialized scoring system tailored to disease-state annotation. A score of 1.0 was awarded for correctly identifying the specific pathological cell state (e.g. Cancer-Associated Fibroblast). A score of 0.5 was given for annotations that correctly identified the cell’s lineage and a reactive state but lacked the specific disease context (e.g. Activated Stromal Fibroblast). A score of 0.0 was assigned for annotations that were either of a completely incorrect lineage or were too general to capture the pathological state (e.g. Fibroblast).

All performance comparisons were based on scores from this system, and the statistical significance of performance differences between models was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Results

Systematic benchmarking of upstream analysis and technical parameters for LLM-based annotation

To establish a robust and optimized framework for LLM-based cell annotation, we conducted a comprehensive benchmark of LLM technical parameters and upstream bioinformatic strategies. As a preliminary step, we evaluated the effect of the temperature hyperparameter. While we observed no statistically significant performance changes on average across a range of settings from 0.0 to 1.0 (Supplementary Fig. S1a), our per-model analysis revealed that this average concealed important individual differences. Although most models performed stably across temperatures, we found a few isolated cases of significant performance decline at higher temperatures (Supplementary Fig. S1b). Therefore, to ensure deterministic and reproducible outputs [50] and to avoid these occasional performance drops, we fixed the temperature at 0.0 for all subsequent experiments.

With this stable baseline, we focused on optimizing the input marker gene list. We first compared methods for identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Markers from the Wilcoxon rank-sum test improved performance for cell subtype annotation and yielded significantly higher accuracy for broad cell type classification compared to the t-test (Fig. 2a, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P < .001; Supplementary Fig. S2a). Next, we assessed three marker filtering strategies: selecting genes by statistical significance alone (P-value), or by combining P-value with a measure of effect size (log₂ fold change, log₂FC) or expression specificity (Δpercentage of expressing cells). The P-value + log₂FC criterion, which prioritizes genes with both statistical significance and substantial expression magnitude change, proved superior. It significantly outperformed the P-value-only approach for broad cell types (Fig. 2b, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P < .01) and significantly surpassed both alternative methods for the challenging task of subtype identification (Fig. 2b, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P < .01, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P < .05). Regarding the number of input genes, increasing from 10 to 30 markers boosted performance for both broad and subtype annotation, with a significant improvement observed between 10 and 30 genes for broad types (Fig. 2c, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P < .05; Supplementary Fig. S2b). We then used top 30 marker genes for all downstream benchmark analyses. Finally, we evaluated the impact of including species context in the prompt. While the aggregate performance showed a non-significant positive trend (Supplementary Fig. S2c), a more granular, per-model analysis revealed a more complex and mixed result (Supplementary Fig. S2d).

We then evaluated the robustness of LLMs to contaminated marker lists. We first tested this using non–highly variable genes (non-HVGs) as the source of noise. For broad cell types, LLM performance was remarkably resilient, remaining stable even with 60% of markers substituted with random genes, and only declining significantly at 80% substitution (Fig. 2d, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P < .001). Subtype annotation was far more sensitive, with accuracy degrading significantly when the substitution rate reached 60% (Fig. 2d, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P < .01). Similarly, when non-informative “noise” genes were added to the marker list, broad-level annotation was unaffected across all noise levels tested. In contrast, subtype annotation showed more sensitivity. Its performance remained stable with 10% noise but exhibited a statistically significant decline at a 30% addition rate compared to the baseline (Supplementary Fig. S3a, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, P < .05). Furthermore, we conducted a complementary robustness analysis using Highly Variable Genes (HVGs) as the source of noise (Supplementary Fig. S3b, c). This more stringent analysis revealed a reversed sensitivity profile compared to the non-HVG test. Broad cell type annotation proved highly sensitive to this noise, showing significant performance drops with both 20% gene substitution (P < .001) and 10% gene addition (P < .01). In stark contrast, cell subtype annotation was remarkably robust, showing no significant decline until 80% substitution (P < .05) and remaining highly stable even when adding up to 50% irrelevant HVGs.

Beyond the optimization of biological inputs and robustness assessments, we evaluated key operational metrics critical for the practical implementation of LLMs. We assessed annotation reproducibility over five independent runs and found that while most models were highly consistent, some exhibited greater variability, and LLM outputs for subtypes were generally more unstable than for broad types (Supplementary Fig. S3d, e). Regarding operational efficiency, response time and cost varied substantially across models. Notably, faster models were not always cheaper; e.g. Claude-4.1 provided rapid responses but at a high cost (Fig. 2e, f). Conversely, models like DeepSeek-R1 were highly cost-effective but had longer response times. These trade-offs are critical for selecting appropriate models for different research scales and budgetary constraints.

Performance evaluation of LLMs in broad cell type annotation

Qualitative assessment on a mouse colon dataset revealed striking differences in annotation fidelity. Top-tier LLMs such as Grok-4 almost perfectly recapitulated the expert-annotated ground truth, accurately delineating closely related mesenchymal lineages such as pericytes, smooth muscle cells, and interstitial cells of Cajal (Fig. 3a). In contrast, ScType failed to resolve these fine distinctions, erroneously merging both pericytes and interstitial cells of Cajal into the smooth muscle cells category. Furthermore, it collapsed the distinct fibroblast population into an overly broad stromal cells group (Fig. 3a). This observed dichotomy—precise annotation from top-tier LLMs versus widespread misclassification and loss of granularity from traditional tools—was consistent across the models evaluated (Supplementary Fig. S4).

We expanded our benchmarking analysis across a total of 23 human and mouse tissue datasets, encompassing a wider range of biological contexts and technical conditions (Supplementary Table S1). Our analyses revealed substantial variation in annotation performance across tissue types. Structurally homogeneous tissues (e.g. spleen, pancreas) showed consistently high accuracy across all models, while highly heterogeneous tissues (e.g. brain, marrow) with greater cellular diversity presented significant annotation challenges (Fig. 3b).

Across all datasets, LLMs achieved substantially higher average performance than traditional bioinformatics tools, with statistically significant differences observed in average annotation scores (Fig. 3c). More importantly, our benchmark revealed clear performance stratification among the LLMs themselves. A top tier of models emerged, with a leading group consisting of Kimi-k2, GPT-5, Claude-4.1, and Grok-4 consistently delivering the highest perfect match rates of 83.5%, 80.6%, 79.5%, and 79.0%, respectively (Fig. 3d). Performance variations were evident even within model families, with newer versions typically outperforming older ones, such as the substantial improvements observed from GPT-4o to GPT-5, reflecting significant advances in newer generation models’ capacity for processing biomedical information.

Performance evaluation of LLMs in cell subtype annotation

The challenge of automated annotation is significantly amplified when resolving closely related cell subtypes. A representative case study on a human kidney dataset revealed striking qualitative differences in performance. While the expert reference delineated a detailed continuum of renal tubule epithelial subtypes, traditional tools struggled to match this resolution. ScType, for instance, failed to resolve these fine distinctions, collapsing populations like the proximal straight tubule and proximal convoluted tubule into a single proximal tubule cells category and thereby losing critical subtype-level information. In stark contrast, a top-tier LLM like Grok-4 successfully recapitulated the expert annotation with high fidelity, correctly distinguishing between these subtypes (Fig. 4a). This pattern was broadly consistent, with other leading LLMs providing granular labels while traditional methods offered vague or incorrect classifications (Supplementary Fig. S5).

This qualitative superiority was validated by our quantitative benchmarking. The performance gulf between LLMs and traditional methods widened dramatically for subtype annotation. Based on average scores, LLMs formed a distinct high-performance group, while traditional tools performed near baseline (Fig. 4c). This disparity is quantified by the perfect match rates, where the top model, Grok-4 (86.2%), was over four times more accurate than the lowest-performing traditional method, SingleR (13.5%) (Fig. 4d).

Crucially, the benchmark revealed a new, task-specific performance hierarchy among LLMs, distinct from that observed for broad-level typing. A clear top tier of models emerged, consisting of Grok-4, and GPT-5, which consistently delivered the highest performance. Notably, a model’s excellence in broad typing did not guarantee top performance in subtype resolution, as a clear performance hierarchy shift was observed between the two tasks (Supplementary Fig. S6). For instance, Kimi-k2, which was the top-performing model for broad cell types, was outperformed by Grok-4 in the more demanding subtype context. This highlights that model capabilities vary with annotation granularity, making task-specific benchmarking essential for selecting the optimal tool for high-resolution single-cell analysis.

An ensemble strategy overcomes individual model limitations for robust annotation

To overcome the limitations and inherent biases of any single LLM, we developed an elite ensemble strategy that integrates annotations from the four models with the best overall performance in cell annotation: Kimi-k2, GPT-5, Claude-4.1, and Grok-4 (Fig. 5a). Quantitative evaluation revealed the clear benefits of this approach. While for broad cell types the ensemble performed on par with the best individual models, for the more challenging cell subtype task, it achieved the highest median performance and exhibited greater stability (Fig. 5b).

The elite ensemble annotation framework and performance comparison. (a) Flowchart of the ensemble annotation framework. Marker genes for a given cell population are independently annotated by four top-performing “elite” LLMs. The resulting predictions are then processed by an ensemble module to obtain the final annotation. (b) Box plots comparing the annotation performance (Average Score) of the ensemble strategy against its four constituent elite models (Kimi-k2, GPT-5, Grok-4, and Claude-4.1) for both broad cell type and cell subtype tasks.

Crucially, the strength of our ensemble extends beyond simple voting to a more sophisticated process we term “Ensemble Reasoning.” This capability is enabled by guiding the integrator model through a multi-step reasoning framework embedded in our prompt. This prompt structure steers the model to first perform a biological validation step before consolidating votes. Consequently, the system can achieve correct results even in complex scenarios where these simple rules would otherwise lead to errors. A definitive example is the annotation of tissue-resident macrophages within a broader myeloid cell population. In this case, the four elite models returned four distinct predictions in sequence: fibroblast, epithelial cell, tissue-resident macrophage, and adventitial cell. In this specific example, a system relying solely on majority voting with a sequential tie-breaking rule would have failed, erroneously adopting the prediction of the first model in the sequence: “fibroblast.” However, our ensemble strategy successfully returned the correct annotation, “tissue-resident macrophage.” This success highlights the power of its robust, multi-step reasoning: the module first executes the biological plausibility check (as defined in our Methods), which successfully filters out the three biologically incongruent cell types (fibroblast, epithelial cell, and adventitial cell) that are not descendants of the ground-truth lineage. This prompt-guided filtering effectively demonstrates the system’s robust reasoning capability, critical for accurate annotation in complex biological datasets (Supplementary Table S6).

Nevertheless, the ensemble strategy’s performance is contingent on the input from its constituent models, showing limitations when faced with challenging pathological states that cause widespread model failure. This is evident in the annotation of pathological fibroblasts in COVID-19 lung tissue. In this case, insufficient correct predictions from individual models prevented a precise consensus, causing the ensemble to fall back to a broader, subtype-unspecific annotation instead of the exact pathological state. This demonstrates that the ensemble’s specificity can be compromised when the collective signal from individual models is too weak (Supplementary Fig. S7).

DeepCellSeek: an integrated platform for state-of-the-art cell annotation

To translate our comprehensive benchmark findings and the superior performance of the ensemble strategy into a practical resource for the research community, we developed DeepCellSeek. This integrated solution is provided as both a user-friendly interactive web platform (available at https://deepcellseek.kiz.ac.cn/) and an open-source R package (Supplementary Fig. S8). A cornerstone feature of DeepCellSeek is the implementation of our validated elite ensemble strategy, which leverages the collective intelligence of the models with the best overall performance to deliver the most robust and accurate cell annotations. The platform also allows users to inspect and compare outputs from the individual top-tier LLMs, ensuring transparent cross-validation. By providing both the web tool and the R package, we aim to empower researchers to harness the advanced ensemble reasoning capabilities identified in our study, moving beyond the limitations of single models to achieve more reliable and nuanced cell type annotation in their own single-cell datasets.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically benchmarked a suite of leading LLMs for single-cell annotation, demonstrating their profound superiority over traditional computational methods for both broad cell type and fine-grained subtype identification. Recently, the field has seen a surge of innovative approaches, including multi-LLM consensus frameworks, and agent-based systems that integrate external databases or aim to discover novel cell types [54–56]. Concurrently, initial benchmarking studies have begun to map the general performance landscape of different models [57]. While these pioneering efforts establish the potential of LLMs, our study provides the first deep and systematic framework for optimizing their application, quantifying their robustness, and establishing input generation best practices, thereby offering a practical roadmap for the research community.

The advantage of LLMs in this domain stems from their ability to synthesize immense volumes of unstructured scientific literature within their training corpora. Unlike traditional tools that rely on structured databases, LLMs learn to associate genes with specific cell types, biological processes, and tissue contexts by recognizing patterns from countless publications. This allows them to interpret a list of marker genes not as isolated identifiers, but as a collective signature whose biological meaning is derived from this learned context.

Our study underscores that unlocking the full potential of LLMs for cell annotation is contingent not only on the model itself, but also on the systematic optimization of upstream bioinformatic strategies. We established a set of best practices that consistently enhance performance across both broad and fine-grained annotation tasks. Specifically, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test provides a more robust foundation for marker identification. As a non-parametric method, it is inherently more robust to the non-normal, and often zero-inflated, distribution of scRNA-seq data. Compared to the t-test, which assumes normality, this likely allows it to identify more biologically relevant markers with fewer false positives, thus leading to higher annotation accuracy. Furthermore, our benchmark demonstrates that providing richer context—by increasing the number of marker genes to at least 20 and including species information—yields superior accuracy. Crucially, our results reveal that prioritizing genes with both high statistical significance and a large magnitude of expression change (log₂FC) is a universally superior strategy. This combined criterion significantly outperformed using significance alone for both broad cell types and fine-grained subtypes. We reason that while statistically significant lineage markers are foundational, incorporating log₂FC provides the LLM with a more functionally informative signature. This becomes particularly critical for distinguishing closely related subtypes, where identity often hinges on quantitative differences in key functional genes rather than the simple presence or absence of a marker. By emphasizing the magnitude of change, this strategy better highlights the core drivers of a cell’s distinct biological state.

Our benchmark revealed clear performance stratification among LLMs, which generally correlates with their release dates and presumed model scale (Fig. 1a), reflecting the rapid pace of AI development. This rapid improvement is exemplified by the substantial performance gain observed from GPT-4o to its successor, GPT-5. The performance differences are likely attributable to proprietary factors that are not publicly disclosed for most commercial models, such as the precise composition of their training data, unique architectural designs, or overall parameter scale [58]. It is plausible that superior models have been exposed to more comprehensive biomedical corpora or possess more advanced reasoning architectures. Furthermore, beyond model-inherent factors, we observed that performance varied across different tissues. This may reflect disparities in the volume of available research for certain tissues within the models’ training data, highlighting a potential knowledge bias against less-studied biological systems [59].

To overcome the limitations and inherent biases of any single LLM, we developed an elite ensemble strategy. At a statistical level, the ensemble mitigates sporadic, model-specific errors through a majority-voting process, where individual erroneous predictions are treated as uncorrelated noise that can be canceled out [60]. Biologically, the ensemble integrates the complementary knowledge bases of the different LLMs. We observed that individual models often capture cellular identity at varying levels of granularity; the ensemble excels at synthesizing these multilevel annotations to construct a final identity that is more comprehensive and precise. Most remarkably, the strategy demonstrated the model’s capacity to successfully follow complex, context-dependent guidance that goes beyond simple voting. We observed critical instances where the integrator model leveraged the biological context provided in the prompt (e.g. the parent cell lineage of “myeloid cell”). As explicitly guided by our prompt, the model first conducted a validation step; it was steered to use the provided lineage context as a strict criterion, leading it to discard biologically incongruent predictions like “fibroblast” and “epithelial cell.” This prompt-guided filtering effectively served as a veto against an erroneous consensus. Consequently, only the contextually correct minority prediction remained as a valid candidate, thereby preventing catastrophic annotation failures when most individual models are misled.

Despite a series of successes, our study also identifies key limitations. We acknowledge that because our benchmark relies on public datasets, some of these data may have been part of the models’ training corpora, a potential source of performance overestimation. Furthermore, generalist LLMs still have room for improvement in the depth and precision of their biological context understanding, sometimes struggling to capture subtle state differences or annotate rare cell types [61]. These challenges become particularly acute when applying the models to complex pathological cell states. As our results demonstrate, even the most advanced models struggle when inputting a marker gene list from disease-specific cells, such as cancer-associated fibroblasts. This may be because pathological cell states are vastly underrepresented in the general scientific literature that forms the bulk of the models’ training data. Consequently, the models may assign a lower probability to these correct but rare annotations, favoring more common, canonical cell type definitions instead. Therefore, we suggest that the direct application of current general-purpose LLMs for annotating novel or pathological cell types in clinical research should be approached with caution and always be subject to expert validation.

These limitations define two critical directions for future research. First is the development of domain-specific LLMs tailored specifically for single-cell analysis. Such models can internalize deeper biological principles through targeted pre-training on comprehensive and specialized biomedical knowledge bases, encompassing genomics, transcriptomics, cellular biology literature, and related databases [62]. Second is the creation of more context-aware prompt strategies that can integrate broader biological context, such as cell–cell interactions, signaling pathway activities, and even patient clinical information [63]. Bridging this gap between general-purpose AI and the deep domain expertise required for translational research will propel the evolution of LLM-based single-cell analysis paradigms—from efficient automated annotation tools to powerful discovery engines capable of assisting in, or even driving, the formulation and validation of novel biological hypotheses, unlocking new possibilities for discoveries in the single-cell field.

Conclusions

In this work, we systematically benchmarked seven leading large language model families for cell type annotation in scRNA-seq data, demonstrating their clear superiority over established bioinformatics tools. We identified a top tier of models—notably Kimi-k2, GPT-5, Claude-4.1, and Grok-4—and leveraged their collective strength in an elite ensemble strategy to achieve state-of-the-art performance. To translate these findings into a practical resource, we developed DeepCellSeek, an open-access R package and web platform. Our findings establish a new performance standard for automated cell annotation and provide a practical roadmap for leveraging LLMs in single-cell research. Ultimately, this work paves the way for the evolution of LLMs from simple annotation tools into powerful discovery engines, capable of assisting in the formulation and validation of novel biological hypotheses and unlocking new frontiers in single-cell omics.
