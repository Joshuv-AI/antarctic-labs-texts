Deep-learning-based diagnostic models for transcutaneous oxygen pressure-defined peripheral arterial disease and lower extremity wound healing in patients with diabetic foot
Authors: Ming-Feng Tsai, Chen-Shen Shih, Yu-Fan Chen, Yu-Chang Chu, Chieh-Ming Yu, Wen-Teng Yao, Ya-Shu Chan, Chia-Meng Yu, Shu-Tien Huang, Liong-Rung Liu, Lang-Hua Chiu, Yueh-Hung Lin, Chin-Yi Yang, Kung-Chen Ho, Wen-Chen Huang, Kwang-Yi Tung, Fei-Hung Hung, Hung-Wen Chiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFew studies have evaluated peripheral artery disease (PAD) and wound healing in patients with lower extremity wounds using a convolutional neural network (CNN)-based deep learning algorithm. We aimed to establish a CNN deep-learning model based on transcutaneous oxygen pressure (TcPO2)-annotated wound images for detecting PAD and wound healing in diabetic patients with lower extremity wounds.MethodsAn extensive database of 1,407 original images from 77 patients with lower extremity wounds were collected to produce CNN deep-learning models (i.e., GoogleNet, ResNet 101V2 and EfficientNet). A framework was constructed, including image pre-processing and TcPO2-based grouping, to establish an optimal training model and to validate each model’s performance for detecting PAD or wound healing.ResultsIn the established CNN deep-learning models, the ResNet101V2 model with original wound images showed the best performance for detecting PAD (sensitivity 93.08%, accuracy 86.20%) or wound healing (sensitivity 96.76%, accuracy 88.14%), although the performance of GoogleNet and EfficientNet models also demonstrated high sensitivity and accuracy.ConclusionsCNN deep-learning algorithm based on objective TcPO2 values and image preprocessing is a promising model for detecting PAD and wound healing for lower extremity wounds, providing an easily implemented and more objective and reliable computation tool for physicians to automatically identify PAD and monitor wound healing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03477-2.Keywords: Convolutional neural network, Lower extremity wound, Peripheral artery disease, Transcutaneous oxygen pressure, Wound healing

Background

In the clinical practice of foot care, peripheral arterial disease (PAD) is recognized as an important diagnostic component for managing diabetic foot (DF), and approximately 50% of patients with DF will have PAD [1]. The identification of macrovascular disease in many patients with DF is often challenging, primarily due to the coexistence of diabetic sensorimotor neuropathy, medial arterial sclerosis in the lower limbs and autonomic neuropathy [2, 3]. Such complications may hinder the timely diagnosis of PAD [4]. The identification of PAD at late stage is associated with a two-fold increase in the risk of lower limb minor amputation among patients with DF [5]. Minor amputation is able to increase the risk of subsequent major amputation ten times [6]. Additionally, the presence of PAD significantly influences the prognosis and mortality associated with DF conditions [7], with an increase in mortality rates of up to 39%–80% within 5 years [8]. Therefore, early diagnosis of PAD is essential for formulating appropriate treatment plans for patients with DF.

Currently, the ankle-brachial index (ABI) is conventionally determined by calculating the ratio of the highest of the dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial artery systolic pressure to the highest of the left and right brachial systolic pressures, which is a widely employed non-invasive technique for PAD screening [9]. An ABI value of < 0.9 or > 1.3 is regarded as normal according to international recommendations [10], although the previous study reported that detecting PAD with ABI may produce high false-negative rates due to calcification of vessel that limits the compressibility of the artery [11]. Transcutaneous oxygen pressure (TcPO2) is another well-established non-invasive method for assessing tissue perfusion/oxygenation, particularly in patients with critical limb ischemia. This technique provides information on oxygen supply to the microvascular circulation by recording the partial pressure of oxygen on the skin surface [12]. There was no evidence retrieved on the adverse events related to usage of TcPO2. Several brands of TcPO2 device in the market had received approval from USFDA and classified as class II medical device [13]. As outlined in the wound, ischemia, and foot infection (WIFI) classification system, DF can be categorized according to TcPO2 levels to predict PAD [14]. A TcPO2 value below 60 mmHg is defined as PAD [15]. Surgeons can also employ TcPO2 measurements to ascertain the amputation level in patients with DF [16]. TcPO2 values below 30 mmHg indicate poor tissue oxygenation, often associated with severe PAD and a high risk of non-healing wounds or the need for above-ankle amputation [17, 18]. The TcPO2 measurement is performed by applying a sensor to the skin using a flat, double-sided adhesive ring. Oxygen diffuses according to its pressure gradient (PaO2) from the capillary loops through the avascular epidermis to the electrode on the skin surface [19]. When PaO2 is normal, there is a direct relationship between TcPO2 values and local perfusion levels. Therefore, a decrease in PaO2 leads to a reduction in TcPO2 measurements [20]. This makes TcPO2 an important indicator of tissue perfusion and oxygenation, which are critical factors in wound healing. However, such non-invasive techniques are highly specialized, costly, and require technologists with training in a specialized vascular lab and/or expensive equipment. Hence, these techniques are not suited to high-throughput PAD screening and surveillance.

In our previous study [21], we proposed the deep-learning-based diagnostic models that are established by ABI (as an objective ground truth)-defined PAD of lower extremity wound images. In the validation diagnosis with additional images of lower extremity wounds, it suggests that the diagnostic models provide an easily implemented, objective and reliable computation tool by which physicians can automatically identify and classify PAD. Although it shows high performance for PAD detection of lower extremity wounds, ABI has been criticized for its poor accuracy and lack of robustness in predicting wound healing [9]. To address this issue, the present study aimed to further establish a modified framework for more accurate detection of PAD and/or wound healing in DF patients with lower extremity wounds using a fine-tuned transfer learning algorithm based on TcPO2 (as an objective ground truth)-defined lower extremity wound images and wound image preprocessing.

Methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective study collected images of lower extremity wounds from 77 patients with DF seen in the department of plastic surgery in our institution between October 2019 and April 2024. We proposed a modified framework for the detection of PAD and wound healing using a convolutional neural network (CNN)-based deep learning algorithm (Fig. 1), including image pre-processing (image resize), grouping (non-PAD, PAD, non-healing wound, and healing wound), establishing a CNN transfer learning model (i.e., GoogleNet, ResNet101V2, EfficientNet), and model validation/performance assessment (precision, sensitivity, specificity and accuracy).

Proposed framework for detection of PAD and wound healing using CNN-based deep-learning architecture and TcPO2-annotated wound images

Ethics considerations

The study protocol complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, and was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of MacKay Memorial Hospital (approval number: 20MMHIS047e, approve date: November 4, 2021). The signed informed consent of patients was obtained to use their images for this research and later publication of the study.

Data collection

A dataset of 1,407 original images was created from 77 DF patients with lower extremity wounds to establish a modified framework for detecting PAD and wound healing. In order to simulate the variability of obtaining images in a clinical setting, no special specifications were established for the conditions of the camera and background light source. The attending physician in each case was responsible for the collection of patient records and wound photographs, and the annotation of image data. TcPO2 values from the Hospital Information System was retrospectively reviewed. Professor Hung-Wen Chiu was responsible for the construction of the CNN, and the execution of deep learning of the image data.

Image pre-processing and grouping

The image pre-processing included image resize. Using XnConvert, the images were resized to 224 × 224 pixels to fit the input of the CNN architecture.

The images were divided into four groups based on the TcPO2 value (as a ground truth) (Fig. 2). Different ground truths were used to classify the images: (1) Images with (794 images) or without (237 images) TcPO2 < 60 mm Hg (PAD or non-PAD) and (2) Images with (858 images) or without (169 images) TcPO2 ≥ 30 (wound healing or hypoxia non-healing).

Wound images of the subtypes. (A) Wounds with/without PAD and (B) with/without wound healing annotated by TcPO2 levels

Since a study suggests that a TcPO2 cut-off value of 30 mmHg can also predict amputation efficiently in patients with PAD [19]. Therefore, image annotated with TcPO2 ≥ 30 may also predict that lower extremity wounds with well-healing may have a lower risk of future major amputation.

Establishing a CNN transfer learning model using GoogleNet, ResNet101V2 or EfficientNet

Of all grouping images, 80% were submitted to GoogleNet, ResNet101V2 and EfficientNet for CNN training to establish optimal models for detecting PAD and wound healing, respectively (Fig. 1). The remaining 20% of all grouping images were used to validate the performance of the established models (Fig. 1).

Performance assessment metrics of the CNN models

Four performance indicators were used to evaluate CNN model-based detections of PAD and wound healing: precision, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, as previously described [22].

Discussion

In our evaluation of the performance of the established CNN deep-learning models used to create a foundation for our CNN deep-learning model based on TcPO2-annotated wound images to detect PAD and wound healing in DF patients with lower extremity wounds, the ResNet101V2 model with original wound images showed the best performance for detecting PAD (sensitivity 93.08%, accuracy 86.20%) or wound healing (sensitivity 96.76%, accuracy 88.14%), although the performance of GoogleNet- and EfficientNet-based models also demonstrated high sensitivity and accuracy. Results of this study support that the CNN deep-learning algorithm based on TcPO2 is a promising model for detecting PAD and wound healing for lower extremity wounds, providing an easily implemented, more objective and reliable computation tool for clinicians to automatically identify PAD and monitor wound healing.

Diagnosing PAD early enables earlier treatment to help slow disease progression, ultimately increasing wound healing [18] and decreasing the risk of major cardiovascular events [23], lower limb amputation [5] and DF-associated prognosis and mortality [7]. However, for 30% of patients with DF seeking medical assistance at tertiary medical centers PAD diagnosis and treatment are still delayed or nonexistent [24]. Although PAD diagnosis in clinical settings requires imaging-based techniques (e.g., diagnostic angiography) [25], the techniques often involve invasive procedures, exposure to contrast material and radiation and/or expensive equipment. The non-invasive approaches available (e.g., ABI or TcPO2) [26, 27], are highly specialized, costly, or time consuming, and require technologists with training in a specialized vascular lab setting. Therefore, another non-invasive screening approach is needed that physicians can use to rapidly identify DF patients at higher risk of PAD during daily activities is needed. To address this need, in the present study, we designed and established an accurately diagnostic model for automatically identifying PAD or wound healing in DF patients with lower extremity wounds using a fine-tuned transfer learning CNN algorithm based on TcPO2-annotated original wound images and wound image resizing (224 × 224 pixels). However, in diabetic patients, a number of metabolic changes lead to alterations in microcirculation – due to processes such as endothelial dysfunction and circulatory disturbances associated with nutritional supply to the skin – and macrocirculation secondary to accelerated atherosclerosis [28]. This indicates that diabetes and its complications may still limit the accuracy of non-invasive diagnoses (e.g., TcPO2 measurement) of micro- and macro-circulatory changes, thereby affecting the accuracy of CNN diagnostic model with TcPO2-defined PAD or wound healing.

Machine learning (ML) models have been developed and employed for the diagnosis of PAD using various data sources such as clinical records [29], interviews/symptom questionnaires/walking distance measures [30], blood samples/Doppler ultrasound data [31], and arterial pulse waveforms [32]. A ML model that uses symptom assessments and six-minute walking-distance test presented fewer obstacles than other models but had a diminished accuracy rate of 69%, necessitating comprehensive evaluation by physicians to obtain symptom scores [30]. Although certain diagnostic ML model-based classification of arterial spectral waveforms for the diagnosis of PAD in the context of diabetes has attained accuracy as high as 88% [31], they are hindered by considerable limitations associated with either the time required (annotating medical images), resource allocation, or the necessity for expert involvement with specialized training to gather the requisite data for model training [29, 31, 32]. Furthermore, an approach with ML and Neural Networks employs computer vision algorithms in conjunction with Doppler waveforms and PAD imaging; however, it requires manual image adjustments, an exceedingly time-consuming process [33]. While a deep learning technique using arterial pulse waveforms has been applied to identify and assess PAD severity, this method is not easier to access than the ABI test [34]. In recent years, with the rapid advancement of computer vision technology, the remarkable effectiveness of deep learning has been demonstrated in the analysis of DF images. Such deep learning-based artificial intelligence (AI) technology has shown great potential in the image recognition and lesion area segmentation of DF [35, 36]. In the respect of lesion area segmentation, the GrabCut algorithm uses texture (color) information and boundary (contrast) information in the image, as long as a small amount of user interaction can get better segmentation results [36]. The GrabCut algorithm uses the Gauss mixture model to model the target and background energy and perform the energy minimization iteratively to better image segmentation. The present study has expanded these findings and further developed CNN deep-learning diagnostic models (GoogleNet, ResNet101V2 and EfficientNet) on the basis of features of original wound images with rapidly manual annotation of TcPO2 (from the Hospital Information System)-defined PAD or wound healing to distinguish lower extremity wounds with PAD from those without PAD, and lower extremity wounds with wound healing from those with hypoxia-related non-healing. In the established CNN deep-learning models, ResNet101V2-based model with TcPO2-annotated original wound images showed the best assessment performance for detecting PAD (sensitivity 93.08%; accuracy 86.20%) or wound healing (sensitivity 96.76%; accuracy 88.14%), although GoogleNet-based and EfficientNet-based models also presented high performance in sensitivity and accuracy values. A similar observation was also found in the assessment performance of the three models for detecting future wound healing in patients with lower extremity wounds. The ResNet101V2 model exhibited strengths in specific configurations, suggesting that architecture depth and layer connectivity should be optimized based on the complexity of input data and target tasks to obtain a better assessment performance [37]. Importantly, it may provide a potential framework for detecting PAD or wound healing to enhance patient management and potentially improve current challenges in the diagnosis and treatment of PAD.

Therapeutic plans for PAD patients are influenced by anatomical locations, symptoms, severity of lesions, results from functional assessments, comorbid conditions, and risks associated with anesthesia and surgical intervention [38]. Despite the existing guidelines and a multidisciplinary management approach, the assessment of the most suitable therapeutic option remains a challenge [39, 40]. Addressing this aspect, the ML algorithm has the potential to introduce new tools that facilitate evidence-based decision-making and assist clinicians in their clinical judgments. Yurkuran et al. [40] report that a radial basis function neural network algorithm has the potential to support evidence-based decision-making processes for the categorization of therapeutic interventions pertaining to PAD. Amato et al. [41] established a ML model that may help to enhance the stratification of patients, thereby aiding in the prevention or anticipation of unplanned readmissions. Compared to these findings, our work may provide a potential CNN-deep-learning-based framework for detecting PAD or wound healing to enhance evidence-based decision-making for management of DF patient with lower extremity wounds, and potentially improve the current challenges in diagnosis and treatment of PAD. The results of these findings are encouraging to further develop new tools to enhance precision medicine in the lower extremity wounds with PAD.

Improving the assessment of patient outcomes and prognosis among those with PAD and lower extremity wounds is a major concern and significant focus is required in order to enhance the detection and diagnosis of PAD. Using medical data, several predictive models have been developed previously for specific cohorts of patients diagnosed with PAD [42–45]. In a retrospective study involving patients diagnosed with PAD, a prognostic scoring system was formulated for assessing the likelihood of amputation and was evaluated using Cox regression analysis [43]. Arruda-Olson et al. [42] established a real-time, individualized risk prediction model that facilitates the stratification of PAD patients’ mortality by subgroups. ML algorithms facilitate the analysis of data without hypothesis, and their application in predicting future mortality among patients with PAD has been documented [45]. Ross et al. [44] formulated an additional model using an ML algorithm to forecast significant adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events in patients diagnosed with PAD, indicating the potential of ML algorithms in predicting complications associated with PAD as well as its mortality. Recently, Cox et al. [46] used the ML model with medical data for the prediction of 30-day amputation risk following lower extremity infrainguinal endovascular interventions for PAD. Somewhat similarly, we further developed CNN deep-learning models based on the features of original wound images with manual annotation of TcPO2 levels to automatically detect future wound healing in patients with lower extremity wounds.

Oxygen modulates wound healing mechanisms, including the proliferation of skin cells, granulation tissue formation, re-epithelialization, angiogenesis, and overall tissue regeneration [47]. However, hypoxic conditions arise frequently within the wound bed, which may slow or prevent these essential healing processes [47]. TcPO2 is a widely recognized non-invasive technique used to evaluate tissue perfusion and oxygenation, and is able to predict PAD and high risk of non-healing [14, 18]. In fact, a TcPO2 value below 60 mmHg is defined as PAD [15], while TcPO2 below 30 mmHg is commonly associated with an increased risk of wound non-healing [17]. In the present study, a TcPO2 cutoff value of ≥ 30 mmHg was used to identify future wound healing. A review study suggested that a TcPO2 cut-off value of 30 mmHg is able to predict amputation efficiently in patients with PAD [19]. Therefore, our framework may also predict that lower extremity wounds with well-healing may have a lower risk of future major amputation, possibly making it a useful tool to identify patients at risk of major amputation and target them for early prevention and intervention to deliver cost-effective medical services. Herein, all CNN deep-learning models based on the features of TcPO2-annotated original wound images are able to automatically detect patients with PAD or future wound healing in patients with lower extremity wounds, with high diagnostic performance, implicating that the present model may reflect a more realistic state of PAD and wound healing in patients with lower extremity wounds.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations. First, the number of wound images used in this study was relatively small for the application of CNN deep-learning algorithms, so the findings may not be generalizable for the identification of PAD or wound healing. Second, other systemic (e.g., anemia, malnutrition, dehydration, hyperglycemia, and medications) and local factors (dressings without antibiofilm action, for example) may profoundly interfere the results of TcPO2-defined PAD or wound healing. Patients with lower extremity wounds included in this study were all pre-annotated with/without PAD or wound healing based on TcPO2 levels, ensuring that PAD was the primary cause of functional impairment rather than other conditions impact function, and, this may also reduce generalizability of the results to patients with other conditions. Nonetheless, results of this study exhibited that this framework-performance in (high sensitivity and accuracy) distinguishing PAD or wound healing, even using only a few TcPO2-annotated wound images.

Conclusion

The framework introduced herein designed based on CNN deep-learning algorithms and TcPO2-annotated wound images provides better performance values (higher sensitivity and accuracy) when applied to identify PAD or wound healing in DF patients with lower extremity wounds, particularly when using the ResNet101V2 algorithm compared to other CNN deep-learning models. Further study should explore the ability of CNN deep-learning models to monitor PAD progression and treatment effectiveness.
