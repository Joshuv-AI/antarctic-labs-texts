Attitude towards menstruation, prevalence of premenstrual syndrome and coping mechanisms among adolescent girls in Kerala, India: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ambily Vijayan Gopika, Sobha George, Brilly Michael Rose, Jeby Jose Olickal, Kavumpurathu Raman Thankappan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComprehensive studies on attitudes towards menstruation and the prevalence of premenstrual syndrome (PMS) and its associated factors among adolescents are limited. Since both attitudes and responses towards PMS are shaped by psychosocial and cultural factors, this study was conducted to assess attitude towards menstruation, prevalence of PMS and coping mechanisms adopted to manage PMS among adolescent girls in Kerala, with a focus on psychosocial experiences rather than the biological aspects of PMS.MethodsAn institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 1100 adolescent girls (aged 13–19 years), selected through multistage cluster sampling. Menstruation-related attitudes were measured using the Menstrual Attitude Questionnaire, which categorises attitudes as positive or negative, while PMS symptoms were assessed using the Premenstrual Symptoms Screening Tool. Multivariable analyses identified the factors associated with attitudes towards menstruation and severe PMS.ResultsIn our study, 51% reported a negative attitude towards menstruation (95% CI 47.8% to 53.8%). Severe PMS was reported by 36.8% (95% CI 33.9% to 39.7%). Severe PMS was significantly associated with negative menstrual attitudes (adjusted prevalence ratio (APR) 1.31, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.54)), nuclear family; defined as those living with only parents and siblings, (APR=1.25, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.51), Muslim religion (APR=1.58, 95% CI 1.03 to 2.42) and low maternal education (APR=1.28, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.59) compared with their counterparts. Adolescents who belonged to the Muslim religion (APR=1.20, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.37) and those from nuclear families (APR=1.21, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.39) were more likely to report negative attitudes towards menstruation compared with their counterparts.ConclusionMore than half of the adolescents reported a negative attitude towards menstruation, and a considerable proportion experienced severe PMS. Implementing targeted educational programmes for adolescents, particularly those from the Muslim religion, nuclear families and with less-educated mothers, may enhance menstrual awareness, promote healthier attitudes and support better coping mechanisms in managing PMS.Keywords: Adolescent, Public Health, Sexual Health

Introduction

Menstruation is a natural physiological and psychosocial process characterised by the cyclical shedding of the uterine lining, typically beginning at menarche, which is defined as the first occurrence of menstruation and marks the onset of reproductive capability. This usually occurs around 12 years of age and continues until perimenopause, occurring usually around the age of 50 years. This reproductive span extends approximately 35–40 years in a woman’s life and symbolises her reproductive capacity. The WHO defines adolescents as individuals aged 10–19 years, and the term ‘adolescent girls’ refers to females within this age group.1 Adolescents account for 16% of the global population2 and one of the most profound physiological transitions for girls during this period is the onset of menstruation. Menstrual attitude refers to an individual’s attitudes and perceptions about menstruation, including levels of acceptance and stigma.3 These attitudes are shaped by cultural, familial and social environments.4 Menstrual attitudes may be positive (viewing menstruation as a natural and manageable process) or negative (perceiving menstruation as debilitating, stressful and shameful). Negative attitudes have been associated with more severe/unmanageable experiences of menstruation-related symptoms, including premenstrual syndrome (PMS), thereby influencing how adolescents cope with these experiences.5

PMS includes a range of physical, cognitive, emotional and behavioural symptoms that occur cyclically during the luteal phase and subside within days of menstruation onset.6 Adolescence is a critical period when beliefs and attitudes towards menstruation are formed, which can directly shape how menstrual symptoms such as PMS are perceived, reported and managed. Emerging evidence suggests that negative attitudes towards menstruation among adolescents are associated with severe experiences of PMS, highlighting the importance of addressing both awareness and perception in this age group. Global differences in PMS prevalence may reflect variation in symptom perception, cultural norms surrounding menstruation and the diagnostic tools used, rather than biological differences in the syndrome itself. A meta-analysis reported PMS rates from 12% in France to 98% in Iran, with an average prevalence of 47.8%.7 Physical symptoms such as fatigue, breast tenderness, abdominal bloating and headaches are frequently reported and often show wide variation based on geographic and cultural context. For example, in Iran, somatic symptoms such as joint and muscle pain are more prominent,8 while studies from Egypt and Jordan show higher reports of breast tenderness and sleep disturbances.9 10

In India, PMS prevalence ranges widely from 14.3% to 74.4%, which has been attributed to differences in study tools used for diagnosis such as the Premenstrual Symptoms Screening Tool (PSST), American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) criteria or self-report checklists, cultural perceptions of menstruation and reporting bias.11 Some studies suggest that low awareness about PMS among adolescents, combined with normalisation of menstrual discomfort, may lead to underreporting, particularly in rural and low-literacy settings. Conversely, studies conducted in urban or semiurban educational institutions often report higher prevalence due to greater awareness and open discussion of symptoms. Social desirability bias and stigma around menstruation also influence whether adolescents are comfortable acknowledging or disclosing their symptoms.11

In a study done in a medical college in Kerala among college girls, the prevalence of PMS was 27.8% and premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD)—a severe, disabling form of PMS characterised by intense emotional and physical symptoms that significantly interfere with daily activities and relationships—was 13.8%. Anger/irritability was found to be the most commonly reported symptom in the study group.12 Studies reveal varied correlations between attitude towards menstruation and PMS, with some indicating a negative relationship while others find no significant association. In a study conducted in Kerala, the students’ most commonly reported coping strategies for reducing PMS included sleeping (81.2%), resting (89.6%), using heat packs (46.5%), listening to music (61.4%) and having a hot shower (52.5%).13

A few regional studies have investigated menstrual health among adolescents in Kerala. One institutional study among college students in Ernakulam district of Kerala reported PMS prevalence of 27.8% and PMDD of 13.8%, with anger/irritability as the most common symptom.12 However, most of these studies were limited to single institutions and did not comprehensively examine the associations between menstrual attitude, PMS and sociodemographic factors across diverse geographic and cultural backgrounds. Kerala, a southern Indian state, was selected for this study due to its distinctive combination of high female literacy rates, favourable health indicators and progressive public health policies. Despite these advantages, menstruation remains a sensitive and often stigmatised topic within many communities in the state. The contrast between Kerala’s overall educational advancement and the persistence of menstrual taboos makes it an ideal setting to explore the intersection of menstrual attitudes, PMS and coping strategies. Moreover, previous studies in Kerala have been limited in scope, often restricted to individual institutions or urban areas. Therefore, this study aimed to assess menstrual attitudes, the prevalence and severity of PMS and coping mechanisms among adolescent girls in Kerala. It also examined factors associated with menstrual attitudes and the severity of PMS.

Methods

Study design and setting

This is an institution-based cross-sectional study conducted in Kollam and Malappuram districts of Kerala state, India.

Study population

The study was conducted among adolescent girls from selected schools and colleges in Kollam and Malappuram districts of Kerala. School participants included students from classes VIII to XII, typically aged 13–17 years, while college participants included first-year and second-year undergraduate students aged 17–19 years.

Study duration

The study was conducted over a 6-month period, from November 2023 to May 2024.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated to estimate the prevalence of negative menstrual attitudes among adolescent girls with adequate precision. Based on a prior study by Wong,4 which reported a prevalence of 14.2% for negative menstrual attitudes among adolescent girls using a validated Menstrual Attitude Questionnaire (MAQ), we used this as our reference prevalence (p=0.142). Assuming a 95% confidence level (Z=1.96) and an absolute error (d) of 3%, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 520. Since cluster sampling was used, a design effect of 2 was applied, resulting in a sample size of 1040. Allowing for non-response and exclusions, we rounded the number to 1064, and subsequently aimed for 28 clusters with ~38 participants each. The calculation was performed using OpenEpi V.3.01, an open-source tool for epidemiologic statistics.

Selection criteria

Inclusion criteria for the study were adolescent girls aged 13–19 years who had attained menarche and provided informed assent or consent to participate. Exclusion criteria included girls aged 10–12 years, as many are unlikely to have attained menarche and may not have consistent menstrual cycles. Additionally, girls with a known history of polycystic ovarian syndrome and those currently on medications for chronic physical or mental illnesses were excluded, as these conditions can significantly alter menstrual patterns, hormone levels and mood-related symptoms, thereby potentially confounding the assessment of PMS and attitudes towards menstruation. The goal was to maintain a homogeneous sample representative of the general adolescent population without clinical disorders that may independently affect menstrual experiences.

Sampling method

This study employed a multistage cluster sampling technique to ensure representation from both rural and urban areas across the selected districts. Kerala state has 14 districts. We selected 1 district from the 7 northern (Malappuram) districts and 1 from the 7 southern districts (Kollam), using the lottery method. From each district, one corporation/municipality (urban) and one Grama Panchayath (rural) were randomly selected. Within these areas, one school and one college were randomly chosen in both urban and rural settings, ensuring geographic diversity.

Each school provided students from classes VIII to XII, with each grade considered a distinct batch or cluster, totalling five clusters per school. For colleges, first-year and second-year batches were similarly treated as clusters, totalling two clusters per college. Thus, the study included a total of 28 clusters across the two districts. From each cluster, 35–45 students who met the inclusion criteria were selected, resulting in a total sample size of 1100 students. The detailed sampling selection process is depicted in figure 1.

Cluster composition and number of participants

The exact number of students per cluster varied based on inclusion criteria, and while an even number across clusters was aimed for, the final counts slightly varied due to exclusions. The details of the exclusion are detailed in online supplemental file 1.

Confidentiality

To address the sensitivity of the topic and the adolescent age group involved, several safety and confidentiality protocols were implemented. All data collection was conducted in a private, quiet setting within classrooms or lecture halls, away from peers and teachers. Participants were given clear verbal and written instructions that they could skip any question they felt uncomfortable answering and could withdraw at any point without any consequence. Questionnaires were completed anonymously and returned in sealed envelopes to ensure that no identifiable information was linked to responses. A female teacher or counsellor was made available onsite during survey administration to provide emotional support if any participant experienced discomfort. Additionally, contact information for a local adolescent counselling service was shared with all participants after survey completion. All participants were assured of the confidentiality and voluntary nature of the study, and questionnaires were administered in a private setting to protect privacy.

Study instrument and process of administration

The data collection was conducted using a semistructured, self-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire consists of closed-ended items across four sections. A few open-ended questions were included, particularly in the section on coping mechanisms, allowing participants to elaborate on any additional personal strategies not listed in the predefined options. After obtaining clearance from the institute ethics committee and appropriate permission from the selected schools and college authorities, informed consent/assent was taken from parents and students in schools and colleges. The menstrual attitude of adolescent girls was assessed using a modified version of the MAQ that was previously adapted and validated by an Indian author to suit the local cultural context. It captures multiple dimensions, including viewing menstruation as natural, bothersome or debilitating. The PSST is a validated screening instrument that identifies the severity of physical, psychological and behavioural PMS based on standardised criteria. These tools were selected over alternatives such as the ACOG criteria or Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) based interviews due to their simplicity, applicability in school-based surveys and proven reliability in adolescent populations across diverse sociocultural backgrounds.

Menstrual Attitude Questionnaire (MAQ) (modified version)

The menstrual attitude of adolescent girls was assessed using a culturally adapted 22-item version of the MAQ, derived from a tool originally developed by Brooks-Gunn and Ruble.14 15 The original 39-item tool was modified based on psychometric validation for use among Indian adolescents, with acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha>0.75 across subscales). This modified version preserved four of the original five conceptual domains: menstruation as a debilitating event, bothersome event, natural event and no effect on behaviour. Responses were scored on a seven-point Likert scale, and total scores ranged from 22 to 154. Based on the mean score cut-off of 90 established in the validation study, scores>90 were considered indicative of a positive attitude and ≤90 as negative.15 16 A positive attitude—considered healthy—reflects acceptance of menstruation as a normal and manageable biological function, which is associated with better coping, reduced stigma and proactive menstrual hygiene practices. Conversely, a negative attitude—termed unhealthy—reflects beliefs that menstruation is debilitating, shameful or disruptive and has been associated in multiple studies with higher prevalence of PMS, anxiety and avoidance behaviours. For example, a respondent who strongly agreed with statements such as “Menstruation interferes with my daily activities,” “I feel embarrassed discussing menstruation,” indicating a negative attitude. This reflects a perception of menstruation as disruptive, embarrassing and emotionally burdensome.

Premenstrual Symptoms Screening Tool (PSST)

The validated PSST developed by Steiner et al17 assesses the severity of PMS. It includes 19 items: 14 on physical, psychological and behavioural symptoms and 5 on life impact. Items are rated on a four-point Likert scale (0–3), with total scores categorising severity as mild (0–18), moderate (19–28) and severe (>28).18 The questionnaire used in the study is provided in online supplemental file 2.

Study variables

Independent variables

Included age in completed years, education level, socioeconomic status, religion, place of residence, type of family (a nuclear family is defined as a household consisting of parents and their children living independently, without extended family members; a joint family, by contrast, includes extended relatives such as grandparents, uncles, aunts and cousins living together and sharing a common kitchen, which is a traditional marker of joint family structure in Kerala), father’s education, mother’s education, father’s occupation, mother’s occupation and age at menarche.

Dependent variables

Included attitude towards menstruation (responses obtained from the participants were categorised into positive/healthy and negative/unhealthy attitude), severe PMS (as per PSST classification) and coping mechanisms for PMS (the responses obtained for the coping mechanisms were categorised as yes or no).

Statistical analysis

Data were entered in EpiData V.4. Statistical analysis was done using STATA V.14. The categorical variables were expressed in frequency and percentage. The continuous variables were summarised using the median (quartile range). The normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The association of categorical variables with menstrual attitude and severe PMS was done using the χ2 test and unadjusted prevalence ratios with 95% CI were calculated using bivariate regression analysis. Variables that had a p value<0.2 in bivariate analysis were included in the multivariate analysis using the log-binomial model to estimate the adjusted prevalence ratios (APRs) with 95% CI. A p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The menstrual attitude of participants in the present study showed that 51% of adolescent girls had a negative or unhealthy attitude towards menstruation. The observed prevalence of negative menstrual attitude in our study (51%) was considerably higher than the anticipated 14.2% used for sample size calculation. This variation highlights the influence of sociocultural and regional contexts on menstrual attitudes. While the referenced study by Wong4 was conducted in Malaysia (majority Muslim population), the higher prevalence observed in our Kerala-based sample may reflect local cultural narratives, school-level menstrual education gaps or greater openness in self-reporting due to anonymity. This difference also reinforces the importance of context-specific data for designing public health interventions. Similar studies were conducted among university students in northeast Iran, where 76.6% of the female students reported that menstruation is a debilitating event (negative).19 The variation in PMS prevalence across studies can be attributed to several factors. First, different studies employ diverse diagnostic tools—such as the PSST, the ACOG criteria or self-reported symptom checklists—which can yield differing prevalence estimates depending on their sensitivity and scoring thresholds. Second, cultural norms may influence how openly adolescents report emotional or physical discomfort associated with menstruation. In conservative settings, symptoms like mood swings or fatigue may be underreported due to stigma or normalisation of menstruation. Third, lifestyle and environmental factors—including dietary habits, stress levels, sleep quality and levels of physical activity—are known to influence both the experience and reporting of PMS. Lastly, awareness levels and health literacy play a crucial role; individuals with better menstrual health education may recognise and label symptoms more accurately, leading to higher self-reported prevalence.

In our study, 58.80% of Muslim adolescent girls reported a negative attitude towards menstruation. While this is lower than the 89.8% reported in a study from Turkey (culturally Muslim population with urbanisation), both findings point towards a prevailing trend of negative menstrual attitudes among Muslim participants in different sociocultural contexts.20 In contrast, a South Indian study using the same tool found no significant variation in menstrual attitudes across religions,21 while in a Malaysian study among multiethnic Asians, 48.6% of Muslim participants reported a positive attitude.22 Factors such as cultural norms, educational access and socioeconomic background may contribute to these varying attitudes within Muslim communities. Meta-analysis suggests a higher tendency for negative attitudes among Muslim adolescents, with limited open discussion on menstruation potentially reinforcing negative perceptions.7

In our study, adolescent girls who attained menarche after 12 years had a 55.30% of negative attitude towards menstruation compared with those who experienced menarche before 12 years (47.79%). A hospital-based cross-sectional study from Nepal—similar to India culturally and geographically—involving 100 adolescent girls who had attained menarche reported that 70.3% held negative attitudes towards menstruation.23 The authors attributed these attitudes primarily to early onset of menarche and a lack of prior preparation, which were associated with the development of unhealthy beliefs and perceptions surrounding menstruation. Although the study was conducted in a clinical setting with a smaller sample size compared with our community-based research, its findings are nonetheless relevant. Both studies underscore the critical role of menstrual preparedness in shaping adolescent girls’ attitudes. Adolescent girls who discussed bodily function with their mothers before menarche were better prepared to experience it. They were aware of what happened during the menarche and knew what to do. Furthermore, females who were aware of what was happening during menarche showed fewer negative or secretive attitudes towards menstruation.24 The association between later menarche and a more negative attitude towards menstruation may reflect underlying psychosocial dynamics. Possible reasons include social comparison with peers, lack of preparedness and internalised stigma due to delayed physical development. Adolescent girls who attain menarche later than their peers may feel socially different or left out, potentially leading to embarrassment, anxiety or stigma surrounding menstruation. Moreover, health education efforts—often timed with average age at menarche —may not adequately reach late-maturing girls, leaving them underinformed or unprepared. In such cases, the lack of anticipatory guidance may foster feelings of fear or discomfort when menstruation eventually begins, contributing to a more negative or unhealthy attitude towards the experience.

In this study, the prevalence of severe PMS was 35.82%, with 38.27% reporting moderate PMS and 24.91% mild PMS. Comparatively, an Iranian study reported 38.7% with mild PMS, 31.7% with moderate and 27.5% with severe PMS.18 A cross-sectional study from five colleges in Bhavnagar, Gujarat, assessed 489 college girls and found a PMS prevalence of 18.4% in the total sample, with 14.7% experiencing moderate-to-severe symptoms.25 In our study, 42.58% of adolescent girls with a negative attitude towards menstruation reported severe PMS, compared with 30.87% with a positive attitude. While the Gujarat study involved older, college-going adolescents and used DSM-IV-TR and International Classification of Diseases 10 criteria, our study focused on a younger, institution-based sample. Nonetheless, both studies suggest a link between negative menstrual attitudes and increased PMS severity. Studies in Chinese (Confucian influence and traditional gender roles) and Arab (Islamic norms, gender-segregated society populations) similarly found that negative menstrual attitudes are associated with more symptoms and negative impacts from menstruation.26 27 Analysis of individual items from the MAQ revealed that certain negative perceptions were more prevalent than others. For example, 64.7% of participants agreed or strongly agreed with the statement “Menstruation is bothersome and restricts daily activity,” and 58.2% felt that “Menstruation makes them feel emotionally low or irritable.” In contrast, only 39.5% agreed that “Menstruation is a natural and healthy process.” Additionally, 51.3% reported avoiding physical activity during menstruation, and 46.8% felt embarrassed to purchase sanitary products. These findings suggest that negative attitudes are strongly tied to physical discomfort, emotional distress and menstrual stigma in public and social settings. On the other hand, only 21.9% felt menstruation had no effect on their daily behaviour, indicating that most adolescents perceived a degree of disruption due to menstruation.

In this study, 41.05% of Muslim adolescent girls reported severe PMS, which was higher than the proportion observed among girls from other religious groups. Similarly, a study conducted in Palestine (Middle Eastern, Muslim majority) reported that 100% of the participants experienced some PMS, a study with a high percentage of the symptoms reported were muscle pain and fatigue28 and a study of young Arab women indicated a high prevalence of PMS, with 73% meeting the criteria for PMS and 35% for PMDD.27 This difference may be influenced by sociocultural factors such as limited discussion about menstruation, stigma and inadequate menstrual education, which are known to shape both menstrual attitudes and symptom perception. Rather than attributing these findings solely to religion, it is important to consider the broader cultural context and communication practices within communities that may affect menstrual health outcomes. These include limited open discussion of reproductive health topics within conservative households, modesty norms that discourage public acknowledgement of menstruation and restricted access to formal menstrual health education—particularly in lower socioeconomic settings. In some cases, religious practices such as restrictions on prayer or fasting during menstruation may reinforce feelings of exclusion or impurity, contributing to a perception of menstruation as shameful or unclean. Furthermore, studies have shown that lower levels of parental education and conservative gender roles—more prevalent in certain cultural subsets—can contribute to stigma and secrecy around menstruation, shaping negative beliefs from an early age.

In this study, 38.5% of adolescent girls from nuclear families reported severe PMS, a trend consistent with findings among young Arab women, where a higher prevalence of PMS was also observed in nuclear family settings.27 According to the National Family Health Survey-5 data, nuclear families constitute approximately 70% of households in Kerala, making this association particularly relevant in the local context. This association may be attributed to the relatively higher psychological stress experienced in nuclear families, where emotional support systems may be more limited compared with joint or extended family environments. Elevated stress levels have been linked to hormonal imbalances and increased sensitivity to cyclic hormonal fluctuations, both of which can exacerbate PMS symptoms. Moreover, adolescents in nuclear families may encounter greater academic pressure, social isolation or household responsibilities, all of which contribute to elevated stress and may worsen the perception or the severity of PMS. In contrast, joint families often provide a broader support network through the presence of extended family members. Additionally, this study found that 38.95% of adolescent girls with mothers having only secondary education or less reported severe PMS. Another study among female college students from South Korea (high academic pressure, changing family structures) noted that students whose mothers had lower levels of education experienced more severe PMS symptoms.29 This suggests that maternal education plays a significant role in shaping menstrual health awareness and self-care practices in daughters. Mothers with higher education levels are more likely to discuss menstruation openly, provide accurate information and encourage healthy coping strategies. In contrast, less educated mothers may have limited knowledge or may not prioritise open communication about menstrual health, leading to poor menstrual preparedness and inadequate symptom management in their daughters. Poor PMS management—such as lack of awareness about dietary changes, physical activity or appropriate use of medication—can intensify the physical and emotional symptoms of PMS, thereby increasing its perceived severity.

The association between joint family living and more positive menstrual attitudes may be partly explained by the increased opportunities for informal discussion and emotional support within larger family networks. In joint families, adolescent girls are often surrounded by female relatives—such as mothers, grandmothers, aunts and elder cousins—who may openly share personal experiences and advice related to menstruation. This intergenerational exchange can help normalise menstruation, reduce fear or stigma and foster a more accepting and informed perspective. In contrast, girls in nuclear families may have fewer female role models or confidants at home, limiting their exposure to supportive conversations about menstruation. The absence of such a support system may lead to reliance on peers or media, which can sometimes reinforce negative or inaccurate beliefs. Therefore, the familial environment plays a critical role in shaping menstrual attitudes and coping behaviours.

In our study, the most commonly adopted coping strategies for managing PMS among adolescent girls included resting (80%), sleeping (63%), watching movies (44%), listening to music (38%) and taking painkillers (22%). These findings indicate a reliance on passive and non-clinical methods of symptom relief. A similar pattern was observed in a study by Molugulu et al in Malaysia, where sleeping (63%), relaxing (62.3%) and listening to music (38.7%) emerged as the most prevalent strategies.30 The consistency between the two studies suggests that adolescents across different regions may gravitate towards easily accessible and comforting coping mechanisms rather than proactive clinical interventions. However, the relatively low use of painkillers in our study highlights a possible gap in awareness or reluctance towards pharmacological management of PMS. This emphasises the need for targeted health education that promotes evidence-based coping techniques, empowers adolescents with knowledge about safe medication use and encourages seeking medical advice when symptoms interfere with daily functioning.

A key strength of this study is its school-based design with a relatively large sample of adolescent girls selected from a northern and southern district of Kerala. The use of validated tools to assess menstrual attitudes and PMS contributes to the reliability of the results. However, the self-administered nature of the questionnaire may have led to over-reporting or under-reporting, and the sensitive nature of menstruation-related topics could have introduced social desirability bias. While the study provides important insights, the generalisability of the findings is limited to school-going adolescents in similar urban and semiurban settings. The results may not represent out-of-school adolescents or those from rural, tribal or socioeconomically diverse backgrounds. Future research should aim to include more diverse populations to enhance external validity and guide inclusive adolescent health strategies. Given that 48% of our participants attained menarche at or before age 12 years, future studies should consider including younger adolescents, as the national trend suggests a declining age at menarche. The lower expected prevalence used during sample size estimation may have led to a conservative estimate, although the final sample size remained adequately powered.

Conclusion

Over half of adolescent girls (51%) reported a negative attitude towards menstruation, and over one-third (36.8%) experienced severe PMS. Girls with a negative attitude were significantly more likely to report severe PMS, indicating a meaningful association between menstrual perceptions and symptom severity. These findings underscore the need for targeted menstrual health education during adolescence to normalise menstruation and address PMS-related challenges. Additionally, strengthening menstrual literacy in the family setting—especially among mothers with lower educational attainment—may improve intergenerational communication and support healthier attitudes and coping mechanisms among adolescents.
