Minimally invasive soft tissue repair using shrunken scaffolds
Authors: Mingjun Xie, Shengxi Jin, Kang Yu, Hui Lin, Yong He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe minimally invasive injection of tissue engineering scaffolds is of interest as it requires a smaller incision and quickens recovery. However, the engineering of scaffolds capable of injection remains a challenge. Here, we report on a shrunken scaffold inspired by the shrinking of puffed food in a humid environment. A scaffold is freeze-dried to remove water then placed in a humid atmosphere. The humidity causes the dry scaffold to shrink by up to 90%. In addition, the humidity treatment reduces the scaffolds modulus minimizing the foreign body response after implantation. The scaffolds can rapidly swell into their original size and shape after application. A tool for the delivery of the minimally invasive scaffolds is developed and we demonstrate the potential for minimally invasive delivery using this shrinking technique.Subject terms: Tissue engineering, Biomaterials, Implants

Introduction

The quest for effective soft tissue defect repair has been a pivotal issue in clinical regeneration research. With advancements in tissue engineering1–3 and three-dimensional (3D) bioprinting4–6, the transplantation of bioprinted hydrogel scaffolds has emerged as a promising strategy. In this approach, hydrogels7–9 are intricately structured via 3D bioprinting prior to implantation, harnessing their exceptional biocompatibility to foster cell growth and facilitate tissue regeneration, thereby achieving soft tissue reconstruction. Studies such as Hamid et al.‘s have demonstrated the potential of porous, biodegradable poly (ethylene glycol) (PEG) and cystamine (Cys) hydrogels in supporting vascular network development for soft tissue engineering10. Similarly, Shin et al. showcased a 3D bioprinted, inflammation-modulating scaffold capable of regulating local inflammatory responses during soft tissue repair11.

The hydrogel scaffold strategies exhibit obvious advantages over traditional approaches for soft tissue repair. Traditionally, autologous tissue transplantation, utilizing sources such as fat and skin, has been the go-to method in clinical practice12–14. This process involves harvesting healthy tissue from the patient. Flap transplantation, for instance, necessitates the removal of a healthy skin flap from the patient15,16. Ensuring the flap’s viability and minimizing complications requires an adequate blood supply, which often translates to a larger donor site excision, creating a significant new trauma area and increasing patient discomfort. Moreover, flap transplantation is a complex procedure best performed by specialists, and its success rate is not guaranteed. Complications like flap necrosis, resulting from blood circulation issues, can be severe. Autologous fat injection in plastic surgery, as another example, also faces its own limitations, primarily a low survival rate and a tendency to form nodules. These drawbacks place it at a disadvantage compared to scaffold-induced spontaneous tissue regeneration, which offers a more natural and efficient healing process.

While hydrogel scaffolds present notable advantages over traditional autologous tissue transplantation methods, their implantation, especially for large defects, also entails large surgical incisions. To address this issue, minimally invasive hydrogel injection techniques have garnered significant attention due to their minimal incision needs, straightforwardness, and rapid recovery rates17,18. Currently, self-healing hydrogels19,20 derived from innovative biomaterials or decellularized adipose tissue21 owns excellent injectability and spontaneous formation capability, enabling it to adapt to diverse target tissues22. For small soft tissue defects, minimally invasive injection of these hydrogels offers efficient repair. However, when addressing large soft tissue defects, the injected self-healing hydrogel crosslinks into a bulky mass within the body. Cells can only grow on its surface, impeding smooth defect repair. Obviously, in such scenarios, large pre-formed hydrogel scaffolds, notably grid scaffolds with precise shapes and ample growth spaces, are more reliable than direct self-healing hydrogel injection (Supplementary Note 1, Supplementary Fig. 1). Unfortunately, such pre-formed scaffolds are also hindered by their large size and lack of fluidity, which prevents them from passing through a thin injection needle. Consequently, achieving this compatibility has been a persistent challenge or, in some instances, deemed an unattainable goal.

Here, we propose a posttreatment method to shrink gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel23–25 scaffolds prepared by projection-based 3D bioprinting (PBP)26–28, namely lyophilization & dampening (L&D). Lyophilization29,30 treatment, also known as freeze-drying, is used to remove water and form a micro-/macro-scale pore network within the hydrogel scaffolds. Dampening treatment, which entails placing the freeze-dried hydrogel scaffolds into a humid atmosphere, promotes the diffusion of water molecules into the pore network, where they bond to the hydrophilic groups on the pore walls. This results in the scaffold shrinking (up to 90% in volume), enabling it to reach a suitable size for minimally invasive injection (Fig. 1). Additionally, the dampening treatment reduces the mechanical modulus of the scaffolds, minimizing the foreign body sensation after injection. Once the treated scaffolds are immersed into an aqueous solution at the soft tissue defect site, they can rapidly re-swell and expand into their original size, filling the defect space and functioning as tissue regeneration media. Accordingly, a specialized minimally invasive injection tool, namely “BioGun”, is designed, so that the soggy scaffold designed as “BioBullet” can be shot out by it. This strategy has the potential to overcome existing technical bottlenecks that currently limit the clinical repair of soft tissue defects through minimally invasive injection in the future.

Results

Miniaturization benefitted from volume shrinking

Having a scaffold size that is small enough is a key factor determining the success of scaffolds purposed for injection into the defect site of a patient, as a small size is what allows the scaffold to pass through a thin injection needle. The dampening-induced shrinking process proposed in this work resembles the phenomenon whereby puffed food (bread, popcorns, chips, etc.) becomes soggy and shrinking after absorbing vapor from the air. This process can be potentially described by the schematic diagram (Fig. 2a). During the lyophilization process, ice crystals develop and sublimate out of the hydrogel scaffold. Macroscale walls with cracks form due to the precipitation of the hydrogel molecule network. After ice crystals have totally sublimated, inner pores from where they were. Air enters inner pores via cracks post-lyophilization. During the dampening treatment, water molecules within the vapor can smoothly enter the lyophilized scaffold through the cracks on the inner pores and continuously drive out air molecules inside, thereby avoiding the generation of bubbles that prevent water molecules from entering. Once the water molecules reach the inner pores, they rapidly bond to the hydrophilic groups on the inner pore walls of the fd-pcGelMA. With the effect of hydrogen bonds, one water molecule can act as a medium and bond to several hydrophilic groups on the fd-pcGelMA inner pore walls, causing cracks and inner pore walls to fold and adhere. As a result, the fd-pcGelMA gradually shrinks on the macroscale and further removes inner air. Once the bonded water volume is equal to the total volume of the inner pores, that is, once the inner air is totally replaced by water, the dampening-induced shrinking process is finished.

Herein, photocrosslinked GelMA structures (pcGelMAs) (10 mm × 10 mm × 1.5 mm) printed by a PBP bioprinter were treated into freeze-dried GelMA structures (fd-pcGelMAs) with a vacuum lyophilizer. Then, the fd-pcGelMAs were placed into a dampening device, which provided thermostatically-controlled vapor (Supplementary Note 2, Supplementary Fig. 2) and transitioned into soggy GelMA structures (soggy-pcGelMAs). Volume shrinking was the most apparent phenomenon observed during the dampening treatment. The equivalent volume rapidly decreased from 129.91 ± 6.44 mm3 within 0.5 hours to 19.27 ± 2.87 mm3 and tended to be stable after 2 hours at 11.66 ± 0.87 mm3, which was approximately 1/11 of the original volume. Later, the equivalent volume began to increase slightly (Fig. 2b). The water content rapidly increased inside the soggy-pcGelMAs during the first 4 hours, after which, the increasing rate plummeted. After 22 hours of the dampening treatment, the water content inside the soggy-pcGelMAs further increased but reached only 47.05% ± 1.17%, which was much lower compared to that inside the originally printed GelMA structures (gel state, above 90%). This demonstrated that the quantity of water absorbed during the dampening treatment was very limited in the vapor atmosphere. In addition, in order to verify the authenticity of the conjectures above, morphologies of soggy-pcGelMAs (10 mm × 10 mm × 10 mm) that underwent different dampening treatment durations were captured using a camera and scanning electronic microscope (SEM), respectively. The results (Fig. 2d) demonstrated that the real phenomenon perfectly matches the conjectures above. From the camera images, the soggy-pcGelMA remained opaque appearance during the first 2 hours. Combined with the volume and water content results discussed above, the water content only reached 22.21% ± 1.97% within the first 0.5 hour, during which the soggy-pcGelMAs rapidly shrunk. This indicated that during the dampening treatment, the absorbed water molecules prioritized inducing a shrinking effect on the soggy-pcGelMAs initially, rather than a gelation effect (displaying expansion and transparent appearance). After 2 hours, the soggy-pcGelMAs gradually presented a transparent appearance, during which the volume and water content slightly increased, indicating the gelation process started. The SEM and the relevant analysis results (Fig. 2c) directly reflect the microscale change of the soggy-pcGelMAs, which was consistent with the macroscale phenomenon above. With the increasing of dampening duration, the porosity obviously decreased and the inner pore walls got closer and closer, eventually becoming dense. The average of pore areas decreased and the pore count increased because the inner pore walls rapidly bonded with water molecules and the pores in the captured view increased within the first 2 hours. After that, the pore count sharply decreased because the inner pore walls attached to each other and some of the pores disappeared in the captured view. The inner pore areas increased slowly, indicating the gelation start. Additionally, the volume change during the immersing treatment process (specifically, by directly placing the fd-pcGelMA in liquid water to induce shrinking) was tested (Supplementary Note 3, Supplementary Figs. 3, 4, Supplementary Video 8), verifying the necessity of gaseous instead of liquid water in the dampening treatment. These results and analysis verified that L&D treatment could effectively reduce the size of hydrogel scaffolds to a suitable dimension, enabling them to subsequently pass through a thin injection needle for minimally invasive scaffold injection.

To further describe the fd-pcGelMA shrinking behavior during the dampening process, a finite element simulation model was established (Fig. 2e) and parametric sweep analysis was carried out (Supplementary Note 5, Supplementary Table 1). Each pore within the pore wall inside the fd-pcGelMA was simplified into a “constant material-volume truss (CMVT)” structure with hydrophilic bonds on the “rods”. The simulation was carried out with the “truss” unit. With the bonding of water molecules onto the “rods”, the rods shortened along the axial direction and the section area was enlarged, keeping the rod volume unchanged. The calculation was set up to stop when the total bonded water volume reached the total pore volume. A crossed shape that was composed of 1 center “truss” and 4 branch “trusses” in each direction was established to simulate the different layers of the pores inside the fd-pcGelMA. Because the water molecules enter from the external environment and can bond to the outer layer pores in advance when they diffuse into the fd-pcGelMA through the pore network, there can be a water molecule quantity decreasing gradient from the outer to inner areas of the fd-pcGelMA. As a result, the water molecule bonding rate decreases from the outer to the inner fd-pcGelMA. From the simulation results (Fig. 2f–h, Supplementary Video 1), every “truss” unit gradually shrunk and the quantity of bonded water molecules on the “rods” gradually increased. Firstly, the pores in the five layers shrunk simultaneously as the bonded water content increased and the total volume rapidly decreased. Then, the pores in the outermost layer reached a balance, such that the pore volume was equal to the bonding water volume. The total volume shrinking rate began to decline. At last, all pores in all layers gradually reached the balance described above and the volume remained unchanged. The simulation trends of the volume change and water content change were similar to the trends observed within the experiment.

Potential foreign body sensation according to mechanical property

The feasibility of injectable scaffolds in tissue engineering involves their ability to avoid causing a foreign body sensation in the patient body31. Tissue engineering scaffolds constructed from diverse biomaterials own different mechanical properties32. In contrast to hard tissues (such as bone and cartilage), soft tissues are hightly sensitive to the implantation of foreign materials, underscoring the importance of injectable scaffolds with a low mechanical modulus to safeguard patients against secondary damage. By applying compressive force to the cylindrical soggy-pcGelMAs that had undergone different dampening durations, differences in their compressed structural morphology indicated that the proposed L&D treatment method could potentially influence the mechanical properties of scaffolds (Fig. 3b). In conjunction with the previously discussed results on volume, water content, and morphology, the principle of mechanical property change during L&D treatment can be illustrated schematically (Fig. 3a), exemplified by a scaffold subjected to compressive force. Initially, following lyophilization, thin inner pore walls with cracks can form within the scaffold, resulting in a porous texture. Due to the absence of the lubrication effect of water, high friction arises as the interfaces of these cracks, leading to structural fragmentation under compression. During the early dampening stage, the bonded water molecules partially act lubricants, reducing friction among inner pore walls under compression. Notably, inner pore walls and cracks rapidly adhere to each other during this stage, resulting in significantly thicker walls, which may potentially impact the mechanical properties of the scaffold. To theoretically substantiate this, the same CMVT structure mentioned above was selected as the mechanical property simulation unit in order to demonstrate the influence of dampening-induced shrinking (inner pore walls accumulation) on the structural compressive modulus (Supplementary Note 6, Supplementary Table 2). The simulation results (Fig. 3c, f, Supplementary Video 2) revealed that as truss length decreased, the compressive modulus of the CMVT unit continually increased. Hence, dampening-induced shrinking can enhance the mechanical properties. During the middle dampening stage, the innermost layer of inner pore walls and cracks bond with additional water molecules and dissolve, initiating the gelation transition. Since the gelation range is initially small, and other parts of the inner pore walls remained relatively dry, the scaffold will exhibit partial gel properties, becoming softer and more elastic. During the late dampening stage, the inner pore walls have fully undergone the gelation transition and slightly absorb more water molecules, exhibiting complete elasticity.

Here, compressive testing was conducted on the soggy-pcGelMAs which had undergone different dampening treatment durations. The results revealed that different dampening treatment durations led to distinct mechanical properties. According to the compressive curves (Fig. 3d) and the compressive modulus results (Fig. 3e), during the early dampening stage (0–0.5 hour), the compressive curve exhibited obvious fluctuations, and the compressive modulus rapidly increased. This indicated that the scaffold remained a hard texture and had notable internal cracks. Subsequently, during the middle dampening stage (0.5–2 hours), the fluctuations in the compressive curve began to subside, and the compressive modulus decreased. Viscous creep and elastic deformation gradually became evident, indicating that the scaffolds were transitioning from a porous and hard state to a gel state. Finally, during the late dampening stage (2–6 hours), the compressive modulus of the scaffold reached a low level, potentially suitable for reducing foreign body sensation in the patient’s body, and it slightly increased as the dampening duration prolonged. Consistent with the previously discussed results, the dampening-induced shrinking process had concluded during this stage, and dampening-induced gelation process had commenced. The scaffold further absorbed a small amount of water molecules and slightly expanded. Additionally, for comparison purposes, the compressive curve of pcGelMA that underwent oven-drying treatment33 (specifically, by placing the pcGelMA in a vacuum oven at a relatively high temperature of 50 °C for 3 hours to remove internal water and induce shrinking) was tested. It is evident that, while the volume of pcGelMA could be effectively reduced through this process, the compressive modulus of the oven-dried pcGelMA was much higher than that of soggy-pcGelMA (Supplementary Note 4, Supplementary Figs. 5–8, 11). This increased modulus would lead to an obvious foreign body sensation after injection, which was precisely why dampening treatment was chosen over oven-drying treatment to shrink hydrogel scaffolds. All of these results and analysis confirmed that soggy-pcGelMAs could be treated in L&D method as to transform them into an ideal mechanical state, thus minimizing the chance of causing a foreign body sensation after scaffold injection.

Tissue-defect-filling ability brought by the re-swelling process

After a soggy-pcGelMA is injected into a soft tissue defect, it should recover to the original volume by absorbing the surrounding bodily fluids or adjuvant injections, subsequently filling the subcutaneous cavity. Gelatin is known for its high solubility in water, attributed to the abundance of hydrophilic groups on its molecular chains34–36. During the dampening process, vapor permeates the inner pores of the fd-pcGelMA, where water molecules bond to hydrophilic groups, leading to the shrinkage and initial gelation transition of the fd-pcGelMA. However, in practical scenarios, water molecules bond to hydrophilic groups randomly, resulting in some hydrophilic groups remaining unsaturated, covered by wrinkled inner pore walls. Consequently, once immersed, the soggy-pcGelMA can continue to absorb more water, a process known as re-swelling. This process is illustrated in the schematic diagram (Fig. 4a). Unlike the vapor employed in the dampening treatment, liquid water offers an ample supply of water molecules and exerts greater external pressure, facilitating its rapid entry into the soggy-pcGelMA and bonding with hydrophilic groups within. Subsequently, the walls of the shrunk soggy-pcGelMA rapidly unfold and re-swell to a larger size.

Here, the surface hydrophilicity of the soggy-pcGelMAs that underwent different dampening treatment durations were tested (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Video 3). The results revealed that the initial contact angle decreased (Fig. 4c), indicating an enhancement in surface hydrophilicity, with an increase in the duration of the dampening treatment. This was attributed to the fact that the surface of soggy-pcGelMAs undergoing longer dampening treatment duration contained more water molecules. For a soggy-pcGelMA with a specific dampening treatment duration, the local surface contact angle diminished as the droplet was absorbed during testing. This signified that as liquid water continuously wetted the surface, the local surface hydrophilicity continually intensified, ensuring the sustained re-swelling process. Furthermore, longer dampening treatment durations led to faster droplet absorption rates (Fig. 4d). This phenomenon was likely due to the higher surface hydrophilicity and the reduced presence of internal residual bubbles. The volumes and masses of soggy-pcGelMAs produced after different re-swelling durations were evaluated (Fig. 4e–g, Supplementary Video 4). Both volume and mass increased with an increase in the re-swelling process duration. The re-swelling speed was rapid, and within just 5 minutes, the soggy-pcGelMAs attained their original sizes (10 mm × 10 mm × 1.5 mm). Additionally, the re-swelling process of oven-dried pcGelMAs discussed previously was also examined (Supplementary Video 7, Supplementary Note 4, Supplementary Figs. 9, 10). The results demonstrated that while oven-dried pcGelMAs could be re-swelled, their re-swelling speed was considerably slower than that of soggy-pcGelMAs, which was restricted by the denser hydrogel network. These results and analyses validated the advantage of the proposed L&D method in ensuring that the shrunk hydrogel scaffold would successfully and rapidly re-swell to its original size and fill the soft tissue defect post-injection.

Physiochemical properties of BioBullets for adipose defects

Considering the convenience and mass production potential (Supplementary Note 7, Supplementary Fig. 12), soggy-pcGelMA was designed as “BioBullet”. That is, the soggy-pcGelMAs were loaded into glass tubes and further flattened inside, resulting in a reduced volume. Here, we took adipose defect repair as an application scenario to demonstrate the feasibility of the proposed strategy. The external dimensions of the BioBullets used in adipose defect repair were designed as a tablet shape (ø5 mm × 2 mm). Moreover, to promote cell migration and nutrient diffusion, a macroscopic grid (600 μm) was designed on the tablet. Then, the printed pcGelMAs underwent lyophilization and dampening (for 2 hours) treatment, thereby turning them into soggy-pcGelMAs. Lastly, the soggy-pcGelMAs were forced into sterilized glass tubes one by one according to the adipose defect size (Fig. 5a, b).

From the SEM images (Fig. 5c), pores could be observed at two scales in the soggy grid GelMA scaffold: millimeter-scale pores built by the computer-aided design (CAD) model and micro-scale pores generated by the hydrogel molecules and the ice crystals after the lyophilization treatment. Obviously, dampening treatment shrunk the inner pore walls and decreased the inner pore size within the lyophilized/soggy grid GelMA scaffold. Furthermore, due to the introduction of the grid structure, the soggy grid GelMA scaffold could be further flattened, making it easier to inject into the tissue defect. Due to irregular morphology after flattening, the overlooking area analyzed by ImageJ was directly used, rather than equivalent volume like cubic soggy-pcGelMAs above, in the analysis of BioBullet size change regularity. The overlooking area results (Fig. 5e) showed that after the dampening treatment, the overlooking area of the structure was reduced from 26.97 ± 1.29 mm2 to 3.35 ± 0.67 mm2; a reduction of 87.58%. Moreover, with further flattening, the overlooking area of the structure decreased to 0.91 ± 0.10 mm2, resulting in a final overlooking area reduction of 96.63%.

The structure re-swelling process of the BioBullet was further analyzed (Fig. 5d, Supplementary Video 5). The results (Fig. 5f) showed that the BioBullet owned excellent re-swelling capability and its average overlooking area expanded by nearly 40 times, from 0.95 ± 0.29 mm2 to 37.68 ± 2.69 mm2. Remarkably, in the first 0.5 minute of re-swelling, due to the small exposed area of the scaffold to water, the re-swelling curve was flat. After 1 minute, the BioBullet gradually unfolded, and the water absorption rate increased. Furthermore, considering the blood environment in vivo, whether blood affects re-swelling process or not needs to be explored. The results (Supplementary Note 11, Supplementary Figs. 17, 18) showed that the blood environment did not have a significant influence on the re-swelling process, although in the first 3 minutes it slightly decreased the re-swelling speed. In addition, the enzymatic degradation performance of the BioBullet was assessed, which is a typical evaluation method used to verify whether a scaffold can be metabolized after implantation in vivo. Here, the re-swelled BioBullet and the grid GelMA scaffold without L&D treatment were degraded in collagenase II solution37,38. The degradation results showed that both of them could be degraded in 4 hours, and the degradation of the two types of structures did not show remarkable difference (Fig. 5g), indicating that L&D treatment would not obviously alter the degradation performance of the GelMA scaffolds.

Biocompatibility of BioBullets to adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs)

Once the BioBullets are “shot” into the adipose defect, they rapidly re-swell into much larger scaffolds to fill up the defect space. Then, cells surrounding the defect area can migrate to the re-swelled scaffolds to rapidly proliferate and differentiate on them. ADSCs stand out as one of the most significant cell types among those involved in adipose defect repair39. Under suitable induction conditions, ADSCs can differentiate into adipose cells, which contain a fat reservoir comprised of triglycerides, and these cells gradually populate the tissue defect area. Therefore, ensuring the proper growth of ADSCs on the re-swelled BioBullets is necessary to guarantee the clinical treatment effect.

The ADSCs were seeded onto the re-swelled BioBullets and cultured in the absence of adipogenic induction. After 3 days of culture, the ADSCs were generally attached to the surface of the scaffold in a stretched state (Fig. 6b, d). After 7 days of culture, the ADSCs had migrated rapidly, attached evenly to the entire surface of the BioBullets, and continued to grow downward along the grid pores in the scaffold. The cells showed clear spreading morphology. The LIVE/DEAD staining results (Fig. 6a, c) showed that the cell viability remained above 95% during the 7 days of culturing, indicating that the proposed BioBullets had negligible biological toxicity to the ADSCs. The MTS testing (Fig. 6f) results demonstrated that in the first 3 days, the proliferation rate of the ADSCs on the BioBullets was essentially the same as those cultured in a 96-well plate. However, on the 5th day, the proliferation rate of the cells seeded on the BioBullets was significantly higher than that of those seeded in the 96-well plate. This result was attributed to the greater specific area of the former enabling more cells to attach.

Importantly, previous studies have demonstrated that ADSCs can express the stem cell marker CD44, but not hematopoietic system and epithelial markers, such as CD1940. In order to verify the cellular functions of the seeded ADSCs on the BioBullets, Western blotting (WB) was used to detect their specific protein expression levels. The results (Fig. 6e) consistently demonstrated stable expression of CD44 and the absence of CD19 expression over the 7-day culture period, both on the BioBullets and well-plates. This affirmed the maintenance of cell stemness in ADSCs throughout the culture process. These results verified the excellent cell compatibility of the BioBullets and the potential of their use in the clinical repair of soft tissue defects.

A portable minimally invasive therapy tool: BioGun

Based on previous systematic research and discussions regarding BioBullets, their physicochemical properties and biocompatibility have confirmed their feasibility as an efficient vehicle for the minimally invasive injection of scaffolds to repair soft tissue defects. However, there was no corresponding surgical tool available to deliver these BioBullets. Therefore, by referencing the structure of a gun, a portable device called BioGun was designed to facilitate the minimally invasive injection of the BioBullets.

The constructed BioGun was composed of pushrod, trigger, slider, barrel, chamber, syringe containing PBS, BioBullet magazine, connector, BioBullet outlet, and PBS outlet (Fig. 7a). Prior to the surgery, BioBullets were loaded into the magazine, and the syringe, loaded with PBS (containing a red dye), was inserted into the chamber (Fig. 7b). The BioBullet/water outlet of the BioGun was then aimed at the soft tissue defect area, ensuring that the tip was positioned in the center of the defect cavity. The pushrod was then pulled to eject the BioBullets from the magazine and inject them into the defect area. Finally, the trigger of the syringe was pressed to inject the PBS into the defect area, accelerating the re-swelling process.

To verify the feasibility of the BioGun, a simulation of a minimally invasive injection surgery was conducted on a pork adipose tissue defect using the assembled BioGun (Fig. 7c). An artificial cavity with a diameter of 5 mm was created in the fat area. Subsequently, a BioBullet was injected into the defect site using the BioGun, followed by the injection of 0.5 mL of PBS. The results demonstrated that only 3 minutes were required for the BioBullet to re-swell and completely fill the entire defect space, confirming the feasibility of the BioGun for performing minimally invasive injections.

Minimally invasive repair of adipose tissue defects

In applications for the repair of adipose tissue defects, the desired therapeutic goals are achieving the restoration of the appearance and the regeneration of adipose tissue. To assess this, 8-week-old female mice obtained from the Institute of Cancer Research (ICR) were used. The subcutaneous fat in the inguinal region of the mice was chosen as the surgical area. The minimally invasive implantation process with BioBullet and BioGun was completed under the guidance of small animal ultrasound imaging system (Fig. 8a, Supplementary Video 6). On B-ultrasound visualization, the defect appeared as a hypoechoic area, whereas the BioGun nozzle appeared as a hyperechoic area. When located, the BioBullet was then injected along with some PBS into the adipose tissue. Images captured by an in-vivo imaging system (IVIS) (Fig. 8b) showed that the BioBullet rapidly expanded within 10 minutes in vivo and by taking out the BioBullet from mice after 10 minutes (Fig. 8d), it can be seen that its overlooking area became nearly four times (from 3.35 ± 0.60 mm2 to 11.27 ± 0.75 mm2), which confirmed that the BioBullet could normally re-swell as usual in vitro and fill the entire defect space.

From a clinical point of view, the shape in which the defect site recovers and the maintenance of this shape during repair are important evaluation indicators for the repair of soft tissue defects, such as those in adipose tissue. The injected BioBullets were taken out at 0 day, 14 days, 28 days, and 40 days, respectively. During the 40-day repair process, the BioBullets maintained their morphology without exhibiting spatial collapse and structural fragmentation (Fig. 8c, e). After 14 days of repair, the pores and periphery of the scaffold were tightly wrapped by the adipose tissue, the scaffold was translucent, and the adipose tissue was milky-white. The H&E results (Fig. 8f, h) showed that on the 14th day, a great number of cells grew only around the scaffold, leaving many cavities inside the scaffold that contained no cells. On the 28th day, the scaffold was essentially filled with cells, but the content of locular adipocytes was only approximately 30%, which mainly appeared in the peripheral area of the BioBullets. On the 40th day, the inside of the scaffold was filled with a large amount of vacuolated adipose tissue, with the cell area accounting for 70%. To demonstrate that the cells grown in the scaffolds were indeed adipocytes, we performed Nile red staining, which employs a fat-specific probe to sensitively capture and stain intracellular lipid droplets. The results (Fig. 8g) verified that during the 40-day implantation period, the adipocytes gradually matured, the lipid droplet size increased, and the cell density increased. This indicated that the implanted BioBullets had good compatibility with the adipocytes and could promote their rapid proliferation and growth.

In addition, a major clinical difficulty in the repair of large soft tissue is the regeneration of vascular structures within the tissue. In the experiment, we observed the formation of blood vessel cells near the newly grown adipose tissue inside the scaffold. In order to characterize the formation of the vascular structure inside the scaffold during the repair process and to explore its relationship with adipose tissue regeneration, CD3141–43 staining and immunohistochemistry were performed on the tissue sections. Obtaining a positive signal confirms the identity of vascular endothelial cells and indicates that they are already performing nutrient exchange functions. The results (Fig. 8i) showed that on the 14th day, a large number of CD31-positive cells had aggregated around the BioBullet, indicating that a high degree of endothelial angiogenesis had occurred by this time. On the 28th day, the CD31-positive cells continued to aggregate around the scaffold and generate more voids inside the BioBullet by degradation. This was accompanied by the formation of large pieces of adipose tissue. On the 40th day, a large amount of adipose tissue had grown into the inside of the BioBullet and endothelial cells had formed blood vessels around the vacuolated adipocytes. This network provides a channel for nutrient loosening and material exchange for the new growth of adipose tissue.

Discussion

The soggy puffed food-inspired L&D treatment method effectively solve the application difficulties of 3D bioprinted hydrogel scaffolds in clinical minimally invasive repair of soft tissue defects. This strategy realizes scaffold size reduction for their subsequent application via minimally invasive injection, avoiding causing a foreign body sensation, re-swelling to fill the defect space, and promoting tissue regeneration in the soft tissue defect area. It is worth emphasizing that the focus of this work lies in the post-treatment method of the hydrogel scaffold, with the BioGun playing a secondary role to complement this strategy. For therapy optimization, we encourage researchers in relevant fields to further develop this strategy, focusing on the optimal design of the BioBullet and the BioGun.

In terms of the optimal design of the BioBullet, it is evident that after 40 days of implantation of the BioBullets into mice, the BioBullets remained in the implantation area, appearing as a dark blue color in the HE staining results. Furthermore, the adipose tissue surrounding the vacuolated cells exhibited a certain thickness and immune cell infiltration, indicating a potential for fibrosis formation in the implanted hydrogel scaffold. However, this can be further solved by modifying the scaffold topology and material. For the former, in this work, the shape of the BioBullet was directly designed as a grid tablet scaffold. Considering the morphology of complex soft tissue defects, this topology cannot perfectly match the defect space. In the future, doctors can first scan the accurate 3D morphology of the soft defect using CT technology to re-building a better 3D model with CAD. For the latter, in this work, the scaffold was only composed of GelMA hydrogel, without any other functional additives. In its future optimization, researchers may add regeneration drugs or biological factors to speed up the regeneration process. Moreover, we have verified that photocrosslinkable hyaluronic acid methacryloyl (HAMA) and ionic crosslinkable sodium alginate can be shrunk in L&D treatment and re-swelled (Supplementary Note 8, Supplementary Fig. 13). Furthermore, for soft tissue with higher modulus, hydrogel scaffolds with higher modulus need to be used. According to the previous exploration of our lab, the scaffold material applied in this work, namely GelMA (EFL-GM-90) with the concentration of 10% w/v, owns relatively medium compressive modulus (27.6 ± 5.0 kPa) in hydrogel state. As an exploration, the feasibility of L&D treatment for scaffolds with higher modulus was examined. The same grid scaffolds based on GelMA (EFL-GM-90) with the concentration of 15% w/v were printed by the same PBP printer, which has a much higher compressive modulus (122.6 ± 8.0 kPa) in hydrogel state. This modulus is similar to that of the thoracic artery. The results (Supplementary Note 9, Supplementary Figs. 14, 15, Supplementary Video 9, 10) showed that the scaffold can be successfully dampened, shrunk, injected and re-swelled. These initial exploration results indicated that the composition of BioBullet can be varied with different hydrogel types to realize a better regeneration effect in the future.

In terms of the optimal design of the BioGun, in this work, we have roughly designed the modular BioGun for minimally invasive injection of the BioBullet and auxiliary fluid. From our perspective, it can be further optimized in terms of surgical guidance and thinner nozzles. For surgical guidance, we suggest that the BioGun can currently be used alongside proven tools like laparoscopic cannula to ensure smooth operation in minimally invasive procedures. In the future, related guidance functional modules can be directly assembled with the BioGun as an integrated tool. For the nozzles, according to ISO 9626:2016, rigid stainless-steel needle tubes ranging from 3.4 mm (10 Gauge) to 0.18 mm (34 Gauge) are suitable for hypodermic needles and medical devices for human use. In this work, the dual nozzle was designed to be approximately 3.3 mm (combined diameter of the two nozzles), meeting international standards for hypodermic injection. To further reduce skin injury, the BioGun with smaller nozzle and the matched BioBullet tube was re-designed as an exploration (Supplementary Note 10, Supplementary Fig. 16, Supplementary Video 11). The results showed that the dual nozzle could be reduced to about 1.6 mm (combined diameter), comparable to the injury caused by blood donation needles. Testing on pork skin revealed that, despite its thickness and hardness, the re-designed BioGun with smaller dual nozzles could successfully penetrate it, reducing the skin injury size to 1037.57 ± 222.37 μm. Additionally, due to the softened texture after L&D treatment, the BioBullet with the same size was verified to fit into a smaller BioBullet glass tube (inner diameter 1.5 mm). These results indicate that the modified BioGun would better match the minimally invasive surgery in the future.

We believe that this strategy will potentially break through existing technical bottlenecks that limit the clinical repair of soft tissue defects by administering a minimally invasive injection.

Methods

All animal protocols were approved by Zhejiang University Experimental Animal Ethics Committee (ZJU20230185) and used the ARRIVE1 reporting guidelines.

Preparation of GelMA bioink

The purchased GelMA (EFL-GM-90, Yongqinquan Intelligent Equipment Co., Ltd., China) powder was removed from a −80 °C refrigerator and then dissolved with deionized water to the concentration of 10% w/v. Photoinitiator lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP, Yongqinquan Intelligent Equipment Co., Ltd., China) was then added into the solution to reach a concentration of 0.5% w/v. Then, the light absorber (Tartrazine, Yongqinquan Intelligent Equipment Co., Ltd., China) was added into the GelMA bioink to reach a concentration of 0.03% w/v. The prepared GelMA bioink was then stored in a 4 °C refrigerator.

3D printing of GelMA scaffolds

The GelMA scaffolds were printed by a desktop PBP printer (EFL-BP8600, Yongqinquan Intelligent Equipment Co., Ltd., China). The printer size is 340 mm × 210 mm × 290 mm. The printing space size is 48 mm × 27 mm × 50 mm. The projection resolution (XY plane) of the printer is 25 μm. Raising axis repeated positioning accuracy is 5 μm. The minimum layer height is 10 μm. The light source band is 405 nm. First, both the deposition platform and bioink pool of the printer were then removed. The inside of the bioink pool and the formation area of the deposition platform were each wiped down with a non-woven cloth dipped in 75% v/v alcohol. They were then dried and placed back on the printer. The 3D digital model was designed with Solidworks and exported to the STL file and imported into the host computer software of the printer to adjust its size, position, and layout. After the printer had been preheated, the prepared bioink was added to the bioink pool. The protective cover was placed and the printing process was started. After printing, the printing deposition platform was carefully removed. The printed GelMA scaffolds were carefully removed with a spatula and then immediately placed into deionized water to wash away the remaining uncrosslinked bioink on the surface and then stored in a 4 °C refrigerator.

Lyophilization treatment of printed GelMA scaffolds

The printed GelMA scaffolds were placed in a −80 °C refrigerator for 1 hour to fix the hydrogel morphology. Then, the frozen GelMA scaffolds were placed in a lyophilizer to be lyophilized at −80 °C. After 24 hours, the lyophilized GelMA scaffolds were taken out of the lyophilizer and vacuum-sealed.

Dampening treatment of lyophilized GelMA scaffolds

The heater temperature of the dampening treatment device was set to 50 °C and the treatment conditions were 25 °C, 99%. The lyophilized GelMA scaffolds were then placed into it. The dampened GelMA scaffolds were removed out of the dampening treatment device at different time points and vacuum-sealed.

Preparation of the BioBullets

BioBullets featuring grids were designed with Solidworks. The general tablet shape was set as ø5 mm × 2 mm. The pore size was set to 600 μm. The 3D digital model was designed with Solidworks and exported to the STL file and imported into the host computer software of the printer to adjust its size, position, and layout. The layer height of the slice was set to 100 μm. The printed pcGelMAs were then carefully scraped off the deposition platform with a razor blade and subsequently washed in PBS to remove any residual bioink. This was followed by immersing the pcGelMAs in PBS for 24 hours to reach swelling equilibrium. The resulting pcGelMAs then underwent L&D treatment, with the dampening process lasting for 2 hours.

Degradation of the BioBullets

The BioBullets (n = 3 independent experiments) were re-swelled in deionized water for 24 hours and then placed in a 1.5 mL EP tubes in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator. Then, 1 mL of PBS containing 2 U/mL type II collagenase was added to each tube. After 0, 1, 2, and 4 hours of degradation, the solution in the EP tube was removed and the tubes were transferred to a −80 °C refrigerator for storage. After removing all the degraded samples, the remaining samples were lyophilized by the lyophilizer for 24 hours. The final weights of the lyophilized samples were then respectively weighed with the electronic balance.

ADSCs seeding on the BioBullets

The BioBullets were placed into a sterile 96-well plate and irradiated with UV light for 30 minutes on a sterile ultra-clean bench. ADSCs were then detached from culture dishes with 0.25% EDTA-trypsin and resuspended with a small amount of medium to reach a final cell concentration of approximately 5 × 106 /mL. Subsequently, 100 μL of the cell suspension was gently pipetted onto the center of the surface of the BioBullets. The cell suspension was rapidly absorbed by the BioBullets, causing the volume to expand. The inoculated scaffolds were placed on a clean table for 30 minutes before being transferred to a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator for 4 hours of incubation. After 4 hours, 100 μL of cell culture medium was slowly added to each well, and the plate was then transferred to a CO2 incubator to continue the incubation. Cell viability was assessed using the calcein/propidium iodide (PI) cell viability/cytotoxicity assay kit. Calcein AM stained live cells with green fluorescence, while PI stained dead cells with red fluorescence. The F-actin was stained by TRITC Phalloidin. The stained areas were captured using a confocal fluorescence microscope (LSM880, Zeiss Co., Ltd., Germany) quantified using ImageJ (n = 3 biologically independent experiments).

Cell proliferation detection using MTS Assay

MTS working solution was prepared by mixing MTS reagent with complete culture medium containing 1% penicillin/streptomycin (100 μg/mL streptomycin, 100 U/mL penicillin) and 10% FBS at a 1:10 volume ratio. The working solution was added into samples and then cultured in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator for 3 hours. After incubation, the absorbance was measured at 490 nm using an ELISA reader (1681130-4B, Bio-Rad Co., Ltd., USA) to determine cell proliferation (n = 3 biologically independent experiments).

WB analysis

Cells were lysed using radio immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer with protease inhibitors, and the lysates were centrifuged to remove cell debris. Protein concentrations were measured with the Bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Protein samples were mixed with loading buffer and boiled for 5–10 minutes. Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) was conducted until the proteins reached the appropriate position. Proteins were then transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. After transfer, membranes were blocked with Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 and 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 1 hour to prevent non-specific binding. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against GAPDH (Ab8245, Abcam Co., Ltd., USA), CD44 (14-0441-82, Thermo Fisher Co., Ltd., USA), CD19 (14-0194-82, Thermo Fisher Co., Ltd., USA). After washing, membranes were incubated at room temperature for 1 hour with the horseradish peroxidase (HRP) labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (33101ES60, YEASEN Co., Ltd., China). Protein bands were imaged with a chemiluminescence imaging system (ChemiDocTM MP, Bio-Rad Co., Ltd., USA) (n = 3 biologically independent experiments).

Modeling of adipose tissue defects

8-week-old female mice from the ICR (Institute of Cancer Research) were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital before their side hair was shaved using a body hair machine (n = 5 biologically independent experiments). All animals were maintained in specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at a stable temperature of 20–25 °C with a 12-hour light-dark cycle. Each cage housed five mice. The substrate consisted of autoclaved corn cob bedding, which was changed and the cages cleaned twice weekly. Then, the surgical area was depilated with depilatory cream and thoroughly cleaned with 75% alcohol. The subcutaneous fat in the inguinal region of the mice was selected as the surgical area. The inguinal fat of each mouse was located, a piece was cut off with sterile scissors, and the incision was sutured.

Minimally invasive injection using BioGun

For the minimally invasive injection process, each part of the BioGun was thoroughly wiped with 75% alcohol and UV-irradiated overnight on a sterile bench. Then, BioBullet was set into the magazine. A certain amount of PBS was drawn with a 2 mL syringe and then set into the BioGun. The minimally invasive injection of the BioBullet was completed under the guidance of small animal ultrasound imaging system (Vevo 3100, FUJIFILM VisualSonics Co., Ltd., Canada). The metal needles of the BioGun were penetrated into the skin around the established adipose tissue defect. Under the ultrasound imaging system, the metal gun barrel has a strong white echo with a sound shadow. A pushrod was pushed forward to inject the BioBullet and the trigger was squeezed to inject a certain amount of PBS.

Samples preparation for histological analysis

BioBullets within new regenerated adipose tissue were removed on the 14th, 28th, and 40th day after their injection, respectively. Part of them were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Another portion was washed with PBS, embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and sectioned at 10 μm thickness. Both paraffin and frozen sections were stored at −20 °C.

HE staining and analysis

Paraffin sections were placed in a 60 °C drying oven for 2 hours to remove moisture. Sections were then immersed in xylene I and II for 10 minutes each in a fume hood. After dewaxing, sections were hydrated through a gradient of alcohol and distilled water. Hematoxylin was applied to fully cover the tissue and incubated at room temperature for 10 minutes, followed by rinsing in water. Sections were then dehydrated through a gradient to 95% ethanol. Eosin was applied to the dehydrated sections to fully cover them, followed by rinsing in 95% ethanol and further dehydration to xylene. After xylene evaporation, sections were sealed with neutral resin and air-dried in a fume hood. The stained sections were photographed and observed under an optical microscope (SVB-7045A, Shijikexin Co., Ltd., China), with at least three fields of view recorded. To assess adipocyte ingrowth over 40 days of scaffold repair, newly formed vacuolar adipose tissue was quantified using ImageJ software.

Nile red staining

Prepare the staining working solution according to the instructions, avoiding light exposure. Remove the frozen sections and let them warm to room temperature for 3 minutes. Fix the sections with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 minutes. Add the Nile red staining solution and incubate in the dark at room temperature for 10 minutes. After washing, apply anti-fluorescence quenching mounting medium containing DAPI. Observe the sections under the confocal fluorescence microscope with an excitation wavelength of 543 nm. Lipid droplets will appear bright red, and nuclei will appear blue.

Immunohistochemistry for CD31

Paraffin sections underwent gradient dewaxing and rehydration, followed by high-pressure antigen retrieval in sodium citrate for 10 minutes. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using 10% hydrogen peroxide. To minimize non-specific binding, the sections were then incubated in 3% BSA in PBS for 30 minutes. After washing, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD31 (Ab182981, Abcam Co., Ltd., USA). Following another wash, the sections were incubated with the HRP labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) at room temperature for 1 hour. Diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining was performed, and the stained sections were examined and imaged under the optical microscope.
