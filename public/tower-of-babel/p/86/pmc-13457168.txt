Guidelines for dietary fiber evaluation: A proposed standard integrating glycan structural diversity
Authors: Haozhen Zhang, Chengxiao Yu, Shiguo Chen, Changhu Xue
Published: 2026

Main text

Dietary fiber is increasingly regarded as an essential nutrient for metabolic and microbial health.1 However, dietary fiber evaluation remains largely quantity based. Current evaluation systems often overlook the glycan structures that determine how fiber behaves in food matrices, during digestion, and within the gut ecosystem. This limitation is particularly evident in refined carbohydrate-dominant diets, where intensive processing reduces the structural diversity of matrix-embedded and microbiota-accessible glycans reaching the colon. This creates a “glycan gap”: foods with similar fiber content can deliver fundamentally different glycan structures to the gut microbiota, driving distinct microbial utilization patterns and host metabolic responses (Figure 1A).2,3,4 Closing this glycan-structure gap requires fiber quality to be evaluated through the chemical, physicochemical, ecological, and evidence-linked properties of dietary glycans. Here, we propose the dietary glycan index (DGI) as a staged, evidence-updated framework that organizes these properties into comparable dimensions of fiber quality. The physiological effects of dietary fiber depend strongly on glycan structure, which links fiber chemistry to physiological function (Figure 1B).5 Structural variation influences how glycans behave before and after they reach the gut. Within foods, glycan architecture contributes to matrix organization, hydration, viscosity, and interactions with starch, lipids, proteins, minerals, and bile acids. During digestion, these properties affect nutrient diffusion, substrate release, transit behavior, and the fraction of glycans that remains available in the lower gastrointestinal tract. In the colon, linkage configuration, polymer architecture, solubility, and matrix embedding influence enzymatic accessibility, microbial substrate allocation, and the spatial and temporal pattern of glycan utilization.2,3 These structure-dependent effects indicate that dietary fiber evaluation should consider both glycan structural diversity and the matrix conditions that shape glycan availability and activity.

A practical DGI should translate dietary glycan diversity into measurable criteria that connect food chemistry with microbial and host physiology. The DGI can provide a framework for developing shared reference resources for food glycans and for linking glycan structures to biological activity and evidence strength. We propose four core DGI scores: structural diversity, matrix accessibility, functional expression, and evidence confidence (Figure 1C). Together, these scores separate four questions that are often mixed in fiber evaluation: what glycan structures are present, how they are presented in the food matrix, what biological activities they support, and how strong the supporting evidence is.

The structural diversity score describes the range of glycan structures present in a food or ingredient. Core features include dominant glycan classes, major monosaccharide composition, glycosidic linkage, polymer architecture, degree of polymerization, molecular-size range, branching, solubility, charge, and chemical modification when such information is available. The matrix accessibility score describes how food structure and processing shape glycan availability during digestion and microbial use. It considers raw material, origin, processing history, extraction method, storage condition, production period, analytical batch, and matrix-related features such as cell-wall integrity, porosity, particle size, solubility, digestive release, and microbial accessibility.

The functional expression score links glycan structure and matrix accessibility to measurable biological activity. These activities include fermentation, hydration, viscosity, gel formation, nutrient diffusion, bile-acid interaction, intestinal transit, enzyme accessibility, microbial utilization, short-chain fatty acid production, and fecal metabolomic signals. The evidence confidence score shows how well each DGI component is supported by analytical reproducibility, experimental validation, human evidence, population data, and defined endpoints. These four scores define the current role of the DGI: to organize glycan structural diversity, matrix presentation, biological activity, and evidence strength within a shared evaluation framework.

The immediate challenge is to organize measurable structural, matrix-related, functional, and endpoint-linked glycan data into a nutrition-relevant evaluation framework. The DGI should be developed through staged standardization, reference-resource construction, weighting, and validation (Figure 1D). Glycan measurements should be reported together with traceable metadata, including raw material source, processing history, extraction procedure, storage condition, and analytical batch. These metadata would support database updating, inter-study comparison, and subdivision of category-level scores when needed.

The first step is to define a minimum analytical panel for dietary glycan characterization, including monosaccharide composition, linkage pattern, molecular weight distribution, branching-related features, chemical modifications, solubility, and matrix accessibility. Such a panel would extend conventional dietary fiber determinations from total or fractional fiber amounts toward structure-resolved glycan information. Processing metadata should be reported in parallel because milling, heating, extrusion, enzymatic treatment, purification, and formulation can alter glycan presentation to gut microbes. The second step is to construct reference resources for calibration and real-food evaluation. Purified and structurally defined glycans, such as representative fructans, pectic polysaccharides, arabinoxylans, β-glucans, resistant starch fractions, cellulose or hemicellulose fractions, and selected marine or microbial polysaccharides, can support method comparison, model testing, and score development. Representative food-matrix samples should also be included to account for the effects of processing and matrix embedding under realistic dietary conditions.

Transparent scoring and weighting rules are essential for the credibility of the DGI. In the early phase, each score component can be reported separately before integration into an overall score. Equal weighting or expert-informed weighting may serve as an initial baseline when assumptions are explicitly stated. As evidence accumulates, weights should be recalibrated against prespecified endpoints, such as glycemic response, short-chain fatty acid output, fecal metabolomic signatures, bowel-function endpoints, or other application-specific readouts, using statistical modeling, intervention data, and external validation datasets. Different applications may require different weighting schemes. A model designed for glycemic control may assign greater weight to matrix accessibility and postprandial metabolic response; a model designed for microbiome resilience may emphasize sustained fermentation, microbial functional capacity, and metabolite diversity.

Weighting should be evaluated through reproducibility, sensitivity analysis, predictive validity, and external validation. Reproducibility testing would determine whether comparable samples receive consistent scores across laboratories. Sensitivity analysis would identify whether the final score is overly dependent on a single component. Predictive validity would test whether DGI scores improve the prediction of prespecified microbial, metabolic, or host-response endpoints. External validation would determine whether the scoring system remains stable across independent cohorts, food categories, and dietary-intervention settings. These criteria would allow DGI weighting to evolve with evidence while keeping the scoring process transparent.

Activity assessment should be matched to the level of claim being made. Structural and accessibility claims can be supported by standardized digestion, glycan-release, and fermentation-readiness assays. Claims about microbial utilization would require fecal fermentation, defined-community systems, or dynamic colon models that measure substrate degradation, fermentation kinetics, and microbial functional responses.5 Host-relevant claims require animal, gnotobiotic, human intervention, or cohort evidence, depending on whether the intended inference concerns mechanism, physiological response, or clinical relevance. This claim-based validation structure helps prevent activity scores from being overinterpreted and provides practical guidance for selecting experiments and models during DGI development.

This integrated structure emphasizes glycan structural diversity while acknowledging the practical limits of current analytical and validation systems (Figure 1D). These steps can improve the resolution and reliability of glycan-based fiber evaluation over time. Once validated, the DGI could support research standardization, dietary guidance, and food formulation. For researchers, it would offer a common framework for comparing dietary glycan exposure across studies. For nutrition science, it would add a structural-quality dimension to current fiber recommendations. For food development, it could guide processing and formulation strategies that preserve or enrich biologically accessible glycan diversity. Consumer-facing labels should be considered only after analytical standards, scoring rules, and validation criteria are sufficiently robust. In this way, the DGI could help connect food glycan structure, matrix accessibility, microbial metabolism, and host-relevant evidence within carbohydrate-quality assessment.
