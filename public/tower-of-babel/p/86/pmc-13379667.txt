Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter-2 Inhibitors Following Acute Myocardial Infarction in Patients Without Diabetes Mellitus: A Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials
Authors: Muhammad Ahmed, Muhammad Hamza Shuja, Shajia Shakil, Huzaifa Ul Haque Ansari, Firzah Shakil, Muhammad Abdullah Naveed, Syed Atta Ur Rehman, Narmeen Shaikh, Syed Hassaan Ali, Haya Waseem Ansari, Faiza Sajid, Mustafa Murtaza, Muhammad Usman, Biruk Demisse Ayalew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSodium-glucose transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors have shown significant cardiovascular benefits in heart failure (HF) patients with diabetes mellitus or chronic kidney disease. However, their safety and efficacy in non-diabetic patients without HF presenting with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) remain understudied.MethodsDatabases including MEDLINE, Scopus, Cochrane Library, and ClinicalTrials.gov were queried through August 2025 to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Data were pooled using a random-effects model using risk ratios (RR) and mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsFive RCTs involving 9,135 patients (SGLT2i: 4,581 and placebo: 4,554) were included. On pooled analysis, SGLT2 inhibitors were associated with significantly reduced first HF hospitalization (RR: 0.74, p=0.02), improved LVEF (MD: 2.08; p=0.002), and reduced NT-proBNP levels (MD: 12.81; p < 0.0001). No significant differences were noted in all-cause mortality and the composite of first HF hospitalization with all-cause mortality.ConclusionIn non-diabetic patients without HF, SGLT2 inhibitors significantly reduce the first HF hospitalization and improve LVEF following AMI. The findings of this meta-analysis suggest that future research should evaluate the cardiovascular benefits of SGLT2 inhibitors in this patient population.

1. Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction remains a major global health burden and contributes substantially to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality worldwide. 1 It is a type of acute coronary event, which involves ischemia that is induced due to an unanticipated interruption of blood flow to the myocardium, leading to cardiac biomarkers of injury such as cardiac troponins.2,3 Pharmacological management of AMI includes medications such as anti-lipemics, antiplatelets, and beta blockers; invasive approaches include percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG). 4

PCI, the standard treatment strategy for AMI, is a minimally invasive revascularization procedure used to restore blood flow to ischemic myocardium and has significantly improved survival and cardiovascular outcomes, particularly in ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). 5 Despite advances in reperfusion therapy and medical management, patients remain at risk for adverse cardiovascular outcomes following AMI, including heart failure (HF), recurrent ischemic events, stroke, and renal dysfunction.5,6 Consequently, there is increasing interest in adjunctive therapies that may further improve cardiovascular recovery and long-term outcomes after AMI.

Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors are a class of oral antihyperglycemic agents that have demonstrated substantial cardiorenal benefits in recent clinical trials. 7 Beyond glycemic control, SGLT2 inhibitors exert cardioprotective effects through modulation of inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways, improvement of sodium and calcium homeostasis, and enhancement of myocardial metabolic function. These mechanisms may reduce endothelial dysfunction, diastolic impairment, myocardial stiffness, and cardiac arrhythmogenesis, all of which contribute to the development and progression of HF. 8

SGLT2 inhibitors offer cardio protection by inducing autophagy in cardiomyocytes. Empagliflozin inhibits sodium-hydrogen exchanger type 1 (NHE1), reducing apoptosis and ischemic injury, improving left ventricular function post-MI.9,10 Dapagliflozin lowers cardiovascular mortality and HF hospitalizations.11,12 In the specific context of non-diabetic post-MI care, the mechanisms most directly relevant to early cardiac recovery are osmotic diuresis and natriuresis, which reduce preload and afterload, together with attenuation of myocardial oxidative stress and inflammation. 12 These effects highlight their therapeutic potential beyond glycemic control.

While previous meta-analyses consider the role of SGLT2 inhibitors in diabetic patients with AMI undergoing PCI, data on the efficacy of SGLT2 inhibitors in the non-diabetic population remain limited. Current AHA/ACC and ESC guidelines for secondary prevention after AMI emphasise dual antiplatelet therapy, high-intensity statins, beta-blockers, and renin–angiotensin system blockade, but do not currently recommend SGLT2 inhibitors for non-diabetic patients without established heart failure, reflecting the limited dedicated evidence in this population. Importantly, SGLT2 inhibitors are not intended to replace PCI; they are being investigated as adjunctive therapy alongside standard post-MI care to mitigate residual risk. This meta-analysis therefore aims to evaluate whether SGLT2 inhibitor therapy, when combined with standard post-MI care including PCI and guideline-directed medical therapy, improves cardiovascular outcomes in non-diabetic individuals compared to standard care alone. We hypothesized that SGLT2 inhibitor therapy initiated following AMI in non-diabetic patients may reduce HF-related events and improve markers of cardiac remodeling without necessarily conferring immediate survival benefits.

2. Materials and Methodology

The protocol for this systematic review and meta-analysis was registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration ID (CRD420251105223). All the findings in our systematic review and meta-analysis were reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Supplementary Table 1). 13

2.1. Data Sources and Search Strategy

An extensive literature search was conducted on databases including MEDLINE (via PubMed), Cochrane’s Central Library, ClinicalTrials.gov, and Scopus, from inception to August 2025. The search strategy used explicit Boolean structure with predefined combinations of Medical Subject Headings and keywords: (‘acute myocardial infarction’ OR ‘STEMI’ OR ‘NSTEMI’) AND (‘SGLT2 inhibitor’ OR ‘dapagliflozin’ OR ‘empagliflozin’). Reference lists of eligible studies and relevant reviews were hand-searched. In addition, citation tracking using Google Scholar was performed to identify additional eligible studies. A detailed search strategy is presented in Supplementary Table S2. In addition, we thoroughly searched the reference lists of the retrieved articles, past review articles, and meta-analyses to find any relevant studies. Two reviewers (M.A. and H.U.A) independently screened the retrieved records through titles and abstracts, duplicate publications and preliminary reports were screened carefully, any discrepancies were resolved by consensus and a third independent reviewer (F.S.). To further confirm eligibility, two reviewers (M.A. and S.H.A) then separately screened the full text of the screened articles andany discrepancies were resolved by consensus and a third independent reviewer (N.S.). In this entire screening process, conference abstracts without complete outcome data were excluded and duplicate publications and overlapping reports were screened carefully and the most complete dataset was selected.

2.2. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

After importing all relevant articles into Mendeley Reference Manager (version 2.136.0; Elsevier, London, United Kingdom) and removing duplicates, two investigators (M.A. & S.H.A.) independently reviewed the titles and abstracts, followed by full-text reviews. The articles were selected for inclusion based on the following inclusion criteria. (i) only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating the use of SGLT2 inhibitors, specifically dapagliflozin or empagliflozin, compared with placebo in non-diabetic patients presenting with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) were included (ii) studies were required to include adult patients (aged ≥18 years) with ST-segment elevation or non-ST-segment elevation AMI, managed with primary PCI or standard acute coronary syndrome management, and with no prior diagnosis of diabetes mellitus (type 1 or type 2) or heart failure (HF). For the purposes of this meta-analysis, “absence of HF” referred to the absence of a documented clinical history or prior diagnosis of chronic HF before the index AMI. Trials enrolling patients with post-AMI ventricular dysfunction, mildly reduced LVEF, or elevated natriuretic peptides following infarction were not excluded, as these findings may represent acute myocardial injury and early remodeling rather than established chronic HF syndromes. We did not require normal baseline left ventricular function; studies enrolling patients with mildly reduced LVEF (41–49%) or other evidence of impaired cardiac function were eligible provided participants were not diagnosed with heart failure. (iii) intervention for each selected was any SGLT2 inhibitors dapagliflozin and empagliflozin. (iv) comparator was placebo for each study (v) Studies reporting at least one of the predefined outcomes of interest were eligible for inclusion. The primary outcome was a composite of all-cause mortality and hospitalization for heart failure (HF), defined as the first occurrence of either death from any cause or hospitalization due to worsening HF, as reported in the original trials. Secondary outcomes included: (i) all-cause mortality, defined as death due to any cause, including both cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular etiologies; (ii) first hospitalization for HF, defined as admission to a hospital for worsening HF symptoms requiring intravenous therapy or escalation of standard care, according to the definitions used in the original studies; (iii) change in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), defined as the difference between baseline and follow-up LVEF measured by echocardiography, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or other validated imaging modalities; and (iv) reduction in N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) levels, a biomarker of myocardial stress and HF severity. Finally, only studies published in the English language were included. This restriction may introduce language bias and should be considered an inherent limitation of the present analysis. Trials enrolling mixed diabetic and non-diabetic populations were eligible only if outcome data for the non-diabetic subgroup were reported separately or could be obtained from the study authors.

Our exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) case reports, observational studies, review articles, editorials, and conference articles; studies for which full articles could not be retrieved (ii) studies including diabetic patients without separate subgroup data for non-diabetics (iii) studies that enrolled populations without acute myocardial infarction, and (iv) did not report an of the predefined outcomes of interest. Discrepancies were resolved by consulting an independent third reviewer (M.H.S.).

2.3. Data Extraction

Using a pre-piloted Microsoft Excel spreadsheet, the following data were extracted: trial name, publication year, clinical trial number, country, total patient population, SGLT2 inhibitor patient population, placebo patient population, mean age, percentage of female/male patients, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, smoking status, and LVEF. For trials that included both diabetic and non-diabetic participants, such as EMPACT-MI (where approximately 32% of participants had type 2 diabetes) and EMMY (where around 13% had type 2 diabetes), we extracted data exclusively for the non-diabetic subgroup from published subgroup analyses, supplementary material of trials or by contacting study authors.

2.4. Quality Assessment

The risk of bias in the RCTs was independently assessed by two investigators (M.A. & S.A.R.) using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool. 14 The five domains of Cochrane’s tool were used in our study, which included bias arising from the randomization process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in measurement of the outcome, and bias in selection of the reported result. Discrepancies were resolved through consultation with a third reviewer (M.H.S.).

2.5. Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Review Manager (Version 5.4.1, Copenhagen: The Nordic Cochrane Centre, The Cochrane Collaboration, 2014). A random-effects model (DerSimonian and Laird method) was prespecified due to anticipated clinical heterogeneity across studies in patient characteristics, infarct type, timing of therapy initiation, and follow-up duration, even where statistical heterogeneity appeared low. For dichotomous outcomes, risk ratios (RRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. For continuous outcomes, mean differences (MDs) were pooled when outcomes were reported on the same scale, while standardized mean differences (SMDs) were used when measurement scales varied. Where continuous variables were reported as medians and interquartile ranges, conversion to mean and standard deviation was performed using established statistical methods when appropriate. Specifically, the methods of Wan et al. and Luo et al. were used to estimate the sample mean and standard deviation from reported medians, interquartile ranges, and sample sizes; no other imputation of missing summary statistics was performed. For NT-proBNP analyses, studies reporting relative percentage changes from baseline were pooled using mean differences in percentage change values rather than absolute biomarker concentrations. No log-transformation was applied to NT-proBNP values, and absolute change scores were not used; the pooled estimate therefore represents a mean difference in relative (percentage) change from baseline between treatment arms.

A sensitivity analysis excluding the EMMY trial was performed because EMMY was a smaller early-phase study with shorter follow-up and a mixed cohort including a minority of patients with type 2 diabetes; EMPACT-MI was retained because non-diabetic subgroup data were available for extraction.

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, with values above 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity and values below 50% considered acceptable. A two-tailed p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. 15 Because fewer than ten studies were available for each outcome, we did not conduct formal tests for funnel plot asymmetry; publication bias assessment is therefore limited, and descriptive funnel plots are provided in the supplementary materials.

Pooling across trials was considered appropriate because all included studies enrolled adults with AMI, randomized SGLT2 inhibitor monotherapy against matching placebo on a background of guideline-directed post-MI care, and reported the predefined outcomes of interest. Anticipated clinical heterogeneity in infarct subtype, timing of SGLT2i initiation, and the small proportion of diabetic participants in two trials provided the rationale for prespecifying a random-effects model and restricting extraction to non-diabetic subgroups; observed statistical heterogeneity remained low (I2 = 0% for the primary and most secondary endpoints), supporting the validity of the pooled estimates. For trials that enrolled mixed diabetic and non-diabetic populations, namely EMPACT-MI (approximately 32% of participants with type 2 diabetes) and EMMY (approximately 13% with type 2 diabetes), data extraction was restricted to the non-diabetic subgroup. Non-diabetic subgroup outcomes were obtained from prespecified subgroup analyses reported in the primary publications, supplementary materials, or by direct correspondence with the trial authors. Where non-diabetic subgroup estimates were not separately reported for an outcome of interest, that outcome from the corresponding trial was excluded from pooling for that endpoint rather than imputed from the overall trial population. The remaining three included trials (DACAMI, DAPA-MI, and EMI-STEMI) enrolled exclusively non-diabetic participants, and full trial-level data were therefore used. This approach ensured that all pooled estimates reflect SGLT2 inhibitor effects in non-diabetic post-AMI populations exclusively, consistent with the prespecified scope of this meta-analysis.

3. Results

3.1. Study Characteristics and Baseline Demographics

A total of 227 records were identified through the initial systematic search. Following title and abstract screening, full-text review, and application of inclusion criteria, five RCTs were included in the final analysis (Figure 1).16-20 At full-text review, records were excluded for the following principal reasons: non-randomized study design (observational, retrospective, or single-arm studies); inclusion of populations with established diabetes mellitus without separately reported non-diabetic subgroup data; absence of an AMI cohort; lack of any predefined outcome of interest; and duplicate or interim reports of trials already included. The five RCT studies collectively enrolled 9,135 patients presenting with AMI, of whom 4,581 received SGLT2 inhibitors and 4,554 were assigned to matching placebo; all five included trials were double-blind, placebo-controlled, with both groups receiving guideline-directed standard post-AMI care. Across the included trials, patient populations were generally comparable in terms of baseline demographics, including mean age, sex distribution, cardiovascular risk factors (e.g., hypertension, smoking), and infarct characteristics. The duration of follow-up and timing of SGLT2 inhibitor initiation varied slightly between studies, but all trials focused on early post-AMI intervention. In the EMPACT-MI trial, 32% of participants had type 2 diabetes and in the EMMY trial 13% had type 2 diabetes. Outcomes for non-diabetic participants were extracted separately, and participants with diabetes were excluded from our pooled analyses. Detailed characteristics and baseline participant demographics are provided in Table 1. To clarify potential effect modifiers across trials, the proportion of STEMI patients ranged from approximately 72% to 100% in trials reporting infarct subtype: DACAMI enrolled 100% STEMI, DAPA-MI approximately 72%, and EMPACT-MI approximately 74%; EMMY enrolled predominantly STEMI patients per protocol but did not formally partition STEMI/NSTEMI, and EMI-STEMI enrolled STEMI patients only as indicated by trial design. Timing of SGLT2 inhibitor initiation relative to AMI also varied: within 72 hours in EMMY, during the index admission (median ∼2 days) in DACAMI, within 10 days in DAPA-MI, within 14 days in EMPACT-MI, and within 24 hours of PCI in EMI-STEMI. Type 2 diabetes was present in approximately 32% of EMPACT-MI and 13% of EMMY participants; these subgroups were excluded from our pooled analyses, which were restricted to non-diabetic participants only. Given that fewer than ten studies were included per outcome, we did not perform formal tests for publication bias. 4. Discussion

This meta-analysis of five recent randomized trials (EMMY, DACAMI, DAPA-MI, EMI-STEMI, and EMPACT-MI)16-20 focused on the cardiovascular outcomes associated with SGLT2 inhibitor therapy in non-diabetic patients with AMI undergoing PCI. Our findings reveal that adjunctive SGLT2i therapy was associated with a reduced risk of first hospitalization for HF, improved LVEF, and decreased NT-proBNP levels. No significant differences were observed for all-cause mortality or the composite of first hospitalization for HF and all-cause mortality. These results extend our understanding of SGLT2 inhibitors beyond their glucose-metabolic actions and highlight their potential cardioprotective role in a population without diabetes, a group that has been underrepresented in prior studies.

Our findings align with emerging evidence supporting the cardioprotective role of SGLT2 inhibitors, regardless of diabetes status. For instance, a meta-analysis of 13 trials with over 90,000 participants found a significant reduction in the risk of cardiovascular death or hospitalisation for HF across diabetic and non-diabetic cohorts. 21 Similarly, other recent meta-analyses corroborate these effects. Specifically, Hu et al. evaluated mixed cohorts of diabetic and non-diabetic AMI patients, while Idowu et al. included a mixed population with and without heart failure comprising 11,204 participants. Coelho Meine et al. similarly pooled trials irrespective of diabetes status and MI type; thus, direct comparison with our strictly non-diabetic cohort should be made cautiously. Hu et al. reported reduced HF admissions and major adverse cardiovascular events, without a mortality benefit in mixed AMI populations, 22 and Idowu et al. observed a reduced risk of HF hospitalization after AMI but no significant effect on all-cause mortality. 23 Concordantly, Coelho Meine et al. conducted a meta-analysis of five RCTs involving 11,211 AMI patients and reported a significant reduction in HF hospitalization but no significant effect on all-cause mortality or cardiovascular mortality. 24 Our findings are consistent with these analyses with respect to the robust benefit of HF hospitalization and the absence of a mortality effect. However, several methodological differences should be noted. The previous meta-analyses included mixed populations of diabetic and non-diabetic patients, whereas our analysis was restricted to non-diabetic individuals. Furthermore, our search strategy extended through August 2025, potentially capturing updated data. Despite these differences, the convergence of findings across analyses reinforces the conclusion that SGLT2 inhibitors reliably reduce HF hospitalization after AMI but that a mortality benefit remains undemonstrated, regardless of the analytic approach or population studied. Although the relative reduction in HF hospitalization was statistically significant (RR 0.74), absolute event reductions were modest. Since baseline risks varied substantially across included trials, absolute risk reduction and number-needed-to-treat estimates could not be reliably calculated. Additionally, another study demonstrates that dapagliflozin significantly affects NT-proBNP, LVEF, and HF rehospitalizations in mixed diabetic and non-diabetic cohorts, 25 further corroborating the improvements in cardiac biomarkers and ventricular function observed in our analysis. Notably, two other meta-analyses26,27 did report significant mortality reductions with SGLT2 inhibitors after AMI. The latter 27 also demonstrated a significant increase in LVEF and reduction of NT-proBNP, consistent with our findings; however, the mortality benefit they observed contrasts with ours. Both analyses included mixed populations comprising patients with diabetes and/or HF, subpopulations known to derive well-established survival benefits from SGLT2 inhibitors.9,28 This broader patient selection likely drove the observed mortality benefit and may account for the discrepancy with our findings. A notable limitation is that two of the included trials enrolled a significant proportion of participants with type 2 diabetes (32% in EMPACT-MI and 13% in EMMY), and although we extracted non-diabetic subgroup data, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Moreover, several trials enrolled patients with mildly reduced LVEF (<50%), which may reflect early post-infarction ventricular remodeling or subclinical ventricular dysfunction rather than established chronic HF; this nuance should be considered when interpreting our results. We performed a sensitivity analysis excluding the EMMY trial because this early phase study included a small sample size, a short follow-up, and a mixed cohort with approximately 13% diabetes, and we sought to confirm the robustness of our findings in strictly non-diabetic populations.

Several proposed mechanisms may partially explain the observed reduction in HF hospitalization with SGLT2 inhibitor therapy. SGLT2 inhibitors promote osmotic diuresis and natriuresis, which may reduce cardiac preload and afterload and improve volume management in the vulnerable post-MI period. 29 Experimental studies have also suggested additional cellular effects, including modulation of sodium-hydrogen exchange, attenuation of oxidative stress and inflammation, improvement in myocardial energy utilization, and regulation of autophagic pathways.30-33 These mechanisms may contribute to improved ventricular remodeling and reduced cardiac stress following MI. Nevertheless, these mechanistic explanations remain largely hypothesis-generating in the context of post-AMI non-diabetic populations and should not be interpreted as proven causal pathways underlying the observed clinical effects.

The present analysis provides modest biomarker and functional correlates for the aforementioned mechanistic effects, as demonstrated by significant reductions in NT-proBNP and improvements in LVEF with SGLT2i therapy. NT-proBNP is a natriuretic peptide released in response to ventricular wall stress and is a well-validated biomarker of cardiovascular prognosis. 34 It is consistently correlated with higher mortality and HF risk35,36; notably, the PARADISE-MI trial data established that post-MI NT-proBNP elevations predict subsequent HF, recurrent coronary events, and death, independent of baseline clinical characteristics. 36 LVEF is a key measure of left ventricular systolic function and indicates enhanced contractile performance and more efficient ventricular emptying. Low LVEF is strongly associated with increased mortality and morbidity, with values of 40% or less post-MI indicating particularly high risk for adverse ventricular remodelling, HF, and cardiogenic shock. 37 This dual improvement in both myocardial stress biomarkers and contractile function in our analysis reflects coordinated cardioprotection, consistent with known SGLT2 inhibitor mechanisms: reduced preload/afterload, optimized ventricular loading, and direct cellular actions that mitigate adverse remodelling.29-32 The observed mean improvement of approximately 2 percentage points in LVEF should be interpreted cautiously, as this magnitude may fall within interobserver variability of echocardiographic assessment and may not necessarily represent clinically meaningful ventricular recovery. Therefore, the observed LVEF improvement should be interpreted primarily as a supportive surrogate signal rather than definitive evidence of substantial reverse remodeling. Although these mechanistic pathways may partially explain the observed findings, they remain largely hypothesis-generating and should be interpreted cautiously. Given the relatively modest improvements observed in surrogate outcomes and absence of mortality benefit, direct causal relationships cannot be established.

In our analysis, no statistically significant mortality benefit was observed following SGLT2 inhibitor therapy. This finding should be interpreted as neutral rather than suggestive of delayed or masked benefit. At present, the available evidence does not demonstrate a mortality benefit of SGLT2 inhibitor therapy in non-diabetic post-AMI populations. The pooled mortality estimate remains underpowered because of relatively low event rates, short follow-up duration, and wide confidence intervals. Contemporary post-MI management strategies, including rapid PCI, dual antiplatelet therapy, statins, beta-blockers, and renin-angiotensin system blockade, have substantially reduced baseline mortality rates, thereby limiting event accumulation and reducing statistical power for mortality detection.38,39 Although improvements in LVEF and reductions in NT-proBNP suggest favorable effects on cardiac structure and function, whether these surrogate changes translate into meaningful long-term clinical outcomes remains uncertain and requires further investigation in adequately powered studies with extended follow-up.

Our findings have important implications for post-MI management in non-diabetic patients. The development of HF post-MI is closely linked to significant morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs. A first hospitalization for HF often starts a cycle of recurrent admissions, progressive functional decline, and escalating economic burden.40,41 Therefore, preventing initial HF hospitalization yields substantial patient-centered and economic benefits, even in the absence of a mortality reduction. The consistent reduction in HF hospitalizations observed with SGLT2i therapy suggests a potential role as an adjunctive strategy in selected high-risk patients; however, current evidence remains insufficient to support routine incorporation into secondary prevention strategies for all non-diabetic patients following MI. The modest improvements in LVEF and reductions in NT-proBNP further support the beneficial impact of SGLT2 inhibitors on cardiac remodeling and function in this population. This recommendation to include SGLT2 inhibitor therapy is also supported by the favourable safety profile observed across trials, with a recent pooled analysis confirming comparable adverse event rates between SGLT2 inhibitors and placebo. 42 The EMMY trial also demonstrated that very early initiation of SGLT2 inhibitor, specifically empagliflozin (within 24 hours post-MI), was not associated with increased safety risks. 43 Nonetheless, clinicians should exercise caution by regularly monitoring patients’ volume status, renal function, and blood pressure to mitigate potential adverse effects. Potential harms should also be considered. Although safety outcomes appeared generally comparable across included studies, SGLT2 inhibitors may increase risks of volume depletion, hypotension, genital infections, and rarely ketoacidosis. Furthermore, cost-effectiveness in non-diabetic AMI populations remains uncertain and warrants dedicated evaluation. Clinically, while these results suggest a potential role for SGLT2 inhibitors alongside beta-blockers and renin–angiotensin antagonists as remodeling modifiers in post-PCI care, the current evidence is not yet sufficient to recommend their routine use in all non-diabetic MI patients. Rather, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and supportive of individualized clinical decision-making in patients at heightened risk for HF, pending confirmation from larger, adequately powered trials with longer follow-up. Given the extensive comparisons conducted between CABG and PCI across various subpopulations,44,45 it is now imperative to similarly investigate and incorporate the role of SGLT2 inhibitors and other evidence-based antidiabetic agents in patients undergoing these revascularization procedures to refine our treatment approach and further improve long-term outcomes. Future research should clarify the optimal timing of SGLT2i initiation post-MI, the durability of benefit, long-term clinical durability, and cost-effectiveness across diverse economies.

4.1. Strengths and Limitations

Our analysis is constrained by inherent limitations in the available evidence and study designs. The limited number of studies specifically designed for non-diabetic MI patients meant that we were unable to perform subgroup analyses based on individual SGLT2 inhibitor agents, variations in dosing regimens, timing of initiation post-MI, or infarct characteristics (STEMI vs. NSTEMI). We performed a sensitivity analysis excluding the EMMY trial because this early phase study included a small sample size, a short follow-up, and a mixed cohort with approximately 13% diabetes at baseline, and we sought to confirm the robustness of our findings in strictly non-diabetic populations. Moreover, follow-up durations across the included trials were relatively short, which may be insufficient to capture delayed mortality effects or the durability of HF prevention. The pooled mortality estimate also remained underpowered because of relatively low event rates, short follow-up durations, and wide confidence intervals. The heterogeneity in trial design, including variations in sample size, geographic setting, and endpoint definitions, also warrants caution when interpreting pooled estimates. Definitions and adjudication criteria for HF hospitalization varied among included studies and may have contributed additional clinical heterogeneity. Furthermore, pooling outcomes across studies with differing follow-up durations may limit interpretation because time-to-event analyses were unavailable. Plus, the limited number of studies specifically designed for non-diabetic MI patients meant that we were unable to perform subgroup analyses based on individual SGLT2 inhibitor agents, variations in dosing regimens, timing of initiation post-MI, or infarct characteristics (STEMI vs. NSTEMI). Although statistical heterogeneity was low for most outcomes, clinical heterogeneity arising from differences in SGLT2 inhibitor type, timing of initiation, and the proportion of STEMI versus NSTEMI patients may have influenced the observed effects. Additionally, the predominance of STEMI patients in the pooled analysis may affect the generalizability of our findings to NSTEMI populations. A notable limitation is that two of the included trials enrolled a significant proportion of participants with type 2 diabetes (32% in EMPACT-MI and 13% in EMMY), and although we extracted non-diabetic subgroup data, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Moreover, several trials enrolled patients with mildly reduced LVEF (<50%), which may represent subclinical heart failure rather than a truly HF-free population; this nuance should be considered when interpreting our results. We performed a sensitivity analysis excluding the EMMY trial because this early phase study included a small sample size, a short follow-up, and a mixed cohort with approximately 13% diabetes, and we sought to confirm the robustness of our findings in strictly non-diabetic populations. Further, only English-language studies were included; this may introduce language bias.

Our study also has notable strengths. Firstly, the focus on non-diabetic patients provides a more population-specific assessment, as most prior research has evaluated diabetic or mixed populations. Secondly, the inclusion of high-quality RCTs (e.g., DACAMI, DAPA-MI) enhances the reliability of our analysis. Thirdly, homogeneous hospitalization outcomes across trials support a class effect that is independent of SGLT2 inhibitor type (empagliflozin/dapigliflozin). Finally, our study highlights important clinical implications that can inform the development of guidelines for secondary prevention in non-diabetic post-MI patients.

5. Conclusion

This meta-analysis demonstrates that SGLT2 inhibitor therapy is associated with a significant reduction in first hospitalization for HF, NT-proBNP levels, and improvement in LVEF in non-diabetic patients following AMI undergoing PCI. Notably, the current evidence is insufficient to draw definitive conclusions regarding a survival benefit, and this remains an important area for future investigation. Nonetheless, the substantial reduction in HF events, a major driver of morbidity, healthcare costs, and long-term mortality, represents a clinically meaningful finding. Our findings suggest a potential role for SGLT2 inhibitors as an adjunctive strategy in selected high-risk non-diabetic patients following AMI; however, current evidence remains insufficient to support routine incorporation into secondary prevention pathways. Larger adequately powered studies with longer follow-up are needed before broader clinical implementation can be considered.
