A case of congenital cholesteatoma in adult patient with intermittent episodes of facial palsy
Authors: Mhd Ammar Dandachli, Mhd Nabil Dandachli
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and importanceCongenital cholesteatoma is a rare entity that arises from epithelial cell rests in the middle ear, parts of temporal bone or even intracranially. However, it can go unrecognized for several years making the consideration of such diagnosis a bit tricky among otologists.Case presentationWe are reporting a case of a young female adult who presented with unilateral hearing loss for one year and intermittent episodes of facial twitching in addition to periods of recurrent unilateral facial palsy that happened about three times in the past two years.Clinical discussionOtoscopic examination revealed a slight whitish mass behind the superior half of an intact tympanic membrane. Introduction

A cholesteatoma is a non-neoplastic but destructive lesion containing layers of keratin in a cavity lined by squamous epithelium and subepithelial connective tissue [1]. It has two forms: congenital and acquired. Congenital cholesteatoma (CC) occurs mainly in the middle ear cleft but has also been reported in other areas of the skull, intracranially, and in the external auditory canal. We are focusing here on congenital middle ear cholesteatoma which is defined as a keratinizing epithelial mass behind an intact tympanic membrane with no prior history of otorrhea, trauma or surgery. CC has an insidious course and can stay undetected for a long period posing a challenge for otolaryngologists to make this diagnosis as early as possible. And when it has grown to a considerable size the most common symptom would be unilateral hearing loss. Other patients may experience tinnitus, aural fullness or even otalgia.

Nevertheless, in this study we are going to highlight a very concerning yet uncommon symptom of CC which is a recurrent facial palsy that could be mistaken for Bell's Palsy.

Case report

A 23-year-old female patient presented to our ENT clinic complaining of intermittent, sudden episodes of twitching in her right face with some facial asymmetry while talking or laughing. She also mentioned a right-sided hearing loss which has been constant for the past year. When taking her medical history, she mentioned three repeated episodes of unilateral facial palsy on the same side of her face which was diagnosed and treated as Bell's palsy by the doctors in her province with complete recovery. However, for about six months now, the facial asymmetry did not resolve on its own or with corticosteroids. On otoscopic examination there was a whitish mass in both anterior superior and posterior superior quadrants behind an intact tympanic membrane (Fig. 1). A provisional diagnosis of congenital cholesteatoma was made. She had right facial paralysis of grade III on House-Brackmann (HB) grading system at the time of presentation. After ordering the necessary imaging; High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the temporal bone revealed a soft tissue density filling the anterior epitympanum medial to malleus head and incus body causing disarticulation in the attic (Fig. 2) and extending towards the middle ear. The mass was centered around the geniculate ganglion and labyrinthine segment of facial nerve which was suggesting also the diagnosis of facial nerve schwannoma especially in the presence of our patient history. For this reason, we ordered magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the temporal bone with contrast in addition to diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) protocol. MRI showed the previously mentioned soft tissue mass in the epitympanum with signal hypointensity on T1-weighted, hyperintensity on T2-weighted images and minimal circumferential enhancement post‑gadolinium (Fig. 3). On diffusion-weighted images (DWI) the mass did not show evident diffusion restriction which is often indicative of a cholesteatoma. Therefore, the differential diagnosis of facial schwannoma could not be excluded.

Otoscopic picture of superior whitish mass medial to right intact tympanic membrane.

Coronal view of HRCT of temporal bone showing soft tissue mass in the epitympanum medial to ossicles and partially extending to the mesotympanum.

T2-weighted axial MRI image of temporal bone showing a mass with high signal intensity in the anterior epitympanum.

We decided to do exploratory tympanotomy with extended atticotomy. Post-auricular approach was used under general anesthesia. The tympano-meatal flap was elevated and the inferior extension of cholesteatoma mass was seen in the upper mesotympanum. We did a large atticotomy and canal wall-up mastoidectomy in a retrograde fashion using a diamond burr. The cholesteatoma mass was occupying the entire epitympanum (Fig. 4) with extensive extension anteriorly causing a separation in the incudomalleolar joint with hard fixation of malleus head and incus body to the medial wall of the attic.

Operative image showing the cholesteatoma mass (arrowhead) in the epitympanum totally encasing incus body (black arrow).

The total cholesteatoma mass was removed meticulously which caused a dehiscence in the fallopian canal of the tympanic segment and peri-geniculate area of the facial nerve. For that, we performed a partial facial nerve decompression to the smallest possible area where the nerve appeared compressed or edematous (Fig. 5), during which we removed the matrix overlying the nerve without incising the epineurium and injected 2 ml of dexamethasone 8 mg in the middle ear. The malleus head was removed in order to reach the supratubal recess. The incus was also removed and Teflon partial ossicular reconstruction prosthesis (PORP) was put on the stapes head. Two cartilage grafts were taken from the concha, first one to prevent prosthesis migration and the other to compensate for the atticotomy area. The tympano-meatal flap was returned with intact tympanic membrane. The patient was put on amoxicillin/clavulanate 875 mg/125 mg BID for seven days after surgery in addition to prednisolone 20 mg BID for 5 days. Facial nerve function returned to grade I on HB grading system within a few days after surgery which is considered a complete recovery.

Operative image showing edematous hyperemic facial nerve (black arrow) in the second genu during partial nerve decompression.

Discussion

Despite being well documented in the literature, congenital cholesteatoma (CC) is considered a rare entity. This may be justified by the fact that CC remains asymptomatic for a long time and often diagnosed by chance during normal otologic examination, or in the set of inserting a ventilation tube (VT).

There are different theories in regards to the pathogenesis and embryologic origin of CC. these theories include: epidermoid formation, migration theory, inclusion theory and amniotic fluid contamination theory [1].

The first theory was the strongest; as CC is thought to occur secondary to failure of normal involution of epidermoid formation within the middle ear cleft. A collection of stratified squamous epidermoid cells appears during embryonic development (weeks 3–5) and are instrumental in the development of middle ear mucosa. These remaining squamous cells grow slowly and become clinically apparent as a cholesteatoma usually during childhood [2]. This theory was strengthened by the presence of the embryonic rests always found in the anterosuperior mesotympanum – the commonest site of the lesion. Hence, there is powerful evidence to support epidermoid formation as the anlage for congenital cholesteatoma. The mean age of presentation of CC is 4.5 years with 3:1 male to female preponderance [3]. Cholesteatoma often remains asymptomatic and undetected for many years until it has grown into a considerable size or has caused complications [4]. Otoscopic recognition of congenital cholesteatoma is often reliable by experienced eyes but this diagnosis may be unfamiliar to other physicians.

The majority of cases involve the anterosuperior quadrant although they can arise from other parts of the tympanic membrane. If located in the posterosuperior quadrant, CC can involve the ossicular chain even in the absence of a conductive hearing loss [5].

However, patients with cholesteatoma may not present with apparent hearing loss, although the cholesteatoma has grown into a large size. This is due to the bridging effect of the cholesteatoma in transmitting and conducting sound in the middle ear despite the disruption of the ossicular chain [6,7]. HRCT of temporal bone is deemed necessary to evaluate the extension of disease, possible complications, and for pre-operative preparation. Unfortunately, it cannot differentiate between cholesteatoma sac and co-presence of granulation tissue, mucosal edema, or effusion [8]. MRI has the advantage of differentiating a cholesteatoma from other entities using the diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) to detect diffusion restriction in case of cholesteatoma.

MRI with contrast injection also plays an important role especially in our case where a facial schwannoma is still a considerable diagnosis. The latter quite enhances the contrast material and usually appears as a well-circumscribed lesion in contrast to cholesteatoma which often has irregular margins.

Facial palsy (FP) is an uncommon but recognized complication of cholesteatoma; its incidence ranges from 1 % to 3.4 % of cholesteatoma presentations [9]. Facial palsy secondary to cholesteatoma has several causes; osteitis and bony erosion from osteoclastic activity, direct compression resulting from edema, and enzymes secreted from the cholesteatoma matrix which can damage the facial nerve [10]. It has been shown that the facial nerve fills 35 to 65 % of the fallopian canal; the remaining portion is filled with extra neural blood vessels and connective tissue, without leaving any empty space; thus, edema can easily affect the neural transmission [11]. Direct pressure on the facial nerve due to cholesteatoma has also been reported; however, FP was expected only after blockage of more than 50 % of facial nerve fibers [12]. In slowly evolving facial palsy, the most likely etiology is erosion of the fallopian canal with compression of the facial nerve [13].

A large-based study of 2500 patients with peripheral facial nerve paralysis of unknown cause, 85 % (1448) of the patients showed improvement within 3 weeks and the other 15 % improved within 3 to 5 months; the point was that there was no further improvement after the average period of 6 months [14]. This fact can lead us to a very important diagnostic principle that any peripheral facial palsy that does not reach full recovery by 6 months should be referred to otolaryngologist for further workup looking for a lesion in the temporal bone.

Nelson et al. discussed surgical approaches to different classification of CC according to the site(s) of involvement and extension of the disease at the time of diagnosis [15]. In their large series of 119 patients, CC Type 1 (limited to the middle ear) was treated successfully via exploratory tympanotomy without the need for second look surgery. Those involving the postero-superior quadrant and the attic (Type 2) required canal wall up mastoidectomy, with or without posterior tympanotomy to control the disease.

According to Potsic et al. [16] grading system for congenital cholesteatoma (Table 1) our case was stage III and needed a definite canal wall up (CWU) procedure with extensive atticotomy to achieve the complete removal of the cholesteatoma. Also, considering that we are dealing with congenital cholesteatoma and no history of infection of otorrhea here, we advocated doing ossiculoplasty in the same primary surgery rather than doing it in a second-look procedure.

Regarding decompression surgery, most authors, as done in our case, prefer opening the fallopian canal at the smallest possible area (partial decompression) until reaching the normal facial nerve without redness or edema [17]; fibrosis, thinning, or interruption of the nerve was rarely found [18]. However, in other studies [11,18] a facial decompression was performed along the entire length of the nerve from the geniculate ganglion to the stylomastoid foramen (near-complete decompression). Wang et al. [10] concluded that there was no statistical significance for the facial recovery between partial decompression and near-complete decompression, supporting that, as observed in some cases, extent exposure of the facial nerve has been found with minimal involvement of the nerve. Controversies exist about whether an incision of the epineurium should be realized; Cawthorne [19] reported that the nerve sheath incision was not necessary for patients suffering from incomplete FP, but might be performed for patients with complete facial paralysis.

The work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [20].

Conclusion

Any patient with a history of recurrent or progressive facial palsy that is refractory to medical treatment for more than 6 months on the same side of a hearing loss should raise the suspicion of congenital cholesteatoma. The progression of facial palsy can range from involuntary twitching of one or all groups of facial muscles to a complete facial palsy on the affected side. Hemifacial spasms have been found to be important in the symptomology of congenital cholesteatoma. Complete removal of the disease combined with partial decompression surgery as soon as possible is indicated to obtain satisfactory results.
