Isolated oculomotor nerve palsy caused by an Onodi cell mucocele: a case report of a rare presentation
Authors: Reem AlJurayyed, Alya AlZabin, Lulwah AlTurki, Abdulrahman AlHumaizi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Onodi cell (OC) mucoceles are a rare subtype of paranasal sinus mucoceles that can compress adjacent neurovascular structures due to their anatomical proximity to the orbital apex. Isolated oculomotor nerve palsy (CN III) resulting from an OC mucocele is exceptionally uncommon and poses diagnostic challenges, especially in patients with vascular comorbidities.Case presentation:We present a rare case of a 71-year-old man with a 5-day history of left eyelid ptosis and diplopia. The patient had a background of well-controlled hypertension and diabetes. Physical and ophthalmologic examinations revealed isolated left CN III palsy without optic nerve involvement. Magnetic resonance imaging was inconclusive, but CT imaging showed complete opacification and expansion of the left OC. The patient underwent endoscopic sinus surgery, revealing a mucocele in the OC. Postoperative recovery was marked by rapid improvement in ptosis, with complete resolution of nerve palsy within 1 month.Clinical discussion:This case highlights the importance of considering OC mucoceles in the differential diagnosis of atypical cranial neuropathies. Delayed diagnosis may result from nonspecific presentations and confounding vascular risk factors. Imaging is critical in detecting these lesions and guiding timely intervention.Conclusion:OC mucoceles that cause CN III palsy are extremely rare. Imaging plays a crucial role in detecting these lesions, and timely surgical decompression is essential for achieving optimal recovery of neural function. This may also aid in accurately assessing these lesions.

Introduction

In 1904, Adolf Onodi introduced the Onodi cell (OC) as an anatomical variant in the paranasal sinuses, defined by the posterior ethmoid’s projection that pneumatizes superolaterally into the sphenoid sinus[1]. This variant is clinically significant due to its proximity to the optic nerve (CN II) and other critical neurovascular structures[2].

Paranasal sinus mucoceles are benign, epithelium-lined, mucus-filled lesions that exhibit expansile and locally invasive behavior[3]. They most commonly originate in the frontal (60–89%) and ethmoidal (8–30%) sinuses, while sphenoidal mucoceles are rare, comprising only 1–2% of cases[4]. Isolated oculomotor nerve (CN III) palsy is a rare presentation, often attributed to microvascular ischemia, particularly in patients with diabetes. However, compressive etiologies should be considered, especially when atypical features are present[6]. To the best of our knowledge, based on a thorough review of various databases, mucoceles in the OC have not been reported as a cause of isolated CN III palsy. Herein, we present the successful diagnosis and management of a patient with an isolated left CN III palsy resulting from an OC mucocele. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist[7].

Case presentation

A 71-year-old man was referred from ophthalmology to the otolaryngology (ENT) department due to a 5-day history of left eyelid drooping and diplopia. The patient had a history of well-controlled hypertension and diabetes. The patient denied nasal symptoms but reported intermittent headaches. Nasal endoscopy and the remainder of the cranial nerve examination were unremarkable. General physical examination revealed left eye ptosis and exotropia. Ophthalmologic examination revealed a visual acuity of 20/25 in the right eye and 20/30 in the left eye. In the extraocular muscle examination, there was restricted adduction, supraduction, and infraduction. Intraocular pressure was normal. The pupil was round, regular, and reactive to light, findings consistent with left CN III palsy (Fig. 1). The CN II, Trochlear (CN IV), and Abducens (CN VI) nerves were all intact. There were no significant abnormalities in laboratory tests. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated chronic small vessel ischemic changes, evidenced by bilateral, symmetrical T2/FLAIR hyperintensities in the frontoparietal periventricular deep white matter. There was no evidence of acute cerebrovascular insult, aneurysm, or space-occupying lesion along the cisternal segments of the CN III. The CN II, chiasm, and tracts showed normal course and signal intensity, with no abnormal post-contrast enhancement. No intraorbital or retrobulbar masses were identified. The extraocular muscles and retrobulbar fat appeared normal. Notably, mucosal thickening was observed in the adjacent paranasal sinuses (Fig. 2). A computed tomography (CT) scan of the head and orbit revealed isolated complete opacification of the left OC, with associated thinning and expansion of the sinus wall. The patient underwent limited functional endoscopic sinus surgery. Intraoperatively, edematous mucosa with mucus discharge was observed in the left OC. Histopathological evaluation was not performed, as both CT imaging and intraoperative findings clearly indicated a mucocele, showing a mucus-filled cavity lined with healthy mucosa and no suspicious features. Postoperatively, the patient’s discomfort with moving the eyeball decreased, but diplopia remained. The patient was prescribed a short course of oral antibiotics and budesonide nasal irrigations. Discussion

Mucoceles are sacs that contain mucus and are lined with epithelium. They occupy entirely a paranasal sinus as a consequence of the sinus ostium being obstructed. The production of osteolytic factors, such as prostaglandins and collagenases, facilitates their expansion. They are distinguished by a gradual increase in size over the course of several years, unless a pyelocele is the result of an infection[3]. Although sphenoid sinus mucoceles are rare, they are clinically significant due to their proximity to critical neurovascular structures. Their expansile nature can result in cranial neuropathies, including CN III, CN IV, and CN II involvement, depending on the direction of lesion extension and adjacent anatomy[1,5].

This case of a 71-year-old man presenting with isolated left CN III palsy highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges associated with OC mucoceles. While isolated CN III palsy has previously been reported in association with sphenoid mucoceles, involvement of an OC without concurrent optic neuropathy, as seen in our patient, remains exceedingly rare[8]. Lee and Au reported that up to 69% of patients with OC mucoceles experienced visual loss due to compression of CN II[9]. In contrast, our patient demonstrated isolated CN III dysfunction without optic involvement, suggesting anatomical variability in the spatial relationship between the mucocele and adjacent neurovascular structures. This case reinforces the importance of recognizing OC variants on imaging, as their proximity to the CN II and orbital apex can result in serious ophthalmologic consequences if diagnosis is delayed[4,9].

Moreover, our findings are consistent with those of Hyo Ku et al, who reported a rare case of isolated CN III palsy caused by OC sinusitis. This case underscores the anatomical vulnerability of the CN III to compressive and inflammatory changes in the posterior ethmoid region near the anterior clinoid process[10]. Our patient, like their case, did not exhibit nasal symptoms, which resulted in an initial misattribution of cranial nerve dysfunction to microvascular causes. The causative lesion was identified in both cases through imaging, and a timely endoscopic sinus surgery facilitated a complete neurological recovery.

The clinical presentation of OC mucoceles is frequently nonspecific, with common symptoms such as retro-orbital pain, visual disturbances, diplopia, or cranial neuropathies, depending on the extent and direction of expansion. Due to their deep location and proximity to CN II, even small lesions can result in significant ophthalmologic complications[11]. In our case, the patient presented with isolated CN III palsy, a rare manifestation of OC pathology. Initial suspicion centered on diabetic microvascular ischemia, a common etiology for third nerve palsy in elderly patients with diabetes[12]. However, the patient’s well-controlled diabetes and lack of typical ischemic features, such as pupillary sparing, prompted further investigation. Imaging subsequently revealed a mucocele in the region of the OC. This underscores the diagnostic value of CT and MRI in evaluating atypical cranial neuropathies and distinguishing mucoceles from neoplasms, aneurysms, or inflammatory lesions[3].

Endoscopic sinus surgery remains the standard of care for managing paranasal mucoceles, including those involving the OC. Several reports, including those by Hyo Ku et al and Rajput et al, have demonstrated complete resolution of cranial nerve deficits following prompt endoscopic drainage[10,13]. Early surgical intervention has been correlated with improved neurologic outcomes. Our patient experienced rapid and complete resolution of CN III palsy following endoscopic decompression, aligning with existing literature advocating for early surgical management. Adjunctive perioperative antibiotics and corticosteroids may further support favorable outcomes by addressing secondary infection and reducing inflammatory edema, respectively[10].

Strengths and limitations

This case highlights a rare presentation of isolated CN III palsy caused by an OC mucocele, underscoring the importance of advanced imaging in differentiating compressive from vascular etiologies and demonstrating the value of multidisciplinary input from ophthalmology, neurology, and otolaryngology. The complete recovery following timely endoscopic sinus surgery further emphasizes the effectiveness of early surgical intervention. However, as a single-patient report, the findings cannot be generalized; histopathological confirmation was not obtained, and the initial diagnostic challenge posed by vascular comorbidities may limit reproducibility. While surgery proved successful in this case, potential complications and uncertain long-term outcomes warrant caution before extrapolating these results to broader populations. Although histopathological confirmation was not obtained, the diagnosis was clear radiologically and intraoperatively, consistent with a typical mucocele presentation.

Conclusion

It is essential to consider OC mucoceles as a rare cause of isolated CN III palsy. This case emphasizes the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for sinus-related pathology in patients presenting with isolated cranial neuropathies. Recognition of OC involvement, appropriate imaging, and timely surgical intervention are critical to prevent permanent neurological sequelae. This case also aids surgical education by emphasizing the importance of recognizing rare anatomical variants and their potential complications. Awareness of OC involvement can improve preoperative planning and help minimize the risk of iatrogenic injury during endoscopic sinus surgery.
