AIPred: comprehensive prediction and analysis of non-histone acetylation via protein language model and interpretable machine learning
Authors: Yuqing Geng, Hao Luo, Feng Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-histone lysine acetylation is a widespread protein post-translational modification that regulates almost all key cellular processes, and its dysregulation is closely associated with various human diseases. Precise identification of non-histone acetylation sites is crucial for understanding their biological functions, but existing computational methods face challenges in prediction accuracy, model interpretability, and usability.ResultsHere, we presented AIPred (Acetylation Interpretable Prediction), an integrated framework that combines ESM Cambrian protein language model embeddings with diverse bioinformatics features through interpretable machine learning for prediction and analysis. Systematic evaluation demonstrated AIPred’s superior performance, achieving improvements of 16.7%, 19.8%, and 20.8% over the state-of-the-art model in F1-score, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), and area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC), respectively. Through Shapley additive explanations and gradient attribution analysis, we revealed key features and sequence patterns driving model decisions. Moreover, we developed a user-friendly online prediction server and a comprehensive prediction database. AIPred analysis of TDP-43 protein revealed functionally important acetylation sites, including novel predictions consistent with recent experimental findings.ConclusionsAIPred provides an accurate, interpretable, and accessible computational framework for predicting non-histone acetylation sites, which is expected to accelerate targeted research on non-histone acetylation-related mechanisms in cellular regulation and disease pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-025-02418-1.

Background

Lysine acetylation is a widespread and functionally diverse protein post-translational modification (PTM) catalyzed by lysine acetyltransferases (KATs) [1]. While histone acetylation’s role in transcriptional regulation has been extensively studied, mass spectrometry analyses have revealed that the majority of acetylation actually occurs on non-histone proteins, involving thousands of distinct proteins that constitute the major portion of the mammalian cellular acetylome [1–6]. Non-histone acetylation affects protein function by regulating protein stability, enzymatic activity, subcellular localization, and protein interactions, thereby participating in almost all key cellular processes [2, 4]. For instance, the acetylation of the tumor suppressor p53 regulates its DNA binding ability and stability [7], while in DNA damage repair, acetylation controls the function of key proteins such as ATM, 53BP1, and APE1 [8, 9]. Given its fundamental role in cellular regulation, dysregulation of non-histone acetylation contributes to disease pathogenesis. In cancer, SMAD3 acetylation promotes breast cancer metastasis [10], while metabolic protein deregulation contributes to multiple cancer types [11–14]. In neurodegeneration, tau protein modifications affect Alzheimer’s pathology [15].

Despite growing recognition of non-histone acetylation’s importance, our understanding of its functional mechanisms remains limited due to the scarcity of experimental data and the inherent high cost, labor-intensive, and time-consuming nature of wet-lab approaches, compounded by the relatively low stoichiometry of these modifications [2, 6]. With the rapid rise of artificial intelligence and bioinformatics [16], computational methods offer an efficient alternative for large-scale identification of non-histone acetylation sites. This computational approach has been successfully applied to diverse modifications including DNA methylation [17], RNA modifications [18–20], and various protein PTMs such as phosphorylation [21], sumoylation [22, 23], and lactylation [24–26], demonstrating the broad applicability of computational prediction methods.

For acetylation site prediction specifically, a series of computational methods have been developed. Early methods, such as ASEB [27], GPS-PAIL [28], and LysAcet [29], were primarily based on sequence similarity and traditional machine learning methods, built on relatively small datasets. The introduction of deep learning models improved prediction performance. MusiteDeep [30] utilized convolutional neural network and capsule neural network architectures, achieving a high area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) value of 0.978 for N6-acetyllysine prediction. Deep-PLA [31] combined deep neural networks with multiple sequence features and used particle swarm optimization for optimization, showing superior performance over GPS-PAIL in five-fold cross-validation. DeepDA-Ace [32] and Semi-ssPTM [33] employed an adversarial learning-based domain adaptation method for species-specific acetylation site prediction. Most recently, TransPTM [34] leveraged a pre-trained protein language model with graph neural networks, becoming the first model specifically focused on non-histone acetylation prediction and outperforming previous methods on non-histone acetylation benchmarks.

Although computational methods for acetylation site prediction have advanced considerably, several challenges persist for non-histone acetylation. Firstly, most existing predictors are developed using datasets that predominantly contain histone acetylation sites, potentially compromising their accuracy for non-histone-specific prediction. Secondly, while advanced deep learning models demonstrate superior performance, they suffer from high parameter complexity, substantial computational requirements, and poor interpretability, hindering researchers’ ability to extract meaningful biological insights about sequence determinants and functional patterns crucial for non-histone acetylation. Finally, the limited availability of dedicated, accessible computational tools specifically designed for non-histone acetylation prediction creates barriers for experimental researchers seeking to integrate computational predictions into their workflows.

To address these challenges, we developed AIPred. This work presents a computational framework that integrates two powerful approaches: ESM Cambrian (ESM C) [35], a state-of-the-art (SOTA) protein language model trained on massive sequence databases to capture deep contextual patterns, and iFeatureOmega [36], a comprehensive platform for extracting diverse bioinformatics features automatically. We utilize LightGBM [37], an interpretable machine learning model, to perform feature selection from these complementary data sources, and subsequently train the predictive model. AIPred is designed not only to achieve SOTA predictive accuracy but also to provide deep biological insights into the model’s decisions, and these capabilities are made accessible to the community via a user-friendly web server and an extensive prediction database.

Results

Overview of benchmark dataset and the AIPred framework

Our model was developed and evaluated on a refined version of the non-histone acetylation collection (NHAC) dataset, originally established by Meng et al. [34] and obtained from the corresponding public repository [38]. The positive samples in the dataset consist of experimentally verified non-histone acetylation sites, covering diverse protein families and cellular functions, while all other lysine residues from the same proteins were designated as negative samples. The dataset comprises peptide fragments of varying lengths centered on the lysine residue. The original NHAC dataset had first undergone homology reduction using CD-HIT [39] and was then partitioned into training, validation, and independent test sets. To further improve dataset quality, we performed additional homology reduction to eliminate highly redundant samples while maintaining the original subset assignments for all retained samples, with the final sample distribution shown in Table 1. We developed AIPred, a computational framework with architecture shown in Fig. 1. At its core, AIPred employs a sophisticated dual-stream feature extraction strategy to capture a wide spectrum of protein sequence information (Fig. 1A). In the first stream, protein sequences are first tokenized and then ESM C is used to generate deep contextual embeddings, where the self-attention mechanism enables the special [cls] token to aggregate contextual information from the entire sequence into a single 1152-dimensional embedding vector. The second stream utilizes iFeatureOmega to generate diverse bioinformatics features. Through systematic training of LightGBM models and evaluation on the validation set, we selected optimal parameters for both streams: the best ESM C hidden layer and input sequence length, and the optimal sequence length for iFeatureOmega features. Following the parallel optimizations of the two feature streams, we fused these two types of features and then leveraged LightGBM for feature dimensionality reduction. We first trained a preliminary LightGBM model on the complete fused feature set and then utilized its inherent feature importance evaluation mechanism to precisely select only those features with non-zero split gain during the model’s decision tree construction. Finally, we trained a new LightGBM model using only this most informative feature subset to create the final, lightweight, and high-performance AIPred predictor. A key innovation of the AIPred framework, distinguishing it from many black-box predictors, is its deep integration of model interpretability (Fig. 1B). We integrated two powerful analytical methods: SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) TreeExplainer and gradient attribution analysis. SHAP analysis systematically deciphers the model’s logic by identifying the key bioinformatics features, such as amino acid composition and physicochemical properties, that drive its predictions. Complementing this, gradient attribution analysis delves into the ESM C embeddings to pinpoint the specific amino acid positions within a sequence that are most influential, thereby revealing critical sequence motifs. These predictive and analytical capabilities are delivered to the scientific community through an integrated online platform (Fig. 1C). This includes a user-friendly web server that provides not only accurate predictions but also real-time, visual interpretability analysis for each submitted site. Alongside the server is the predicted non-histone acetylation database (PNAD), a comprehensive resource containing pre-computed predictions for 18,367 human non-histone proteins, where each predicted site is also accompanied by a full interpretability report.

Optimizing sequence length and ESM C layer depth for feature extraction

To derive the most informative features, we systematically optimized key hyperparameters for both the ESM C language model and the iFeatureOmega platform.

First, we explored the impact of input sequence length and hidden layer depth on embeddings from the ESM C model. We systematically evaluated 396 combinations by pairing 11 input sequence lengths (from 11 to 61 amino acids, aa) with each of the 36 hidden transformer layers. For each pair, we trained a LightGBM model and assessed its performance on the validation set. The results, visualized as a heatmap of AUC values in Fig. 2A, reveal that performance does not linearly correlate with sequence length or layer depth. The global optimum AUC (0.7739) was achieved with an intermediate sequence length (41 aa) and a mid-to-deep hidden layer (layer 21). Our analysis revealed a significant interaction effect between input sequence length and optimal layer depth. Notably, very short sequences (11 aa) performed best with features from deeper layers (layer 30), suggesting a need for more extensive feature abstraction. Conversely, longer sequences (55 aa) benefited more from shallower layers (layer 7), which likely capture essential local motifs more effectively without diluting the signal for a localized event like acetylation. Based on this evaluation, we selected embeddings from layer 21 with a 41 aa input sequence for our final model. Fig. 2Feature extraction optimization. In parallel, we optimized the features generated from the iFeatureOmega platform. For each sequence length dataset, we extracted 71 types of protein sequence features (totaling between 25,126 and 32,576 features, detailed in Additional file 1: Table S1), trained a corresponding LightGBM model, and evaluated its performance. Figure 2B and C illustrate the changes in AUC and area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) with respect to sequence length, with specific metrics available in Additional file 1: Table S2. Overall, the performance trended upward with increased sequence length, indicating that longer fragments capture more discriminative information. However, the improvement was not strictly monotonic, with notable fluctuations around a sequence length of 41. This suggests a trade-off where longer sequences add both useful information and potential noise from distant regions. At greater lengths (55 or 61 aa), the benefits of broader context ultimately outweighed the noise, leading to further performance gains. We chose the 61 aa sequence length for feature extraction from iFeatureOmega, as AUPRC peaked at 0.5714 for this length, notably higher than the second-best performance of 0.5586 at length 55, while AUC (0.7949) remained nearly optimal compared to its maximum (0.7966 at length 55).

Our strategy of using different sequence lengths is designed to maximize the unique strengths of each feature type. ESM C best captures deep contextual information within a moderate 41 aa window, avoiding the signal dilution that can occur with longer sequences. In contrast, iFeatureOmega statistical features benefit from a wider 61 aa window to aggregate richer long-range patterns. This approach allows our model to synergize the deep, local insights from ESM C with the broad, statistical patterns from iFeatureOmega, thereby enhancing overall predictive power.

Feature dimensionality reduction and ablation study

To enhance model efficiency and potentially boost predictive accuracy by eliminating noise from our high-dimensional feature set, we implemented a data-driven feature selection strategy. We began by integrating the optimal features from the ESM C language model (sequence length 41, hidden layer 21, 1152 dimensions) and the iFeatureOmega platform (sequence length 61, 32,576 dimensions), creating an initial set with 33,728 features. A predictive model trained on this complete set, named the Fusion model, served as a performance baseline for further optimization. We then leveraged the inherent “split” feature importance metric from LightGBM, which provides reliable assessments without additional computational overhead. Our cumulative importance analysis (shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S1) revealed that the top 9.6% of features accounted for the vast majority of the model’s predictive power. Therefore, we selected only those features with non-zero importance, successfully reducing the dimensionality from 33,728 to 3259. By retraining the model on this refined feature subset, we created our proposed predictor, AIPred.

We then conducted an ablation study to systematically evaluate our strategy. We compared the performance of AIPred on the independent test set against three other models: one using only ESM C features, one using only iFeatureOmega features, and the Fusion model containing all features. Figure 3 shows the performance of these four models versus their feature dimensionality.

The results of the ablation study demonstrate the value of each step in our strategy. Among the baseline models, the one using only ESM C features performed poorly. The model with only iFeatureOmega features achieved good performance, with an AUC of 0.8426 and an AUPRC of 0.6457, but at the cost of high dimensionality. The effectiveness of feature fusion was validated with the Fusion model, which improved sensitivity, AUPRC, F1-score, and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) by 1.3%, 1.87%, 1.25%, and 1.02% respectively, compared to the iFeatureOmega model, confirming the complementarity of the two feature sources. Ultimately, by reducing the feature set to just 9.6% of the Fusion model’s size, AIPred achieved an optimal balance of performance and efficiency, not only maintaining but actually improving upon the full-featured model’s performance. This is primarily because removing numerous redundant and noisy features allows the model to focus on genuinely discriminative signals, reducing the risk of overfitting and enhancing its generalization ability on the independent test set. Specifically, compared to the Fusion model, AIPred achieved substantial improvements across all metrics: sensitivity increased by 3.89%, F1-score by 3.61%, MCC by 3.0%, accuracy by 0.32%, and AUC by 0.40%. Although its AUPRC showed a slight decrease of 0.48%, it still significantly outperformed the baseline model that used only iFeatureOmega features. Performance comparison of different machine learning models

To systematically evaluate the LightGBM classifier and explore alternative architectures, we benchmarked six representative machine learning models using ESM C embeddings and iFeatureOmega features: support vector machine (SVM) [40], random forest (RF) [41], multi-layer perceptron (MLP) [42], CatBoost [43], XGBoost [44], and a custom convolutional neural network (CNN) [45]. The CNN model combines two CNN layers with a self-attention module to capture sequence patterns, fusing them with iFeatureOmega features through an MLP, and incorporates an asymmetric loss function to address class imbalance (see Additional file 1: Information S1).

To ensure robust statistical evaluation, we implemented a comprehensive bootstrap resampling framework. We performed 1000 iterations of bootstrap resampling with replacement on the independent test set, maintaining the original sample size in each bootstrap iteration. For each method, we computed the mean and standard deviation across six key performance metrics (accuracy, F1-score, MCC, AUC, AUPRC, and sensitivity) based on these 1000 bootstrap samples (detailed results in Additional file 1: Table S3). To assess the statistical significance of performance differences between methods, we applied the Wilcoxon signed-rank test to the paired bootstrap distributions (comprehensive p-values reported in Additional file 1: Tables S4–9).

As presented in Fig. 4, AIPred achieved the best mean performance across all key metrics except for sensitivity, including accuracy (0.9564), F1-score (0.6358), MCC (0.6566), AUC (0.8435), and AUPRC (0.6578) (p < 0.001). Notably, as shown in Fig. 4H, AIPred maintained a precision of nearly 100% at a recall below 40%, outperforming other methods that showed a marked decline in precision in this range. Among all tested models, gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT) algorithms (AIPred, XGBoost, CatBoost) delivered outstanding overall performance, significantly surpassing traditional SVM, RF, and simple MLP models. This superiority stems from their inherent advantages in handling high-dimensional, sparse bioinformatics feature data characterized by complex non-linear relationships [46]. Within the GBDT family, LightGBM demonstrated optimal performance due to its unique optimization mechanisms, including histogram algorithm and leaf-wise tree growth strategy (detailed in the Methods section), which provide greater efficiency and stronger model expressiveness when processing high-dimensional feature spaces compared to other GBDT implementations [37]. While our custom CNN model achieved the highest sensitivity (0.5077), demonstrating the potential of asymmetric loss functions for identifying minority-class samples, its overall performance was constrained by severe overfitting. The mismatch between the model’s vast parameter count and our dataset scale caused training loss to decrease steadily while validation loss exhibited significant fluctuations and upward trends in later stages (see Additional file 1: Fig. S2). Performance comparison with existing methods

To further evaluate the performance of AIPred, we compared it with four SOTA methods for acetylation site prediction: GPS-PAIL [28], Deep-PLA [31], MusiteDeep [30], and TransPTM [34]. To ensure the most objective and fair comparison possible, the performance of all methods was evaluated on the independent test set from the original NHAC dataset, which contains 1092 samples, consisting of 173 positive samples (acetylation sites) and 919 negative samples (non-acetylation sites).

We obtained prediction results for MusiteDeep and TransPTM via their online service and source code, respectively. Performance metrics for GPS-PAIL and Deep-PLA were cited from Meng et al. [34] due to service inaccessibility and lack of probability scores. For MusiteDeep, TransPTM, and AIPred, we applied the same bootstrap resampling strategy (1000 iterations) and Wilcoxon signed-rank test as described previously.

The detailed performance comparison of all methods is presented in Table 2, with the comparison between MusiteDeep, TransPTM, and AIPred visualized in Fig. 5. AIPred demonstrated superior performance across all metrics. Compared to the recent TransPTM, AIPred improved by 3.57% in accuracy and 5.76% in AUC (p < 0.001). For key metrics on imbalanced datasets, such as F1-score, MCC, and AUPRC, AIPred achieved greater breakthroughs with improvements of 16.7%, 19.8%, and 20.8% respectively (p < 0.001). A similar advantage was observed in the comparison against MusiteDeep. Compared to early methods such as GPS-PAIL and Deep-PLA, the advantages of AIPred are more significant; for example, its MCC value increases by 42.2% and 47.2%, respectively. Interpretable analysis of AIPred reveals key biological characteristics of non-histone acetylation

To gain deeper insights into the key biological information underlying AIPred’s decision-making process, we conducted an in-depth interpretability analysis on the final LightGBM model using SHAP TreeExplainer [47]. This method directly calculates feature contributions based on LightGBM’s inherent tree structure, providing efficient and accurate explanations (detailed in the Methods section). We calculated the feature importance distribution for all samples in the dataset, averaged them to obtain the global feature importance, and visualized the results in Fig. 6 alongside a Two Sample Logo analysis [48], which shows the significant differences in sequence patterns between positive and negative samples, to provide a biological context for our computational findings.

First, we analyzed the importance distribution of different feature types in the final AIPred model (Fig. 6A, detailed data in Additional file 1: Table S16). Features based on enhanced amino acid composition (EAAC) contributed the highest importance at 31.28%, indicating that local amino acid composition information is crucial for prediction. These were closely followed by embedding features from the ESM C model with an importance of 18.10%, confirming the significant contribution of contextual dependency information captured by the language model to prediction performance. Additionally, features from adaptive skip dipeptide composition (ASDC, 8.50%), cross covariance (CC, 5.28%), physicochemical properties (ZScale, 4.03%; AAIndex, 3.28%), and evolutionary conservation information (BLOSUM62, 3.43%) also showed notable importance in the model. This diverse feature importance distribution ensured that the model could capture acetylation-related signals from multiple dimensions, demonstrating the effectiveness of our feature fusion strategy.

Next, we analyzed the contribution of individual features to the model’s predictions and presented the top ten most important features (Fig. 6B), revealing the underlying biochemical principles of substrate recognition learned by AIPred for KATs. The most influential features are those related to EAAC, such as EAAC_SW.26.K and SW.19.K, which collectively indicate a significant lysine (K) enrichment in the region upstream of the acetylation site (positions 14–30 in Fig. 6C). This finding is consistent with the observation that major KATs show a strong preference for lysines located within basic residue clusters (i.e., local environments rich in positive charge), where this electrostatic attraction is considered key to promoting enzyme–substrate binding [49]. The top ten features also include two ASDC features, ASDC_EL and ASDC_SR, which represent the normalized frequencies of the dipeptides E-L and S-R, respectively. The enrichment of ASDC_SR reveals a synergistic mechanism. On one hand, arginine (R), as a basic amino acid, further enhances the aforementioned basic residue cluster effect. On the other hand, serine (S) is a well-known phosphorylation target. This suggests that the model may have learned a signal pattern of acetylation-phosphorylation crosstalk, where the phosphorylation of a neighboring serine could act as a pre-processing signal to promote substrate recognition and acetylation by KATs, a regulatory mechanism that has been reported in key proteins like p53 [50]. Conversely, the significant depletion of ASDC_EL points to a negative selection mechanism. The negatively charged glutamic acid (E) can cause electrostatic repulsion with the acidic regions in the active pockets of many KATs (like p300/CBP), thereby hindering substrate binding. Meanwhile, the bulky and hydrophobic leucine (L) could introduce steric hindrance in certain conformations [51].

Additionally, the feature OPF7_t2_p29_Polarizability, a binary value, indicates whether the 29th amino acid in the sequence belongs to the Type 2 group based on polarizability classification (including A, G, P, S, T)—essentially identifying whether this position contains a low-polarizability amino acid. Figure 6C shows significant enrichment of A and G at position 29 in positive samples, a pattern successfully captured by AIPred. To further analyze differences in amino acid polarizability upstream and downstream of acetylation versus non-acetylation sites, we generated Fig. 6D, where blue represents high-polarizability amino acids, red represents low-polarizability amino acids, and black represents other amino acids with medium or mixed polarizability. As shown in Fig. 6D, there is significant enrichment of low-polarizability amino acids in the context of acetylation sites, particularly in the upstream vicinity, representing another important sequence feature beyond lysine enrichment.

Gradient analysis reveals position-specific contributions within ESM C embeddings

To gain a deeper understanding of the significant ESM C embeddings in the AIPred model, we analyzed how their signals originate from the input protein sequence. To achieve this, we constructed an end-to-end interpretability framework that combines SHAP values with gradient attribution analysis (see Methods for details). The framework attributes abstract feature contributions to specific sequence locations by performing a weighted sum of the contribution gradients for each amino acid position, using the SHAP value of each embedding dimension as the corresponding weight. This approach enables us to move beyond feature-level analysis and directly understand the language model’s attention patterns at the sequence level.

By calculating and visualizing the average position-specific importance scores for positive (acetylation) and negative (non-acetylation) sites (Fig. 7), we uncovered significant position-dependent patterns that the model learned from ESM C embeddings to distinguish between the two site types. For positive samples, the model identified notable positive contribution peaks in regions upstream of the acetylated lysine (position 0), such as at positions −14 to −13 and −10 to −7 (particularly at −7 with a score of 0.0243), as well as in downstream regions like + 2 to + 3. These signals may correspond to distal motifs that promote acetyltransferase recognition or represent a deeper, more precise contextualization of the upstream lysine enrichment previously discovered through EAAC features. In stark contrast, the region proximal to the central lysine (the −3 to + 1 region), especially at position + 1 (score = −0.0411), exhibited a strong negative contribution. This suggests the model learned a critical proximal gating mechanism, which may constitute a necessary condition for acetylation: the process requires not only favorable distal signals but also the avoidance of proximal inhibitory signals, thereby providing more refined regulatory information than traditional enrichment patterns. To maximize the utility of our model for the scientific community, we have developed and deployed the AIPred online prediction service, a user-friendly and openly accessible web server available at http://tubic.tju.edu.cn/AIPred. This platform is specifically designed to serve life science researchers in the fields of protein function, post-translational modification, and disease pathogenesis by providing instant and interpretable prediction results to guide wet-lab experiments.

The service features a concise and intuitive workflow. Initially, users submit protein sequences through the web interface, with options to either paste sequences directly into a text box or upload a FASTA file. Upon submission, our service asynchronously executes time-consuming tasks—including protein feature extraction, model inference, and gradient attribution analysis—leveraging a robust and efficient technology stack to ensure processing efficiency. Once the prediction is complete, users are directed to a results page. Additionally, historical results can be retrieved at any time using the assigned task ID.

Predicted non-histone acetylation database

To provide the research community with a convenient resource on human non-histone acetylation, we constructed the predicted non-histone acetylation database (PNAD). This database was built based on 18,367 human non-histone protein sequences obtained from UniProt, and we used the AIPred model developed in this study to predict acetylation for all valid lysine residues that meet the context-length requirements. To balance precision and recall, we determined the optimal probability threshold (0.304625) by maximizing the F1-score on the independent test set. All sites predicted by AIPred with a probability exceeding this threshold were considered potential acetylation sites, resulting in 158,786 predicted sites being stored in PNAD.

PNAD offers convenient browse and search functionalities. Users can browse via a protein list or a list of predicted acetylation sites. They are able to quickly locate target proteins by protein name, UniProt ID, or gene name and can also filter for high-confidence sites by setting a prediction probability range. Furthermore, the database provides detailed information pages for each protein and every predicted acetylation site, where users can quickly access all predicted sites within a specific protein and obtain the same interpretability analysis charts as the online prediction service.

The model’s novel prediction, site K97, is located within the classic bipartite nuclear localization signal (NLS) of TDP-43. Discussion

While histone acetylation is well-understood, non-histone acetylation research faces challenges due to experimental limitations and low modification stoichiometry. AIPred addresses key computational challenges: accuracy, interpretability, and accessibility. A cornerstone of AIPred is its feature fusion strategy, which combines deep contextual information from ESM C with diverse traditional bioinformatics features, providing a multi-dimensional perspective on acetylation recognition patterns. Furthermore, we leveraged LightGBM’s inherent interpretability to intelligently select only the most informative features. By retaining only features with demonstrated predictive value (9.6% of the original set), we not only improved computational efficiency but also enhanced model performance, demonstrating that in the high-dimensional feature space of protein modification prediction, more features do not necessarily yield better results.

Our choice of a LightGBM-based model over deep learning architectures was a deliberate decision validated by our comparative experiments. Although deep learning models can be powerful, they often operate as “black boxes” and carry a significant risk of overfitting. This was evident in our tests: a custom CNN model suffered from severe overfitting due to a mismatch between its large number of parameters and our dataset’s size, leading to poor generalization, even though it is considered a small model in the context of deep learning. In contrast, GBDT-family algorithms like LightGBM are exceptionally well-suited for handling the high-dimensional, sparse, and non-linear feature data typical in bioinformatics, delivering robust performance without the same level of overfitting risk. This approach aligns with our core objective of creating a transparent model. By applying advanced interpretability methods such as SHAP TreeExplainer and gradient attribution analysis, we were able to connect the model’s predictions to specific biological contexts and sequence patterns—an outcome that is difficult to achieve directly with less transparent deep learning methods.

Despite AIPred’s significant advances, some limitations remain and warrant discussion. Firstly, while the model excels in comprehensive performance metrics like F1-score and MCC, its sensitivity is not the highest among all compared methods, which indicates a conservative prediction strategy that prioritizes high precision. This implies that while the sites predicted by the AIPred service are of high confidence, a fraction of true acetylation sites may be missed. Users should be aware of this limitation, as the absence of a prediction for a site does not definitively rule out its potential for acetylation, especially when other biological evidence suggests it. Secondly, the current model training relies on existing experimentally verified data, and the overall scale of the dataset remains limited, which may affect its generalization ability. As more high-quality experimental data becomes available in the future, we hope to train models with potentially further improved performance. Thirdly, AIPred currently utilizes only protein sequence information; future research could explore integrating three-dimensional structural features to capture patterns at the structural level [58]. Finally, the framework established by AIPred has excellent scalability and could be applied in the future to the prediction and analysis of other important PTMs, ultimately contributing to the construction of a unified, interpretable PTM prediction platform.

Conclusions

In this study, we presented AIPred, a comprehensive framework for predicting non-histone acetylation sites. The framework integrates ESM C language model embeddings with bioinformatics features from the iFeatureOmega platform through an interpretable LightGBM classifier. Our feature selection strategy reduced dimensionality from 33,728 to 3259 features while maintaining optimal performance. AIPred surpassed all state-of-the-art models, achieving average AUC and AUPRC values of 0.8615 and 0.6907, respectively. Beyond prediction performance, we employed SHAP TreeExplainer and gradient attribution analysis to reveal key features and sequence patterns driving model decisions. This analysis provided new insights into non-histone acetylation recognition mechanisms. The publicly available web server and database provide the research community with accessible tools for hypothesis generation and experimental design. The framework’s practical utility was validated through a case study, successfully identifying functionally important sites. These included novel predictions consistent with recent experimental findings. AIPred establishes a solid foundation for predicting potential non-histone acetylation sites and understanding their functional complexity. The framework has the potential to accelerate scientific discoveries in protein regulation and disease mechanisms.

ESM C model and embedding extraction

ESM C is a next-generation protein language model that captures biological properties in protein sequences through deep representation learning [35]. The model integrates Pre-Layer Normalization structure, rotary position embeddings, and SwiGLU activation functions, enhancing generalization capabilities. ESM C was trained on extensive protein sequence datasets using masked language modeling, learning to predict randomly masked amino acids to understand sequence patterns and biological properties. In this study, we employed the ESM C 600M parameter model, which contains 36 hidden transformer layers, a model width of 1152, 18 attention heads, and a context processing length of 2048 tokens.

For embedding extraction, we developed a systematic processing pipeline to generate optimized representations from protein sequences. Protein sequences were batch-processed through the ESM C model, capturing hidden states from all 36 transformer layers. We systematically explored different combinations of sequence lengths (11 to 61 aa) and hidden layers (layers 0–35) to determine optimal parameters through validation set performance evaluation.

For each sequence, we extracted the [cls] token representation from the selected optimal hidden layer. In the transformer architecture, the [cls] token is added as a special token at the beginning of the sequence and aggregates information from the entire sequence through self-attention mechanisms [59]. The [cls] token interacts with all sequence positions layer by layer, forming a representation containing global contextual information that serves as a comprehensive summary of the protein sequence. This approach generates a 1152-dimensional feature vector for each sequence, effectively capturing complex contextual dependencies and long-distance interactions within protein sequences, which is particularly important for identifying sequence patterns that distinguish acetylation sites from non-acetylation sites in non-histone proteins.

iFeatureOmega feature extraction

iFeatureOmega is a comprehensive feature engineering platform, specifically designed for extracting feature representations from molecular sequences, structures, and ligand datasets [36]. In this study, we utilized iFeatureOmega to extract features from protein sequences for non-histone acetylation site prediction. iFeatureOmega supports 71 different protein sequence feature encoding schemes, which can be divided into several main categories, including amino acid composition, sequence correlation, physicochemical properties, and others. Based on optimization research, we extracted features from the dataset with a sequence length of 61 using iFeatureOmega, forming a high-dimensional feature space (32,576 dimensions). These features, combined with ESM C embedding features, provided multi-dimensional information sources for AIPred.

LightGBM model

LightGBM, developed by Microsoft Research, is a highly GBDT framework designed to address efficiency and scalability challenges in large-scale machine learning tasks [37]. Traditional GBDT implementations face high computational complexity when processing high-dimensional features and large datasets, as they require scanning all data instances for each feature to estimate information gain at all possible split points. This computational bottleneck makes these implementations inefficient in big data environments. LightGBM significantly enhances training speed and memory efficiency through algorithmic innovations while improving prediction accuracy, establishing itself as an excellent tool for large-scale data processing in machine learning.

LightGBM’s excellent performance primarily stems from two core algorithmic innovations. Its histogram algorithm differs from traditional pre-sorting methods by discretizing continuous feature values into a fixed number of bins and constructing feature histograms based on these bins. This approach reduces the computational complexity of finding split points from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(\mathrm{data})$$\end{document}O(data) to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(\mathrm{bin})$$\end{document}O(bin), significantly decreasing computational demands. The histogram algorithm also implements histogram subtraction technique, obtaining child node histograms by subtracting sibling node histograms from parent node histograms, avoiding redundant calculations. For memory management, the histogram algorithm stores only bin values of features rather than original values and sorting positions, significantly reducing memory requirements. The leaf-wise tree growth strategy represents another important innovation, selecting the leaf with the maximum gain for splitting from all leaves, rather than using the traditional level-wise approach. This strategy ensures maximum loss reduction with the same number of splits, building more efficient tree structures. While leaf-wise growth may result in deeper trees, increasing overfitting risk, LightGBM effectively mitigates this issue through regularization mechanisms such as depth limits and leaf count controls.

Unlike black-box models, LightGBM provides inherent interpretability through its decision tree-based ensemble structure. Each prediction results from traceable decision paths, enabling clear analysis of feature contributions to model decisions. We employed the “split” importance method, which quantifies feature importance by accumulating the frequency of each feature appearing as a split point across all decision trees. Features frequently used for splitting indicate greater contribution to model decisions and decision boundary construction. LightGBM automatically tracks these statistics during training without additional computational overhead, providing intrinsic interpretability essential for biological insight extraction.

Performance metrics

Sensitivity (also known as recall), measures the proportion of actual positive samples that are correctly identified. Given our imbalanced dataset, we utilized the F1-score, which is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, offering a balanced measure particularly suited for uneven class distributions. MCC, ranging from −1 to 1, offers a reliable measure for binary classification with imbalanced data, with higher values indicating better performance. AUC evaluates the model’s discriminative ability across various thresholds by plotting true positive rate (TPR) against false positive rate (FPR). We also calculated AUPRC, which proves especially informative for imbalanced datasets as it maintains sensitivity to minority class performance by plotting precision against recall at different thresholds. These metrics collectively provide a comprehensive assessment of AIPred’s capabilities, with particular attention to the challenges posed by class imbalance in acetylation site prediction. To establish connections between abstract ESM C embedding features and sequence positions, we employed an end-to-end interpretability framework combining SHAP values with gradient analysis.
