Synergistic construction of g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO ternary nanocomposite for enhanced photocatalytic degradation under visible light
Authors: Ying Lei, Qijing Bao, Feng Lin, Jian Zhang, Yanji Zhang, Yuechao Wu, Liang Lv, Liyong Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGraphite carbon nitride (g-C3N4) has emerged as a promising photocatalyst for visible-light-driven applications due to its suitable band structure. However, the rapid recombination of photogenerated electron-hole pairs in bulk g-C3N4 significantly hinders its photocatalytic efficiency. In this study, we report the fabrication of a ternary g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO (CMG) nanocomposite via in situ photoreduction-induced exfoliation. The introduction of MoS2 and rGO not only facilitates the exfoliation of g-C3N4 into ultrathin nanosheet, thereby exposing more active sites, but also enables the formation of tightly coupled heterojunctions that promote charge separation and transfer. Photoluminescence (PL) analyses confirm the suppressed recombination of charge carriers, indicative of enhanced charge dynamics within the heterojunction between MoS2, rGO and g-C3N4. Among the synthesized materials, the optimized CMG-175 composite exhibits remarkable photocatalytic activity, achieving a 95.7% degradation rate of Rhodamine B (RhB) within 30 min under visible light irradiation, which is 23.6-fold enhancement compared to pristine g-C3N4. Mechanistic studies reveal that photogenerated holes and superoxide radicals are the predominant reactive species driving the degradation process. This work highlights a rational design strategy for constructing high-performance g-C3N4-based heterojunction photocatalysts and provides valuable insight into the synergistic interactions among MoS2, rGO, and g-C3N4 for efficient organic pollutant removal.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13065-025-01658-0.Keywords: Ternary g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO composite, Heterojunction photocatalyst, Visible-light-driven degradation, Charge separation

Introduction

The rapid pace of industrial globalization and the extensive consumption of fossil fuels have led to increasingly severe environmental issues. In response, the development of sustainable technologies for environmental remediation and clean energy production has become a global priority. Among various approaches, semiconductor-based photocatalysis has garnered significant attention due to its potential to harness solar energy for pollutant degradation and fuel generation in an environmentally benign manner.

g-C3N4, a metal-free polymeric semiconductor with a moderate band gap (~ 2.7 eV), stands out as a promising photocatalyst for visible-light-driven applications. It offers several desirable features such as excellent chemical and thermal stability, resistance to photo-corrosion, non-toxicity, and low fabrication cost [1, 2]. These advantages make g-C3N4 particularly attractive for photocatalytic environmental remediation. However, its practical applications are severely limited by intrinsic drawbacks, including the high recombination rate of photogenerated electron-hole pairs and its relatively large particle size, which results in a low surface area and insufficient exposure of active sites.

To address these limitations, numerous strategies have been explored to enhance the photocatalytic efficiency of g-C3N4. These include exfoliation to obtain ultrathin nanosheets [3–5], elemental doping [6–8], noble metal (e.g. Au, Ag, etc.) deposition [9, 10], and the construction of heterojunctions with other semiconductors (e.g. TiO2/g-C3N4, ZnO/g-C3N4, etc.) [11, 12]. Among these, the formation of heterostructures is particularly effective, as it enables improved charge separation and extended light absorption by coupling g-C3N4 with suitable co-catalysts [13–16].

Reduced graphene oxide (rGO), with its high electrical conductivity and large specific surface area, serves as an excellent electron acceptor and transporter in photocatalytic systems. It has been shown to significantly suppress charge recombination when coupled with g-C3N4, thereby improving photocatalytic activity [17, 18]. Nevertheless, a large graphene loading amount is required to improve catalyst performance, which limits its practical application [19, 20].

Transition metal chalcogenides, particularly molybdenum disulfide (MoS2), have also been intensively studied as co-catalysts due to their narrow band gaps (1.2–1.8 eV) and unique two-dimensional layered structures. MoS2 can form efficient heterojunctions with semiconductors like TiO2 and g-C3N4 [21, 22], enabling enhanced light absorption and accelerated charge separation. However, conventional methods for synthesizing MoS2 composite synthesis, such as hydrothermal or thermal, often require high energy input and yield relatively large particles, which are less favorable for efficient photocatalytic activity. Therefore, the development of low-energy, solution-based methods to fabricate MoS2 with abundant active sites remains a critical challenge. The combination of MoS2 and rGO with g-C3N4 integrates visible-light harvesting, efficient charge transport, and abundant active sites, thereby facilitating charge separation and boosting photocatalytic activity. Such ternary systems have attracted considerable attention for their synergistic charge-transfer properties and demonstrate excellent potential in environmental remediation [23–25].

In this study, we synthesized a ternary g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO (CMG) nanocomposite via an in situ photoreduction-driven exfoliation method. Under visible light illumination, photoexcited electrons from g-C3N4 reduce Mo and graphene oxide precursors, leading to the synchronous formation of MoS2 and rGO on the g-C3N4 surface. Notable, this photochemical process induces the exfoliation of bulk g-C3N4 into ultrathin nanosheets due to the nanoscale interaction with MoS2 precursors. The resulting CMG nanocomposites exhibit higher specific surface area and improved charge separation efficiency.

Photocatalytic experiments using Rhodamine B (RhB) as a model pollutant demonstrate that the CMG nanocomposites exhibit dramatically enhanced degradation performance compared to bulk g-C3N4. Mechanistic investigations suggest that the synergistic effect among g-C3N4, MoS2, and rGO effectively facilitates charge carrier separation and transport, thereby accelerating the generation of reactive oxygen species responsible for pollutant degradation. Overall, this work provides a rational strategy for the design of high-performance ternary photocatalysts through in situ photochemical synthesis and highlights the potential of g-C3N4-based heterojunctions in addressing pressing environmental issues through efficient visible-light-driven photocatalysis.

Experimental

Chemicals

Ammonium tetrathiomolybdate ((NH4)2MoS4), Rhodamine B (RhB), Graphene oxide, 1,4-Benzoquinone (C6H4O2) and DL-lactic acid (C3H6O3) were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). Triethanolamine (C6H15NO3), tert-butyl alcohol (C4H10O), Sodium sulfate (Na2SO4) and melamine were provided from Macklin (Shanghai, China). All chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Preparation of bulk g-C3N4

Bulk g-C3N4 was prepared via a two-step thermal polymerization of melamine. Typically, 5 g of melamine was heated to 500 ℃ at a ramping rate of 5 ℃/min and maintained for 2 h. After naturally cooling to room temperature, the resulting light-yellow powder was thoroughly ground. The powder was then subjected to a second heating step under the same conditions (500 ℃ for 2 h at a ramping rate of 5 ℃/min) to obtain the final g-C3N4 product.

Preparation of ternary g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO, g-C3N4/MoS2 and g-C3N4/rGO nanocomposites

Ternary g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO nanocomposites were synthesized via a photo-reduction deposition method. Typically, 100 mg of g-C3N4, varying amounts of ammonium tetrathiomolybdate ((NH4)2MoS4) (X = 125, 150, 175 and 200), and 0.5 mg of graphene oxide (GO) were dispersed in 50 mL of deionized water. Subsequently, 5 mL of lactic acid was added to the mixture, which was then stirred for 10 min to ensure homogeneity. The resulting solution was irradiated under a Xenon lamp for 2 h while being continuously stirred and purged with N2 to facilitate the photoreduction reaction. After the reaction, the mixture was repeatedly washed with deionized water, filtered, and dried in an oven at 60 ℃ for 12 h to yield the final g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO product. The samples were denoted as CMG-X, where X corresponds to the amount (in mg) of ammonium tetrathiomolybdate precursor used. For comparison, g-C3N4/MoS2 was synthesized under the same conditions without the addition of GO (X = 175 mg), and g-C3N4/rGO was prepared without the addition of (NH4)2MoS4.

Characterization

The powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were obtained by an X-ray diffractometer (Bruker D8 advance), and Cu Kα ray was used as a radiation source. The morphologies of the samples were obtained by scanning electron microscope (SEM, Zeiss Sigma 300) associated with an energy dispersive X-ray spectroscope (EDS, Oxford Xplore 50) and transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEOL JEM-F200). The chemical states of elements were investigated through an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS, Thermo Scientific K-Alpha), while C 1 s (284.8 eV) was employed for charge corrections. Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area was measured on a Quantachrome Autosorb IQ3 at 77 K. Fourier transform infrared spectra (FTIR) was performed with a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Nicolet 6700). Photoluminescence (PL) spectra were obtained on Steady/transient fluorescence spectrometer (FLS1000) with a 365 nm of excitation wavelength. UV-vis absorption spectra were recorded by using UV-vis spectrophotometer (T3202). Raman spectra were recorded on a Raman spectrophotometer (LabRAM, HR Evolution) with a laser operating at λ = 532 nm as the excitation source.

Photocatalytic performance tests

The photocatalytic activities of the as-prepared samples were evaluated under a 300 W Xe arc lamp (CEL-HXF300, Beijing Aulight Co., Ltd.) equipped with a cut-off filter (λ > 420 nm). RhB was selected as a model pollutant for the photocatalytic degradation test. Typically, 8 mg of the catalyst was dispersed in 50 mL of RhB (10 mg/L) solution and stirred in the dark for 30 min to establish adsorption-desorption equilibrium. Subsequently, the solution was irradiated for 30 min. At certain time intervals, 1 mL of the suspension was taken for test after removing the catalyst powder with a syringe filter. To investigate the roles of reactive species in the photocatalytic process, scavenger experiments were performed. Triethanolamine (TEOA, 1 mM), tert-butyl alcohol (TBA, 1 mM) and 1,4-Benzoquinone (BQ, 1mM) were used as the quenchers for photogenerated holes (h+), hydroxyl radicals (‧OH) and superoxide radicals (‧O2−), respectively.

Photoelectrochemical measurements

Electrochemical measurements were performed using a standard three-electrode system on electrochemical workstation (CHI 660E). The catalysts served as the working electrode, while a platinum mesh and an Ag/AgCl electrode were used as the counter and reference electrodes, respectively. The working electrode was prepared by dispersing 10 mg of the samples in a mixture of 250 µL deionized water and 750 µL ethanol to form a homogeneous suspension. Then, 100 µL of the suspension was dropped on indium tin oxide (ITO) glass substrate and dried at room temperature. A 0.1 M Na2SO4 solution was used as the electrolyte.

Results and discussion

Synthesis process of the g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO

The ternary g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO nanocomposite was successfully synthesized via an in situ photoreduction deposition method (Fig. 1). Typically, the precursor of the reactant g-C3N4, graphene oxide, and (NH4)2MoS4 were dispersed in an aqueous solution, and lactic acid was added as an electron donor. Upon irradiation with a Xenon lamp, g-C3N4 generates electron-hole pairs, and the photogenerated electrons and holes are subsequently transferred to the surface of g-C3N4. Due to the random and irregular dispersion of graphene oxide and (NH4)2MoS4 in the solution, the photogenerated electrons on the surface of g-C3N4 can react with graphene oxide and (NH4)2MoS4, reducing GO to rGO while releasing NH3 and H2S from the (NH4)2MoS4 precursor, leading to the formation of a g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO ternary nanocomposite photocatalyst [26, 27]. Structural studies

The XRD patterns of the as-prepared samples are shown in Fig. 2a. Bulk g-C3N4 displays two characteristic peaks at 13° and 27.5°, corresponding to the (100) plane of the in-plane structure stacking of tri-s-triazine units and the (002) plane of interplanar stacking of conjugated aromatic segments of g-C3N4, respectively [28, 29]. For the CMG-175 nanocomposite, a wide characteristic peak at 2θ = 13.9° is observed, which is ascribed to the (002) facet of MoS2 (JCPDS Card No.73–1508). The broadness of this peak indicates a smaller crystallite size, while the leftward shift of the MoS2 (002) peak indicates an increased interlayer spacing in CMG-175 [30]. Meanwhile, the diffraction peak at 2θ = 27.3° corresponds to the (002) plane of g-C3N4. The reduced intensity and slight shift of this peak in the g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO nanocomposite, compared to bulk g-C3N4, indicate exfoliation and partial disruption of the interlayer structure, confirming the transformation of bulk g-C3N4 into nanosheets during the photoreduction deposition process [4, 5]. In addition, no distinct diffraction peaks of rGO are observed in the CMG-175 nanocomposite, which is ascribed to its relatively low content. The presence of rGO in CMG-175 was confirmed by Raman spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 2b, the CMG-175 nanocomposite exhibits two characteristic Raman peaks at 1352 cm−1 and 1543 cm−1, corresponding to the disordered (D-band) and graphitic (G-band) sp2 carbon structures, respectively. The intensity ratio of ID/IG is less than 1, confirming the presence of reduced graphene oxide in the nanocomposite [31].

BET studies

Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms of g-C3N4 and CMG-175 nanocomposite are presented in Fig. 3a. Both g-C3N4 and CMG-175 nanocomposite show type Ⅳ isotherms, indicating the mesoporous structures of the catalysts. From the pore size distribution curves in Fig. 3b, it can be found that the g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO nanocomposite possesses a larger average pore size compared to bulk g-C3N4. A comparison of specific surface area, pore volume and pore size between g-C3N4 and g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO are illustrated in Table 1. The specific surface area of g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO is 26.723 m2 g−1, which is higher than that of g-C3N4 (21.703 m2 g−1). Additionally, g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO also exhibits a larger pore volume and pore size. The increases in specific surface area, pore volume, and pore size can be attributed to the exfoliation of bulk g-C3N4 and the deposition of MoS2 nanoparticles, which provide more active sites for photocatalysis reactions.

FTIR studies

The FTIR spectra of g-C3N4, g-C3N4/rGO, g-C3N4/MoS2 and g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO are shown in Fig. 4 to characterize their chemical structures. The sharp peak at 809 cm−1 is indexed to the breathing mode of s-triazine units. The peaks observed in the range of 1000–1700 cm−1 for g-C3N4, g-C3N4/rGO, g-C3N4/MoS2 and g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO correspond to the stretching mode of C-N and C = N bond in the heterocycles structure of C3N4 [32]. Moreover, the similar absorption profiles of g-C3N4 and g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO indicate that the exfoliation of bulk g-C3N4 does not significantly alter its molecule structure. The broad bands between 3100 and 3500 cm−1 are assigned to the N-H stretching vibrations [2]. In addition, no distinct absorption peaks corresponding to oxygen-containing functional groups, such as the C-O stretching vibrations at 1075 cm−1 and the C = O stretching vibrations at 1724 cm−1, were observed in the g-C3N4/rGO and CMG-175 sample. This indicates the successful reduction of GO to rGO [33], which is further confirmed by XPS analysis.

XPS studies

The chemical states of CMG-175 nanocomposite were further investigated using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The high-resolution C 1 s spectrum (Fig. 5a) can be fitted into five peaks at 284.5, 284.9, 285.4, 288.3, and 288.9 eV, corresponding to the C-C, C-OH, C-N, C-S, and N-C = N, respectively [28, 33]. The presence of weak signals from oxygen-containing functional group indicates that GO was effectively reduced to rGO [34, 35]. The high-resolution N 1 s spectrum (Fig. 5b) shows three peaks located at 398.9, 399.6, and 401.3 eV, which are assigned to sp2-hybridized aromatic N bound to C (C = N-C), N-(C)3, and C − NH2 functional groups, respectively [23, 28]. The presence of graphitic N indicates the formation of nitrogen-related bonding between g-C3N4 and graphene [33]. The high-resolution spectrum Mo 3 d (Fig. 5c) displays a pair of peaks at 229.4 and 232.5 eV, corresponding to Mo4+ species in the composite material [36]. Additionally, a peak at 226.7 eV is observed and attributed to the S 2 s orbital. As shown in Fig. 5d, two characteristic peaks at 162.3 and 163.5 eV are assigned to S 2p3/2 and S 2p1/2, respectively, confirming the formation of MoS2 in CMG-175 [37].

Morphological and compositional analysis

The morphology of CMG-175 was examined using SEM and TEM. As shown in Fig. 6a, the SEM image of bulk g-C3N4 exhibits a multilayered block-like structure. Figure 6b-f show the SEM and TEM images of the CMG-175 ternary composite. The SEM images (Fig. 6b and c) and TEM image (Fig. S1) display that the bulk g-C3N4 was exfoliated into thin nanosheets, with MoS2 nanoparticles deposited on their surface. This indicates that during the photoreduction process, not only can MoS2 and rGO be in situ integrated with g-C3N4, but the layered structure of g-C3N4 is also exfoliated into nanosheets, which is consistent with the XRD results. The TEM images (Fig. 6d-f) further confirm the layered structure of the ternary g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO composite. In Fig. 6e, the red-circled edge region is magnified in Fig. 6f, revealing that MoS2 consists of randomly oriented layers with an interlayer distance of 0.65 nm, which matches well with the (002) plane identified in the XRD analysis [38]. These layers are typically composed of 1 to 5 monolayers and have a longitudinal size of 5–10 nm. The distinctive layers structure of MoS2 is expected to provide more active sites for photocatalytic reaction, thereby enhancing catalytic performance. Fig. S2 shows that the lattice spacing of g-C3N4 is 0.35 nm, further confirming the presence of exfoliated g-C3N4. Element mapping (Fig. 6g) further shows the presence and uniform distribution of g-C3N4 and MoS2 within the ternary composite. Collectively, these results demonstrate the successful synthesis of the g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO ternary nanocomposite photocatalyst.

Photocatalytic studies

The photocatalytic activities of the as-prepared samples were evaluated by comparing their performances in the degradation of RhB under visible light irradiation. To optimize the catalyst dosage, we compared the photocatalytic performance at 5, 8, and 10 mg (Fig. S3). Although 10 mg gave the highest efficiency (95.5% degradation efficiency within 10 min), the excessively rapid removal hindered clear comparison among different samples. In contrast, 8 mg achieved a similar efficiency while avoiding issues of light shielding and mass transfer limitations commonly associated with excessive catalyst loading. Therefore, 8 mg was selected as the optimal dosage for subsequent experiments. As shown in Fig. 7a, CMG-175 exhibited the highest photocatalytic performance, achieving a RhB degradation efficiency of 95.7% within 30 min. In contrast, bulk g-C3N4 and g-C3N4/rGO exhibited degradation efficiencies of only 6.4% and 8.6%, respectively. This result indicates that the minor addition of rGO does not significantly improve the photocatalytic activity of g-C3N4. The g-C3N4/MoS2 binary composite demonstrated a considerably improved degradation efficiency of 75.7% after 30 min of irradiation, indicating that the incorporation of MoS2 effectively boosts the photocatalytic performance. Furthermore, when a g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO ternary composite was employed as the photocatalyst, its performance was significantly improved. By varying the amounts of (NH4)2MoS4 during synthesis, the influence of MoS2 content on the photocatalytic activity was investigated. The results revealed a gradual increase in photocatalytic efficiency with increasing (NH4)2MoS4 content, reaching a maximum at 175 mg, where CMG-175 achieved a degradation efficiency of 95.7%. The ternary catalyst we synthesized exhibits superior activity compared to other reported catalysts composed of g-C3N4, MoS2, and graphene, as shown in Table S1. However, further increasing the MoS2 content led to a decline in photocatalytic performance, confirming that CMG-175 possessed the optimal MoS2 loading for photocatalytic activity. These findings demonstrate that the CMG-175 ternary composite catalyst, with a well-optimized MoS2 content, exhibits a strong synergistic effect among g-C3N4, MoS2, and rGO, significantly enhancing the overall photocatalytic performance. As illustrated in Fig. 7b, the pseudo-first-order kinetic plots show that CMG-175 has the highest reaction rate constant of 0.05227 min−1, which is 23.6 times greater than that of bulk g-C3N4, indicating a great improvement in photocatalytic activity.

The cyclic stability during the degradation of organic compounds is an important parameter for evaluating the long-term performance of photocatalyst. The stability of the CMG-175 composite was assessed through repeated degradation cycles of RhB. As shown in Fig. 8, the degradation efficiency of CMG-175 composite decreased slightly after four consecutive cycles, yet it still maintained a high degradation of 91.9%. Therefore, the synthesized CMG-175 composite possesses excellent cyclic stability.

Scavenger studies

To investigate the mechanism of photocatalytic degradation of RhB by the g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO nanocomposite, active species trapping experiments were conducted. Triethanolamine (TEOA), 1,4-Benzoquinone (BQ), and tert-butyl alcohol (TBA) were used as trapping agents for photogenerated holes (h+), superoxide radicals (•O2−), and hydroxyl radicals (•OH), respectively. As shown in Fig. 9, the degradation rates remained relatively high at 82.6% in the presence of TBA, suggesting that •OH was not the dominant reactive species. However, when TEOA and BQ were introduced, the degradation rates dropped significantly to 15.5% and 54.13%, respectively. These results indicate that h+ and •O2− play major roles in the photocatalytic degradation of RhB [1, 39].

PL studies

To further analyze the mechanism behind the enhanced photocatalytic activity of the g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO nanocomposites, photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy was performed with an excitation wavelength of 365 nm to evaluate the recombination efficiency of photogenerated charge carriers. As shown in Fig. 10, the PL emission intensities of g-C3N4 and g-C3N4/rGO are nearly identical under light excitation, indicating that the small amount of rGO does not significantly enhance charge separation, which is also consistent with our photoactivities results. In contrast, the markedly reduced PL intensity of the g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO nanocomposite indicates more effective separation of photogenerated electrons and holes, thereby contributing to its improved photocatalytic activity.

Transient photocurrent responses studies

The transfer and separation efficienvy of photogenerated charge carriers was measured using transient photocurrent responses tests of g-C3N4, g-C3N4/rGO, g-C3N4/MoS2, and CMG-175. As shown in Fig. 11, the g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO ternary nanocomposite exhibited the highest photocurrent among the tested samples. This enhanced photocurrent indicates more efficient charge separation and transfer, resulting from the in situ deposition of MoS2 nanoparticles and rGO onto exfoliated g-C3N4. Such improvements in charge dynamics contribute to the superior photocatalytic performance of the composite.

Possible mechanism of photocatalytic degradation

Based on the above analysis, a possible mechanism for the photocatalytic degradation of RhB by g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO ternary nanocomposites is proposed (Fig. 12). Under light illumination, both g-C3N4 and MoS2 in g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO are photoexcited, generating electrons and holes [40]. The photogenerated electrons transfer from the conduction band (CB) of g-C3N4 to the CB of MoS2, and subsequently to the conductive graphene sheets [6, 24, 41]. Simultaneously, photogenerated holes migrate from the valence band (VB) of MoS2 to the VB of g-C3N4. These continuously separated electrons and holes then participate in redox reactions with organic pollutants adsorbed on the surface of g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO, leading to the degradation of organic dyes (RhB) into CO2, H2O, and other intermediates. The transfer and separation of electrons and holes, as well as the good conductivity of GO, endow the ternary catalyst with excellent degradation performance.

Conclusions

In summary, we have successfully synthesized highly active g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO ternary nanocomposite photocatalysts via a photoreduction deposition method. Owing to the small size of the MoS2 precursor, it can intercalate into the bulk g-C3N4 layers during synthesis, leading to the exfoliation of g-C3N4 into nanosheets and formation of a ternary nanocomposite with MoS2 nanoparticles closely integrated with the layered g-C3N4 structure. The resulting g-C3N4/MoS2/rGO nanocomposite exhibits excellent photocatalytic activity for the degradation of organic pollutants, achieving a RhB degradation rate of 95.7% within 30 min under visible light irradiation. Mechanistic analysis indicates that the synergistic interaction between MoS2, rGO, and g-C3N4 effectively suppresses the recombination of photogenerated carriers, thereby enhancing the separation efficiency of electrons and holes. This photoreduction deposition strategy for fabricating multicomponent, noble-metal-free photocatalysts offers a promising approach for designing advanced materials for energy and environmental applications.
