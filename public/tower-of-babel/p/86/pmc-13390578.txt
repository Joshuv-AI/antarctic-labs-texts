The Lived Experience of Cognitive Impairment Associated With Schizophrenia: A Conceptual Model
Authors: Nicholas Langevin, Anubha Shukla, Nataliya Bogoeva, Péter Kéri, Michael Kostikas, Simona Sgarbi, Walter Lawhorn, Vanessa Pott, Justin Corey Fowler, Stephanie Sommer, Jenny Wang, Arundati Nagendra, Alice Medalia, Matthew Reaney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCognitive impairment associated with schizophrenia (CIAS) has a substantial impact on people living with the condition. We aimed to update a 2012 conceptual model of CIAS with consideration of lived experience using insights from multinational cohorts of healthcare stakeholders and people living with schizophrenia.MethodsTo update the 2012 conceptual model, we used insights from a targeted literature review, concept elicitation interviews with adults living with schizophrenia (n = 26) and healthcare stakeholders (n = 83), 3 patient advisory boards (n = 32), and clinical trial exit interviews of participants (n = 37) from the CONNEX-X clinical trial.ResultsSix relevant articles were identified that reported 52 CIAS signs and symptoms and 33 impacts of CIAS. CIAS domains highlighted in the concept elicitation interviews as affecting participants’ lives included attention (92% of participants), social cognition (84%), processing speed (80%), and memory (76%). Advisory board participants reported impacts of CIAS on daily life and independent living, including career goals and relationships. CONNEX-X exit interviewees reported memory problems (51% of participants), difficulties in learning new things (46%), and concentration difficulties (43%). These insights were used to expand the CIAS model and provide more nuance and granularity.ConclusionThe findings of this study highlight the substantial burden of CIAS on the daily lives of people living with schizophrenia and the impact on their quality of life. A modern CIAS conceptual model is important for the assessment of cognitive impairment in both clinical trials and clinical practice.

Introduction

Cognitive impairment associated with schizophrenia (CIAS) is a core feature that affects over 80% of people living with the condition.1,2 These impairments often predate the onset of positive symptoms, such as hallucinations, disorganized thinking, and delusions.2-6 CIAS refers to impaired granular cognitive skills such as attention, processing speed, and memory, and is differentiated from higher-level cognitive skills such as decision-making.7 Together, CIAS and higher-level cognitive skills contribute to cognitive health, which refers to the brain’s ability to support thinking, learning, and the cognitive skills needed to perceive, acquire, understand, and respond to information.8

CIAS, and cognitive health more generally, has a substantial impact on patients’ daily lives and health-related quality of life (HRQoL).3,5,9,10 The degree of cognitive impairment in people living with schizophrenia is considered to be an accurate predictor of long-term functioning.2,3,11 Functional impairment resulting from CIAS interferes with activities of daily living (ADLs), such as shopping, paying bills, and using public transport, and may lead to reduced independence and a diminished ability to work.11,12 Despite its prominence in schizophrenia, cognitive impairment is not included as a diagnostic criterion in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (Fifth Edition [DSM-5]).13 This is in part because CIAS is not sufficiently distinct from cognitive impairment associated with other conditions or with negative symptoms of schizophrenia; however, the discussion about whether CIAS should be included as a criterion is ongoing.3,14

The Measurement and Treatment Research to Improve Cognition in Schizophrenia (MATRICS) Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB)15 is a standardized tool developed to measure cognitive functioning in people living with schizophrenia and is frequently used in schizophrenia clinical trials. Although the MCCB assesses the granular cognitive deficits of CIAS, it does not explain the burden of the deficits. To describe the humanistic burden of CIAS, a conceptual model was developed by Kitchen and colleagues following a literature review of research published between 1999 and 2009.16 The model included 7 cognitive domains (memory, learning, executive function, problem solving, processing speed, social cognition, and attention) and 6 impacted areas of functioning (ADLs, occupational functioning, social functioning, relationships, HRQoL, and adherence to treatment).16 This conceptual model marked an important step in understanding CIAS and prompted further interest; however, in the years since its creation, advances in the treatment and management of schizophrenia, and an increasing importance of qualitative evidence from people with lived experience, warrant a refinement of this model.

In recent years, there has been increased attention paid to the subjective experience of schizophrenia as it relates to CIAS, including cognitive concerns, functional challenges, identity, and stigma.17,18 These experiences provide an opportunity to consider the extent to which changes in sense of self and perception interact with cognition to shape real-world interactions and functioning. Taking these factors into consideration, the aim of this study was to develop an updated conceptual model of CIAS that includes insights from people living with schizophrenia. The specific aims were to (1) identify the most common signs, symptoms, impacts, and management strategies that reflect the experiences of people living with CIAS, and (2) develop an updated conceptual model of CIAS.

Methods

Study design

This updated conceptual model of CIAS was built from the following sources (Figure 1): (1) the existing Kitchen model, (2) a targeted literature review of articles published after those included in the review completed by Kitchen and colleagues, (3) concept elicitation interviews with people living with schizophrenia and healthcare stakeholders, (4) patient advisory boards, (5) clinical trial exit interviews, and (6) relevant information gathered from a series of Boehringer Ingelheim-sponsored qualitative studies.

(2) Targeted literature review

Using PubMed®, we conducted a targeted literature review to identify key publications related to CIAS, disease management, and disease burden. The review covered a period from December 2009, where the Kitchen et al. review ended, to December 2024.

Published articles included in the review involved adults living with schizophrenia, where the outcome of interest included cognition or quality of life, and a qualitative study design was used. This included, but was not limited to, patient-experience studies, instrument development studies, and reviews of qualitative articles. Animal studies, editorials, case reports, and studies outside of the literature review scope were excluded. Full selection criteria and search strings are in Tables S1 and S2.

(3) Concept elicitation interviews

Concept elicitation interviews were conducted with people living with schizophrenia and healthcare stakeholders. Owing to the qualitative nature of the data collection, no formal calculations were performed to determine the statistical power for the interview data. Data saturation was assessed continually throughout data collection, systematically monitored and assessed by the interviewers using visual aids featured within the qualitative data-management software MAXQDA. Increasingly fewer new concepts were elicited as interviews progressed and saturation was approached by the full target sample. Interviews were conducted in English by trained qualitative interviewers.

Interviews with People Living with Schizophrenia

Qualitative, non-interventional, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 26 adults living with schizophrenia in the United Kingdom or United States and explored the cognitive domains outlined in the Kitchen model. This model was selected as a basis for these interviews, rather than the MCCB, because the Kitchen model was specifically developed to reflect the cognitive concepts most important to people living with schizophrenia, making it a more appropriate basis for concept elicitation.

Participants were those who (1) had previously received or were currently receiving pharmacological treatment for schizophrenia; (2) were considered to be clinically stable outpatients with no hospitalization or increase in care due to worsening of schizophrenia or uncontrolled symptoms within the past 12 weeks; (3) resided in the United Kingdom or United States; and (4) were willing and able to attend a remote audio interview. Candidates were excluded if they were actively taking part in a current pharmacological trial for schizophrenia. Participants were identified and contacted through a specialist patient recruitment partner. Before the interview, ethical approval was sought to ensure this study was designed and conducted in accordance with best ethical research practices, and participants provided informed consent.

After brief demographic questions, interviews followed a discussion guide comprising 3 core sections. The first section explored the symptoms of schizophrenia and their day-to-day impact. Participants were asked which symptoms they experienced on a typical day as a result of schizophrenia, which symptom was the most severe, and to what extent living with schizophrenia impacted their ADLs, social skills/interaction, relationships, and work/productivity. The second section explored the 7 cognitive domains included in the Kitchen model. For each domain, participants were asked to what extent they had experienced any difficulty with that domain due to schizophrenia, the extent to which the symptoms impacted the areas of their life described above, and whether there had been periods of time when symptoms had improved. Each domain was explained in accessible language, using open-ended questions to explore the concepts, and then targeted prompts to explore the participant’s perceived importance of the symptom and the impact of that symptom on daily living. The third section explored the participant’s experiences of previous or current treatments and the impact of these on cognitive symptoms.

Healthcare Stakeholder Interviews

A total of 83 online interviews, each approximately 1 hour in length, were completed with non-patient stakeholders and aimed to understand the impact of cognitive impairment on people living with schizophrenia, their families/caregivers, the healthcare system, and society. Participants came from 12 countries (Belgium, China, France, Germany, Greece, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands, Portugal, Spain, Sweden, and the United Kingdom) and included psychiatrists, psychologists, psychotherapists, social workers, occupational therapists, healthcare administrators, payers, and pharmacists.

(4) Patient advisory boards

Three patient advisory boards were conducted in English and convened by GAMIAN-Europe between December 2023 and July 2024. The first advisory board, held on December 14, 2023, involved 10 people living with schizophrenia from 6 countries (Belgium, Czech Republic, Hungary, Israel, the Netherlands, and Romania). The focus was on symptoms and impact of schizophrenia, cognitive function, treatment decisions, and informal support, along with feedback on Boehringer Ingelheim materials. Participants were asked which symptoms had been the most challenging for them and why, whether they had noticed changes in cognitive function, and whether they had discussed these changes with a mental health professional.

The second advisory board, held on February 22, 2024, involved 9 participants residing in 6 countries (Czech Republic, Hungary, Israel, the Netherlands, Portugal, and Romania). The focus here was on understanding the cognitive domains of schizophrenia, the impact of cognitive impairment on daily living, and information and access to information. Participants were asked whether they knew of the 3 symptom domains of schizophrenia concerning positive, negative, and cognitive symptoms, and asked to provide examples for each domain and insights into how the symptoms affected their daily lives. Cognitive impairment and its effect on day-to-day life were explored, with participants prompted for examples of cognitive difficulties and their impact on various aspects of daily living.

The third advisory board, held on July 16, 2024, involved 13 participants from 9 countries (Czech Republic, Germany, Hungary, Israel, the Netherlands, Malta, Poland, Portugal, and Romania) and focused on understanding the 7 cognitive domains in people living with schizophrenia. Each domain was described with examples of symptoms, and participants were asked to reflect on their own experiences, the impact of these symptoms, and treatment considerations for cognitive symptoms.

(5) Exit interviews from the CONNEX trials

CONNEX-X (NCT05211947) was an open-label, rollover safety trial in which people living with schizophrenia who had completed 26 weeks of treatment with iclepertin or placebo in CONNEX-1 (NCT04846868), -2 (NCT04846881), or -3 (NCT04860830) trials were offered an opportunity to continue or start receiving (for those who received placebo) iclepertin for 52 weeks. CONNEX-X trial exit interviews were conducted with 37 participants (all were patients) from Brazil, the Czech Republic, Germany, Japan, Poland, and the United States. These exit interviews aimed to understand study participants’ experience of treatment and explore any perceived changes in daily functioning and cognition. Interviews lasted approximately 45 min and were conducted in the local language by trained interviewers from IQVIA, then translated into English as applicable for analysis, with quality checks of the transcripts and translations. Data on concepts associated with experiences of CIAS were extracted from the interviews, which were structured using the following themes: (1) daily function, in which participants were asked about their daily routine and changes following treatment; (2) cognition, in which participants were asked about their current cognitive abilities and changes following treatment; (3) treatment experience, involving questions on overall adherence to medications and trial medication; and (4) clinical trial experience, in which participants were asked about their overall experience in the CONNEX-X trial.

Although these interviews were not originally intended as formal concept elicitation interviews, they yielded rich, spontaneous insights into cognitive symptoms and their functional impacts. Domains mentioned in the interviews were: attention/concentration, impaired perception, memory, learning, communication/social cognition, executive function/problem solving, and processing speed/sharpness of thought. Participants described changes in memory, attention, learning, and communication. Given the alignment of these spontaneously reported experiences with the domains of interest in the conceptual model, the findings were used to enrich and validate the updated CIAS conceptual model. This approach is consistent with best practices in qualitative research, where triangulation of data sources enhances the robustness and relevance of conceptual frameworks.19

The interviews were conducted using a semi-structured guide, and transcripts were analyzed using a mixed inductive and deductive coding approach. Thematic analysis was facilitated by MAXQDA software, and findings were integrated with other qualitative sources to ensure the model reflected both structured and naturally emerging patient experiences.

(6) Boehringer Ingelheim-sponsored qualitative studies

In addition to the above sources, relevant information was gathered from Boehringer Ingelheim-sponsored qualitative, non-interventional studies conducted in the United States involving 40 online interviews with primary (family, unpaid) and secondary (trained professional) caregivers, and qualitative data from the references used for the Boehringer Ingelheim Patient Experience Assessment, a patient-experience assessment report that gathered and analyzed patient-generated evidence (Table 1).16,20-27

Refinement of the Conceptual Model

A structured, multistep methodology was employed to develop the updated CIAS conceptual model. Concepts were identified from the targeted literature review, extracted using standardized procedures with quality control, and iteratively refined through successive review. Identified qualitative concepts were coded at the most granular level as individual signs/symptoms or impacts, and then grouped based on conceptual similarity and functional meaning. The DSM-5 cognitive domain framework was used as the overarching structure for organizing concepts within the CIAS model, including complex attention, executive function, learning and memory, language, perceptual motor function, and social cognition. Domain headers and sub-headers were applied to organize concepts within this framework, with minor modifications informed by the qualitative evidence. Sub-domains were defined within DSM-5 cognitive domains only when qualitative data consistently supported distinct and recurrent clusters of experience (eg, sustained versus divided attention within complex attention). Where qualitative evidence reflected a more unified construct without clear differentiation, concepts were retained at the domain level. As a result, the level of sub-domain granularity may vary across domains. The number of signs and symptoms associated with each domain or sub-domain will reflect the breadth and specificity of qualitative descriptions rather than relative importance or weighting. The compiled dataset underwent quality control by a secondary reviewer to ensure accuracy. A draft conceptual model was developed based on these curated data and reviewed through multiple rounds by experts from IQVIA and Boehringer Ingelheim. Feedback from these internal reviews informed subsequent iterations. The model was further refined following review and input from external experts, culminating in the final version of the updated CIAS conceptual model.

Results

Targeted Literature Review

The targeted literature search identified 732 articles that were screened for inclusion (Figure 1); of these, 6 articles met the selection criteria and were reviewed (Table 1). Two additional articles, which had been identified in a separate unpublished study, were included in the review due to their relevance here. These 2 articles were not captured in this literature search as they did not contain relevant keywords related to “cognitive.” The reviewed literature collectively identified 52 CIAS signs and symptoms and 33 impacts of CIAS on daily life.

Of the 26 people living with schizophrenia who participated in the concept elicitation interviews, 16 were from the United States and 10 from the United Kingdom. The mean age of participants at the time of diagnosis was 40 years (range: 23-48; Table 2). Participants reported 39 signs and symptoms and 46 impacts.

The initial Kitchen model comprised 7 cognitive domains: memory, learning, executive function, problem solving, processing speed, social cognition, and attention. At least half of participants in the concept elicitation interviews endorsed each of the 7 cognitive domains and expressed impairments in them, the most prevalent being difficulties with attention (92% of participants; Table 3). Participants who reported impairment in attention had difficulties with focus and concentration, including not watching television or reading books due to lack of focus, and concentration issues due to stress and “brain fog,” or due to distractions such as boredom, paranoia, or the presence of hallucinations. Impairment in attention impacted multiple domains of HRQoL with participants frequently reporting a substantial impact on performing ADLs due to difficulties in maintaining concentration during tasks such as driving, performing household chores, hobbies, and shopping. Attention difficulties also impacted participants’ relationships and social interactions due to difficulties in maintaining focus in conversations or in engaging in social activities and work.

Difficulties with processing (endorsed by 80% of participants) were most frequently described as a slowed processing speed, although a minority of participants reported their processing speed being faster than most people. Participants reporting slower processing speed had difficulties in following conversations and were slower in carrying out ADLs than they had been before developing symptoms. Participants who reported faster processing speed often had difficulties with impulse control.

Memory impairment (endorsed by 76% of participants) was reported as having a substantial burden on daily living, with many participants describing forgetfulness, difficulty retaining short-term information, and difficulty managing finances. Participants reported forgetting names or things others had just said, which often led to embarrassment and difficulties in expressing themselves. Memory problems caused participants to miss deadlines and tasks, requiring them to rely heavily on reminders and timelines to stay organized. Some participants reported difficulty securing a job or remembering what they had studied, affecting career progression.

Difficulties with executive functioning (endorsed by 76% of participants) were reported for both large and small decisions, which affected participants’ ability to manage ADLs and life tasks effectively. Participants reported having difficulties with planning, executing, and finishing tasks, financial management, and making decisions. Many participants described difficulties making rational choices, leaving them feeling overwhelmed and lacking confidence in their own decision-making abilities.

Impacts on learning (endorsed by 72% of participants) were largely reported within work or productive activity domains. Difficulties with learning included slower comprehension, poor recall, and retention of new information.

Social cognition impairments (endorsed by 84% of participants) were associated with avoidance of tasks outside of the home, avoidance of social interactions, self-isolation, and difficulties in interpreting social cues. Social situations were described as overwhelming and social contact led to anxiety, stigma, and a fear of judgment.

Approximately half of participants reported difficulties with problem solving (52%), which included challenges in focusing, starting tasks, and completing tasks. Participants described a lack of self-confidence in their own problem-solving abilities and challenges in arriving at rational solutions in the workplace.

Healthcare Stakeholders

In-depth interviews were held with 83 healthcare stakeholders from 12 countries (Belgium, China, France, Germany, Greece, Italy, Japan, Netherlands, Portugal, Spain, Sweden, and the United Kingdom): 20 psychiatrists, 20 psychologists/psychotherapists, 20 social workers/occupational therapists, 10 representatives from patient advocacy groups, 10 healthcare administrators/payers, and 3 pharmacists.

The interviews highlighted poor awareness of CIAS, with participants reporting confusion between CIAS and negative symptoms or attributing symptoms to side effects of antipsychotics. Negative impacts of CIAS on a patient’s functioning were highlighted, including coordination of daily routines, self-care, decision-making, ability to sustain a career, and patient safety. Participants noted a greater neglect of personal health (from patients) compared with the general population, unhealthier lifestyle choices, increased substance-abuse rates, elevated suicide rates, and more frequent incidence and poorer management of physical comorbidities.

Patient Advisory Boards

Sociodemographics for the participants of the advisory boards are detailed in Table 4. The patient advisory boards revealed that most participants understood positive, negative, and cognitive symptoms of schizophrenia. Participants described positive symptoms as perceptual difficulties that disrupted function, while negative symptoms were described as an absence or reduction of normal functioning. Participants also discussed the interconnectedness of positive, negative, and cognitive symptoms, which some participants considered to be separate entities; it was suggested that positive symptoms were much more treatable than negative symptoms and cognitive impairment. Cognitive impairment was reported as having a substantial impact on daily life, and cognitive issues were reported to hinder career goals and relationships. The advisory boards revealed that cognitive symptoms worsened during relapse, low mood, fatigue, and stressful situations, with insomnia being a significant exacerbating factor.

Boehringer Ingelheim-Sponsored Qualitative Studies

A total of 40 online, qualitative interviews were conducted in the United States with primary (family, unpaid) and secondary (trained professional) caregivers. All caregivers who participated in the interviews reported a substantial physical, social, and emotional burden impact of caring for someone with schizophrenia. Cognitive and negative symptoms were reported to increase the dependence of the person living with schizophrenia on the caregiver. Caregivers felt powerless to change their current situation and that schizophrenia had agency over their own lives. A patient-experience assessment report highlighted that schizophrenia causes significant distress and impairment in personal, family, social, educational, occupational, and other important areas of life, as well as a high caregiver burden.

Refinement of the Conceptual Model

The sources described above provided a robust foundation for updating the initial Kitchen model. Combined findings from these sources identified 7 core domains of cognitive impairment in which people living with schizophrenia experience challenges: attention and concentration, executive functioning, memory and learning, processing speed, language, social cognition, and perceptual motor control. The 7 cognitive domains included in the Kitchen model were used as the foundation for the updated model, which introduces a more detailed structure by categorizing newly identified granular concepts into sub-domains of updated main domains (Figure 3).

The initial Kitchen model identified 6 key impact domains associated with CIAS (ADLs, occupational functioning, social functioning, relationships, HRQoL, and adherence to treatment). These domains have been retained in the updated model and have been expanded to reflect a broader and more nuanced understanding of how cognitive impairment affects daily life. Many new, more granular concepts were identified and organized into sub-domains under each main impact domain, and 4 new domains (psychological and emotional, independence, economic burden, and mental fatigue) were added (Tables S3 and S4).

In response to these challenges, participants reported employing a range of coping strategies (Table S5). Coping and management strategies were not included in the original Kitchen model but have been introduced in the updated conceptual model to reflect the various ways individuals with CIAS attempt to manage their cognitive challenges. These strategies have been grouped into 8 distinct domains—cognitive strategies, support systems, personal strategies, healthy lifestyle, leisure activities, self-awareness, social skills, and therapeutic interventions.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to identify the most common concepts related to CIAS that reflect the experiences of people living with schizophrenia, and to update a previously published conceptual model with insights obtained from a literature review, concept elicitation interviews, patient advisory boards, and clinical trial exit interviews. This comprehensive approach was conducted in line with the US Food and Drug Administration Patient-Focused Drug Development guidance28 and is the first approach in updating the conceptual model of CIAS to include the perspectives of people living with schizophrenia.

The findings of this study built upon the initial Kitchen model, refining and broadening it to provide more nuance and granularity of the experiences of people living with schizophrenia. Additionally, we introduced a novel category to describe the coping and management strategies employed by people living with schizophrenia to mitigate the impacts of CIAS on their daily lives. The inclusion of the perspectives of people living with schizophrenia provided new insights into CIAS and its impacts. For people living with schizophrenia, the understanding of cognitive impairment is not limited to the discrete cognitive skills associated with CIAS, which are typically the focus of pharmacologic or cognitive remediation therapies. While they agreed that those skills are impacted, they also spoke about struggles with higher-level cognitive skills, such as use of language, rational thought, and decision-making.

In line with other published studies, this updated model highlights the heterogeneity in the lived experience29,30 and shifts our understanding of the impact of CIAS. This study included participants from numerous countries and consequently includes a broad range of cultural perspectives. Across all the sources used, participants highlighted the impact of CIAS on daily living, relationships, productivity, and career. A multimodal approach to care—which included medication, talking therapies, physical therapies, and lifestyle strategies—was considered important by participants.

The updated conceptual model presented here is a visual depiction of the way people living with schizophrenia feel and function in their everyday lives. It is intended to serve as a foundation for developing or refining clinical outcome assessments in CIAS. By capturing both granular and higher-level cognitive domains, and their functional impacts, the model supports the identification of meaningful endpoints for clinical trials and real-world studies.

This study used a mixed-method qualitative approach, which enabled a rich and nuanced understanding of CIAS; however, the modest sample size may limit the generalizability of findings to broader, more diverse populations. Furthermore, saturation was not assessed in all qualitative samples (eg, exit interviews, advisory boards). As a result of this, and because participants were from a breadth of countries and cultures, the conceptual model may not reflect the experiences of all people with CIAS, particularly individuals in regions not represented in the sample.

CIAS is complex, so it can be challenging to determine which signs and symptoms fall under CIAS and which may be deemed positive or negative symptoms. Categorization of the domains of CIAS varies depending on the assessment used. MATRICS15 and the Kitchen model16 both include 7 cognitive domains but with differences in domain groupings, whereas DSM-5 includes 6 neurocognitive domains.13 Not all of the domains included in these models align precisely; for example, memory and learning are categorized in MATRICS as 3 domains (working memory, verbal learning, and visual learning),15 whereas they are included as 2 domains in the Kitchen model (memory [declarative and working] and learning)16 and under one domain (memory and learning) in DSM-5.13 Throughout this study, we strived to categorize signs and symptoms appropriately; however, some symptoms may belong to different categories depending on the cause of the symptom itself. Furthermore, cognitive changes in any individual are not always indicative of CIAS and may instead be adaptive or reactive responses.31-33 It is therefore important to be mindful of the risk of unintentionally reinforcing pathologization of adaptive or reactive cognitive states. Additionally, while CIAS is not a diagnostic category, the conceptual model of CIAS presented here is structured with clear categories and symptoms. If applied uncritically, this may enable unofficial screening or exclusion mechanisms in work, education, or insurance, based on traits that are neither pathological nor dangerous, but simply neurodivergent.

Finally, given the complexity of CIAS and the heterogeneity of the experiences of people living with schizophrenia, it is worth considering whether the terminology currently used is the most appropriate. CIAS does not address one key finding: cognition means different things to different people, and people have different coping and management strategies, some of which are more adaptive than others. As such, cognitive health concerns associated with schizophrenia may be a more appropriate term.

Conclusions

The findings of this study highlight the substantial burden that CIAS places on the daily lives of people living with schizophrenia and its impact on HRQoL and functional capacity. A modern, CIAS conceptual model is important for the assessment of cognitive impairment in both clinical trials and clinical practice.
