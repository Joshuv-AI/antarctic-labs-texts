CAKE: a flexible self-supervised framework for enhancing cell visualization, clustering and rare cell identification
Authors: Jin Liu, Weixing Zeng, Shichao Kan, Min Li, Ruiqing Zheng
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractSingle cell sequencing technology has provided unprecedented opportunities for comprehensively deciphering cell heterogeneity. Nevertheless, the high dimensionality and intricate nature of cell heterogeneity have presented substantial challenges to computational methods. Numerous novel clustering methods have been proposed to address this issue. However, none of these methods achieve the consistently better performance under different biological scenarios. In this study, we developed CAKE, a novel and scalable self-supervised clustering method, which consists of a contrastive learning model with a mixture neighborhood augmentation for cell representation learning, and a self-Knowledge Distiller model for the refinement of clustering results. These designs provide more condensed and cluster-friendly cell representations and improve the clustering performance in term of accuracy and robustness. Furthermore, in addition to accurately identifying the major type cells, CAKE could also find more biologically meaningful cell subgroups and rare cell types. The comprehensive experiments on real single-cell RNA sequencing datasets demonstrated the superiority of CAKE in visualization and clustering over other comparison methods, and indicated its extensive application in the field of cell heterogeneity analysis. INTRODUCTION

Cell representation learning and clustering involve transforming raw single-cell data into a lower dimensional representation, and grouping cells with similar biological characteristics, and are powerful tools to explore the cellular heterogeneity and functional diversity [1–3]. A well-designed cell representation learning and clustering method would facilitate the discovery of novel cell types, rare cell populations and transitional states that may not be apparent in the raw data. Indeed, the high dimensionality and sparsity of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data caused by dropouts make it difficult to extract meaningful biological insights.

Recently, numerous methods have been proposed to address these challenges, which can be categorized into either classical machine-learning-based or deep-learning-based groups [4–6]. Classical machine-learning-based methods applied traditional dimension reduction approach or similarity learning approach, followed by k-means, Louvain or spectral clustering to visualize and group cells. For instance, SIMLR [7] employs multi-kernel learning to construct an enhanced cell-to-cell similarity matrix, then applies the spectral clustering to obtain the final clusters. Seurat[8] and Scanpy [9] transform the cell clustering task into community detection problem, and leverage the Leiden [10] or Louvain [11] after principal component analysis dimensionality reduction. Besides, GiniClust [12–14] is a series of cluster-aware, weighted consensus clustering approaches that combine Gini index and Fano factor-based feature selection methods and Louvain method to identify both major and rare cell types. However, most of these machine-learning-based clustering methods heavily depend on some preconceived assumption, such as linear relationships among cells or low rank constraint, which may not fully capture the biological complexity of data and usually deliver suboptimal performance on cellular heterogeneity analysis.

Compared with classical machine-learning-based clustering methods, deep-learning-based approaches have provided promising results in modeling the noisy and complex scRNA-seq data while requiring weaker assumptions. Moreover, the rapid increase in the number of cells using advanced scRNA-seq techniques has provided a substantial data foundation for training a well-fitting deep learning model. For example, Deep Count Autoencoder [15] applies a deep count autoencoder that denoises and imputes the original data by incorporating with a zero-inflated negative binomial loss. In addition to data characteristic, scDeepCluster [16] integrates a Kullback–Leibler (KL)-divergence-based clustering loss to assign cells to each cluster, which is originally described in the DEC algorithm [17]. For scziDesk [18], it aims to learn more clustering-friendly cell representations by adding a similarity constraint term, and replaces the hard clustering by soft K-means clustering. In addition to autoencoder-based approaches, contrastive learning is a more powerful and flexible self-supervised approach to create informative embeddings for downstream analysis task by capturing the similarity and dissimilarity between cells [19]. Typically, scNAME [20] utilizes a neighborhood contrastive loss and an ancillary mask estimation loss to reveal the uncorrupted data structure. In contrast to the methods mentioned above, graph-neural-network-based methods attempt to learn cell representation from cell-to-cell graph in an explicit way. However, raw scRNA-seq data lack the cell-to-cell graph, and using K-nearest neighbor (KNN) [21] to model the relationships between cells may oversimplify the complexity of the cell-to-cell graph. ScGNN [22] formulates the cell-to-cell graph based on cell embeddings and dynamically prunes the graph by isolation forest. Graph-sc [23] maps the preprocessed scRNA-seq data into a cell-to-gene graph, and uses a graph autoencoder network to learn the cell embedding from this bipartite graph.

Although the aforementioned methods have made efforts to accomplish cells clustering task from different perspectives, some benchmarking studies have demonstrated that those existing methods still encounter challenges in achieving high accuracy, scalability and robustness, especially when dealing with certain biological scenarios, such as identifying cell subtypes or rare cell types. To address these limitations, we present a novel deep-learning-based cell clustering method called CAKE, which combines the power of a mixture neighborhood augmented Contrastive leArning and self-Knowledge DistillEr models. CAKE consists of a flexible two-stage self-supervised learning algorithm. In the first stage, CAKE utilizes a contrastive learning model with a combination of task-specific and a mixture neighborhood augmentation strategy to derive enhanced cell representations, making a compromise of inter-cluster discrimination and intra-cluster homogeneity. Meanwhile, the Leiden algorithm assigns coarse-grained labels to each cell based on the learned representations. To further refine the clustering results, CAKE proceeds to train a self-Knowledge Distiller Model (KD model) on a subset of high-quality data, consisting of high-density anchor cells from each cluster. The primary objective of this KD model is to generate the final soft-clustering results, which represents a more accurate and reliable representation of the data’s inherent structure. The results from multiple real scRNA-seq datasets have demonstrated that CAKE outperforms state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods in terms of accuracy and robustness. Notably, CAKE also exhibits superior performance in identifying cell subtypes and rare cell types, thereby furnishing researchers with more profound biological insights.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

CAKE employs a two-stage self-supervised learning algorithm to learn cell representation and clustering labels, as illustrated in Figure 1. Firstly, CAKE utilizes an enhanced contrastive learning model inspired by the principles of MOCO [24], and integrates a task-specific data augmentation strategy aimed at learning clustering-friendly cell representation. Subsequently, CAKE uses a preliminary clustering approach to derive pseudo clustering labels based on the learned cell representation. In the second-stage, CAKE introduces a self-KD model, which is trained on high-density anchor cells from preliminary clusters, and finally assigns the soft and refined clustering labels to cells.

Framework of CAKE. CAKE adopts a two-stage prediction strategy. In the first stage, CAKE trains a contrastive learning model with a mixture neighborhood augmentation on the preprocessed gene expression matrix. Then, CAKE clusters the learned latent features using Leiden to obtain coarse-grained labels. In the second stage, CAKE proceeds to train a self-KD model on a subset of high-quality data, consisting of high-density anchor cells from each cluster.

Data Preprocessing

Before being input into CAKE, we perform several traditional preprocessing procedures to ensure the quality of data. Firstly, we filter out genes that express in fewer than three cells. This step helps remove genes with low expression levels or limited biological relevance. Next, we normalize the filtered gene count expression data to guarantee uniform total counts across cells. This normalization step alleviates any potential bias arising from variations in sequencing depth. The log transformation is applied to further stabilize the means and variances of gene expression. Based on the normalized gene expression data, we select the top 2000 highly variable genes for subsequent analysis. These genes are assumed to be highly correlated with cell type identification and fundamental biological processes. Generally, the positive samples consist of the sample augmented by random noise or corruptions, while the negative samples consist of other samples and their augmentations [24, 25, 28]. Theoretically, the InfoNCE loss in Equation (1) can be decomposed into two terms: the alignment term, promoting the proximity of positive pairs, and the uniformity term, encouraging instances to be uniformly distributed on a unit hypersphere by pushing negative pairs apart [29–31]. However, in unsupervised tasks, the inclusion of the uniformity term could potentially result in class collision. This would happen when some negative pairs belong to the same class and are erroneously pushed apart, consequently complicating the downstream clustering analysis [32].

Therefore, CAKE takes the MOCO framework as the backbone, then employs a task-related augmentation strategy and an enhanced loss function to address the issues. Previous study, like scNAME [20], introduced the neighborhood contrastive learning to solve the class collision. The fundamental concept behind these methods is that cells displaying similar gene expression profiles are highly probable to share the same cell type, so these similar cells could be considered as candidate positive pairs. However, these operations need to be approached cautiously, as using a naïve KNN graph would also introduce cells from different classes. This could potentially lead to the formation of large mixed clusters and consequently hinder the clustering performance, such as the detection of rare cell types.

Thus, we proposed a mixture augmentation strategy, which considers both the cooperation of the original sample and its neighborhood. Cell clusters refinement by self-knowledge distillation

While the clustering approach offers pseudo-labels for each cell, it may still suffer from errors caused by the KNN graph. Thus, we move forward by identifying cell anchors to create a high-quality training dataset for the second stage of CAKE. The cells with higher density values in the clusters are assumed to be the representative cells, denoted as anchors. Intuitively, those cells distributed at the edges of the clusters are more susceptible to being wrongly clustered, while these anchor cells are usually located in the center of clusters, which are more likely to have the correct labels. We select the top 50 percent of cells with the highest densities in each cluster as anchor cells, then the scRNA-seq profile of these cells together with their pseudo-labels constitutes a high-quality training dataset for the second-stage training. Notably, the distribution of cells across the pseudo clusters may be biased or skewed, which is characterized as imbalanced classification problem. To address this issue, we calculate the average number of cells per pseudo label, then perform oversampling on cells with fewer pseudo labels to meet this average number.

Based on well-labeled anchor cells, we employ a self-KD model in the second stage to handle the wrongly clustered problem. The KD model [36] has conventionally found application in imparting knowledge from a complex teacher model to a more lightweight student model. This is achieved by considering the predictive outcomes of the teacher model as ‘soft labels’ during the training of the student model. Nevertheless, we utilize KD model to extract the reliable information from teacher–student model and obtain the refined soft-clustering labels by the student model. In terms of implementation, the self-KD model transfers the encoder from the first stage with a linear classifier layer as teacher model and build the student model with the same architecture. EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS

Datasets

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of CAKE in scRNA-seq data analysis, we conducted experiments using a diverse set of 11 real well-annotated scRNA-seq datasets that were carefully curated from recently published studies. These datasets exhibit a wide range of cell types and biological processes, originating from various species and organs, including the human pancreas, mouse heart, mouse retina mouse spleen, etc., which provides a representative collection for our analysis. Additionally, the datasets are derived using different sequencing techniques, including Smart-seq2, 10x Genomic, inDrop, Drop-seq and CEL-seq2. For instance, the Muraro [37] dataset was derived from human pancreas and performed with CEL-seq2 suquencing technology, the Quake_Smart_seq2_Heart [38] dataset originated from the mouse heart, sequenced by Smart-seq2 and the Shekhar [39] was derived from mouse retina, sequenced by Drop-seq technique. The more detailed information about these datasets is listed in Supplementary materials table S1.

Comprehensive benchmarking on real scRNA-seq data

For comparison, we selected eight SOTA method with different strategies to demonstrate the superiority of CAKE, including GiniClust3 [12], Leiden, SC3S [40], scNAME [20], scziDesk [18], scDeepCluster [16], graph-sc [23] and Clear [41]. Among these methods, scNAME, scziDesk, scDeepCluster, graph-sc and Clear are based on deep neural networks; furthermore, scNAME and Clear also employ the contrastive learning framework. We selected two widely recognized metrics to quantify the clustering performance: the adjusted rand index (ARI) and the normalized mutual information (NMI). These metrics measure the consistency between the clustering labels and the actual cell types. To ensure fairness, we presented the actual number of clusters into each method, allowing for a comparison of clustering performance at the same level of resolution. A set of CAKE’s hyperparameters for each dataset is provided in Supplementary materials Table S2. Hyperparameters for the comparison methods were set to the default or recommended values in the original studies. For each dataset, we repeated each method for five runs and recorded the mean values.

The comprehensive overview of clustering results across 11 real datasets are summarized in Figure 2A and B. As depicted in Figure 2A, the dot plots, where the scatter size indicates the ARI ranking among the nine methods and the color represents ARI values, demonstrate that CAKE achieves top2 ARI values in all datasets, and ranks first in eight datasets and second in three datasets for details. The similar results upon NMI could be found in Supplementary materials Figure S1A. Furthermore, the variations upon ARI values across 11 datasets are shown in Figure 1B (See NMI in Supplementary materials Figure S1B). It is worth highlighting that CAKE stands out as the sole method that achieves ARI values exceeding 0.8 consistently across all datasets with the lowest variance, which demonstrates the superior accuracy and robustness of CAKE. To provide an intuitive understanding of the cell representation, we generate 2D scatter plot of these learned cell representations by umap. Here, we select scNAME, scziDesk, scDeepCluster, graph-sc, Clear, CAKE and naïve umap with top 2000 highly variable genes. The visualization of Muraro [37] dataset is shown in Figure 2C. The colors of cells denote the true labels provided by the dataset. In Figure 2C, both scNAME and CAKE show highly compacted clusters internally and notable gaps between clusters, with CAKE achieving the better clustering performance. Other methods like graph-sc and scDeepCluster exhibit the fragmented clusters of alpha cells and beta cells, respectively. Notably, Clear is also a contrastive-learning-based method, but it struggles to clearly differentiate between alpha, beta, delta and gamma cells. The primary enhancement of CAKE is largely attributed to the mixture neighborhood augment strategy. Similar conclusion could be drawn in Supplementary materials figure S2. Moreover, we evaluate the running time of CAKE and compare it with other eight methods. And the running time results can be found in the Supplementary materials figure S6. Overall, our method demonstrates a certain level of advantage in efficiency among the deep learning methods, and can be easily extended to the large scale of datasets. To summarize, all these results confirm that CAKE effectively captured the underlying cell representations and successfully clustered cells with high accuracy and reliability.

CAKE can effectively distinguish more subgroups of cells

In addition to comprehensively benchmarking the clustering performance, the identification of cell subtypes also plays a crucial role in revealing the biological insights of scRNA-Seq data. We took the Quake_Smart_seq2_Heart [38] dataset originated from the mouse heart. This dataset totally contains 4365 cells, originally annotated by smooth muscle cell, myofibroblast cell, cardiac neuron, fibroblast, endothelial cell, cardiac muscle cell, endocardial cell and leukocyte. We employed CAKE to visualize the learned cell representations, and the UMAP plot with annotated cell types and clustering labels were presented in Figure 3A and Figure 3B, respectively. Obviously, CAKE categorized the cardiac muscle cells into cluster 5 and 6, and concurrently partitioned the leukocyte cells into cluster 2, 8 and 10. To further validate the biological significance of these subtypes, we performed the differential expression gene (DEG) analysis on these subtypes using Scanpy. By intersecting those DEGs with well-known marker genes, finally, we annotated cluster 5 to ventricular cardiomyocytes (VCM) and cluster 6 to atrial cardiomyocyte (ACM). Cardiomyocytes in the atrium are generally smaller and have a more loosely arranged structure compared with VCM. Additionally, they display distinct gene expression patterns involving transcription factors, structural proteins, ion channels, etc. [42]. Three typical marker genes, namely Nppa, Myl4 and Myl7, exhibited significantly higher expression levels in atrial cardiomyocytes (ACM) compared with VCM [43]. For instance, the expression fold change of Nppa was 17.8 times (with ) higher in ACM than in VCM. Similarly, the expression fold change of Myl4 was 77.8 times higher (with ), Myl7 was 81.8 times higher (with ), compared with VCM. Differently, VCM was located in the muscular walls of the heart, mainly enriched in genes encoding sarcomere proteins (Myl2,Myh7), and also exhibited significantly high expression level of the LIM domain gene family Fhl2 [43, 44].

CAKE IS SENSITIVE TO IDENTIFY RARE TYPE CELLS

Most of unsupervised clustering methods have been proven effective in identifying major cell types. However, comprehensive characterization of minor/rare cell types continues to pose challenges. A rising number of research highlight the critical role played by these rare cell types, which contains progenitor cells, stem cells and circulating tumor cells, as significant driving factors in the development and progression of tissues or diseases [47, 48]. To validate the capability of rare cells type identification, we select three datasets, including Shekhar [39], Baron_Human [49] and Macosko [50], as benchmarking. Additionally, we employ GiniClust3, a well-designed method for the identification of rare cell types, alongside other deep learning based methods, including Clear, graph-sc, scNAME, scziDesk and scDeepCluster, for the purpose of comparison.

The Shekhar dataset was derived from mouse retina, containing a total of 26 830 cells, with a diverse of 18 cell types, such as RBC (Rod Bipolar cell), MG (Mueller Glia), a varied series of BC (Cone Bipolar cell), RP (Rod Photoreceptors) and CP (Cone Photoreceptors), etc. We assessed these methods using the RP and CP cells at a rare cell concentration of 0.34% and 0.18%. The really limited number of cells posed significant challenges for cell representation learning and clustering, especially for deep learning based methods. To provide a comprehensive visualization of the clustering results, we employed UMAP to project the cell representations of all these methods onto a 2D space, as illustrated in Figure 4A. Remarkably, among these methods, only CAKE and GiniClust3 distinctly differentiated RP and CP cells, and lead to the accurate clustering results. Furthermore, we calculated Jaccard index between the prediction clustering labels and the ground truth cell labels, and recorded the maximum Jaccard index for each pair in Figure 4B. The Jaccard index provided a clear quantification of the clustering performance for each subtype. We abandoned scziDesk because it failed to recover the majority of cell types. In Figure 4B, the Jaccard index matrix of CAKE exhibits apparently diagonal block structure, which indicates the consistency of clustering results both in both major and rare cell types. On the other hand, GiniClust3 accurately discerns the rare cell types, but shows a tendency to mixture some certain major cell types. Moreover, other deep learning based methods exhibit limitations in effectively handling both major and rare cell types.

Baron_Human dataset, derived from human pancreas, comprises a total of 8569 cells, including four rare cell types (macrophage 0.64%, mast 0.29%, epsilon 0.21%, schwann 0.15% and t cell). While Macosko dataset consists of 44 808 mouse retinal cells, and the percentages of four rare cell types fibroblasts, microglia, pericytes and astrocytes are 0.19%, 0.15%, 0.14% and 0.12%, respectively. Likewise, CAKE clearly distinguished the major and rare cells in most cases, and outperformed than other methods. In summary, our comprehensive analysis of three scRNA-seq containing rare cell types, demonstrating that CAKE is a powerful tool for detecting major and rare cell types from normal tissues and disease samples simultaneously.

ABLATION STUDY

While some contrastive learning-based methods have been applied in scRNA-seq data analysis[20, 41, 51], challenges still persisted regarding the augmentation strategy and their application in the context of cell clustering tasks. In this study, CAKE employed the mixture neighborhood augment strategy and self-KD model to further improve the performance of contrastive learning methods. To validate the effectiveness of the proposed strategies within CAKE, we performed three types of ablation experiments, which involved scenarios with only KNN, with/without KNN and with/without the self-KD model. All the ablation experiments were conducted on the same parameter settings. Firstly, we run CAKE on Plasschaert dataset, enabling us to directly visualize the impacts of these strategies. In Figure 5A, the top row is annotated with the actual cell types, providing a ground truth reference, while the bottom row is annotated with the clustering results. The umap plot of CAKE without KNN augmentation show a loose structure for major cell types, especially ciliated cells, basal cells and secretory cells, and struggles to clearly distinguish the minor cell types, such as cycling basal cells. This finding is in line with the hypothesis that corruption augmentation may not be an optimal approach for cell clustering task. We also conducted a experiment that exclusively assessed data augmentation only within the neighborhood of cells. The KNNs graph would introduce some false neighbors, and these erroneous associations would also reduce the distance between cells of different types during model training, which would increase the difficulties of distinguishing and classifying them. Fortunately, the mixture group of secretory cells and ciliated cells, as well as Krt4/12+ cells, also serves as compelling evidence to support the assumption. Meanwhile, we also compare the visualization and clustering result with/without self-KD model (See in Supplementary materials Figure S5). We can observe that CAKE with self-KD model has accurately corrected the cell types at the interface between basal cells and secretory cells, resulting in an improvement of nearly 0.03 in ARI (0.941 compared with 0.914).

Finally, through ablation experiments, we have demonstrated that the proposed mixture neighborhood augment strategy generated more distinguishable cell representations, which are suitable for cell clustering tasks, while the self-KD model further provided self-correcting capability of confusable cell types.

DISCUSSION

Single-cell sequencing technology has provided unprecedented opportunities for comprehensive studies of cell types, functions, interactions, etc. Among these fields, deciphering cell heterogeneity from single cell RNA-seq data is one of the most critical steps in the analysis. Despite the numerous methods that have been proposed, there is still substantial room for improvement in both cell representation and clustering performance. In this study, we propose a novel two-stages self-supervised model, named CAKE, integrating a contrastive learning model and self-KD model.

The novelty of CAKE lies in two significant aspects. Firstly, we introduce a mixture neighborhood augmentation strategy along with a corresponding loss function into the conventional contrastive learning architecture. These components take into account both the cell-specific characteristics and the similarities among neighboring cells. This augmentation results in more condensed and cluster-friendly cell representations. Subsequently, we assemble anchor cells with high-quality clustering labels to train a self-KD model. This self-KD model further aids in the refinement of wrongly clustered cells. The vivid ablation experiments have confirmed the efficacy of the augmentation strategy and the self-KD model in terms of cell representation learning and clustering accuracy. Moreover, 11 real scRNA-seq datasets and eight competitive methods were selected to comprehensively assess the superiority of CAKE. The experimental results proved that CAKE outperforms in terms of accuracy and robustness the other SOTA methods in visualization and cell clustering across a wide range of biological scenarios. The running time analysis indicates that CAKE is the most efficient among these deep learning methods, which demonstrates that CAKE can easily extend to process large-scale datasets. Notably, CAKE could also identify more biologically meaningful cell subgroups, even for those rare type cells comprising just a minuscule fraction of the total. All of these experiments prove the extensive applicability of CAKE in the field of cellular heterogeneity analysis. With the advancement of single cell multi-omics techniques [52, 53], we intend to expand the application of CAKE to the analysis of single-cell multi-omics data. Additionally, we aim to integrate biological interpretability into the model, thereby enabling researchers to achieve a deeper and more comprehensive understanding of cellular properties [54, 55].
