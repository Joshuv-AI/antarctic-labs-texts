Endovascular stent-graft repair of anastomotic pseudoaneurysms after aortic arch replacement: A case series of 3 patients
Authors: Takaya Shintani, Akira Ikoma, Yamato Tsunoda, Kodai Fukuda, Ryuta Okuhira, Nobuyuki Higashino, Atsufumi Kamisako, Hirotatsu Sato, Ryo Nakamura, Hideki Kunimoto, Kentaro Honda, Yoshiharu Nishimura, Hiroshi Doi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pseudoaneurysm formation after total aortic arch replacement (TAR) with a frozen elephant trunk (FET) is a rare complication; however, we have experienced 3 cases who developed pseudoaneurysms at graft-to-graft anastomoses in the remote postoperative period. In all 3 cases, the pseudoaneurysms formed several years after FET-assisted TAR at sites adjacent to the sternum, specifically at the anastomoses between the prosthetic graft branches and the left axillary artery (LAxA) bypass graft. Pseudoaneurysms usually occur at anastomoses between native vessels and prosthetic grafts; however, a distinctive feature of these cases is that they all developed pseudoaneurysms at the graft-to-graft anastomoses, an extremely rare location. Because surgical reconstruction by re-sternotomy was considered highly invasive, all 3 cases underwent stent-graft endovascular treatment (EVT). Complete exclusion of the pseudoaneurysms was achieved in all 3 cases, and no re-expansion or rebleeding was observed during their follow-up. This report presents 3 rare cases in which EVT was effective for repairing pseudoaneurysms occurring at prosthetic graft anastomoses, an extremely rare location, in the remote postoperative period after FET-assisted TAR.

Introduction

Total aortic arch replacement (TAR) with a frozen elephant trunk (FET) has become widely adopted as a surgical technique that enables one-stage treatment of extensive aortic diseases involving the aortic arch and the proximal descending aorta. Because late complications, such as anastomotic pseudoaneurysm formation, have been reported in the remote postoperative period [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]], long-term follow-up is essential.

In standard FET-assisted TAR, reconstruction of the left subclavian artery (LSA) is usually performed. However, many cases are technically difficult due to the need for dissection and anastomosis in a deep operative field, and the procedure carries some risk of intraoperative complications, including recurrent laryngeal nerve injury and vascular injury. In the 3 cases presented in this report, we adopted a surgical strategy in which a bypass to the left axillary artery (LAxA) was created proximal to zone 2, in order to reduce the above risks and enable safer and more reliable reconstruction. This approach allowed us to avoid the deep dissection normally required for LSA anastomosis, and enabled the distal anastomosis to be created at a more superficial location closer to the surgeon. This approach facilitates intraoperative manipulation and hemostatic control, with technical characteristics that contribute to safer surgical procedures.

All 3 cases in this report developed pseudoaneurysms several years after FET-assisted TAR, at sites adjacent to the sternum, specifically at the anastomoses between prosthetic graft branches and the LAxA bypass graft. Pseudoaneurysms occur mainly at the anastomoses between native vessels and prosthetic grafts. However, in these 3 cases, the pseudoaneurysms developed at the graft-to-graft anastomoses, an extremely rare location.

Because surgical reconstruction by re-sternotomy was considered highly invasive and associated with increased risk of rupture, stent-graft (SG) endovascular treatment (EVT) was selected in all 3 cases. Using this method, we achieved complete exclusion of the pseudoaneurysms in all 3 patients. Re-expansion or rebleeding were not observed during the follow-up. This report describes our experience of 3 cases and demonstrates the effectiveness of EVT for pseudoaneurysms occurring at a rare site (ie, the prosthetic graft anastomoses) in the remote postoperative period after FET-assisted TAR.

Case summary

Case 1

A man in his 70s underwent TAR with the FET technique for an aortic arch aneurysm 5 years earlier. There was no medical history of connective tissue disease (eg, Marfan syndrome, Loeys-Dietz syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome) or other disease that may predispose to late anastomotic pseudoaneurysm formation. At the time of surgery, a Gelweave Plexus 4 Branch 28 mm graft (Terumo Vascutek, Renfrewshire, Scotland, UK) was used as the main graft, a Gelweave straight graft 8 mm (Terumo Vascutek, Renfrewshire, Scotland, UK) was used for bypass to the LAxA, and a Frozenix J Graft open SG measuring 39 mm × 120 mm (Japan Lifeline, Tokyo, Japan) was used as the FET device. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was followed regularly with contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT).

Five years after surgery, the patient presented with anterior chest bulging and pain. Laboratory testing revealed mild anemia with a hemoglobin level of 10.2 g/dL. Contrast-enhanced CT revealed a pseudoaneurysm (50 mm in size) around the prosthetic graft anastomosis, between the second branch graft and the LAxA (Fig. 1A). The pseudoaneurysm was located adjacent to the posterior aspect of the sternum and was associated with thinning of the aneurysmal wall. The left axillary artery was then exposed and punctured under direct vision. A 0.035-inch guidewire (PIOLAX Hydrophilic Guidewire; SURF, Yokohama, Japan) and a 4 -Fr catheter (Tempo; Cordis, Miami Lakes, FL) were used to insert the catheter from the bypass vessel into the aorta, over the pseudoaneurysm. The guidewire was then replaced with a 0.035-inch extra-stiff Amplatz guidewire (Cook Medical, Bloomington, IN). The 5Fr. sheath was changed to an 8 Fr. guiding sheath (Destination; Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) that was advanced to the anastomotic site. Heparin was administered during the procedure to maintain an activated clotting time (ACT) of ≥ 200 seconds. The antibiotic cefazolin (1 g) was administered during the perioperative period.

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 6 without complications. After the procedure, dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin and clopidogrel) was administered for 6 months and followed by single antiplatelet therapy (aspirin). Case 2

A woman in her 70s underwent TAR with the FET technique for an aortic arch aneurysm 4 years earlier. There was no medical history of connective tissue disease (eg, Marfan syndrome, Loeys-Dietz syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome) or other disease that may predispose to late anastomotic pseudoaneurysm formation. The devices used included a Gelweave Plexus 4 Branch 26 mm graft (Terumo Vascutek, Renfrewshire, Scotland, UK) as the main graft, a Gelweave straight graft 8 mm (Terumo Vascutek, Renfrewshire, Scotland, UK) for bypass to the LAxA, and a Frozenix J Graft open SG measuring 33 mm × 90 mm (Japan Lifeline, Tokyo, Japan) as the FET device. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was regularly followed with contrast-enhanced CT.

Four years after surgery, the patient presented with anterior chest bulging. Laboratory tests revealed mild anemia with a hemoglobin level of 9.7 g/dL and an elevated inflammatory response with a C-reactive protein level of 7.10 mg/dL. Contrast-enhanced CT revealed the formation of a pseudoaneurysm around the prosthetic graft anastomosis between the 4-branched graft and the LAxA, accompanied by thinning of the aneurysmal wall (Fig. 2A). Because the pseudoaneurysm (20 mm in size) was located behind the sternum and re-sternotomy was considered highly invasive due to the high risk of rupture, we performed EVT with SG placement. Under general anesthesia, the left axillary artery was exposed and punctured under direct vision. A 0.035-inch guidewire (PIOLAX Hydrophilic Guidewire; SURF, Yokohama, Japan) and a 4-Fr catheter (Tempo; Cordis, Miami Lakes, FL) were used to insert the catheter from the bypass vessel into the aorta, over the pseudoaneurysm. The guidewire was then replaced with a 0.035-inch extra-stiff Amplatz guidewire (Cook Medical, Bloomington, IN). The 5Fr. sheath was changed to an 8 Fr. guiding sheath (Destination, Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) that was advanced to the anastomotic site. The catheter was then replaced with a sizing catheter (EG MultiSizer; Medicos Hirata, Osaka, Japan). A self-expanding SG (Viabahn 9 mm -5 cm; W. L. Gore & Associates, Flagstaff, AZ) was inserted to sufficiently cover the pseudo-aneurysm (Figs. 2B and C). The catheter was then dilated with an 8 mm × 40 mm balloon (Mustang; Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA). Post-deployment angiography demonstrated the disappearance of contrast filling within the pseudoaneurysm, and no evidence of endoleak. The intraoperative fluoroscopy time was 10 minutes. The volume of contrast medium (Iopamidol-370; Bayer, Nordrhein-Westfalen, Germany) was 70 mL. Heparin was administered during the procedure to maintain an ACT of ≥ 200 seconds. The antibiotic cefazolin (1 g) was administered during the perioperative period.

Postoperative contrast-enhanced CT confirmed the disappearance of the pseudoaneurysm, indicating its complete exclusion by EVT. Postoperatively, the patient underwent sternal wire removal and negative pressure wound therapy, resulting in satisfactory wound healing. The patient’s general condition remained stable, and she was discharged on postoperative day 20. After the procedure, dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin and clopidogrel) was administered for 6 months, followed by single antiplatelet therapy (aspirin). The patient was followed up for 4 years after surgery, during which time the pseudoaneurysm remained visible and no endoleak was observed on contrast-enhance CT (Table 1).

Case 3

Postoperatively, the patient was followed with regular contrast-enhanced CT.

Contrast-enhanced CT revealed a pseudoaneurysm (30 mm in size) around the prosthetic graft anastomosis between the second branch graft and the LAxA, accompanied by thinning of the aneurysmal wall and close proximity to the posterior surface of the sternum (Fig. 3A). Discussion

FET-assisted TAR is a valuable surgical technique that enables single-stage treatment of extensive aortic pathologies involving the aortic arch and descending thoracic aorta. However, late complications, such as anastomotic pseudoaneurysm formation, have been reported during the remote postoperative period [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]].

Pseudoaneurysms mostly occur at anastomotic sites between the native arteries and prosthetic grafts [6]. However, all 3 cases reported here developed pseudoaneurysms at graft-to-graft anastomoses, representing an extremely rare pathological condition.

One proposed mechanism for the formation of pseudoaneurysms at prosthetic graft anastomoses is late suture failure occurring within a fibrous capsule, which forms as a result of the chronic inflammatory changes in the surrounding tissue [7]. In addition, when prosthetic SGs are positioned close to the posterior aspect of the sternum, repetitive mechanical friction may lead to suture rupture or graft separation, ultimately resulting in pseudoaneurysm formation [8]. Bonkohara et al. [9] reported a patient with Marfan syndrome and pectus excavatum who developed a pseudoaneurysm behind the sternum 1 year and 4 months after Bentall’s procedure and hemi-arch replacement. They reported that the vascular prosthesis injury, only ∼ 4 mm long, was located directly below the sternal wire. It is important to consider the possibility that the posterior sternum or sternal wire may cause a potentially fatal mediastinal injury after surgery. Possible countermeasures include isolating the posterior sternum from the pericardial cavity with a thick expanded polytetrafluoroethylene sheet and sternal closure without metal wires [9].

In TAR, reconstruction of the LSA usually requires deep surgical exposure and technically demanding dissection and anastomosis. Consequently, this procedure is associated with an increased risk of intraoperative complications, including recurrent laryngeal nerve injury and vascular injury [10].

To mitigate these risks, all 3 cases underwent reconstruction by using a bypass to the LAxA rather than direct LSA reconstruction, and the distal anastomosis was created proximally to zone 2 [11]. This approach eliminates the need for deep dissection around the LSA and allows the distal anastomosis to be positioned at a more superficial location closer to the surgeon, thereby facilitating surgical manipulation and hemostasis. Moreover, reconstruction using a LAxA bypass is technically straightforward, and its clinical utility has increasingly been reported [12].

However, this surgical configuration may also predispose the graft-to-graft anastomosis located behind the sternum to concentrated pulsatile flow and mechanical stress, potentially contributing to pseudoaneurysm formation during the remote postoperative period. In all 3 cases, the pseudoaneurysms developed several years after FET-assisted TAR at graft-to-graft anastomoses located immediately posterior to the sternum. In resource-constrained settings where SGs are not available, an open approach may be the only option. This approach has been reported to be safe and feasible when performed by an experienced team [13]. In addition, if multiple branch vessels require treatment, endovascular treatment may be difficult and open -surgery may be more effective. However, in cases where extensive thoracotomy is required or severe adhesions are expected, endovascular treatment may be the safer treatment [14]. In the 3 cases presented here, surgical repair via re-sternotomy was considered extremely invasive because of the presence of severe adhesions, the difficulty in securing an adequate operative field, and the high risk of perioperative complications. Therefore, EVT using SG implantation was selected for all 3 cases, and resulted in complete exclusion of the pseudoaneurysms. Notably, access via the LAxA was feasible through the pre-existing bypass graft, allowing safe and reliable treatment, while avoiding additional surgical invasiveness. Postoperative contrast-enhanced CT confirmed the complete disappearance of the pseudoaneurysms in all patients, without major complications.

These findings indicate that EVT represents a minimally invasive and effective treatment option, even for rare pseudoaneurysms arising at graft-to-graft anastomoses. Furthermore, the present cases underscore the importance of careful evaluation of the SG configuration and angulation during surgical planning. For this EVT, a self-expandable SG was used instead of balloon-expandable covered stents, such as the Viabahn VBX (W. L. Gore & Associates, Flagstaff, AZ). The VBX has a low profile, making it easy to place according to the lesion, and even if endoleak is detected, it can be treated by additional balloon expansion. Therefore, we believe it is more appropriate to use this stent. However, in Japan, the Viabahn VBX stent is not covered by insurance for use in iatrogenic injury. Therefore, we used self-expandable SGs instead. Device selection was based on ∼ 20% oversizing of the bypass graft diameter (8 mm) to the left axillary artery to prevent endoleaks, and a 9 or 10 mm Viabahn was selected for an 8 mm axillary bypass graft. The graft was placed to sufficiently cover the pseudoaneurysm and fit within the axillary bypass graft to avoid blocking branch vessels.

Conclusion

We have presented 3 cases with pseudoaneurysm at prosthetic graft-to-graft anastomoses that occurred in the late postoperative period after FET-assisted TAR. All 3 pseudoaneurysms were located behind the sternum at the junction of an arch branch graft and a LAxA bypass graft. EVT using SGs achieved complete exclusion of the pseudoaneurysms, with excellent clinical outcomes. These cases demonstrate that endovascular SG repair is a safe and effective alternative for treating anastomotic pseudoaneurysms, even in challenging locations.
