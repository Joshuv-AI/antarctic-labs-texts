A Case Series of Hypermucoid Klebsiella pneumoniae Liver Abscess in Caucasian Americans Without Travel History: An Epidemiologic Shift?
Authors: Krishna Sai Kiran Sakalabaktula, Sameer Kumar Majety, Sri Ratna Divya Nanduri, Divya Durga, David Kreps, Devi Prasad Botu, Gopichand Muppana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae (hvKp) is an emerging cause of community‐acquired pyogenic liver abscess (PLA), classically described in East and Southeast Asia. We report two cases of hvKp‐associated PLA in Caucasian women of American origin with no history of international travel, animal exposure, or Asian food consumption. Both patients presented with fever and right upper quadrant abdominal pain and were found to have solitary hepatic abscesses on imaging. Blood and abscess cultures confirmed K. pneumoniae with a positive string test (> 5 mm), K2 capsular serotype, and rmpA gene, establishing the hypermucoid phenotype. While one patient had diabetes mellitus, the other had no major underlying health condition apart from the bacterial infection reported here. Both patients were successfully treated with intravenous antimicrobial therapy, initially broad‐spectrum and subsequently de‐escalated based on susceptibility results, in combination with image‐guided percutaneous drainage. Neither patient developed metastatic complications. These cases suggest a broader geographic and demographic distribution of hvKp‐associated PLA than previously recognized. The string test served as an early diagnostic clue, facilitating timely intervention. Given the potential for rapid clinical progression, early recognition and aggressive management, including antimicrobial therapy and source control, remain essential to reducing morbidity associated with hvKp PLA.Keywords: community‐acquired infection, hypermucoid phenotype, hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae , pyogenic liver abscess, string test

Introduction

A pyogenic liver abscess (PLA) is a potentially life‐threatening intra‐abdominal infection. Although it was previously well associated with polymicrobial infections, such as Escherichia coli and anaerobes, hypervirulent strains of Klebsiella pneumoniae (hvKp) have emerged in recent decades as dominant causative agents, particularly in East and Southeast Asia [1, 2]. These strains have frequently been associated with a hypermucoviscous phenotype and invasive disease, classically linked to capsular serotypes K1 and K2 and enhanced virulence potential. However, accumulating evidence suggests that K. pneumoniae virulence exists along a phenotypic and genotypic spectrum rather than as a strict dichotomy between classical and hypervirulent strains, with documented overlap in virulence‐associated features [3, 4].

The clinical presentation of an hvKp liver abscess typically includes fever, right upper quadrant pain, nausea, and leukocytosis. Infections with this pathogen are notable for their ability to cause metastatic complications such as endophthalmitis, meningitis, or septic pulmonary emboli [1, 5]. While initially described predominantly in Asian populations, hvKp has increasingly been reported in Western countries and Europe, including among individuals without Asian ethnicity or recent travel history [6].

Diagnosis of PLA relies on abdominal imaging with ultrasound or computed tomography, which typically demonstrates unilocular or multiloculated hepatic lesions, most commonly involving the right hepatic lobe [5]. Confirmation is obtained through blood or abscess cultures. A hypermucoviscous phenotype may be identified using the “string test,” in which a viscous string > 5 mm is formed when a colony is stretched with a loop, while polymerase chain reaction (PCR) can detect selected virulence‐associated genes in specialized laboratory settings [6].

Treatment requires prompt initiation of appropriate antimicrobial therapy, commonly third‐generation cephalosporins, β‐lactam/β‐lactamase inhibitor combinations, or carbapenems in severe cases [7]. Abscesses larger than 3–5 cm or those demonstrating a poor response to medical therapy may benefit from image‐guided percutaneous drainage. Based on the clinical and radiological response, the duration of the treatment may range from 4 to 6 weeks [8].

Here, we report two cases of K. pneumoniae liver abscesses exhibiting a hypermucoviscous phenotype in Caucasian individuals of American origin without recent international travel. These cases underscore the importance of maintaining clinical awareness of invasive K. pneumoniae infections outside traditionally recognized epidemiological settings.

Case Presentation

Case 1

Case History and Physical Examination

A 62‐year‐old American‐origin Caucasian female with a past medical history of migraine headaches and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) presented to the hospital with a three‐day history of diffuse abdominal pain and chest pain, associated with shortness of breath, chills, and fever (maximum recorded temperature of 38.9°C [102°F]). She did not report any history of recent travel, alcohol usage, or animal exposure. On physical examination, the lungs were clear to auscultation. She had right upper‐quadrant tenderness without guarding or rebound. The spleen tip was not palpable, and the neurological examination was unremarkable.

On admission, the patient was hemodynamically stable but later developed hypotension and signs of sepsis, requiring transfer to the intensive care unit (ICU). Electrocardiogram revealed sinus tachycardia with a heart rate of 107 bpm. Serial troponins were negative. After ruling out cardiac causes, a left upper quadrant ultrasound also came back inconclusive. Laboratory studies showed leukocytosis with a rising neutrophil count, a prothrombin time of 15.1 s, and an INR of 1.4. The electrolyte panel revealed hypophosphatemia (phosphate 1.0 mg/dL), which was corrected with intravenous potassium phosphate. Her transaminases continued to climb from a baseline AST of 135–179 and an ALT of 233, which was 165 on admission.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Several differential diagnoses were considered, including sepsis secondary to a urinary tract infection (UTI), intestinal perforation with or without fistula formation, amebiasis, echinococcosis, and other parasitic infections. However, urinalysis was normal, effectively ruling out UTI‐related sepsis. Imaging studies ruled out cholecystitis and intestinal perforation. Amebiasis and other parasitic causes were ruled out due to the patient's gender, lack of travel to endemic areas, and absence of characteristic stool features such as hematochezia or melena. Later, blood cultures came back positive in all four bottles for gram‐negative rods that were identified as K. pneumoniae.

A contrast‐enhanced abdominal CT scan (Figure 1) revealed a 4.6 × 4.8 cm lesion in the left hepatic lobe near the porta hepatis, concerning for a hepatic abscess or malignancy. A colonoscopy was also performed to evaluate for a gastrointestinal source or underlying malignancy, which showed normal findings. Hepatic abscess was later confirmed by magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) (Figure 2).

This guided the diagnosis of a hypermucoviscous Klebsiella pneumoniae liver abscess.

Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) from Case 1, confirming the presence of a 4.59 cm hepatic abscess in the left lobe near the porta hepatis. The image demonstrates the lesion's extent and location adjacent to the bile ducts.

Interventional radiology (IR)‐guided percutaneous drainage was performed to relieve the abscess. Later, an IR kept a Jackson‐Pratt (JP) drain in place to drain abscess fluid, whose culture also confirmed K. pneumoniae . The positive “string test” (> 5 mm) (Figure 3) established the diagnosis of a hypermucoviscous strain (hmKp). A positive result for the K2 capsular antigen and the genetic test for rmpA also confirmed this. The case was reported to public health authorities due to its epidemiologic significance.

String test was performed in Case 1 on Klebsiella pneumoniae colonies isolated from abscess fluid culture on agar media. The visible string > 5 mm confirms the hypermucoviscous phenotype associated with the hypervirulent strain.

She was initially started on intravenous piperacillin‐tazobactam. However, the patient developed increasing abdominal pain and drainage site discomfort a few days following the procedure. Repeat abdominal CT demonstrated an enlarging abscess. Surgery was consulted, and IR upsized the drain along with flushes every 12 h and antibiotics were escalated to meropenem due to worsening clinical status. Once antibiotic susceptibilities were available, therapy was de‐escalated to ertapenem for its comfortable once‐daily dosing and subsequently to ceftriaxone 2 g IV daily. She also tested positive for Lyme disease, which led us to include a 10‐day course of doxycycline in the treatment regimen.

During her hospitalization, the patient also developed worsening right‐sided headaches, painful lateral gaze, and difficulty focusing. A comprehensive ophthalmologic evaluation was performed, including an assessment of visual acuity and dilated funduscopic examination, which demonstrated no evidence of endophthalmitis, retinal lesions, or optic disc edema. Neurological examination was nonfocal, with intact cranial nerve function and no signs of meningeal irritation. Ophthalmology and neurology consultations were obtained to evaluate for potential metastatic or central nervous system complications. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain and orbits was unremarkable, with no radiologic evidence of intracranial, orbital, or meningeal involvement. No metastatic infectious foci were identified.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

She was hospitalized for 17 days and discharged on a 3‐week course of IV ceftriaxone via a peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC). The JP drain was also removed after completion of the ceftriaxone course due to a significant reduction in output and resolution of bloody discharge. She was transitioned to oral cefpodoxime for four additional weeks. The patient was followed up at the outpatient clinic 67 days after the initial presentation, during which the repeat imaging demonstrated complete resolution of the liver abscess.

Case 2

Case History and Physical Examination

A 57‐year‐old American‐origin Caucasian female with a past medical history of type 2 diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, and hypothyroidism presented to the emergency department with a 1‐day fever, chills, generalized body aches, and nausea. She denied experiencing abdominal pain, chest pain, vomiting, diarrhea, recent travel, or contact with animals. She appeared acutely ill and febrile, with a highest recorded temperature of 103.6°F (39.8°C) and a heart rate of 137 bpm, meeting the criteria for Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS). Her blood pressure was 108/63 mmHg, oxygen saturation was normal on room air, and her respiratory rate was 20 breaths per minute.

On physical examination, the patient had right upper quadrant tenderness on deep palpation without rebound or guarding. No Murphy's sign was elicited. Cardiopulmonary and neurological examinations were unremarkable.

Laboratory studies revealed leukocytosis with a WBC count of 15,300/μL and 86% neutrophils. Liver function tests showed a mildly elevated AST of 88 IU/L, ALT of 102 IU/L, alkaline phosphatase of 180 IU/L, and total bilirubin of 1.4 mg/dL. Serum lactate was also mildly elevated at 2.1 mmol/L. Her HbA1c was 8.4%, which is consistent with poorly controlled diabetes. C‐reactive protein and procalcitonin were markedly elevated at 174 mg/L and 5.6 ng/mL.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

An initial right upper quadrant ultrasound revealed hepatomegaly (liver span 19.8 cm) with increased echogenicity suggestive of hepatic steatosis, but no clear abscess. The patient was admitted with a course of empiric IV antibiotics (cefepime and doxycycline) and analgesics for pain. On day 2, she continued to have persistent fever and tachycardia despite treatment, and a contrast‐enhanced CT scan (Figure 4) of the abdomen and pelvis was performed. It revealed a 3.6 × 3.4 cm hypodense lesion in segment VI of the right hepatic lobe that is consistent with a liver abscess. A Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) was also done to know more precise borders, which revealed a 4.8 cm × 4.0 cm multi‐loculated abscess in the caudate lobe of the liver (Figure 5).

Contrast‐enhanced axial CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis from Case 2, revealing a 3.6 × 3.4 cm hypodense lesion in segment VI of the right hepatic lobe, consistent with a PLA. The lesion appears to be surrounded by mild inflammatory changes.

Axial T2‐weighted MRI of the abdomen from Case 2, showing the hyperintense liver abscess in segment VI, with crosshair measurements and associated perilesional edema, supporting the diagnosis and planning for IR drainage.

IR performed a CT‐guided aspiration of the lesion, which yielded 20 mL of thick, mucoid pus. Gram staining of the aspirate showed abundant gram‐negative rods, and culture confirmed K. pneumoniae . A string test (Figure 6) performed on the cultured colonies demonstrated a positive result (> 5 mm), confirming the presence of the hypermucoid strain of K. pneumoniae . Blood cultures drawn on admission also returned positive for K. pneumoniae , matching the abscess isolate. The hypermucoid phenotype was also confirmed by the presence of the K2 antigen and positive rmpA in the genetic test.

Positive string test performed in Case 2 on bacterial colonies obtained from the liver abscess aspirate. The string formation > 5 mm confirms the presence of the hypermucoviscous strain of Klebsiella pneumoniae .

Given the diagnosis of a PLA due to hypermucoviscous K. pneumoniae (hmKp) and in the context of underlying diabetes, the patient was transitioned to IV ertapenem 1 g daily for its once‐daily dosing due to its effective gram‐negative coverage. Interventional radiology placed a 10 Fr pigtail catheter for continued drainage. The patient received IV fluids and electrolyte correction, including potassium phosphate for mild hypophosphatemia and IV magnesium for borderline low levels during her hospital course.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The patient remained afebrile after day 3 of treatment and also showed steady clinical improvement. Daily drain output gradually decreased over the next 2 weeks. Repeat imaging on day 14 revealed a significant reduction in abscess size and resolution of perilesional edema. The drain was removed, and she was discharged home on oral cefpodoxime 200 mg twice daily to complete a total of 4 weeks of antibiotic therapy.

During her 3‐week follow‐up visit, the patient reported no symptoms or new right upper quadrant discomfort. Repeat LFT came back normal, and a follow‐up ultrasound showed no residual abscess.

Discussion

PLA due to hvKp has emerged over recent decades as a global public health concern. While K. pneumoniae (classical Kp) has long been established as a nosocomial opportunistic pathogen causing pneumonia, urinary tract infections, and intra‐abdominal sepsis in individuals who are immunocompromised or have comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus or alcohol use disorder, hvKp strains have been increasingly associated with community‐acquired, invasive infections, including liver abscesses, even in previously healthy hosts. hvKp infections were initially described in Taiwan during the 1980s and were predominantly reported in East and Southeast Asian populations, particularly among individuals with diabetes mellitus or other chronic conditions [9, 10]. In our report, one patient had diabetes with poor glycemic control, whereas the other had no known comorbidities. Similar case series were subsequently reported across Southeast Asia, describing invasive monomicrobial liver abscesses associated with K. pneumoniae , often with significant morbidity and mortality. These observations suggested a distinct virulence profile associated with certain K. pneumoniae strains, and a significant association (p = 0.001) between community‐acquired K. pneumoniae liver abscess and diabetes mellitus was reported in one such series [11]. More recently, cases of community‐acquired invasive K. pneumoniae infections have been reported in non‐Asian individuals without travel history, prompting consideration of a broader geographic and demographic distribution [5, 12].

From a microbiological perspective, hypervirulence in K. pneumoniae has traditionally been associated with a hypermucoviscous phenotype and the presence of specific virulence determinants, including capsular serotypes K1 or K2, the regulator of mucoid phenotype A (rmpA), the mucoviscosity‐associated gene A (magA), and siderophores such as aerobactin [13]. However, recent studies indicate that K. pneumoniae virulence exists along a continuum rather than as a binary distinction between classical and hypervirulent strains, with overlapping phenotypic and genotypic features described between these groups [3, 4]. This overlap limits the specificity of hypermucoviscosity as a standalone marker of hypervirulence.

K1 serotypes are observed more commonly in Asian populations, whereas the K2 serotype has been increasingly reported in Western countries, including the United States and Europe [5, 14]. Our two cases involved American‐origin Caucasian females who presented with liver abscesses caused by K. pneumoniae isolates exhibiting a hypermucoviscous phenotype, confirmed by a positive string test, along with K2 capsular serotype and rmpA gene positivity. Importantly, hypermucoviscosity has also been described in classical clinical K. pneumoniae strains lacking the full complement of hypervirulence‐associated genes, highlighting a key limitation of relying on this phenotype alone to identify hvKp [15]. Accordingly, phenotypic findings must be interpreted within the broader clinical, microbiological, and epidemiologic context.

The rmpA gene regulates extracapsular polysaccharide synthesis and plays a role in capsule overproduction, contributing to the mucoid phenotype [16]. The function of magA is restricted to capsular synthesis and is limited to the K1 serotype gene cluster [17]. Aerobactin has been shown to markedly enhance virulence in experimental models; however, its presence was not assessed in the present cases and is therefore discussed here in the context of established literature rather than as a study finding [18]. Both of our isolates tested positive for rmpA and the K2 serotype, findings that are consistent with previously described invasive K. pneumoniae strains, although molecular confirmation of full hypervirulence profiles was not performed.

Clinically, both patients presented with fever, leukocytosis, and right upper quadrant tenderness, with imaging revealing solitary hepatic abscesses measuring 4.6 and 3.6 cm, respectively. Invasive Liver Abscess Syndrome (ILAS) is characterized by monomicrobial, cryptogenic liver abscesses, most commonly associated with hvKp in the community setting [1]. In both cases, no underlying biliary tract disease or gastrointestinal pathology was identified, findings consistent with the cryptogenic nature typically described in ILAS [5, 10].

Siu et al. defined ILAS as a definitive invasive syndrome in patients with extrahepatic manifestations, while probable invasive syndrome refers to patients with an isolated abscess [1]. ILAS can present solely as a liver abscess, manifesting as fevers, chills, and abdominal pain, or with extrahepatic manifestations, including but not limited to endophthalmitis, meningitis, necrotizing fasciitis, and septic pulmonary emboli [19]. These complications are often thought to be established early in the disease course, potentially limiting the impact of delayed antimicrobial intervention [20]. In Case 1, the patient developed worsening right‐sided headaches and visual strain, raising concern for metastatic spread; however, prompt neuroimaging and ophthalmologic and neurologic evaluations excluded central nervous system or ocular involvement. This case underscores the importance of early and thorough evaluation for metastatic complications in suspected hvKp infections.

Kp is part of the normal gut microbiome and may cause infection through endogenous translocation. Approximately 50% of K. pneumoniae infections in ICU settings have been traced back to the patient's gastrointestinal flora [21]. In a murine oral infection model, several genes involved in metabolic pathways, membrane protein synthesis, and transcription regulation were found to be essential for successful colonization and abscess formation [22]. These include central metabolism genes (sdhC), amino acid biosynthesis (argH, leuC, ilvC), membrane proteins (YfgL), and efflux systems (AcrA), highlighting a complex pathogenic process that extends beyond capsule production and siderophore activity.

Most hvKp strains belong to distinct hypervirulent clonal lineages, particularly clonal group 23 (CG23), which includes sequence types ST23, ST26, ST57, and ST1633 [23]. Among these, ST23 is the most frequently identified sequence type associated with liver abscess worldwide.

The string test, used in both of our cases, is a simple and effective screening tool that allows for a rapid diagnosis of hvKp. A retrospective study by Tan et al. demonstrated that a cutoff of > 5 mm showed a sensitivity of 90.5% and had higher reproducibility than a cutoff of > 10 mm or > 15 mm. Although the string test had a poor positive predictive value, it had a high negative predictive value when a cutoff of > 5 mm or > 10 mm was used [24]. This is a cost‐effective, simple, and easily reproducible test that can be a diagnostic clue. Its use may facilitate earlier initiation of appropriate antimicrobial therapy, potentially reducing the risk of metastatic complications while awaiting molecular or immunologic confirmation.

The mortality rates are reported to be between 2.8% and 10.8%. An APACHE II score of 20 or higher, acute respiratory failure, and shock have been identified as strong predictors of metastatic infection. An APACHE II score of 16 or higher with evidence of metastatic infection, septic shock, acute respiratory failure needing ventilatory support, or gas‐forming abscess on imaging has been associated with increased mortality. Although neither of our patients had these complications, Case 1 required ICU transfer due to hypotension and met criteria for sepsis. These findings highlight the importance of early recognition and timely intervention to mitigate the risk of severe extrahepatic manifestations [20]. Another important predictor for a poor prognosis is thrombocytopenia (platelets less than 150 × 109/L) [25]. In selected high‐risk patients, some reports have suggested that aggressive surgical resection may be associated with improved outcomes compared with percutaneous drainage, particularly among those with APACHE II scores of 15 or higher [1].

The management of hvKp PLA requires both appropriate antimicrobial therapy and timely source control. Empirical antibiotics should cover gram‐negative pathogens, with third‐generation cephalosporins or carbapenems preferred, especially if extended‐spectrum beta‐lactamase (ESBL) production is suspected [26]. Antibiotic therapy duration typically depends on the patient's response to treatment and is individualized, ranging from two to four weeks for uncomplicated solitary abscesses and extending to six weeks or longer in cases of complicated or multifocal disease [1]. hvKp isolates are generally susceptible to a broad range of antimicrobial agents, including third‐ and fourth‐generation cephalosporins, monobactams, carbapenems, and fluoroquinolones, while they are uniformly resistant to ampicillin [27]. Empirical therapy often favors an extended‐spectrum cephalosporin over cefazolin, even when isolates appear susceptible, to mitigate the risk of severe complications such as endophthalmitis, meningitis, and pneumonia [26].

In both cases presented, treatment was initiated with broad‐spectrum β‐lactam antibiotics and escalated to meropenem in the setting of clinical deterioration. Following availability of culture susceptibilities, antimicrobial therapy was de‐escalated to targeted agents, including ertapenem and ceftriaxone, with subsequent oral cefpodoxime used for consolidation. Treatment duration was tailored to disease severity and clinical response. The first patient required a prolonged course of approximately 7 weeks due to abscess enlargement and the need for repeated drain interventions, whereas the second patient completed a 4‐week total course following rapid clinical and radiologic improvement. This individualized approach is consistent with current recommendations advocating longer treatment durations for complicated disease [1, 27].

Source control through image‐guided percutaneous drainage is a cornerstone of treatment and was performed in both cases. The presence of drainable fluid collections larger than 3 cm, worsening clinical status, or rising inflammatory markers often warrants intervention [1]. Our patients responded well to catheter drainage, with no need for surgical resection. Importantly, early drainage has been associated with a reduced risk of metastatic complications and improved clinical outcomes [1, 20].

Over the past few decades, cases of hvKP infections and ILAS have been rising in the United States and Europe, even in individuals not of Asian ethnicity, but most have a history of travel or Asian ethnicity [12]. Our patients had no such history, suggesting that hvKp is becoming a global pathogen. Neither of our patients had a history of travel to endemic regions or identifiable epidemiologic risk factors, suggesting that hvKp may be encountered outside traditionally recognized populations. Both patients were Caucasian women of American origin with no known epidemiologic linkage, who were treated at the same hospital in Long Island, New York, within a 3‐month period. While this temporal clustering does not establish causality or confirm a true epidemiologic shift, it raises awareness of the potential for sporadic cases of hypermucoviscous K. pneumoniae infections in non‐endemic regions of the United States. Given that neither patient had recent travel to endemic regions such as East Asia, these cases underscore the need for increased clinical awareness of hmKp even in non‐Asian, community‐acquired settings in the United States, and the string test can detect this critical illness early to treat the condition promptly to prevent complications and optimize outcomes.

Conclusion

These cases highlight the evolving epidemiology of hvKp, demonstrating its capacity to cause severe, community‐acquired PLAs in non‐Asian individuals without traditional risk factors or travel history. The hypermucoid phenotype, identified via the string test and molecular markers such as K2 and rmpA positivity, served as an important early diagnostic clue and facilitated timely intervention. Successful outcomes were achieved through the prompt initiation of broad‐spectrum antibiotics, source control with image‐guided drainage, and appropriate de‐escalation based on culture sensitivities. Together, these cases underscore the importance of maintaining clinical awareness of hvKp infections outside traditionally recognized epidemiologic settings, as well as the practical utility of simple bedside diagnostic tools such as the string test in expediting care and potentially improving outcomes.

Limitations

This report is limited by its small sample size and single‐center scope, which may not reflect broader epidemiological trends. Molecular subtyping beyond rmpA and K2 was not performed, limiting insight into specific clonal lineages (e.g., ST23). In addition, selected virulence factors, including aerobactin, were not assessed in our isolates, limiting conclusions regarding their contribution to pathogenicity. Long‐term follow‐up beyond initial recovery was not conducted, so late complications or recurrence could not be assessed. Environmental or community reservoirs of hvKp were not investigated, leaving the source of infection undetermined.
