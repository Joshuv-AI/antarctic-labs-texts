Energy intake and expenditure of Japanese male soccer, long-distance running, and road cycling athletes across multiple teams
Authors: Naoki Nakamura, Satoko Ozawa, Soshi Tokiwa, Kazuya Yamamoto, Kei Yui, Rikako Yoshitake, Taketo Yamaguchi, Kazuki Kioka, Naomi Omi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
[Purpose]This study aimed to estimate the energy expenditure, intake, and balance of Japanese athletes to assess their intake of carbohydrate, protein, and fat as energy sources.[Methods]Ninety male soccer players, long-distance runners, and road cyclists who belonged to multiple teams were included in this study. The study period covered one training day and one rest day. The energy, carbohydrate, protein, and fat intakes were assessed using food photographs and food records. Energy expenditure was calculated using metabolic equivalent and physical activity level based on practice questionnaires. Energy balance was calculated by subtracting the energy expenditure from the energy intake.[Results]The median values (25th-75th percentile) for energy balance on training day were negative: soccer, −767 (−1,079-98) kcal/day; long-distance running, −367 (−907-169); and road cycling, −948 (−2,577-329). The median daily carbohydrate intake were 5.0 (4.3-6.2), 6.4 (5.2-7.1), and 8.3 (6.6-10.0) g/kg for soccer, long-distance running, and road cycling, respectively.[Conclusion]Our results indicated that the energy balance on training day may have been negative for the Japanese soccer players, long-distance runners, and road cyclists. Considering training time, carbohydrate intake was insufficient or tended to be insufficient compared to the recommended amounts for athletes. These findings should be incorporated into the development of nutritional support strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Athletes have a high energy expenditure for training and competition [1]. To maintain physical function, manage body composition, and provide nutrients for energy, it is important for athletes to match their diet to their energy expenditure [2]. Although long-distance running and road cycling are endurance sports, endurance athletes have the highest energy expenditure among athletes [3]. Soccer also involves intermittent but explosive movement [4]. In Japan’s professional soccer teams, the average team mileage per game exceeded 110 km [5]. Thus, soccer is an endurance sport that requires endurance and high energy expenditure. In a previous study, soccer players [6,7], long-distance runners [3,8,10], and road cyclists [9] were reported to have a negative energy balance, with a low energy intake relative to their energy expenditure.

The intake of carbohydrates, protein, and fat (macronutrients) as a source of energy is important to athletes as well as to the general population [11], and the recommended amounts should be provided for athletes. The International Olympic Committee (IOC) and Joint Statement of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, Dietitians of Canada and American College of Sports Medicine [Nutrition and Athletic Performance (NAP)] provide guidelines for carbohydrate intake [2]. The IOC recommends [12], the NAP [2], and the International Society of Sports Nutrition [13] provide guidelines for protein intake. The NAP recommends the dietary reference intakes [2], and the Japanese dietary reference provides guidelines for fat intake [11]. Previous studies have reported inadequate carbohydrate intake on training days compared with the recommended amounts, but adequate or excessive protein intake in soccer players [7,14] and road cyclists [9,15]. Furthermore, the distribution of protein intake by meal and complementary timing within a day was skewed in soccer players, indicating that the distribution of protein intake within a day needs to be improved [7]. However, adequate amounts of carbohydrates, protein, and fat as energy sources, as well as planning and consuming a balanced intake throughout the day, are important for athletic condition maintenance [2], exercise performance, and muscle mass building and maintenance [2,12,13]. Studies investigating energy balance and carbohydrates, protein, and fat intakes in soccer players, long-distance runners, and road cyclists [3,6-10,14,15] have been limited to single teams or uniform training sessions and do not reflect the results across these sports as a whole. Furthermore, regarding carbohydrate, protein, and fat intakes, while studies on Japanese long-distance runners have been reported, these involved only seven university students from the same team [8]. Japanese soccer players or road cyclists have not been reported.

Thus, the present study aimed to determine the energy expenditure, intake, and balance of Japanese soccer players, long-distance runners, and road cyclists who belonged to multiple teams and to assess their intakes of carbohydrates, protein, and fat as energy sources. The novelty of this study is novel in its focus on the dietary habits of athletes from multiple teams in real-world sports environments. Unlike previous studies limited to single teams or controlled environments, it draws on data from athletes across different teams, better capturing the challenges they face in daily life. In particular, the study aimed to highlight practical challenges in nutritional support by examining meal timing, including breakfast.

METHODS

Study Design and Participants

This cross-sectional study included 90 male athletes (median age, 22 years; 25th-75th percentile, 20-25), comprising soccer players (n = 45; 50.0%), long-distance runners (n = 24; 26.7%), and road cyclists (n = 21; 23.3%). The study sample was collected from 15 soccer teams, 10 long-distance running teams, and 10 road cycling teams. The study’s participation criteria were male college students or employed persons who were not on weight loss or other dietary restrictions, were not taking any medication, and did not receive nutritional support from dietitians or other nutrition professionals. In general, 25 (55.6%), 6 (25.0%), and 9 (42.9%) participants prepared most of their own meals in the soccer, long-distance running, and road cycling groups, respectively. Individuals living in dormitories were not included in this study. Table 1 shows the participants’ competitive level. All participants were classified based on training and performance classification [16] as Tier 3. Tier 3 is defined as competing at the national level but not at the international level.

Study approval was obtained from the persons responsible for each sports club after they were briefed on the study objectives and methods by the researcher. The study objectives and methods were also explained to the participants. Written consent was obtained from all participants. The present study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and with the approval of the committee of the Institute of Health and Sport Sciences, University of Tsukuba (No. 023-42).

This survey was conducted between February and March 2022. Requests to teams and athletes were made only on days that did not fall immediately before or after matches or races. The researcher determined the survey dates after confirming each team’s schedule and selected one available training day and one off day, resulting in either consecutive or nonconsecutive 2-day periods. In the present study, the following survey items were obtained using a web-based questionnaire: (1) demographic data, (2) dietary information, and (3) training data. For the demographic information, the participants’ age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), and body fat percentage were obtained through self-report. The researcher verbally explained these recording methods to the participants prior to the survey.

Energy Intake and Nutrient Components

In the dietary survey, the participants were asked to submit photographs of their meals and complementary foods (e.g., snacks, desserts, and supplements) and to submit the completed questionnaires comprising questions related to their meals and complementary foods. The following three photographs of the meals and complementary foods were taken: one taken from directly above the food before consuming the meals; one taken at a 45° angle to check the depth of the plates before consuming the meals; and one taken from directly above the food after consuming the meals. In addition to these three pictures, the store-bought meals and beverages as well as the nutrition facts label on the package were also photographed. A 10-yen coin was included in the photograph to provide a size reference for the meals. Refills were also photographed in these same methods. To prevent the omission of photography, the participants sent the photos of their meals to the researcher via a web system after consuming each meal and complementary food. Apart from submitting the photographs, the participants recorded the meals and complementary foods they consumed before bedtime each day. For breakfast, lunch, and dinner, the following information were recorded: (a) the time they started eating, (b) the name of the place or store where they ate or purchased the meals, and (c) the name of the meal and ingredients (the name of the menu if they ate out). For the complementary foods, the participants were asked to record (d) the name of the dish, the name of the product, the company name of the food, the amount consumed, and (f) the timing of the consumption.

The energy intake, macronutrient calculations, and 18 food groups were assessed using a common set of nutrition calculation rules by four registered dietitians with experience in dietary surveys and nutrition support for athletes. After 2 days of research, the participants were interviewed by the registered dietitians to check for forgotten records or mistakes in accordance with the established rules. The common rules for nutrient calculation were established by referring to the Dietary Survey Manual: From the First Steps to Practice and Application, supervised by the Japanese Society for Nutrition Improvement, July 2008, 2nd edition [17]. Supplements were also included in the energy and nutrient calculations. The Excel add-in “Nutrition Plus® Ver 1” (Kenpakusha, Tokyo, Japan) compatible with the standard tables of food composition in Japan (2020) (Eighth Revised Version) [18] was used to calculate the nutrient intake. For the intake status of macronutrients per day, the intake (g), intake per body weight (g/kg), and EI % were obtained. The 18 food groups were classified using the Excel add-in “Nutrition Plus® Ver 1” according to its system, while supplements such as protein powder, sports drinks, or nutrient tablets were categorized into the “others” food group based on the classification of the supplements for athletes [19]. Based on the dietary guidelines for athletes [20], among the 18 classified food groups, those predominantly contributing to the carbohydrate, protein, and fat intakes were selected for analysis. Specifically, the carbohydrate-rich groups included cereals, tubers, and fruits; protein-rich groups included legumes, fish and shellfish, meat, eggs, and dairy; and fat-rich groups included oils and fats. The intakes of these selected food groups were used for the analysis.

Energy Expenditure

To estimate the total daily energy expenditure, the participants were asked to answer a questionnaire before bedtime each day. The questionnaire items were as follows: (a) whether the questionnaire was completed on a training day or a rest day?; (b) last night’s bedtime; (c) the time they woke up today; (d) total time of sleep taken throughout the day, except for that during the night; (e) the start and end time of team training and the team training program (1st-3rd: if the participants did); (f) whether or not they did their voluntary training, its total duration, and training program; and (g) main transportation methods and times. The total daily energy expenditure was calculated as the sum of (A) energy expenditure during exercise and (B) energy expenditure during nonexercise periods.

(A) Energy expenditure during exercise

The metabolic equivalents (METs) were used to calculate the energy expenditure during exercise. The activity closest to the one recorded in the training program was selected from the National Institute of Health and Nutrition’s revised METs Table of Physical Activity, the exercise intensity [21] was applied, and the amount of energy expended during the exercise was calculated based on body weight and activity duration. The formula is as follows [22]:

Energy expenditure during exercise (kcal) = METs × body weight (kg) × training hours (h) × 1.05

(B) Energy expenditure when not doing any exercises

The energy expenditure when not doing any exercises was calculated from the basal metabolic reference, age, and physical activity level (PAL), excluding the training time from a 24-h period and referring to the method used to calculate the estimated energy requirement of the Dietary Reference Intakes for Japanese (2020) [14]. The PAL was selected with reference to previous research assessing athletes’ PAL outside of exercise [23] and the PAL value of 1.5 presented in the Dietary Reference Intakes for Japanese (2020) [14], which corresponds to a day spent mostly in a sedentary position with only light activities. The formula is as follows:

Energy expenditure during nonexercise hours throughout the day (kcal) = basal metabolic reference value by age (kcal/kg of body weight/day) × body weight (kg) × 1.5* × (24 − training hours) / 24

*PAL was uniformly set as low (I) = 1.5

The PAL of participants in this study was calculated using their total daily energy expenditure, the basal metabolic reference value by age [11], and body weight. The formula is as follows:

PAL = total daily energy expenditure (kcal/day) / basal metabolic reference value by age (kcal/kg of body weight) × body weight (kg)

Statistical Analysis

Since the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test results showed that the data were not normally distributed, a nonparametric test was applied for all statistical analyses. Consequently, the results were presented as the median (25th-75th percentile). The energy expenditure and intake were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test [24]. The energy expenditure, energy intake, energy balance (energy intake − energy expenditure) and PAL between training day and off day were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test [24]. Differences in energy expenditure, energy intake, energy balance, and PAL among soccer, long-distance running, and road cycling were examined using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. When the Kruskal-Wallis H test showed a significant difference, the post hoc Mann-Whitney test with Bonferroni correction was used to compare the soccer, long-distance running, and road cycling [25]. The energy, carbohydrate, protein, and fat intakes were compared between breakfast, lunch, and dinner meals using Friedman’s test. Moreover, the intakes of the food groups were also compared. When a significant difference by Friedman’s test was obtained, the post hoc Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Bonferroni correction was used to compare the breakfast, lunch, and dinner meals [26]. All statistical analysis were conducted using SPSS version 29 (IBM Japan, Tokyo, Japan). Statistical significance was set at a p value of < 0.05 for all tests.

RESULTS

Participants’ Characteristics

Table 2 shows the age, height, weight, BMI, and body fat percentage. Eight participants did not respond to the body fat percentage. On training day, the median times of training were 2.0 (2.0-2.8), 2.8 (2.4-4.0), 4.3 (3.0-7.0), and 2.8 (2.0-3.8) h for soccer, long-distance running, road cycling, and all sports, respectively. The median of the rest days during the week were 1.0 (1.0-1.5), 1.0 (0.9-1.6), 1.0 (1.0-2.5), and 1.0 (1.0-2.0) days for soccer, long-distance running, road cycling, and all sports (data not shown in table), respectively.

Energy expenditure, intake, balance and PAL per day

Table 3 shows the energy expenditure, intake, balance and PAL. These results represent estimated 1-day values that reflect the unique periodization and training content of each sport. On training day, the energy expenditure was significantly higher than the energy intake for soccer (p < 0.001), long-distance running (p = 0.043), and road cycling (p < 0.001). Energy balance was negative on training day (-948 to -367 kcal) and positive on rest day (129-232 kcal) for each sport. In all three sports, energy expenditure was significantly higher on training day than on rest day (p < 0.001 for each sport), and energy balance was significantly negative (p < 0.001, p = 0.003, and p < 0.001 for soccer, long-distance running, and road cycling, respectively). On training day, energy balance was significantly higher in road cycling than in soccer (p = 0.037) and long-distance running (p = 0.014). On training day, the PAL was significantly larger for long-distance running than for soccer (p = 0.004), and road cycling was significantly larger than for soccer (p < 0.001) and long-distance running (p = 0.001).

Intake status of daily macronutrients (carbohydrates, protein, and fat)

Table 4 shows the daily carbohydrate, protein, and fat intakes. On training day, the carbohydrate and protein intakes were 5.0 (4.3-6.2), 6.4 (5.2-7.1), and 8.3 (6.6-10.0) g/kg of body weight and 1.9 (1.6-2.3), 2.1 (1.7-2.3), and 2.4 (1.9-2.7) g/kg of body weight in soccer players, long-distance runners, and road cyclists, respectively. The proportion of fat to total energy intake ranged from 26.7% to 32.2%.

Status of energy intake and carbohydrate, protein, and fat intakes by meal timing

Table 5 shows the energy, carbohydrate, protein, and fat intakes by meal timing. On training day, carbohydrate intake was significantly lower at breakfast and lunch than at dinner for soccer (vs. breakfast (p < 0.001) and vs. lunch (p = 0.014)) and long-distance running (vs. breakfast (p = 0.003) and vs. lunch (p = 0.011)). Moreover, for soccer, breakfast intake was significantly lower than lunch intake (p = 0.008). No significant differences were observed for the road cycling group. Protein intake in soccer was significantly lower at breakfast than at lunch and dinner (vs. lunch (p = 0.016) and vs. dinner (p < 0.001)) and lower at lunch than at dinner (p < 0.001), whereas fat intake was significantly lower at breakfast and lunch than at dinner (vs. breakfast (p < 0.001) and vs. lunch (p = 0.008)). No significant differences in protein and fat intake were observed in long-distance running or road cycling.

Status of the intake of the food groups by meal timing

Cereal intake was significantly lower at breakfast than at lunch (soccer (p = 0.001) and long-distance running (p = 0.019)) and dinner (soccer (p < 0.001) and long-distance running (p = 0.001)) for soccer and long-distance running, whereas no significant differences were observed in road cycling. However, a trend toward lower intake at lunch than at breakfast (p = 0.046) and dinner (p = 0.015) was observed. Fruit intake was significantly higher at breakfast than at lunch across all sports (p = 0.002-0.012); it was also higher at breakfast than at dinner for soccer (p = 0.008) and long-distance running (p = 0.025). For soccer, the intake of legumes and fish and shellfish was significantly lower at breakfast and lunch than at dinner (p < 0.001-0.015). Meat intake was lower at breakfast than at dinner across all sports (p < 0.001-0.003), while for soccer and long-distance running, it was also lower at breakfast than at lunch (p = 0.001).

DISCUSSION

The present study is the first to assess the energy expenditure, intake, and balance, as well as carbohydrate, protein, and fat intakes, of Japanese soccer players, long-distance runners, and road cyclists who belonged to multiple teams. It was observed that energy balance on training day was negative and that carbohydrate intake tended to be lower in Japanese soccer players, long-distance runners, and road cycling athletes.

The doubly labeled water (DLW) method has been considered a reliable technique for assessing energy expenditure and used to measure energy expenditure in athletes [6,7,27]. However, in the present study, the implementation of the DLW method was not feasible due to the large number of participants (n = 90) and the activity area being widely distributed. The METs method is used to estimate the energy expenditure during exercise in athletes, with previous studies calculating energy consumption using this method for female elite athletes during training [28], soccer referees during matches [29], and triathletes during spin bike training [30]. However, due to the lack of studies calculating the energy expenditure during nonexercise periods in athletes using the METs method, and since previous research has investigated the athletes’ PAL outside of exercise [23], the energy expenditure in the present study was estimated using the METs method during exercise and PAL during the nonexercise periods. The PAL was uniformly set as 1.5 for nonexercise hours. However, individual differences in nonexercise activity thermogenesis—including those associated with commuting and occupational physical activity—may influence total energy expenditure. Therefore, the reported negative energy balance should be interpreted as a “minimum estimate,” and the actual negative energy balance may be even greater.

On training day, the energy expenditure was significantly higher than the energy intake in soccer players, long-distance runners, and road cyclists, and the energy balance was negative. On rest day, a significant difference was observed between the energy expenditure and energy intake in the soccer players only, but the energy balance was not negative in the soccer players, long-distance runners, and road cyclists. Among the soccer players, the energy balance on training day was −767 (−1,079-98) kcal/day. A previous study reported no significant difference between the energy expenditure and intake during the study period, including match and training days, in Korean professional soccer players. However, their participants ate their meals at the team’s cafeteria [27], whereas our study participants were free to eat their meals anywhere, indicating that the dietary intake situation was different between these two studies. A previous study involving professional soccer players in the Dutch Premier League has reported that the energy balance during the study period, including match, training, and rest days, was 18% negative [7]. Moreover, in a previous study involving Japanese professional soccer players, a significant difference in energy expenditure and intake during the study period, including match days and training days, was observed, and the energy balance was −430 ± 287 kcal/day [6]. In the present study involving employed and college soccer players, the energy balance was negative, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies. In long-distance running, the energy balance on training day was −367 (−907-169) kcal/day. Previous studies have reported that the energy balance on training days was −730 ± 777 kcal/day [3] in Japanese college middle and long-distance runners and −1,444 ± 396 kcal/day [8] in Japanese college long-distance runners. In the present study, which included college and employed runners as participants, the energy balance was negative, which is consistent with the findings in previous studies. In road cycling, the energy balance on training day was −948 (−2,577 to 329) kcal/day, which is the highest negative difference obtained when comparing the three sporting events. In a previous study, the energy balance of professional cyclists during camp training was −1,720 kcal [9], which is similar to our negative energy balance result. To highlight the characteristics of each sport and provide useful data for nutritional support, we compared energy expenditure, intake, and balance, and PAL across different sports. On training day, road cycling had the highest PAL among the three sports and the most negative energy balance, suggesting that this sport, characterized by high activity levels, is particularly prone to insufficient energy intake relative to energy expenditure compared with the other sports examined in this study. Although the energy balance was not negative on the rest day in each sport, the median of the rest days per week were 1.0 (1.0-1.5), 1.0 (0.9-1.6), and 1.0 (1.0-2.5) days for soccer, long-distance running, and road cycling, respectively. However, in our study period, there were more training days than rest days per week. These data suggest that our study participants may have had more days in a week with a negative energy balance than with a positive energy balance. Although the observation period was short, as in previous studies that reported a negative energy balance within a single team or under uniform training sessions [3,6-10], the present study, which included multiple teams observed during the same period but under different training programs, also showed comparable results across all sports. Regarding the assessment of energy intake in athletes, previous reviews comparing energy expenditure measured by the DLW method with self-reported dietary surveys have reported that energy intake was underreported by 0.4%-35.9% relative to energy expenditure [31]. However, a chronic negative energy balance in athletes can lower metabolism, which may prevent weight loss even under a negative energy balance [32]. Previous studies have reported that, despite a negative energy balance, changes in body weight were less than expected, attributed not only to underreporting of food intake but also to the effects of metabolic changes [8,9]. The observation period in the present study was only two days, and changes in body weight were not assessed. Since the participants did not intend to lose weight, they were unlikely to have deliberately consumed a diet resulting in a negative energy balance. Thus, although the possibility of the underestimation of energy intake from the dietary survey cannot be excluded, it was suggested that the participants may have had a negative energy balance. In a previous study, it has been suggested that in a negative energy balance, it is possible to use more energy to improve performance, and that by not lowering the basal metabolism, the athlete’s condition can be improved [33,34]. Thus, it is suggested that the study participants need to improve their negative energy balance by increasing their energy intake.

The median training times were 2.0 (2.0-2.8), 2.8 (2.4-4.0), 4.3 (3.0-7.0) h for soccer, long-distance running, and road cycling, respectively. The carbohydrate intakes on training day were 5.0 (4.3-6.2) and 6.4 (5.2-7.1) g/kg of body weight for soccer and long-distance running, respectively. Using the carbohydrate intake requirement of 6-10 g/kg of body weight, which is recommended for 1-3 h of moderate-to-high-intensity training as reference [2], the carbohydrate intake value obtained for soccer was lower and that for long-distance running was within the recommended range. However, considering the training time, it is suggested that the recommended amount for athletes was not fully met. Moreover, the carbohydrate intake on training day for road cycling was 8.3 (6.6-10.0) g/kg of body weight, which was within the range of the 8-12 g/kg of body weight recommended for 4-5 h of moderate-to-high-intensity training [2]. However, considering that the median training time in the present study was 4.3 (3.0-7.0) h, it was suggested that the intake tended to be lower than the recommended amount. The protein intake on training day were 1.9 (1.6-2.3), 2.1 (1.7-2.3), and 2.4 (1.9-2.7) g/kg of body weight for soccer, long-distance running, and road cycling, whereas the recommended amount for soccer was 1.2-2.0 g/kg of body weight [2,12,13]. For long-distance runners and road cyclists, the values were above the recommended amounts. The fat percentages of energy on training day were 29.3% (26.1%-34.0%), 32.2% (28.0%-38.3%), and 26.7% (20.3%-31.2%) for soccer, long-distance running, and road cycling, which were higher than that recommended for long-distance running at 20%-30% [2,11]. However, for soccer and road cycling, this was within the recommended range but was slightly closer to the upper limit of the recommended amount for soccer. Previous studies involving soccer players [7,14] and road cyclists [9,15] have also reported that carbohydrate intake on training days was insufficient compared with the recommended amount for these athletes, but their protein intake was sufficient or excessive. Moreover, in cyclists, the higher their exercise intensity, the more insufficient their carbohydrate intake [35]. The insufficient carbohydrate intake may be compensated for by the intake of more amounts of protein [7,14]. The present study also showed similar results, with the carbohydrate intake in each sport being insufficient or tending to be insufficient compared with the recommended amount for athletes, and the protein intake being sufficient or excessive. Carbohydrates as an energy source are important for achieving optimal athletic performance [2]. When the energy balance is negative due to the inadequate intake of carbohydrates and fat, even if a sufficient amount of protein is consumed, it will be used for energy rather than for maintaining muscle and body structure and composition [36]. Thus, a sufficient carbohydrate intake is important for performance and body composition, and it is suggested that the participants in the present study may need to improve their carbohydrate intake on training days.

On training day, for soccer and long-distance training, the carbohydrate intake at breakfast and lunch were significantly lower than those at dinner. For soccer, the breakfast carbohydrate intake was significantly lower than the lunch intake. This suggests that increasing the carbohydrate intake at breakfast and lunch is necessary to increase the energy intake. The cereal intake was significantly lower at breakfast than at lunch or dinner in the present study. This indicates that nutritional support, such as increasing the intake of cereals like rice at breakfast, may need to be planned and implemented. Although no significant differences were observed between lunch and dinner for cereals, potatoes, and fruits, this suggests that nutritional support measures, such as reviewing and increasing the intake of each food group at lunch and dinner, are necessary to ensure adequate carbohydrate intake throughout the day. Previous studies have reported that the protein intake in professional soccer players was skewed, with the lowest intake at breakfast [7], a pattern that is also observed in the present study. Since protein is recommended to be consumed in equal amounts across meals throughout the day [2,12,13], these results suggest a need to improve the skewed protein distribution in the soccer players studied. On training days, since the intake of protein-rich foods such as legumes, fish and shellfish, and meat tended to be low at breakfast, increasing their consumption at breakfast and adjusting the intake throughout the day may help reduce this skewness. For road cycling, no significant differences were observed in the carbohydrate, protein, or fat intakes among meals on training days. However, given that their training duration was longer than that of the other sports in the present study and that the daily carbohydrate intake tended to be lower than the recommended amount, the findings suggest a need to increase the daily carbohydrate intake. Furthermore, as cereal intake on training days tended to be lower at lunch compared with breakfast and dinner. Because road cycling practice sessions are lengthy, athletes may not have sufficient time for lunch. Therefore, nutritional support plans could be developed and implemented not only for lunch but also for snacks, such as by increasing carbohydrate intake through additional cereals.

The present study had three major limitations. First, since our study participants were limited in terms of their competency levels, the levels were not representative of the entire athletic population in each sport. Thus, future studies should include participants with different competency levels. Second, our survey period was limited to one training day and one rest day. This was due to the consideration of the burden on the participants, and the survey time for the training day was set as the day when the participants were doing standard training routines determined by each team and participant. However, in the future, it will be necessary to increase the survey period. Finally, the energy expenditure was estimated using the METs method during exercise and PAL during the nonexercise periods. Since it cannot be denied that this approach is not as accurate as the DLW method [6,7,27], it may have some errors. The energy expenditure was particularly high for the road cyclists, as they have longer training periods, with some cyclists spending up to 10 h on the road. Although it is assumed that this would result in high energy expenditure, the error may be correspondingly larger. Based on these findings, generalization is limited. However, this study provides real-world data on the nutritional status of Japanese endurance athletes across multiple teams without activity restrictions, underscoring the need for further investigation and improvement in their energy balance.

In conclusion, our study data showed that the energy balance between energy expenditure and intake on training day may have been negative. In each sport studied, the carbohydrate intake on training day was lower than the recommended amount for the Japanese soccer players, long-distance runners, and road cyclists who belonged to multiple teams. Furthermore, these findings provide a useful reference for considering nutritional support plans aimed at improving athletic performance.
