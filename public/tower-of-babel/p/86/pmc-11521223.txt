Dear Laparoscopic Surgeons: Caution with the Use of Glutaraldehyde!!!
Authors: Shivani Phugat, Prativa Choudhury, Vishesh Jain, Anjan Kumar Dhua, Devendra Kumar Yadav, Hemanga Kumar Bhattacharjee, Sachit Anand, Harpreet Singh, Sandeep Agarwala, Prabudh Goel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:The occupational hazards of glutaraldehyde are well known; the possibility of harm to the patients has been highlighted in the form of isolated reports only.Objective:To synthesize evidence for contact mucosal injury or injury due to intraperitoneal instillation of glutaraldehyde following its use during laparoendoscopy.Materials and Methods:The current review is Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) compliant. PubMed, PubMed Central, and Google Scholar were interrogated for animal and human studies upon the harmful effects of glutaraldehyde during laparoscopy and proctosigmoido-colonoscopy.Results:Thirty-five studies substratified into animal experiments (n = 2), glutaraldehyde-induced colitis (G-iC) postendoscopy (n = 30), and laparoscopy (n = 3) were included. Rats suffered mucosal injury following colonic injection of glutaraldehyde which was time- and concentration-dependent quantum and developed bloody diarrhoea. Omental and renal injury was observed due to glutaraldehyde instillation during simulation of intra-peritoneal insufflation in rats; the serum leucocytes, CRP and creatinine were also elevated. G-iC following colonoscopy was related to contact mucosal injury due to failure (human or machinery) to rinse the chemical off the instrument surface or as a case of mistaken identity (glutaraldehyde was mistaken for saline or another reagent). The incubation period was <24 h in 90% of patients; clinical presentation was related to the initiation of intense inflammation. Intraperitoneal instillation during laparoscopy also leads to intense inflammation. One patient was asymptomatic, the second required laparotomy and peritoneal irrigation for 48 h, while the third case underwent multiple laparotomies for enteric cutaneous and enteroenteric fistulae, adhesions, and multiple intra-abdominal abscesses. Hydronephrosis consequent to retroperitoneal fibrosis has been reported.Conclusions:The use of glutaraldehyde in the patient care facilities should be discontinued; when unavoidable, the standard processes of disinfection and rinsing must be stringently followed.

INTRODUCTION

Glutaraldehyde [(CH2)3 (CHO)2] has broad-spectrum bactericidal, tuberculocidal, fungicidal, virucidal, and sporicidal properties in a buffered alkaline solution (pH: 7.5–8.5). It can even fix bloodstains.[1] It is a cold-sterilant and noncorrosive to metals or instruments with lenses (scopes), rubbers (gas tubing), and plastics. Within the health-care facilities, glutaraldehyde is used in view of low cost and for high turnover between cases.

The occupational hazards of glutaraldehyde are well known.[234] Glutaraldehyde exposure and consequent adverse effects have been reported through isolated reports in situations such as (i) it has been mistaken for saline or (ii) when glutaraldehyde used for disinfection was incompletely rinsed off the surface of laparoendoscopes, and (iii) if glutaraldehyde gains access into the peritoneal cavity during laparoscopy. However, the potential problems with the use of glutaraldehyde have not been addressed optimally.

The current review attempted to generate a comprehensive source of information on the health hazards with the use of glutaraldehyde for cleaning instruments related to laparoscopy. The clinical spectrum of adversities, the possible complications, and their management with outcomes were studied systematically.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The review is compliant with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.[5] Search, data extraction, and analysis were conducted in duplicate. Discrepancy was resolved by consensus and review of the original source in the presence of the senior author. The search strategy and the results were outlined in the PRISMA flow diagram [Figure 1].

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flow diagram

Prior reviews were excluded through searches across PubMed, Cochrane, and Google. PubMed, PubMed Central, and Google Scholar were interrogated for primary research on the subject. Missing publications were identified through snowballing and reverse snowballing. Published abstracts and proceedings of conferences were also searched for, subject to open access availability online.

Clinical (restricted to laparoscopy or procto-sigmoido-colonoscopies) and experimental studies reporting on the results of glutaraldehyde contact with body tissues were included. No restrictions pertaining to human or animal-based studies, year or language of publication, or study design were imposed. Google translator was used for the manuscripts not published in English.[6]

Data extraction

The data were extracted into Excel sheets (Microsoft Excel for Mac, version 16.9.1) and were collated.

RESULTS

Animal studies

Two experimental studies in Sprague–Dawley rats[8] were identified [Table 1].[17] Intracolonic injection of glutaraldehyde resulted in bloody diarrhea after 24 h. Characteristic features included mucosal injury, hemorrhage in the lamina propria, epithelial necrosis, dilatation and neutrophilic infiltration inside the intestinal crypts and villous atrophy. The extent of injury was dependent upon glutaraldehyde concentration. Six animals died of the ensuing pathology; hydrothorax and massively congested lungs were observed in three animals.

The gas pipe was sterilized with glutaraldehyde during laparoscopic simulation; elevated serum levels of white blood cells, C-reactive protein (CRP), and creatinine were observed. Histopathological injury to the kidney (tubules, glomeruli, tubulointerstitial area, and endothelial cells) and the omentum was documented.[19]

Glutaraldehyde injury during procto-sigmoido-colonoscopy

The searches identified 30 reports of glutaraldehyde-induced colitis (G-iC) (n = 160 patients) [Table 2];[71011121314151617181920212223242526272829303132333435363738] the earliest report dated back to 1986.[10] Dolcé et al.[11] reported an outbreak of hemorrhagic proctocolitis (n = 6) and identified additional 37 cases in a cohort of 450. The incidence of G-iC varied from 0.1% (n = 2400)[12] to 12.4% (n = 299).[11] The male to female ratio was 1:1.34 (M:F = 32:43). The males were older to the females (males: mean ± standard deviation [SD]: 61.1 ± 9.69 years, n = 16; females: mean ± SD: 53.2 ± 15.09 years, n = 25). It was observed that 87.8% of the patients were >40 years and 3/4th (75.6%) of the patients were >50 years of age. Within the study cohort, 32% (8 of 25) of females were <50 years, while only 6.25% (1 of 16) of males were <50 years of age.

Peripheral blood picture (n = 38 patients) was suggestive of inflammation (neutrophilic leukocytosis, elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate and CRP, and anemia).[1819] Follow-up colonoscopy (41 patients in 24 studies) demonstrated features of acute colitis with edema, inflammation, bleeding and membrane-covered mucosa, inflammatory exudates, colonic ulcerations as large as 12-25 cm,[2021] mucosal friability or patchy large-bowel involvement. Mucosal necrosis,[1921222324] multiple pseudo-angiomatous superficial hemorrhagic petechial pan-colonic lesions[16] and spasm of isolated colonic segments[21] have also been reported.

Computed tomography (CT) imaging demonstrated the “target-sign” appearance,[122526] essentially characterised by irregular or heterogeneous, circumference, colonic wall thickening with a predilection for the left colon and pericolonic inflammatory fat stranding.[182229]

The histopathological picture (31 patients in 17 studies) was suggestive of acute ischemic or pseudomembranous colitis.[2232] The “white plaques” depicted extracellular vacuoles in the lamina propria; the vacuoles were widely variable in size with a tendency to coalesce.[32] Regenerative epithelium could be visualized in the crypt bases with cell activity.[14]

The colitis was self-limiting with single reported casualty[25] due to fulminant pseudomonas sepsis in a patient with chronic lymphatic leukemia.

Contact mucosal injury due to glutaraldehyde retention inside the channels or on the surface of the endoscope[7121314151618202123283233343536] was attributed to inadequate rinsing of the scopes after glutaraldehyde treatment or failure to dry.[1421] Nearly 6–12 ml of the chemical may be retained inside the channels of the endoscope.[142427] Glutaraldehyde was also identified on the upper surface of the endoscope holding tray (3–4 ml),[14] inside the drain opening or tubing of the scope disinfecting machine[14] and in the tube connecting the water bottle to the endoscope.[21] Residual glutaraldehyde inside the endoscope channels has been instilled intra-rectally during the procedure;[2131] glutaraldehyde has also been inadvertently used to flush the air-water suction channel[12] and for coloclysis.[17] Recent change of technician,[71214] change in concentration of the chemical,[21] frequent change of disinfectant,[13] and infrequent change of rinse water[1135] were also implicated. The concentration of glutaraldehyde in the rinse water may reach up to 6%–9% after 5 cycles.[1430] A faulty irrigation filter[14] and other machinery-related faults[343537] have been described. The chemical may also be retained within the fissures and crevices of the scope due to use-related wear and tear.[28]

Glutaraldehyde injury during laparoscopy

Inadvertent intraperitoneal instillation of glutaraldehyde during laparoscopy was reported in three studies [Table 4].[394041] Of these, two boys aged 2.5 and 3 years, respectively, were operated for cryptorchidism. The third patient was a 36-year-old female who suffered the event during bilateral tubal ligation procedure.

The source of glutaraldehyde was the insufflation tubing (n = 2) and retained chemical due to inadequate drying of the instrument. Clinical presentation and course of the disease were highly variable between the three cases. An asymptomatic, localized serosal lesion behind the right fallopian tube following tubal ligation was detected during review of the operative video.[40] The other two patients developed symptoms[3941] within 24 h of laparoscopy. Peculiar “khaki” discoloration and edematous thickening of the bowel alternating with thin and hyperemic segments were described.[39] Brown patches (devitalized burn eschar) over the visceral peritoneum were also described.[41] The patient required multiple laparotomies for enterocutaneous fistulae, extensive adhesions, and intra-abdominal abscesses. Eventually, the muscular layers were replaced by fibrous tissue and the intestinal was transformed into a mucosa-lined fibrous tube. Bilateral hydronephrosis following retroperitoneal fibrosis was also reported. The amount of glutaraldehyde was not ascertained in either study.

DISCUSSION

A contact time of 20 min (2.4% glutaraldehyde; alkalinized pH: 7.5–8.5) with glutaraldehyde (≥20°C) can afford “high-level” disinfection only. This is acceptable for bronchoscopes and colonoscopes which do not breach the tissue integrity.[42] Sterilization requires a higher concentration (3.4%) and a contact time of 10 h.[43] Flow of the chemical within the channels of the instrument is mandatory.[44]

The active component within glutaraldehyde (1,5-pentanedial) reacts with the cell surfaces and diffuses intracellularly, leading to an intense inflammatory cascade and disrupts the cell function.[12345674546] The mucosal cells of the intestine synthesize a variety of chemicals such as the arachidonic acid metabolites and cytokines, growth factors, and nitric oxide, which will lead to healing, restoration of anatomy, and functional recovery.[47]

The extent of glutaraldehyde-induced injury is time and concentration dependent. Direct contact of 2% glutaraldehyde with colonic mucosa results in crypt epithelial necrosis and neutrophilic infiltration. Lesser concentrations are less damaging. Systematic absorption of glutaraldehyde from the colon into the vascular and lymphatic circulation can lead to hydrothorax and pulmonary congestion.[4849] The omentum and the superficial bowel loops are usually the first point of contact for the glutaraldehyde retained inside the gas tubing.[1] The liver and kidneys are vulnerable due to their role in elimination of the toxin.

G-iC postcolonoscopy closely mimics pseudomembranous colitis. While the median incubation of Clostridium difficile infection is 3 days, G-iC usually presents within 24 h of colonoscopy.[50] The clinical presentation varies in severity from simple proctocolitis to severe hemorrhagic proctocolitis.[11] The highest volume and concentration of glutaraldehyde instilled into the rectum has been reported as 200 ml and 8%–10% when used for coloclysis inadvertently.[17] The younger patients seem to tolerate glutaraldehyde better and the prevalence of such procedures is lesser in children.

The differentials of G-iC include other causes of ischemic colitis or ischemic enteritis or inflammatory bowel diseases including pseudomembranous colitis, vascular disorders, intramural hemorrhage, and radiation injury. For postcolonoscopy problems, it is recommended to first perform an abdominal radiograph to rule out intestinal perforation. CT should be the next imaging modality.[25] CT may be delayed in cases with milder presentation to allow time for iatrogenic changes to settle.[16]

The mechanisms responsible for G-iC described in all the reports can broadly be classified into two categories: (a) failure (human or machinery) to rinse the glutaraldehyde off the instruments post-disinfection and (b) case of mistaken identity wherein glutaraldehyde was identified as normal saline or any another reagent. The gas-insufflation tubing has a large dead space and can retain a significant volume of glutaraldehyde. There is a nozzle with a small bore attached to one end of the tube. The chances of error are higher considering the time and effort required to rinse the tube thrice. Moreover, the gas insufflation is started after insertion of the first port and before insertion of the camera. Consequently, the event is not likely to be documented or captured in the recording.[3941] The retained chemical will form an aerosol with the pressurized carbon dioxide and the volume of the chemical delivered into the abdomen will govern the pattern and extent of clinical damage.[51]

There is no antidote for glutaraldehyde. Furthermore, there are no specific guidelines for the management of such cases. However, the uniform success of conservative management across the study cohort and the self-limited clinical course support the concept of expectant management in such situations. Anecdotal use of specific drugs such as steroids, mesalamine, and loperamide has been reported; their use may be customized to the requirements of the specific patient.

Glutaraldehyde is an avoidable complication. Unless the machinery is at fault, the error is likely to be human (iatrogenic). The process is usually executed by the technician. The concentration of glutaraldehyde should be carefully calculated before clinical use.[38] A regular inspection of the machinery as per the manufacturer’s instructions is indispensable. Following disinfection, all instruments should be rinsed thoroughly with potable or sterile water. The inbuilt channels within the endoscopes should also be flushed adequately. Each rinse should be at least 1 min long.[52] Large volumes of water have to be used subject to a minimum of two gallons (one gallon = 3.78 l). The procedure should be repeated twice for a total of three rinses.[53] Best practices for safe use of glutaraldehyde to prevent or minimize occupational hazards have been outlined by the www.osha.gov.[53] The residual fluid within the channels may be removed with the use of suction or insufflation of filtered air.[43] Alcohol is not currently recommended for drying in view of its fixative properties.

CONCLUSIONS

Although glutaraldehyde has broad-spectrum antimicrobial including bactericidal, tuberculocidal, fungicidal, virucidal, and sporicidal properties, the best it offers following a 20-min contact in-between cases is “high-level disinfection.”

It is known to damage the body tissues; depending on its concentration and time of contact, the injury may be self-limited to grievous. Considering its limitations, occupational hazards, and the potential of harm to the patient, the use of glutaraldehyde in the patient care facilities should be discontinued.
