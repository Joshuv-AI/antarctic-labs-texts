Anterior quadratus lumborum block at the lateral supra-arcuate ligament versus thoracic paravertebral block for postoperative analgesia after laparoscopic nephrectomy: a randomized non-inferiority trial
Authors: Chun-Ying Zheng, Shu-Qin He, Peng Ye, Xuan Pan, Han-Liang Fan, Xing Zhang, Xiao-Chun Zheng, Ting Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study compared the analgesic effects of the anterior quadratus lumborum block at the lateral supra-arcuate ligament (QLB-LSAL) and thoracic paravertebral block (TPVB) for laparoscopic nephrectomy to verify whether the new quadratus lumborum block is an efficient technique for managing laparoscopic nephrectomy pain as an alternative to TPVB.MethodsThis study was a prospective randomized noninferiority clinical trial. Before general anaesthesia, 80 participating patients were randomly allocated to receive QLB-LSAL (20 mL of 0.5% ropivacaine) or TPVB (20 mL of 0.5% ropivacaine). The primary outcome was the mean visual analogue scale (VAS) score in the first 24-hafter surgery. The secondary outcomes included the cutaneous sensory block area, intraoperative haemodynamic changes, consumption of sufentanil at 24-h postoperatively, and postoperative recovery data.ResultsThe mean VAS score during the first 24-h was 1.92 ± 0.28 in the QLB-LSAL group and 1.79 ± 0.25 in the TPVB group. The mean treatment difference (QLB-LSAL–TPVB) in the primary outcome was 0.13 (95% CI 0.02 to 0.25). The upper limit of the 95% CI remained below the non-inferiority margin. After 10 and 30 min, the TPVB group had significantly greater cutaneous sensory block area (p < 0.001). The consumption of sufentanil at 24-h postoperatively was similar between the two groups (p > 0.05). Neither intraoperative hemodynamic alterations nor postoperative recovery metrics showed significant differences between the two groups (p > 0.05).ConclusionsAt 24-h postoperatively, QLB-LSAL had analgesic effects that were not inferior to those of TPVB for patients undergoing laparoscopic nephrectomy.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry website; registration number: ChiCTR2100048226.

Introduction

Laparoscopic nephrectomy (LN) is the predominant surgical approach for renal tumours but often leads to moderate to severe postoperative pain [1,2]. Sole reliance on opioids for pain relief can prolong recovery due to adverse effects like paralytic ileus, nausea, vomiting, and itching [3]. Recently, pain management has shifted from solely using opioids to incorporating multimodal analgesia, among which ultrasound-guided regional nerve blocks play a significant role in this approach [4,5].

The thoracic paravertebral block (TPVB) has gained popularity as a multimodal analgesic management method, providing effective pain relief for patients undergoing LN [6]. However, TPVB has been associated with several clinically significant complications, including: inadvertent vascular puncture (6.8%), hypotension (4.0%), haematoma formation (2.4%), and signs of epidural or intrathecal spread (1.0%) [7]. As a result, clinicians are investigating and implementing alternative pain management approaches for LN perioperative care.

Recently, Li H et al. [3] reported the anterior quadratus lumborum block approach at the lateral supra-arcuate ligament (QLB-LSAL). This technique involves injecting the local anaesthetic (LA) into the apposition zone of the diaphragm with the quadratus lumborum (QL) under parasagittal ultrasound guidance to circumvent the arcuate ligament barrier. The LA may spread more easily in the lower thoracic paravertebral space (TPVS) than in the traditional quadratus lumborum block, allowing the new approach to provide wider visceral and somatic coverage. The range of dermatomal sensory blockade with this approach was T6–T7 to L1–L2.

QLB-LSAL and TPVB are both effective analgesic methods for LN [3,8]. If QLB-LSAL demonstrates an analgesic effect equivalent to that of TPVB for LN and is simpler to administer, it will be more suitable for LN. To our knowledge, comparative studies on the analgesic effects of QLB-LSAL and TPVB are currently lacking. Therefore, we hypothesise that QLB-LSAL provides analgesic effects comparable to those of TPVB in patients undergoing elective LN. The results of this noninferiority clinical study may help anaesthesiologists better understand the analgesic efficacy and mechanisms of QLB-LSAL, thereby accelerating postoperative recovery.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

Ethics

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Provincial Hospital, Fuzhou, China, on 27 May 2021 (reference number: K2021-05-035)

Clinical trial registration

The study protocol was registered on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry website (registration number: ChiCTR2100048226; registration date: July 5 2021; principal investigator: XC Zheng). The study enrolled the first patient on July 15, 2021. The study was conducted according to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) statement and the Helsinki Declaration.

Study design

The study was a prospective randomized non-inferiority clinical trial.

Study setting

The study was conducted at a single university teaching hospital from July 2021 to June 2022.

Subjects

Patients diagnosed with renal tumours undergoing LN, with American Society of Anaesthesiologists (ASA) Grades I–II were included in the study. Patients were asked to sign an informed consent statement for the study. Written consent to publish participant images had been obtained. Patients with a BMI > 28 kg/m2, age > 70 years, previous spinal deformity or surgery, LA allergy, prolonged use of opioids, and coagulation disorders, as well as patients who refused to provide informed consent, were excluded from the study.

Study intervention

A computer-generated randomization list (https://www.randomizer.org) assigned eligible patients at a 1:1 ratio to receive therapy on the afflicted side (either QLB-LSAL or TPVB). The anaesthesiologists were blinded to the allocation list, which was recorded in a sealed envelope. Twenty millilitres of 0.5% ropivacaine were provided in each syringe, which was marked with the patient’s randomization number for the block. Both groups of patients received an injection of 20 mL of 0.5% ropivacaine on the surgical side before general anaesthesia induction [9,10]. All blocks were performed by an investigator proficient in regional anaesthesia (Ye P). The investigator did not participate in outcome assessment or further patient management. All other individuals involved in the intraoperative and postoperative care of the patients, including anaesthesiologists, surgeons, and nursing staff, as well as those who assessed the study outcomes, were unaware of the group assignments.

Anaesthesia management and block procedure

All patients received a standardized anaesthetic regimen. In the operating room, intravenous access was established, and monitors were connected.

The patients were placed laterally before the administration of general anaesthesia. QLB-LSAL or TPVB was performed via a 22-gauge puncture needle while strictly adhering to antiseptic protocols. Trained regional anaesthesia specialists performed all procedures with the assistance of US guidance using a curvilinear probe (SonoSite, Inc., WA, USA). Throughout the study observation period, patients remained unaware of their group allocation.

Initially, the transducer was positioned on the L1 transverse process (TP) and on the T12 rib on the affected side to perform the QLB-LSAL block. Then, a sagittal scan was performed after proper sterilization [10]. In a sonogram, the acoustic shadows of the T12 rib, the tip of the L1 TP, and the target compartment between the QL and diaphragm were visualized (Figure 1(A)) [10–12]. The needle was subsequently inserted in alignment with the US beam from the caudal edge of the transducer. The objective was for the needle tip to penetrate the QL and enter the target compartment located between the diaphragm and the QL. The accuracy of needle positioning was confirmed by injecting small aliquots (2–5 mL) of 0.9% saline. To avoid injection into the local vasculature, ropivacaine (20 mL; 0.5%) was gradually administered, ensuring repeated gentle aspiration. A significant downwards shift of the diaphragm during drug injection was observed (Figure 1(B)).

The TPVB was conducted by disinfecting the skin and then placing the transducer in the T10 spinous process. After identifying the TP, the transducer was slightly shifted towards the head to avoid the acoustic shadow of the TP. The paravertebral space and pleura were then distinguished [13–15] (Figure 1(C)). The in-plane long-axis technique was used. The needle was inserted in the plane and advanced from the lateral to the medial direction until the needle tip reached the paravertebral space. The tip of the needle passed through the costotransverse ligament, resulting in a loss of resistance. The needle was then advanced until it reached the target paravertebral space. After the correct needle tip position was confirmed via hydrodissection, 20 mL of 0.5% ropivacaine was injected. A significant downwards shift of the pleura during the injection was observed (Figure 1(D)).

After the block was complete, the patient was immediately returned to the supine position. Ten and thirty minutes after the block, ice was used to measure the patient’s cutaneous sensory block area (CSBA).

Intravenous propofol (2–2.5 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.2 μg/kg), and rocuronium (0.8 mg/kg) were used to induce general anaesthesia. Remifentanil (0.1–0.3 μg/kg/min) and propofol (0.1–0.15 mg/kg/min) were used to maintain general anaesthesia. During surgery, if the mean arterial pressure (MAP) rose over 20% from baseline, sufentanil (5–10 µg) was administered by IV intermittently, or remifentanil infusion was adjusted. Conversely, if the MAP fell over 20% from baseline, interventions included IV fluids, the intravenous administration of norepinephrine to regulate blood pressure, or reduced propofol and remifentanil doses. We administered patient-controlled intravenous analgesia for postoperative pain with a regimen consisting of 1.0 µg/kg sufentanil, 10 mg tropisetron hydrochloride, and 100 mg flurbiprofen in 100 mL of 0.9% saline, delivered at a basal rate of 2.0 mL/hour. A 2 mL bolus of 2 µg of sufentanil was administered if needed, with a lockout interval of 15 min. In patients with a visual analogue scale (VAS) score of at least 4, 50 mg flurbiprofen was injected intravenously.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was the analgesic effect in the first 24-h after surgery, which was assessed via the mean VAS score.

Secondary outcomes

The secondary outcomes included the CSBA, CSBA/body surface area (BSA), intraoperative haemodynamic changes, consumption of sufentanil at 24-h postoperatively, and postoperative recovery data. CSBA, CSBA/BSA and level of sensory block in patients 10 min and 30 min after blockage.

The following abdominal landmarks were important for this process: the anterior midline, posterior midline, vertical line through the anterior superior iliac spine, horizontal line through the umbilicus, costal margin line, horizontal line through the nipples, and horizontal line through the symphysis ossium pubis [16] (Figure 2). A cold stimulus was used at a rate of 2 cm/s 10 and 30 min after blockade to identify sensory changes. On the ventral side, a cold stimulus was applied to the left anterior axillary line, then to the anterior midline, and finally to the right midaxillary line. On the dorsal side, the cold stimulus was applied from the left posterior axillary line to the midline and then to the right midaxillary line. The cold sensation was evaluated on a three-point scale: 0 = loss of cold sensation, 1= decrease in cold sensation compared with sensation in unaffected areas; and 2 = normal sensation [16,17]. The CSBA was identified by marking sensation changes between 2 and 1. A transparent rectangular plastic film was used to map the CSBA surface landmarks. A scanner (CamScanner, CS) was used to transform the transmitted transparency image into transparent digital images, which were saved as JPG files. Photoshop CS (Adobe Systems, San Jose, CA, USA) was then used to calculate the CSBA of the photographs. In each digital image, the area of the targeted region was calculated by dividing the pixel value of the selected region by the fixed pixel value and then multiplying it by the actual area of the image [16].

The patient’s BSA was then calculated according to the BSA formula (BSA (m2) =√height (cm) × weight (kg) ÷ 3600) published by Mosteller in 1987 [18–20]. The CSBA was then divided by the BSA to obtain the percentage of CSBA/BSA. The number and segments of blocked dermatomes were also recorded at 10 and 30 min after QLB-LSAL. However, the measurement of CSBA may be biased due to differences in BSA between patients. Using the CSBA assessment alone, more segments would need to be blocked in patients with a smaller BSA than in those with a larger BSA and vice versa. Correcting the CSBA by adjusting for the BSA can help mitigate this bias and provide a more accurate reflection of the actual blockade region [19,20]. Therefore, in this study, BSA correction was performed based on the CSBA to reduce bias.

Intraoperative haemodynamic changes, including the MAP and heart rate (HR), of patients 5 minutes before and after incision, were measured.

The total 24-hour postoperative sufentanil consumption and VAS scores for patients at 0, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 hours after surgery were recorded.

The postoperative recovery data, including incidences of postoperative nausea and vomiting, the rate of use of rescue analgesics, the time to first ambulation, and the length of hospital stay, were collected.

Anaesthesia-related satisfaction scores were evaluated on a 1- to 5-point Likert scale, with 1 point defined as very unsatisfied, 2 points defined as unsatisfied, 3 points defined as acceptable, 4 points defined as satisfied, and 5 points defined as very satisfied [21].

The quality of recovery was evaluated by the self-assessment 15-item quality of recovery scale (QoR-15) on postoperative days 3 and 5 [22].

2.6. Sample size calculation and statistical analysis

The primary outcome of this noninferiority trial was the mean VAS score in the first postoperative 24-h between the two groups. The study used a statistical power of 80% and a one-sided α of 0.025. To determine the sample size, this study was based on preliminary data from our pilot study (n = 10 per group, total N = 20). The mean ± standard deviation (SD) of the VAS score were 2.0 ± 0.33 and 1.83 ± 0.31 for the QLB-LASL group and the TPVB group, respectively. The noninferiority margin was set to 0.37 (20% of the VAS score in the TPVB group). Considering a 20% dropout rate, 40 patients were needed per group, totalling at least 80 patients. Sample sizes were calculated with PASS software, V.15.0 (Digital Cruncher statistical software).

All the data were analysed via SPSS 26.0 statistical software (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). To test for normality, the Shapiro–Wilk test was used. Continuous variables that are distributed normally are expressed as the mean ± SD, and skewed data are expressed as the median (interquartile range [IQR]). Categorical variables are expressed as numbers (percentages). Continuous variables were compared using t-­test or Wilcoxon rank sum test. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. The primary outcome was evaluated via the noninferiority approach [23]. The hypothesis of noninferiority was tested via a one-sided t-test at a significance level of 2.5%. The two-sided 95% CI, the upper limit of which was equivalent to the upper limit of the 97.5% CI one-sided mean difference in the mean VAS score at the first postoperative 24-h, was determined with respect to the predefined noninferiority limit. For all analyses, a P value of <0.05 was used as the significance threshold.

Finally, 80 patients were randomized and divided into two groups (n = 40 each), and a total of 80 patients were included for subsequent data analysis in this study (n = 40 each) (Figure 3). At 30 min after blockage, the most cephalad dermatomes reached T2 in the two groups, and the most caudal dermatomes reached L3 in the two groups (Figure 5(C,D)). The blocked segment ranged from T8–L1 in all patients in the QLB-LSAL group and from T4–T12 in the TPVB group (Figure 6(B)). The total number of affected dermatomes was greater for patients in the TPVB group (12 [12, 13] vs. 9 [9,10], P < 0.001) (Table 2).

At 10 min after blockage, 72.5% of the CSBA of the patients reached the anterior midline, whereas 65% reached the posterior midline blockade in the QLB-LSAL group. The blocks reached the anterior midline in all patients, whereas it reached the posterior midline in 90% of patients in the TPVB group. An abnormally extensive blockade phenomenon was observed, and the CSBA reached the contralateral dermatome in both groups. Compared with the QLB-LSAL group, the TPVB group had a greater number of patients whose CSBA reached the contralateral dermatome (P < 0.001) (Table 2).

At 30 min after the block, the CSBA reached the anterior midline in 95% of patients, whereas it reached the posterior midline blockade in 80% of patients in the QLB-LSAL group. The blocks reached the anterior midline in all patients, whereas it reached the posterior midline in 95% of the patients in the TPVB group. Compared with the QLB-LSAL group, the TPVB group had a greater number of patients whose CSBA reached the contralateral dermatome (P < 0.001) (Table 2).

In this study, 10 min after the block, the CSBA in the TPVB group was greater than that in the QLB-LSAL group (0.24 ± 0.04 m2 vs. 0.12 ± 0.05 m2, P < 0.001). At 30 min after the block, the CSBA in the TPVB group was greater than that in the QLB-LSAL group (0.34 ± 0.05 m2 vs. 0.19 ± 0.05 m2, P < 0.001) (Table 2).

At 10 min after the block, the CSBA/BSA in the TPVB group was greater than that in the QLB-LSAL group (14.17 ± 0.02% vs. 7.11 ± 0.03%, P< 0.001). At 30 min after the block, the CSBA/BSA in the TPVB group was greater than that in the QLB-LSAL group (20.10 ± 0.01% vs. 11.01 ± 0.03%, P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Intraoperative haemodynamic changes

No significant differences were observed in the MAP or HR between the two groups at either T1 (5 min pre-incision) or T2 (5 min post-incision) (P > 0.05) (Supplementary Materials Table 1).

Postoperative observation indicators

The VAS scores at rest did not significantly differ between the two groups at 0, 2, 4, 8, 12 and 24-h after the operation (P > 0.05) (Table 3). The cumulative sufentanil consumption at 24-h after surgery was similar between the two groups (P > 0.05). The incidence of nausea and vomiting, the rate of rescue analgesia, the time to first ambulation after surgery, or the length of hospital stay also did not significantly differ between the two groups (P> 0.05). The anaesthesia-related satisfaction scores evaluated 48h postoperatively and the QoR-15 scores evaluated 3 and 5 days postoperatively were similar between the two groups (all P > 0.05) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study is the first prospective, randomized, controlled trial to compare perioperative analgesia and recovery between QLB-LSAL and TPVB in LN patients. The 24-hmean VAS score after the LN revealed that the performance of the QLB-LSAL was noninferior to that of the TPVB.

In this study, VAS score were recorded at 0, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24-h after surgery for both groups. Over time, the VAS score in both groups gradually increased, with higher score observed from 12–24-h than from 0–8-h after surgery. The pain was more severe 12-h after surgery, leading to the administration of rescue analgesia with 50 mg flurbiprofen ester for several patients. This phenomenon may be related to the development of rebound pain after the effect of nerve block fades [24]. Neither the consumption of sufentanil at 24-h postoperatively nor the postoperative recovery data significantly differed between groups.

In addition to the VAS score, the CSBA is a commonly used indicator to evaluate the effectiveness of nerve blocks [25,26]. However, several previous studies in CSBA measurement [16,27,28] did not consider the effect of BSA on CSBA. Although blocked segmental dermatomes are the same, patients with a larger BSA also have a larger CSBA and vice versa. In this study, the percentage of CSBA in relation to the total BSA was used to assess the sensory area of the block, correcting for the bias caused by the variability of the BSA [19,20].

In this study, although the CSBA, CSBA/BSA and blocked segments in the TPVB group were greater than those in the QLB-LSAL group, the opioid consumption, postoperative VAS score, and postoperative recovery data (such as the incidence of side effects, the timing of the first ambulation, the duration of hospital stay, anaesthesia-related satisfaction, and quality of recovery scores) did not significantly differ between groups. This finding may be due to the following reasons: (1) The sensory nerves that dominate the skin and renal areas differ. The sensation of skin in the chest wall and abdomen is dominated by the lateral and anterior branches of the intercostal nerves, which originate from the thoracolumbar spinal nerves, whereas the visceral pain of the renal area is dominated by the small branches of the visceral nerve that originate from the preganglionic fibres of the sympathetic ganglia from T9 to T11 [29]. However, the sensory blocked segments and blocked areas measured in this study were based on changes in skin sensation and therefore could not fully represent the extent of blocking visceral pain in the renal area innervated by the small visceral nerve [30]. (2) In the QLB-LSAL group, although sensory blockage of the dermatomes was less pronounced than that in the TPVB group, the concentration of LA required for sympathetic nerve blockage was lower than that required to block skin nerves [31]; therefore, for the same concentration of LA, the number of segments in the QLB-LSAL group that underwent sympathetic nerve blockage should be greater than the number of segments in the skin that underwent sensory blockage, resulting in a blocking effect similar to that of TPVB. In summary, although the QLB-LSAL group had a smaller CSBA and sensory blockage of the dermatomes than the TPVB group, the CSBA achieved was able to meet the analgesic requirements for LN.

As previously mentioned, the associated complications of TPVB limit its use in clinical practice. Hence, the transmuscular quadratus lumborum block (TQLB), an alternative to TPVB, is becoming increasingly popular for perioperative analgesia management in LN [32,33]. Nevertheless, the TQLB still has limitations. First, LN can cause somatic and visceral pain from dermatomes T6 to T12 [34], which requires greater analgesic coverage than the TQLB (T9 to L1) [16] and can result in inadequate pain relief. Furthermore, the TQLB is highly dependent on the imaging quality of the thoracolumbar fascia (TLF) on ultrasound. However, in clinical practice, visualization of the anterior layer of the TLF on ultrasound is often unclear. Finally, LN can disrupt the anterior layer of the TLF, leading to the loss of LA within the lumbar fascia, which affects the effectiveness of the anterior QLB [35].

Unlike for a TQLB, in the case of a QLB-LSAL, the LA can be injected into the diaphragm apposition zone with the QL muscle, and the layers of fascia in ultrasonic images are easily identified. Moreover, the block can bypass the arcuate ligament as a barrier to LA diffusion [10]. With this approach, reliable sensory block coverage can be achieved after 5–10 min.

This study is subject to several limitations. First, patients with a BMI > 28 kg/m2 were excluded from this study. In the future, the technique described above should be evaluated in obese patients. Second, the pilot study results for the sample size calculation in this study are based on unpublished data. Finally, the shorter distance to the TPVS may explain the rapid onset time of QLB-LSAL. More research in cadavers is needed to confirm this hypothesis.

Conclusions

QLB-LSAL offers an analgesic efficacy comparable to that of a TPVB for patients undergoing elective LN. It can deliver precise and extensive CSBA and sensory blockage of dermatomes, satisfying the analgesic needs of the LN.
