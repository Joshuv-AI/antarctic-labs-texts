Cardiac arrhythmia and hypoglycaemia in patients receiving haemodialysis with and without diabetes (the CADDY study): protocol for a Danish multicentre cohort study
Authors: Dea Haagensen Kofod, Søren Zöga Diederichsen, Tobias Bomholt, Mads Ørbæk Andersen, Marianne Rix, Ylian Liem, Kristine Lindhard, Henrik Post Hansen, Casper Rydahl, Morten Lindhardt, Kristine Schandorff, Theis Lange, Kirsten Nørgaard, Thomas Peter Almdal, Jesper Hastrup Svendsen, Bo Feldt-Rasmussen, Mads Hornum

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPatients receiving haemodialysis are at increased risk of arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death, but data on arrhythmia burden and the pathophysiology remain limited. Among potential risk factors, hypoglycaemia is proposed as a possible trigger of lethal arrhythmias. The development of implantable loop recorders (ILR) and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) enables long-term continuous ECG and glycaemic monitoring. The current article presents the protocol of a study aiming to increase the understanding of arrhythmias and risk factors in patients receiving haemodialysis. The findings will provide a detailed exploration of the burden and nature of arrhythmias in these patients including the potential association between hypoglycaemia and arrhythmias.Methods and analysisThe study is an investigator-initiated, prospective, multicentre cohort study recruiting 70 patients receiving haemodialysis: 35 with diabetes and 35 without diabetes. Participants are monitored with ILRs and CGM for 18 months follow-up. Data collection further includes a monthly collection of predialysis blood samples and dialysis parameters. The primary outcome is the presence of clinically significant arrhythmias defined as a composite of bradycardia, ventricular tachycardia, or ventricular fibrillation. Secondary outcomes include the characterisation of clinically significant arrhythmias and other arrhythmias, glycaemic characteristics, and mortality. The data analyses include an assessment of the association between arrhythmias and hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia, baseline clinical variables, and parameters related to kidney failure and the haemodialysis procedure.Ethics and disseminationThe study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of the Capital Region of Denmark (H-20069767). Introduction

Patients with end-stage renal disease receiving haemodialysis (HD) have a high rate of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, especially those with concomitant diabetes.1 2 The mechanisms underlying the high rate of cardiovascular outcomes remain poorly understood. Sudden cardiac death (SCD) accounts for up to 25% of all deaths in the HD population, suggesting that increased arrhythmogenicity constitutes a significant role in the increased mortality.3 4 Increased arrhythmogenicity may also contribute to other frequent cardiovascular events in patients receiving HD, including syncope and stroke. Previous studies in HD populations have reported significant variability in the prevalence and incidence of arrhythmias, and data regarding the burden of specific types of arrhythmias, their pathophysiology, and clinical significance remain limited.5

Multiple cardiovascular risk factors, common in the HD population, may predispose to the development of both atrial and ventricular tachyarrhythmias and bradyarrhythmias, including high age, male sex, low physical activity, hypertension, and diabetes.5–7 A high proportion of the patients have structural heart diseases such as left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy, LV diastolic dysfunction, increasing LV mass index, myocardial fibrosis, and coronary artery disease, which further may contribute to the increased risk of arrhythmias.5 8–10 In addition, several parameters related to kidney failure and the HD procedure itself have been proposed as possible triggers of arrhythmias, particularly fluid, electrolyte, and acid–base imbalances.4 11 12 Furthermore, sympathetic overactivity and autonomic dysregulation, which can be measured indirectly by heart rate variability (HRV), have arrhythmogenic effects and may particularly affect patients with diabetes.13–15 However, the impact of different risk factors on the burden of arrhythmias in patients receiving HD is not yet understood.

An association between hypoglycaemia, ECG changes, and arrhythmias has been reported in patients with type 1 and type 2 diabetes as well as healthy controls. In studies of patients with and without diabetes, experimentally induced hypoglycaemia, applying the hyperinsulinemic hypoglycaemic clamp, caused corrected QT (QTc) interval prolongation and changes in T-wave morphology, suggesting an abnormal cardiac repolarisation during hypoglycaemia which may increase the risk of ventricular arrhythmias and cardiovascular mortality.16–18 A recent study in patients with type 1 diabetes further found that the prolonged QTc interval during insulin-induced hypoglycaemia was sustained during a 60 min recovery period.19 In addition to ECG changes, studies employing 4–5 days of simultaneous Holter and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) in patients with diabetes have reported an association between hypoglycaemia and bradycardia as well as between hypoglycaemia and ventricular tachycardia.20–22 One proposed mechanism is that hypoglycaemia causes a counter-regulatory adrenergic response that contributes to the correction of blood glucose but also may trigger arrhythmias.16 18 The same mechanism may be involved in the high incidence of arrhythmias and cardiovascular mortality in patients receiving HD. Indeed, these patients are at increased risk of hypoglycaemia regardless of diabetes status.23 24 However, major knowledge gaps exist regarding the relationship between hypoglycaemia and arrhythmias, and, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have examined the association between hypoglycaemia and arrhythmias in patients receiving HD with and without diabetes.

In the CADDY study, we employ implantable loop recorders (ILR) and CGM simultaneously for long-term, continuous arrhythmia and glycaemic monitoring in a Danish multicentre study. The current article presents the protocol of the study.

Study objectives

The primary objective is to examine the presence of clinically significant arrhythmias (defined as a composite of bradycardia, ventricular tachycardia, or ventricular fibrillation) in patients receiving HD with and without diabetes. Secondary objectives include (1) characterisation of clinically significant arrhythmias and other arrhythmias in terms of onset, duration, ventricular rate, and burden; (2) description of glycaemic characteristics including time in ranges, glycaemic variability, and hypoglycaemic events; (3) assessment of the association between hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia and arrhythmias; (4) identification of factors associated with arrhythmias, including baseline clinical variables, ECG and echocardiographic measures, HRV, and parameters related to kidney failure and the HD procedure.

Study hypothesis

The primary hypothesis is that monitoring with ILR will lead to a high detection rate of clinically significant arrhythmias. Secondary hypotheses include that (1) monitoring with CGM will detect a high rate of hypoglycaemic events; (2) diabetes and hypoglycaemia are associated with an increased risk of arrhythmias; (3) ILR monitoring will identify predictive factors of arrhythmias such as ECG, echocardiographic and HRV abnormalities, predialysis electrolyte disturbances, dialysis prescription parameters, intradialytic hypotension, and ultrafiltration rate.

Methods and analysis

Overall study design

The CADDY study is an investigator-initiated, prospective, observational, multicentre, cohort study recruiting 70 patients receiving HD: 35 with diabetes and 35 without diabetes. Participants are monitored with ILR and CGM for 18 months follow-up. Online supplemental table S1 shows the overall study schedule.

Participating centres

The study participants are recruited from four Danish dialysis centres: Copenhagen University Hospital—Rigshospitalet; Copenhagen University Hospital—Herlev; Copenhagen University Hospital—Holbaek and Copenhagen University Hospital—North Zealand. Copenhagen University Hospital—North Zealand was included as an additional site during the recruitment period with a protocol amendment due to slow enrolment.

Study population

The study population consists of patients receiving in-centre maintenance HD with or without diabetes. Eligibility criteria are listed in table 1. Patients with diabetes have a diagnosis of type 1 or type 2 diabetes in accordance with the criteria of the WHO and must be in glucose-lowering treatment at inclusion. Initially, only patients with insulin-treated diabetes were recruited because this group were expected to have the highest risk of hypoglycaemia. However, due to slow enrolment, this criterion was changed with a protocol amendment. Patients without diabetes have no known diagnosis of diabetes, no record of previous treatment with glucose-lowering drugs, and a haemoglobin A1c<48 mmol/mol within 3 months prior to inclusion.

Participant enrolment

All patients receiving in-centre HD at the above-mentioned centres are screened according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Eligible patients are provided with oral and written information about the study and invited to participate during their regular outpatient dialysis therapy. Patients are offered a reflection period before consenting, and oral and written consent is obtained from all participants before inclusion in the study.

Baseline evaluation

Patients willing to participate are scheduled for an inclusion visit that takes place at Copenhagen University Hospital—Rigshospitalet. At the inclusion visit, baseline data are obtained from the medical record as well as patient interviews regarding demographic and clinical characteristics, dialysis parameters, most recent predialysis blood samples, and most recent 24-hour urine collection. Furthermore, the inclusion visit involves a baseline cardiovascular evaluation with 12-lead resting ECG, applanation tonometry (SphygmoCor; AtCor Medical, Sydney, NSW, Australia), and transthoracic echocardiography. The transthoracic echocardiography is performed by an echocardiograph technician at Department of Cardiology, Copenhagen University Hospital—Rigshospitalet.

The applanation tonometry examination is used to assess arterial stiffness. It is conducted by the first author (DHK) on all participants and includes pulse wave analysis of the radial artery and pulse wave velocity of the carotid-femoral pulse wave. The pulse wave analysis and pulse wave velocity are non-invasive measures of arterial stiffness and cardiovascular risk and contribute to the overall baseline cardiovascular status of the participants.25 The applanation tonometry method has been described in detail elsewhere.25–27 In short, the pulse wave analysis is based on a recording of the radial pulse waveforms with the generation of the corresponding central pressure waveform by the system software. From this, the aortic augmentation index, time to the foot of the reflected wave, and subendocardial viability ratio can be derived. The aortic augmentation index is adjusted for heart rate. Pulse wave velocity is calculated by the system as the pulse wave travel distance divided by the difference in travel time of the pulse wave between the recording sites at the carotid and femoral arteries, respectively, and the heart. The reproducibility of the pulse wave analysis and pulse wave velocity is assessed based on two repeated scans of 15 participants using the Bland-Altman method.

Cardiac rhythm monitoring

All participants receive an ILR (Reveal LINQ; Medtronic, Minneapolis, USA) at the inclusion visit. The Reveal LINQ is a small (1.2 CC), wireless, cardiac monitor with more than 3 years of battery life. The device is implanted subcutaneously on the left side of the chest in local anaesthesia using standard sterile technique. In accordance with local guidelines, preimplantation INR is measured in patients treated with warfarin, and all patients receive 1000 mg of oral dicloxacillin (or 400 mg of oral moxifloxacin in case of penicillin allergy) prior to implantation. The implantation is performed by the first author (DHK) on all participants.

When implanted, the ILR continuously monitors the heart rhythm to register arrhythmias and record ECGs according to prespecified algorithms. In the CADDY study, the following arrhythmia detection algorithms are applied: bradycardia (≥4 beats at<30 beats/min), pause (>3 s), tachycardia (≥16 beats at >150 beats/min) and atrial fibrillation (AF) (≥2 min). The device’s AF detection is based on the variability in the consecutive RR intervals.28 Initially, the tachycardia detection rate was set at >130 beats/min, but during follow-up changed to >150 beats/min due to an excessive registration of sinus tachycardia that occupied the device’s memory. The device memory can store up to 27 min of automatic ECG recordings. If the device memory is occupied when detecting a new arrhythmia, the oldest stored arrhythmia will be overwritten. However, the longest recorded episode of every arrhythmia type will be preserved. Study participants are also encouraged to use the device’s ‘Patient Assistant’ if they experience symptoms of arrhythmia by which additional ECG information is collected from 6.5 min before activation until 1 min after.

Data from the ILR are transmitted automatically at night-time when the patient is in proximity of their bedside monitor (MyCareLink Patient Monitor; Medtronic, Minneapolis, USA). Hence, the ILR clears the recorded data and restores its memory. In addition, participants are instructed to transmit ILR data manually every week, and, at the request of the investigators, additional manual transmissions are performed to ensure transmission of all data in the ILR’s memory. In the event of death, the chapel in question is requested to send the ILR to the investigators thereby enabling postmortem transmission. The transmitted data are stored in an online, secured database, ‘Medtronic CareLink Network’ (Medtronic, Minneapolis, USA), where data are accessible to the investigators. The data include rhythm strips of any device-detected arrhythmia or symptom annotation, onset and duration of events, heart rate during events, HRV (ms) (using the SDANN method29), daytime and night-time heart rate, and the patient’s physical activity.

Monthly collection of blood samples and dialysis parameters

Predialysis blood samples and dialysis parameters will be collected once monthly during the study. The blood samples are drawn in the dialysis unit directly from the dialysis machine just prior to a regularly scheduled dialysis session and include sodium, potassium, phosphorous, ionised calcium, total carbon dioxide, albumin, creatinine, urea, haemoglobin, leukocytes, platelets, C-reactive protein, haemoglobin A1c, and parathyroid hormone. Dialysis parameters are entered into the medical record by the dialysis nurses and include data on dialysate electrolyte concentrations and temperature, ultrafiltration, blood pressure predialysis and postdialysis, start weight, target dry weight, weekly frequency, and duration of dialysis sessions.

Continuous glucose monitoring

CGM (Dexcom G6; Dexcom, San Diego, USA) will be performed for up to 10 days every second month on all participants during the follow-up. The Dexcom G6 system features a 10-day wear sensor, a transmitter and a receiver. The sensor measures interstitial glucose level every 5 min through a small wire inserted subcutaneously on the back of the upper arm. Interstitial glucose equilibrates within a few minutes with blood glucose, whereby sensor data reflect blood glucose levels. The sensor is applied by the investigators during a regularly scheduled dialysis session.

Glucose readings from the sensor are transmitted automatically from the sensor’s transmitter to the receiver. Participants are instructed to bring the receiver with them at any time during the 10-day period to ensure all glucose readings are transmitted. If the participant is away from the receiver, the transmitter can store up to 3 hours of data which will be automatically transmitted when in proximity of the receiver again. After the 10-day period, data are uploaded from the receiver to a database, where data are stored during follow-up. The receiver will be set to blinded mode, and the glucose readings will be blinded to the participants and the investigators during follow-up.

Participants with diabetes monitored with the Dexcom G6 system prior to inclusion are invited to share their data with the investigators. In that case, CGM data are obtained throughout the study period and data are not blinded to the participant because that participant has been granted a Dexcom G6 for clinical purposes. Participants monitored with a CGM device other than the Dexcom G6 will continue using their usual CGM device without interference from the investigators, and the Dexcom G6 sensor will be applied every second month as for the other participants.

Biobank blood samples

Participants are asked for separate consent to collect blood samples for a biobank for future research. These samples are collected predialysis within the first months from inclusion and are stored at minus 80°C. For each participant two Nunc tubes (size 1 mL) of serum, EDTA plasma and heparin plasma will be stored, respectively.

Follow-up

Participants are followed for 18 months from the inclusion visit; however, follow-up is terminated earlier in case of death, withdrawal, kidney transplantation, dialysis modality change or removal of the ILR, whichever occurs first.

Trial status

Enrolment started in June 2021 and ended in October 2022. Expected end of follow-up is April 2024.

Outcomes

The primary outcome measure is the presence of clinically significant arrhythmias defined as a composite outcome of significant bradycardia (pause>3 s or ≥4 beats at rate<30 beats/min), ventricular tachycardia (sustained: rate>150 beats/min lasting ≥30 s or non-sustained: rate>150 beats/min lasting<30 s) or ventricular fibrillation. All ILR-recorded ECGs are manually reviewed by the first author (DHK) to confirm whether the ILR-detected possible arrhythmic events meet the criteria defined in the outcomes. In case of uncertainty, the ECG is further reviewed by a physician at the Department of Cardiology (SZD or MØA). If a serial of episodes with AF is recorded, a new episode is only registered after >2 hours without reported AF. Registered arrhythmias without an accompanying ECG are discarded. Glycaemic characteristics derived from CGM data are reported based on international consensus guidelines including time in range (sensor glucose 3.9–10.0 mmol/L), time below range (sensor glucose<3.9 mmol/L and sensor glucose<3.0 mmol/L), glycaemic variability, and episodes of hypoglycaemia (sensor glucose<3.9 mmol/L for ≥15 min).30 31 The cause of death will be assessed by a review of all available information in the medical record as well as data from the ILR. SCD is defined as a sudden, natural, unexpected death of cardiac cause; in unwitnessed cases as a person last seen alive and functioning normally within 24 hours before being found dead, and if witnessed, as an acute change in cardiovascular status within 1 hour before death. In non-autopsied deaths, SCD is considered when presumed cardiac cause. In case of autopsy, SCD is considered when unknown or established cardiac cause.32 Cases of SCD are evaluated by the first author (DHK) and a senior cardiologist (JHS).

Statistical considerations

A previous study employing ILRs in patients receiving HD using a similar definition for clinically significant arrhythmias as the CADDY study reported an incidence proportion of significant arrhythmias of 60% during a mean follow-up of 18 months.33 With this, a sample size of 70 patients enables the estimation of the incidence proportion of clinically significant arrhythmias with a precision of +/-11%-points (ie, the width of the 95% CI).

The data will be analysed in accordance with primary and secondary outcomes. All implanted participants will be included in the analyses. A p-value<0.05 will be considered statistically significant. The assessment of the association between hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia and arrhythmias will include analyses of the association between arrhythmias and diabetes; arrhythmias occurring during hypoglycaemia compared with euglycemia and hyperglycaemia and the temporal distribution of arrhythmias in relation to hypoglycaemic events (arrhythmic events 1 hour prior to hypoglycaemia, during hypoglycaemia and 1 hour post-hypoglycaemia). Glycaemic characteristics will be compared between patients with and without diabetes. All available CGM data for each participant will be included in the analyses.31 The assessment of the association between arrhythmias and parameters related to kidney failure and the HD procedure will include analyses of the correlation between arrhythmias and predialysis blood samples, dialysis parameters, decline in systolic blood pressure≥20 mm Hg during a dialysis session, ultrafiltration rate and the temporal distribution of arrhythmias in relation to dialysis sessions. Furthermore, the association between arrhythmias and baseline variables including clinical variables, residual diuresis, ECG markers and echocardiographic measures will be assessed, as well as the association between HRV, daytime and night-time heart rate, physical activity and risk of arrhythmias.

The incidence proportion of AF will be reported for the total study population as well as separately in patients who did not have a history of AF at baseline. Patients with >90% of time in AF will be excluded from analyses concerning time to AF, number of AF events, onset, duration and ventricular rate of AF, including temporal distribution analyses. Patients diagnosed with persistent or permanent AF during the follow-up will be excluded from the above-mentioned analyses from the date of diagnosis.

Baseline data will be compared between patients with and without diabetes applying Fishers’ exact test for categorical data and two sample t-test or Wilcoxon test for continuous data. The association between arrhythmias and risk factors will be examined in multiple regression models adjusted for baseline variables.

Patient and public involvement

A patient panel of four participants is established during the study. The panel will focus on (1) recruitment and inclusion; (2) the overall course of the study and (3) the communication and implementation of study results.

Ethical considerations and dissemination

The CADDY study will contribute with important knowledge regarding the burden of arrhythmias in patients receiving HD and the role of diabetes, hypoglycaemia and other relevant risk factors. To this end, we find the use of invasive monitoring ethically acceptable. All participants receiving an ILR will be monitored with a physician review of ILR data on a weekly basis. Patients with arrhythmias are asked if they experience symptoms, and all arrhythmias detected during the study are treated according to standard clinical practice. This ensures timely intervention for participants, but it also entails the risk of overdiagnosis and overtreatment with the inherent risk of side effects. There is a low risk of procedure-related complications after implantation.34

Glucose data are blinded during follow-up, except for participants granted a GCM for clinical purposes. The participants with diabetes will attend their regular diabetes checkups and changes in treatment will be based on standard clinical practice throughout the follow-up without interference from the investigators. The study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of the Capital Region of Denmark (H-20069767) and the Knowledge Centre on Data Protection of the Capital Region of Denmark. The findings of this study will be presented at national and international congresses as well as in international peer-reviewed scientific journals.

Discussion

This investigator-initiated, multicentre, cohort study will increase our knowledge regarding the arrhythmia burden and the pathophysiology in patients receiving HD with and without diabetes. These patients have a substantially reduced life expectancy, which is partly attributable to the increased risk of arrhythmias and SCD.5 Understanding the mechanisms of the increased arrhythmogenicity is critical to reduce the cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in the HD population.

Study strengths

The CADDY study is unique in combining long-term continuous ECG monitoring with long-term CGM in patients receiving HD both with and without diabetes. The study will provide new insight of several aspects of the increased arrhythmogenicity in this population. First, the study will document in detail the burden of arrhythmias and their characteristics in terms of onset, duration and ventricular rate for a long-term follow-up period of 18 months. Second, it will explore the pathophysiology of arrhythmias including the impact of baseline variables, ECG markers, echocardiographic measures and parameters related to kidney failure and the dialysis procedure. This may help identifying those patients at highest risk of arrhythmias and could provide suggestions for alterations in HD prescription that might reduce the risk of arrhythmias. Third, with up to 90 days CGM data on 70 patients, the study will contribute with important knowledge regarding the burden of hypoglycaemia and the association between hypoglycaemia and arrhythmias, both in patients with and without diabetes. The study will also document other important glycaemic characteristics including time in range and glycaemic variability. This will elucidate the extent of hypoglycaemia and its potential detrimental effects and may provide new guidance to clinicians regarding how to manage diabetes treatment and hypoglycaemia in this population.

Study limitations

The ILR monitoring may have some limitations regarding the detection of arrhythmias. Especially for patients with frequent daily arrhythmic events, the device’s memory constraints may cause arrhythmic events to be overwritten and thereby not transmitted for review. Another limitation of the study is that CGM data are only collected for a part of the follow-up period due to the practical burden associated with CGM use on the participants. This implies that analyses regarding the association between arrhythmias and hypoglycaemia can only be assessed for the period with CGM use. Furthermore, we do not collect postdialysis blood samples and are therefore not able to examine how electrolyte shifts during dialysis may trigger arrhythmias. Finally, the patients who are consenting for participation in this study may be the healthier part of the HD population which may introduce a healthy user bias.
