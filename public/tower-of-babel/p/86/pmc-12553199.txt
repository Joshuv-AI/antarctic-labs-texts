Assessing stakeholder perceptions and contextual factors in implementing post-discharge malaria chemoprevention in children with severe anaemia in Malawi
Authors: Myness Kasanda Ndambo, Jenna Hoyt, Emmanuel Nyirongo, Kamija Samuel Phiri, Jenny Hill, Lucinda Manda-Taylor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere anaemia poses a significant threat to children under five in sub-Saharan Africa, serving as one of the leading causes of hospitalisation and mortality in Malawi. In endemic regions, malaria complicates recovery in children admitted with severe anaemia and increases relapse risk. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends post-discharge malaria chemoprevention (PDMC) in moderate to high malaria transmission areas to reduce post-discharge mortality and readmissions. Still, it offers limited guidance on its implementation. This formative research study aimed to assess stakeholders’ perceptions of the WHO recommendation on PDMC and considerations for its practical implementation in Malawi.MethodsThis exploratory qualitative study, conducted in May 2024, employed in-depth and key informant interviews across various stakeholder levels in Malawi. We purposively sampled 31 participants. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and thematically analysed using inductive coding in NVivo 14.ResultsStakeholders revealed a continued high burden of malaria and anaemia, with significant gaps in knowledge among Health Surveillance Assistants and follow-up care. Caregivers faced numerous barriers to timely healthcare access, including traditional beliefs, financial constraints, and logistical challenges. Four broad themes emerged from the data: (i) understanding the dual challenge of malaria and anaemia in Malawian children, (ii) barriers to timely healthcare access, (iii) stakeholder perspectives on strategies to improve caregiver adherence to the PDMC regimen, and (iv) PDMC implementation considerations.ConclusionsFindings underscore operational challenges such as logistical constraints, medication supply issues, and targeted health worker training. It emphasises the importance of community engagement, strengthening supply chains, and integrating PDMC into health systems. Effective implementation will require collaboration among government agencies, health organisations, and local communities to foster sustainable practices, improve caregiver support, and ensure equitable access. These insights advance implementation science by illustrating how interventions like PDMC can be adapted and scaled in low-resource, high-burden settings to reduce childhood malaria and anaemia-related morbidity and mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13577-w.Keywords: Post-discharge malaria chemoprevention, Dihydroartemisinin piperaquine, Malaria, Severe anaemia, Children, Implementation, Delivery strategies, Malawi

Background

Malaria remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with the greatest burden concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that in 2022, there were approximately 249 million cases of malaria and 608,000 related deaths globally, with Africa accounting for about 94% of these deaths, primarily among children under five years of age [1, 2]. Severe anaemia is one of the most life-threatening complications of malaria in young children, contributing significantly to hospital admissions, repeat illnesses, and death in malaria-endemic regions [3–7].

Globally, it is estimated that approximately 40% of children aged 6 to 59 months are anaemic, with the highest prevalence observed in Africa [8, 9]. In malaria-endemic areas, severe malaria-associated anaemia often requires hospital-based management, including blood transfusion and antimalarial treatment [10]. However, full recovery of red blood cell production may take up to six weeks post-treatment [11], during which reinfection can lead to recurrent anaemia and increase the risk of mortality [4, 12, 13]. Recent studies have demonstrated that post-discharge malaria chemoprevention (PDMC) can effectively reduce mortality rates and hospital readmissions among children recovering from severe anaemia in malaria-endemic areas [14–17].

PDMC entails the administration of a full therapeutic and prophylactic course of antimalarial medication at predetermined intervals for children hospitalised with severe anaemia in settings with moderate to high malaria transmission following hospital discharge. This approach aims to minimise the chances of readmission and death [18]. Recognising the significance of this intervention, the WHO has recently endorsed PDMC as a preventive strategy for these high-risk children, offering a promising solution to mitigate the burden of severe malaria and its associated complications in vulnerable populations [18]. Despite the progress marked by the WHO recommendation, guidance to support country-specific policy adoption and practical implementation is not available. A notable gap is the absence of specific recommendations regarding the drug or regimen to be used and the contextual delivery strategy for PDMC, as these are typically country-led decisions.

In response to this need, the Ministry of Health (MoH), through the National Malaria Control Programme (NMCP) in Malawi, has recently co-designed an implementation trial for PDMC [19] to establish a clear roadmap for nationwide rollout. A key outcome of this co-design process is a pilot implementation in the Mangochi district, where children will be randomised to receive PDMC via two arms: (i) a community-based approach, wherein mothers receive all PDMC courses at discharge from the hospital (intervention arm), and (ii) a combination of community-based and facility-based approaches, where mothers obtain monthly PDMC supplies from Health Surveillance Assistants (HSAs) at village clinics and return to health facilities for each monthly PDMC dose (control arm). This approach will also involve clinical reviews of the child and referrals if the child is ill [19]. The targeted outcome of this implementation trial is optimal adherence to the PDMC regimen. Insights from the implementation trial will inform and guide the development of optimal delivery strategies for relevant stakeholders in Malawi.

To inform the design and planning of the upcoming implementation trial, we assessed stakeholders’ perceptions of the WHO PDMC recommendation and practical considerations for implementation in Malawi. The aim was to provide actionable recommendations to inform the pilot implementation study and contribute to the broader understanding and effective implementation of PDMC strategies.

Methods

Study design, setting, and sampling

This was a qualitative formative research study employing an exploratory research design. It involved in-depth interviews (IDIs) and key informant interviews (KIIs) across various stakeholder groups, including national, health facility, community health workers, and caregivers. Twelve IDIs for healthcare providers (HCPs), eight Health Surveillance Assistants (HSAs) involved in the provision of health care in the community, and nine caregivers of children under five years of age who have been hospitalised for treatment for all-cause severe anaemia within the previous three months were carried out at eight health centres in Zomba District of Southern Malawi. The health centres were: Likangala, Bimbi, Lambulira, Domasi, Naisi, Matawale, City Clinic, and Sadzi (Fig. 1) [20].

Approximate locations of the health centres in Zomba

We also conducted KIIs with two malaria programme stakeholders/policymakers in Lilongwe district, Malawi. Together, we purposively sampled [21, 22] 31 participants (Table 1) to explore stakeholders’ perceptions of the WHO PDMC recommendations and considerations for their implementation in Malawi. The first author collaborated with the District Medical Officer (DMO) at the Zomba District Health Office (DHO) to develop a list of potential participants. The first author initially contacted potential participants by phone to explain the study and obtain verbal informed consent, which was not audio-recorded. Once this was done, signed consent forms were collected during in-person meetings, and interviews were subsequently scheduled. All participants contacted in Zomba agreed to participate in the study.

Data collection

Data were collected in May 2024, guided by pre-tested interview guides which were piloted with an HCP, an HSA, and a caregiver at the Zomba DHO. The pretesting process helped identify ambiguous questions and allowed the study team to adjust the flow and sequencing of questions. A separate question guide was developed for each participant category (Supplementary file 1). The questions explored participants’ views on severe malaria and severe anaemia among Malawian children, health-seeking behaviours, suggestions for improving caregiver adherence to the PDMC regimen, and factors to consider when implementing PDMC in Malawi. The KIIs and IDIs provided an opportunity for a detailed overview of stakeholders’ perspectives [23] on the WHO recommendation for PDMC.

Interviews were conducted in English or Chichewa, depending on the person being interviewed and their language preference. Questions were asked by trained members of the research team. The interviews averaged 48 min in duration, with HCPs lasting 37 min, HSAs lasting 49 min, caregivers lasting 28 min, and policymakers lasting 77 min. All interviews were audio-recorded with the participants’ consent. To ensure privacy and create a safe environment for participants to share their perceptions openly, interviews with Health workers were conducted in spaces of their choice within the hospital premises. Caregiver interviews were conducted at Zomba DHO, in a private room, with transport costs reimbursed and lunch allowances provided to all participants. The first KII was conducted face-to-face at a place recommended by the participant, and the second KII was conducted online via Zoom, as suggested by the participant. The research team conducted both KIIs and IDIs. In all instances, notes were taken to summarise the key points after every interview to identify data saturation, where interviews do not offer new data [24].

Data analysis

Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim in both English and Chichewa using Microsoft Word 2016. Chichewa transcripts were translated into English by a research assistant fluent in the language. To ensure accuracy, the first author, also a native Chichewa speaker, cross-checked translations against the original audio. All transcripts were anonymised before analysis. An exploratory, inductive approach was employed [25], allowing themes to emerge organically without imposing pre-set assumptions—particularly important as this was the first implementation of the WHO PDMC recommendation in Malawi. The research team systematically examined the data to identify patterns, themes, and insights related to stakeholders’ perspectives on PDMC implementation.

During the familiarisation phase, preliminary codes were noted in the transcript margins. To ensure rigour and reduce bias [26], four transcripts—one from each stakeholder category (HCPs, HSAs, caregivers, and policymakers)—were manually coded independently by different team members. Following consensus discussions, a unified coding framework was developed collaboratively [27]. All transcripts were uploaded into NVivo 14, and the agreed-upon codebook was applied to the full dataset. The codebook included four columns: code, description, illustrative quotes, and theme. This structure enabled systematic organisation of data into areas such as malaria and anaemia challenges, caregiver adherence to PDMC, and contextual factors affecting implementation. The coded dataset was reviewed to identify and refine content-oriented themes [28]. Crosstab reports were generated in NVivo to explore similarities and differences across stakeholder groups. Illustrative quotes are used throughout the findings to support and contextualise the analysis.

Results

A total of 31 participants, including Policymakers (n = 2) at the national level in the Lilongwe district, Healthcare providers (n = 12), Health Surveillance Assistants (n = 8), and Caregivers of children recently discharged after treatment for severe anaemia within the previous three months (n = 9) from eight health centres in the Zomba district, were interviewed. This section presents the results emanating from the data organised into four broad themes: (i) understanding the dual challenge of malaria and anaemia in Malawian children, (ii) barriers to timely health care access in Zomba district, (iii) stakeholder perspectives on strategies to improve caregiver adherence to the PDMC regimen, and (iv) PDMC implementation considerations.

Understanding the dual challenge of malaria and anaemia in Malawian children

Participants recognised the persistent burden of malaria and anaemia in children under five. Health workers reported frequent malaria cases despite community sensitisation efforts and distribution of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs).

Malaria is our number one burden. We do not even understand what is happening. We teach a lot about malaria, and we are wondering if people are using bed nets, as the number of cases is increasing. They tell us that they use bed nets… cases of children with malaria are just too much… (Health Surveillance Assistant, Health Centre 2).

Caregivers emphasised the urgent need to address malaria to prevent further loss of life. They recognised malaria and anaemia as major health challenges, particularly among children under five, and expressed deep concern about the continued impact of these illnesses on their communities.

… because malaria and anaemia among children under five pose a serious burden in our communities. If malaria is not addressed, it will continue to claim lives. (Caregiver, Health Centre 5).

In contrast to perspectives from health surveillance assistants and caregivers, who emphasised the ongoing burden of malaria in their settings, the policymaker highlighted a perceived reduction in severe malaria cases, particularly severe anaemia, attributing this to the successful implementation of community-level interventions and improved access to treatment. From this higher-level viewpoint, the burden on health facilities has decreased. However, this contrasts with the experiences shared by those on the front lines, suggesting a gap between policy-level perceptions and the day-to-day realities of healthcare delivery.

There are not many cases of severe malaria anymore. With severe anaemia I think we have done a very good job bringing effective treatment to the community, so we are not seeing the burden of admission at the health facilities any more with malaria. (Policymaker, Lilongwe).

Likewise, HSAs expressed concern over the prevalence of anaemia and their limited training to manage it.

Anaemia needs attention. Maybe I would say out of five children with severe malaria, two children would be severely anaemic. However, there is nothing about anaemia that we are told. We do not have expertise in anaemia as HSAs, even our seniors, do not handle anaemia. Doctors deal with anaemia. As for me, the knowledge and information I have given about anaemia is from my personal experience growing up… (Health Surveillance Assistant, Health Centre 6).

Primary care providers indicated that while they initiate malaria treatment, cases requiring transfusion are referred to central hospitals due to a lack of capacity.

We first check the cause of severe anaemia by testing for malaria. After a clinical review, we get a blood sample to confirm and then refer the child to the Central Hospital. Those with severe anaemia get Artesunate; the other drugs depend on the severity of the anaemia. So, if the anaemia is severe in children, with a haemoglobin level below seven grams per decilitre, those children are eligible to receive blood. Still, those who are not eligible for blood transfusion get ferrous sulfate. Those discharged are given ferrous sulfate and asked to return to the hospital for review after a month or two weeks. (Healthcare Provider, Health Centre 5).

Referral to central hospitals limits continuity of care, particularly as follow-up systems post-discharge are poorly coordinated or absent as confirmed by health workers.

Most of the time, we don’t follow them, unless the child is sick. Since they can’t go directly to the tertiary hospital, they first come here. That is when they tell us they were admitted, and the situation is not changing… So, when they come again, you take their history, give them the first doses, and then refer them to the tertiary hospital again. (Healthcare Provider, Health Centre 2).

… there is no follow-up in the community. We just know that the child has been discharged and we assume that the child is now fine. Sometimes we do not even know that the child is back… (Health Surveillance Assistant, Health Centre 8).

Health workers reported that the dual burden of malaria and anaemia remains a significant health concern for children under five in Malawi. While frontline health workers recognise the severity of these conditions, significant gaps persist in HSA training on anaemia management and post-discharge follow-up systems, underscoring the need for targeted support and system-level interventions to improve continuity of care.

Barriers to timely healthcare access in Zomba district

Caregivers demonstrated an ability to recognise danger signs such as fever, seizures, pallor, and swelling. However, delays in care-seeking were influenced by deeply rooted traditional beliefs, which sometimes led caregivers to seek help from traditional healers before formal health services.

The child’s body felt hot, she looked pale, swollen, and had seizures… so I decided to go to the hospital. (Caregiver, Health Centre 3).

I did take the child to a traditional healer… the medicine did not work, so I then opted to go to the hospital. (Caregiver, Health Centre 5).

Even when caregivers intended to seek hospital care, they faced financial burdens related to transportation, food, and long distances, compounded by poor road conditions.

I had to sell maize to get to the hospital. Transport alone cost 5,500 Malawi Kwacha, and I sold the maize at 6,000 Malawi Kwacha. (Caregiver, Health Centre 4).

We walked from home to the hospital, and the distance is long… I had no money for transport. (Caregiver, Health Centre 5).

Due to poor road conditions in Malawi, caregivers also put their lives at risk while seeking care for the sick child.

When we were coming here, I got involved in an accident, so when we arrived here, the nurse took the child because I was failing to handle the child, as I was also in pain. So the nurse did all the processes by herself, and then when she was done, she brought the child to me. I was involved in an accident as I used a public motorbike. When I was about to get off, I didn’t know what the cyclist had done, so I fell off. Since I tried hard to protect the child, my hand got dislocated and the other one got some cuts, but the child did not get that injured; she had a small cut. (Caregiver, Health Centre 8).

Some even postponed hospital visits until nighttime in hopes of receiving faster service.

Most of the anaemia cases we see here are those that had gone to a private hospital first, and they have noticed that the condition is not changing. That is when they come here. (Healthcare Provider, Health Centre 7).

The findings indicated that, despite caregivers’ ability to recognise danger signs, timely access to care is hindered by entrenched traditional beliefs, financial and logistical constraints, and inadequate transport infrastructure. These barriers may compromise the effectiveness of PDMC and other community-level health interventions if unaddressed.

Stakeholder perspectives on strategies to improve caregiver adherence to the post-discharge malaria chemoprevention regimen

Participants proposed multi-level strategies to enhance adherence to PDMC. These included structured community sensitisation through HSAs and local leaders, counselling during drug dispensing, and use of visual aids such as calendars or entries in the health passport book.

Community awareness is helpful. Informing the chiefs, churches and even radios. People would easily access the information in that way. Even at the hospital during health talks. This information can also be provided before conducting the clinic. In that way, information can reach the community quickly. (Healthcare Provider, Health Centre 7).

Mobile phone reminders were also suggested, although network and airtime issues were acknowledged.

You should use phones to remind people. Those who do not have phones should use phones for their neighbours or relatives. (Caregiver, Health Centre 3).

HSAs were widely viewed as key to PDMC delivery given their community presence and trust.

The HSAs work with people in the community and are the most trusted by the people in the community. People will listen to whatever the HSA is telling them. This is the new intervention, and the materials cannot just come here at the facility and start working before involving the HSA and the community. I want to emphasise that the HSA should be allowed to sensitise community members and ask them if they have received the intervention. Again, we are referring to the child who has been discharged and is now in the community; that child needs to be monitored, and the HSA must monitor them and check adherence. If any problem arises, the HSA must link the child to the facility for further care. So, the HSA is needed in this intervention. (Health Surveillance Assistant, Health Centre 2).

However, all participant categories expressed concern about the inconsistent presence of HSAs in their respective catchment areas, identifying this as a key to effective PDMC service delivery.

… but we also have other challenges with the HSAs because they do not even reside in the catchment area. They only go there at specific times in the week. So, while it might work, it will also have some challenges, but it is at least the best among the worst. I don’t see health facilities reminding these women. I don’t see this happening. (Policymaker, Lilongwe).

… most HSAs do not reside in their catchment areas. For instance, on the day that the child should get the drug, the HSA does not conduct a clinic in the area so the caregiver will be under panic and the child will miss the dose. (Healthcare Provider, Health Centre 3).

Caregivers recommended involving visual aids, such as calendar drawings that could be hung on the wall in the house, and providing detailed instructions in passport books to remind mothers to administer medication, thereby reinforcing adherence.

When a child has been discharged, for some of us it is hard to remember to give the doses, so it would also help to make a calendar and paste it on a wall in her house. So that when she sees it, and moves around, she should be reminded. She will be reminded that on this day, I will give medicine. She cannot forget when she sees the calendar pasted in the house daily. (Caregiver, Health Centre 2).

You should be writing details about the instructions in the passport book so that the woman can be reminded by returning to the page. So, if I give medicine today, I will see the next date and it will be easily remembered. (Caregiver, Health Centre 8).

Additionally, some caregivers suggested strategies to involve household members and neighbours in the medication process.

It is true that women easily forget to give their children medication. However, we can use the people who stay within their households. Like in my case, when I forget, my mother reminds me. So you should be telling another person in the house to remind the mother. (Caregiver, Health Centre 3).

It would be important to involve the neighbours also, so they are aware of what is happening, so they can also take part in reminding them to make sure that the child is taking the drugs. (Health Surveillance Assistance, Health Centre 1).

The Stakeholders proposed strategies, including community sensitisation, reminder systems, counselling at the point of drug dispensing, and leveraging household members and neighbours to improve PDMC adherence.

Post-discharge malaria chemoprevention implementation considerations

Participants identified several critical factors influencing the implementation of PDMC. Drug supply chain integrity was emphasised as essential to ensure availability.

The usual challenge is supply. The cases can be identified, but the drugs are out of stock at the facility. Imagine transporting drugs from district health offices to small facilities. Once the intervention has started, there should be continuity. In other words, there should be sustainability. (Health Surveillance Assistance, Health Centre 3).

Health Surveillance Assistants and Healthcare Providers advocated for training and supportive supervision. These elements were seen as essential for ensuring effective and confident delivery of the PDMC intervention.

I think we need to have supportive supervision, and the training must be there… when someone is trained… You won’t have any problem with Dihydroartemisinin-Piperaquine (DP). (Healthcare Provider, Health Centre 5).

Logistical concerns were significant, particularly the difficulty of accessing remote areas even with bicycles among HSAs.

Distance from the facility to the communities where we work is a great challenge. Sometimes, we give services in remote areas that are very far away, where even if you are given a bicycle, you cannot cycle. So, reaching such places is not easy. For services that we are supposed to give frequently, we allocate them at long intervals, so they are manageable. (Health Surveillance Assistant, Health Centre 8).

To support caregiver understanding, job aid suggestions such as visual flipcharts, leaflets in local languages, and posters were offered.

Posters and flip charts explaining how the drug should be administered to the children, so that we can use them to teach the caregivers. Leaflets should be provided to parents, in addition to those included in the drug package, and Chichewa should be the primary language used on the leaflets. We also need to have DP dispersing registers, stock cards for those storing the drug to track the drugs, patient registers, and charts showing DP dosing. (Healthcare Provider, Health Centre 5).

The integration of monitoring systems was also recommended for tracking adherence and facilitating follow-up.

If we could have data concerning these children, I would be happy that the HSAs, in conjunction with the environmental team, should be visiting villages where these cases come from. Check their adherence and ask for their views on how satisfied they are with the assistance they received at the hospital, as well as their suggestions in general. They might have been given nets, but they do not use them, so it would be good to hear their views. (Health Surveillance Assistant, Health Centre 7).

Stakeholder perspectives indicate that successful PDMC implementation in Malawi requires a strong foundation in the health system, including uninterrupted drug supply, HCP training and supervision, HSA capacity and presence in communities, and integration into existing care pathways. Job aids and robust monitoring systems are crucial for ensuring quality delivery, adherence, and sustainability.

Discussion

The findings from this study have highlighted that the successful implementation of the World Health Organization (WHO) post-discharge malaria chemoprevention (PDMC) recommendation in Malawi requires tackling several impediments. These include fragmented understanding among stakeholders regarding malaria and anaemia trends among children under five, poor drug supply chain management, limited HSA capacity in anaemia management, weak post-discharge follow-up systems for children under five, and delayed hospital care seeking. To overcome these, participants/stakeholders suggested practical, community-informed strategies such as sensitisation campaigns, counselling at drug dispensing points, family engagement, and reminder tools like calendars, health passport notes, and mobile alerts.

A notable discrepancy emerged between frontline health workers and policymakers regarding trends in malaria and anaemia. While frontline health workers and caregivers reported increasing cases, policymakers perceived a decline, attributing this to improved access to treatment and care. For instance, National programme data indicate a sustained decline in malaria incidence from 407 cases per 1,000 population in 2016 to 220 per 1,000 in 2022 [29, 30]. Yet, spatial heterogeneity remains: the 2023 burden-stratification still classified Zomba as a high-burden area [31], which aligns with frontline reports of persistent caseloads. Furthermore, more recent programme statistics presented at the National Paramedical Private Practitioners Association of Malawi (NAPPPAM) meeting suggest a rebound in reported national cases—from 4.6 million (2022) to 6.49 million (2023) and 9.47 million (2024) [32], indicating either a reversal of earlier gains or shifts in detection/reporting. Taken together, the juxtaposition of (i) earlier national declines, (ii) district-level persistence, and (iii) the recent national uptick helps explain the divergence we observed between policy narratives and facility-level experience. While policymakers operate at a distance from daily realities, frontline providers are at the “coalface” of health service delivery. These inconsistencies highlight a well-documented divergence between policymakers and service providers, suggesting a possible gap in disease surveillance, reporting systems, or differing interpretations of available data. This disparity highlights the need for stronger feedback loops between policymakers and frontline staff, ensuring that national malaria policies remain responsive to on-the-ground trends —a point also emphasised in prior malaria policy implementation research in Malawi [33].

Our findings indicate that implementing PDMC will encounter significant drug supply chain management challenges. Malawi already faces a complex task in distributing pharmaceuticals with seasonal demand through a centralised public health delivery system [34]. The drug supply chain is exacerbated by an underdeveloped transportation infrastructure, unpredictable demand across time, and constrained financial resources [34]. Stakeholders emphasised the importance of integrating PDMC into existing health systems, such as the integrated management of childhood illnesses (IMCI), to boost efficiency and sustainability. This approach is supported by findings of previous PDMC stakeholder meetings [19, 35] and by prior research on PDMC in Malawi, suggesting that leveraging existing health delivery frameworks can enhance program sustainability [36]. Together, these insights underscore that PDMC implementation is most likely to succeed when embedded within and supported by the broader health system network.

HSAs in this study expressed deep concern over the high prevalence of severe anaemia and their limited capacity to manage it, highlighting the need for targeted training and supportive supervision to implement PDMC effectively. Participants perceived empowering HSAs as vital for the successful implementation of PDMC, similar to a prospective study in rural Mexico [37], where a Community Health Worker-led intervention, just like HSAs in Malawi, was associated with improved patient medication adherence. This is particularly important because PDMC is a community- and home-based intervention that requires the establishment of new linkages between referral hospitals, community health facilities, and CHW networks to ensure effective delivery and uptake.

Although our study suggests the existence of a functional referral system for severe malaria and severe anaemia in under-five children, health workers revealed substantial gaps in follow-up care post-discharge. Participants emphasised that this lack of continuity in treatment can contribute to poor health outcomes, underscoring the need for improved communication and tracking mechanisms between healthcare facilities and HSAs to support PDMC implementation. Strengthening these follow-up systems and ensuring ongoing monitoring of discharged patients were seen as critical to improving treatment adherence and long-term outcomes for affected children. Consistent with studies from Malawi and India, robust monitoring, follow-ups, and caregiver feedback are key to improving adherence and care quality [38, 39].

Although caregivers frequently recognise signs of severe anaemia, this study observed persistent delays in seeking timely care, driven by traditional beliefs, financial hardship, and transport barriers. These findings echo prior research showing limited timely access to child health services, especially in rural areas where travel times are longer [40–42]. Cultural preferences for traditional healers may further complicate hospital care-seeking. Strengthening community-based delivery, improving health literacy, and reducing financial and logistical barriers are critical to promoting timely and effective care for children under five.

Multi-level, community-centered strategies such as sensitisation campaigns, counselling at drug dispensing points, family engagement, and reminder tools like calendars, health passport notes, and mobile alerts—serve as powerful enablers for improving caregiver adherence to PDMC. Complementing these efforts, practical job aids—such as educational materials in local languages and visual tools also play an effective role in enhancing caregiver understanding and engagement, especially in low-literacy settings like Malawi [43, 44]. Evidence shows that culturally relevant, pictorial instructions significantly improve comprehension, retention, and adherence to treatment regimens [45–48]. These tools not only reinforce verbal guidance from health workers but also serve as ongoing reminders in the home, ultimately supporting better health outcomes for children post-discharge.

Contribution to knowledge

This study advances our understanding of the factors likely to affect the implementation of PDMC in similar resource-limited, high-burden contexts to Malawi. While the WHO provides broad guidance on PDMC, there remains limited insight into how the strategy can be operationalised in countries like Malawi. Our findings offer contextualised implementation insights, such as, addressing key system-level gaps including fragmented understanding of severe malaria and severe anaemia trends among under five children, poor drug supply chain management, limited HSA capacity in anaemia management, weak post-discharge follow-up systems for under five children, and delayed hospital care seeking. The study provides culturally relevant, stakeholder-informed strategies, including sensitisation campaigns, counselling at drug dispensing points, family engagement, and reminder tools like calendars, health passport notes, and mobile alerts that are practical and adaptable. Importantly, it has caregiver voices at the centre, ensuring the intervention is responsive to the lived experiences and needs of end-users.

Study limitations and strengths

Although purposive sampling is well-suited for qualitative research, its use, along with the limited availability of policymakers, presents some limitations. This may have introduced selection bias, potentially impacting the breadth of the findings among national stakeholders. However, the qualitative nature of this research allows for nuanced insights that numerical data might overlook, facilitating a more comprehensive exploration of attitudes and experiences related to the implementation of PDMC. Although qualitative research inherently limits generalisability, the insights gained are supported by evidence from similar studies and can provide valuable guidance for implementing PDMC in diverse settings.

Conclusion

In summary, implementing PDMC in Malawi presents critical opportunities and systemic challenges. This study highlights the importance of understanding and addressing sociocultural beliefs, health system context and constraints, and logistical barriers that affect timely care and treatment adherence. Stakeholder perspectives reveal that successful implementation hinges on comprehensive community engagement, capacity building for frontline providers—especially HSAs, integration of PDMC into existing health system structures, and linkage between referral and community-level health facilities. Future efforts should prioritise strengthening drug supply chains, supporting health worker training and supervision, and embedding PDMC within routine child health services. By addressing these priorities, Malawi can improve post-discharge outcomes for children recovering from severe anaemia and serve as a model for similar high-burden contexts.

Data availability

The dataset generated and analysed during this study is not publicly available. Even without direct identifiers like names, the dataset could contain identifiable information in aggregate due to detailed narratives from stakeholders in senior positions within the Ministry of Health in Malawi. We believe publicly sharing this data could ethically compromise our stakeholders’ identities if accessed by individuals within Malawi. However, the anonymised dataset or part of it may be available upon reasonable request to the corresponding author, pending permission from the authors’ institute.
