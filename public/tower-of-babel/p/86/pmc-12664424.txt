How to set up a pregnancy heart team
Authors: K Rijs, CD van der Marel, ALMJ van der Knijff-van Dortmont, IJJ Dons-Sinke, VFR Adriaens, JMJ Cornette, AGMGJ Mulders, JJ Duvekot, MMP Driessen, AE van den Bosch, JAAE Cuypers, RM Kauling, JW Roos-Hesselink
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of maternal morbidity and mortality in developed countries, complicating 4–11 % of pregnancies. Women with CVD are at increased risk of maternal complications, including heart failure and arrhythmias, as well as adverse fetal outcomes such as preterm birth and perinatal mortality. Traditionally, care for pregnant women with CVD has been fragmented across specialties, leading to discontinuity and missed opportunities for optimization. To address this, recent European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines strongly recommend the implementation of a pregnancy heart team: a dedicated multidisciplinary team providing integrated care for this high-risk population.We describe the implementation and organization of a pregnancy heart team in our academic tertiary hospital. The core team consists of a cardiologist specialized in cardio-obstetrics, a maternal-fetal medicine specialist, and an obstetric anesthesiologist, with additional experts consulted as required. Patients are discussed at predefined time points: preconception, during pregnancy, and postpartum. Standardized risk assessment using the modified World Health Organization (mWHO) classification guides counseling, management, and delivery planning. Shared decision-making is central, balancing maternal and fetal risks with patient preferences. The team establishes individualized peripartum care plans covering timing, mode, and location of delivery, anesthesia strategies, anticoagulation management, and postpartum monitoring.This structured, multidisciplinary model ensures continuity of care and offers a practical framework to improve outcomes in women with CVD during pregnancy.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the most important cause of maternal morbidity and mortality in developed nations [1,2]. In the Western world it complicates 4–11 % of all pregnancies [3,4], while cardiovascular-related conditions contribute to approximately one-quarter to one-third of the pregnancy-related deaths [1,5]. Diseases include congenital heart defects, valvular disease, aortic pathology, pulmonary hypertension, cardiomyopathies, arrhythmias, venous thromboembolism and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy. Aside from the maternal risks of heart failure, arrhythmia or even death, cardiovascular disease in pregnancy also endangers fetal health and is associated with increased rates of preterm birth, low birth weight, admission to a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), and perinatal mortality [6]. CVD during pregnancy is becoming more common, largely due to increased maternal age and a rise in chronic cardiovascular risk factors, such as hypertension, diabetes, and obesity, as well as the use of assisted reproductive techniques and/or underlying causes [7]. As a result, the proportion of high-risk pregnancies linked to pre-existing conditions, including CVD, increased from 0.7 % to 10.9 % between 2007–2010 and 2015–2018 [8]. This trend has been further underscored by recent real-world evidence from a large EHR-based pregnancy cohort, which demonstrated a marked rise in both maternal cardiovascular comorbidities and pregnancy-related cardiovascular complications over the last two decades [9]. Heart failure is the most common complication, affecting approximately 13 % of pregnant women with pre-existing heart conditions [10]. The effect of heart disease on both mother and baby underscores the importance of pre-pregnancy counseling and a tailored approach to manage pregnancy.

Traditionally, the care of pregnant women with cardiovascular disease has been fragmented across specialties. Obstetricians focus on pregnancy management, cardiologists address the cardiovascular condition, and anesthesiologists prepare plans for safe labor analgesia and perioperative care. Yet, coordination among these disciplines is often limited, resulting in discontinuity of care and missed opportunities to optimize maternal and fetal health. This integration can be achieved through a pregnancy heart team, a specialized multidisciplinary group dedicated to the collaborative management of high-risk pregnancies. This was first recommended in the ESC guidelines on pregnancy and cardiovascular disease in 2018 and further strengthened in the new guidelines of 2025 [4,11]. Early involvement of such a team is essential to improve both maternal and fetal outcomes and in preventing pregnancy-related morbidity and mortality. In this article, we describe the implementation and organization of the pregnancy heart team in our academic tertiary hospital.

Pregnancy heart team

Composition

At our hospital, the pregnancy heart team consists of congenital cardiologists specialized in cardio-obstetrics, a maternal-fetal medicine specialist with expertise in high-risk pregnancies, and an obstetric anesthesiologist. This composition represents the minimum team structure according to the ESC guideline for cardiovascular disease management during pregnancy [4]. Depending on the patient's specific condition, other specialists are involved, for instance, a cardiothoracic surgeon, interventional cardiologist, neonatologist, intensivists, hematologists, or experts from a referring center. When appropriate, allied health professionals, such as specialized nurses, midwives, or clinical psychologists, are also engaged to provide comprehensive care and address psychosocial needs.

Communication and coordination

Our pregnancy heart team gathers every two weeks to review patients, both referred and those receiving care at our own center. Each case is discussed at defined time points.

•Before pregnancy: to evaluate pregnancy risks, discuss contraceptive or reproductive options including genetic testing, and if applicable interventions to optimize pregnancy outcomes.

Before pregnancy: to evaluate pregnancy risks, discuss contraceptive or reproductive options including genetic testing, and if applicable interventions to optimize pregnancy outcomes.

•During pregnancy: to determine follow-up frequency, guide ongoing management, and prepare an individualized delivery and postpartum care plan.

It is crucial to work in a standardized manner, ensuring consistency in both documentation and decision-making, and to guarantee that the report is accessible in the electronic health record 24/7 for all professionals involved in the patient's care. Patients are typically referred to our pregnancy heart team by one of our cardiologists or maternal-fetal medicine (MFM)-specialists. Patients may also be referred from other hospitals. These referrals can be discussed purely based on external chart review and imaging that are digitally sent for the meeting. The referring physician is invited to participate in real-time through a live video communication. This collaborative approach means that local hospitals can rely on the expertise of the dedicated referral team for the strategy planning of less complex cases and manage them according to this plan. The electronic health record of each patient is updated after every case. After the meeting, the cardiologist or MFM specialist briefs the patient and/or referring specialist regarding the outcome.

Preconception

Patients with a known CVD will be discussed preferably before pregnancy to determine their risk class. The modified World Health Organization 2.0 (mWHO 2.0) system is used [11] (see Table 1). Risk is estimated based on the cardiologic condition in combination with additional risk factors, imaging results, arrhythmias and exercise tests. The mWHO is mainly based on the cardiologic diagnosis and status.

Concurrent obstetric or other medical risks and conditions are however included in our discussion to have a complete overview and further refine the individual risk assessment and classification and will be taken into account in the final advice for the patient.

Women who are considered low-risk (mWHO class I and II) can be managed in a local hospital during pregnancy and delivery, according to the plan established in the expertise center. We recommend to establish a local dedicated multidisciplinary team of obstetricians, cardiologists and anesthesiologists to discuss the plan and manage the patient accordingly. All applicable documentation, including the formal recommendations from the pregnancy heart team, is distributed to the referring specialists. The referring clinicians are welcome at all times to contact the pregnancy heart team for questions.

Patients classified as mWHO II-III are informed preconceptionally about the maternal, obstetric and offspring risks. Management is conducted by their cardiologist and MFM specialist.

Women at higher risk (mWHO III or IV) are invited, with their partner or trusted person for extensive pre-pregnancy counseling. This counseling is then conducted by the cardiologist and obstetric specialist together. Adaptation to pregnancy, maternal, obstetric and offspring chances and risks as well as available treatment options are discussed. Given the importance of reproductive autonomy the final decision regarding fulfilling the desire to have children or giving it up will be taken by the patient and is always respected. The emotional impact of (unwanted) childlessness is addressed and psychological support is offered and alternative options such as surrogacy and adoption/foster parenting are mentioned. Appropriate pregnancy care will always be offered in an expert center, should the woman choose to pursue a pregnancy despite the high risk. It is also discussed that reproductive assistance, if needed, is often not an option in mWHO IV patients.

All women with any form of heart disease are advised to have pregnancy follow up in a medical setting. mWHO I and II in local hospitals, mWHO II-III to IV in centers with cardio-obstetric expertise. In addition to preconception counseling that addresses cardiac risk factors, prospective parents are also informed about general preconception measures, such as folic acid supplementation. Furthermore, couples are screened and, when relevant, counseled regarding medical, obstetric, social, and hereditary risk factors.

The preconception phase also provides an opportunity to optimize or adjust medication regimens to ensure maternal and fetal safety. In cases of increased risk for congenital (cardiac) anomalies, advanced prenatal diagnostic (advanced ultrasound or genetic testing) are discussed and arranged when indicated.

During pregnancy

Patients in mWHO class II-III and higher are discussed in the pregnancy heart team again, when they actually are pregnant, preferably between week 20–24 weeks of gestation. Discussions before 20 weeks are generally avoided because diagnostic information may still be incomplete due to ongoing cardiovascular adaptation. Conversely, postponing the discussion beyond 24 weeks is not advised, since it is essential to have a peripartum care plan when a woman presents in labor at a time of week 24 of gestation and onwards. In our protocol, pregnant women are at least evaluated once by a cardiologist at about 20 weeks of gestation. An echocardiogram is performed at this visit. The pregnancy is managed by a MFM specialist with appropriate prenatal screening or diagnostic testing arranged and coordinated obstetric follow-up planned.

Patients in mWHO class II-III cardiovascular disease or higher are referred to an anesthesiologist for consultation, including a discussion of pain management options and anesthesia planning if a cesarean section becomes necessary. In many cases, early initiation of epidural analgesia during labor is recommended for high-risk cardiac patients to control pain and mitigate sympathetic surges. The anesthesiologist also advises on the safest anesthetic approach should cesarean delivery be required, for example, avoiding general anesthesia in selected patients. Beyond anesthesia management, the anesthesiologist serves as the perioperative physician, able to promptly recognize and treat peripartum cardiovascular complications. During cesarean section, they manage heart rate, blood pressure, and fluid balance on a minute-to-minute basis.

Patients are discussed at least once during pregnancy, but if necessary, more often. The multidisciplinary meeting starts by discussing new referrals, followed by updates on ongoing cases. First, the treating cardiologist or MFM specialist, who has already prepared the patient in advance, introduces her by outlining the primary concern and providing a brief medical history, both on cardiovascular diagnosis as on obstetric history or other relevant medical issues including medication use. They then review the most up-to-date clinical information, including the current patients' status, echocardiography and other imaging, lab results, and, when applicable fetal assessments. Further diagnostic investigations are advised if required. All information is documented in a standardized peripartum care plan (see Fig. 1). Because of the dynamic course of cardiovascular disease during pregnancy, the peripartum care plan will be updated or revised during the course of gestation. Roles and responsibilities

Each member of the team has a clearly defined role, although there is overlap. The most important aspect is shared decision-making.

•The cardiologist is responsible for cardiovascular imaging and other diagnostics when indicated and medical care of the cardiovascular disease. This involves conducting or arranging cardiac diagnostics, including imaging (echocardiography, CT/CMR), biomarkers such as NtproBNP, cardiopulmonary exercise testing, and cardiovascular medication management.

The location of delivery

It is recommended that all women with CVD give birth in a hospital setting, rather than at home, to ensure access to specialized care if needed. Low to intermediate maternal risks (mWHO class I and II) are usually managed in referring local hospitals. High-risk deliveries are conducted in an expert center for pregnant women with CVD and with the capabilities to provide 24/7 advanced care for these patients. In some cases, this includes the opportunity to undergo cardiothoracic surgery. In others, obstetric intensive care is required.

Timing of delivery

The timing of delivery is individualized depending on both fetal well-being and the cardiovascular status of the mother. In general, delivery around 39–40 weeks is ideal for fetal/neonatal outcomes, but antepartum maternal or fetal indications may necessitate earlier delivery. Although induction of labor prior to 39 weeks is not recommended, a pregnancy duration of more than 40 weeks is also not preferred as this carries additional risks without clear advantages [12]. Women are counseled regarding the timing of delivery by the MFM specialist, while maternal preferences are taken into account should expectant management be desired.

Mode of delivery

The mode of delivery (vaginal versus cesarean section) will be recommended by the team with taking into account cardiac and obstetric status. By default, vaginal delivery is preferred as it is accompanied by less blood loss, lower infection risk, and less risk of venous thromboembolism, faster recovery and appropriate fetal stimulation compared to cesarean delivery. Cesarean delivery is reserved for obstetric indications or specific high-risk cardiac care issues like decompensated heart failure, severe aortic dilatation, or pulmonary hypertension [4]. The use of a therapeutic anticoagulant such as a vitamin K antagonist, when it cannot be safely discontinued or bridged within 2 weeks before delivery, is also an indication for cesarean delivery. Epidural anesthesia combined with second stage-assisted delivery is advisable if forceful or prolonged Valsalva maneuvers are to be avoided to reduce cardiac load.

Postpartum care

The team determines the most appropriate post-delivery care setting and advised length of stay based on each patient's cardiovascular risk and their likelihood for developing heart failure post-delivery. While many women can recover on a standard maternity ward, those at higher risk may require a high-dependency unit, such as an obstetric critical care unit (OCCU) or a (cardiothoracic) intensive care unit (CICU). An OCCU or ICU allows for further monitoring or administering medications not available on a routine delivery ward setting. For hemodynamic monitoring, non-invasive measurements of blood pressure, heart rate, and pulse oximeter are typically adequate. However, in certain cases, for example, severe aortic stenosis, continuous arterial blood pressure monitoring is used.

Deciding on the post-delivery location in advance allows teams to prepare accordingly and ensures a smooth and well-coordinated handover.

Anticoagulation

Anticoagulation management around the time of delivery can be highly complex, which is why it is carefully discussed within the team in close collaboration with a hematologist. The various types of anticoagulants used during pregnancy each have distinct advantages and disadvantages. Vitamin K antagonists offer the best protection for the mother but are associated with fetal abnormalities and an increased risk of miscarriage and stillbirth. In contrast, low-molecular-weight heparins are safer for the fetus but carry a higher risk of valve thrombosis and adverse outcomes for the mother [13]. A detailed, individualized peripartum anticoagulation plan is developed for each patient. This plan specifies.

In accordance with ESC guideline principles, the plan also addresses postpartum anticoagulation resumption, taking into account delivery-related bleeding, hemostasis, breastfeeding safety, and the elevated thromboembolic risk in the puerperium.

The finalized plan is documented in the electronic health record and made available to all professionals involved in the patient's care.

Postpartum follow up

Standard outpatient postpartum follow up comprises of an obstetric and eventual cardiologic visit 6 weeks after delivery. As most cases of heart failure, rhythm disturbances, hemorrhage and thrombosis occur in the first days or weeks post-partum, a plan for intensified outpatient follow-up is necessary in high-risk patients. During our bi-weekly meetings, we discuss these high-risk postpartum patients if indicated. This not only enables us to continue offering valuable input during the recovery period but also allows for reflection on the entire care trajectory. By evaluating what went well and identifying areas for improvement, we can optimize care for future pregnancies and similar clinical cases.

Conclusion

CVD in pregnancy carries risk for both the mother and the fetus. By establishing a pregnancy heart team – a dedicated multidisciplinary team of cardiologists, obstetricians, anesthesiologists, and other specialists – risks can be reduced and delivery tailored, dedicated to achieve optimal individualized care for these high-risk patients. In this process, the wishes and preferences of the patient should be central, whenever feasible. Timing and teamwork are essential. This structured, team-oriented approach can really make a difference in improving outcomes for both mothers and their babies.
