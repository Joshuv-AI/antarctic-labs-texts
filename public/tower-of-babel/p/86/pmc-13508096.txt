Spatiotemporal and Digital Electrochemiluminescence Imaging for Intelligent Quantitative Biosensing
Authors: Yu Zheng, Jinwei Xiao, Tadele Eticha, Dejana Bešlić, Saman Hosseinkhani, Yiran Guan, Guobao Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrochemiluminescence (ECL) imaging has progressed from a sensitive-intensity-based detection method to a powerful analytical platform capable of resolving biological and chemical heterogeneity at the single-entity level. This review maps the evolution of ECL imaging along three major axes: (i) spatiotemporal resolution, enabled by advanced luminophores, confined and built-in coreactant pathways, nanozyme catalysis, and 3D emissive-layer control; (ii) digitalization, in which analog luminescence is converted into discrete, statistically robust events for ultrasensitive quantification; and (iii) intelligent analysis, where artificial intelligence enhances denoising, emitter localization, dynamic tracking, multiplexed decoding, and kinetic inference. Mechanistic innovationssuch as nanoscale confinement, intramolecular coreactant design, in situ conversion of endogenous metabolites, and asymmetric nanostructuresprovide brighter, more localized, and biocompatible emission sources. Parallel advances in materials (metal nanoclusters, quantum dots, perovskites, AIE luminogens, Janus particles), imaging platforms (SECL/PECL modes, super-resolution strategies, microfluidics, smartphone/Raspberry-Pi devices, and large-scale bipolar-electrode arrays), and digital/AI pipelines collectively enable the high-fidelity capture of dynamic processes from single molecules to complex 3D spheroids. Together, these developments establish a coherent spatiotemporal–digital–intelligent framework and position ECL imaging as a versatile, scalable, and excitation-free modality for next-generation biosensing, clinical diagnostics, drug screening, environmental monitoring, and functional material characterization.

Introduction

Electrochemiluminescence (ECL) is light emission initiated by electrochemical processes, produced when excited species relax to the ground state; in essence, it is an analytical modality of “electrochemical initiation + optical readout”. Unlike conventional optical bioanalysis, ECL requires no external excitation light, thereby eliminating Rayleigh scattering, Raman scattering, and stray light from the source at their root, which yields an exceptionally low optical background and provides the physical basis for its ultrahigh detection sensitivity. , Since the systematic elucidation of ECL phenomena and mechanisms by Hercules and Bard in the 1960s and the subsequent maturation of the Ru­(bpy)3 2+/tripropylamine system in the 1990s, ECL has achieved large-scale commercial deployment in clinical immunoassays, establishing a robust methodological and technological foundation for further imaging and portable applications. −

Today, the value of ECL has extended from macroscopic average-intensity measurements to precise dissection of the microscale heterogeneity. Its high sensitivity and broad linearity make it particularly well suited for visualization of spatial distributions and high-throughput analysis at the single-entity level (e.g., single cells, single particles), and it has been systematically summarized for imaging and single-cell analysis scenarios. Within this context, small-molecule organic emitter systems further accentuate ECL’s near-zero background, high sensitivity, and wide linear range. For complex tasks such as chiral recognition, ECL is being integrated with separation techniques and polarization readouts due to its real-time, sensitive, and low-background characteristics, including capillary electrophoresis–electrogenerated chemiluminescence (CE–ECL), capillary electrochromatography–electrogenerated chemiluminescence (CEC–ECL), and circularly polarized electrochemiluminescence (CP-ECL), thereby expanding the boundaries of bioanalytical applications. In summary, through mechanistic innovations such as confined self-sensitization and built-in coreactants, together with the development of new luminophores, ECL has gained the capability to deliver bright and stable emission imaging under complex physiological conditions. This increasingly rich toolbox of mechanisms and materials establishes a solid physicochemical foundation for directly visualizing microscale heterogeneity of biological activity at the single-cell and single-molecule levels.

Against this backdrop, ECL imaging has undergone a paradigm transition from macroscopic population-averaged measurements to microscale heterogeneous analysis. This transition can be broadly understood as an evolution from exploiting overall “intensity” to extracting information from “images,” and ultimately advancing toward spatiotemporal, digital, and intelligent analysis which can be summarized in three interconnected stages:

Stage I (Intensity Detection): Establishing a Highly Sensitive Quantitative Tool

At this stage, ECL primarily functioned as an exceptionally sensitive photometric tool, enabling ultrasensitive quantitative detection through the measurement of total luminescence intensity. The success of this stage, particularly in clinical immunoassays, validated the reliability of ECL methodologies and laid the essential foundations for subsequent imaging research.

Stage II (Spatial Imaging): From Mere “Seeing” to Spatially Resolved Observation

The second stage of this evolution is marked by the emergence of spatially resolved ECL imaging. With improvements in luminophore brightness, electrochemical control, and optical detection, ECL imaging systems achieved progressively higher spatial and temporal resolution, enabling direct visualization of electrode heterogeneity, cells, subcellular structures, and discrete micro/nanoscale objects. ,,, ECL microscopy (ECLM) configurations, typically comprising an electrochemical module and a low-light imaging module, benefit from the absence of external illumination and can be flexibly adapted to diverse imaging tasks. − Within this framework, shadow ECL (SECL) and positive ECL (PECL) have emerged as two representative imaging modes, exhibiting opposite image contrasts and complementary application scopes, thereby substantially expanding the versatility of ECL imaging. −

Stage III (Spatiotemporal–Digital–Intelligent Paradigm): The Inevitable Path toward Precise Analysis

ECL imaging is now undergoing its most profound transformation. Traditional ECL centers on average intensity, yet biological systems and electrode interfaces exhibit intrinsic heterogeneity: A key limitation of population-averaged measurements is that it obscures both interindividual differences and critical rare events, from the presence of circulating tumor cells to the dynamics of transient cellular communication. To precisely unveil microscale nonuniformity in living processes, the research focus must shift from population averages down to single entities and even single molecules, enabling the direct observation of spatial distributions, dynamic behaviors, and interactions. Benefiting from excitation-free operation and high signal-to-noise ratios, ECL imaging is particularly well suited for resolving low-abundance molecules, rare subpopulations, and dynamic heterogeneity at the single-entity level. Beyond static spatial localization, ECL enables spatiotemporal tracking of biomolecular dynamics within individual cells, exemplified by trajectory analysis of membrane proteins using ruthenium-based metal–organic framework (MOF) nanoemitters, which reveals heterogeneous migration modes and state transitions. At the resolution limit, super-resolution ECL imaging has surpassed the optical diffraction barrier, achieving spatial resolution down to 24 nm and enabling nanoscale dissection of biomolecular organization and interactions. Furthermore, by converting analog intensity signals into countable discrete events, digital ECL imaging provides statistically robust quantification and ultralow detection limits for low-abundance analyses. As imaging data sets grow increasingly complex and low in signal-to-noise, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine-learning algorithms are being integrated into ECL workflows for image denoising, automated feature recognition, target tracking, and advanced data interpretation, collectively driving the evolution of ECL imaging toward a spatiotemporal–digital–intelligent analytical paradigm. ,, Scheme illustrates the integrated framework of this paradigm, encompassing key elements from mechanism/material design to intelligent data interpretation.

Spatiotemporal–Digital–Intelligent Paradigm for ECL Imaging

The realization of this new paradigm relies on synergistic innovation across mechanisms, luminophores, electrode materials, instrumentation, and data science. Materials advances have markedly extended the boundaries of the spectrum–efficiency–compatibility space for ECL imaging, , while scalable fabrication of disposable electrodes (e.g., laser-processed screen-printed carbon electrodes (SPCEs) , and bipolar-electrode (BPE)-based multidimensional encoding , provide replicable and translatable engineering pathways.

In this review, we aim to systematically synthesize and articulate the spatiotemporal–digital–intelligent paradigm of ECL imaging. Section 2 focuses on mechanistic, materials, and electrode innovations that underpin enhanced imaging performance. Section 3 analyzes imaging platforms and system architectures that enable high spatiotemporal resolution across laboratory and portable settings. Section 4 examines digital and intelligent analysis pipelines that convert ECL images into precise quantitative and mechanistic insights. Finally, Section 5 summarizes current challenges and outlines future directions for the evolution of ECL imaging toward scalable, intelligent, and real-world applications.

Mechanistic, Luminophore, and Electrode Material Innovations Driving the Paradigm Shift

Driven by the paradigm shift outlined above, innovations in mechanisms and materials are crucial for generating the bright, stable, and biocompatible signals required for advanced ECL imaging. The fundamental driving force is a deeper, more controllable understanding of the emission processshifting from macroscopic, homogeneous “mix-and-measure” toward microscopic, heterogeneous precision design. This shift requires unifying reaction-pathway design (molecular/nanoscale) with imaging physics (space/time dimensions) and systems engineering. In recent years, research centered on the efficient management of radicals/electrons, controllable supply of coreactants, and synergistic amplification of reaction flux and signal-to-noise ratio, yielding innovative strategies such as confined self-sensitization and built-in coreactants. By precisely tailoring the reaction microenvironment, these strategies provide the needed mechanistic assurance and material solutions to achieve high brightness, low perturbation, and precisely localized emission required by the new paradigm.

Specifically, ECL emission mainly relies on two core reaction pathways: the annihilation route, which does not require external coreactant and relies on the redox reaction between the oxidized and reduced forms of luminophores, and the coreactant route, which generates reactive radicals via coreactant electrolysis to trigger luminophore emission and is further divided into anodic oxidation–reduction and cathodic reduction–oxidation subtypes.

Owing to its operational simplicity and efficiency, the coreactant route has become dominant in bioimaging. To meet the stringent demands of high spatiotemporal resolution, signal intensity, stability, and biocompatibility, recent pathway design has moved beyond simple bulk mixing toward spatiotemporally controlled strategies:

Confined Self-Sensitization: Compressing Mass-Transfer Paths to Boost Local Efficiency

The core idea is to coconfine the luminophore and its coreactant within nanoscale space, drastically shortening diffusion distances to enhance reaction efficiency and reduce energy loss. A representative example is to codope Ru­(bpy)3 2+ and S2O8 2– into silica nanoparticles and employs cationic poly­(diallyldimethylammonium chloride) (PDDA) to stabilize the generated anionic radical SO4 •– during the reaction. Confinement enables ECL at lower cathodic potentials and yields 2.9-fold signal enhancement, which was successfully applied to “blanket-style” imaging of membrane proteins (e.g., EGFR) at adherent cell–substrate interfaces, achieving a full loop from “confined design” to “imaging application”. Another example is to confine Ru complexes inside zeolite nanopores, which (Ru@zeolite) enhances excited-state formation and stability, and enable single-molecule ECL imaging (Figure a) and trajectory tracking, it is a direct materials-to-resolution breakthrough.

Built-in Coreactants: Shifting from External Supply to Intramolecular Transfer

This strategy replaces diffusion-limited external supplying with intramolecular or intramasion electron transfer fundamentally overcoming transmembrane transport limits and uncontrolled spatial distribution of coreactants. A novel strategy achieves the covalent embedding of TPA fragments within the backbone of polymer dots (Pdots). A strong ECL response is observed at +1.2 V in PBS without added coreactant, arising from a two-step intramolecular electron transfer. This converts the conventional “edge-lighting” (confined to cell peripheries due to coreactant diffusion limits) into whole-membrane imaging (HER2 as a model), substantially improving image quality (Figure b). In parallel, luminol-ECL enhancement can be systematized into self-enhancement/solid anchoring, intramolecular coreactants, new green coreactants (e.g., PLL, BNQDs, water, OH–), and coreactant accelerators (e.g., metal, carbon, framework, single-atom and diatomic sites), offering a clear framework for rational design.

Beyond molecular and polymer-dot emitters, similar concepts of confined or built-in coreactant activation have also been realized in aggregation-induced emission electrochemiluminescence (AIECL) systems.

For instance, integrating aggregation-induced emission (AIE) luminogens (TPE) within NH2-MIL-88 creates a nanoconfinement reactor whose atomically dispersed Fe sites catalyze S2O8 2– → SO4 •– in situ, shortening diffusion paths and amplifying AIECL. Coupled with a smartphone-adopted imaging system and deep-learning analysis, this design links confined coreactant acceleration to point-of-care AIECL imaging.

Nanozyme Catalysis: Biomimetic Catalysis to Amplify Reaction Flux

Leveraging enzyme-like nanomaterials (nanozymes) to catalyze coreactant conversion is a powerful strategy to boost ECL. For example, Co3O4 nanoparticles efficiently catalyze H2O2 decomposition to generate O2 •– and OH•. These ROS rapidly couple with L012 radicals to form emissive intermediates, delivering 3-fold ECL enhancement on ITO electrodes. Coupled with antibody probes, this enabled selective imaging of Carcinoembryonic Antigen (CEA) membrane proteins on MCF-7 (positive) versus HeLa (negative) cells, demonstrating potential for specific biodetection.

In Situ Conversion: Enabling Biocompatible, On-Site Imaging

In living cells or complex biosystems, the cytotoxicity of exogenous coreactants (e.g., TPrA) and diffusion-induced spatial blurring are key obstacles. The in situ conversion strategy transforms endogenous or cell-released molecules into effective coreactants. In one representative approach, electrodes are functionalized with poly amidoamine (PAMAM) dendrimers. The amines on the dendrimer surface specifically sequester cell-released formaldehyde (FA) and convert it on-site into electrochemiluminescent coreactants for Ru­(bpy)3 2+. This avoids TPrA toxicity, suppresses FA diffusion loss while preserving its metabolic gradient, and achieves the first time-resolved visualization of FA release from single live cells under pharmacological stimulation, markedly improving spatial fidelity and biological relevance in ECL imaging.

Extending this concept to endogenous redox pools, yeast glycolysis drives NAD+ conversion to NADH that acts as an efficient coreactant for Ru­(bpy)3 2+, enabling additive-free ECL imaging of metabolism with quantitation across 6.25 × 106–6.25 × 108 CFU mL–1 and selective readouts under toxic inhibition (Figure ).

Structural Heterogeneity and Field Enhancement: Reshaping the Microenvironment at the Single-Particle Scale

Beyond homogeneous materials, nanostructures with asymmetric architectures or plasmonic effects can create unique reaction microenvironments at the single-particle level, thereby enhancing the ECL.

One representative example is Janus particles. In Au/Pt Janus particles, the two ends possess markedly different electrocatalytic activities (differences in k 0). Under applied fields, this drives asymmetric reaction fluxes on the two faces, establishing local concentration gradients. Such gradients induce subtle fluidic slip, accelerate mass transport, and suppress side reactions, yielding higher ECL intensity and stability during long acquisitions, and offering direct evidence for the coupling among structure–activity–mass transfer at the single-particle level (Figure ).

Illustration of ECL imaging of electrocatalysis at Au–Pt JNPs. Reprinted with permission from ref . Copyright 2018, John Wiley and Sons.

Another important strategy is plasmonic enhancement. Noble-metal nanostructures such as gold nanorods exhibit a localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) under suitable excitation, generating intense local electromagnetic “hot spots”. When the ECL emitter’s spectrum matches the LSPR band, these fields can markedly increase the rate of excited-state generation or radiative decay. Studies show LSPR hot spots can raise the apparent ECL rate constant k eff from 4.0 to 7.5 s–1, evidencing strong signal enhancement. A caveat is that plasmonic amplification may also elevate any nonspecific emission, raising the background. Thus, the key to success lies in ensuring the signal is amplified far more than the background, namely, achieving a high signal-to-background ratio.

New Luminophores and Functional Nanomaterials

Materials are the source of ECL signals; their physicochemical properties directly determine imaging sensitivity, resolution, multiplexing capacity, and biocompatibility. Confronted with the intrinsic constraints of the classic Ru­(bpy)3 2+/TPrA commercial system, such as a modest photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY ≈ 4%), a relatively broad emission band (≈ 200 nm), and limited wavelength tunability, and the fact that many luminol-based formulations still operate optimally under alkaline conditions and require careful engineering to function in neutral media, the development of high-performance new luminophores and functional nanomaterials has become pivotal to advancing the paradigm shift.

One important direction is upgrading traditional systems and developing high-performance alternatives. Classical complexes (e.g., Ru­(bpy)3 2+, Ru­(phen)3 2+) and their derivatives remain indispensable in many contexts, owing to mature synthesis, robust electrochemistry, and good aqueous solubility, particularly scenarios requiring special optical activity. For instance, in enantiomer recognition, when combined with chiral organic macrocycles, these chiral complexes enable circularly polarized electrochemiluminescence (CP-ECL) as an emerging polarization-dimension readout, thereby allowing direct discrimination of chiral molecules via differences between left- and right-circularly polarized emission. Nevertheless, to meet the more exacting demands of the new paradigm, a series of new luminophores has emerged.

Metal Nanoclusters (Metal NCs)

Metal NCs represent an important class of emerging ECL luminophores. Consisting of a few to a few dozen metal atoms with sizes approaching the Fermi wavelength, metal nanoclusters such as gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) exhibit distinctive size-dependent emission, excellent stability, and good biocompatibility. Beyond serving as ECL emitters, their core–shell–ligand architectures permit precise performance tuning. Studies show that AuNCs can deliver an impressive ECL quantum yield approaching 66% with triethylamine as the coreactant, and their emission can extend into the near-infrared, offering attractive opportunities for deep-tissue imaging. − Their ECL behavior is triply modulated by the ligand layer: an anchoring layer (e.g., thiols) stiffens the cluster and facilitates electron transfer; a scaffold layer induces collective effects such as AIE via hydrogen bonding/π–π/hydrophobic interactions to raise efficiency; and outer functional groups regulate electrochemical behavior and coreactant interactions through electrostatic and polarity effects, collectively enabling predictive structure–property mapping. For Au38 clusters, the anodic ECL mechanism with TPrA involves formation of TPrA radicals/radical cations, stepwise redox of Au38, and radiative recombination of the excited state *Au38, thereby yielding near-infrared emission at 930 nm.

Quantum Dots (QDs)

Semiconductor QDs and perovskite QDs constitute another major family of ECL emitters. Owing to size-tunable wavelengths, narrow emission bands, and high PLQY, QDs are ideal ECL candidates. Their mechanisms encompass both annihilation and coreactant routes. In a representative reduction–oxidation path, S2O8 2– is reduced to SO4 •–, which reacts with negatively charged QD anions (QD•–) to form excited QD*. Silicon quantum dots (SiQDs) were among the first shown to exhibit ECL via both annihilation and coreactant modes. Subsequent systems (CdSe, CdTe, PbS; II–VI and IV–VI families) broadened spectral coverage, while surface-defect passivation and inorganic shelling improved ECL efficiency toward their intrinsic photoluminescence performance. − In recent years, halide perovskite QDs (e.g., CsPbBr3) have surged ahead, exhibiting an ultranarrow ECL full width at half-maximum and very high efficiencies, and offering a competitive alternative to traditional Ru complexes and luminol systems. − Notably, QD ECL may originate from both band-edge and defect states, with their proportions governed by delicate control over defect passivation. Correspondingly, shell/ligand engineering requires balancing radiative recombination efficiency against effective charge injection/transport, directly constraining subsequent material choices and imaging pathways.

Aggregation-Induced Emission Luminogens (AIEgens)

AIEgens offer yet another route to obtaining high-performance ECL emitters. In contrast to the aggregation-caused quenching (ACQ) typical of conventional dyes, AIEgens emit weakly in the dispersed state but strongly upon aggregation. This unique trait makes them well suited for constructing highly loaded, high-brightness ECL nanoprobes, effectively overcoming signal loss from dye aggregation during labeling and opening new avenues for next-generation ECL imaging reagents.

One representative strategy is the use of carriers and composite nanostructures. Using supports such as mesoporous SiO2 or polystyrene (PS) microspheres to load or encapsulate molecular luminophores (e.g., Ru­(bpy)3 2+) or nanoemitters (e.g., QDs) allows construction of high-loading hybrid emitters. This expands the accessible luminophore library while providing protection, improved dispersibility, and facile surface functionalization. For example, densely loading Ru­(bpy)3 2+ into SiO2 nanospheres and coupling AuNPs to enhance conductivity and bioconjugation yields ultrabright nanoemitters. Such probes visualize single protein molecules (e.g., cytokeratin-19, CK19) as discrete ECL spots with a signal-to-noise ratio about an order of magnitude higher than comparable photoluminescence methods, providing a powerful tool for single-molecule digital counting.

Stimuli-responsive DNA nanomachines wrapped a Ru­(bpy)3 2+-doped MOF core with an ATP-gated DNA-hydrogel shell that blocks ECL until intracellular ATP triggers shell disassembly, thereby restoring ECL for targeted intracellular imaging. This “nanoconfinement reactor × gated interface” blueprint generalizes to other intracellular analytes.

Janus

Janus and other asymmetric nanoparticles exemplify this structure–function integration. As discussed in the mechanistic section, asymmetric nanostructures such as Au–Pt Janus particles effectively couple electrocatalytic activity with reaction kinetics via anisotropic composition/structure, producing more stable and enhanced ECL. This reflects a design shift from homogeneous materials to heterostructures, wherein precise control of nanoscale morphology and composition is used to actively regulate macroscopic imaging outputs, forming an amplification chain of morphology/composition → reaction kinetics → imaging signal.

Plasmonic Nanostructures

Plasmonic nanostructures provide another powerful means of enhancing the ECL signals. As noted earlier, noble-metal nanostructures (e.g., gold nanorods, nanospheres) create intense local electromagnetic fields via localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR). When ECL occurs within such “hot spots” signals can be amplified by orders of magnitude. The enhancement depends not only on spectral matching but also on nanostructure shape, size, and orientation, offering another potent physical handle for ultrasensitive ECL detection.

Design of Electrode Materials and Interface Engineering

ECL imaging relies not only on efficient emitters and reaction pathways but also critically on the electrode, which serves as the interface for electron transfer and the “platform” for reactions. The material properties and surface state of the electrode directly influence the initiation efficiency, spatial localization, signal-to-noise ratio, and system integration and applicability of ECL reactions. Therefore, the rational design of electrode materials and interfaces represents another critical pillar for achieving high-performance ECL imaging.

Conventional electrodes, including indium tin oxide (ITO), gold (Au), platinum (Pt), and glassy carbon (GC), remain the most widely used substrates in ECL imaging due to their well-defined electrochemical behavior, good conductivity, and compatibility with optical microscopy. These electrodes establish fundamental parameters, such as the electrochemical window, background current, and charge injection efficiency, thereby providing a key performance benchmark for ECL systems.

To meet the demands for high spatial resolution, high throughput, portability, and biocompatibility, novel electrode materials and structures continue to emerge.

Micro-/Nanostructured Electrode

Micro-/nanostructured electrodes, through constructing surface morphologies such as nanoparticles, nanowires, porous, or three-dimensional networks, can increase the effective surface area by several orders of magnitude. This significantly enhances reactant mass transport, intensifies the local electric field, and optimizes the loading and distribution of emitters or catalytic sites. For example, Liu et al. utilized chitosan and nano-TiO2 to modify an electrode, effectively reducing spatial hindrance from cells and improving the signal-to-noise ratio in single-cell ECL imaging. Pan et al. developed an integrated “nanokit” electrode based on a nanocapillary, which combines detection reagents with electrochemical sensing to achieve highly sensitive analysis of intracellular glucose and sphingomyelinase activity. Cao et al. employed two-photon 3D printing to fabricate carbon nanoelectrodes, combined with atomic layer deposition insulation and focused ion beam polishing, successfully enabling real-time in vivo monitoring of dopamine in the fruit fly brain. Ko et al. designed a three-dimensional microelectrode array, through the current-focusing effect and enhanced spherical diffusion resulting from pyramidal microstructures, greatly improved the sensitivity and reproducibility of RNA detection. Together, these studies demonstrate the important role of micro- and nanostructural design in enhancing the spatial resolution, detection limit, and biocompatibility of electrochemical sensing.

Printing and Flexible Electrodes

Screen printing, inkjet printing, and related technologies offer efficient pathways for the large-scale and low-cost manufacturing of electrodes. The resulting disposable carbon or metal electrodes can be easily patterned at micro/nanoscale and integrated with microfluidic systems, making them key sensing components in portable ECL imaging devices. For instance, Luthfianty et al. developed a folic acid sensor based on screen-printed carbon electrodes (SPCEs), which requires only 45 μL of sample for detection, highlighting its potential for microscale analysis. Laser direct writing or post-treatment can further modulate the electrode surface properties. As shown by Santo et al., CO2 laser treatment selectively removes binders from SPCE surfaces, significantly enhancing electron transfer efficiency and improving both ECL signal intensity and imaging uniformity. On the other hand, various polymer substrates enable the fabrication of flexible electrodes, which exhibit good mechanical flexibility and biocompatibility, allowing them to conform to irregular biological surfaces and paving the way for in vivo, in situ ECL monitoring and wearable sensing. For example, Ji et al. designed a PVDF-HFP/MXene composite flexible electrode that can be directly integrated into the detection chamber of a microfluidic chip, achieving a highly sensitive and flexible ECL imaging analysis of piR-651 in thyroid cancer-derived small extracellular vesicles. Therefore, the continued advancement of printed and flexible electrode technologies is driving ECL imaging toward greater system integration, real-time detection capability, and in situ application scenarios.

Sustained innovation and synergy at the levels of mechanisms, materials, and electrodes ensure access to ECL “light sources” that operate at low trigger potentials, exhibit low biological toxicity, and deliver high brightness and stability. This expanding library provides the material basis and design space for achieving high spatiotemporal resolution, digital readouts, and intelligent analysis at the instrumentation/system level. However, turning these excellent “sources” into structured, metrological, high-information-throughput images urgently requires coevolution of imaging techniques and system platforms, so that chemical signals can be faithfully captured as streams of spatial and temporal information. Building on these mechanistic and materials advances, the next step is to consider how such optimized ECL “light sources” are actually converted into images. In practice, achieving high spatiotemporal resolution and quantitative reliability critically depends on the design of the imaging system itself, including excitation modes, optical and electronic readout, and device architectures. The following section therefore focuses on technologies and system platforms that capture, regulate, and digitize ECL signals, bridging the gap between molecular-level emission processes and high-information-content imaging.

Technologies and Systems for High Spatiotemporal-Resolution Imaging

The leap in ECL imaging capability relies not only on advances in emission mechanisms and materials but also on sustained progress in imaging techniques and system platforms. Converting stable, efficient emission “building blocks” into structured, metrological, and high-information-throughput images requires end-to-end engineering optimizationfrom imaging modes and resolvability to system integration. This section systematically elucidates the core imaging techniques, diversified system platforms, and their cooperative mechanisms that underpin the spatiotemporal–digital–intelligent paradigm, revealing how they jointly propel ECL from a laboratory tool toward broad application.

Imaging Modes and Resolvability

Imaging Modes: Contrast Mechanisms and Applications of SECL vs PECL

In practice, ECL imaging primarily adopts two complementary modes, whose contrast mechanisms afford flexible solutions for different scenarios:

PECL (positive ECL). Typically employing luminophore tags (e.g., Ru­(bpy)3 2+ derivatives, quantum dots) linked to targets such as cell-surface antigens or specific nucleic-acid sequences, PECL produces bright “positive” features on a dark background (Figure a). This direct and intuitive mode suits precise localization, distribution mapping, and relative quantification of specific biotargets. For example, antibody-conjugated Ru­(bpy)3 2+ nanoparticles have been used to image the cell-membrane protein EGFR, yielding clear maps of positive signal distributions.

SECL (shadow ECL). It is often implemented as a label-free approach using dissolved luminophores (e.g., Ru­(bpy)3 2+, [Ir­(sppy)3]3–) in solution. When entities such as cells, liposomes, or bacteria adhere to or approach the electrode surface, their physical presence blocks local electron transfer or reactant diffusion, casting “shadows” against a uniform bright ECL background (Figure b). SECL is particularly suited to interrogating interactions at bio–electrode interfaces (e.g., cell-adhesion morphology and area), adhesion-strength gradients and heterogeneity on differently functionalized surfaces (e.g., RGD, OEG-modified SNM/ITO), and dynamic processes such as the three-stage permeability evolution (“blocking → dilution → backflow”) of giant liposomes at electrode surfaces. Aligned collagen matrices induce pronounced contact guidance and anisotropic cell morphologies, offering a biomechanical reference frame for interpreting SECL-observed adhesion and migration patterns in tissue-engineering contexts. In a model-membrane system, parallel ECL and photoluminescence (PL) readouts synchronously tracked giant liposome leakage/permeability kinetics, providing an independent optical validation of SECL’s negative-contrast dynamics. Interface engineering with a chitosan spacer (to relieve steric blocking) and nano-TiO2 (to catalytically amplify luminol ECL) enabled direct single-cell ECL imaging on FTO and quantification of H2O2 release at the single-cell level with improved SNR. Beyond static adhesion mapping, label-free SECL visualized endothelial tubulogenesis on Matrigel, resolving cell–cell and cell–matrix adhesion contrasts during lumen formation as negative ECL prints. This expands SECL to morphogenetic processes without labels or external illumination. Ultrasensitive SECL recorded sharp negative images of single cells and even mitochondria with Ru­(bpy)3 2+ down to nM–pM (i.e., only hundreds of luminophore molecules in view), while preserving clear membrane contours and podes, thus pushing label-free ECL toward single-molecule microscopy. Label-free ECLM imaged T-cell immune synapses on VMSF/ITO electrodes, by the confinement of TEL (low Ru­(bpy)3 2+, POPSO coreactant), it quantified spreading area and recognition intensity to discriminate antigen-specific TCR–pMHC interactions at the single-cell level.

Resolution Limits and Strategies for Surpassing Them

Conventional ECL microscopy (ECLM) is constrained by the optical diffraction limit of 200 nm. Against this background, measuring the TEL using ECL microscopy (ECLM) is critical for interpreting ECL mechanisms and refining the major coreactant pathways. A limitation of the technique is that, ECLM is not suitable for measuring TEL values smaller than 200 nm.

Single-molecule localization super-resolution is a key route to overcome this barrier. The core idea is to activate sparsely distributed single emitters and fit their point-spread functions (PSFs) to localize their centroids with nanometer precision; accumulating many such localizations reconstructs images beyond the diffraction limit. This approach has achieved ECL imaging with 24 nm spatial resolution, which is an order-of-magnitude improvement over conventional ECLM.

Beyond optical strategies, the electrochemical control of the ECL emission zone can also sharpen spatial resolution. Increasing coreactant concentration (e.g., TPrA) thins the ECL reaction layer, shrinking the lateral emissive area and improving image sharpness. − In array imaging, this effectively contracts each nanoelectrode’s independent ECL domain, suppressing crosstalk and yielding cleaner spatial separation. Site-selective photothermal heating at the electrode–solution interface boosted Ru­(bpy)3 2+/TPrA ECL by up to 63× and advanced the trigger potential by 0.2 V, yielding 20.54× higher single-cell contrast and enabling cell-by-cell site-addressable imaging via a movable laser hot spot.

3D ECL Imaging and Control of the Emissive Layer

Three-dimensional imaging is crucial for faithfully capturing the true spatial organization of biological samples. In ECL, 3D capability is primarily realized by axial (z-axis) control of the emissive layer.

One practical approach is axial scanning of the focal plane. By tuning the anodic potential and cathodic pretreatment parameters, one can regulate the electrochemical flux and confine ECL emission to a thin layer parallel to the electrode (the ECL focal plane). The axial tuning range can reach 0–63 μm and, with more stable coreactants, can extend to the hundred-micrometer scale. The axial intensity profile is “bell-shaped,” with the peak located within the diffusion layer; over a 1 s window, the full width at half-maximum (fwhm) is measured to be 3.2 μm, indicating that homogeneous reactions between counterdiffusing precursors dominate axial resolution. Based on this, researchers have reconstructed single-cell 3D morphology, resolved heterogeneous molecular distributions in tissue sections (e.g., renal tubules), and captured morphological evolution during cell-polarity establishment.

The characteristics of the 3D emissive layer are tightly linked to the chosen reaction pathway. In an annihilation-type ECL, the TEL is constrained by electroactive intermediates generated at a single electrode that possess short diffusion lengths. In coreactant-type ECL, the TEL depends on the specific pathway adopted: for oxidation–reduction and low-oxidation-potential routes it is governed primarily by the diffusion length of TPrA+•, whereas for catalytic routes it is determined by the diffusion length of Ru­(bpy)3 3+. Mariani et al. prepared GC/Au microelectrode arrays via thermal nanoimprint lithography, showing Au’s broader ECL emitting layer than GC in the Ru­(bpy)3 2+/TPrA systemattributed to GC’s oxidative–reductive and Au’s catalytic pathways. Lower Ru­(bpy)3 2+ concentration barely altered GC’s profile but narrowed Au’s emitting layer (mechanism transition), validated by COMSOL simulations. This identifies electrode material and luminophore concentration as key tools for 3D ECL imaging emissive layer control. This mechanistic insight highlights the intrinsic connection between rational pathway selection and control over the thickness and shape of the 3D emissive layer.

Using sequential potential steps (O2 → H2O2 generation at −0.3 V, followed by L-012 oxidation at +0.7 V), ECL time-lapse imaging visualized oxygen-consumption-driven suppression around living cellular spheroids, enabling parallel, high-throughput readouts comparable in spatial detail to those of SECM but without raster scanning. This provides a practical route to dynamic respiratory mapping in 3D constructs.

In 3D tumor spheroids, ECL captured respiratory activity, while secreted species produced time-dependent negative prints on the electrode, jointly reporting metabolism and surface composition in a single readout. Such “activity + imprint” dual contrast substantiates ECL’s suitability for thick, multicellular constructs.

Diversified Development of Imaging System Platforms

The described imaging modes and resolvabilities above ultimately depend on concrete technological platforms. To meet needs ranging from fundamental research to field applications, ECL imaging systems have undergone significant diversification and specialization, ranging from high-end research platforms that push performance limits to portable devices that drive widespread adoption. To accommodate use cases from frontier fundamental research to on-site and immediate testing, ECL imaging system platforms have exhibited pronounced trends of diversification and specialization.

High-End Research Platforms: Pursuing Ultimate Performance and Deep Integration

These platforms aim for the highest detection sensitivity, spatiotemporal resolution, and compatibility with hyphenated techniques.

To improve the sensitivity of low-light detection, the core is the use of EMCCD (electron-multiplying CCD) or ICCD (intensified CCD) detectors (Figure a). This high-end system is typically built on photosensitive substrates like hematite, matched with DMD light modulators to realize spatiotemporal confinement of ECL reactions, further boosting imaging resolution down to submicron scale. EMCCDs employ on-chip charge multiplication to achieve near–single-photon sensitivity with very low noise, making them the workhorse for micro/nanoscale low-light ECL imaging (e.g., single-particle/single-molecule imaging). ,, Coupled with high-NA objectives (including water-immersion), they markedly improve light collection, reduce aberrations, and further enhance image quality.

Coupling with scanning electrochemical microscopy (SECM) provides another powerful configuration. Deep integration of ECLM with SECM has yielded “scanning optical ECL microscopy.” An ultramicroelectrode (UME) tip acts as a movable, localized ECL excitation source; raster scanning maps the chemical and electrochemical activity of the substrate at high resolution. Spatial resolution is governed by tip size (down to 100 nm) and tip–substrate separation, thereby circumventing the optical diffraction limit and making it well suited for probing electrode heterogeneity and interfacial kinetics. ,

Large-scale closed bipolar-electrode (cBPE) ultramicroelectrode arrays further extend the throughput. By integrating on the order of 105 (or more) UMEs on 1.0 cm2 chips, a high-throughput imaging platform can be constructed. An EMCCD records, with millisecond time resolution, the ECL intensity (I ECL)–potential (V) and/or current (I)–potential (V) relationships across the entire array synchronously, enabling crosstalk-free, parallel analysis of diffusion kinetics and electrochemical behavior for every microelectrode within a wide field of view.

Portable and Field-Deployable Platforms: Enabling Diffusion and Real-World Use

This direction carries ECL imaging beyond the lab into rapid diagnostics and environmental monitoring, with core attributes including low cost, high integration, ease of use, and robustness.

Smartphone-centric platforms leverage the phone’s camera, compute, display, and internal power to form highly integrated, portable systems. In a food-safety scenario, an ECL imaging sensor built on a single gold microsphere functionalized with lactate oxidase achieved visual lactate quantitation (0.1 mM to 50 mM; LOD 0.02 mM) using the L012–H2O2 route and a CCD-coupled microscope. This illustrates how imaging-mode ECL can translate directly into practical assays on real food samples. A typical setup comprises a smartphone (for image capture, processing, and display), a mini boost/regulator module (to supply excitation potentials), disposable screen-printed electrodes (SPEs), and a 3D-printed dark box. With second-scale exposures and high ISO, the phone camera reliably captures ECL signals; a dedicated app analyzes images, extracting RGB gray-level values from regions of interest for quantitative readout. ,, Such platforms have supported rapid imaging assays for targets, including NADH and melamine. A Zn-MOF@Ru emitter combined with an MIP selector delivered smartphone-read ECL quantitation with a quenching readout (LOD ≈ 0.29 μM), demonstrating that carrier/material design can directly translate to robust phone-based assays. A smartphone-based ECL imaging device (Figure b) with core components of disposable ITO single electrodes and RuSiO2–Au/Lu@MIL-NH2 emitters was further used to guide surface regulation via the “proportion of emission area (PEA)”; increasing PEA to 94% boosted sensing performance, shrinking the LOD by 4 orders of magnitude for a CNNS–MIP sensor. This establishes a concrete device-to-surface feedback loop for portable ECL optimization.

Raspberry-Pi-centric platforms use a Raspberry Pi single-board computer for system control and data processing; these integrate a custom PCB for potential excitation, a compact CCD/CMOS camera, a 3D-printed dark chamber, and a rechargeable battery to realize a programmable, portable ECL imaging system. This portable platform employs a Raspberry Pi 3B+ as the central control framework, coordinating electrochemical excitation, signal acquisition, and optical imaging via the GPIO/USB interfaces; a 3D-printed dark box (reinforced PLA) and a 5200 mAh battery (8.2 h runtime) provide end-to-end support for the system. GPIO-generated TTL signals precisely synchronize potential excitation with camera exposure, and a lightweight artificial intelligence algorithm (e.g., MobileNet for real-time valid/invalid frame filtering; improved U-Net for subpixel emitter segmentation) can be embedded for intelligent analysis. Overall, the platform is low-cost yet maintains sufficient performance for scalable and field-deployable ECL imaging. ,

High-Throughput and Parallel Platforms: Toward Multitarget Screening and Combinatorial Analysis

Bipolar-electrode (BPE) arrays provide a disruptive route to high-throughput parallel ECL imaging. A BPE is a “wireless” electrode: an unconnected conductor immersed in an electrolyte. When a sufficient electric field is applied between the two driving electrodes in solution, the BPE becomes polarized. This polarization generates anodic and cathodic poles, which simultaneously drive oxidation and reduction reactions at the two opposite ends of the BPE, thereby triggering the ECL without any physical wiring. This “wireless” property eliminates complex routing in large arrays, by enabling the miniaturized integration of tens of thousands of independent sensors, this platform is inherently designed for high-throughput applications, including parallel imaging (Figure c) and multianalyte screening. The core configuration adopts EC-ECL twin cells, where the cathodic and anodic ends of BPEs are separately placed in two chemically isolated cells, eliminating mutual interference between the electrocatalytic and ECL reaction systems while maximizing the performance of each unit.

Recent advances in BPE–ECL device design, driving modes, and associated high-throughput platforms can be summarized in four main aspects:(1)Low-voltage activation with closed configurations. Open BPEs typically require tens to hundreds of volts, limiting practicality. Closed BPEs, combined with microfluidic channel resistances as precise dividers, reduce the needed voltage to trigger ECL across entire arrays to 6–10 V. This low-voltage operation lowers power consumption, simplifies peripheral electronics, improves safety, and paves the way for portable integration. (2)Large-scale, low-cost manufacturing. To scale BPE deployment, advanced fiber drawing (thermal pulling) methods have been introduced. Continuous composite fibers with embedded microelectrodes can be drawn over hundreds of meters and then diced to mass-produce tens of thousands of uniform closed-BPE array devices, offering an engineering pathway toward low-cost, disposable ECL imaging chips. (3)Spatially selective excitation. Microfluidic design can finely shape electric-field distributions on-chip to selectively excite ECL in designated regions or chambers of a BPE array. This spatiotemporal gating enables patterned, localized imaging or surface-functionalization validation without physical contacts, expanding applications in bioprinting and gradient analyses. (4)Multidimensional encoding and microfluidic integration based on BPE platforms. Layering encoding strategies atop BPE hardware yields powerful multitarget parallel detection frameworks. One representative implementation is color–potential dual encoding: by pairing emitters of different colors (e.g., RuMOFs, red; L@ZrMOFs, blue) with distinct triggering scan potentials, multiple targets can be discriminated along two orthogonal axes on a single BPE platform. For instance, red ECL from RuMOFs under forward scans can report the mecA gene, whereas blue ECL from L@ZrMOFs under reverse scans reports ARGAMP; these dual-color signals can be directly captured by a smartphone camera. In addition, spatial encoding can be straightforwardly implemented, as each BPE unit in an array acts as an independent sensor; prepatterning with distinct probes enables multianalyte detection encoded by spatial position.

From Images to Insight: A Digital Pipeline for Data Analysis and Intelligent Interpretation

As outlined in Section 3, advances in ECL imaging systems now enable the routine acquisition of high-dimensional data sets I(x, y, t) that encode spatial distributions, temporal evolution, and intensity information across large fields of view. While such platforms greatly expand the observable spatiotemporal window, they also generate massive numbers of low-SNR frames, for which manual or ad hoc analysis is increasingly impractical, time-consuming, and prone to bias. As the field moves toward a spatiotemporal–digital–intelligent paradigm, there is thus a pressing need for systematic digital pipelines and AI-assisted strategies that can convert raw pixels into robust physicochemical descriptors of biological architecture, kinetic rates, and mass flux. This section examines digital readouts and event analytics, multidimensional encoding and multiplexing strategies, and the deep integration of artificial intelligence, thereby laying out an end-to-end transformation from raw data to biological insight.

Digital Readout and Event Analysis

From Analog Intensity to Digital Counting: Paradigm Shift and Statistical Advantages

The core philosophy of digital ECL imaging is to shift from continuous analogue-intensity measurements to discrete event counting. Conventional intensity integration averages emission over a region of interest; at low target abundance, weak signals are easily buried by background noise, and population averaging obscures rare events and spatial heterogeneity. By contrast, digital imaging treats each emitting entity (e.g., a single protein molecule labeled with an ECL probe, a single virion, or a highly loaded nanoemitter bead) as a resolvable event. Accurate identification and counting of these discrete events enabled absolute quantification of the target.

The most profound benefit of this shift is its inherent Poisson-governed quantitative performance.

When targets are resolved as discrete events, the uncertainty (standard deviation, σ) of the total event count N obeys σ=N . This simple relation yields two key consequences:

First, the signal-to-noise ratio becomes deterministic. The measurement SNR equals Nσ=NN=N , and thus SNR increases in a predictable manner with counts. For example, at N = 100, SNR = 10; at N = 10,000, SNR = 100. This clarity allows for precise assessment of measurement precision even at low counts, conferring strong robustness.

Second, the limits of detection became physically meaningful. Count-based LODs can be derived directly from the blank standard deviation (σblank) using conventional criteria (e.g., 3σ), with clear statistical meaning. This approach avoids the ambiguous thresholds that plague analogue-intensity methods under fluctuating backgrounds.

Accordingly, digital counting not only achieves very high sensitivity but also furnishes a fundamentally more reliable and robust quantitative basis for discriminating true low-abundance signals from stochastic background fluctuations. ,

In addition to these statistical advantages, digital analysis directly reports spatial nonuniformity of targets (e.g., wide cell-to-cell variations in a surface marker) that average-intensity measurements cannot reveal.

Key Processing Pipeline for Event Detection, Counting, and Quantification

Reliable digital readout rests on a mature image-processing workflow. First, raw ECL frames are preprocessed to boost SNR: flat-field correction is applied to remove pixel-response nonuniformity, dark-current subtraction is used to eliminate camera offsets, and denoising (e.g., Gaussian filtering) is performed to suppress stochastic noise. , After these steps, event identification and segmentation are performed.

In a basic implementation, thresholding is applied to flag suprathreshold pixel clusters as putative events. This foundational approach has enabled high-throughput quantification in microwell-array assays of single-cell dopamine (DA) secretion by cleanly separating signal from background.

Edge detection and connected-component analysis can then be used: Sobel, Canny, or Laplace operators to delineate emitter boundaries then label connected pixel regions as single events. In portable ECL pipelines, optimized edge detectors yield smoother boundaries, higher linearity (R 2), and faster processing versus classical operators (Figure ).

Multidimensional Encoding and Multiplexing

To simultaneously, rapidly, and information-richly analyze multiple targets in complex biological samples, ECL imaging has developed powerful multidimensional encoding strategies. By judiciously combining information along potential, color (spectrum), and space, ECL imaging makes a leap from single-analyte detection to true multiplexing.

Potential Encoding

Different ECL emitters possess distinct onset emission potentials or redox potentials (Figure a). By programming the potential waveform applied to the working electrode (e.g., staircase pulses or cyclic voltammetric scans), signals from different emitters can be temporally separated. For example, during a forward scan, one emitter is preferentially oxidized and lights up; during the reverse scan or at higher potentials, another emitter begins to emit. This time-division excitation discriminates species in the potential domain, even when their emission spectra overlap.

Color (Spectral) Encoding

Using emitters with different peak emission wavelengths is the most intuitive path to multiplexing. Band-pass filters positioned at each emitter’s peak, or spectral separation using a spectrometer or metasurface spectroscopic chips, enables spatial or spectral discrimination of signals from different targets (Figure b). Classical Ru­(bpy)3 2+ emits at 620 nm (orange-red), whereas certain quantum dots or iridium complexes can emit in blue or green.

Spatial Encoding

Through microfabrication, microelectrode arrays, microwell plates, or microfluidic channel networks are patterned on-chip, and recognition probes for different targets (e.g., antibodies or nucleic-acid probes) are immobilized at predesigned, known spatial addresses. Each location corresponds to a specific analyte; detecting whether ECL arises at that address achieves absolute position–based multianalyte detection. Representative Cases of Fused Multidimensional Encoding

A prime example is the color × potential dual encoding scheme (Figure d), in which Ning et al. demonstrated a highly integrated approach. Two high-performance nanoemitters were constructed: Ru-based metal–organic frameworks (RuMOFs) (red emission, 620 nm) and luminol-loaded Zr-based metal–organic frameworks (L@ZrMOFs) (blue emission, 450 nm). With a carefully designed scan program, the forward scan selectively enhances red ECL from RuMOFs (reporting the mecA gene), whereas the reverse scan selectively enhances blue ECL from L@ZrMOFs (reporting ARGAMP). Thus, within the same reaction medium and on the same platform, a single potential sweep resolves two targets along the color and potential dimensions. The strategy offers very high sensitivity (with limits of detection (LODs) of 0.2 fM and 0.16 fM) a wide linear range (10–3–102 picomolar (pM)), and red–green–blue (RGB) smartphone readout compatibility, highlighting strong translational potential.

In addition to color-potential dual encoding, spatial/potential encoding on large-scale arrays provides another powerful route to high-throughput analysis. As noted in section 3, large-scale closed BPE ultramicroelectrode arrays provide ideal hardware for high-throughput encoding. Each ultramicroelectrode (UME) functions as an independent sensing unit, naturally enabling spatial encoding. Further, by synchronizing potential pulses with electron-multiplying charge-coupled device (EMCCD) imaging, I ECL–V curves for all electrodes can be acquired in parallel, superimposing potential encoding onto spatial addresses. This allows millisecond-scale, simultaneous kinetic analysis across tens of thousands of sites, which make it well suited for high-throughput screening and kinetics studies.

Beyond these hardware-centric schemes, microfluidics × ECL × AI hybrid encoding offers an intelligent, fully integrated solution. Jia et al. integrates microfluidic spatial control, the specificity/amplification of CRISPR-Cas12a, ECL imaging, and AI-based data analysis into an intelligent multidimensional encoding framework. Microfluidics automates sample handling and spatially partitions reaction zones (spatial encoding); Cas12a confers mechanism-triggered specificity; ECL provides the optical readout; and AI algorithms process complex images for precise quantification, forming a complete, automated analysis loop from sample input to result output.

Empowerment by Artificial Intelligence

Digitalization and multidimensional encoding provide the “raw material” for precise quantification and a framework for multiplexing in ECL analysis. However, automatically and reliably extracting information from these massive, complex data and addressing intrinsic challenges such as low SNR and signal overlap require a more powerful toolkit: artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning.

Confronted with ECL-specific hurdles, for example, low photon flux, diffraction-broadened signals, spatially uneven backgrounds, and complex kinetics, AI, especially deep learning, has been embedded deeply into ECL workflows for image enhancement, automated recognition, and data interpretation, producing transformative gains.

Image Enhancement: From Low SNR to High-Quality Images

Before applying AI models, physics-based priors are commonly used for preprocessing, such as Gaussian blurring to suppress high-frequency noise, followed by adaptive thresholding (e.g., Otsu) for binarization to preliminarily extract valid emissive regions. For low-illumination frames, enhancement methods such as Low-Light Image Enhancement (LIME) can be used, together with color-cast correction.

A breakthrough in deep learning for denoising and super-resolved reconstruction is the deep enhanced ECL (DEECL) approach. In the Ru­(bpy)3 2+/TPrA system, DEECL involves training single-frame (DEECL-SF) and multiframe (DEECL-MF) neural networks to reconstruct high-quality images from very short exposures (0.02–0.2s) with poor SNR, approaching results otherwise requiring long exposures (e.g., an effective 4.96s or longer). By the structural similarity index (SSIM), DEECL improved the quality of 0.2 s images by 24.8×. Practically, 50 ms frames enhanced by DEECL enabled classification of L02 vs Hep3B cells with 85% accuracy (close to 90.5% for 10s exposures), implying 200× higher acquisition efficiency, it is highly favorable for live-cell dynamics (Figure ).

On-device optimization is crucial for portable hardware such as smartphones. Multiple enhancement pipelines were tested, and a specific workflow (e.g., a Photoshop-style pipeline) yielded the best linear quantification on phones. When combined with You Only Look Once v5 (YOLOv5) for object detection and red-green-blue/hue-saturation-value (RGB/HSV) regressors, phone-based analysis achieved R 2≈0.99 with a limit of detection (LOD) of 0.25 μM112.

Automatic Recognition and Tracking: Enabling High-Throughput, High-Precision Analysis

For regular arrays (e.g., microwell plates), Gaussian blurring followed by Otsu binarization achieves up to 98.7% accuracy for well localization and region-of-interest (ROI) segmentation. In the case of irregular targets such as bacteria or cells, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are employed to learn morphological features, enabling precise segmentation and classification. For example, a CNN was able to classify bacterial events in single-molecule ECL images with 90.5% accuracy, clearly surpassing k-nearest neighbors (KNN) and other classical algorithms.

To meet real-time needs on portable devices, a lightweight CNN, called the Fast ECL Judgment Network (FEJ-Net), was developed. Using fast convolutions and residual blocks, trained on 1000 images, it achieved 99.2% accuracy on 125 test images, making it well suited for real-time QC on Raspberry Pi or smartphones.

Purpose-built automation tools such as ECL Stat integrate machine learning (ML) modules to batch-process hundreds of ECL images. These tools extract features (e.g., mean intensity, event counts) and provide concentration predictions within minutes, greatly enhancing throughput (e.g., < 2 min). For dynamic phenomena, such as nanoparticle collision ECL, AI can track single-particle trajectories, distinguish spike (elastic collision) vs step (adhesion) modes, and reconstruct 3D motion.

Data Interpretation and Prediction: From Data to Insight and Decisions

The ultimate value of AI lies in discovering patterns difficult for humans to see and building predictive models.

One important area of application is optimized quantitation and uncertainty evaluation. AI can refine the entire quantitation pipeline. For instance, using MobileNet to classify acquired ECL frames as valid/invalid removes blurred or perturbed frames, narrows the intensity distribution, raises the calibration R 2 from 0.982 to 0.991, and lowers the LOD in melamine assays.

Another emerging direction is statistical modeling fused with physical mechanisms. A stochastic–particle simulation framework provides mechanistic understanding of ECL imaging noise: fluctuations of reactive species exhibit 1f3/2 characteristics, while photon counting approximates white noise, distinct behaviors in the frequency domain. This motivates a minimal information set (geometry, diffusion coefficients, rate constants, excited-state lifetimes, etc.) to standardize reporting for cross-lab comparability and reproducibility, strengthening rigor across the field.

AI also enables flux inversion and deep parameter extraction. By coupling physical models (e.g., extended spherical diffusion theory) with AI algorithms, deeper physicochemical parameters can be inverted from ECL images. In a glucose oxidase (GOx)–gel bead array, this framework inferred the H2O2 flux from each bead (400 fmol·s–1), in close agreement with numerical simulations, enabling scan-free, multitarget high-throughput quantitation.

Through the deep integration and synergy of digital readout, multidimensional encoding, and AI, ECL imaging establishes a complete pipeline for transforming data → information → insight. This not only elevates sensitivity, throughput, and reliability but also equips ECL to handle complex biological systems and decode dynamic heterogeneity, offering an unprecedentedly powerful tool for life and medical sciences.

Taken together, digital readout, multidimensional encoding, and AI have built an intelligent, closed-loop pathway from data to information, showcasing the exceptional capability to resolve microscale heterogeneity. On this solid foundation, we can clearly foresee both the challenges and vast opportunities ahead and sketch a roadmap for the next stage of ECL imaging development.

Conclusion and Outlook

This review systematically articulates the paradigm shift of ECL imaging, whose development trajectory has evolved from traditional average-intensity detection to a new stage of spatiotemporal–digital–intelligent analysis. This transformation is not the result of a single technological breakthrough but an inevitable outcome of the in-depth integration of mechanistic innovation, materials engineering, instrumentation, and data science. As discussed earlier, new mechanisms such as confined self-sensitization and built-in coreactants, combined with advanced materials including metal nanoclusters and perovskite quantum dots, have provided brighter, more stable, and more biocompatible “light sources” for ECL imaging; at the system level, smartphone/Raspberry Pi portable platforms, large-scale bipolar electrode arrays, and coupling with microfluidics/SECM have enabled a leap from laboratory to field applications and from single-point measurements to high-throughput parallel analysis; digital event counting, multidimensional encoding, and artificial intelligence-driven image analysis technologies have completed the intelligent transformation of ECL imaging from analog images to precise quantitative information. Today, ECL has evolved from a highly sensitive detection method to a powerful imaging platform capable of spatiotemporal analysis of single entities, such as single cells and single molecules.

As shown in Figure , the future development trajectory of ECL imaging focuses on four interconnected core dimensions, but these directions are currently constrained by unresolved key bottlenecks that not only slow down the progress of the field but also restrict its practical application in dynamic, translational microscale analysis.

Future development trends in electrochemiluminescence imaging technology.

The development potential of this field is still limited by these key gaps: in the pursuit of higher spatiotemporal resolution, existing super-resolution technologies have inherent trade-offs, making it difficult to capture dynamic biological events, while the insufficient precision of 3D axial control fails to resolve the layered characteristics of subcellular structures; in terms of in vivo and clinical translation, the biocompatibility issues of coreactants make long-term dynamic tracking difficult to achieve, material stability defects affect the reliability of longitudinal monitoring, and the high cost and scalability challenges of high-end detection platforms limit their applications to professional laboratories, making it difficult to extend to clinical or field scenarios; in the field of synthetic biology and closed-loop control, the insufficient reproducibility of confined nanoreactors undermines the credibility of quantitative analysis, and most existing systems separate imaging from intervention, failing to exert the unique potential of ECL in real-time feedback-driven applications; in the evolution of the data science-driven paradigm, artificial intelligence models lacking physical constraints lead to unreliable cross-scenario analysis results, while fragmented and nonstandardized data sets exacerbate the isolation of research and hinder the advancement of reproducible research.

Promoting the development of ECL imaging requires addressing these limitations at their roots and closely aligning with the directions outlined in Figure : for spatiotemporal resolution, it is necessary to develop new electrochemical control strategies for emitters and hybrid optical-ECL technologies to achieve a balance between speed and precision; for translational applications, it is advisable to construct systems based on endogenous coreactants, optimize material stability, and develop miniaturized, low-cost detection devices to expand the accessibility of the technology; in terms of closed-loop control, it is necessary to improve the reproducibility of nanoreactors through automated microfabrication technology and integrate imaging and intervention functions to build an integrated real-time feedback system; at the data science level, electrochemical and photophysical constraints should be embedded into the architecture of artificial intelligence models, and a community-shared standardized data set repository should be established to make analysis results more biologically meaningful and reproducible.

In summary, these limitations are not minor technical gapsthey are the core obstacles that prevent ECL imaging from moving beyond incremental laboratory advances to realize its potential. By focusing on these key and insufficiently addressed challenges as the core of future research, ECL imaging is expected to shift from incremental progress to a transformative, accessible tool for microscale biological analysis, playing an increasingly important role in biosensing, clinical diagnosis, and basic life science research.
