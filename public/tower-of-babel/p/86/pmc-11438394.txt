Continuous adoption of online healthcare platforms: an extension to the expectation confirmation model and network externalities
Authors: Yunqiu Zhang, Pei Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOnline healthcare platforms (OHPs) promote the accessibility of health services while integrating online and offline service delivery. Continuous adoption of these platforms can reduce the information asymmetry between patients and healthcare providers. However, existing study has rarely focused on how the network externalities of these platforms influence patients’ continuous adoption. Expectation Confirmation Model (ECM) explains the continuous behavior after initial adoption and reveal the factors influencing satisfaction and continuous adoption intention. Few studies have integrated network externalities with the ECM to understand patients’ continuous adoption of OHPs.ObjectiveThis study aims to explore what factors affect the continuous adoption of OHPs based on an extension to the ECM and network externalities. We propose a comprehensive model for the continuous adoption of OHPs by patients with chronic diseases. We extend the conceptual framework by incorporating constructs related to perceived value, habits, and switching costs.MethodsThis study utilized a web-based survey to collect data from participants in China. We selected a sample of individuals who had experience with OHPs using a random sampling method. This method ensured that participants was randomly chosen from a pool of patients without any regional bias. A total of 568 questionnaires were collected and 518 valid questionnaires were obtained. Structural equation modeling was applied to assess the relationships among the constructs. Hierarchical regression analysis was utilized to examine the moderating effects, while the mediating effects were investigated using the bootstrapping approach.ResultsDirect network externality had significant positive impacts on the confirmation (β = 0.232, p < 0.001) and perceived value (β = 0.167, p < 0.010). Cross network externality had significant positive impacts on confirmation (β = 0.307, p < 0.001) and perceived value (β = 0.120, p < 0.05). Indirect network externality had significant positive impacts on confirmation (β = 0.169, p < 0.010) and perceived value (β = 0.270, p < 0.001). Confirmation had a significant positive impact on perceived value (β = 0.205, p < 0.001) and satisfaction (β = 0.508, p < 0.001). In addition, satisfaction had a significant positive impact on continuous adoption intention (β = 0.579, p < 0.001). Continuous adoption intention had a significant positive impact on continuous adoption behavior (β = 0.547, p < 0.001). Confirmation and perceived value significantly mediate the relationship between network externalities and satisfaction. Moreover, both confirmation and perceived value enhance continuous adoption intention by positively influencing satisfaction, which serves as a mediator. Additionally, confirmation directly influences satisfaction through the mediating role of perceived value. Habits and switching costs positively moderate the relationship between continuous adoption intention and behavior.ConclusionsThis study contributes by expanding the incorporation of network externalities into the ECM. Results enrich the existing literature on the continuous adoption of professional online platforms.Keywords: Online healthcare platforms, Continuous adoption, Expectation confirmation model, Network externalities

Introduction

Information technologies (IT) have facilitated the sharing of health-related information online [1]. In China, a staggering 364 million individuals actively use internet healthcare services, with over 60 million engaging monthly. This marked increase in usage reflects a broader trend of people increasingly accessing health-related information and services online [2].

Online healthcare platforms (OHPs) significantly support physical hospitals by offering primary screening, triage, and chronic diseases management [3]. For patients with chronic conditions, OHPs enhance communication with healthcare professionals, improving service accessibility by merging online and offline delivery [4, 5]. OHPs cater to medical industry users, including physicians and patients, who rely on the Internet for information and services. These platforms enable patients to access information about physicians and medical conditions, while physicians use computers and mobile devices to deliver services [6, 7]. In addition, OHPs record interactions between physicians and patients [8].

Despite the potential to enhance healthcare delivery, the adoption and perceived value of OHPs have not met expectations. Many patients primarily use OHPs for appointment scheduling and information retrieval [9, 10]. Over time, the adoption of OHPs has seen a decline [11]. However, for patients with chronic diseases that has a key demographic requiring ongoing monitoring and regular communication with healthcare providers, OHPs are increasing used for comprehensive healthcare management. These platforms offer patients preliminary insights into their condition prior to professional consultations.

Previous studies indicate that patients dissatisfied with hospital diagnoses often turn to OHPs [12]. Factors like perceived ease of use and convenience positively influence OHPs adoption [13], while constraints related to time, finances, and training deter it [14]. Although extensive research has been conducted on online health communication [4, 15, 16], there is a lack of a theoretical framework addressing the continuous adoption of OHPs, particularly from the perspective of network externalities. Our focus on patients with chronic diseases aims to bridge this gap in the literature.

Network externalities enable users to gain additional utility from services without an equivalent value exchange [17]. These externalities are categorized into direct, cross, and indirect types, each driven by unique feedback mechanisms [18–20]. Beyond a certain user threshold, network expansion may reduce the utility for existing users [21]. In the context of OHPs, network externalities refer to the perceived value by patients with chronic diseases, influenced by the current user base. As OHPs are relatively new, patient adoption remains limited, and the network has not yet reached saturation.

Drawing on psychological theories, the Expectation Confirmation Model (ECM) has been extensively used to study the continuous adoption of information systems and technologies [22–24]. This model elucidates users’ intentions and behaviors post-initial adoption, identifying key drivers of satisfaction and continuous adoption intentions [25]. The continuous adoption of OHPs is a dynamic process, and the ECM offers insights into the psychological and behavioral aspects during this phase. Despite its potential, few studies have combined ECM with platform network effects to explore factors and mechanisms affecting the continuous adoption of OHPs. Our research fills this gap by integrating perceived value from network externalities into the ECM.

Perceived value significantly influences patients’ decisions to continuously adopt OHPs. For patients with chronic diseases, the perceived value provided by OHPs encourages their continuous adoption. By enhancing patients’ perceived value, physicians and OHPs managers can improve healthcare services, thereby boosting patient satisfaction and promoting continuous adoption. There is a strong correlation between perceived value and network externalities [26]; higher perceived value from OHPs often leads to positive network externalities. In addition, switching costs, the perceived expenses that discourage users from changing products or technologies, are critical [27]. Previous research has examined switching costs within platform adoption but has not fully explored their impact on the continuous adoption among patients with chronic diseases. This study addresses this gap by analyzing how switching costs affect continuous adoption behaviors on OHPs. At this stage, the primary OHPs is retaining users, prompting this study to explore three research questions:

RQ1:

What factors affect patients’ continuous adoption of OHPs based on an extension to the ECM and network externalities?

RQ2:

What are the moderating roles of switching costs and habits on patients’ continuous adoption of OHPs?

RQ3:

What are the mediating roles of confirmation, perceived value, and satisfaction on patients’ continuous adoption of OHPs?

This study aims to explores how network externalities affect the continuous adoption of OHPs by patients with chronic diseases, using an extended framework of the ECM that includes network externalities. We broaden the conceptual framework to encompass constructs such as perceived value, habits, and switching costs. Our findings indicate that confirmation and perceived value are positively impacted by three types of network externalities, leading to increased satisfaction and continuous adoption as patients’ expectations are confirmed. In addition, confirmation and perceived value significantly mediate the relationship between network externalities and satisfactions, and also mediate the impact of satisfaction on continuous adoption intentions. Specifically, confirmation enhances satisfaction through the mediating role of perceived value.

This study contributes to the existing literature in three significant ways. First, this study addresses a research gap by exploring factors that influence patients’ continuous adoption of OHPs and the limited application of network externalities in the context of OHPs. Our findings develop nuanced models of continuous adoption that differentiate the effects of direct, indirect, and cross-network externalities. Second, by applying the ECM in a novel context, this study investigates how network externalities influence confirmation processes to understand continuous adoption among patients with chronic diseases. This extension of the ECM deepens insights specific to OHPs. Third, we introduce new insights by examining how switching costs and habits motivate continuous adoption, analyzing their moderating effects on the relationship between adoption intentions and behaviors. This study fills a notable gap and clarifies the roles of switching costs and habits in sustaining patients’ adoption of OHPs.

The structure of this study is outlined as follows. First, this study commences with a comprehensive literature review encompassing network externality, OHPs, perceived value, switching costs, and theoretical background. Second, this study delineates the hypotheses and provides an in-depth elucidation of the causal relationships among the constructs. Third, the methods and results are explicated. Finally, this study engages in a discussion of the major findings, elucidates the theoretical and practical implications.

Literature review

Network externalities

Externalities occur when the economic actions of primary actor impact other entities’ welfare outside market mechanisms [28]. In IT networks, new users often choose networks with larger user bases because the value of the network increases as it grows, benefiting both existing and potential users [19]. The value of a product escalates with its consumers scale [19, 29]. Shankar and Bayus highlighted the interplay between network size and usage intensity [30]. Joining and expanding a network positively enhances the individual’s perceived value from participation [31, 32].

Scholars distinguish network externalities into two dimensions: direct and indirect [33]. Direct network externalities arise as more individuals use similar products or services [34, 35]. Indirect network externalities emerge from increases in complementary or compatible goods and services, enhancing overall system value [36, 37]. Specifically, the presence of direct network externalities is linked to the growing user base of similar social networking services [38], and studies have shown that these externalities positively affect individual perceived value and satisfaction [39, 40]. Furthermore, indirect network externalities boost user loyalty on mobile platforms, as increased user numbers enhance complementarity and functionality, creating added value [34, 41].

Network externality theory pertains to two-side markets [42]. Armstrong detailed the dynamics among platform users, introducing the concept of cross-network externalities where the user count on both sides of a platform enhances benefits for each group [20]. Such externalities are center to platform economics, turning participants into valuable assets and initial network size into a source of competitive advantage [43]. Unlike traditional markets, OHPs function as two-sided markets exhibiting cross-network externalities between different user groups [44]. Platforms must utilize these externalities among their bilateral users to foster growth [45]. Song et al. [46] explored how platform updates affect user participation and the strength of cross-network externalities. Anderson et al. [47] investigated the impact of cross-network externalities on platform performance investments, finding that strong cross-network externalities could deter performance enhancements. Dou et al. [48] assessed analyzed the effects of cross-network externalities on the utility for both buyers and sellers on platforms, including the impact on investments in value-added services.

Existing studies have explored how network externalities influence individual adoption behaviors. For example, Kim et al. [49] demonstrated that network externalities significantly affect consumers’ intentions to continue using short messaging services. Lin and Lu [50] incorporated network externalities into incentive theory, verifying their effect on continuous adoption intentions for social networking sites. In electronic banking, Kauffman et al. [32] identified network externalities as crucial in adoption decisions, revealing that larger network sizes can both deter and promote early adoption depending on market conditions. However, few studies have simultaneously addressed direct, indirect, and cross-network externalities as three distinct dimensions and their specific impacts on the continuous adoption of OHPs by patients with chronic diseases.

Online healthcare platforms

OHPs represent a blend of applications and digital technologies that deliver specialized, comprehensive healthcare solution [51]. Research on OHPs can be categorized into three streams. First, definitions of OHPs emphasize their role as information system-based forums highlighted. For example, Abedin et al. [52] described OHPs as platforms where users exchange health information, experiences, and emotions, offering informational and emotional support. Yan et al. [53] described these platforms as virtual communities providing social support and healthcare information. Demiris [54] considered them as social platforms for chronic patients that facilitate healthcare consultation and shared experiences. McCarroll et al. [55] highlighted the impact of social media structure on the dissemination of personal health information, underscoring its importance in healthcare communication.

Second, the literature places emphasis on the adoption behaviors of OHPs. For example, Peng et al. [56] analyzed the design and content of health apps to identify elements that either promote or deter user engagement. McMullan [12] reviewed how patients use the Internet for health information, noting that they consult online resources both before and after medical visits to prepare and address dissatisfaction with the information received during in-person consultations [12]. Martinez et al. [14] studied online information usage among healthcare workers, finding that perceived ease of use and convenience positively influence adoption, whereas resource constraints such as time, financial, and training limitations hinder it. Mandhana et al. [57] discovered that patients’ attitudes towards online platforms moderate the impact of perceived usefulness on post-adoption behavior of patient portals.

Third, the literature in the field of OHPs delves into patients’ engagements. For example, Mirzaei and Esmaeilzadeh [58] utilized channel expansion and social exchange theories to study patient engagement in online healthcare communities, finding that the willingness to share information and health status significantly influenced engagement levels. Shirazi et al. [59] examined social and technical factors that affect users’ intentions to co-create value in healthcare, emphasizing social support, government IT infrastructure, and perceived control of information as precursors to value co-creation. Yang et al. [60] highlighted health consciousness and information competence as key drivers of patient empowerment in OHPs. Savoli et al. [61] argued that the effectiveness of chronic disease self-management is impacted by self-management information systems, which influence patient outcomes. Moreover, OHPs play a vital role in allocating health resources effectively and reducing disparities between urban and rural healthcare access [62], becoming critical channels for accessing health information and support [2].

The emergence of OHPs has revolutionized the traditional healthcare model by significantly improving communication and interaction between physicians and patients. This study examines physician-patient communication platforms that are integrated with conventional healthcare services, highlighting how OHPs serve both physicians and patients by providing access to healthcare information and services.

Perceived value and switching costs

The literature on perceived value is divided into three main aspects. First, perceived value is understood as arising from a user’s experiential process, encompassing functional, emotional, social, conditional, and epistemic values of products [63, 64]. Second, the dimensions of perceived value have been elaborated, with Sweeney and Soutar [65] identifying four key dimensions—functional price value, functional quality value, emotional value, and social value. Flint et al. [66] proposed a three-level model of perceived value related to product attributes, outcomes, and purposes, while Petrick [67] defined five dimensions: quality, emotional response, monetary price, behavioral price, and reputation. Third, the literature indicates inconsistencies in the factors associated with perceived value. Kotler and Levy [68] linked user satisfaction to perceived value, Dodds et al. [69] viewed it as a key predictor of user behavior, and Zhao et al. [70] highlighted social identity as influential in patients’ engagement in value co-creation within online health communities. Cognitive value assists patients in understanding health-related information [71]. This study focuses on functional, emotional, social, and cognitive values, reflecting the ways OHPs enhance user engagement. OHPs provide functional value through access to professional consultations, emotional value through interactive communications and shared experiences among patients, and social value by connecting users to a broader healthcare and social community.

Switching costs have become a significant focus within management, e-commerce, and marketing [72, 73]. Based on the diffusion of innovation theory, Bölen [74] found that comparative advantages and financial switching costs significantly impact the decision to switch from traditional wristwatches to smartwatches, serving as effective retention strategies [74]. In addition, Zhou et al. [75] constructed a model for the sustained use of mobile instant messaging that integrates direct and indirect network externalities with switching costs.

Furthermore, Zou et al. [76] introduced a regret perspective in post-adoptive technology evaluations, discovering that post-adoption regret—driven by outcomes and processes—is closely linked to user switching, while satisfaction tends to promote retention. Yen [77] defined switching costs as the time and psychological burdens from uncertainty when users face new online platforms. In the context of e-commerce, Kaur and Soch [78] categorized switching costs into procedural, financial, and relational types. Procedural costs include risks, installation, and learning expenses; financial costs cover losses like foregone membership benefits and discounts; and relational costs involve the potential loss of valuable partnerships. Dressler and Weiergraeber [79] quantified the impact of switching costs and awareness on consumer inertia in retail electricity markets, using a novel dataset from Belgium to show that limited awareness and switching costs hinder efficient decision-making among electricity consumers.

In addition, Edward and Sahadev [80] highlighted that user confusion negatively impacts mobile service retention by affecting satisfaction and increasing switching costs, which are critical in creating barriers to switching and influencing continuous adoption [75]. Switching costs, encompassing financial, temporal, and psychological expenditures, along with perceived barriers and potential disruptions in physician-patient relationships, impose significant burdens on users considering platform transitions. This study investigates the moderating role of switching costs on the relationship between users’ intentions and their actual behaviors, drawing from existing literature [76–78, 81].

Expectation confirmation model (ECM)

Oliver [82] developed the Expectation Confirmation Theory (ECT) to evaluate user satisfaction by comparing pre-purchase expectations with actual usage outcomes, where pre-adoption expectations are shaped by media and others’ opinions, and post-adoption expectations by personal experiences. ECT primarily addresses the impact of pre-adoption expectations without considering post-adoption expectations, which are crucial for continuous adoption intentions [83]. Bhattacherjee [84] expanded on this by introducing the Expectation Confirmation Model of Information System Continuance (ECM-ISC), which integrates perceived usefulness from both ECT and the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM). In ECM-ISC, confirmation influences continuous use intentions indirectly through its effects on perceived usefulness and satisfaction, with perceived usefulness directly enhancing user satisfaction [85]. This model emphasizes the role of continuous adoption intention and behavior post-adoption.

Adoption intention is necessary but not sufficient for sustained IT behavior. Limayem et al. [86] and Bhattacherjee et al. [87] enhanced the Expectation Confirmation Model of Information System Continuance (ECM-ISC) by incorporating elements like IT self-efficacy and facilitating conditions into the Expectation Confirmation Model of IT Continuance (ECM-ITC). In this framework, perceived usefulness is seen as a long-term belief based on prior experiences of usefulness post-adoption, while satisfaction encompasses short-term emotional responses from past experiences. The transition from intention to behavior often necessitates resource utilization [87]. The ECM has been used to predict continuous adoption behaviors and understand user discontinuation after initial adoption [22, 83, 88, 89]. To adapt the ECM to the continuous adoption in OHPs, this study introduces a theoretical extension incorporating network externality, perceived value, switching costs, and habits to enhance the model’s explanatory power, focusing on the determinants of patients’ continuous adoption of OHPs.

Hypotheses development

Network externality and confirmation, perceived value

This study identifies three types of network externalities: direct, cross-network, and indirect [35, 90]. Katz and Shapiro [18] argued that network externalities significantly enhance users’ perceived value. Zhou et al. [75] found that direct network externalities, particularly the expanded network of chronic patients on the same platform, significantly increase perceived usefulness and value. Similarly, direct network externalities significantly enhance user experience on e-commerce platforms [91] and social networking services, where a larger user base improves security and overall experience [92]. In OHPs, the growing number of patients enhances social participation and information exchange, thus increasing perceived value. This value represents a balance between the perceived benefits and costs of using OHPs. The strength of direct network externalities in OHPs grows with the patient network, improving access to health information and services. Additionally, the alignment and enhancement of support services like integrated self-service registration, payment devices, and health management tools further bolster adoption, confirmation, and perceived value [33, 50].

The confirmation of expectation measures how patients’ anticipations about OHPs align with their actual experiences. Patients initially join OHPs with expectations shaped by perceived benefits and the experiences of existing users. Direct network externalities, where the value of a service increases with user numbers, play a crucial role. In environments like social networks and collaborative platforms, the utility and richness of the network are enhanced as more users join [93, 94]. Research indicates that direct network externalities significantly boost users’ perceived value by improving user experiences and expanding network benefits [34, 95]. As the user base grows, new patients often find their expectations met or surpassed, benefiting from increased content, community support, enhanced features, and a more dynamic platform. This growth not only enhances the technical quality of the service but also provides social proof, thereby increasing its perceived value. New users view the network as more valuable, attracted by the large and engaged user base. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

H1a:

Direct network externality positively affects the confirmation of expectations.

H1b:

Direct network externality positively affects perceived value.

Cross-network externalities arise from interactions between different user groups on a platform, enhancing the overall experience by increasing connectivity between physicians and patients and diversifying available services [96]. This diversity not only enriches new patients’ experiences but also often exceeds their initial expectations, thereby positively influencing their confirmation of expectations. In two-sided markets, as the variety and volume of services expand, patients gain access to more comprehensive information and services. Simultaneously, a growing patient base further enhances the experiences of new users, reinforcing their expectations.

Cross-network externalities enrich the platform by integrating complementary services, broadening the availability of healthcare resources and choices for patients [96]. This expansion not only enhances the utility of the platform but also increases patient satisfaction [97]. As interactions between patients and physicians intensify, patients gain significant benefits from the physician group, substantially elevating their perceived value of OHPs. These externalities thus heighten patients’ awareness of the advantages offered by OHPs, further boosting their perceived value. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

H2a:

Cross network externality positively affects the confirmation of expectations.

H2b:

Cross network externality positively affects perceived value.

Indirect network externalities enhance the value of OHPs by adding complementary healthcare services. As the user base expands, these externalities allow the platform to enhance its functionalities and services, thus boosting its appeal and user experience. When the platform consistently meets patient needs with suitable services, it confirms their expectations, improving their perception of the platform’s value and satisfaction. Molina-Castillo et al. [98] discussed the interaction between direct and indirect network externalities, and Li et al. [91] showed that indirect network externalities positively affect perceived value.

Indirect network externalities encourage OHPs to expand their range of applications and enhance compatibility, significantly enhancing perceived value among chronic patients. As more relevant physicians and services become available on the platform, it enriches the variety of choices and quality of experience for patients, thus increasing their perceived value of the platform. The provision of effective medical services assures patients that the platform can consistently meet their evolving needs, thereby deepening their reliance on and valuation of it. Consequently, the continuous expansion of services and functionalities through indirect network externalities effectively bolsters patients’ confirmation of expectations and perceived value, reinforcing their continuous engagement with OHPs. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

H3a:

Indirect network externality positively affects the confirmation of expectations.

H3b:

Indirect network externality positively affect perceived value.

Confirmation, perceived value, satisfaction and continuous adoption intention

Confirmation refers to the degree to which users’ initial expectations about a service or product are met [84]. When these expectations are confirmed, users perceive greater value in the service because it surpasses their anticipated benefits. According to the ECM, confirmation acts as a subjective measure of alignment between perceived usefulness after adopting OHPs and initial expectations. Research across various sectors like e-commerce and online services has demonstrated that confirmation positively affects perceived value [49, 99]. However, in the context of OHPs, perceived usefulness alone does not sufficiently encourage patients to continue using the services. Therefore, this study adopts the concept of perceived value, which includes broader and more substantial dimensions [67, 100], in place of perceived usefulness. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

H4:

Confirmation has a positive impact on perceived value.

According to the ECM, when a service meets users’ expectations, they experience increased satisfaction due to the alignment between anticipated and actual service performance [82]. Empirical research confirms that this alignment positively affects user satisfaction across diverse fields such as information systems and online platforms [84, 101]. Specifically, when expectations are consistently met, chronic patients tend to perceive higher value and experience greater satisfaction. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

H5:

Confirmation has a positive impact on satisfaction.

Perceived value reflects users’ evaluation of benefits relative to costs, and in high-risk settings like healthcare, it significantly impacts patient satisfaction, more so than perceived usefulness [102, 103]. The relationship between perceived value and satisfaction is documented in marketing and consumer behavior literature [104, 105]. Studies consistently show that high perceived value leads to increased satisfaction among users [106]. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

H6:

Perceived value has a positive impact on satisfaction.

Satisfaction is a determinant of users’ intention to continue using technologies. The ECM suggests that satisfied users are likely to develop a positive attitude towards the service and exhibit higher intentions for continued use [84]. Research in information systems has confirmed a strong positive link between user satisfaction and ongoing usage intentions [107]. In markets with network transparency, it’s crucial for platform operators to enhance perceived value to retain users and encourage their continuous engagement [108]. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

H7:

Satisfaction has a positive impact on the intention of continuous adoption.

Habits and switching costs

Behavioral intention exerts a favorable influence on actual behavior [109]. Empirical studies by Limayem et al. [86] and Bhattacherjee et al. [87] have confirmed that continuous adoption intention positively impacts the continuous adoption behavior of information systems. Furthermore,, research has consistently shown that habits play a crucial role in sustaining adoption behaviors over time, as prolonged and repeated use leads users to engage in actions driven more by subconscious routines than by complex decision-making processes [110, 111].

Existing research has only minimally explored the impact of subconscious behaviors on adoption patterns. Limayem and Hirt [112] have emphasized the necessity of considering both conscious (intention) and subconscious (habits) aspects when investigating adoption behavior. The initial adoption of OHPs face perceived risks and self-efficacy challenges [113, 114]. Over time, as engagement with the platform continues, these behaviors can become internalized and habitual. Thus, the continuous adoption of OHPs may be driven not only by deliberate intentions but also by established user habits. Thus, this study proposes:

H8:

Continuous adoption intention has a positive impact on the continuous adoption behavior.

H9:

Habits play a moderating role in the relationship between continuous adoption intention and behavior.

Switching costs, which include monetary, temporal, cognitive, and psychological barriers, play a critical role in user retention by imposing constraints when considering a change in service providers [80, 115]. Research across sectors like real estate, e-commerce, and mobile services shows that switching costs significantly enhance platform loyalty and retention [77, 80, 116]. In the context of OHPs, high switching costs create a cognitive lock-in effect, crucially facilitating the transition from mere adoption intentions to actual continuous usage behaviors [117]. Thus, this study proposes:

H10:

Switching costs play a moderating role in the relationship between continuance adoption intention and behavior.

Methods

Study design

This study targeted long-term users of OHPs as its primary respondents. The recruitment process began with identifying a sample of potential respondents who had significant experience using OHPs. To achieve this, we collaborated with a tumor hospital, where we initially identified the initial sample of 47 respondents to participate in a preliminary test of the survey questionnaire. For the main study, recruitment was expanded to a larger pool of respondents. Invitations to participate in the survey were disseminated through various online channels, including social media, healthcare forums, and email newsletters associated with healthcare services. The method ensured a diverse and representative sample of OHPs users, encompassing various demographics and geographic regions. Feedback from this preliminary test was used to refine the survey instrument, ensuring clarity and relevance. The revised questionnaire was then reviewed by three specialists from a leading Chinese hospital, whose input enhanced the reliability and validity of the items.

Measurement scales

We created questionnaire items based on the context of OHPs in Chinese to guarantee the validity and reliability of the scale. We adjusted the scales by consulting analogous scales from earlier research. A 7-point Likert scale with at least three items was used to measure the constructs. Responses are scored on a scale of 1 to 7, with 1 denoting “strongly disagree” and 7 denoting“strongly agree.” We used the same approach for translation as previous studies [118]. First, the original texts were translated into Chinese. Second, the Chinese texts were then retranslated into English by two researchers. Third, we carried out a pilot study to enhance participants’ understanding of the questionnaires. We distributed the surveys to 5 professionals of online healthcare services to make sure they were complete and correct. The questionnaires were given to 10 students to examine for readability and format. Fourth, we made revisions to the questionnaires based on input from the pilot study. Appendix A provided specifics regarding the measurement items.

Data collection

The survey was accessible to participants via a link provided in the recruitment materials. The questionnaires comprised demographic profiles and scales designed to capture the respondents’ experiences, attitudes, and perceptions related to their adoptions of OHPs. The final version of the questionnaire was distributed to the respondent pool. The online format facilitated efficient data collection, allowing respondents to complete the survey at their convenience. Data collection remained open for a predetermined period, ensuring ample time for participants to respond. Informed consent was obtained from all respondents before they commenced the survey. They were provided with detailed information about the study, including its purpose, procedures, and the voluntary nature of their participation.

This study employed an online survey methodology to collect data from respondents. The questionnaire and scales were developed and hosted on the Sojump platform (www.wjx.cn), a widely used tool for online surveys in China. The collected data formed the basis for the analysis conducted in this study. We analyzed the surveys data using SPSS 26.0 and Mplus 8.3. First, descriptive statistics were conducted to assess sample characteristics. Second, we examined an examination on correlation of constructs and implemented procedures to assess common method bias. Third, we tested the hypotheses using Structural Equation Modeling (SEM), which is an analytical approach for path analysis and factor evaluation, contributing to the validity and reliability of the findings.

Respondents were assured of the confidentiality and anonymity of their responses, and measures were implemented to protect their privacy throughout the data collection and analysis phases. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the affiliated academic institution. The IRB reviewed the study’s objectives, methodology, and potential risks to participants, ensuring compliance with ethical standards. A total of 568 questionnaires were collected. After screening, 16 questionnaires with incomplete responses or identical answers for all items were discarded. 34 questionnaires were excluded due to excessively short or long response times, indicating potential inaccuracy. Finally, 518 valid questionnaires were obtained, resulting in an effective response rate of 91.20%. The basic characteristics of the sample are summarized in Table 1.

The gender ratio was nearly balanced, with a slight predominance of males over females. Regarding age distribution, the respondents who continued to use OHPs were in the age groups of 25–35 years and 36–49 years, accounting for 46.1% and 31.7%, respectively. The next largest group was those under 24 years of age, comprising 11.8% of the sample. Discussion

Major findings

This study has validated most of the hypotheses. First, the three dimensions of network externalities positively influence confirmation and perceived value. Among these, cross-network externality has a greater positive impact on confirmation compared to direct and indirect network externalities. However, indirect network externality exerts the strongest influence on perceived value. During the continuous adoption of OHPs, an increase in the number of patients (physicians) strengthens cross network externality, leading to improved physicians (patients) experience and enhanced confirmation. The physicians’ usage of OHPs exerts a significant cross-network externality on patients’ continuous adoption of these platforms. The patients’ continuous adoption of OHPs would attract physicians’ services to these platforms, exemplifying the strong cross-network externality at play. The presence of complementary, compatible, and supportive elements in OHPs, along with increased indirect network externality, significantly enhances perceived value. Patients’ continuous adoptions of OHPs have been highly recognized and influential [122].

Second, patients’ confirmation of expectation positively impacts perceived value and satisfaction with OHPs. Increased user satisfaction leads to stronger intentions for continued adoption, therefore promoting adoption behavior. However, the impact of perceived value on satisfaction is not significant. Due to the relatively complex healthcare decision-making process among patients on OHPs, they do not simply change their attitude toward the platform based on perceived value [123]. In other words, although patients view OHPs as useful as these platforms provide better access to healthcare resources, make it easier to get services, and improve social value perception. However, these value does not translate into patients’ satisfaction with OHPs. This may be related to dissatisfaction with the service attitude of doctors during the consultation process, the responsiveness of the platform system, and the attitudes of the final consultations obtained after the visit.

Third, this study highlights habits and switching costs significantly moderate the positive relationship between continuous adoption intention and behaviors. Specifically, as habits and switching costs associated with OHPs increase, they strengthen the transformation of patients’ continuous adoption intentions into actual behavior. The more ingrained a patient’s habits are with using OHPs, and the higher the switching costs, the more likely the patient is to follow through on their intention to continuously adopt these platforms. By addressing these uncertainties and guiding patients through personalized recommendations and interactions, OHPs should encourage patients to form habits. Over time, as patients become accustomed to relying on these platforms for their health demands, their continuous adoption intentions are likely to translate into continuous adoption behaviors. Moreover, high switching costs, which may include the effort and inconvenience of switching to a different platform, further reinforce this relationship. When patients perceive significant barriers to switching, such as losing access to their medical history or familiar user interface, they are more inclined to remain with their current OHPs, thereby turning their continuous adoption intentions into behaviors.

Theoretical implications

The theoretical implications are multifaceted contribute to the existing literature on the continuous adoption of OHPs. First, by extending the ECM, this study enhances the understanding of the factors influencing patients’ continuous adoption. The traditional ECM, which focuses on confirmation, satisfaction, and intention to adopt, is expanded in this study to incorporate network externalities and perceived value specific to the context of OHPs. This extension of the ECM is particularly important as it allows for a comprehensive examination of the factors that drive continuous adoption.

Second, by considering three categories of network externalities, this study enriches the literature on how different externalities influence patient behavior. This differentiation highlights the varied ways in which network externalities can affect patients’ continuous adoptions of OHPs. Our findings inform the development of nuanced models of continuous adoption that account for the distinct effects of direct, indirect, and cross-network externalities. For example, cross-network externality has a greater positive impact on confirmation compared to direct and indirect network externalities. Indirect network externality exerts the strongest influence on perceived value. During the continuous adoption of OHPs, an increase in the number of users strengthens cross network externality, leading to improved user experience and enhanced confirmation.

Third, this study explores the moderating role of habits and switching costs in the transformation of patients’ continuous adoption intentions into actual behaviors. By investigating these moderating effects, the study proposes new research questions and hypotheses that extend the understanding of the continuous adoption of OHPs. The theoretical implications of our findings suggest that future research should continue to explore the dynamic interplay between habits, perceived value, and switching costs to understand continuous adoption behavior. The study extends existing theories related to the ECM and network externalities and offers a robust framework for examining the continuous adoption of OHPs.

Practical implications

This study offers valuable practical implications for managers of OHPs. First, during the continuous adoption phase, network externalities play a crucial role in bolstering patients’ confirmation and perceived value. An increase in the number of healthcare professionals on these platforms and the strengthening of cross-network externality can significantly enhance the confirmation of expectations among patients, which elevating perceived value and satisfaction. For managers, this means focusing on strategies that amplify network externalities. Increasing the number of healthcare professionals on the platform provides resources and options for patients. This can be achieved by creating incentives for healthcare professionals to join and actively participate in the platform, such as offering professional development opportunities, exposure to a broad patient base, and streamlined service processes.

Second, it underscores the importance of prioritizing the augmentation of switching costs and the cultivation of patient habits, as these factors positively moderate the relationship between patients’ continuous adoption intentions and actual adoption behavior. By increasing switching costs, including through the integration of exclusive features, personalized medical histories, and loyalty programs, managers can make it challenging for patients to switch to alternative platforms, thereby enhancing patient retention. In addition, the cultivation of habits can be achieved by encouraging regular use through features like reminders, follow-up notifications, and engaging user interfaces that make the platform an integral part of the patient’s routine.

Third, this study highlights the critical role of network externalities in sustaining patient continuous adoption. Managers of OHPs should focus on strategies that stimulate network externalities, such as facilitating patient-to-patient and patient-to-physician interactions, promoting user-generated content, and leveraging social proof to enhance the perceived value of OHPs. In addition, managers of OHPs can create a dedicated Q&A knowledge base and provide personalized healthcare services on a regular basis. OHPs should improve switching costs by implementing real-name registration and maintaining online physician-patient relationships. A stronger physician-patient relationship discourages users from switching to other platforms and encourages continuous adoption.

Limitations and future research directions

This study is subject to several limitations and offers avenues for future research. First, regarding the underlying mechanisms that influence chronic patients’ continuous adoption of OHPs, this study has constructed a model that encompasses factors from both platform and patient perspectives. Nevertheless, real-life scenarios involve numerous factors that can impact patients’ continuous adoption behavior, extending beyond the constructs selected in this study. Future research should consider incorporating additional factors, such as relevant healthcare policies and the quality of online and offline services.

Second, this study is limited by its reliance on cross sectional data collected through questionnaires. The questionnaire method may introduce self-report bias, with respondents’ answers affecting the accuracy of the data. The sample selection may also restrict the generalizability of the findings. Future studies should consider expanding the sample size and employing longitudinal research methods to reveal the dynamic process of patient behavior change. By incorporating experimental approaches, case analyses, and econometric models, some studies can provide robust empirical evidence to support the findings in the future.

Third, the results of this study are limited to the factors influencing patients’ continuous adoption of OHPs. Empirical investigations into the factors affecting continuous adoption by patients and physicians in OHPs catering to specific medical conditions should be conducted in the future. Some studies may propose new hypotheses that build upon our findings and suggest avenues for future theoretical and empirical work.

Conclusion

This study elucidates the factors influencing the continuous adoption of OHPs by patients with chronic disease by integrating the network externalities and the ECM. The aim is to identify the key factors affecting continuous adoption, extending the ECM framework to include constructs related to perceived value, habits, and switching costs. By combining insights from network externality and healthcare literature, this study provides a detailed understanding of how network externalities impact patients’ confirmation and perceived value in OHPs. The findings reveals that three dimensions of network externalities, direct, indirect, and cross-network, positively influence confirmation and perceived value. Cross-network externality has a more significant impact on confirmation than direct and indirect network externalities. For patients with chronic disease, confirmation positively influences perceived value and satisfaction, which in turn affect their intention and behavior of continuous adoption. For platform managers, it is crucial to enhance patients’ confirmation of expectations to elevate satisfaction. In addition, habits and switching costs strengthen the positive relationship between patients’ intentions for continuous adoption and their actual behaviors. This study contributions to the literature by expanding the ECM to incorporate network externalities, enriching the understanding of the continuous adoption of OHPs.
