Loss of α-cell GRK2 modulates glucagon response and supports cardiac function
Authors: Jonathan Snyder, Lilly Underwood, Chun-sun Jiang, Victoria Kaml, Snekha Rajasekaran, Lily Xie, C Reid Dotson, Priscila Y Sato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPancreatic α-cells secrete glucagon to maintain glucose homeostasis, yet the molecular mechanisms regulating hormone release are understudied. G-protein coupled receptor kinases (GRKs) regulate receptor desensitization; however, their role in α-cells remains unknown. Here, we generated an inducible α-cell–specific GRK2 knockout (αGRK2KO) mouse model to investigate the role of GRK2 in islet physiology, systemic metabolism, and cardiac function. Loss of GRK2 in α-cells reduced islet GRK2 protein by ∼20%, consistent with α-cell islet abundance, and produced negligible alterations in glucose tolerance without affecting insulin secretion. Notably, αGRK2KO mice had altered fast/fed glucagon responses, reduced adiposity, and lower body weight. Despite minimal effects on systemic glucose handling, αGRK2KO animals exhibited improved cardiac function, characterized by enhanced ejection fraction and fractional shortening, without signs of hypertrophy. High-fat, high-sucrose diet feeding abated these changes, underscoring the diet-dependent impact of α-cell GRK2. Together, these findings identify GRK2 as a previously unrecognized regulator of α-cell glucagon secretion that influences systemic energy balance and cardiac performance through endocrine crosstalk. This work establishes a framework for α-cell G protein-coupled receptor regulation and highlights GRK2 as a potential therapeutic node linking islet function, metabolism, and the heart.Significance StatementThis study explores the role of GRK2 in α-cell biology as a regulator of glucagon dynamics and cardiac function, thereby establishing a new endocrine link between islet signaling and cardiac biology.Key words: GRK2, Islet, Inter-organ crosstalk, Glucagon, Heart, Metabolism

Introduction

Pancreatic α-cells secrete glucagon, a peptide hormone that maintains glucose homeostasis by stimulating hepatic glucose output during fasting and hypoglycemia.1, 2, 3, 4 Glucagon secretion is regulated by intrinsic electrical activity, paracrine signals, and systemic factors. Dysregulated α-cell activity contributes to impaired counter-regulation in hypoglycemia and to inappropriate hyperglucagonemia in diabetes.5 However, compared to the extensive knowledge of β-cells, the molecular mechanisms underlying α-cell G-protein coupled receptor (GPCR) signaling and hormone release are less well understood.

GPCR activity is regulated by GPCR kinases (GRKs), which phosphorylate activated receptors for desensitization and internalization.6 Among the GRK family, GRK2 is widely present and serves as a key regulator of receptor responsiveness in various metabolic tissues. Various GPCRs are known to be regulated by GRK2 and alter α-cell function, including FF4R, somatostatin, adrenergic, and incretin receptors.7, 8, 9, 10 In pancreatic islets, GRK2 has been linked to β-cell function, affecting glucose-stimulated Ca2+ responses and insulin secretion.11,12 However, no study so far has directly investigated the role of GRK2 in α-cells. It remains unknown whether GRK2 is present at functional levels in α-cells or how this regulation impacts cellular signaling and glucagon release. Addressing this gap is crucial for developing a mechanistic understanding of α-cell signaling and how receptor regulation influences islet hormonal coordination.

The physiological reach of glucagon goes beyond glucose metabolism.13,14 Circulating glucagon directly impacts the heart.13, 14, 15, 16, 17 Activation of these receptors triggers adenylyl cyclase, increases cAMP, and improves calcium handling, leading to positive inotropic and chronotropic effects.18 Importantly, the effects of glucagon in the heart are independent of β-adrenergic receptor (βAR) activation, as evident in studies showing positive ionotropic effects even in the presence of βAR antagonists.19,20 These effects have been recognized in clinical practice, with glucagon historically being used as a cardiotonic agent in cases of β-blocker and calcium channel blocker overdoses.13 More recently, long-term changes in glucagon signaling have been linked to cardiac remodeling and metabolism,16,21 suggesting that variations in α-cell glucagon output and length of exposure may influence cardiovascular function in both adaptive and maladaptive ways.

Cardiac GRK2 upregulation contributes to the progression of heart failure (HF).6 Given current GRK2 inhibitory strategies for treating HF, the intersection of α-cell GRK2, glucagon secretion, and cardiac function is clinically relevant. By regulating glucagon release, α-cell GRK2 may impact systemic glucose control and influence cardiac performance through endocrine crosstalk. In this study, we contribute to filling this knowledge gap by investigating the role of GRK2 in pancreatic α-cells. We propose that GRK2 participates in the sigmoidal glucagon response to fast/feed cycles, thereby modulating glucagon release, shaping systemic glucose regulation, and influencing cardiac function via acute and rapid changes in circulating glucagon levels.

Materials and methods

Generation of inducible alpha cell GRK2KO (αGRK2KO)

All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Drexel University College of Medicine and the University of Alabama at Birmingham, and all methods were performed under the relevant guidelines and regulations. The αGRK2KO mouse model was constructed by crossing the Tre-Cre containing the Pre-Proglucagon (PPG)-rtTA sequence22 (kind gift of Philipp Scherer) with the GRK2 Flox line. Notably, the PPG mouse model targets islet α-cells, as previously reported.22 All mice were accordingly genotyped before enlisting in the study. Regular and high-fat, high-sucrose (HFHS) diets were formulated as published.11 Both diets contained doxycycline and were initiated between 6 and 8 weeks of age. For regular chow studies, 4–5 weeks of diet exposure defined the study window, unless otherwise noted. For HFHS studies, mice were terminated after a 12-weeks diet regimen.

Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), insulin tolerance test (ITT), and insulin/glucagon measurements

OGTT was performed as described in the published study.11 Briefly, glucose was given via oral gavage and blood glucose was measured at 0-, 15-, 30-, 60-, and 120-minute time points. Serum was collected for insulin measurements. For ITTs, mice were fasted for 2 hours and given an intraperitoneal injection of insulin (1 unit/kg), and glucose levels were measured at 0, 15, 30, 60, and 120 minutes. Glucagon measurements were performed in overnight fasted and nonfasted animals by ELISA (Crystal Chem).

Isolation of primary islets and adipose quantification

Islet isolation was performed as previously described.11 Briefly, the common bile duct was ligated prior to animal sacrifice by exsanguination via cardiac dissection. The pancreas was perfused with collagenase and digested. Islets were hand-picked using a Leica S9i Stereoscope and cultured for 1 day in RPMI 1640 with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 3 mM glucose, and 2 mM glutamine. Adipose tissue was isolated as per published work.11

Tissue preparation and immunofluorescence

Organs were fixed in 10% formalin overnight, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 10 μm. Antigen retrieval was performed as published.11 Primary antibody incubation was performed overnight, followed by 3 washes in PBS with 1% Tween. Secondary antibody incubation was for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by 3 washes in PBS with 1% Tween and mounting medium containing DAPI. Imaging was performed using a Hamamatsu ORCA-Spark camera equipped on an Olympus BX43F microscope. Quantification was performed using ImageJ. The following antibodies were used: insulin (Abcam ab7842), glucagon (Abcam ab92517), anti-Cy3 (Jackson Immuno Research 706-165-148), anti-AlexaFluor 488 (Abcam ab150077). Insulin and glucagon mark β- and α-cells, respectively.

Western blots and quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Western blots were performed as previously described.11 Isolated islets were lysed in RIPA buffer. Protein quantification was performed using BCA (Pierce) and 30 μg of protein was loaded onto Tris–glycine gels. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C, washed 3 times in PBS with 1% Tween-20, incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature, and washed 3 more times. Imaging was performed using a LI-COR Odyssey Fc and analysis was performed off-line using Image Studio. The following primary antibodies were used: Sigma GRK2 G0296 and β-actin Santa Cruz 47778. qRT-PCR measurements were performed as published.11

Echocardiography

Echocardiography was performed as previously published using the Vevo 3100 system.11 Mice were anesthetized using 2% isoflurane, and measurements were obtained at the level of the papillary muscles. Quantification was performed using VisualSonics software.

Statistical methods

All data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using Prism (GraphPad Software version 10). Statistical analyses were performed using Student’s t test, two-sided unpaired t test with Welch’s correction (comparisons between two groups), or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc tests (for three or more groups). For OGTTs and ITTs, area under the curve was analyzed when comparing two conditions. A P value of less than .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Defining the role of α-cell GRK2 in physiology

Animals were exposed to a chow diet containing doxycycline (Fig. 1A). Confirmation of α-cell GRK2 knockout reveals approximately 20% reduction in GRK2 protein (Fig. 1, B and C), which corresponds to the murine content of α-cells in islets of Langerhans.23,24 This agrees with decreased islet GRK2 at the message level (Fig. 1D). Terminal body weight analysis shows that αGRK2KO animals gain less weight than controls (Fig. 1E). OGTT experiments showed glucose handling of αGRK2KO comparable to control animals (Fig. 1F). Insulin levels measured during the OGTT are comparable to those of control animals (Fig. 1G). ITT results show comparable responses between the groups, with a trend toward increased glucose levels in the αGRK2KO at 120 minutes after challenge (Fig. 1H). Terminal adipose tissue analysis shows no statistical difference in visceral adipose tissue (VAT); however, in both males and females, there was a trend toward a decrease in VAT in the αGRK2KO mice (Fig. 1I). Subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) was statistically less in the αGRK2KO compared to controls in male mice (Fig. 1J). No statistical difference was observed in Brown adipose tissue (BAT; Fig. 1K).

Defining circulating and islet glucagon levels

Islet quantification revealed comparable islet areas in control and αGRK2KO mice (Fig. 2, A and B). Glucagon-positive islet areas and glucagon fluorescent intensities were comparable between the groups (Fig. 2, C and D). Insulin fluorescence was also comparable between the groups (Fig. 2E). Circulating glucagon levels were measured under both fed and fasting conditions. Fasting led to a statistically significant increase in circulating glucagon levels in the control mice. The αGRK2KO animals did not exhibit a statistically significant increase in the fasted state (Fig. 2F). Notably, in the αGRK2KO mice, circulating glucagon levels in the fed state trended higher than in control mice, and fasting did not elicit a statistically significant increase in circulating glucagon levels in the αGRK2KO animals (Fig. 2F).

Cardiac function is improved with loss of α-cell GRK2

Blinded myocardial analysis was performed using in vivo echocardiography.11 Cardiac function measured by ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS) was higher in αGRK2KO animals when compared to control mice (Fig. 3, A–C). Heart rate was comparable between the groups during these measurements (Fig. 3D). Gross hypertrophy was not observed in these hearts in males or females (Fig. 3E).

A HFHS regimen mitigates glucagon differences elicited by α-cell GRK2

Figure 4A shows the experimental paradigm for HFHS regimen implemented. These experiments were conducted in age-matched male mice. Glucose and insulin levels during OGTTs were comparable between control and GRK2KO animals (Fig. 4, B and C). ITTs revealed comparable insulin resistance in both control and αGRK2KO animals (Fig. 4D). Terminal analysis between groups revealed no changes in the rate of weight gain or adiposity (Fig. 4, E–H).

Discussion

Pancreatic islets of Langerhans are essential for maintaining systemic metabolic homeostasis by sensing fluctuations in nutrient availability and adjusting hormone secretion. This regulation depends on the reciprocal actions of α- and β-cells through the secretion of glucagon and insulin, respectively. We previously demonstrated that whole-pancreas knockout of GRK2 results in spontaneous islet decompensation and systemic metabolic impairment.12 Interestingly, inducible and specific knockout of GRK2 in β-cells, bypassing developmental effects, confers cardiac protection, but only under high-fat diet conditions.11 The aforementioned study highlighted the pivotal role that β-cells and insulin secretion play in contexts where substrate availability is in excess. Building on these findings and considering the well-established functional crosstalk between α- and β-cells within the islet,25,26 the present study examines the complementary role of GRK2 in α-cells. Alpha cells are the second most abundant endocrine cell type in both mouse and human islets.23,24,27 During fasting conditions, when substrate availability is scarce, α-cells secrete glucagon, a peptide hormone that primarily targets the liver to mobilize glycogen and maintain circulating glucose levels.28

Given our findings on GRK2 in β-cells, this study focused on the other arm of substrate sensing, in α-cells. We specifically and inducibly knocked down GRK2 in α-cells (Fig. 1, B–D). Alpha cells compose approximately 20% of the murine islet.23,24 Hence, knocking down GRK2 equated to approximately 20% decrease in islet GRK2 protein levels (Fig. 1C). No statistically significant changes were observed in glucose levels nor insulin secretion during OGTTs (Fig. 1, F and G). ITTs resulted in comparable insulin tolerance between the groups, with an apparent faster rebound in glucose levels at 120 minutes postinjection in the αGRK2KO animals (Fig. 1H). This could potentially be due to a persistent liver stimulation by glucagon levels in αGRK2KO animals during hypoglycemic stages, as suggested by Fig. 2F. Although speculative at this time, these mechanisms warrant further clarification in future studies.

Glucagon promotes lipolysis and increases energy expenditure.4,29 This is partly due to the rise in cAMP, activation of protein kinase A, and activation of lipolytic enzymes.4 Glucagon is known to rise during fasting, as first described in humans over 50 years ago.30 In control mice, there is a significant change in glucagon levels between the fed and fasted states. This rapid delta is not observed in the GRK2KO animals (Fig. 2F). Instead, a slight but consistent elevation in glucagon is seen in the fed state compared to control mice. We suggest that this persistent level of glucagon, at least in part, contributes to limiting rapid changes in glucagon levels during fasting states. Furthermore, we propose that this lack of sigmoidal changes in prandial glucagon results in reduced adipose tissue (Fig. 1, I and J) and lower body weight (Fig. 1E), particularly in males. Notably, in age-matched mice, susceptibility to weight gain differs between males and females.31 In fact, ovariectomized females gain weight similar to males.31 This difference in weight gain is consistent with adipose tissue differences between males and females.32,33 Although sex differences are essential to be investigated, this study focused on male mice, where adipose tissue changes produced the most significant alterations. Further studies will interrogate sex-specific differences.

Adult cardiomyocytes may express the glucagon receptor (GCGR),34,35 and preclinical studies have demonstrated the importance of cardiomyocyte GCGR in regulating cardiac fatty acid utilization.16 Notably, glucagon can also directly modulate cardiac performance by activating GCGR, leading to increased heart rate and contractility.35,36 This is clinically evident, as glucagon has historically been used as a cardiotonic agent in settings such as β-blocker and calcium channel blocker overdose.37 The αGRK2KO mice have elevated EF and FS compared to control mice (Fig. 3, A and C), consistent with various studies demonstrating that glucagon may increase myocardial cardiac output in healthy animals.35,38

To increase the availability of metabolic substrates and reduce glucagon (α-cell) activity, we subjected mice to an HFHS regimen. Because insulin is a physiological suppressor of glucagon secretion,25 HFHS resulted in glucose and insulin tolerance curves that were similar between the groups, eliminating α-cell glucagon changes caused by GRK2 (Fig. 4). These data further emphasize the role of GRK2 in α-cells during conditions of substrate scarcity. It is essential to note that the animals in this study are healthy, meaning they are free from any cardiac injury. Notably, cardiomyocyte-specific GCGR knockout mice exhibit improved survival and reduced hypertrophy following myocardial infarction,16 and glucagon receptor antagonist therapies have been proposed for HF with preserved EF.15 At the same time, it is unclear whether there are any developmental compensatory changes in the cardiomyocyte-specific GCGR or the level of islet activity in the HF with preserved EF models in the aforementioned studies above. Future studies will delineate how short- versus long-term glucagon exposure may differentially alter cardiac physiology. Thus, further research is crucial to elucidate the specific mechanisms of action of glucagon in the heart, thereby enabling a more precise understanding of which clinical pathologies could benefit from glucagon and/or glucagon receptor antagonist therapeutic strategies.

Conclusions and future studies

Alpha cells are a hub for glucagon production and secretion. Glucagon is a peptide hormone essential for fasting conditions, that is, when substrate availability is limited. The metabolic status of many cardiac diseases is similar to that of fasting conditions, that is, when the heart is in a state of substrate deprivation. This work supports the hypothesis that GRK2 participates in α-cell function and in glucagon responses during fast-feed cycles. Key to future research is identifying specific GPCRs within the α-cell that are regulated by GRK2 and determining whether the decreased response during fasting impacts liver biology and how this in turn may participate in the progression of specific cardiac diseases.
