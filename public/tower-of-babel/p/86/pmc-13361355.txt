A FRET Aptasensor Based on N-CQDs for Detection of Cardiac Troponin I in Acute Myocardial Infarction Diagnosis
Authors: Min Yuan, Yijing Tao, Zhisong Wang, Qianwen Yao, Qiuqian Gu, Ping Zou, Leng Han, Juan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, posing a significant burden on global healthcare systems.1–7 Rapid, accurate, and early diagnosis of AMI is critical for timely clinical intervention, such as reperfusion therapy, which can substantially improve patient outcomes and reduce mortality rates. Cardiac troponin I (cTnI) is currently recognized as the gold-standard biomarker for AMI due to its high cardiac specificity, excellent sensitivity, and early appearance in circulation after myocardial injury.8–14 Despite its clinical utility, conventional detection methods for cTnI, including chemiluminescence immunoassays and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA), often rely on sophisticated instrumentation, lengthy assay procedures, and enzymatic amplification steps. These limitations hinder their deployment in point‑of‑care (POC) or resource-limited settings, where rapid decision-making is essential.15–18

In recent years, aptamer-based biosensors (aptasensors) have emerged as promising alternatives to antibody-based assays. Aptamers are single-stranded DNA or RNA oligonucleotides that can bind to target molecules with high affinity and specificity. Compared with antibodies, aptamers offer several advantages, including facile chemical synthesis, low batch-to-batch variation, high thermal stability, and ease of functionalization with various nanomaterials.19,20 Among various transduction techniques, fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) has gained particular attention for the development of “turn‑on” sensing platforms. In a typical FRET system, energy transfer from a donor to an acceptor occurs when they are in close proximity (typically 1–10 nm). Target-induced separation of the donor-acceptor pair leads to the recovery of donor fluorescence, enabling sensitive and specific detection.21,22

Carbon quantum dots (CQDs), a class of zero-dimensional carbon-based nanomaterials, have shown great potential as FRET donors owing to their tunable photoluminescence, high photostability, good water dispersibility, and low toxicity. Doping with heteroatoms, particularly nitrogen, has been demonstrated to further enhance the fluorescence quantum yield and modulate the electronic properties of CQDs, giving rise to nitrogen-doped CQDs (N-CQDs) with superior optical performance.23–26 On the acceptor side, Black Hole Quenchers (BHQ) are non-fluorescent dark quenchers that effectively suppress background signals through broad absorption spectra matched to common fluorophores, thereby improving the signal-to-noise ratio of FRET-based assays.27

Aptamer-based strand displacement provides a simple yet efficient recognition mechanism that eliminates the need for enzymatic amplification or washing steps.28–32 Among the Tro-family aptamers (Tro1-Tro6) identified for cTnI recognition, Tro4 exhibits the highest binding affinity (Kd = 270 pM) and structural stability, making it the most widely used cTnI aptamer in biosensing applications.33–35 In this design, a short complementary strand is partially hybridized with the aptamer; target binding triggers a conformational change in the aptamer, displacing the complementary strand and generating a measurable signal. However, to the best of our knowledge, a FRET aptasensor that integrates N-CQDs as the energy donor, BHQ as the acceptor, and cTnI-specific strand displacement for the non-enzymatic detection of cTnI has not yet been reported.

Recent advances in nanomaterial-based biomedical applications have been reported in various fields.36,37 Herein, we report the design and construction of a FRET-based aptasensor using N-CQDs (donor) and BHQ (acceptor) for the non-enzymatic, turn‑on detection of cardiac troponin I. The proposed aptasensor exhibits high sensitivity, excellent selectivity toward cTnI over other potential interfering biomolecules, and satisfactory analytical performance in complex biological matrices such as human serum. Unlike many previously reported enzyme-free cTnI biosensors that either require multiple washing steps, rely on additional signal amplification strategies (eg., hybridization chain reaction or catalytic hairpin assembly), or employ complex electrode modifications, our design integrates three advantageous features into a single platform: (i) truly wash-free operation due to the FRET turn-on mechanism; (ii) high signal-to-noise ratio enabled by the N-CQDs/BHQ donor-acceptor pair; and (iii) direct target-triggered strand displacement without auxiliary amplification. Given its simplicity and enzyme-free operation, this N-CQD-BHQ FRET aptasensor represents a useful strategy for cTnI detection in laboratory settings, with potential for future adaptation to point‑of‑care diagnosis after further optimization of assay speed and portability.

Method

Preparation of N-CQDs-Apt Conjugate

N-CQDs (1 mg/mL) were dispersed in MES buffer (0.1 M, pH 6.0). EDC (20 mM) and NHS (10 mM) were added and stirred at room temperature for 30 min to activate carboxyl groups. Tro4 aptamer (5 μM, 5′‑NH2) was then added at a molar ratio of N-CQDs:Tro4-Apt = 1:2, and the mixture was stirred at 4 °C for 12 h in the dark. The resulting N-CQDs-Apt conjugate was purified by ultrafiltration (3 kDa).

FRET Assembly and Optimization

The FRET system was assembled by mixing N-CQDs-Apt (50 nM) with various concentrations of T1-BHQ (0–200 nM) in PBS (10 mM, pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl). After incubation at 37 °C for 40 min, fluorescence was measured at 450 nm. Optimization of the molar ratio, N-CQDs volume, temperature, and incubation time was performed by one‑factor‑at‑a‑time experiments using FL as the criterion.

cTnI Detection

Under optimized conditions, the sensor (N-CQDs-Apt 50 nM + T1-BHQ 100 nM) was incubated with various concentrations of cTnI (0, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 10.0 ng/mL) for 40 min at 37 °C. Fluorescence intensity at 450 nm was recorded. The limit of detection (LOD) was calculated as 3σ/k, where σ is the standard deviation of the blank, and k is the slope of the calibration curve.

Selectivity Study

Interference was assessed using cTnT (5 ng/mL), BNP (5 ng/m), albumin (5 ng/m), glucose (5 mM), creatinine (5 mM), Na⁺ (5 mM), K⁺ (5 mM), Mg2⁺ (5 mM), and Ca2⁺ (5 mM). The mixture group contained all interferents plus 5 ng/mL cTnI.

Serum Analysis

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Changshu No.1 People’s Hospital (approval No. X202127) and the Ethics Committee of The First Hospital of Hunan University of Chinese Medicine (approval No. HN-LL-KY-2024-025-01). Human blood samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to separate the serum. The supernatant (serum) was carefully collected and stored at −20 °C until use. Prior to analysis, the frozen serum was thawed at 4 °C and then centrifuged again at 10,000 rpm for 5 min to remove any precipitated proteins or particulate matter. The clarified serum was subsequently diluted 50‑fold with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 10 mM, pH 7.4) to minimize matrix effects for this initial feasibility study. We acknowledge that such dilution substantially alters the physiological environment, and the results obtained under these conditions do not directly represent actual clinical scenarios. Future work will focus on reducing the dilution factor (eg., 5-fold or 10-fold) or developing strategies to enable detection in undiluted serum. The diluted serum was filtered through a 0.22 µm syringe filter to further eliminate potential interfering aggregates. The FRET aptasensor assay was then performed under identical conditions as described for the buffer system (eg., same aptamer concentration, incubation time, and fluorescence measurement parameters).

Results

Design Principle of the FRET Aptasensor

The working principle of the proposed FRET-based aptasensor for cTnI detection is illustrated in Scheme 1. The sensor relies on two key elements: (i) efficient fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) between a donor (N-CQDs) and an acceptor (BHQ), and (ii) target-induced strand displacement for signal turn-on.

Schematic illustration of the FRET aptasensor for cTnI detection. The sensor relies on target-induced strand displacement to restore N-CQDs fluorescence upon cTnI binding.

Construction of the FRET probe: Nitrogen-doped carbon quantum dots (N-CQDs) were covalently conjugated with a cTnI-specific aptamer (Apt, Tro4) via amide bonds formed between the carboxyl groups (-COOH) on the N-CQDs surface and the amino group (-NH2) at the 5′ end of Tro4-Apt (Table S1). A complementary DNA strand (T1) was labeled at its 3′ end with Black Hole Quencher-1 (BHQ-1) (Table S1). In the absence of cTnI, the N-CQDs-Apt conjugate hybridizes with T1-BHQ through partial complementary base pairing, bringing the N-CQDs (donor) and BHQ (acceptor) into close proximity. Under these conditions, excitation of N-CQDs at 380 nm leads to energy transfer to BHQ via the FRET mechanism, resulting in effective quenching of the donor fluorescence (turn-off state).

Target recognition and signal restoration: When cTnI is introduced into the system, the aptamer Tro4 recognizes and binds to cTnI with high specificity and affinity. The formation of the aptamer-target complex induces a conformational change that triggers the displacement of the T1-BHQ strand from the hybridized duplex, a process known as target-induced strand displacement. As a result, BHQ is separated away from N-CQDs, breaking the FRET pair and restoring the intrinsic fluorescence of N-CQDs (turn-on state). The recovered fluorescence intensity at 450 nm is directly proportional to the concentration of cTnI, enabling quantitative detection.

This design offers several distinctive features. First, the use of N-CQDs as the FRET donor provides bright, stable, and excitation-tunable fluorescence. Second, BHQ-1 is a non-fluorescent acceptor, eliminating background interference from the acceptor itself. Third, the strand displacement mechanism operates without any enzyme, simplifying the assay and reducing cost. Fourth, the partial complementary design of Apt and T1 minimizes nonspecific hybridization, reducing background signal and improving signal-to-noise ratio.

In summary, the proposed aptasensor translates the recognition event (cTnI binding) into a measurable fluorescence increase through a simple, enzyme-free, and rapid FRET switching mechanism, making it highly suitable for point-of-care diagnosis of acute myocardial infarction.

Characterization of N-CQDs

The morphology and microstructure of the prepared nitrogen-doped carbon quantum dots (N-CQDs) were first characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). As shown in Figures 1A–C, the N-CQDs exhibited a uniform spherical morphology with excellent monodispersity, revealing an average diameter of approximately 22 nm. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis (Figure 1D) confirmed the presence of carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and oxygen (O) elements, respectively. The successful incorporation of nitrogen provides direct evidence for effective nitrogen doping, which is known to enhance the fluorescence quantum yield and modulate the electronic properties of CQDs. It is worth noting that while the average particle size of our N-CQDs is approximately 22 nm-larger than the typical size range (<10 nm) for conventional carbon quantum dots-the strong blue fluorescence (quantum yield ~18%) is not primarily derived from classical quantum confinement effects. Instead, as will be discussed in Discussion, the photoluminescence of N-CQDs arises predominantly from nitrogen-doping-induced surface/edge states and the formation of isolated sp2 domains that limit the effective conjugation length.

(D) EDS spectrum of N-CQDs, showing the representative elements C, N, and O. The optical properties of the N-CQDs were systematically investigated. The fluorescence excitation spectrum, monitored at an emission wavelength of 450 nm, exhibited a maximum excitation peak at 380 nm (Figure 1E). Therefore, 380 nm was selected as the optimal excitation wavelength for all subsequent FRET experiments. The UV-Vis absorption spectrum of the N-CQDs (Figure 1F) displayed a characteristic absorption peak at approximately 340 nm, which is typically attributed to the n-π* transition of C=O or C=N chromophoric groups on the surface of the N-CQDs. To further elucidate the surface chemical composition and nitrogen doping configuration, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed. The XPS survey spectrum (Figure 1G) confirmed the presence of C 1s (284.8 eV), N 1s (399.5 eV), and O 1s (531.5 eV) peaks, consistent with the EDS results. The coexistence of these three nitrogen forms is characteristic of successful nitrogen doping, with pyridinic and pyrrolic N being particularly effective at introducing active sites and enhancing the photoluminescence properties of the N-CQDs.

Conjugation of Aptamer and FRET Assembly

Having established the successful synthesis of N-CQDs with favorable optical properties, we next proceeded to conjugate the cTnI-specific aptamer (Tro4) to the N-CQDs surface via carbodiimide-mediated amide bond formation. Successful conjugation was first verified by UV-Vis absorption spectroscopy. As shown in Figure S1 (Supporting Information), the pristine N-CQDs exhibited a broad absorption peak centered at 340 nm. In contrast, the N-CQDs-Apt conjugate displayed a new absorption shoulder at approximately 260 nm, which is characteristic of the DNA backbone (aromatic bases), thereby confirming successful covalent attachment of the aptamer to the N-CQDs. The conjugation efficiency was quantitatively determined by measuring the absorbance at 260 nm of unbound aptamers in the supernatant after the conjugation reaction. As shown in Figure S2, the conjugation efficiency was calculated to be 70.0% ± 1.5% (n = 3).

Figure 2 provides a comprehensive summary of the stepwise nanoprobe fabrication and its characterization. Given the intended application in physiological fluids, we first assessed the colloidal stability of the N-CQDs in serum-containing media. Figure 2A and B demonstrate that the N-CQDs remained well-dispersed without noticeable aggregation over 10 hours, even in the presence of 40% serum, indicating excellent colloidal stability suitable for biological and point-of-care applications. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to monitor the chemical modifications during conjugation. As shown in Figure 2C, the pristine N-CQDs exhibited characteristic absorption bands corresponding to C=O (carboxyl, 1720 cm−1) groups. After aptamer conjugation, the N-CQDs-Apt spectrum revealed new prominent peaks at approximately 1540 cm−1, confirming the formation of amide bonds between the carboxyl groups of the N-CQDs and the amino group-functionalized Tro4 aptamer.

Zeta potential measurements (Figure 2D) provided further evidence for successful stepwise surface modification. The pristine N-CQDs exhibited a zeta potential of −21.5 mV, attributable to the presence of surface carboxyl and hydroxyl groups. Following aptamer conjugation (N-CQDs-Apt), the zeta potential became more negative (−24.6 mV), consistent with the additional negative charges contributed by the phosphate backbone of the immobilized DNA aptamer. After hybridization with the complementary strand Tro4c-BHQ (denoted as N-CQDs-Apt-T1), the zeta potential further shifted to −29.7 mV, as expected from the incorporation of an additional negatively charged oligonucleotide. This progressive increase in surface charge density confirms the successful assembly of the FRET nanoprobe.

The efficient assembly of the FRET system was directly confirmed by fluorescence emission spectroscopy. As presented in Figure 2E, the N-CQDs-Apt conjugate (without BHQ) showed strong fluorescence emission at 450 nm upon excitation at 380 nm (pink line). However, upon addition of the BHQ-labeled complementary strand (T1-BHQ), a dramatic fluorescence quenching was observed (yellow line), with a quenching efficiency of approximately 85%. This efficient quenching indicates that hybridization between the surface-bound Tro4 and Tro4c-BHQ brings the BHQ acceptor into close proximity to the N-CQD donor, enabling efficient FRET. To confirm that the quenching mechanism was indeed FRET (rather than static or collisional quenching) and to differentiate it from other non-radiative processes, fluorescence lifetime measurements were performed. Figure 2F shows that the average fluorescence lifetime of N-CQDs-Apt was 4.35 ns (green line). After hybridization with Tro4c-BHQ, the average lifetime significantly decreased to 1.58 ns (pink line). This reduction in lifetime is characteristic of dynamic quenching and unequivocally supports a FRET-based mechanism, where energy transfer from the donor (N-CQDs) to the acceptor (BHQ) occurs via a non-radiative pathway. From the fluorescence lifetime data, the FRET efficiency (E) was calculated as E = 1-(τDA/τD) = 1-(1.58/4.35)≈63.7%, confirming efficient energy transfer from N-CQDs to BHQ. Using a Förster distance (R0) of 5.0 nm for the N-CQDs/BHQ pair, the donor-acceptor distance (r) was estimated to be approximately 4.55 nm, which falls well within the 1–10 nm range required for efficient FRET. Collectively, these results confirm the successful construction of a FRET-active aptasensor platform suitable for subsequent cTnI detection.

Feasibility and Optimization of the FRET Aptasensor

To verify the feasibility of the proposed FRET-based detection strategy, we compared the fluorescence responses of different system configurations under identical conditions. As shown in Figure 3A and B, the N-CQDs alone (red curve) exhibited the highest fluorescence intensity at 450 nm (1950 a.u)., serving as the reference for maximum signal. Upon the addition of the BHQ-labeled complementary strand (T1-BHQ) to the N-CQDs-Apt conjugate (blue curve), the fluorescence was strongly quenched to 465 a.u., corresponding to a quenching efficiency of 76.2%. This result confirms that hybridization between the surface-anchored Tro4 aptamer and T1-BHQ brings the BHQ acceptor into close proximity to the N-CQD donor, enabling efficient FRET. More importantly, when the target protein cTnI (5 ng/mL) was introduced into the quenched N-CQDs-Apt/T1-BHQ system (yellow curve), a significant fluorescence recovery to 1720 a.u. was observed. The displacement efficiency was quantified as (1720–465) / (1950–465) × 100% = 84.5%, confirming that the majority (84.5%) of T1-BHQ strands were displaced upon cTnI binding. This target-induced turn-on response demonstrates the successful displacement of the T1-BHQ strand upon specific recognition and binding of cTnI by the aptamer, consistent with the strand displacement mechanism illustrated in Figure 2.

Following the feasibility confirmation, we systematically optimized the key parameters governing the performance of the FRET aptasensor to achieve the best possible signal-to-noise ratio. The optimization results are summarized in Figures 3C–H. First, the molar ratio of the aptamer (Tro4) to the complementary strand (Tro4c-BHQ, denoted as T1) was investigated (Figures 3C and D). Various ratios (1:0.5, 1:1, 1:2, 1:3, and 1:4) were examined. The fluorescence recovery upon cTnI addition was maximal at a ratio of 1:2. Therefore, an aptamer-to-T1 molar ratio of 1:2 was selected as optimal. This corresponds to a donor-to-acceptor (N-CQDs-Apt: T1-BHQ) molar ratio of 1:2 (50 nM: 100 nM). Second, the volume (and thus the concentration) of N-CQDs-Apt conjugate used in the assay was optimized (Figures 3E and F). Volumes ranging from 20 to 60 μL (corresponding to final concentrations of approximately 20–60 nM) were tested. The fluorescence recovery signal increased with N-CQDs-Apt volume up to 50 μL, after which the signal plateaued. Using 50 μL provided a sufficient number of donor-acceptor pairs for reliable detection while avoiding unnecessary reagent consumption. Hence, 50 μL (50 nM N-CQDs-Apt) was adopted as the optimal volume.

Third, the incubation temperature was evaluated (Figure 3G). Temperatures from 15 °C to 60 °C were examined. The highest fluorescence enhancement was observed at 37 °C, which corresponds to physiological temperature and favors both aptamer-target binding and strand displacement kinetics. Thus, 37 °C was used for all subsequent experiments. Fourth, the reaction time required for target-induced fluorescence recovery was determined (Figure 3H). The fluorescence intensity was monitored continuously for 60 min after the addition of cTnI. The signal increased rapidly during the first 30 min and reached a plateau at approximately 40 min, indicating that the binding and displacement processes were essentially complete within this period. Extending the incubation time beyond 40 min did not result in any further significant increase in fluorescence. Therefore, an incubation time of 40 min was selected for all subsequent measurements. While this duration is acceptable for laboratory-based analysis, further reduction of assay time will be required for true point-of-care applications. To further confirm that the observed fluorescence recovery is specifically triggered by target-induced strand displacement rather than passive desorption of the T1-BHQ strand, we performed a control experiment in which the FRET probe was incubated at 37°C for 60 min in the absence of cTnI. As shown in Figure S3, the fluorescence intensity remained stable throughout the incubation period, with no significant increase observed, confirming that the T1-BHQ strand does not passively dissociate from the N-CQDs-Apt conjugate under the assay conditions.

Analytical Performance for cTnI Detection

Under the optimized conditions, we evaluated the sensitivity and quantitative detection capability of the FRET aptasensor for cTnI. Figures 4A and B display the fluorescence emission spectra of the aptasensor upon the addition of increasing concentrations of cTnI ranging from 0 to 10 ng/mL. The fluorescence intensity at 450 nm increased progressively with higher cTnI concentrations, demonstrating a clear target-dependent turn-on response. Notably, even at the lowest tested concentration (0.01 ng/mL), a detectable increase in fluorescence was observed above the background, indicating the high sensitivity of the proposed platform. The calibration curve, constructed by plotting the fluorescence intensity (FL) at 450 nm versus the cTnI concentration (C, ng/mL), is presented in Figure 4C. A good linear relationship was observed over the concentration range from 0.01 to 5.0 ng/mL, which covers the clinically relevant range for cTnI in AMI diagnosis (typically the 99th percentile upper reference limit is around 0.01–0.04 ng/mL). The regression equation was determined as: FL = 2935.5·C + 521.08 (R2 = 0.991). The limit of detection (LOD) was calculated using the 3σ/k method, where σ is the standard deviation of the blank measurements (n = 11) and k is the slope of the calibration curve. The LOD was found to be 0.005 ng/mL (5 pg/mL). This detection limit is comparable to or even superior than many previously reported cTnI detection methods, including certain commercial immunoassays and other nanomaterial-based aptasensors (see Tables S2 and S3 in the Supporting Information for a detailed comparison). Specifically, commercial high-sensitivity chemiluminescence immunoassays achieve excellent LODs as low as 0.3–2 ng/L, but they require expensive instrumentation, multiple washing steps, and skilled operators, with total assay times typically exceeding 60 minutes. In contrast, our aptasensor offers a simple, enzyme-free, and wash-free protocol with a 40-minute assay time and a buffer LOD of 5 pg/mL, significantly reducing operational complexity and cost. Compared with previously reported electrochemical aptasensors that require electrode modification and signal amplification strategies (eg., HCR), our design eliminates these additional steps while achieving a comparable or better LOD in buffer. However, we acknowledge that our LOD in serum (0.8 ng/mL) is higher than that of commercial hs-cTnI assays, reflecting matrix interference that requires further optimization. Despite this limitation, the broad linear range and low LOD in buffer make this aptasensor suitable for early-stage AMI diagnosis where low levels of cTnI are clinically significant, particularly in resource-limited settings where sophisticated immunoassay systems are not accessible.

The selectivity of the FRET aptasensor toward cTnI was assessed by challenging the sensor with various potential interfering substances commonly found in human blood or associated with cardiac conditions. As shown in Figure 4D, only cTnI (at a concentration of 5 ng/mL) produced a strong fluorescence signal. In contrast, common interferents tested at physiologically relevant or elevated concentrations-including cardiac troponin T, brain natriuretic peptide, human serum albumin, glucose, creatinine, hemoglobin, bilirubin, and various metal ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Mg2⁺, Ca2⁺ at 5 mM each)-yielded negligible fluorescence responses for cTnI. Importantly, competitive binding studies were performed by testing a mixture containing all tested interferents together with cTnI (5 ng/mL). The fluorescence signal from this mixture was comparable to that obtained with cTnI alone (Figure 4D), confirming that the presence of abundant potential interferents does not compromise the specific recognition of cTnI by the aptasensor. This observation confirms that the presence of a complex matrix does not result in significant interference or cross-reactivity. These results unequivocally validate the excellent selectivity of the proposed aptasensor for cTnI, a performance attribute attributable to the high intrinsic specificity of the Tro4 aptamer for its target protein.

Batch-to-batch reproducibility. To evaluate the consistency of the sensor performance across different batches of N-CQDs, three independent batches were synthesized under identical conditions. The fluorescence response to 5 ng/mL cTnI was measured for each batch. As shown in Table S4, the fluorescence intensities were 1920 ± 35 a.u., 1880 ± 42 a.u., and 1850 ± 28 a.u. for Batch 1, Batch 2, and Batch 3, respectively. The overall relative standard deviation (RSD) was 1.9% (n=3 batches), confirming excellent batch-to-batch reproducibility of the N-CQDs synthesis and the FRET aptasensor fabrication.

Performance in Human Serum

To evaluate the practical applicability of the FRET aptasensor for clinical diagnosis, we tested its performance in a complex biological matrix- specifically, human serum. As detailed in Figures 5A, healthy human serum was diluted 50-fold with PBS to reduce matrix effects while still maintaining a physiologically relevant environment. The fluorescence responses of the aptasensor to varying concentrations of cTnI spiked into diluted serum are shown in Figures 5B and C.

Figure 5B displays the full fluorescence emission spectra of the aptasensor upon addition of increasing cTnI concentrations (0, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 5.0, 10.0, 15.0 and 20.0 ng/mL) in 50-fold diluted serum. Compared with measurements in buffer (Figure 4A), a slightly higher background fluorescence was observed in the serum matrix at zero cTnI concentration, likely due to the presence of residual autofluorescent components or proteins that may partially interact with the nanoprobe surface. Nevertheless, as shown in Figure 5C, the turn-on fluorescence response remained robust, concentration-dependent, and clearly distinguishable across the tested concentration range, demonstrating that the aptasensor retains its functionality even in the presence of abundant serum proteins and other biomolecules.

The calibration curve constructed for serum (n=3 independent replicates) is presented in Figure 5D. A linear relationship between fluorescence intensity (FL) and cTnI concentration (C, ng/mL) was established over the range from 5 to 20.0 ng/mL, with the following regression equation: FL=24.28·C + 721 (R2 = 0.988). The limit of detection in 50-fold diluted serum was calculated to be 0.8 ng/mL (based on 3σ/k). This LOD is approximately 160-fold higher than that obtained in buffer (0.005 ng/mL), reflecting the expected matrix interference in complex biological samples. This dramatic loss of sensitivity is primarily attributed to: (i) non-specific adsorption of serum proteins onto the N-CQDs surface; (ii) increased background fluorescence from autofluorescent components in serum; and (iii) potential ionic strength effects on FRET efficiency. From a clinical perspective, the 50-fold dilution used in this study means that the effective LOD in undiluted serum is approximately 40 ng/mL (0.8 ng/mL×50). This value is higher than the clinical diagnostic threshold for AMI (typically 0.04–1.0 ng/mL according to ESC/ACC guidelines). Therefore, in its current format, the sensor is not yet suitable for direct clinical detection of AMI. However, this study was designed as a proof-of-concept feasibility study to establish analytical performance in a complex biological matrix. The successful detection of spiked cTnI in diluted serum with good recoveries (94–97%) demonstrates that the sensor retains functionality in serum. Future work will focus on reducing the dilution factor (eg., 5-fold or 2-fold) and developing antifouling surface modifications to minimize matrix interference, which would bring the effective LOD into the clinically relevant range for AMI detection.

To further validate the accuracy and precision of the proposed aptasensor for real-sample analysis, spike-recovery experiments were performed. Known concentrations of cTnI (0.1, 0.5, 2.0, and 5.0 ng/mL) were spiked into the 50-fold diluted serum, and the concentrations were determined using the serum calibration curve. As summarized in Table S5, the recoveries ranged from 94% to 97%, with relative standard deviations (RSDs) below 4.5% for all spiked levels. These results demonstrate good accuracy (recoveries close to 100%) and excellent precision (low RSDs) of the FRET aptasensor in a complex biological matrix, confirming its potential for reliable cTnI quantification in clinical serum samples.

In addition, batch-to-batch reproducibility and long-term storage stability were evaluated. As summarized in Table S5, three independent batches of N-CQDs showed excellent batch-to-batch reproducibility with an overall RSD of 1.9%. Furthermore, the FRET aptasensor retained approximately 90.9% of its initial activity after one month of storage at 4°C, demonstrating good shelf-life.

Discussion

In this work, we developed a FRET-based aptasensor using N-CQDs as the donor and BHQ as the acceptor for cTnI detection. The sensor achieves a detection limit of 0.005 ng/mL in buffer and 0.8 ng/mL in 50-fold diluted human serum, with good selectivity and reproducibility. Compared with previously reported cTnI aptasensors (Tables S2 and S4), our design offers a unique combination of enzyme-free, wash-free, and direct strand displacement features. While many enzyme-free cTnI biosensors exist in the literature, most still require multiple washing steps, additional amplification strategies (eg., HCR or CHA), or complex electrode modifications. In contrast, our sensor integrates: (i) truly wash-free operation due to the FRET turn-on mechanism, which eliminates user intervention and reduces assay time; (ii) a high signal-to-noise ratio enabled by the N-CQDs/BHQ donor-acceptor pair, where N-CQDs provide bright and stable fluorescence while BHQ contributes zero background; and (iii) direct target-triggered strand displacement without auxiliary amplification, which avoids potential false positives and reduces reagent costs. This synergistic combination simplifies the assay procedure and makes the platform particularly suitable for rapid, low-cost cTnI detection in decentralized settings.

Origin of Fluorescence in N-CQDs

We acknowledge that the average size of our N-CQDs (~22 nm) exceeds the conventional size range (<10 nm) typically associated with quantum confinement effects in pristine carbon dots.38 However, the strong blue photoluminescence observed in our N-CQDs can be explained by mechanisms beyond classical quantum confinement. First, nitrogen doping disrupts the carbon network, creating numerous isolated or small conjugated π-domains within the larger particles. These domains act as spatially confined emissive centers-effectively reducing the “effective conjugation length” regardless of the overall particle size, which has been reported to generate strong fluorescence even in larger carbon nanoparticles.39 Second, surface and edge states introduced by nitrogen doping (eg., amino and carboxyl groups) create localized energy levels that facilitate electron-hole recombination, a mechanism often dominating the fluorescence in heteroatom-doped carbon dots.40 Third, recent reviews on carbon dot photoluminescence have established that fluorescence can arise from multiple mechanisms-including surface states, molecular states, and crosslink-enhanced emission effects-rather than quantum confinement alone.41,42 Therefore, we attribute the strong fluorescence of our N-CQDs primarily to nitrogen-doping-induced surface/edge states and the formation of confined sp2 domains, rather than to quantum confinement from the overall particle size.

Quantitative FRET Analysis

Based on the fluorescence lifetime data (Figure 2F), we calculated a FRET efficiency of approximately 63.7% and an estimated donor-acceptor distance of approximately 4.55 nm. These quantitative results provide strong evidence for the FRET mechanism, as the calculated distance falls within the typical 1–10 nm range for efficient resonance energy transfer and is consistent with successful hybridization between the N-CQDs-Apt conjugate and the T1-BHQ complementary strand.

Specificity of Target-Induced Strand Displacement

We further considered whether non-specific desorption of the T1-BHQ strand could contribute to the observed fluorescence restoration. To directly address this possibility, we performed a control experiment in which the FRET probe was incubated at 37°C for 60 min in the absence of cTnI. As shown in Figure S2, the fluorescence intensity remained stable throughout the incubation period, indicating that the T1-BHQ strand does not passively desorb from the N-CQDs-Apt conjugate. In contrast, the addition of cTnI (5 ng/mL) induced a strong fluorescence recovery (Figure 3A and B). Furthermore, the selectivity data (Figure 4D) demonstrated that non-target proteins such as albumin, cTnT, and BNP did not trigger any significant fluorescence increase. Collectively, these results unequivocally demonstrate that the fluorescence restoration is specifically induced by cTnI-triggered strand displacement rather than by non-specific desorption or structural changes.

However, the limit of detection in serum is approximately 160-fold higher than in buffer. This dramatic loss of sensitivity can be attributed to three main factors. First, non-specific adsorption of abundant serum proteins (eg., albumin, immunoglobulins) onto the N-CQDs surface may partially block aptamer recognition sites or interfere with the FRET donor-acceptor pair, reducing the efficiency of target-induced strand displacement. Second, residual autofluorescent components in serum, even after 50-fold dilution, contribute to a higher background fluorescence signal at zero cTnI concentration, thereby elevating the calculated LOD (3σ/k). Third, the high ionic strength and complex composition of serum may alter aptamer conformation or the hybridization stability between Tro4 and T1-BHQ, potentially reducing both FRET quenching efficiency and signal-to-noise ratio upon target binding. To minimize matrix interference in future work, several strategies can be pursued: (i) pre-incubation of the N-CQDs-Apt probe with blocking agents such as BSA or PEG; (ii) systematic optimization of the serum dilution factor (eg., testing 5-fold or 10-fold dilution); (iii) introduction of a simple washing step or magnetic separation; (iv) design of a dual-aptamer sandwich format; and (v) surface modification of N-CQDs with antifouling polymers.

Assay time and clinical applicability. One additional limitation worth noting is the total assay time of 40 minutes required for optimal fluorescence recovery. In the context of emergency AMI diagnosis, where point-of-care testing platforms typically aim for turnaround times of 15–20 minutes, the current system does not yet meet the speed requirement for bedside use. However, for centralized laboratory testing or for use in settings where rapid triage is not the primary constraint, a 40-minute assay remains acceptable, particularly given the enzyme-free and wash-free nature of the protocol. Future efforts will focus on shortening the incubation time to within 20 minutes by strategies such as increasing the reaction temperature (eg., 45 °C), introducing microfluidic mixing to accelerate hybridization and strand displacement kinetics, or designing aptamers with faster binding rates. We acknowledge that the one-variable-at-a-time optimization approach used in this study cannot identify potential interactions between experimental parameters. Future work will employ multivariable statistical designs (eg., response surface methodology) to systematically optimize sensor parameters and explore synergistic effects among factors such as temperature, incubation time, and probe concentrations.

Comparison with Conventional Immunoassays

To further highlight the practical advantages of our FRET aptasensor, it is instructive to compare it with conventional methods such as ELISA and immunochromatographic assays (lateral flow immunoassays, LFIAs). ELISA is widely used for cTnI detection due to its high sensitivity and reliability. However, it requires multiple washing steps, enzyme-labeled antibodies, and long incubation times (typically 2–4 hours), resulting in higher cost and operational complexity. In contrast, our aptasensor offers a wash-free and enzyme-free protocol with a total assay time of 40 minutes and a buffer LOD of 5 pg/mL, significantly reducing time and labor. Immunochromatographic assays (LFIAs) are rapid (15–30 min) and user-friendly, making them suitable for point-of-care testing. However, they generally suffer from lower sensitivity (LOD typically 0.1–1.0 ng/mL for cTnI) and are primarily qualitative or semi-quantitative. Our platform provides quantitative detection with a wide linear range (0.01–5.0 ng/mL in buffer) and a sensitivity comparable to ELISA. While the 40-minute assay time is longer than that of LFIAs, the higher sensitivity, quantitative capability, and low-cost aptamer-based design offer distinct advantages for laboratory-based cTnI detection, particularly in resource-limited settings where automated ELISA systems are not accessible.

Serum Dilution and Clinical Relevance

We acknowledge that 50-fold dilution of human serum significantly alters its physiological composition (eg., protein concentrations, ionic strength, pH buffering capacity). Therefore, the analytical performance obtained under these diluted conditions does not directly reflect the sensor’s behavior in undiluted patient serum, nor does it represent actual clinical scenarios. The primary purpose of using 50-fold dilution in this initial study was to reduce matrix interference (eg., protein adsorption, autofluorescence) to a manageable level, allowing us to validate the feasibility of the FRET aptasensor in a complex biological matrix. Despite this limitation, the successful detection of spiked cTnI in diluted serum with acceptable recoveries (94–97%) suggests that the sensor retains functionality in the presence of serum components. Future studies will systematically reduce the dilution factor (eg., 5-fold, 2-fold) and ultimately aim for detection in undiluted serum, potentially with the aid of sample pre-treatment steps (eg., protein precipitation, filtration) or antifouling surface modifications.

Instrumentation and Point-of-Care Applicability

We acknowledge that the current system requires a fluorescence spectrophotometer and controlled incubation conditions (37 °C), which significantly limits its practicality for real bedside implementation. Accordingly, we have revised the manuscript to remove claims that the sensor is immediately suitable for point-of-care testing. Instead, we now position this work as a laboratory-based feasibility study. Comparison with current POCT devices. Recent advances in portable biosensing have demonstrated the feasibility of integrating FRET-based aptasensors into handheld devices. Smartphone-based fluorescence detectors43 and microfluidic paper-based analytical devices (μPADs)44 have been developed for rapid cardiac biomarker screening. Compared to these systems, our aptasensor offers a simpler design (no enzyme, no washing steps) and a competitive assay time (40 min), making it compatible with such portable platforms. Future integration strategies. For future translation to point-of-care settings, several approaches can be pursued. First, the FRET aptasensor could be integrated into a handheld or smartphone-based fluorescence reader, as demonstrated in recent portable biosensing platforms. Second, temperature control could be simplified using a portable incubator or by developing room-temperature protocols. Third, the aptasensor reagents could be lyophilized into a ready-to-use format. Fourth, incorporation into a microfluidic chip would reduce sample and reagent volumes, accelerate mixing and reaction kinetics (potentially shortening the 40 min incubation time), and enable automated sample processing. These modifications would enable true bedside deployment in resource-limited settings.

Additional Limitations and Future Directions

We acknowledge that independent affinity comparisons between Tro4 and other cTnI aptamers (eg., Tro1-Tro6) were not performed in this study, as the superior performance of Tro4 has been well established in the literature.33,34 Another limitation is the lack of validation using actual patient serum samples. All experiments were performed using cTnI-spiked healthy human serum. While the use of spiked healthy serum allowed us to precisely control cTnI concentrations and establish analytical performance in a complex biological matrix, we fully recognize that clinical validation with genuine AMI patient samples is essential for translational development. Future studies will collect serum samples from AMI patients with confirmed elevated cTnI levels (as determined by clinical chemiluminescence immunoassays) and evaluate the clinical sensitivity, specificity, and correlation of our FRET aptasensor with the gold-standard clinical method. Furthermore, the current assay still requires a fluorescence spectrophotometer, which limits its portability. Integration of the aptasensor into a handheld fluorescence reader or smartphone-based detection module would represent a significant step toward point-of-care applicability. We also acknowledge that HR-TEM, SEM, and AFM were not performed for morphological characterization due to the limited availability of these instruments in our laboratory. However, conventional TEM (Figures 1A–C) clearly shows the uniform spherical morphology and size distribution of the N-CQDs. Given the amorphous nature of hydrothermally synthesized N-CQDs, lattice fringes are not expected even under HR-TEM. Future studies will include SEM and AFM analyses for more comprehensive morphological characterization.

Conclusion

We have developed a nitrogen-doped carbon quantum dot-based FRET aptasensor for the non-enzymatic, turn-on detection of cTnI in acute myocardial infarction diagnosis. The sensor integrates several key advantages: (i) high sensitivity (LOD 5 pg/mL in buffer), (ii) excellent selectivity against common interferents, (iii) enzyme-free strand displacement mechanism, (iv) low-cost nanomaterials, and (v) reliable performance in human serum. Furthermore, the platform is modular and can be adapted for other biomarkers by simply changing the recognition aptamer. This aptasensor provides a simple, enzyme-free fluorescence turn-on strategy for cTnI detection in laboratory settings. Although the current 40-minute assay time and reliance on fluorescence instrumentation limit its immediate use for point-of-care testing, the platform represents a promising starting point for future development toward portable AMI diagnostics after appropriate optimization.
