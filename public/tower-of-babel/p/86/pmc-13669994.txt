Awake Blue Light vs. KTP Laser Treatment for Vocal Fold Lesions: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: R Jun Lin, Peter Y F Zeng, Patrick MacInnis, Garret Horton, Jennifer Anderson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo compare clinical outcomes of a 445‐nm blue light (BL) laser with the established 532‐nm potassium titanyl phosphate (KTP) laser for office‐based treatment of benign vocal fold lesions.MethodsIn this parallel‐group, open‐label randomized controlled trial, adults (≥ 18 years) with benign vocal fold lesions underwent baseline VHI‐10 and laryngeal stroboscopy, followed by in‐office laser treatment. Pain visual analogue scale (VAS) scores were collected at baseline and daily for 7 days after the procedure. The primary outcome was the between‐group difference in the change in Voice Handicap Index‐10 (VHI‐10) scores. Secondary outcomes included between‐group comparisons of post‐procedural pain and stroboscopic parameters at 90 days after treatment. Linear mixed‐effects model assessed changes in VHI‐10 and pain VAS over time, while Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel and logistic regression tests compared stroboscopy outcomes.ResultsNinety‐eight patients were enrolled (49 per group). Baseline characteristics were similar aside from a slightly younger age in the KTP group (p = 0.048). VHI‐10 scores improved significantly in both groups at postoperative day (POD)7 (−5.98), POD30 (−9.28), and POD90 (−12.43) (all p ≤ 0.001), with no between‐group differences (p = 0.596). Pain decreased significantly over time (p < 0.001), without group differences (p = 0.47). At POD90, complete vocal fold closure occurred in 91.2% (BL) and 85.7% (KTP) of patients (p = 0.74). Normal mucosal wave was observed in 79.4% (BL) and 81.0% (KTP), with no significant differences.ConclusionBL laser demonstrated clinical outcomes comparable to KTP, with similar voice improvement, pain reduction, and stroboscopic results.Level of Evidence2Keywords: benign vocal fold lesions, blue light laser, KTP laser, office‐based laryngology, randomized controlled trial

Background

The potassium titanyl phosphate (KTP) laser, operating at a wavelength of 532 nm, was first introduced into Laryngology in 2006 for office‐based treatment of recurrent respiratory papillomatosis (RRP), vocal fold leukoplakia, and hemorrhagic polyps under local anesthesia [1, 2]. The KTP laser's therapeutic effect is based on selective absorption by oxyhemoglobin in red blood cells, enabling targeted ablation of abnormal vasculature while preserving the integrity and pliability of the surrounding vocal fold tissue [3, 4]. It has since been widely adopted for awake and operative laryngeal procedures due to its photoangiolytic properties, pulsed emission, and fiber‐based delivery [5]. A recent 5‐year retrospective study reported that approximately 90% of in‐office KTP laser procedures were well‐tolerated and only 1.9% of patients experienced short‐term complications, all of which resolved within 6 months [6]. A 2021 systematic review further highlighted the laser's versatility in treating various laryngeal conditions such as Reinke's edema, dysplasia, early glottic cancers, etc., consistently demonstrating low complication rates and recovery of vocal fold vibratory function [5]. Unfortunately, Boston Scientific Corporation (Marlborough, MA, USA), the manufacturer of the Aura XP KTP laser console, discontinued production of the device in 2018. The absence of ongoing manufacturing support has prompted the need for alternative photoangiolytic platforms.

A novel 445 nm blue light (BL) laser was first reported in clinical use in 2018 as a promising alternative to the KTP laser. Like the KTP laser, the BL laser is photoangiolytic and can be delivered through a flexible fiber. It has also been proposed to offer improved cutting precision and greater portability [7]. A preclinical study compared the scarring effects of BL and KTP lasers on normal vocal folds in 24 Sprague–Dawley rats [8]. By postoperative day (POD) 90, both laser groups showed increased protein deposition versus controls, with the KTP laser significantly higher than the BL laser. Early fibrosis appeared in the KTP group by POD 30, but not in the BL group. This study was the first to demonstrate that the BL laser produces less vocal fold scarring than the KTP in animal models, suggesting it may have improved outcomes for laryngeal procedures.

Clinical studies supporting the use of BL laser in Laryngology remain limited to retrospective case series [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. A recent systematic review and meta‐analysis by Bhat et al. synthesized data from 45 studies comparing photoangiolytic lasers, including the BL laser, in the treatment of benign laryngeal lesions [17]. While the review concluded that the BL laser demonstrated the highest lesion resolution rate and the greatest improvement in VHI‐10 scores among commonly used laser modalities, interpretation was limited by study heterogeneity, short follow‐up, and a lack of randomized comparisons, underscoring the need for prospective randomized studies with standardized treatment protocols and outcome measures to validate the clinical utility of the BL laser relative to other established modalities.

To date, no prospective studies have directly compared BL and KTP lasers for treatment of vocal fold lesions. This pilot study represents the first clinical comparison of treatment outcomes between the BL and KTP lasers for benign vocal fold lesions in an awake, office‐based setting. The study evaluates between‐group differences in patient‐reported voice outcomes, pain, and stroboscopic measures, while assessing feasibility to inform the design of a larger randomized trial.

Methods

This parallel‐group, randomized controlled study was conducted at a tertiary Laryngology center located at Unity Health Toronto—St. Michael's Hospital. The clinical trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov, and ethics approval was obtained from the local Institutional Research Ethics Board (#21‐131).

Participants

Eligible participants were adults (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with benign vocal fold lesions, such as RRP or vocal fold polyps, and who were candidates for elective office‐based laser treatment for these lesions under awake conditions. Patients taking anticoagulants were instructed to hold their medication according to standard protocols. Participants were excluded if they: (1) were < 18 years old; (2) had or were suspected to have vocal fold cancer; (3) were taking systemic steroids; or (4) had systemic illnesses known to impair wound healing.

Randomization Methods and Blinding

Participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive treatment with either the BL laser (445 nm, A.R.C. Laser, Nuremberg, Germany) or the KTP laser (532 nm, Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA). Randomization was performed using a permutated block design with randomly varying block sizes generated by the REDCap software [18]. Allocation was concealed by the research coordinator, who was not involved in patient care. The randomization code was disclosed to both the participant and the treating Laryngologist at the time of treatment. Given the nature of the intervention, blinding of the treating physician and participants was not possible, making this an open‐label study.

Experimental Arm—BL Laser Treatment

Participants completed the VHI‐10 questionnaire in paper format and underwent baseline laryngeal stroboscopy examination at their initial visit. Video stroboscopy was performed using either flexible chip‐tip or rigid endoscopes. Glottal closure and mucosal vibration were assessed by the treating physician during sustained phonation of the vowel /i/ at the patient's most comfortable pitch and loudness.

At the treatment visit, local anesthesia was administered per standard protocol for in‐office laryngeal procedures. Lesions were treated using the BL laser set at 10 W, 30 ms pulse duration, and 150 ms inter‐pulse interval, parameters the primary author had found most effective for most laryngeal pathologies. Minor intraoperative setting adjustments were made as needed based on tissue response. Immediately following the procedure, participants rated intraoperative pain using a paper‐based VAS scale. Post‐procedure instructions included 3 days of absolute voice rest and participants were asked to record pain daily using the VAS for 7 days after treatment.

Follow‐up visits occurred on PODs 7, 30, and 90. At each visit, VHI‐10 was completed by the participant and laryngeal stroboscopy was performed by the treating Laryngologist to assess vocal fold vibratory characteristics, closure patterns, and residual lesions. The follow‐up visits were consistent with standard clinical care. VHI‐10 scores from each visit were included in the final analysis, while stroboscopic parameters were compared from baseline to the POD90 visit.

Control Arm—KTP Laser Treatment

An identical protocol was followed using the KTP laser. Standard settings were 40 W, 15 ms pulse width, and 2 pulses per second, with minor intraoperative adjustments made based on desired tissue response.

Outcome Measures

Baseline patient demographics were collected, including age, sex, and vocal fold lesion diagnosis. Vocal fold lesions, such as RRP, vocal fold polyp, and leukoplakia, were defined as previously reported [19]. Specific outcome variables included the following: (a) VHI‐10 (range 0–40) was collected at baseline, approximately PODs 7, 30, and 90 [20]; (b) VAS pain scale: Intraoperative pain was rated immediately post‐procedure using a 10‐point VAS (0 = no pain; 10 = worst imaginable pain) [21] and daily for 7 days after the procedure. While specific energy density (J/cm2) was not the focus of this clinical trial, the superior absorption of 445‐nm light suggests a more efficient photoangiolytic effect, which could result in differences in patient‐reported procedural pain. (c) Laryngeal stroboscopy: Glottal closure and mucosal vibration were assessed during a sustained /i/ at most comfortable pitch and loudness at baseline and on POD90 by the participant's treating laryngologist. Glottal closure was rated as complete or incomplete. Mucosal vibration was scored using an ordinal scale from 0 to 4, where 0 indicated normal vibration, and higher values reflected increasing severity of vibratory impairment (1 = mildly reduced, 2 = moderately reduced, 3 = severely reduced, and 4 = adynamic).

Although stroboscopy is subject to observer variability, glottal closure and mucosal vibration are relatively robust parameters. Rosen's study in 2005 identified these two parameters as more visually distinct and less subjective than others, such as periodicity, with the highest intra‐rater reliability [22]. This was further supported by Kelley et al.'s work, which highlighted that vibratory pattern and glottal closure were valuable for diagnostic accuracy and treatment decisions [23]. Guided by these findings, the present study selected glottal closure and mucosal vibration as secondary outcome measures.

Objective acoustic and aerodynamic measures, while informative, are sensitive to noise, equipment, and patient factors, and may not align with self‐perceived voice impairment. These measures were excluded to maximize feasibility in this pilot study but may be included in future trials.

All data were recorded on paper forms and entered into the study's REDCap database by a research coordinator on the day of collection.

Sample Size Estimate

Using conventional methods for sample size calculation for feasibility and pilot studies [24], and using the formula of n = z 2 p(1 − p)/d 2, where n = sample size, with z = 1.96 (95% confidence interval [CI]), p = 0.70 (estimated accrual rate), and d = 0.20 (margin of error), we estimated a sample size of 40 participants (20 per group). However, to account for anticipated noncompliance and incomplete follow‐up, the enrollment target was increased to 90 participants, 45 per group.

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize patient demographics. Continuous variables were compared using a Student's t‐test and categorical variables using chi‐square. The primary analysis followed an intention‐to‐treat approach, with missing data excluded due to the small sample size. Two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed in R (v4.3.1).

The primary outcome (VHI‐10) was analyzed using a linear mixed‐effects model including fixed effects for treatment, time, their interaction, sex, age, and lesion type, with participant as a random effect to account for repeated measures. VAS pain scores were analyzed using the same model structure.

Changes in glottal closure were evaluated using the Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test to account for baseline closure, while changes in mucosal vibration from baseline to POD90 were analyzed using logistic regression with treatment, time, and interaction effects. Significance was assessed via the Wald test.

Results

A total number of 98 participants were prospectively enrolled in the study from August of 2021 to May of 2024, with 49 randomized to each treatment arm and receiving the intended treatment. Procedural completion rate was 100% in both treatment arms and none of the patients had any unexpected adverse effects from the treatment.

Primary Outcome: VHI‐10

Linear mixed‐effects modeling demonstrated significant improvement in VHI‐10 scores over time across both treatment groups (Table 2). At baseline, there was no statistically significant difference in VHI‐10 between BL and KTP laser groups. Compared to baseline, mean VHI‐10 scores decreased by 5.98 points at POD 7 (95% CI: −9.53 to −2.43, p = 0.001), 9.28 points at POD30 (95% CI: −12.85 to −5.70, p < 0.001), and 12.43 points at POD90 (95% CI: −15.87 to −8.98, p < 0.001). Figure 1 illustrates the trajectory of VHI‐10 improvement over time for both groups. The BL laser treatment group had a more modest reduction of VHI‐10 score at 1.16 compared to the KTP laser group. However, this was not statistically significant (95% CI: −5.44 to +3.13; p = 0.60; Table 2). No significant effects of sex, age, or lesion diagnosis on VHI‐10 scores were observed. The interaction between treatment group and time point did not reveal any evidence that the change in VHI‐10 scores over time differed significantly between BL and KTP (all p > 0.24).

Secondary Outcome: VAS

In the linear mixed‐effects model evaluating VAS pain scores, time point was associated with a significant reduction in pain (estimate, −3.10; 95% CI, −3.69 to −2.53; p < 0.001), indicating a mean decrease of 3.10 points in VAS score with each postoperative time point (Table 3). However, there was no significant difference in overall pain scores between the BL laser group and the KTP laser group (estimate, 2.51; 95% CI, −4.39 to 9.41; p = 0.47). Sex, age, and vocal fold lesion diagnosis were also not significantly associated with pain outcomes (all p > 0.40). The interaction between time and treatment group was not statistically significant (estimate, 0.18; 95% CI, −0.66 to 1.03; p = 0.67), suggesting similar rates of pain reduction over time across treatment groups.

Secondary Outcome: Stroboscopy Parameters

Stroboscopic parameters, including glottic closure and mucosal wave, were evaluated at baseline and POD90. At baseline, the distribution of complete versus incomplete glottic closure was similar between groups, with incomplete closure present in 68.2% of patients in the BL group and 78.0% of patients in the KTP group (p = 0.40). By POD90, substantial improvement in glottic closure was observed in both cohorts. In the BL group, 31 of 34 patients (91.2%) demonstrated complete closure at POD90, compared with 3 patients (8.8%) who continued to exhibit incomplete closure. Similarly, in the KTP group, 36 of 42 patients (85.7%) achieved complete closure, while 6 patients (14.3%) had persistent incomplete closure. These findings suggest that the majority of patients with baseline glottic insufficiency regained complete closure after treatment, with no significant difference between laser modalities in the degree of improvement over time (Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test, p = 0.74).

A similar pattern was observed for mucosal wave function. At baseline, normal (grade 0) mucosal vibration was present in 31.8% of participants in the BL group and 35.4% of participants in the KTP group, while the remaining patients demonstrated mildly or moderately reduced vibration. Baseline mucosal wave distributions did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.89). At POD90, normal mucosal vibration was observed in 27 of 34 patients (79.4%) in the BL group and 34 of 42 patients (81.0%) in the KTP group, indicating that most patients experienced recovery or preservation of normal vibratory function following treatment. Logistic regression analysis demonstrated no significant difference between treatment groups in the change in mucosal wave from baseline to POD90 (Wald test, p > 0.05).

Discussion

This randomized controlled pilot study represents the first direct clinical comparison of the BL and KTP lasers for the treatment of benign vocal fold lesions in an awake, office‐based setting and demonstrates the feasibility of randomized laser trials. We successfully randomized 98 patients and maintained relatively high follow‐up adherence across multiple timepoints. The pragmatic design reflects real‐world clinical practice and enhances generalizability, though it may introduce heterogeneity that limits sensitivity to detect small between‐group differences. These findings will help guide the design of future, larger‐scale trials by informing sample size estimation, stratification strategies, and outcome selection. Although the present study evaluated these technologies in an awake setting, the same application principles and anticipated clinical outcomes are translatable to the operating room context.

Both treatment arms experienced statistically and clinically meaningful improvements in VHI‐10 scores over time, with the greatest gains (a reduction of 12 points in VHI‐10 score) observed by POD90. The degree of improvement exceeded the threshold for minimum clinically important difference of 6 for VHI‐10 [25], confirming that both laser types effectively ameliorated vocal fold pathology, resulting in voice improvement. The lack of statistically significant differences between groups supports our hypothesis that the BL laser may be non‐inferior to the KTP laser with respect to voice outcomes. This study provides the first prospective evidence supporting comparable voice outcomes between modalities. Pain trajectories were also similar between groups, with a significant decrease in self‐reported pain over the course of 1 week and no appreciable difference in overall discomfort. These findings align with prior studies demonstrating high tolerability of in‐office BL laser procedures and support its continued use in awake settings [26].

Objective stroboscopic assessments confirmed comparable efficacy between the two lasers. By POD90, high rates of complete glottal closure and normal mucosal vibration were observed in both groups, with no evidence of increased vibratory impairment. The preservation of glottal closure and mucosal wave function in both treatment arms, both reliable indicators of vocal fold healing and pliability, suggests that neither laser appears to increase the risk of scarring or vibratory impairment. However, larger trials are needed to confirm these findings. These clinical results complement prior preclinical work in a rat model, which showed that the BL laser induced significantly less subepithelial fibrosis compared to the KTP laser in normal rat vocal folds [8]. While histological differences in scarring were observed in the preclinical setting, such differences may not translate into detectable differences in clinical function, at least within the 90‐day follow‐up window.

There are several limitations to consider. First, the study was open‐label, and blinding of participants and treating physicians was not possible due to the visible differences between laser systems. Stroboscopic evaluations were not performed in a blinded manner, which could introduce potential observer bias and should be addressed in future studies. Second, follow‐up was limited to 90 days and may not capture long‐term voice outcomes, recurrence rates, or delayed fibrosis. Third, objective acoustic and aerodynamic measures were not included due to previously mentioned considerations. These measures could provide complementary insights into vocal function and may be incorporated into future trials. Finally, the study population included a range of benign vocal fold lesions, introducing variability in lesion response and healing characteristics. Future larger studies may benefit from stratification by lesion type to allow for more nuanced comparisons. Despite these limitations, this study provides compelling evidence that the BL laser and the KTP laser are safe and effective treatments for benign vocal fold lesions. Given its comparable clinical outcomes, fiber‐based delivery, and proposed advantages in cutting abilities and portability, the BL laser may be particularly appealing in settings where cost or equipment footprint is a consideration. These findings support broader adoption of BL laser technology and lay the groundwork for larger trials that can assess long‐term outcomes and lesion‐specific responses more robustly.

Conclusion

In this pilot randomized controlled clinical trial, the BL laser demonstrated clinical efficacy comparable to the KTP laser for office‐based treatment of benign vocal fold lesions. Both laser modalities were associated with significant improvements in patient‐perceived voice handicap, minimal post‐procedural pain, and favorable stroboscopic outcomes. Both lasers were safe, effective, and well‐tolerated, with the BL laser having potential advantages in accessibility and portability. These findings support the continued evaluation of the BL laser in larger, lesion‐specific studies with longer follow‐up to assess durability of outcomes and long‐term vocal function.
