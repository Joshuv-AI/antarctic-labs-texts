Systematic Map of Urban Connectivity Research Reveals a Dearth of Validation of Connectivity Estimates
Authors: Andrew K. Habrich, Lenore Fahrig
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose of ReviewIncreasing ecological connectivity among urban greenspaces is a global conservation priority to protect urban wildlife. However, effective monitoring remains a challenge, as connectivity models are rarely validated against biological data despite the need for these models to represent real wildlife movement. Here, we systematically reviewed the urban connectivity literature to assess (i) the extent of connectivity model validation; (ii) how validation varies by study objectives; (iii) where urban connectivity research is conducted; and (iv) what types of connectivity metrics, taxa, and biological data are used.Recent FindingsOf the 430 studies reviewed, nearly half validated their connectivity models using biological data, but few used movement data. Structural connectivity metrics dominated, although use of functional metrics has increased in recent years. A clear taxonomic bias was also evident, with a disproportionate focus on birds. When validation was conducted, most relied on species richness or other biodiversity metrics. Such approaches offer ambiguous evidence for actual connectivity, as biodiversity patterns are often influenced by confounding factors like greenspace size and the speciesarea relationship. As such, direct empirical support for connectivity models capturing wildlife movement remains limited.SummaryUrban connectivity models are often applied without clear evidence that they represent actual ecological processes. To address this, future studies should incorporate a broader range of taxa and test multiple model types to disentangle how movement patterns align with different connectivity frameworks. Integrating biological validation, particularly movement data, into connectivity modelling is essential to tracking progress toward global goals for ecologically resilient cities. Introduction

Humans and other organisms benefit from access to patches of urban greenspaces (parks, gardens, riparian areas, remnant forest) and bluespaces (rivers, lakes, ponds). These spaces are psychologically restorative for people [1], provide communal places for neighbours to gather [2], and supply ecosystem services such as flood control and carbon storage [3, 4]. Wildlife in the city also benefit from these spaces as a refuge for such as breeding and foraging habitat [5, 6], in an otherwise humandominated landscape. In turn, these other organisms help to maintain the quality and integrity of these spaces. Recognizing the key role of greenspace in human and urban-ecosystem health, countries have signed onto the COP15 Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, which includes an explicit target to “increase the area and quality, and connectivity of green and blue spaces in urban and densely populated areas” (our italics, [7]). While a laudable goal, this raises questions of how countries can achieve this target. Specifically, we need to know what to measure and how to measure our progress in improving connectivity of urban ecosystems, otherwise we risk wasting limited time, money, and effort.

Evaluating progress towards the connectivity target is challenging because connectivity has been defined and measured in a wide variety of fundamentally different ways [8–11]. Connectivity approaches can be placed in five bins: (i) structural, (ii) graph-based, (iii) potential, (iv) functional, and (v) actual connectivity, that differ in their data requirements and their theoretical underpinnings [11]. Structural connectivity measures Euclidean distances between patches of natural habitat (greenspaces in cities), or the amount of habitat within a buffer around a focal habitat patch [12, 13]. Critically, structural connectivity metrics do not explicitly incorporate species information, other than selecting ‘habitat’ and buffer sizes thought to be relevant to most wildlife in the region. Structural metrics assume that the spatial structure of natural habitat on the landscape influences movement of organisms in general, but they do not consider the needs or behaviours of different species [14]. Graph-based connectivity [15, 16] represents patches as nodes, and linkages between patches, i.e., traversable gaps or corridors, as edges. It assumes that movement can occur only between patches that are linked by edges. Graph-based metrics are often applied to species where maximum dispersal distances or corridor-use are known or assumed [17, 18]. Potential connectivity metrics, such as the Incidence Function measure [19], build on structural and graph-based measures by explicitly incorporating species-specific information on population sizes and assumed dispersal distances to weight possible connections between patches.

A shortcoming of these first three types of connectivity (i.e. structural, graph-based, and potential connectivity) is that they use binary habitat/non-habitat maps to describe the landscape and extract connectivity metrics. While this may be a reasonable assumption in some situations, in others the landscape is more correctly viewed as a mosaic of different cover types that vary for a given species in their utility as habitat, and in their risk level during movement. Urban areas are a case in point, representing a spectrum of cover types at many scales [20, 21]. Functional connectivity metrics move away from binary maps, commonly using heterogenous multi-class land cover maps with species-specific costs or resistance scores for cover types based on how much they impede movement or increase mortality risk or energetic costs for a species. Metrics like least-cost paths [22] or current flow [23] can model the connectivity of entire landscapes for species or groups of species by assessing the relative resistance to movement for many land cover classes. Lastly, connectivity metrics can represent actual (or realized) connectivity, quantified through direct empirical evidence of pathways taken by the species of interest through the landscape, using tracking, translocation, or gene flow data [17, 24]. Given that all connectivity types are meant to represent likely movement pathways in the city, validation of the other four connectivity types is best done using actual connectivity data [25].

Although many metrics have been used to model urban connectivity [26, 27], meaningful progress toward connectivity targets cannot be evaluated unless we know that the metric of connectivity is linked to actual connectivity as a biological outcome [28]. E.g. if connectivity is incorporated into spatial urban planning, with the objective of promoting population or meta-population persistence for urban species, the connectivity metric used as an indicator should be linked to measures of population health (e.g. abundance, genetic diversity etc.) if the indicator is to provide robust recommendations to action plans [29, 30]. The many connectivity metrics used to model urban landscapes for planning often remain unvalidated and assume a direct link to movement and ultimately to species persistence or biodiversity [31–33]. Furthermore, such linkages are often assumed to hold across different taxonomic groups and different urban landscapes. Without explicit validation of connectivity models, i.e., evaluating the strength of association between the connectivity estimates and independent, empirical biological data [25], we risk committing limited resources to improve urban ‘connectivity’ in ways that do not meaningfully increase actual connectivity, i.e. successful movement of species through cities. Here we review the literature and develop a systematic map of the urban connectivity research to assess four research questions: (i) To what extent have connectivity models been validated against empirical biological data? (ii) How does connectivity model validation vary by the stated study objectives? (iii) Where are urban connectivity studies conducted and how is model validation geographically distributed? (iv) Does connectivity model validation vary by the type of connectivity metric, taxonomic groups, or the biological data used for evaluation? We then use the results to identify key research gaps and propose future research directions that could aid in building connectivity measures that are suitable for measuring progress towards global targets of increasing urban connectivity.

Methods

Search Strategy

We used an initial set of naïve search strings to find terms that encapsulate the three main concepts of the review: (1) urban environments, (2) connectivity types, (3) biological outcomes (Table S1 for search terms). A preliminary search was then conducted using the search string in the Web of Science Core Collection and results downloaded as [.bib] files. Using these search results, we identified additional keywords using keyword co-occurrence networks with the ‘litsearchr’ R package [34] to reduce bias in keyword selection and improve the comprehensiveness of the search [35]. After assessing the updated search terms, we searched databases for both primary peer-reviewed literature (Web of Science Core Collection, SCOPUS) and unreviewed grey literature (bioRxiv, ProQuest, and ConservationCorridor.org), adjusting the search syntax according to the specific databases. Screening the grey literature was critical, because connectivity modelling is increasingly being used by government and non-governmental organizations, and for student thesis projects to help inform on-the-ground conservation efforts [29, 36]; Peer-reviewed publication is often not the end-goal of these types of projects and so they would have been missed if we had excluded the grey literature.

Our searches were restricted to publications in English but included literature with abstracts in English but other languages in the full text. This search strategy may miss regional studies, especially localized reports, but we tried to limit this bias by looking for all records citing key review articles on the topic of urban connectivity [31, 33, 37] and by searching through records referenced in the review articles using the ‘citationchaser’ R package [38].

Systematic Screening and Eligibility

Search results from databases were downloaded as [.bib] files, and duplicate records were removed using the ‘synthesisr’ and ‘revtools’ R packages [39, 40], which flag potential duplicates based on DOI, title, and abstract similarity. The resulting deduplicated studies were screened systematically at two stages, first on the titles/abstracts and second on full text, using a set of pre-defined inclusion criteria (Table 1). All potentially relevant studies, including uncertain studies were retained for full text screening. At the title/abstract screening stage we used four broad criteria to remove irrelevant studies, as well as studies that describe computer programs, reviews, theoretical articles, and descriptions of novel connectivity metrics (Table 1). The resulting list of relevant studies was imported into a reference management software for full-text evaluation.

Full-text Evaluation and Data Extraction

For each included study, we extracted data regarding the bibliographic information, geographic (country and city) and temporal scope, stated objectives, connectivity metrics and analytical approaches, assessed biological outcomes, and taxonomic focus (See supplementary Table 2 for more description). For the connectivity metrics, we recognized five conceptual categories: structural, graph-based, potential, functional, and actual connectivity (Fig. 1) and grouped metrics based on the authors’ description of the methodology to specific definitions of well-established connectivity metrics [11, 18] (see Table S2 for a list of connectivity metrics described in the reviewed articles). In addition, we extracted information regarding if and where independent empirical biological data were used as a response variable as validation to estimate the strength of association between the biological outcomes and the connectivity model. We recognized three bins for study validation: (i) validated studies, where connectivity model output were statistically evaluated against independent, empirical biological data using a validation approach (e.g., validation biological data should have higher predicted connectivity value, or stronger relationship than random locations) [25]; (ii) unvalidated studies, where connectivity was estimated, but not compared to biological data; and (iii) indirect studies, where ‘isolation’ or ‘connectivity’ were mentioned in the title, abstract, or discussion, but no measure of connectivity was used as a variable in analyses. Note that studies estimating actual connectivity are implicitly validated because they measure movement through the landscape, which is the ultimate test of a connectivity metric. Nevertheless, actual connectivity estimates do not explicitly include information about landscape structure and therefore do not directly estimate how landscape structure facilitates or impedes movement (connectivity definition; [14]). Therefore, for actual connectivity studies we considered a validated study to be one that compared movement or gene flow against at least one other connectivity estimate [25]. Taxonomic information was extracted at the finest resolution described by the authors. Information was extracted from the main text, tables, figures or supplementary files.

Results

Our final search was conducted in April 2023 across all databases and resulted in 7907 records. After removing duplicates (n = 1436) and screening studies for eligibility based on the titles and abstracts, we identified 430 studies that either directly measured urban connectivity (validated or unvalidated studies), or discussed aspects of connectivity within urban ecosystems but did not include connectivity in their analyses (indirect studies). We found an increasing number of publications over time, with a marked increase in the proportion of unvalidated connectivity estimates after 2012 (Fig. 2A). Eight journals published > 80% of all eligible studies (Fig. 2B). We found that 49.5% of studies validated the connectivity estimates using some form of biological data (validated studies). 24% of studies generated connectivity estimates but did not validate the output (unvalidated studies). Note that within the unvalidated group were a few studies (n = 5) that used biological data to select sites for connectivity assessment but did not use the biological information to evaluate connectivity. The remaining 26.5% of the studies did not directly evaluate connectivity; instead, they either only mentioned connectivity as a potential explanation for their results, or they conflated connectivity with other landscape measures (indirect studies) (see Table S3 for individual studies).

Connectivity Model Validation and Study Objectives

Of the four distinct study objectives in the urban connectivity literature (above), the most frequent objective, in > 50% of studies, was to estimate the impacts of connectivity on a biodiversity outcome (Fig. 3). Most biodiversity studies were validated (70%), i.e. they compared their connectivity estimates to biological data. However, 29% of studies with this objective were indirect studies, i.e. they did not directly evaluate connectivity, by combining the connectivity measure with other variables. The second most frequently stated objective was to evaluate the overall connectivity of a city as a baseline, often to predict how future landscape change due to climate change or urban planning, might affect connectivity. This objective was frequently stated for studies in cities experiencing rapid urbanization that have not had previous city-wide connectivity evaluations. These types of studies became more common over time, and often estimated functional connectivity metrics, but rarely validated the connectivity model with only 10.7% studies being validated. Similarly, studies with the objective of identifying key areas for protection, often used functional connectivity approaches with high-resolution land cover data to model connectivity, but were mostly unvalidated (82.6%) and did not explicitly compare models to empirical data. Studies with stated objective to compare biodiversity across sites in a city, frequently used biological data as a response in analyses, with fewer than 16% unvalidated. However, > 60% of such studies were indirect studies in that they did not directly evaluate and compare connectivity, often comparing sites that were a priori determined to be connected or unconnected to other sites in the urban area.

Geographic Distribution of Urban Connectivity Studies

We found urban connectivity research conducted in 226 different cities across 44 countries (Fig. 4). Nearly a fifth of all eligible studies (n = 81) were conducted in the USA, and approximately 57% of those studies validated their connectivity estimates with some empirical biological data. Comparatively, China had the second highest number of studies (n = 54) and 24% used empirical data to validate connectivity estimates. Urban connectivity research in China, and Asia more generally, has greatly expanded in the past decade [30]. Despite the continued growth of urban connectivity research globally, connectivity in urban areas in the southern hemisphere, particularly in Africa, Southeast Asia, and South America (excluding Brazil) remains relatively understudied compared to in urban areas from the USA, Western Europe, and China.

Connectivity Metrics, Taxonomic Groups, and Biological Data Used for Validation

Across all studies that quantified a measure of connectivity, structural connectivity was assessed (n = 211), e.g. nearest-neighbour distances or habitat amount in a buffer (Fig. 5A). Many of these studies validated the connectivity measures against a biological response (84% validated). Relative to the other connectivity types, we found relatively few assessments using graph-network connectivity in urban areas (n = 45), with > 70% unvalidated. We found 28 assessments of potential connectivity in urban areas. These studies generally based maximum dispersal distance for a focal species on expert opinion or from the literature, but 28% did not compare their connectivity estimates against actual empirical observations of the species. The second most frequently used metrics were based on functional connectivity (n = 85), and often used landcover data, expert opinion, and species-specific habitat associations to model permeability of the urban landscape. Interestingly, we found that 65% of functional connectivity models were unvalidated. The resulting connectivity maps were simply inferred to have a positive effect on biodiversity. Recall that for actual connectivity studies we considered a validated study to be one that compared movement or gene flow against at least one other connectivity estimate. Of the 64 studies of actual connectivity, > 80% were validated.

Species richness was the most frequently assessed biological response compared against connectivity metrics (n = 104; Fig. 5B). Comparatively few studies assessed connectivity against behaviour (n = 5), space-use (n = 5), or mortality (n = 3) (Fig. 5B). Although the most accurate method of validating connectivity estimates of types 1 to 4 would be to compare them to actual connectivity (type 5), specifically movement data, only 9 studies performed this type of validation.

When studies identified a focal taxon, birds were by far the most frequently assessed group (n = 139 of 430) followed by mammals (n = 72) and insects (n = 60) (Fig. 5C). For the insects, research was mostly focused on orders capable of flight, particularly Lepidoptera, Hymenoptera, and Coleoptera. Connectivity for plants was overwhelmingly assessed using structural measures (40 of 59 studies); those using potential measures (n = 9) typically limiting the potential dispersal range by the method of seed dispersal, e.g. wind or animal dispersal, rather than using actual measures of dispersal distance. Of taxa represented in the data, arachnids, amphibians and reptiles were the least represented (combined n = 61 of 439). Actual connectivity metrics were most used in mammal studies (35 of 83), using genetic and tracking (VHF and GPS tracking) movement data.

Discussion

Urban areas are expected to continue expanding [41], presenting an opportunity shape and design cities and green spaces to maximize urban connectivity with biodiversity-informed perspective [7, 42]. However, while connectivity can shape urban biodiversity [43–45], there is no consensus on how to measure or implement urban connectivity to promote desired biological outcomes [32, 37]. To identify challenges to in understanding the relationship between urban connectivity and biodiversity, we conducted a systematic review of 430 studies. The research reviewed here highlight three key findings that pose significant challenges to our understanding of how urban connectivity influences biodiversity. First, approximately half of reviewed studies did not conduct any model validation using empirical biological data (e.g., the connectivity model or map was the output of the study). Second, evaluations of urban connectivity are largely assessed using structural connectivity (e.g., Euclidean distances between patches, or the amount of habitat within a buffer around a focal habitat patch). Thirdly, urban connectivity research is taxonomically biased towards birds as focal study species.

Validation of Urban Connectivity Models

Despite the recognized importance of connectivity in urban landscapes [7, 46] to promote the persistence of wildlife populations, we found that less than half of urban connectivity studies directly evaluated whether the connectivity models produced were associated with a biological response. This reflects a larger trend in the connectivity literature, where complex models are produced but many lack robust validation with independent empirical data [47, 48]. In urban connectivity research, we found an increase in the proportion of studies with unvalidated connectivity models after 2012 (Fig. 2A), which may reflect the urgency to implement connectivity assessments in natural and urban areas where no validation data are available [46, 49]. For instance, studies whose objective was to evaluate baseline urban connectivity as an initial guideline to future urban planning frequently cite the Convention on Biological Diversity and Aichi targets [27, 36] the as an impetus to model functional connectivity, but rarely use empirical biological data to validate the resultant model. We recognize that there is a critical need to evaluate the connectivity of the existing urban ecological network, however the suitability of these metrics to evaluate progress towards global biodiversity targets is unknown in the absence of empirical validation. Furthermore, while we found an expansion of regions where urban connectivity research is conducted [31, 33], with 226 cities across 44 countries represented in the review, there was a geographic bias with nearly one third of all the studies conducted only two countries, the USA and China.

Types of Urban Connectivity Models Assessed

The urban connectivity literature is dominated by assessments of structural connectivity, typically based on Euclidean inter-patch distances or the habitat within a buffer. Although these connectivity metrics do not explicitly include species-specific information, they are commonly validated against biological responses such as species richness, abundance, or occurrence, using correlation or regression approaches. Structural connectivity measures i.e. nearest-neighbour distance, or habitat amount, have been analyzed against abundance and species richness of birds and insects using regression analysis and have found mixed results in both significance and magnitude of the effect [50–52]. While assessing the relationship between structural connectivity and biodiversity is valuable for identifying broad patterns, structural metrics have a spurious correlation with actual organismal movement [48, 53] and generally underperform compared to more functional connectivity measures [54].

Encouragingly, the second most frequently assessed connectivity metrics were functional metrics, like least-cost path or circuit-theory, can incorporate information about species movement behaviour through design of the resistance layer used in modelling [22, 53, 55, 56]. Functional connectivity metrics may be well suited to represent the mosaic of diverse land use across urban areas [57], instead of a binary habitat/non-habitat classification typically modelled by structural connectivity metrics. Functional connectivity models explicitly assign to land cover based on observed individual movement behaviour [58, 59]. For example, Grafius et al. [44] designed their resistance layer for three cities in the UK for their focal species, great tits (Parus major) and blue tits (Cyanistes caeruleus) using expert opinion and information from empirical urban bird movement studies [60, 61], validating their resultant connectivity model against abundances. The use of functional connectivity is encouraging, as there have been calls to incorporate more species-specific information, such as species interactions [62], dispersal behaviour [63], population demographics [64], and temporal dynamics [65, 66] into connectivity modelling. However, many of the functional connectivity models we reviewed were not validated against biological data, and extent to which more species-specific information improves the accuracy of a connectivity model remains unclear [58, 67, 68].

Taxonomic Group Representation

Urban connectivity research has high representation of studies with birds as the focal taxa. Structural, graph-based, and functional connectivity metrics have been most frequently evaluated using birds, underscoring how the knowledge base is heavily influenced by how birds navigate cities [60, 69, 70]. In contrast, connectivity for mammals was frequently assessed using direct measures, for example movement of European hedgehogs (Erinaceus europaeus) [71], or gene flow between white-footed mouse (Peromyscus leucopus) populations [72, 73]. However, because movement success does not directly translate gene flow [24, 65], disentangling how urban connectivity affects the movement process relative to environmental factors remains an outstanding question [74].

Compared to birds and mammals, insects and plants were largely assessed using structural connectivity, revealing opportunity for future urban connectivity research to evaluate multiple modelling approaches. Overall, there is a paucity of connectivity research on urban amphibians and reptiles, and to date there are very few cases in which connectivity models were validated for amphibians and reptiles. Amphibians and reptiles are disproportionately threatened by urbanization [75, 76], likely because of their habitat requirements throughout their life cycle expose them to threats like roadkill [77]. Filling this knowledge gap by evaluating how connectivity models capture movement for amphibians and reptiles in urban ecosystems presents a conservation opportunity.

Validation with Biological Data

While nearly half of the reviewed studies evaluated the connectivity model using some biological data, only nine studies used observed movement data, i.e. actual connectivity (Table S4). The most evaluated biological response used in the reviewed studies was species richness, typically using correlation or regression-based analyses to compare against connectivity metrics. However, this validation approach complicates the interpretation of the effects of connectivity [10, 24, 65] and does not actually demonstrate a direct link between the connectivity metric and the biological response, i.e. the degree to which the landscape facilitates or impedes movement [14]. For example, the relationship between the most common metric, structural connectivity measured as either habitat amount within a buffer or inter-patch distances, and species richness is affected by other local and landscape factors [78–80] (Fig. 6). Both inter-patch distance and indirectly habitat amount, affect actual connectivity, species richness is also directly influenced by habitat amount through the species-area relationship, and indirectly through increasing habitat heterogeneity, rather than from any effect on movement success [81, 82]. This can produce a positive relationship between structural connectivity and species richness that is unrelated to actual connectivity. In general, we urge caution when interpreting the effect of connectivity on biodiversity outcomes, connectivity models should match the spatial and temporal scale of the biological data used for validation the underlying ecological process of interest [48, 66].

The effects of landscape structure on biological outcomes are only partially due to connectivity. Landscape structure directly affects biological responses and influences connectivity metrics. Landscape structure is composed of habitat patches interspersed in the non-habitat matrix. The pattern and quality of the matrix indirectly affect biological outcomes by affecting movement across the landscape and can implicitly (dashed line) be incorporated in graph-based and potential connectivity, and explicitly in functional connectivity modelling. Landscape structure, such as habitat amount, quality, and heterogeneity can also directly affect biological outcomes, beyond their indirect effects on movement. Future Research on Urban Landscape Connectivity

Increased uptake of validation for urban connectivity models will provide more confidence that incorporation of connectivity in urban planning will have the desired outcome (i.e. population persistence) at the ecological scale of interest. To Increasing taxonomic representation in connectivity models across cities would close a gap in understanding and provide a more nuanced perspective on when species-specific or general (e.g. species-agnostic [83]) approaches would be most effective. Additionally, future research on urban connectivity should be explicit about the type of connectivity being modelled and use the most suitability empirical data to evaluate whether connectivity models capture the ecological process of interest (e.g., species movement or gene flow) [29, 63]. Acquiring independent empirical data continues to become more feasible, and the increasing accessibility of online repositories and databases (e.g. Global Biodiversity Information Facility, iNaturalist, etc.) [28, 48, 84] and presents an opportunity for connectivity researchers to validate their models at local and regional scales. With the increasing availability and accessibility of connectivity modelling tools [56, 85–87], we recommend that multiple types of connectivity metrics should be modelled to evaluate the relative influence of the different underlying processes that the models capture. Lastly, as eight journals published > 80% of all urban connectivity studies, we encourage editors of these journals to require some form of model validation to help move this field forward.

Conclusions

The validation of how well these measures of connectivity capture the underlying movement process of organisms across urban ecosystems is still lacking. We encourage researchers, modellers, and conservation practitioners to fill these research gaps, by including some form of validation during the study design, whenever producing a connectivity model is the objective. Furthermore, Consideration of the influence of urban structural features in the matrix for difference taxa, and how they can be incorporated in all types of connectivity may provide clarity on when, and for which taxa specific connectivity metrics are appropriate. We are optimistic that doing so will yield better understanding of the relative importance of connectivity affecting urban wildlife and provide effective conservation strategies that are suitable for biodiversity-informed urban planning and for monitoring progress towards global targets of increasing urban connectivity.
