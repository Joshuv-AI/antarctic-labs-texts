MicroRNA signatures of invasiveness in PitNETs: a focus on miR-186-5p, miR-191-5p, miR-454-3p, and miR-590-5p
Authors: Beata Rak-Makowska, Filip Garbicz, Dawid Mehlich, Emir Sajjad, Zofia Kuśmierczyk, Grzegorz Zieliński, Maria Maksymowicz, Sabrina Doboszek, Tomasz M. Grzywa, Paweł Włodarski, Dominika Nowis, Urszula Ambroziak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposePituitary neuroendocrine tumors (PitNETs) are usually benign tumors of the anterior lobe of the pituitary gland, but some exhibit invasive behavior. Research increasingly highlights microRNAs (miRs) as promising markers of PitNET invasiveness. Our study aimed to identify and validate miR expression differences in invasive versus noninvasive PitNETs, independent of their hormonal activity.MethodsWe evaluated the global expression of 754 miRs in 12 human PitNETs (6 invasive and 6 noninvasive) using TaqMan Low-Density Array cards. We further validated the expression of the 9 most dysregulated miRs in an expanded cohort of 75 human PitNETs using quantitative PCR. Moreover, we examined the expression of 6 mRNAs, for which our miRs had potential binding sites. This was followed by in vitro functional validation using colony formation, migration, and invasion assays in the RC-4B/C rat pituitary adenoma cell line.ResultsOf the 754 miRs, 22 showed significant dysregulation in the initial cohort. In the expanded cohort (n = 75) miR-191-5p was significantly downregulated in invasive PitNETs (p = 0.0062**) across all hormonal activity types, while miR-186-5p was upregulated in gonadotrophs and somatotrophs (p = 0.0264*). In vitro experiments revealed that miR-454-3p and miR-590-5p increased invasiveness of the model cell line RC-4B/C. Coexpression analysis revealed associations between specific miRs and targeted genes such as PTEN, TIMP2, and TGFB1, which may suggest their potential role in PitNETs pathogenesis. Yet, negative correlation among invasive tumors between mir-26a-5p and PTEN expression may suggest its regulatory mechanisms.ConclusionsThis study identifies miR-191-5p and miR-186-5p as potential biomarkers for invasive PitNETs, while functional studies highlight miR-454-3p and miR-590-5p as potential contributors to tumor invasiveness. Correlations with target genes PTEN, TIMP2, and TGFB1 suggest miRs-mediated regulatory pathways in PitNET pathogenesis. These findings provide insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying PitNET invasiveness, with implications for biomarker development and therapeutic targeting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12020-025-04435-0.

Introduction

Pituitary neuroendocrine tumors (PitNETs) aka pituitary adenomas (PAs) account for the third most common intracranial tumors after meningiomas and gliomas. PitNETs can be classified according to size as microadenomas (< 10 mm in diameter) or macroadenomas (> 10 mm) or by the hormonal activity as functioning or non-functioning PitNETs (NF-PitNETs) [1].

While predominantly benign, PitNETs can exhibit invasive behavior characterized by extension into adjacent anatomical structures such as the cavernous sinus, sphenoid sinus, dura mater, and bone. Invasiveness in PitNETs significantly complicates clinical management by reducing the likelihood of complete surgical resection and increasing the risk of tumor recurrence and resistance to standard therapies [1]. Despite extensive research, the molecular underpinnings of PitNETs invasiveness remain poorly understood, and reliable molecular biomarkers associated with this challenging disease presentation have yet to be established. Currently, the assessment of invasiveness largely relies on radiological imaging, predominantly magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), with the Knosp classification system serving as the most widely adopted tool to evaluate cavernous sinus invasion [1, 2]. While the Knosp grading provides valuable preoperative information for surgical planning and prognosis, it inherently lacks sensitivity for subtle infiltrations and cannot replace histological verification. Intraoperative assessment by expert neurosurgeons further aids in detecting cavernous or sphenoid sinus invasion.

The Trouillas classification, proposed by Trouillas et al. in 2013, represents an important step toward an integrated clinico-pathological grading system for PitNETs that combines both proliferative activity and invasiveness to better predict aggressive tumor behavior [3]. Apart from abovementioned invasiveness evaluated based on MRI and intraoperative evidence of cavernous or sphenoid sinus invasion, the classification includes proliferative phenotype, assessed by histopathological markers including Ki-67 labeling index (Ki-67 index ≥ 3%), mitotic count (> 2 per 10 high-power fields), and p53 immunoreactivity (> 10 strongly positive nuclei per 10 HPF)[3]. Currently, there is no single reliable biomarker available that can unequivocally predict the invasive behavior of pituitary neuroendocrine tumors (PitNETs). The most recent 5th edition of the WHO Classification of Endocrine and Neuroendocrine Tumors recommends evaluation of Ki-67 with immunohistochemistry, but at the same time highlights that unlike for other neuroendocrine tumors, PitNETs are not graded based on proliferation indices [4].

MicroRNAs (miRNA, miRs) are small non-coding polynucleotides that can regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally. Growing evidence suggests that dysregulation of miRs expression may play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of PitNETs, with specific miRs linked to invasive tumor phenotypes [5–7].

Certain miRs have previously been linked to invasive PitNET types [8–10]. Our previous study demonstrated that miRs from the oncogenic cluster miR-106b/25/93 are associated with tumor invasiveness in corticotroph PitNETs [11]. Furthermore, miR-410-3p has been shown to exhibit dual roles, acting both as a tumor suppressor and an oncogene in different PitNET types [12]. Zhu and colleagues further demonstrated that alterations in mir-410-3p are associated with the phenotype of pituitary adenomas that invade bone tissue [13]. Additionally, it has been demonstrated that increased expression of miR-34a reduces the expression of AIP (aryl hydrocarbon receptor-interacting protein) in invasive and treatment-resistant somatotropinomas [14].

The current study aimed to investigate both invasive and noninvasive PitNETs, independently of their hormone secretion activity and cellular lineage origin, to identify miRs that could differentiate invasive from noninvasive tumors and shed light on the pathogenesis of PitNETs.

Materials and methods

Clinical data and samples’ collection

Pituitary tumor samples were retrieved from 75 patients undergoing planned transsphenoidal surgery at the Department of Neurosurgery, Military Institute of Medicine, Warsaw during the years 2014–2018. Tumor samples were collected in Cryotubes with RNAlater solution (Invitrogen TM Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to protect against RNA degradation, and stored at -80 °C until the subsequent procedures.

Each patient provided informed consent for the study. A local ethics committee of the Medical University of Warsaw approved all aspects of this study, following the Helsinki Declaration. The overall characteristics of the patients are presented in Table 1. Tumors were classified according to the Knosp’s scale grades [2] and divided into two categories: invasive or noninvasive (0 and 1 were considered as noninvasive, while the Knosp grades 2–4 as invasive).

Pathology evaluation and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The pathological diagnosis of PitNETs was performed by a single pathologist at the Department of Cancer Pathomorphology, Maria Sklodowska-Curie National Research Institute of Oncology, Warsaw, Poland. Pathological evaluation included routine immunohistochemical staining on GH, PRL, ACTH, β-TSH, β-FSH, β-LH, alpha subunit, and Ki-67. In selected cases, immunostaining was extended to detect Cam5.2, SSTR2A, SSTR5, p53, MGMT, Collagen IV, and others. The standard immunohistochemical evaluation was performed as published previously [15, 16].

Patients were diagnosed according to WHO criteria applied during the time of tissue sampling, thus none of the tumors were evaluated for the expression of transcription factors (TF)[17]. Only 2 out of 75 samples were classified as null cell adenomas in which evaluation of the TF could be crucial. Nevertheless, the purpose of our study was to identify miRs independently on both tumors’ lineage background and the presence or lack of hormonal activity, which is why this missing information (compared to current WHO classification 5th edition [4]) does not affect the results of our study.

RNA isolation

Total RNA was isolated from 75 fresh frozen tissues: 36 gonadotrophs, 28 somatotrophs, 5 corticotrophs, 2 plurihormonal, 2 lactotrophs, and 2 null cell pituitary adenomas using AllPrep DNA/RNA Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The quality of the samples was evaluated with the measurement of the absorbance at wavelengths of 260–280 nm using NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Only samples having OD 260/280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.1 were enrolled for further analysis.

TaqMan array human MicroRNA cards

cDNA was synthesized using the TaqMan™ MicroRNA Reverse Transcription Kit in conjunction with Megaplex™ RT Primers Human Pools A v2.1 and B v3.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The cDNA was then pre-amplified using Megaplex™ PreAmp Primers Human Pools A v2.1 and B v3.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to enhance detection sensitivity. Global miRs’ expression was evaluated in 12 PAs samples (6 invasive and 6 noninvasive; Table 1) using The TaqMan Array Human MicroRNA A + B Cards Set v3.0 (Cat. No 4444913, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), each card included 384 wells with a total number of 754 human miR genes. The reactions were performed according to the manufacturer protocol TaqMan® Fast Advanced Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) on the Applied Biosystems ViiA 7 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Next, we selected housekeeping genes (miR-484 and miR-24), which showed the most stable levels (0.428, and 0.471 accordingly) using Normfinder Software (https://moma.dk/normfinder-software). Those two miRs were used for the subsequent experiments as reference genes for our tissues.

The map of miRs expression levels was prepared using freely available tools in open access, specifically “ClustVis” (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/clustvis/) and (http://heatmapper.ca/expression/)[18]. The colors on the map represent the difference in expression levels calculated relative to the average expression levels of two control genes: miR-484 and miR-24. The calculations were performed using the method described in the literature [19].

Reverse transcription and qPCR for MiRs

Reverse transcription was performed using a TaqMan MicroRNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and the appropriate assays according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA): hsa-miR-25-3p (assay ID: 477994), hsa-miR-26a-5p (assay ID: 477995), hsa-miR-29a-3p (assay ID: 478587), hsa-miR-93-3p (assay ID: 478209), hsa-miR-186-5p (assay ID: 477940), hsa-miR-191-5p (assay ID: 477952), hsa-miR-373-3p (assay ID: 478363), hsa-miR-454-3p (assay ID: 478329), hsa-miR-590-5p (assay ID: 478367) in a total volume of 15 µl. All real-time PCR reactions were conducted in MicroAmp Fast Optical 96 Well Reaction Plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), using a machine with the Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR system and 7500 Software V2.0.6 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Each sample underwent the reaction in triplicates. The obtained CT values for the target genes were normalized against the reference genes (miR-24 and miR-484, as identified by NormFinder analysis) using the relative expression level calculated through the standard method of 2^−ΔΔCT as described previously [19].

For the targeted mRNA genes, reverse transcription was performed using 3 µl of isolated RNA, oligonucleotide primers (at a concentration of 1 µM), deoxyribonucleotide triphosphates (dNTPs at a concentration of 1 mM), reverse transcription buffer, and magnesium chloride. The reaction mixture was supplemented with RNA-free distilled water to the final volume of 15 µl. Next, cDNA was used in subsequent real-time PCR according to the manufacturer’s protocol with mRNA SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The sequence of primers is listed in supplementary data (Supplementary Table 1).

Cell culture and transfections

Rat plurihormonal pituitary adenoma cell line RC-4BC (CRL-1903 ™) was purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC Manassas, VA, USA) and cultured according to the manufacturer’s instructions with culture media purchased from Gibco™ company (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA ). Cells were seeded into 6-well plates (500 × 103 cells/well) 24 h before transfection. All transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to manufacturer’s protocol and appropriate miR mimics (50nM) obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA): rno-miR-93-3p (Assay ID: MC19859), hsa-miR-93-5p (Assay ID: MC10951), hsa-miR-186-5p (Assay ID: 477940), hsa-miR-373-3p (Assay ID: MC11024), hsa-miR-454-3p (Assay ID: MC12343), has-miR-590 (Assay ID: MC 11386), and mirVana miRNA Mimic Negative Control. Twenty-four hours after transfection, transfection efficiency was assessed using qPCR with targeted miRs probes (as described in the qPCR Methods section above).

Cell migration and invasion assay

For cell migration assay, VWR® Tissue Culture Plate Inserts, PET Membrane 8.0 μm (VWR International, Radnor, PA, USA) were rehydrated in a serum-free medium. 150 × 103 cells in 250 µl medium (7.5% FBS) were seeded 24 h after transfection in inserts in 24-well plates filled with medium (15% FBS). After overnight incubation, 250 µl of the serum-free medium was added to inserts. After 24 h, cells were removed from the upper surface of the membrane. The cells on the lower surface of the membrane were fixed with methanol and were stained with 0.1% crystal violet in 20% ethanol. Five photographs of each insert were taken with the Nikon Eclipse TE2000-U (Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The pictures were analyzed using ImageJ and additionally verified manually to exclude any artifacts. For the invasion assay, membranes were coated with 100 µl of Matrigel (Corning, NY, USA) in a serum-free medium to a final concentration of 250 µl/ml. Each experiment was independently repeated at least three times, with three technical replicates per condition in each experiment.

Colony formation assay

For the colony formation assay (CFA), 2.5 × 103 cells per well were seeded in triplicates in 6-well plates 24 h after transfection. The medium was changed every three days. After 14 days of incubation, cells were fixed with 4% PFA and stained with 0.1% crystal violet in 20% ethanol. Photos of each plate were taken and analyzed using the ImageJ plugin Colony Area (10.1371/journal.pone.0092444). They were additionally verified manually to exclude any artifacts. As a colony, we considered at least 50 cells or more visible under the microscope. Each experiment was independently repeated at least three times, with three technical replicates per condition in each experiment.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, including mean, median, and standard deviation, were used for clinical data analysis. Statistical tests, such as Spearman’s correlation, Kruskal–Wallis, Mann–Whitney, and ANOVA, were applied where appropriate for experimental data. Benjamini-Hochberg correction was used for array card analysis, while Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons to in vitro experiments. All calculations were conducted using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) and GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Global miRs expression in PitNETs

The global miRs expression was analyzed in 12 tissue samples of PitNETs (6 invasive and 6 noninvasive), for which summary pathological data are provided in Table 1. Among patients with invasive PitNETs, only 22 out of 754 human miRs showed statistically significant dysregulation in expression levels compared to noninvasive tumors (Mann-Whitney U test with Benjamini Hochberg correction, Fig. 1). Based on statistical significance and expression levels, we selected 9 of the most dysregulated miRs for a confirmatory study on a larger group of 75 PitNETs, for which summary pathological data are provided in Table 2.

Correlation between Knosp’s grade and miRs expression

We analyzed the correlation between the expression levels of the selected 9 miRs and Knosp’s scale grades in 75 PitNET samples with available data for both parameters. A weak but significant inverse correlation was observed between miR-191-5p expression and Knosp’s grade (r = -0.3864, p = 0.0006***, Spearman’s rank correlation test) (Fig. 4). Subgroup analysis in somatotroph and gonadotroph PitNETs revealed a weak inverse correlation between miR-191-5p and Knosp’s grade (r = -0.3146, p = 0.0231*, Spearman’s rank correlation test). In contrast, miR-186-5p expression showed a weak but significant positive correlation with Knosp’s grade (r = 0.3412, p = 0.0133*, Spearman’s rank correlation test) (Fig. 5).

Invasiveness, migration, and CFA of the RC-4B/C cells

After a thorough literature search of published data regarding all 9 miRs as oncogenes among different types of cancers, as well as a careful search of their potential binding sites in targeted mRNA genes possibly responsible for oncogenesis (based on TargetScan), we selected the top 5 candidates for further in vitro experiments [20–27].

In vitro experiments showed that miR-454-3p and miR-590-5p increased while miR-373-3p decreased invasiveness of RC-4B/C cells (Fig. 6A-G). However, we did not observe significant changes neither in cells’ migration, nor in the ability to form colonies after transfection (ANOVA test with Tukey correction, Fig. 6H-I).

Correlation between selected miRs and their predicted target genes

Based on a literature search and analysis of available databases (TargetScan and miRTarBase), we selected the following six potential target genes for the nine miRs selected in the confirmatory study PTEN, TGFB1, HIF1A, EPAS1, VEGFA, and TIMP2. The expression levels of these target mRNAs did not differ significantly between the invasive and noninvasive PitNET groups (Mann-Whitney test, Supplementary Fig. 1).

However, strong expression correlation was observed between PTEN and miR-590-5p, miR-26a-5p, miR-25-3p, and miR-29a-3p in all PitNETs regardless of their invasiveness (Spearman’s rank correlation test, Supplementary Fig. 2A). Additionally, miR-454-3p and miR-590-5p exhibited a moderate positive correlation with TGFB1 expression (Spearman’s rank correlation test, Supplementary Fig. 3A-B). An inverse correlation was also identified between miR-454-3p and TIMP2 within the same tissue samples (Spearman’s rank correlation test, Supplementary Fig. 3C). Nevertheless, when we took into account invasive behavior, only mir-26a-5p negatively correlated with PTEN expression (p = 0.015*, r=-0.4879; Spearman’s rank correlation test, Supplementary Fig. 2B). Furthermore, we have found positive correlation between TGFB1 and miR-454-3p as well as miR-590-5p in invasive PitNETs (p = 0.0007***, r = 0.6219 and p = 0.0042**, r = 0.5426 accordingly; Spearman’s rank correlation test, Supplementary Fig. 4).

Additionally, we calculated the correlation of coexpression levels for selected 9 miRs among all 75 tissue samples to see which of those molecules are expressed together and exhibit a strong positive correlation (Spearman’s rank correlation test). The results are shown as a correlation matrix in Supplementary Table 2 with the strength of correlation for each miR and p-value.

Discussion

Our study provides new insights into the role of miRs in the invasiveness of pituitary neuroendocrine tumors (PitNETs). By analyzing global miRs expression and validating key candidates in an expanded cohort, we identified miR-191-5p and miR-186-5p as potential biomarkers associated with tumor invasiveness. Additionally, functional studies in the RC-4B/C pituitary adenoma cell line highlighted miR-454-3p and miR-590-5p as candidates for PitNET progression.

The observed downregulation of miR-191-5p in invasive PitNETs aligns with previous reports linking this miR to tumor suppression in other malignancies. Lutsenko et al. demonstrated that circulating miR-191-5p levels are reduced in patients with somatotroph PitNETs compared to healthy individuals [28]. Our data extend these findings by showing that miR-191-5p is downregulated in invasive tumors regardless of hormonal activity, suggesting a broader role in PitNET pathogenesis.

The upregulation of miR-186-5p in invasive somatotroph and gonadotroph PitNETs raises questions about its role in tumor biology. Prior studies have linked miR-186-5p to the regulation of follicle-stimulating hormone secretion in the pituitary and to the modulation of trophoblast invasion via PTTG1 [29, 30]. Given the established role of PTTG1 in PitNET invasiveness, it is possible that miR-186-5p contributes to tumor progression through this pathway. To date, no studies have directly linked miR-186-5p to PitNET invasiveness. In the rat GH3 cell line and human somatotroph PitNETs, miR-186-5p has been shown to downregulate SKP2, a key regulator of the cell cycle. However, these findings were based on a limited cohort (n = 20) and did not assess correlations with Knosp’s scale grades or Ki-67 proliferation index [31]. Our results confirm a significantly higher expression of miR-186-5p in invasive gonadotroph and somatotroph PitNETs (p = 0.0264*). Despite this, in vitro transfection of RC-4B/C cells with miR-186-5p did not enhance invasiveness, migration, or colony formation. While these findings suggest that miR-186-5p may not be a direct driver of tumor aggressiveness, its consistent upregulation in invasive tumors highlights its potential as a biomarker for PitNET invasiveness.

Among the miRs analyzed functionally, miR-454-3p and miR-590-5p promoted tumor invasiveness in vitro in RC-4B/C cell line. Although a trend toward lower miR-454-3p expression was noted in invasive tumors, the difference was not statistically significant. This contrasts with previous findings by Zhu et al., who reported reduced miR-454-3p levels in invasive PitNETs and suggested its involvement in TNFα regulation [13, 32]. Despite these discrepancies, our data indicate that miR-454-3p may play a role in PitNET invasiveness at the cellular level, reinforcing its potential functional relevance.

In contrast, miR-590-5p did not show differential expression between invasive and noninvasive PitNETs, but its overexpression in RC-4B/C cells resulted in increased invasiveness in our cell line model which may suggest its potential engagement in tumor invasion. It is important to highlight that to ultimately confirm its role, further experiments should be performed. While miR-590-5p has not been previously studied in PitNETs, its role in tumor progression has been documented in colorectal cancer, cervical cancer, and acute lymphoblastic leukemia [33–35].

At the molecular level, we observed significant correlations between miRs and their predicted target genes in PitNETs, regardless of their invasiveness or hormonal activity. Specifically, miR-26a-5p, miR-29a-3p, and miR-590-5p correlated inversely with PTEN, while miR-454-3p showed an inverse correlation with TIMP2. These findings may suggest potential regulatory interactions, that require further mechanistic experimental validation. Both PTEN and TIMP2 are established tumor suppressors, with reduced expression linked to increased invasiveness in various cancers, including PitNETs. Additionally, positive correlations of miR-454-3p and miR-590-5p with TGFB1 may imply that these miRs may potentially indirectly modulate the TGF-β signaling pathway, a known driver of tumor progression. A schematic summary of the analyzed miRs and their potential target genes, illustrating how miRs may contribute to tumor invasiveness is provided in Fig. 7. Importantly, among invasive tumors we have found negative correlation between mir-26a-5p with PTEN expression. Furthermore, we have found positive correlation between TGFB1 and both miR-454-3p and miR-590-5p.

While our study identifies key miRs associated with PitNET invasiveness, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the reliance on just a single rat cell line, RC-4B/C, for functional assays, while informative, may not fully reflect human PitNET biology. Moreover, in different pituitary adenoma cell lines, the same miRs may play different roles. That is why future studies using other cell lines, and preferentially involving primary human pituitary adenoma cell cultures, could provide more physiologically relevant findings. Second, although we identified correlations between specific miRs and target genes at the mRNA level, miRs may modulate target gene protein level without affecting mRNA expression. Therefore, quantitative proteomic studies such as immunohistochemistry or Western blot could provide more insight into the miR-target gene correlations. Our study focused on tissue-based miRs expression; investigating circulating miRs associated with PitNET invasiveness represents a promising avenue that could be explored in the future.

What is more, we used miR-484 and miR-24 as reference genes for normalization, as identified by NormFinder. It should be noted that miR-24 has been previously associated with certain pathologies, including circulating miR-24-3p increase among patients with multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome type 1 (MEN1), while miR-484 was elevated among patients diagnosed with endometrial cancer [36, 37]. Although, these miRs were stably expressed across our tissue cohort, their use may limit extension to further studies on circulating miRs or in contexts where these pathologies co-occur.

Although recent advances in miR-specific RNA sequencing technologies enable accurate, transcriptome-wide profiling, our use of TaqMan Array Cards offered high specificity and consistent quantitation for our cohort at the time of samples collection. We acknowledge that RNAseq allows broader detection of novel and less abundant miRs, but it can be limited by sequence biases and requires further validation by qPCR [38]. Thus we believe that our method was well suited to the purpose of this research.

To sum up, our study expands the understanding of miRs involvement in PitNET progression by identifying miR-191-5p and miR-186-5p as potential biomarkers of invasiveness and indicating the roles of miR-454-3p and miR-590-5p in the RC-4B/C cell line. Correlations of particular miRs with PTEN, TIMP2, and TGFB1 may suggest that miR-mediated regulatory pathways can contribute to PitNET pathogenesis. Negative correlations among invasive tumors between mir-26a-5p with PTEN expression may suggest its involvement in their aggressive behavior, but this finding requires further validation. Our study offer valuable insights for future research on PitNET biomarkers and therapeutic targets.
