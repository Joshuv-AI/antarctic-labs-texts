Label-Free Detection of Waterborne Pathogens Using an All-Solid-State Laser-Induced Graphene Potentiometric Ion Flux Immunosensor
Authors: Pattarachaya Preechakasedkit, Chaiwat Pulsrikarn, Suphachai Nuanualsuwan, Nawarat Rattanadilok Na Phuket, Daniel Citterio, Nipapan Ruecha
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWaterborne pathogens are harmful microorganisms transmitted through water sources. Early and rapid pathogen detection is important for preventing illnesses and implementing stringent water safety measures to minimize the risk of contamination. This work introduces a miniaturized all-solid-state potentiometric ion flux immunosensor for the rapid and label-free detection of waterborne pathogens. A screen-printed silver/silver chloride electrode coated with a reference electrode membrane and polyurethane as an all-solid-state reference electrode was combined with a solid-state contact ion-selective electrode (ISE). An all-solid-state ISE was constructed on laser-induced graphene by coating it with a cationic marker and a carboxylated poly(vinyl chloride)-based membrane for immobilizing antibodies and controlling ion fluxes through the membrane. Proof-of-concept was achieved by detecting Escherichia coli and Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium using the assembled immunosensors within 10 min. The potentiometric response shift attributed to the blocking effect in the ion flux caused by pathogen–antibody interaction corresponded to pathogen concentration, indicating detection limits of 0.1 CFU/mL and working ranges of 0.1–105 CFU/mL. Furthermore, the developed sensors revealed high selectivity and were directly applied in groundwater and tap water without any sample preparation, demonstrating high recovery percentages. The simple operation and elimination of sample preparation are key benefits to further usability of the developed immunosensors for efficient pathogen detection.

Waterborne illnesses are common conditions caused by consuming water contaminated with harmful pathogens found in natural water sources and drinking water supplies.1,2Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Salmonella spp. are two common pathogens that cause disease outbreaks, resulting in hospitalizations and deaths every year.3’4 The neglected consumption of contaminated water happens because the standard methods, including the most probable number and colony counting, are time-consuming (3–7 days required for complete analysis).5,6 Other detection methods such as enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay and polymerase chain reaction can reduce analysis time (below 24 h); however, these methods require enrichment and preconcentration steps, expensive equipment, and advanced expertise.7,8 Therefore, rapid and reliable detection methods are crucial for ensuring water safety and preventing illness outbreaks.

In past decades, electrochemical biosensors have gained attention due to their rapid and accurate analysis, sensitive responses, cost-effective manner, and capability for on-site measurement.9,10 Various electroanalytical techniques are widely employed in biosensors, including potentiometry, amperometry, voltammetry, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy.11−13 Among electroanalytical techniques, potentiometry is a controlled-current technique in which a potential is measured between two electrodes, whereas other techniques require the application of a potential or current during the measurement.14 A benefit of the controlled-current technique is the capability of using low-cost measurement instruments.

Potentiometric biosensors based on ion-selective electrodes (ISEs) have been recognized as powerful tools for detecting ionic analytes due to their simple instrumentation, easy operation, fast response, low cost, and portability.15 The potentiometric biosensor performance relies on the measurable potential of the ISE versus the constant potential of a reference electrode (RE).16,17 Regarding ISEs, laser-induced graphene (LIG) electrodes based on photothermally converting a carbon-rich precursor into a graphene carbon matrix have received more attention as miniaturized electrodes with benefits over other electrodes in terms of higher sensitivity and faster production without toxic waste.18−20

Recently, potentiometric biosensors have been integrated with antibody–antigen interaction to achieve a potentiometric immunosensor for a label-free strategy.21 The detection principle relies on the surface-blocking effect of zero-current ion fluxes through a polymeric membrane.22 The antibody–antigen interaction at the membrane surface modulates ion fluxes; thus, the ion concentration change at the electrode surface corresponds to the analyte concentration, resulting in a potential response shift.23 In previous reports on potentiometric immunosensor fabrication, Silva and coworkers developed a homemade pipette-tip electrode as an ISE consisting of antibody-immobilized gold nanoparticles formed on a poly(vinyl chloride) (PVC) membrane, filled with a marker ion solution, leading to the inability to be a disposable device.24 The same group also fabricated an all-solid-state ISE as a disposable device by integrating a paper strip electrode containing antibody-immobilized carboxylated PVC (PVC–COOH) with a marker ion-soaked paper.25 However, these prior approaches still use a double junction silver/silver chloride (Ag/AgCl) reference electrode (RE), making them unsuitable as portable miniaturized devices for on-site analysis.

In this work, the first report of an all-solid-state ion flux potentiometric immunosensor for waterborne pathogens is presented, combining an all-solid-state RE and an ISE on a single miniaturized device. The all-solid-state RE was a screen-printed Ag/AgCl electrode coated with a reference electrode membrane (REM) and polyurethane (PU). The all-solid-state ISE was an LIG electrode coated with tetrabutylammonium chloride (TBACl) as a cationic marker layer and a PVC–COOH-based membrane as a polymer membrane layer for immobilizing antibodies and controlling ion fluxes across the membrane. The controlled passive flow of TBACl as a lipophilic marker ion through the sensing membrane can generate the potentiometric signal.25 Proof-of-concept was achieved by detecting E. coli (EC) and Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium (S. Typhimurium, ST) as model pathogens. Based on the surface-blocking effect after pathogen–antibody interaction, the potentiometric response corresponded to pathogen concentrations due to the accumulated cations at the electrode surface. The assay was completed within 10 min, revealing a rapid analysis with a broad working range and high sensitivity. Furthermore, the application in groundwater and tap water analysis was achieved without any sample preparation, revealing high recovery percentages.

Electrode Fabrication

A polyimide tape (PI) was attached to a polyvinyl chloride sheet (PVC) as a PI/PVC substrate for the fabrication of the reference electrode (RE) and the ion-selective electrode (ISE), and electrode patterns were designed using CorelDRAW® X6 (Alludo HQ, Canada). The RE was fabricated by screen printing Ag/AgCl ink on the substrate, followed by baking at 55 °C for 1 h. Afterwards, the laser engraving was performed on the substrate containing the RE using a 50 W mini CO2 laser machine (LRT-2030 Plus, ROBO Technologies Automation (Suzhou) Co., Ltd., China) with a lens-to-material distance of ∼8 mm to generate an LIG as the solid contact ISE base. Finally, the working area of the electrodes was limited by coating them with nail polish as an insulator (Figure 1a).

(a) Schematic diagram of the fabrication process and (b) working mechanism of the all-solid-state potentiometric ion flux immunosensor.

All-Solid-State Potentiometric Ion Flux Immunosensor Fabrication

An all-solid-state potentiometric ion flux immunosensor was fabricated, as shown in Figure 1a. The solid-state contact RE was fabricated according to a previously reported method.26 The REM cocktail was prepared by dissolving PVB (78.1 mg/mL) and supersaturated NaCl (50 mg/mL) in MeOH. The REM was then drop-cast three times (1 μL each at 10 min intervals) on the deposited Ag/AgCl ink, followed by drop-casting 1 μL of PU (1 mg/mL) to minimize chloride ion leakage.

ISE fabrication was adopted from a previous method with some modifications.25 The PVC–COOH-based membrane cocktail containing 2-NPOE plasticizer (≈66.6 wt %) and PVC–COOH (≈33.4 wt %) was prepared with a solute content of 5 wt % using THF as the solvent. A cationic marker (TBACl) was drop-casted twice (1 μL each at 10 min intervals) on LIG. Afterward, the PVC–COOH-based membrane was drop-casted (1 μL each at 10 min intervals) as a carboxylic acid-functionalized surface and a polymer membrane layer. The carboxylic acid groups were then activated by incubating with the coupling agent (10 mM EDC:70 mM NHS in 0.1 M MES, pH 6.0, 3 μL) at 4 °C for 2 h, followed by washing off the excess EDC twice with MES and water, respectively. Next, the antibody was immobilized by drop-casting anti-E. coli antibody or anti-salmonella antibody (3 μL) and incubating at 4 °C under a moist atmosphere overnight (16 h). After washing twice with 0.01 M PBS (pH 7.4) to remove weakly adsorbed antibodies, the unbound surface was blocked with 1 wt % BSA (3 μL) for 30 min. Finally, two washing steps with PBS were performed to obtain a ready-to-use sensor.

Potentiometric Measurement

The potentiometric measurements were performed using a potentiostat (Sensit BT, Palmsens, The Netherlands) controlled with PStrace software. For pathogen detection, each sample solution (200 μL) was dropped onto the sensor, and potentiometric measurement was initiated 300 s after sample loading to allow for pathogen–antibody binding. The potential was constantly recorded for over 300 s. To obtain a potentiometric response curve, all measurements were performed using three independent sensors for pathogens in the concentration range of 10–2 to 106 CFU/mL. The response relies on the potential difference (ΔE) before and after the pathogen–antibody reaction, and the ΔE value was calculated by subtracting the E value measured in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB, pH 7.4) as background (BG) and the E value measured in the presence of pathogens (ΔE = Esample – EBG). The ΔE data were reported as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The limit of detection (LOD) was estimated as the concentration resulting in a potential change higher than three times the standard deviation measured with three independent devices in phosphate buffer (3SDBG),25 and sensitivity was obtained from the slope of ΔE as a function of pathogen concentration.

Water Sample Analysis

Groundwater and tap water samples were collected from different sources in Pathum Thani province, Thailand, and directly examined using the developed sensor without any sample preparation. As a proof-of-concept for quantitative analysis, samples spiked with different amounts of pathogens to final concentrations of 1, 102, and 104 CFU/mL were analyzed.

Results and Discussion

Potentiometric Ion Flux Immunosensor Working Mechanism

The key strategies to establish the all-solid-state potentiometric ion flux immunosensor are to miniaturize the device and rapidly reach a stable steady-state potential. The detection principle is based on the blocking effect of the marker ion flux across a polymer membrane after the analytes bind to antibodies on the ISE surface.22 TBACl was used as a cationic marker because it can passively flow through the sensing membrane, leading to a potential response change.25 A commercial PVC–COOH with 1.8% carboxylic groups was selected as a polymer membrane due to its suitability to immobilize biomolecules through various protocols.23 In the designed immunosensor, antibodies against pathogens were covalently bound to the PVC–COOH-based membrane using an intermediate EDC/NHS for stable coupling between carboxylic groups of the membrane and amino groups of the antibodies.27 The working mechanism based on surface blocking is shown in Figure 1b. In the absence of a pathogen, the cationic marker can easily leach out into the aqueous sample solution, resulting in a measurable low potential response. In the presence of pathogens, the binding between pathogens and antibodies can hinder cation flux across the membrane, leading to the accumulation of the cations on LIG and a measurable potential response increase. Therefore, the increase in the potential responses caused by the increase in the cation concentration corresponds to the pathogen concentration.

Electrode Characterization

To effectively fabricate the LIG electrode based on photothermally converting a PI carbon-rich precursor into a graphene carbon matrix using a CO2 mini laser, laser power and speed were investigated using cyclic voltammetry (CV) in the presence of 5 mM ferri/ferrocyanide ([Fe(CN)6]3–/4–) in 0.1 M KCl.28 The peak currents increased after increasing laser power until 2.5 W and were then constant due to the complete conversion to graphene at 2.5 W (Figure S1a,c). After investigating laser speed using 2.5 W power (Figure S1b,d), the peak currents were constant until 160 mm/s speed and then decreased at a higher speed due to the incomplete conversion from the PI to graphene.29 Therefore, 2.5 W power and 160 mm/s speed were selected as optimal conditions for the high-quality fabrication of LIG used as the ISE base with the smallest error bars, referring to high reproducibility.

The ISE foundation is the transducing layer fabricated by coating LIG with TBACl and the PVC–COOH-based membrane, respectively. Each step-by-step modified layer was characterized by SEM. Figure 2a shows the porous 3D graphene sheets of LIG. After coating LIG with TBACl, a thin layer appeared on the graphene sheet, leading to a denser surface with reduced porosity (Figure 2b). The PVC–COOH modified on TBACl/LIG revealed that the polymer membrane completely coated the surface (Figure 2c).25 The SEM cross-sectional image (Figure 2d) also shows each layer of the modified LIG, representing PVC, PI, TBACl/LIG, and PVC–COOH, respectively. Furthermore, the chemical structure was studied by FTIR spectroscopy (Figure 2e). The LIG spectrum showed no characteristic peaks due to the complete graphene formation.28 The TBACl/LIG spectrum displayed the characteristic peaks at 1228, 1118, and 897 cm–1, attributed to C–N stretching, C–C stretching, and C–H bending, respectively,30 related to the TBACl (C16H36NCl) structure. After coating the PVC–COOH, several characteristic peaks of PVC were observed including the C–H stretching vibration at 2855–2927 cm–1, C=C stretching at 1522 and 1607 cm–1, C–H bending at 855 cm–1, and C–Cl stretching at 744 cm–1.31 The characteristic peaks at 1767, 1425, and 1254 cm–1 were attributed to C=O stretching, O–H stretching, and C–O stretching, respectively, referring to the carboxylic group.32 These results verify the successful modification of TBACl and PVC–COOH on LIG.

Generally, Ag/AgCl electrodes are used as REs in electrochemical biosensors for cost-effective and miniaturized approaches.16 For the potentiometric measurement, all-solid-state REs without liquid junctions have been designed to provide a stable potential using a saturated chloride ion and a polymer membrane to allow ion fluxes without significant effects from the RE potential or interfering ions in samples.33 This work fabricated the all-solid-state RE by drop-casting the REM and PU on a screen-printed Ag/AgCl ink trace deposited on the PI/PVC substrate. Each step-by-step modified layer was characterized by SEM. Figure S2a displays laminated flake structures of the Ag/AgCl ink.34 The REM-coated Ag/AgCl ink (REM/Ag/AgCl ink) revealed a smooth surface of the PVB membrane containing a crystalline NaCl deposit, and the presence of NaCl was confirmed by EDX (Figure S2b). After drop-casting PU (PU/REM/Ag/AgCl ink), the surface was completely coated with the polymeric membrane to avoid chloride ion leakage (Figure S2c). The SEM cross-sectional image (Figure S2d) also shows the presence of each layer of the modified RE consisting of PVC, PI, Ag/AgCl ink, REM, and PU, respectively. To investigate the potential stability, the uncoated Ag/AgCl ink, the REM/Ag/AgCl ink, and the PU/REM/Ag/AgCl ink were individually used to measure various concentrations of KCl solutions against a commercial Ag/AgCl RE (Figure S2e). Compared to the uncoated Ag/AgCl ink trace with a response to changes in chloride ion concentrations, the REM/Ag/AgCl ink still responded at a high concentration (10–1 M) of chloride due to the possibility of chloride ion leakage. The finalized all-solid-state RE system (PU/REM/Ag/AgCl ink) revealed constant potential values (38.4 ± 3.4 mV) in the presence of KCl solutions ranging from 10–6 to 10–1 M, because the PU coating presumably hindered the chloride ion leaching out of the RE and enhanced the potential stability.35 Therefore, reliable all-solid-state RE fabrication was achieved with the small error bars demonstrating the reproducibility and suitability for potentiometric measurements with a sample-independent potential response.

Parameter Optimization

The time-dependent potentiometric response in the presence of various E. coli concentrations (1–104 CFU/mL) was investigated to select the appropriate incubation and measurement times required to reach a stable steady-state potential.25 During the measurement (Figure S3), the potential significantly decreased between 0 and 300 s and reached stable values at 300 s in the case of high E. coli concentrations (≥103 CFU/mL) because the complete binding between pathogens and antibodies can fully block the cation fluxes. Thus, 300 s (5 min) was selected as the optimal incubation time. At E. coli concentrations below 103 CFU/mL, the potential continued to slightly decrease from 300 to 600 s due to the gradual flux of cations until reaching a stable value at 600 s. Therefore, the range between 300 and 600 s (5 min) was selected as the optimal measurement time. The obtained results demonstrate that the all-solid-state potentiometric immunosensor enables rapid measurements by quickly reaching stable potential values for all pathogen concentrations within 10 min, indicating the completion of pathogen–antibody interactions and cation fluxes on the ISE surface.

To achieve the highest sensitivities, various parameters were investigated, including the TBACl concentration, the amount of PVC–COOH, and the concentration of antibodies. The optimization was individually performed by measuring five concentrations (1–104 CFU/mL) of pathogens using three independent devices. The sensitivities (slope) of each response curve were recorded and are summarized in Table S1. Since the potentiometric response relied on the ion fluxes, the cation concentration was studied between 2 and 8 mg/mL of TBACl in the presence of E. coli (Figures S4a and S5a–d). The sensitivities increased with the TBACl increase until 4 mg/mL and then decreased at higher TBACl concentrations due to the difficult fluxes of the high density of ions, resulting in the BG response increase and the ΔE decrease. Likewise, the ion fluxes depended on the surface-blocking ability of the PVC–COOH-based membrane for antibody immobilization. After investigating the amount of PVC–COOH between three and nine layers in the presence of E. coli (Figures S4b and S6a–d), the sensitivities increased with the increase of the PVC–COOH until 5 layers. At a higher number of layers of PVC–COOH, the sensitivities decreased because the thick layer might hinder the ion fluxes in the presence and absence of a pathogen as well. Thus, 4 mg/mL TBACl and five layers of PVC–COOH were selected as optimal values. Furthermore, the potentiometric immunosensor also relied on the pathogen–antibody interaction at the membrane surface, and the number of antibodies used affected the response. The concentrations (0.1–5.0 μg/mL) of anti-E. coli antibody (Figures S4c and S7a–d) and anti-Salmonella antibody (Figures S4d and S8a–d) were investigated in the presence of E. coli and S. Typhimurium, respectively. The sensitivities increased with the antibodies increase until 0.5 μg/mL and were also constant at 1.0 μg/mL. Then, the sensitivities decreased at the high density of antibodies (5.0 μg/mL) because of the hindrance effect.36 Therefore, 0.5 μg/mL was selected as the optimal concentration of both anti-E. coli and anti-Salmonella antibodies.

After the optimal conditions were obtained, the performance of the all-solid-state potentiometric immunosensors was compared to that of the potentiometric immunosensor using a commercial Ag/AgCl RE (Figure S9). All measurements were performed using an equal sample solution volume of 200 μL. Comparing the data shown in Figure S10a,b, there were no significant differences in sensitivities between the two different REs, verifying the high potential of the reliable all-solid-state RE. Furthermore, each ISE modification step was electrochemically characterized by measuring the charge transfer resistance (Rct) using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy in the presence of 5 mM [Fe(CN)6]3–/4– in 0.1 M KCl, and Nyquist plots were fitted with the Randles equivalent circuit (Figure 3a,b). The Rct of the bare LIG was 0.94 ± 0.02 kΩ. After coating with TBACl and PVC–COOH, the Rct increased to 4.11 ± 0.10 kΩ because the thick layer of PVC–COOH coated on TBACl/LIG hindered the electron transfer. The Rct values also increased after immobilizing antibodies to 9.80 ± 0.14 kΩ (EC) and 9.69 ± 0.16 kΩ (ST). The BSA blocking affected the Rct values, leading to increases to 12.81 ± 0.13 kΩ (EC) and 12.57 ± 0.18 kΩ (ST). After pathogen–antibody interaction, the increase in the EC concentration led to the Rct values increasing to 17.73 ± 0.26 kΩ (102 CFU/mL) and 20.55 ± 0.28 kΩ (103 CFU/mL). Similarly, the Rct values also increased with the ST concentration increase (102 CFU/mL: 16.53 ± 0.25 kΩ and 103 CFU/mL: 18.95 ± 0.29 kΩ). These results indicated that the Rct values significantly increased after each modification step as well as with increasing pathogen concentration due to the electron transfer hindrance, verifying the successful modification of the immunosensor and the pathogen–antibody interaction.

Potentiometric Immunosensor Performance

For proof-of-concept, E. coli and S. Typhimurium were detected in the concentration range of 10–2 to 106 CFU/mL. Each measurement was completed within 10 min (5 min binding interaction and 5 min measurement time). As seen in the potentiometric response curves of EC (Figure 4a) and ST (Figure 4b), the increase of the TBACl concentration at the electrode surface caused by the surface-blocking principle correlated with increasing pathogen concentrations, leading to an increase in the stable potential as well. Linear response curves of the potential difference (ΔE = Esample – EBG) as a function of the logarithmic concentration of pathogens were observed in the ranges of 10–1–105 CFU/mL (7 orders of magnitude) with sensitivities of 16.172 (EC, Figure 4c) and 15.578 mV/decade (ST, Figure 4d). The LODs estimated from a potential change higher than 3SDBG (4.9 mV for EC and 4.6 mV for ST) were found to be 0.1 CFU/mL for both pathogens. The sensor also exhibited desirable reproducibility, with relative standard deviations (RSDs) below 6.0% over the full range of concentrations using three independent sensors.

The selectivity for EC and ST detection (1 CFU/mL) was tested in the presence of other waterborne pathogens (105 CFU/mL). Besides the pathogens, potentially interfering cations existing in water were also investigated, including NaCl, KCl, CaCl2, and MgCl2 (10–2 M).42 There were no significant potential differences observed (below 4.9 mV for EC and 4.6 mV for ST) when sampling other pathogens and the interfering cations (Figure 5a,b), verifying excellent selectivity. Negligible effects were also observed after detecting mixtures of EC or ST containing other pathogens, demonstrating the anti-interference capability. Furthermore, the stability was investigated by storing the sensors under ambient conditions and detecting five low concentrations (10–1–103 CFU/mL) of pathogens (Figure S11a,b). The sensitivities remained over 93% after 2 weeks and decreased to over 70% after 3 weeks (Table S2), which were larger (64%, 3 weeks) than in the case of a previous report on the potentiometric immunosensor.25

To compare the performance (Table 1), most previous reports on electrochemical immunosensors of pathogens revealed higher LODs and longer analysis times due to the multistep procedures required for incubating, washing, and adding labeling antibodies or redox probes.37,40,41 Although another report on a single-step labeled electrochemical immunosensor provided an LOD as low as the proposed method, it required two antibodies and gold-deposited gold nanoparticles as labels, leading to an increase in the operation cost.28 Compared to the current approach, previous reports on potentiometric immunosensors relied on a conventional double junction Ag/AgCl RE, preventing the miniaturization of the sensor.24,25 To the best of our knowledge, this work represents the first achievement of an all-solid-state potentiometric ion flux immunosensor fabricated as a single miniaturized device, providing a short analysis time, low LODs, and wide working ranges. Hence, the developed sensor represents a significant advancement in the field of electrochemical immunosensors for detecting pathogens with high sensitivity and selectivity.

Water Sample Analysis

To guarantee practical applicability, groundwater and tap water were directly tested using the proposed sensors without sample preparation. Samples with ΔE values below the cutoff levels (4.9 mV for EC and 4.6 mV for ST) were interpreted as not detectable, and spiked samples were examined by adding different concentrations of pathogens. As summarized in Table S3, both pathogens were not detectable in all collected water samples, verified by the standard methods as outlined by APHA 9221:2017 for E. coli and ISO 19250:2010 for Salmonella spp.43,44 The spiked samples displayed high recovery percentages of 94–109% with RSDs below 5.9%. Consequently, the developed sensor has the potential to serve as a valuable tool, allowing rapid and accurate measurements for monitoring water quality and ensuring water safety.

Conclusions

A label-free potentiometric immunosensor based on the blocking effect of ion flux was successfully developed by constructing all-solid-state ISE and RE on a single miniaturized device. The all-solid-state ISE consisted of a cationic marker and a PVC–COOH-based membrane for antibody immobilization and ion flux control. As proof-of-concept, E. coli and S. Typhimurium detections were achieved under optimal conditions, exhibiting a short analysis time, broad working ranges, and low LODs. The developed sensor revealed good sensitivities of 16.172 mV/decade (EC) and 15.578 mV/decade (ST) with high selectivity and has the potential for the rapid and reliable detection of pathogens in water samples without the need for complex sample preparation or time-consuming analysis. By providing simple operation and a miniaturized design, this sensor could be a promising prototype, allowing easy integration into portable and automated systems for future advancements in sensor technology.
