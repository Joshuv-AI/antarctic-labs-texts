We present an older male with severe aortic regurgitation (AR) scheduled for transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). Preoperative imaging identified a giant abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) at high risk of iatrogenic rupture during TAVR. To address both pathologies concurrently, a combined procedure of TAVR and endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) was successfully performed. Table 1 shows a timeline of the case.

Case Summary

The patient was a 68-year-old man who experienced chest tightness and shortness of breath after activity for approximately 5 years, with a 10-year history of hypertension. He was diagnosed with symptomatic severe AR and heart failure. His heart failure symptoms persisted without clinical improvement despite receiving guideline-directed medical therapy. Echocardiography demonstrated an aortic valve regurgitation area of 10.3 cm2. The ascending aorta and aortic sinuses measured 39 mm in diameter. The left ventricular end-diastolic diameter was 56 mm, the left ventricular end-systolic diameter was 41 mm, and the left ventricular ejection fraction was 52%. The computed tomography angiography (CTA) showed scattered calcification in the aortic root and the aortic valve, an annulus perimeter of 76.4 mm, and a circumference of the left ventricular outflow tract of 78.5 mm (Figures 1A and 1B). The diameter of the left, right, and noncoronary sinus was 32.0, 34.0, and 35.3 mm, respectively (Figure 1C). The height of the left and right coronary artery was 17.9 and 19.5 mm, respectively (Figures 1D and 1E). The angle of the aortic annulus was 44° (Figure 1F). Our cardiac team planed for a TAVR procedure to address the patient's severe AR. However, during the preoperative aortic CTA examination, we unexpectedly revealed a large eccentric AAA with a diameter of 61 mm (Figures 2A and 2B). The length of the aneurysm neck of the AAA was 47 mm, and the height of the left and right common iliac artery was 39 and 28 mm, respectively (Figure 2C). To address both pathologies concurrently, a combined procedure of TAVR and EVAR was successfully performed. We achieved the following results at the end of the procedure: abdominal aortic angiography showed no endoleak, and there was no paravalvular leakage in the valve prosthesis. The patient was discharged 7 days after EVAR and TAVR. The symptoms of the patient were relieved, and NYHA functional class was improved from class III to class II at discharge. No major adverse cardiovascular events were observed during the follow-up period. At 3-month postoperative follow-up, echocardiography and cardiac CTA showed that the position of the valve prosthesis was normal and functioning well, and no obvious regurgitation was detected (Figures 3A to 3C). Procedural Steps

We proceeded under general anesthesia and with transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) guidance. Predefined computed tomographic fluoroscopic angles and TEE imaging were used to guide the positioning and release of the valve.

The right internal jugular vein was punctured, a 6-F sheath was inserted, and a temporary pacemaker was implanted into the right ventricle. Two 6-F arterial sheaths were placed in the right femoral artery (RFA) and left femoral artery (LFA), respectively, using a modified Seldinger technique. A Perclose ProGlide device was then deployed at each of the arteriotomies in the fashion of preclosure with knots tagged to the drape. A J wire was placed through each Perclose ProGlide device, and 8-F sheaths were advanced through the RFA/LFA access sites.

Deployment of the abdominal aortic stent

A 6-F pigtail catheter was inserted via contralateral femoral access to the upper edge of the renal artery, for use in aortography. A 6-F JR 4 angiographic catheter was advanced along the ipsilateral femoral access to the ascending aorta, and then exchanged with a superstiff guidewire to establish an interventional pathway. We then sequentially dilated the ipsilateral femoral access using 14-F, 18-F, and 20-F introducer sheaths to ensure adequate tract preparation. Under fluoroscopic guidance, the bimodular Endurant II stent-graft system (Medtronic) was introduced via the ipsilateral femoral access, and the proximal end of the main body was deployed immediately below the lowest renal artery, ensuring an adequate infrarenal neck seal. Then, deployment of the ipsilateral and contralateral limbs followed in standard fashion. Because of underexpansion, we post dilated with a 46-mm postexpansion balloon. Final aortography revealed the abdominal aortic stent was well apposed without evidence of endoleak.

Coronary angiogram

The 6-F JL 4.0 and 6-F JR 4.0 angiography catheters were separately advanced to the aortic root. After adjusting the catheter tip position to the coronary artery ostium, angiography revealed approximately 50% to 60% stenosis at the distal left main coronary artery, 70% to 80% stenosis in the proximal to mid segment of the left anterior descending artery, and no significant stenosis in the left circumflex artery.

Deployment of the prosthetic aortic valve

A 6-F pigtail catheter was inserted along the contralateral femoral access into the right coronary sinus, and aortic root angiography was performed. A 6-F JR 4.0 angiography catheter was inserted along the ipsilateral femoral access into the aortic root, and then a 20-F sheath was inserted into the RFA. We inserted the Lunderquist guidewire into the left ventricle to enhance support, followed by delivery of the valve system Evolut Pro+ 34 mm (Medtronic). Rapid cardiac pacing was initiated followed by unsheathing of the valve to the 2/3 position. After confirming proper placement via aortography and TEE assessment, the valve was fully unsheathed and disengaged from the delivery catheter. Final aortography and TEE revealed excellent flow in the left and right coronary arteries and no paravalvular regurgitation.

The RFA and LFA sheaths were removed, followed by deployment of Perclose ProGlide sutures to secure hemostasis at the arterial access site, with subsequent application of a pressure dressing. The overall procedural time was 115 minutes, and the total amount of contrast administrated was 200 mL.

Potential Pitfalls

Because transfemoral devices are not commercially available, the off-label use of a prosthetic aortic valve for severe AR may lead to severe paravalvular leakage, and valve displacement or detachment.1

Considering the AAA with a maximum diameter of 61 mm and the presence of eccentric dilation, if TAVR was performed first, the 20-F delivery sheath might damage the abdominal aortic wall during device deployment. Furthermore, the hemodynamic changes resulting from improved severe AR after TAVR could elevate intra-aortic pressure. This combination of factors could significantly elevate the risk of AAA rupture, which would be potentially fatal. We therefore performed EVAR first to stabilize the AAA followed by TAVR for severe AR.

Conclusions

TAVR is a safe and effective minimally invasive interventional treatment method. It has become the preferred treatment option for symptomatic older patients with aortic stenosis.2 However, the application of TAVR in severe AR remains limited.3 Studies have shown that using domestic J-Valve prosthesis for transapical access TAVR in patients with severe AR is safe and effective, but transapical access is still not the preference.4 Regrettably, a transfemoral device is not commercially available. Nevertheless, with accumulating experience and ongoing advancements in medical technology, off-label use of TAVR in severe AR is increasingly being implemented.5 To achieve the desired effect, we planned for TAVR with a 34-mm Evolut Pro + transcatheter heart valve (Medtronic).

AAAs increase with advancing age, and it is well established that both the rupture rate and mortality associated with AAA are significantly elevated. For patients with AAA measuring 50 to 69 mm in diameter, the annual risk of rupture ranges from 3.0% to 20.0%.6 Studies have shown that approximately 6% of patients undergoing TAVR have concomitant AAA.7 Concomitant severe AR and AAA represent a clinically uncommon condition. Herein, we report a case of a patient diagnosed with severe AR concomitant with a giant AAA. Given the patient's exceptionally high surgical risk as indicated by a Society of Thoracic Surgery score of 11.8% and perceived intolerance to open-heart surgery, the cardiac team opted for a combined TAVR and EVAR procedure to minimize perioperative risks.

In our experience, compared with staged TAVR and EVAR procedures, simultaneous intervention demonstrates distinct clinical advantages, including the ability to use the same vascular access site for both interventions, compatibility of interventional devices between the 2 techniques, reduced overall costs through procedural integration, and elimination of the need for a second anesthetic administration.8 Our case demonstrates that single-stage combined TAVR and EVAR may be considered a novel therapeutic paradigm for surgically high-risk patients or those ineligible for open-heart surgery with concomitant AAA and severe aortic valve disease. However, more observational data are required to validate the long-term clinical outcomes of this combined TAVR and EVAR in patients with prohibitively high surgical risk.
