Metallic clip removal 40 years after scleral encircling surgery for retinal detachment: A case report
Authors: Mikito Matsumoto, Hisashi Matsubara, Yukiko Muramoto, Yoko Mase, Kumiko Kato, Mineo Kondo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo report our findings in a case in which a metallic clip used for scleral encircling surgery was accidentally detected and surgically removed more than 40 years after its use in the initial retinal detachment surgery.ObservationsThe patient was a 54-year-old woman who had undergone surgical treatment for a rhegmatogenous retinal detachment in both eyes during childhood. Her preoperative decimal best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 0.5 in the right eye and 0.01 in the left eye. Postoperatively, the BCVA improved to 1.0 in the right eye, but remained at 0.01 in the left eye. During follow-up period, the BCVA in the left eye improved to only 0.03. Due to the progression of a cataract, the BCVA in the left eye had been reduced to hand motion for more than 10 years and to light perception for the past 2 years. She sustained a mild head injury in a traffic accident and underwent plain skull radiography. A metallic-like foreign body was detected in the left orbit in the radiographs. The site of the foreign body corresponded to the site of a previous scleral encircling procedure. Although the patient was asymptomatic, the presence of the retained metallic implant raised concerns regarding magnetic resonance imaging safety and resulted in significant patient anxiety. Surgical exploration demonstrated that the metallic clip was partially located beneath the inferior rectus muscle and was encapsulated by fibrotic tissue. The clip, along with the associated silicone band, was successfully removed without intraoperative or postoperative complications.Conclusions and importanceThis case underscores the long-term clinical and psychological implications of retained surgical materials from historical surgical procedures. Awareness of legacy implants and accurate documentation of surgical materials are essential to support safe, informed clinical decision-making in long-term postoperative care.Keywords: Retinal detachment, Encircling, Metallic clip, Silicone band, MRI

Introduction

Scleral encircling surgery is a surgical technique in which a silicone band is placed around the globe to induce scleral indentation and was standardized in the 1950s.1,2 It has been widely used for conditions such as juvenile retinal detachment and refractory proliferative vitreoretinopathy. Even today, scleral encircling is sometimes used in combination with vitrectomy for difficult or recurrent retinal detachment cases. Initially, non-absorbable sutures were used to secure the ends of the silicone bands in the encircling procedures. However, in the 1960s, metallic fixation clips were introduced to simplify the surgical procedures.1,2 This method involved mechanically crimping and securing the overlapping portions of the silicone band with metallic clips. However, silicone sleeves have become the predominant method for band fixation, and the use of metallic clips has largely fallen out of favor.1,2

Beginning around 1980, the clinical application of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) prompted examination of the magnetic properties of metallic implants. However, some metallic implants, introduced before the widespread use of MRI raised safety concerns including implant displacement or thermal injury during MRI examinations.3 We report a case in which a metallic clip placed during childhood scleral encircling surgery was accidently discovered more than 40 years later. It was subsequently removed because of concerns regarding potential complications during future MRI examinations.

Case report

The patient was a 54-year-old woman who had undergone scleral encircling surgery and cryotherapy for a retinal detachment in her left eye at age 11 years and in her right eye at age 20 years at the Mie University Hospital. At presentation, a macular detachment was present in the left eye but not in the right eye. The preoperative decimal best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 0.5 in the right eye and 0.01 in the left eye. The retina was reattached in both eyes after the surgery. Postoperatively, the BCVA improved to 1.0 in the right eye, but remained at 0.03 in the left eye because of the macular atrophy. She attended regular follow-up examinations at 3- to 12-month intervals. The BCVA of the left eye gradually decreased due to cataract progression, decreasing to hand motion about 10 years earlier and to light perception by age 52 years.

At age 54 years, she sustained a head contusion in a traffic accident. Plain skull radiography performed for head evaluation revealed a metallic foreign body within the left orbit (Fig. 1a and b). Her decimal best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 1.0 in the right eye and light perception in the left eye. The intraocular pressures (IOPs) were 12 mmHg and 13 mmHg in the right and left eyes, respectively. Slit-lamp examination showed a mild cataract in the right eye and a hypermature cataract in the left eye (Fig. 2a). Fundus examination revealed choroidal atrophy along the arcade vessels in the right eye (Fig. 2b), but the hypermature cataract obscured the fundus of the left eye. The other ophthalmic findings were unchanged compared with those at the most recent routine follow-up visit.

Orbital computed tomography (CT) showed a high-density structure corresponding to a silicone band encircling the left eye globe along with a streak artifact inferior to the eye globe suggestive of beam hardening caused by a metallic object (Fig. 3). A review of the historical surgical records showed no documentation regarding the fixation method for the silicone band during the scleral encircling procedure of the left eye. Based on the imaging findings suggesting the presence of a metallic clip, inquiries were made to physicians familiar with the surgical technique used during that era and to a medical device manufacturer (Inami & Co., Ltd.). Their responses suggested that a custom-made stainless steel clip may have been used. Consultations with radiologic technologists at our institution, we learned that MRI is generally not recommended when the material composition of an implanted metallic device is unknown or when the manufacturer's safety information is unavailable. The patient expressed marked anxiety regarding the potential restriction on future MRI examinations, and she strongly requested the removal of the metallic clip. Before surgery, we explained the risks associated with clip removal including a recurrence of the retinal detachment, scleral perforation, and ocular injury. We also explained the potential benefits of cataract surgery and the disadvantages of not performing cataract surgery. However, the patient did not wish to undergo cataract surgery but requested removal of the metallic clip alone. A surgical plan was therefore formulated to remove the clip alone by spreading its jaws, following a previously described clip-removal technique using forceps.1

Surgical findings

After conjunctival peritomy, a silicone band and an associated metallic clip encapsulated within fibrotic tissue were identified. The fibrotic sheath was dissected to expose the clip. Approximately one-half of the clip was buried beneath the inferior rectus muscle (Fig. 4a), making exposure of the clip jaws technically difficult. Moreover, the jaws were highly rigid and immovable; therefore, extraction of the clip alone proved technically difficult. To avoid excessive traction and to reduce the risk of extraocular muscle injury, both ends of the encircling silicone band were transected, and the clip was removed en bloc with the silicone band. No thinning or perforation of the sclera was observed at the site of contact with the metallic clip (Fig. 4b). The excised silicone band measured approximately 9 mm in length, and the clip measured approximately 4 mm (Fig. 4c). No intraoperative or postoperative complications occurred. Postoperatively, the BCVA of the left eye was light perception, and the IOP in the left eye remained unchanged. B-scan ultrasonography confirmed the absence of a recurrent retinal detachment. No postoperative complications were observed during the one-year follow-up period.

Discussion

Before the widespread clinical introduction of MRI in the 1960s and 1970s, metallic implants used in surgical procedures were commonly manufactured from stainless steel, titanium, or cobalt-chromium alloys. In ophthalmology, titanium clips were used for scleral shortening procedures to treat retinal detachments.4 In addition, procedures using silver clips were reported for macular holes and macular schisis associated with high myopia.5 Among these materials, stainless steel was particularly favored because of its relatively low cost, ease of processing, and stable availability.6 Therefore, after its safety has been confirmed, it is used today as an implant in specific ophthalmic surgeries.7 These materials were selected primarily for their corrosion resistance and mechanical strength, but their interaction with magnetic fields did not need to be considered. Around 1970, metallic fixation clips made of tantalum were commercialized to be used to secure silicone encircling bands.1,2 However, for custom-made products, stainless steel was likely selected because of its low cost and availability. Many stainless steel variants are generally non-ferromagnetic and considered relatively safe in terms of magnetic attraction and dislodgment. In an animal study, magnetic metallic fragments smaller than 3× 1 × 1 mm in the vitreous cavity were reported not to migrate during MRI.8 In addition, MRI has been performed without complications in patients with intraorbital or intraocular metallic foreign bodies.9,10 These findings suggest that the presence of metallic foreign body does not invariably preclude MRI. However, the risk should be assessed individually according to the material, size, location, and fixation status of the foreign body because they may still pose a risk of radiofrequency-induced heating during MRI.11 Therefore, prior evaluations and specialist judgments are required to determine whether MRI examinations are permissible in patients with implants of material of unknown composition.12

In our case, the patient was relatively young and expected to undergo MRI examinations in the future. In addition, she had not been informed that metallic clips were used during her original surgery, and the unknown composition and size of the implant precluded definitive confirmation of its magnetic properties and safety for MRI examinations. Under these conditions, it is understandable that the patient had uncertainty of future MRI feasibility and requested the removal of the clip despite being asymptomatic. Our case illustrates that clinical decision-making may be influenced not only by medical rationality but also by patient values and perceptions of future risks.

Previous literature has described extraction techniques that separate the clip jaws to enable the removal of the clip alone.1,2 Based on the literature, we initially planned to extract the clip alone. However, the limited mobility of the silicone band and clip prevented exposure of the clip's jaws, and the jaws themselves were rigid and could not be spread, making extraction of the clip alone difficult. This rigidity may have been related to the device's custom-made nature, which likely differed in mechanical properties from commercially available tantalum clips. Accordingly, when clip removal is considered in similar cases, preoperative planning should include the possibility of en bloc removal with the silicone band rather than the removal of the clip alone.

Removal of silicone buckling elements is generally reserved for symptomatic cases. Previous studies have reported postoperative complications, including recurrent retinal detachment (12%), ocular hypertension (8%), and diplopia (4%).13 The risk of complications may be higher when removing the encircling elements than when removing segmental buckles.14 In addition, transscleral erosions caused by chronic pressure of rigid materials against the globe have been reported.2 In our case, no postoperative complications were observed. This may be attributed to the minimal step between the band and the clip, which resulted in limited chronic mechanical stress to the sclera. Postoperatively, neither an IOP reduction nor recurrent retinal detachment was observed, indicating that the clip removal was performed safely. However, detailed fundus examination remained difficult because of the residual mature cataract. Although this interpretation is based on a single case, it suggests that metallic clips may exert relatively limited chronic mechanical stress on the sclera under certain conditions and that elective removal can be performed safely with appropriate surgical planning.

Finally, concerns have been raised about future MRI examinations because it has become impossible to determine whether a metallic implant was used for a previous retinal detachment.15 This case highlights the so-called “legacy device problem,” in which medical materials once considered safe become potential constraints on future diagnostic or therapeutic options as technology evolves. Clinicians should recognize that such issues may arise in long-term postoperative care and should consider both technical and patient-centered factors when managing retained surgical materials.

Limitations

In this case, the indication for surgery was not primarily visual recovery but the reduction of potential future risks associated with a long-standing metallic foreign body in the periocular region and the preservation of future diagnostic options, including MRI examinations. Although no postoperative complications have been observed to date, the possibility of a recurrence of a retinal detachment cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, cataract surgery may be considered in the future if the patient wishes to undergo the procedure after being fully informed of the potential risks and benefits.

Conclusions

Advances in medical technology may render previously acceptable surgical materials incompatible with future diagnostic or therapeutic modalities. Careful long-term selection of materials and accurate documentation of surgical records are therefore essential to support informed decision-making by both physicians and patients.
