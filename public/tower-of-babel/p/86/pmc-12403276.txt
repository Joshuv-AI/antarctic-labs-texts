The potential of probiotic derivatives of Bacillus coagulans Hammer on induction of apoptosis in colorectal adenocarcinoma cell line in vitro
Authors: Maryam Mashhoori Vayghan, Parvaneh Saffarian, Maryam Tajabadi Ebrahimi, Hamid Asadzadeh Aghdaei, Abbas Yadegar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) remains as one of the leading causes of human morbidity and mortality worldwide. Dysbiosis in the structure of the gut microbiota resulting in increased abundance of harmful bacteria and decreased beneficial bacteria has been frequently associated with CRC development. Recently, probiotics and their derivatives have received great attention as anticancer agents for prevention and treatment of various cancers. This study aimed to assess the potential effects of probiotic Bacillus coagulans Hammer and its derivatives on induction of apoptosis in human colon adenocarcinoma cell lines in vitro.MethodsCell viability of HT-29 and Caco-2 cells was examined by MTT assay after exposure to varying concentrations of pasteurized and UV-killed B. coagulans Hammer, its extracellular vesicles (EVs), and cell-free supernatant (CFS). The apoptotic effects of different B. coagulans Hammer derivatives on HT-29 and Caco-2 cells were determined by analyzing the expression level of apoptosis-related genes (Bad, Bax, Bcl-2, Caspase-3, Caspase-9) using RT-qPCR assay. Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry was performed to assess apoptosis. Protein expression of Bax and Bcl-2 was measured by Western blotting.ResultsHigh concentrations of B. coagulans Hammer derivatives significantly (P < 0.05) reduced viability of HT-29 and Caco-2 cells compared to untreated control. The results of RT-qPCR assay showed significant decrease in the expression level of antiapoptotic gene Bcl-2, and notable increase in the expression level of proapoptotic genes Bax, Bad, Caspase-3, and Caspase-9 in HT-29 and Caco-2 cells by B. coagulans Hammer derivatives. Flow cytometry demonstrated a high percentage of apoptotic HT-29 cells compared to untreated cells after exposure to B. coagulans Hammer derivatives. Western blotting analysis confirmed that treatment with EVs, UV-killed cells, and CFS significantly decreased Bcl-2 expression, and increased Bax expression in HT-29 cells.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that derivatives of probiotic B. coagulans Hammer can exert anticancer activity by induction of apoptosis in colon cancer HT-29 and Caco-2 cells. Background

Cancer is a heterogeneous disease with unique genomic and phenotypic features characterized by cell death disorder [1]. Colorectal cancer (CRC) is already the third most frequently cause of cancer death and fourth diagnosed cancer with steadily rising in many developing countries [2]. Resistance to apoptosis (programmed cell death) and uncontrolled cell proliferation was recognized as an important trait of cancer cells [3, 4]. Therefore, a factor that can induce apoptosis in cancer cells can be used as an anticancer medication [5]. Surgery with or without radiation therapy is typically performed to improve outcomes for patients with CRC [6]. Moreover, conventional chemotherapeutic treatments lead to most common side effects and gastrointestinal toxicities such as nausea and vomiting, oral ulcers, diarrhea, hepatotoxicity, myelo suppression and immunosuppression [7]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore novel therapeutic strategies against CRC to promote treatment efficiency while having less adverse effects.

Probiotics are non-pathogenic microorganisms that have beneficial effects to human health by modulating the immune system, enhancing gut barrier function, restoring the microbiota structure, and exerting antipathogenic and anti-inflammatory activities [8, 9]. Most probiotic species are classified as lactic acid bacteria (LAB) that are an important group of probiotic organisms, which belong to various bacterial genera such as Lactococcus, Streptococcus, Lactobacillus, Pediococcus, and Leuconostoc [10]. It is known that some cytopathogenic Bacillus species, which are not well known as LAB and yeasts, are being used as probiotics [11]. Bacillus coagulans Hammer is a spore-forming probiotic that has demonstrated strong gastrointestinal resilience and immunomodulatory potentials [12]. Recent evidence has demonstrated that different probiotic strains of B. coagulans can contribute to cancer suppression through production of metabolites such as lactic acid and bacteriocins, which modulate oxidative stress and promote apoptosis in cancer cells [13–17]. Notably, it has been reported that B. coagulans represents significant anticancer activity, including the inhibition of proliferation and induction of apoptosis in colorectal cancer models [14].

Several studies have addressed the functional properties of byproducts, derivatives and inactivated forms of probiotic strains, including culture supernatant [18], exopolysaccharide extracts [19], bacterial cell wall components [20], and heat-killed bacteria [21], in inhibition of cancer cell proliferation through induction of apoptosis and ultimately cell death [22]. Moreover, bacterial extracellular vesicles (EVs), which are membrane-enclosed and submicron-sized structures produced by both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, have been demonstrated to modulate the host immune system by suppressing inflammation and pathological effects [23]. EVs play critical roles in host-microbe intercellular communication and signal transduction, which can effectively deliver active biomolecules and regulatory factors and bioactive to target tissues and cells that modulate tumor-related pathways and biological processes [24]. Some probiotics strains were shown to produce EVs carrying effector molecules, which can trigger host immune responses, alleviate food allergy response [25, 26], and induce apoptosis [27]. Apoptosis is an evolutionarily conserved sequential cell suicidal process that maintains cellular homeostasis and regulates cell fate [28]. However, one of the important mechanisms of cancer cells is the negative regulation or resistance to apoptotic cell death [29]. Apoptosis pathway genes are driven by Bcl-2 family, which include antiapoptotic genes (Bcl-2) and apoptotic genes (Bax).

In the last few years, great consideration has been paid to the use of nonviable bacterial supplementation as substitute products to reduce the potential hazard of live bacteria, especially in high-risk individuals such as immunocompromised patients [30]. Thus, the present study aimed to investigate the apoptotic effects of pasteurized and ultraviolet (UV)-killed probiotic strain B. coagulans Hammer, and its EVs and cell-free supernatant (CFS) on human colon adenocarcinoma cell lines (HT-29 and Caco-2 cells) by measuring the expression level of apoptosis-related genes (Bad, Bax, Bcl-2, Caspase-3, Caspase-9) using quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) assay. Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry was carried out to examine apoptosis. Protein expression of Bax and Bcl-2 was quantified by Western blotting.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strain and culture

B. coagulans Hammer was purchased from Natures Only, INC (USA). The strain was cultured in brain heart infusion agar (BHI, Merck, Germany) after 24 h under aerobic atmosphere. A loopful of grown colonies on BHI agar was cultured in 200 ml of BHI broth (Quelab, Canada) supplemented with 0.5% vitamin K1 with shaking (150 rpm) under aerobic conditions for 24 h until an OD600 of 0.5 (an equivalent of ~ 1.5 × 108 cfu/ml) was reached.

Isolation of EVs derived from B. coagulans Hammer

The isolation of EVs was performed using polyethylene glycol (PEG)-based enrichment method as described previously [31]. Briefly, PEG powder (Sigma, Germany) with molecular weight of 6000 Da (MV = PEG 6000) and sodium chloride (1 M) was homogenized in distilled water and sterilized at 121 °C for 15–20 min. In order to obtain sufficient biomass, a large portion of B. coagulans Hammer colonies were inoculated into BHI broth for 24 h. After that, the supernatant-containing medium was obtained by several centrifugations: first 45 min at 6000 × g then 30 min at 10,000 × g to remove the cellular debris. After centrifugation, the medium was added to an equal volume of 16% PEG solution and was completely mixed via inversion and was incubated for a minimum of 12 h at 4 °C. The suspension was centrifuged at 3200 ×g for 1 h at 4 °C and was allowed to drain for 5 min. Finally, the remaining pellet was suspended in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and the isolated EVs were stored at −80 °C for further analysis [32]. Protein concentrations of EVs were quantified using bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (DNA biotech, Tehran, Iran). Sodium dodecyl-sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) was performed on 15% SDS gel to evaluate the protein content of extraction of EVs.

Pasteurization and UV inactivation of B. coagulans Hammer

For pasteurization of B. coagulans Hammer, the grown colonies were inoculated in BHI broth under abovementioned aerobic conditions for 24 h with shaking (150 rpm). The culture supernatant was pasteurized using a temperature of 70 °C for 30 min; then it was cooled to 4 °C for 60 min and stored at −80 °C [30]. For preparation of UV-killed B. coagulans Hammer, the culture supernatant was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. Then, bacterial pellets were washed two times in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH = 7), and diluted in PBS to 1.5 × 108 cfu/ml. The bacterial supernatant was disposed under UV light at 254 nm, four times for 30 min and 5 min rest between the periods. To confirm the elimination of live bacteria, the suspension was cultured on BHI agar [33]. The collected supernatant stored at −80 °C for further analysis.

Preparation of bacterial CFS

The CFS from B. coagulans Hammer was prepared as previously described [30]. Briefly, the overnight bacterial culture in BHI broth (50 ml) was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was further collected by sterile filtration through a 0.45 μm filter (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and stored at −80 °C.

Scanning electron microscope

SEM analysis was used to identify the morphological changes of EVs as previously described. Briefly, samples were washed gently with PBS and submerged in fixation solution that includes 4% paraformaldehyde, 1% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH = 7.2) on a slide and incubated at room temperature (RT) for 24 h. The samples were incubated with 1–2% osmium tetroxide for 1 h at RT, after being washed three times with PBS and dehydrated completely via a graded ethanol series and dried using the cool critical point method. Finally, a thin gold layer (20–30 nm) was coated on the samples and scanned under a field emission SEM system (TESCAN VEGA3 SEM, Brno-Kohoutovice, Czech Republic).

Transmission electron microscopy

The shape and size of the EVs were evaluated by TEM analysis. In brief, 10 µL of EVs were pipetted onto a 400-mesh copper grid with carbon-coated formvar film. After 2 min incubation and removing the extra liquid by blotting, the grid was placed on 10 µL of 2% (w/v) uranyl acetate. After blotting and repeating the last step, the grid became air-dried. Finally, we used a TEM microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkohen, Germany) to further analyze the isolated EVs.

Size distribution of EVs

The size distribution of the extracted EVs (diluted in 1 ml of PBS) was measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (ZEN3600, Malvern Instruments, UK).

Cell culture

HT-29 and Caco-2 cell lines were sourced from the Research Institute for Gastroenterology and Liver Diseases, Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran. The cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco-Invitrogen, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ until approximately 80% confluence was achieved. Sub-culturing was performed using trypsin-EDTA. To ensure experimental reliability, cells between passages 20 and 30 were used for cell experiments [34].

Cell viability assay

The cytotoxicity of B. coagulans Hammer on HT-29 and Caco-2 cells was determined by MTT assay. Cells were treated with various concentrations of B. coagulans Hammer EVs (1, 10, 50 and 100 µg/ml), UV-killed and pasteurized bacteria (MOI 1, 10 and 100), and CFS 20% (v/v) for 4, 8, 12 and 24 h time points. The culture medium was replenished with fresh media containing 10% MTT solution (0.5 mg/ml in PBS) (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and incubation continued for a further 4 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Finally, the supernatant was eliminated and the solubilized MTT-formazan in 200 µl of DMSO was added to each well. The absorbance was evaluated by a microplate reader (Eon, BioTeck, USA) at a wavelength of 570 nm and a reference wavelength of 630 nm [35].

Cell treatments

HT-29 and Caco-2 cells were counted and seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well in 24-well plates and maintained in a humidified incubator (37 °C/5% CO2). Then, cells were treated for 24 h with pasteurized and UV-killed of B. coagulans Hammer (MOI 10), its EVs (10 µg/ml), and CFS 20% (v/v). The untreated HT-29 and Caco-2 cells were harvested as control groups. Experiments were performed in triplicate.

Detection of apoptosis by Annexin V-FITC/PI

Apoptosis detection was carried out according to protocol described for Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis kit with propidine iodide (PI) (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). Briefly, HT-29 cells (1 × 105 cells/well) were treated with pasteurized and UV-killed, EVs, and CFS of B. coagulans Hammer for 24 h. Thereafter, harvested cells were collected to Eppendorf tubes and centrifuged (1200 rpm, 5 min). Cold cell staining buffer used to homogenize cell suspension, and washed twice with this solution. Then, cells were resuspended in 100 µL of binding buffer, and solutions of PI stain (10 µL) and FITC-Annexin (5 µL) were added to cells. The samples were mixed vigorously, and incubated at RT in the dark for 20 min. The flow cytometry analysis was performed on a BD FACS Calibrator (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), and data was analyzed with FlowJo 8.8.6 software (Tree Star Inc., Ashland, OR, USA) [36].

Total RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from treated HT-29 and Caco-2 cells using Total RNA Extraction kit (Parstous, Mashhad, Iran), cDNA synthesis was performed by Easy cDNA synthesis kit (Parstous, Mashhad, Iran) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quantity and quality of the extracted RNA was assessed by NanoDrop® ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis. The RNA was reverse-transcribed to cDNA using the Easy cDNA synthesis kit (Parstous, Mashhad, Iran) per the manufacturer’s instructions. All cDNA preparations were frozen at −20 °C until further analysis. To study the expression level of the Bax, Bad, Bcl-2, Caspase-3, and Caspase-9 genes, RT-qPCR assay was employed using specific primers (Table 1). The Rotor-Gene Q (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) thermocycler was applied to amplify targeted cDNAs in a 20 µl-reaction mixture containing 10 µl SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix 2X (Ampliqon, Odense, Denmark), 0.5 µl of each primer, 2 µl of cDNA and DDW. The standard amplification program was as follow: initial denaturation 95 °C for 10 min followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 20 s, annealing at 59–61 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 20 s. Reactions were carried out in duplicate and the transcriptional level of each gene was normalized to β-actin. The relative expression level of each gene was analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blotting

To examine apoptosis-related protein expression in HT-29 cells, Western blotting was carried out following treatment with B. coagulans Hammer derivatives. After washing with cold PBS (pH = 7.2), cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (BioBasic, Markham, Canada), and the resulting lysates were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA assay kit (DNAbiotech, Tehran, Iran). Equal amounts of protein were resolved on SDS-PAGE gels (Bio-Rad, USA) and transferred to PVDF membranes using a wet transfer apparatus (Bio-Rad, USA). Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat milk prepared in TBS-T for 2 h at RT, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies specific for Bax, Bcl-2, and β-actin. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT. Signal detection was performed using a chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Scientific, USA), and protein bands were visualized with the ECL imaging system (Merck, Germany). The blotting bands were quantified using ImageJ software (Fujifilm, USA), and values were normalized to β-actin expression.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, USA) version 9 was used for statistical analysis. To measure the statistical significance between groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied. The data were presented as the mean standard error of mean (SEM) of the average of three independent experiments. The differences were considered statistically significant when *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 and ****P < 0.0001.

Results

Characterization of B. coagulans Hammer EVs

The protein profile of the isolated EVs was assessed using SDS-PAGE, revealing a diverse range of proteins with molecular weights ranging from 10 to 200 kDa (Fig. 1 A). Furthermore, the total protein concentration of the EVs was quantified to be approximately 685.08 µg/ml using a BCA protein assay kit. Visualization of the EVs by SEM, TEM, and DLS analysis confirmed their spherical morphology with diameters ranging from 100 to 300 nm (Fig. 1B-D).

Impact of inactivated B. coagulans Hammer and its derivatives on HT-29 and Caco-2 cell viability

MTT assay was applied to assess cell viability after exposure to different concentrations of B. coagulans Hammer EVs (1, 10, 50, and 100 µg/ml), various MOIs of pasteurized and UV-killed B. coagulans Hammer (1, 10, and 100), and CFS 20% (v/v) compared to control groups. Notably, treatment with concentrations 10, 50 and 100 µg/ml of B. coagulans Hammer EVs after 24 h resulted in a significant reduction in HT-29 (P < 0.01, P < 0.0001, P < 0.0001, respectively), and Caco-2 (P < 0.01, P < 0.01, P < 0.01, respectively) cell viability (Fig. S1A, Fig. S2A). Interestingly, overnight exposure to UV-killed B. coagulans Hammer MOI 10 and 100 (P < 0.0001, P < 0.0001, respectively) and pasteurized B. coagulans Hammer MOI 10 and 100 (P < 0.0001, P < 0.0001, respectively) significantly decreased the viability of HT-29 cells (Fig. S1B-C). Similarly, UV-killed B. coagulans Hammer MOI 10 and 100 (P < 0.01, P < 0.01, respectively) and pasteurized B. coagulans Hammer MOI 100 (P < 0.001) significantly decreased the viability of Caco-2 cells (Fig. S2B-C). Also, B. coagulans Hammer-derived CFS caused a significant reduction in the viability of HT-29 and Caco-2 cells after 24 h (P < 0.01) (Fig. S1D, Fig. S2D). Based on MTT results, B. coagulans Hammer EVs at 10 µg/ml, and pasteurized/UV-killed B. coagulans Hammer at MOI 10 were used as the optimal concentrations for further treatment experiments.

Induction of apoptosis-related genes by B. coagulans Hammer derivatives in HT-29 and Caco-2 cells

Following 24 h of treatment, B. coagulans Hammer EVs and UV-killed cells significantly reduced Bcl-2 gene expression in HT-29 (P < 0.001 and P < 0.001, respectively) and Caco-2 cells (P < 0.0001, P < 0.0001, respectively) (Fig. 2 A). In addition, the CFS of B. coagulans Hammer caused a significant reduction in Bcl-2 gene expression in Caco-2 cells (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2 A). A notable increase in the expression of Bax was detected following exposure to B. coagulans Hammer UV-killed cells (P < 0.001) in both HT-29 and Caco-2 cells (Fig. 2B). B. coagulans Hammer EVs, pasteurized cells, and CFS significantly increased Cas-3 gene expression in HT-29 (P < 0.01, P < 0.001, P < 0.001, respectively) and Caco-2 cells (P < 0.0001, P < 0.001, P < 0.0001, respectively) (Fig. 2 C). Moreover, a significant increase in Cas-3 gene expression was detected after treatment of Caco-2 cells with B. coagulans Hammer UV-killed cells (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2 C). Treatment with B. coagulans Hammer EVs, pasteurized cells, and CFS caused significant upregulation in Bad gene in both HT-29 (P < 0.05, P < 0.0001, P < 0.001, respectively) and Caco-2 cells (P < 0.015, P < 0.001, P < 0.001, respectively) (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, Cas-9 gene expression was significantly upregulated by B. coagulans Hammer pasteurized cells (P < 0.01) and CFS (P < 0.05) in HT-29 cells (Fig. 2E), whereas only pasteurized cells (P < 0.01) induced a statistically significant increase in Cas-9 expression in Caco-2 cells (Fig. 2E).

The apoptosis rate of HT-29 cells in different treatment groups

The apoptosis rate of HT-29 cells was assessed by flow cytometry using Annexin V-FITC/PI assay. Figure 3 shows the flow cytometry analysis, indicating an increased frequency of apoptotic cells after treatment with UV-killed and pasteurized B. coagulans Hammer as well as its isolated EVs and CFS compared to untreated cells. In the control group, 84.1% of cells were alive, 1.86% were in early apoptosis, 3.03% were in late apoptosis, and 11% were necrotic cells (Fig. 3 A). In contrast, cells treated with EVs showed a decrease in live cells (35.6%), an increase in early apoptosis (13.3%), and late apoptosis (22.7%), with a notable increase in necrotic cells (28.4%) (Fig. 3B). Cells treated with UV-killed B. coagulans Hammer showed 42.7% live cells, 20.6% in early apoptosis, 24.2% in late apoptosis, and 12.6% necrotic cells (Fig. 3 C). Pasteurized cells of B. coagulans Hammer resulted in 32.6% live cells, 11.5% in early apoptosis 17.4% in late apoptosis, and 38.6% of necrotic cells (Fig. 3D). The CFS of B. coagulans Hammer led to a decrease in live cells (44.7%), an increase in early apoptosis (12.2%) and late apoptosis (29.6%), and 13.4% of cells being necrotic (Fig. 3E). Compared to control group, the rate of early and late apoptosis significantly increased in all treatment groups (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3F-G). Overall, total apoptosis was significantly higher in all treatment groups compared to control cells (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3H).

Modulation of Bcl-2 and bax protein expression after treatment with B. coagulans Hammer derivatives

To support the RT-qPCR results at the protein level, Western blotting was performed to assess the expression of Bcl-2 and Bax proteins in HT-29 cells following treatment with B. coagulans Hammer derivatives (Fig. 4 A). Consistent with the gene expression data, B. coagulans Hammer EVs, UV-killed cells, and CFS significantly reduced Bcl-2 protein level (P < 0.01, P < 0.001, and P < 0.05, respectively) (Fig. 4B). Moreover, the expression of Bax protein was significantly upregulated in HT-29 cells after treatment with B. coagulans Hammer EVs, UV-killed cells, and CFS (P < 0.01, P < 0.0001, and P < 0.01, respectively) (Fig. 4 C). Discussion

Growing concerns over the safety of live probiotic strains, mostly in at-risk populations such as neonates, the elderly, and immunocompromised individuals, have encouraged the exploration of non-viable probiotics and their derivatives as safer alternatives [38]. Among these options, EVs secreted by probiotic bacteria have been widely explored as promising anticancer candidates due to their capacity to mediate intercellular communication and modulate host cellular responses [37, 39–41]. Nevertheless, isolating EVs from Gram-positive bacteria remains technically challenging because of the structural complexity of their thick peptidoglycan layer [42]. In this study, we employed a PEG-based precipitation method to isolate EVs from B. coagulans Hammer. This technique was selected as a practical alternative to ultracentrifugation for its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and compatibility with bacterial systems. The resulting vesicles exhibited a spherical morphology and a size range of 100–300 nm, with protein diversity confirmed by SEM, TEM, DLS, and SDS-PAGE analyses, which validated the reliability of our isolation protocol.

While probiotics like Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium have been well studied for their beneficial effects against colon cancer, the effects of spore-forming, heat-resistant probiotic B. coagulans are not well understood [16]. Here, we aimed to evaluate the beneficial effects of pasteurized and UV-killed B. coagulans Hammer, as well as its EVs and CFS, on the expression level of apoptosis-related genes (Bad, Bax, Bcl-2, Caspase-3, and Caspase-9) by RT-qPCR and flow cytometry in vitro using HT-29 cells.

In the present study, the preliminary characterization of B. coagulans Hammer EVs revealed that these nanostructures can contain a variety of proteins, which may be implicated in intercellular communication and the modulation of host immune responses [42–44]. Additionally, the biophysical properties of EVs, including their size and protein content, are crucial for their interactions with target cells and cargo delivery [44, 45]. However, further investigations are required to identify the specific protein content of B. coagulans Hammer EVs and their potential roles in inducing apoptosis in cancer cells.

Apoptosis is a highly controlled process characterized by a series of signaling events and the activation of caspase enzymes, which are pivotal mediators of apoptotic cell death [46]. The Bcl-2 gene family is pivotal in regulating the intrinsic mitochondrial apoptotic pathway. Within this family, Bcl-2 functions as an antiapoptotic protein, whereas Bax is a proapoptotic protein [47]. The balance between these two gene groups determines the cell’s fate, either survival or apoptosis [48]. Furthermore, downregulation of Bcl-2 is a key event promoting apoptosis through the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway [49]. The results of the present work demonstrated that treatment with B. coagulans Hammer derivatives including EVs, UV-killed cells and CFS can markedly reduce the expression of antiapoptotic Bcl-2 molecule in both HT-29 and Caco-2 colon cancer cells. We also found that these bioactive molecules and cells significantly enhanced the expression of proapoptotic Bax molecule in both cell lines. These initial findings highlight the importance of further exploring the EV-associated and soluble components derived from inactivated cells of B. coagulans Hammer as potential inducers of apoptosis. Moreover, pasteurized cells of B. coagulans Hammer induced apoptosis in both cell lines at a lesser extent in comparison to other bacterial derivatives investigated in this study. This finding may be attributed to structural or biochemical changes induced during heat treatment of pasteurization, which can alter the profile of bacterial components and consequently affect their interaction with host cells through post-translational mechanisms or stress-adaptive responses.

Nearly similar changes in the gene expression of antiapoptotic and proapoptotic factors were seen in HT-29 and Caco-2 cells following treatment with different derivatives of B. coagulans Hammer. The caspase-3 is an executioner enzyme that cleaves substrates leading to apoptosis, while caspase-9 is an initiator caspase linked to the mitochondrial pathway [50]. Our findings are in agreement with previous studies demonstrating that probiotic-derived bioactive components promote apoptosis in colon cancer cells by targeting mitochondrial pathways [51]. In agreement with our findings, Behzadi et al. showed that EVs from Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG upregulated Bax and downregulated Bcl-2 to induce apoptosis in hepatic cancer cells [52]. In addition, we showed that UV-killed B. coagulans Hammer caused a noticeable increase in the gene expression of proapoptotic protein Bax in comparison to other bacterial derivatives examined in this study. These variable effects underscore the need to refine manufacturing and processing methods to retain maximal anticancer activity in non-viable probiotic formulations. Mechanistically, the results suggest that B. coagulans Hammer EVs may trigger apoptosis through the mitochondrial intrinsic pathway and the death receptor-mediated extrinsic pathway [53].

The flow cytometry analysis provided further evidence to substantiate the proapoptotic effects of inactivated forms of B. coagulans Hammer, its EVs and metabolites on HT-29 and Caco-2 cells. The flow cytometry data revealed that all treatment groups, including B. coagulans Hammer EVs, UV-killed cells, pasteurized cells, and CFS, induced a significantly higher frequency of apoptotic cells compared to the control group, with UV-killed cells and CFS showing the most pronounced apoptotic effect. Treatment with pasteurized B. coagulans Hammer resulted in the lowest occurrence of cells undergoing apoptosis compared to control cells and other treatment groups. Moreover, the presence of necrotic cells observed in this assay is likely attributed to secondary necrosis following apoptosis, which is a phenomenon that occurs during extended incubation when apoptotic cells are not rapidly cleared and eventually lose membrane integrity [54].

Nevertheless, it is essential to acknowledge that there might be variations in the processes that underlie the anticancer properties of different strains of probiotics. Each probiotic strain may exert therapeutic effects through distinct mechanisms and pathways, possibly due to variations in genomic, proteomic and metabolomic composition [55]. For instance, several studies have shown the ability of different probiotic strains to modulate host immune response, inhibit inflammation, and regulate the gut microbiota structure [56, 57]. These effects can potentially impact the behavior of cancer cells, tumor growth and tumor microenvironment. Moreover, the specific types and abundance of effector molecules found in bacterial EVs might potentially influence their functional characteristics and potential as anticancer agents [44, 58]. Therefore, it is essential to conduct a comparative analysis of the distinct components and mechanisms of action of B. coagulans Hammer EVs with other probiotics to comprehensively understand their prospective utilization in cancer treatment. Moreover, while our study demonstrated significant antiproliferative and proapoptotic effects of different B. coagulans Hammer derivatives on HT-29 and Caco-2 cells, several critical aspects need to be further investigated before considering these products in preclinical and clinical setting.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our research suggests potential anticancer effects of B. coagulans Hammer derivatives on HT-29 and Caco-2 cells in vitro. Also, our results demonstrate that B. coagulans Hammer derivatives, especially UV-killed cells, have potent antiproliferative effect on these cancer cell lines, and can promote apoptosis by modulating the expression of apoptosis-related genes and markers. These findings also support the exploration of B. coagulans Hammer EVs as a promising anticancer agent for CRC prevention and treatment. Nevertheless, further experimental studies are warranted to achieve in-depth molecular characterization of the EVs cargo, including proteomic and transcriptomic analyses. Future research should focus on identifying the specific bioactive components of B. coagulans EVs, elucidating their mechanisms of action, and assessing the stability of their therapeutic effects over time. In particular, the durability and pharmacodynamic profile of EV-induced apoptosis require thorough investigations. Cell experiments using non-tumorigenic cell lines are critical to ensure the safety and specificity of these treatments. Additionally, applying appropriate in vivo tumor model, and syngeneic or xenograft models are required to assess the therapeutic effects of B. coagulans derivatives on tumor growth and augment the translational relevance of the present findings.
