Immunological Heterogeneity in Gastric Cancer: Context‐Dependent Role of CD8+ T Cells
Authors: Xiaofei Sun, Zihao Yang, Hongtao Qu, Jinhui Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCD8+ T cell infiltration in gastric cancer shows subtype‐dependent prognostic and predictive significance across TCGA molecular subtypes. Its clinical value is context‐dependent and requires integrated immune profiling to guide modern chemoimmunotherapy strategies.

Dear Editor,

We read with profound interest the recent article by Ling et al. [1] published in Cancer Science, which elegantly demonstrates that CD8+ T cell infiltration elicits molecular subtype‐biased clinical outcomes in patients with gastric cancer (GC). The authors should be commended for their comprehensive evaluation of CD8+ T cell infiltration within the framework of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) molecular classification. By revealing that high CD8+ T cell density confers a favorable prognosis specifically in the Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)‐positive and chromosomal instability (CIN) subtypes, but not in the microsatellite instability (MSI) or genomically stable (GS) subtypes, this study provides a crucial conceptual advancement: the prognostic and predictive value of immune infiltration is fundamentally context‐dependent.

As practicing gastrointestinal oncologists, we find these results highly relevant, yet their clinical translation must be urgently contextualized within the current treatment paradigm. Since Ling et al.'s data were generated primarily in the adjuvant chemotherapy setting, we must acknowledge a fundamental paradigm shift: immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)‐based chemoimmunotherapy has rapidly become the standard first‐line treatment for advanced GC, supported by landmark trials such as CheckMate‐649 and KEYNOTE‐859 [2, 3]. The prognostic and predictive values of CD8+ T cells established under chemotherapy regimens cannot be directly extrapolated to the immunotherapy era. CD8+ T cell biology, exhaustion patterns, and ICI responsiveness differ profoundly across TCGA subtypes. Therefore, interpreting Ling et al.'s findings through the lens of modern chemoimmunotherapy is essential to avoid extrapolation risks and to optimize patient selection in contemporary practice.

First, the unexpected prognostic benefit of CD8+ T cell infiltration in the CIN subtype merits deeper mechanistic exploration, particularly regarding its implications for immunotherapy. The CIN subtype, which accounts for approximately half of all GC cases, is classically characterized by marked aneuploidy, focal amplification of receptor tyrosine kinases, and a generally immunosuppressive or “immune‐desert” microenvironment [4]. Previous studies have consistently shown that chromosomal instability drives tumor aggressiveness and promotes immune evasion through various mechanisms, including the activation of the cGAS‐STING pathway and subsequent chronic inflammation [5]. The finding by Ling et al. that high CD8+ T cell infiltration in CIN tumors correlates with improved survival and superior response to adjuvant chemotherapy challenges the conventional paradigm. This suggests that within the CIN category, there exists a distinct immunogenic subgroup capable of mounting an effective anti‐tumor response. In the context of ICIs, where CIN tumors generally exhibit lower response rates (10%–20%) compared to MSI or EBV+ tumors [2], identifying this immunogenic subgroup is critical. Future investigations should focus on characterizing the functional status (e.g., exhausted versus effector memory phenotypes) and spatial distribution (tumor center versus invasive margin) of these CD8+ T cells in CIN tumors, as this could identify a subset of patients who might benefit from ICI‐based chemoimmunotherapy despite their CIN status.

Second, the lack of significant prognostic benefit from high CD8+ T cell infiltration in the MSI subtype, despite this group exhibiting intense immune infiltration and high neoantigen burden, highlights the complexity of the MSI immune contexture and its resistance mechanisms. While early studies suggested a straightforward correlation between high tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and favorable outcomes in MSI GC [6], more recent evidence indicates that the prognostic impact of CD8+ T cells in this subtype is heavily modulated by other factors. For instance, Kwak et al. [7] demonstrated that high CD8+ TIL infiltration was significantly associated with good prognosis only in MSI GC patients with preserved human leukocyte antigen class I (HLA‐I) expression and programmed death‐ligand 1 (PD‐L1) positivity. The loss of HLA‐I or β2‐microglobulin expression, which frequently occurs in MSI tumors as an acquired immune escape mechanism against both chemotherapy and ICIs [8], renders CD8+ T cells functionally inert regardless of their density. Therefore, evaluating CD8+ T cell density in isolation may be insufficient for MSI tumors; a composite biomarker approach incorporating HLA‐I status, PD‐L1 expression, and CD8+ T cell density is essential for accurate prognostic stratification and predicting durable ICI responses.

Third, the findings regarding the EBV‐positive subtype underscore the phenomenon of immune exhaustion in virus‐driven oncogenesis. Ling et al. observed the highest levels of CD8+ T cell infiltration in EBV‐positive tumors, yet this did not translate into a statistically significant survival advantage compared to medium infiltration. This aligns with previous reports showing that EBV‐positive GCs, despite robust lymphocytic infiltration, frequently exhibit profound immune evasion mechanisms, notably the massive upregulation of PD‐L1 driven by CD274 gene amplification [9]. The intense but functionally impaired CD8+ T cell infiltrate in these tumors explains their exceptional responsiveness to ICIs, with response rates often exceeding 50% in clinical trials [10]. Recent spatial transcriptomics and single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) studies have further elucidated this by revealing distinct exhaustion trajectories of CD8+ T cells in EBV+ microenvironments [11]. Consequently, while CD8+ T cell density alone may lack prognostic power in EBV‐positive GC under conventional chemotherapy regimens, it likely serves as a powerful predictive biomarker for ICI efficacy in the modern treatment landscape.

As we have outlined, the integration of Ling et al.'s findings into contemporary practice requires a nuanced understanding of how chemoimmunotherapy alters the tumor‐immune dynamic. In conclusion, Ling et al. have provided compelling evidence that the clinical significance of CD8+ T cells in GC is inextricably linked to the underlying molecular subtype. Their work strongly advocates for a departure from the “one‐size‐fits‐all” approach to immune biomarkers. To fully harness the potential of these findings in the ICI era, future clinical trials must stratify patients not only by molecular subtype but also by comprehensive immune profiling—incorporating spatial transcriptomics, HLA‐I status, and functional T cell states [12]. Only through such multidimensional biomarker integration can we accurately predict chemoimmunotherapy responses and guide more precise and effective therapeutic strategies for patients with gastric cancer.
