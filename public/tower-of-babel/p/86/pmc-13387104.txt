Evolution and future directions of bispecific antibodies in breast cancer: a global clinical trial landscape analysis
Authors: Xubin Zhu, Shiyou Luo, Yiheng Li
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Breast cancer remains the most frequently diagnosed malignancy in women worldwide, with over 2.3 million new cases annually [1]. Despite the success of endocrine therapies, chemotherapy, and monoclonal antibodies (e.g., trastuzumab, pertuzumab), a significant therapeutic gap persists for advanced, metastatic, and treatment-refractory cases [2]. Resistance mechanisms and tumor heterogeneity necessitate the development of next-generation biologics [3]. Bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) offer a transformative approach by simultaneously engaging two different epitopes, thereby redirecting immune effector cells (T/NK cells) to the tumor microenvironment or dual-inhibiting compensatory signaling pathways [4]. Here, we present a systematic landscape analysis of global BsAb clinical trials in breast cancer to identify emerging trends and strategic development gaps.

Data was extracted from TrialTrove, a curated clinical trial intelligence platform that combines records from ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO ICTRP, and EU Clinical Trials Register [5,6]. We focused on the period from 2015 to October 14, 2025. The year 2015 was selected as the starting point to mark the “modern era” of bispecific antibody (BsAb) research and development, a booming wave driven by the FDA approval of the first T-cell engager on December 3, 2014 [7]. We queried for Drug Type: Bispecific Antibody Disease: Breast until October 14th, 2025, and found 164 records. We searched interventional clinical trials related to breast cancer and BsAbs, then manually screened records for eligibility. Inclusion criteria were: (i) breast cancer as the target disease; (ii) an interventional design evaluating a BsAb either as monotherapy or in combination; (iii) any clinical phase; and (iv) any geographic region. Exclusion criteria were observational studies, preclinical studies, duplicate records, and withdrawn records without usable trial information. We excluded 3 observational studies, 15 trials before 2015, and 22 single target investigations, resulting in 124 valid BsAb trials. Two reviewers independently verified target combinations, clinical phase, sponsor type, and geographic distribution, with discrepancies resolved by discussion.

The BsAb pipeline is expanding rapidly, with trial initiations peaking at 29 in 2025. Currently, the landscape is heavily weighted toward early-phase research: Phase I (41.9%) and Phase II (30.0%) trials dominate, while Phase III (4.8%) and Phase IV (0.8%) remain scarce. This means that 71.9% of the current pipeline remains in Phase I/II development, underscoring that breast cancer BsAbs are still predominantly in the proof-of-concept stage.

Regarding molecular targets, immune-focused combinations are the most prevalent. The CTLA-4 × PD-1 axis leads with 24 trials (19.4%), followed by PD-1 × VEGF (15 trials, 12.1%) and PD-L1 × VEGF-A (11 trials, 8.9%). These three target pairs alone account for 40 trials, or 32.3% of the entire dataset. Importantly, this concentration reflects development activity rather than proven clinical superiority: high trial volume for immune checkpoint pairs is driven by their relative technical maturity, established regulatory pathways, and commercial investment priorities, and does not inherently indicate greater therapeutic value. Emerging strategies also include microtubule-targeting BsAbs (alpha-tubulin × β-tubulin, 4.8%). Safety and efficacy remain the primary clinical endpoints. Among efficacy readouts, objective response rate (ORR) is the most frequently used endpoint, followed by progression-free survival (PFS); overall survival (OS) is less common and is mainly reserved for later-phase studies. Safety endpoints commonly include treatment-emergent adverse events, dose-limiting toxicities, infusion reactions, and immune-related toxicities.

Among records with available subtype annotation (n = 89, 71.8% of total trials), BsAb trials were more commonly concentrated in HER2-positive breast cancer (42 trials, 47.2%) and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC; 37 trials, 41.6%), which are clinically relevant subtypes with substantial unmet needs. In contrast, hormone receptor-positive/HER2-negative disease appears relatively underrepresented (10 trials, 11.2%). The remaining 35 trials (28.2%) lacked complete subtype annotation, limiting definitive subtype-level inference.

Notably, non-top 20 pharmaceutical companies sponsor 87.1% of these trials (108/124), indicating that innovation is largely driven by biotech firms rather than “Big Pharma.” Sponsor classification was based on the 2024 global pharmaceutical industry revenue ranking published by Pharmaceutical Executive, with the top 20 entities by global prescription drug revenue defined as “Top 20” and all other sponsors classified as non-top 20. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously: sponsor type alone does not fully capture the development ecosystem, because many programs also involve licensing agreements, academic partnerships, and co-development efforts.

Geographically, China has emerged as the global leader with 76 trials, followed by the United States with 45. Together, these two countries account for 121 of 124 trials (97.6%), demonstrating a highly concentrated global development footprint. This pattern should be interpreted in context: China’s rapid trial growth reflects a decade of expanding biotech investment and accelerated regulatory pathways for innovative biologics, while potential registration bias in TrialTrove toward Chinese and US databases may also contribute. Additionally, international multicenter trials are often registered under the lead sponsor’s home country, which may amplify apparent single-country concentration.

The shift from traditional monoclonal antibodies to BsAbs in breast cancer reflects a strategic move to tackle tumor evasion. The high concentration of trials in China suggests a robust regulatory and investment environment for biotech innovation. At the same time, the current “early-phase bottleneck” is evident from the dominance of Phase I/II trials (71.9% combined) and the scarcity of Phase III/IV studies (5.6% combined), indicating that substantial clinical validation is still needed before routine adoption can be considered. The industry must move beyond redundant PD-1/CTLA-4 combinations and explore novel mechanisms, such as T-cell engagers (TCEs) for cold tumors or BsAbs targeting intracellular neoantigens via MHC presentation. Neoadjuvant settings represent another growing frontier: recent data from ASCO 2025 demonstrated encouraging pathological complete response rates with the PD-1/CTLA-4 bispecific cadonilimab in early-stage TNBC, supporting further investigation of BsAbs in curative-intent treatment [8].

Several translational challenges remain. Manufacturing BsAbs is technically complex because correct chain pairing, structural stability, purity, and scalable production are difficult to optimize simultaneously. In addition, immunogenicity, off-target binding, cytokine release risk, and overlapping toxicities with combination regimens remain major concerns. These issues may be especially important for immune-engaging BsAbs, where efficacy gains must be balanced against toxicity risk. Cost and access considerations will also affect future implementation.

Resistance to BsAb therapy presents another critical barrier. Primary resistance includes low target antigen expression, immune-excluded microenvironments and preexisting T-cell exhaustion; acquired resistance may arise from antigen loss, impaired immune synapse formation and upregulation of alternative inhibitory pathways. Combination strategies with antibody-drug conjugates, epigenetic modulators or cancer vaccines are being explored to reverse resistance.

Patient stratification will be central to maximizing clinical benefit. Genetically defined subgroups such as BRCA1/2-mutant breast cancers with high tumor mutational burden may derive enhanced benefit from immune-targeting BsAbs, though prospective stratified data remain limited. Robust predictive biomarkers—including target antigen expression, immune infiltration signatures and genomic markers—are urgently needed to guide patient selection and real-time monitoring.

The limitations of this analysis should also be acknowledged. First, the study relies on a single curated database (TrialTrove), which may not fully capture all registered or updated trials. Second, reporting bias and incomplete annotation may affect the completeness of certain variables, particularly subtype and endpoint details. Third, real-world evidence and unpublished data were not included. Finally, because this is a landscape analysis rather than a patient-level meta-analysis, the clinical efficacy of individual BsAbs cannot be directly compared across studies.

Based on our expert interpretation of the current pipeline, we project the following trends over the next five years, subject to regulatory, clinical, and economic uncertainties: Clinical maturation: several Phase III trials will read out, which may potentially lead to the first BsAb approvals specifically for metastatic breast cancer. Technological integration: AI-driven platform technologies are expected to accelerate design of “common light chain” BsAbs to improve stability and manufacturability. Combination synergies: research will likely pivot toward combining BsAbs with antibody-drug conjugates or mRNA vaccines to achieve deeper molecular responses. These predictions are supported by the current trial distribution, which remains dominated by early-phase studies but already shows substantial diversification in targets, sponsors, and geographic participation.

The BsAb landscape in breast cancer is dynamic and rapidly maturing. Current research is concentrated in early-phase immune-targeting trials, led by CTLA-4 × PD-1, PD-1 × VEGF, and PD-L1 × VEGF-A combinations, with China and the United States accounting for the vast majority of registered studies. While current research is concentrated in early-phase immune-targeting trials, the next decade will require rigorous Phase III validation and global collaboration to translate this technological promise into survival benefits.
