Patient-reported outcomes decline over time following arthroscopic Bankart repair for anterior shoulder instability
Authors: Sahil Dadoo, Ryan Gilbert, Ryan T Lin, Tyler C Williams, Andrew Liu, Neel Bhardwaj, Kyle E Andrade-Bucknor, Shaquille Charles, Adam Popchak, Bryson P Lesniak, Mark Rodosky, Jonathan D Hughes, Albert Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo evaluate how patient-reported outcomes (PROs) trend over time following arthroscopic Bankart repair (ABR) for anterior shoulder instability with on-track Hill-Sachs lesions (HSLs).MethodsA retrospective review was performed of patients undergoing ABR with or without remplissage between 2007 and 2023. Exclusion criteria included age <14 years, glenoid bone loss >20%, off-track HSLs, and prior shoulder surgery. PROs and clinical outcomes, including Western Ontario Shoulder Index (WOSI), pain visual analog scale (pVAS), Subjective Shoulder Value (SSV), and recurrent instability, were collected at final follow-up. Spearman rank correlation assessed relationships between PROs and time from surgery. Rates of achieving Patient Acceptable Symptom State for WOSI and pVAS were compared across follow-up intervals (<5 years, 5-9 years, ≥10 years) and between patients with and without recurrent instability. Significance was set at P < .05.ResultsA total of 121 patients (age: 23 ± 8 years, 27% females) were included at mean follow-up of 7.1 years (range: 1.6-17.2). Longer follow-up duration was associated with worsening WOSI (r = 0.239; P = .008), pVAS (r = 0.180; P = .049), and SSV (r = −0.186; P = .041) scores. Patients undergoing isolated ABR demonstrated significant deterioration in PROs over time (WOSI: r = 0.331, P = .002; pVAS: r = 0.261, P = .015; SSV: r = −0.216, P = .045), whereas those undergoing remplissage showed no change in PROs over time (all P > .05). Patients with recurrent instability had lower rates of achieving WOSI Patient Acceptable Symptom State at <5 years (56% vs. 88%; P = .029), 5-9 years (22% vs. 87%; P < .001), and ≥10 years (20% vs. 86%; P < .001) compared to patients without recurrent instability. On linear regression analysis, longer follow-up length remained an independent predictor of worse WOSI scores after controlling for recurrent instability.ConclusionPROs may decline over time following ABR for anterior shoulder instability for patients who experience recurrent anterior shoulder instability and undergo isolated ABR compared to ABR with remplissage. These findings may be relevant for pre-operative counseling regarding long-term subjective outcomes.Keywords: Bankart repair, Shoulder instability, Patient-reported outcomes, Remplissage, Recurrent instability, WOSI, pVAS, SSV

Arthroscopic Bankart repair (ABR) is a widely utilized surgical treatment for anterior shoulder instability, often combined with concomitant remplissage in patients with off-track Hill-Sachs lesions (HSLs) and/or significant glenoid bone loss.10,19,37,41 Short- to mid-term (≤5 years) clinical and patient-reported outcomes (PROs) following ABR demonstrate high rates of return to sport, low rates of recurrent anterior shoulder instability, and significant improvement in PROs from pre-operatively to post-operatively.30,33,35 However, clinical studies on long-term outcomes (≥7 years) following ABR have raised concern about the effectiveness of ABR over long-term follow-up.9,12,34 Recent literature has indicated recurrent anterior shoulder instability rates may exceed 30% at minimum 10-year follow-up,12,14 with over 60% of recurrent anterior shoulder instability events occurring after 4 years following ABR.9,34

Despite increasing literature evaluating long-term clinical outcomes following ABR,12,14,31 there remains limited evidence demonstrating how PROs may trend over time following ABR for on-track HSLs. In addition, studies reporting on long-term PROs following ABR are conflicting, with some studies indicating long-term PROs may be inferior to short-term PROs,23 while other studies report PROs may remain stable at long-term follow-up.3,31 Previous literature has indicated that inferior PROs following ABR may be closely related to sustaining recurrent anterior shoulder instability;12,23 however, the trajectory of PROs following ABR over time remains incompletely understood. Furthermore, it remains unknown how concomitant remplissage or revision stabilization surgery may influence the trend in PROs over time.2,6

The primary purpose of this study was to evaluate how PROs trend over time following primary ABR for anterior shoulder instability. The secondary purpose of this study was to evaluate how trends in PROs over time may be influenced by undergoing concomitant remplissage, sustaining recurrent anterior shoulder instability, or undergoing revision stabilization surgery. The primary hypothesis was that there would be an overall decline in PROs over time following primary ABR for anterior shoulder instability and on-track HSLs. The secondary hypothesis was that there would be a larger decline in PROs over time among patients who underwent isolated ABR (compared to ABR with remplissage), sustained recurrent anterior shoulder instability, and/or underwent revision stabilization surgery.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study was approved by the institutional review board at the University of Pittsburgh (No. STUDY20030061). All consecutive patients who underwent surgery for shoulder instability from 2007 to 2023 by 5 fellowship-trained academic sports medicine surgeons were retrospectively reviewed. First, all nonanterior stabilization procedures were excluded. Second, all patients who underwent open surgery, had history of ipsilateral shoulder surgery, concomitant fracture fixation, concomitant rotator cuff repair, diagnosis of posterior or multidirectional shoulder instability, history of uncontrolled seizures or seizures within the last 2 years, and connective tissue disorders were excluded. All remaining patients were then screened for study-specific exclusion criteria including age <14 years, glenoid bone loss >20%, off-track HSLs, revision procedures, and missing PROs (Fig. 1). As the purpose of the study was to evaluate how PROs trend over time after ABR, follow-up length was not considered an exclusion criterion for the study. However, all included patients had minimum follow-up of 18 months from ABR, as detailed below.

Variables collected from patient charts included demographics (age, sex), contact athlete status, number of pre-operative anterior shoulder instability episodes, instability type (glenohumeral dislocation vs. subluxation), concomitant remplissage during ABR, and number of anchors utilized during ABR with or without remplissage. Among patients who met inclusion criteria, contact sport types included football, hockey, lacrosse, wrestling, basketball, and soccer. Glenohumeral dislocation was defined as requiring manual shoulder reduction, and glenohumeral subluxation was defined as not requiring manual shoulder reduction. Finally, radiographic variables including percent glenoid bone loss and distance to dislocation (DTD) were measured on pre-operative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), as outlined below.

After obtaining informed consent, all patients included for analysis were contacted for completion of a clinical outcome and PRO questionnaire. Patients were asked if they sustained recurrent anterior shoulder instability, defined as sustaining recurrent glenohumeral dislocation and/or subluxation after the index procedure, and/or underwent revision stabilization surgery for recurrent anterior shoulder instability. Patients were additionally assessed for post-operative PROs, including Western Ontario Shoulder Index (WOSI),25 pain visual analog scale (pVAS),38 and Subjective Shoulder Value (SSV).17 WOSI and pVAS scores were reported as both raw values and if the score achieved the Patient Acceptable Symptom State (PASS), which previous literature has identified as ≤ 480 and ≤ 1 following ABR, respectively.28 As there is no reported PASS for SSV following ABR, SSV was reported as raw value only. All questionnaires were completed using an institutional REDCap database (REDCap, Vanderbilt University, Nashville, TN, USA). Follow-up length was determined using date of questionnaire completion relative to date of ABR.

Measurement of glenoid bone loss and distance to dislocation

tMeasurement of percent glenoid bone loss was performed as previously outlined using T1-weighted MRI sequences.4 Glenoid bone loss was measured on sagittal MRI using the best-fit circle method on the slice in which the maximum diameter of the glenoid face was visualized. The percentage of glenoid bone loss was calculated as the diameter of maximum anterior glenoid bone loss (d) divided by the diameter of the glenoid using the best-fit circle (D) times 100 and recorded as a percentage.4,7,19 DTD was calculated as previously described as the glenoid track (0.83D – d) minus the Hill-Sachs interval (HSL width plus width of intact bone bridge between the rotator cuff attachment and the lateral margin of the HSL) and was recorded in mm. Based on prior literature, off-track HSLs were defined as DTD ≤0 mm and were excluded from the study, whereas near-track HSLs (defined as 0 mm < DTD ≤10 mm) and on-track HSLs (defined as DTD >10 mm) were included in the study.1

Surgical technique

All ABR procedures were performed in the lateral decubitus or beach chair position, based on surgeon preference, using standard arthroscopic suture anchor techniques with a minimum of 3 suture anchors. The decision to perform concomitant remplissage was based on a combination of surgeon discretion and assessment of patient risk factors, including younger age, contact athlete status, number of pre-operative anterior shoulder instability episodes, DTD, and glenoid bone loss.7 All patients underwent a standard post-operative physical therapy protocol, including use of a sling for 4-6 weeks followed by gradual initiation of range of motion around 4 weeks. Return to sport was guided by criteria-based return-to-sport testing and/or surgeon discretion.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29). Descriptive statistics were reported for all demographic and surgical variables, imaging measurements, PROs (including raw values and percent of patients who achieved PASS), and clinical outcomes (including rates of recurrent anterior shoulder instability and revision stabilization surgery) across the total included cohort. Continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations (SDs) and categorical variables were reported as numbers and percentages. Spearman rank correlation analysis was performed to determine the correlation between PROs and time from surgery for the total cohort, as well as between patients with and without concomitant remplissage, with and without recurrent anterior shoulder instability, and with and without revision stabilization surgery. Rates of achieving PASS for WOSI and pVAS were then evaluated among patients with <5 years, 5-9 years, and ≥10 years of follow-up, and were compared between patients with and without recurrent anterior shoulder instability at each time interval using chi-square analysis. Finally, linear regression analysis was performed to determine the independent impact of follow-up length and recurrent anterior shoulder instability on raw WOSI scores. Significance for all tests was set to P < .05.

An a priori power analysis was conducted and identified that a total sample size of 84 patients was needed to detect an expected correlation coefficient of 0.300 between PRO scores and follow-up length at an alpha level of 0.05 and power of 80%.

Results

Total cohort

After applying exclusion criteria, a total of 121 patients (mean age: 23 ± 8 years, 32 (27%) females) were included at median follow-up of 6.8 years (interquartile range: 3.8-10.0 years) (Fig. 1). Among the total cohort, 48 (40%) patients were contact athletes, 78 (65%) patients sustained more than one pre-operative anterior shoulder instability event, and 76 (63%) patients sustained at least one pre-operative glenohumeral dislocation event. Mean percent glenoid bone loss and DTD were 5.8% (SD: 6.2%) and 12.2 mm (SD: 6.5 mm), respectively. Thirty-four (28%) patients underwent ABR with remplissage, and 87 (72%) patients underwent isolated ABR (Table I).

Trend in patient-reported outcomes over time

Among the total cohort, there was a statistically significant worsening in WOSI (r = 0.239; P = .008), pVAS (r = 0.180; P = .049), and SSV (r = −0.186; P = .041) with longer follow-up duration following ABR (Table III). When stratified by patients with and without concomitant remplissage, patients without concomitant remplissage had a statistically significant worsening in WOSI (r = 0.331; P = .002), pVAS (r = 0.261; P = .015), and SSV (r = −0.216; P = .045) with longer follow-up duration following ABR. However, there was no significant correlation between WOSI, pVAS, or SSV scores with longer follow-up duration for patients who underwent ABR with remplissage (all P > .05) (Table III). When stratified by patients with and without recurrent anterior shoulder instability, there was no significant correlation between WOSI, pVAS, or SSV scores with follow-up duration for either cohort (all P > .05) (Table III). Finally, when stratified by patients with and without revision stabilization surgery, a statistically significant worsening in WOSI (r = 0.196; P = .039) was found with longer follow-up duration in patients who did not undergo revision stabilization surgery; however, no other PRO measures were found to correlate with follow-up duration for either cohort (all P > .05) (Table III).

Achieving Patient Acceptable Symptom State for Western Ontario Shoulder Index and pain visual analog scale over time

A total of 42 patients had <5 years of follow-up, 48 patients had 5-9 years of follow-up, and 31 patients had ≥10 years of follow-up. Rates of recurrent anterior shoulder instability for each cohort of follow-up length were 21% (9/42 patients), 19% (9/48 patients), and 32% (10/31 patients), respectively. Patients with recurrent anterior shoulder instability had significantly lower rates of achieving PASS for WOSI at <5 years (56% vs. 88%; P = .029), 5-9 years (22% vs. 87%; P < .001), and ≥10 years (20% vs. 86%; P < .001) of follow-up compared to patients without recurrent anterior shoulder instability (Fig. 2). Furthermore, patients with recurrent anterior shoulder instability had significantly lower rates of achieving PASS for pVAS at 5-9 (33% vs. 69%; P = .045) and ≥10 years (20% vs. 67%; P = .015) of follow-up compared to patients without recurrent anterior shoulder instability (Fig. 3).

Discussion

The most important finding of the present study was that PROs may decline over time following ABR for anterior shoulder instability, especially among patients who underwent isolated ABR compared to ABR with remplissage, which did not demonstrate the same correlation. Notably, however, correlations between follow-up length and PROs were found to be weak to moderate in strength. In addition, patients who sustained recurrent anterior shoulder instability had significantly lower rates of achieving PASS for PROs at <5 years, 5-9 years, and ≥10 years of follow-up compared to patients who did not sustain recurrent anterior shoulder instability. The findings of this study support the hypothesis that PROs experience decline over time after ABR, which may be informative to patients and surgeons about the expected subjective outcomes over time following ABR for anterior shoulder instability.

Previous studies have identified that recurrent anterior shoulder instability rates increase with longer-term follow-up after ABR.34 While short-term and mid-term follow-up studies have identified rates of recurrent anterior shoulder instability from 3% to 15% after ABR,5,26,36,43 longer-term studies have demonstrated rates of recurrent anterior shoulder instability exceed 30% after ABR.12,14 This suggests that short-term follow-up studies may underestimate the true rates of recurrent anterior shoulder instability after ABR.34 The findings of our study align with the established temporal relationship between time from surgery and risk of recurrent anterior shoulder instability, indicating PROs and subjective patient outcomes may experience a parallel deterioration over time after ABR among patients who sustain recurrent anterior shoulder instability. In addition, while recurrent anterior shoulder instability was a significant predictor of higher WOSI scores, longer follow-up length was also identified as an independent predictor of higher WOSI scores. This indicates deterioration in PROs may still occur over time despite not sustaining recurrent anterior shoulder instability.

An interesting finding of the present study was that ABR with remplissage appeared to offer protection against the decline in PROs over time compared to patients who underwent isolated ABR. This is consistent with previous studies demonstrating a lower rate of recurrent anterior shoulder instability following ABR with remplissage compared to isolated ABR.27,29,40 While available literature appears conflicted regarding the impact of concomitant remplissage on PROs compared to isolated ABR,8,21,24 the findings of our study suggest PROs may experience a slower decline over time following ABR with remplissage compared to isolated ABR. While initial studies evaluating clinical outcomes after ABR with remplissage raised concerns regarding potential restriction in external rotation compared to isolated ABR,11,15,16 recent studies have challenged this relationship, suggesting equivalent range of motion may be achieved after ABR with or without remplissage.18,42 As a result, given increasing evidence that remplissage may reduce rates of recurrent anterior shoulder instability while preserving external rotation motion and strength, the use of concomitant remplissage may be warranted in broader populations beyond young, high-risk populations.7,13 However, it should be noted that the correlation between PROs and follow-up length among patients who underwent isolated ABR were noted to be weak to moderate in strength, despite reaching statistical significance. In addition, the correlation between PROs and follow-up length among patients who underwent ABR with remplissage was likely underpowered to achieve statistical significance. Therefore, the finding that PROs may decline more significantly among patients undergoing isolated ABR versus ABR with remplissage must be interpreted with caution, and future study may be warranted to further evaluate this potential relationship.

Finally, the low rate of achieving PASS for WOSI and pVAS among patients with 10-year follow-up, especially among patients who sustained recurrent anterior shoulder instability, is an important finding of the present study. In addition, only 67% of patients who did not sustain recurrent anterior shoulder instability achieved PASS for pVAS at minimum 10-year follow-up, indicating subjective pain scores remain higher than acceptable to patients in the long term. This may be explained by the high rates (>60%) of glenohumeral osteoarthritis (OA) established in the literature among patients with minimum 10-year follow-up after ABR.31,32,39 Furthermore, previous literature has identified that recurrent anterior shoulder instability results in higher rates of radiographic glenohumeral OA at long-term follow-up,20 which may explain the inferior long-term PROs among patients who sustained recurrent anterior shoulder instability. Based on the present results, future studies correlating long-term PROs to rates of glenohumeral OA following ABR with vs. without remplissage may help further delineate both the cause of inferior long-term PROs after ABR and the potential protective effect of remplissage on rates of long-term glenohumeral OA.

There are several important limitations of the present study. First, only PROs at final follow-up were available for the present analysis, which limits understanding of repeated measures of PROs over time for individual patients. Second, while the total included sample size met sufficient power to detect significant correlations in PROs over time, subgroup analysis of patients who underwent remplissage or sustained recurrent anterior shoulder instability were underpowered based on a priori power analysis. Third, previous literature has demonstrated PROs, namely Constant scores, may decline as a function of age and therefore require age-related normalization, which was not captured in the present study for the included PROs.22 Fourth, although statistically significant, correlations between PROs and follow-up length were noted to be weak and therefore may be interpreted with caution. However, our finding that longer follow-up length and recurrent instability were both independent predictors of inferior WOSI scores on regression analysis may indicate that PROs experience a statistically and clinically significant decline over time even in patients without recurrent anterior shoulder instability. Fifth, the PASS values utilized for WOSI and pVAS in the present study, while reflecting the best available evidence of PASS values following ABR, may only pertain to patients with minimum 1-year follow-up; therefore, extrapolation to longer-term follow-up must be interpreted with caution. Sixth, intraoperative complications and comorbidities were not included as variables in analysis, which have potential to confound results. Finally, administration of a questionnaire to obtain PROs and clinical outcomes may be subject to response and recall bias.

Conclusion

PROs may decline over time following ABR for anterior shoulder instability for patients who experience recurrent anterior shoulder instability and undergo isolated ABR compared to ABR with remplissage. These findings may be relevant for pre-operative counseling regarding long-term subjective outcomes.
