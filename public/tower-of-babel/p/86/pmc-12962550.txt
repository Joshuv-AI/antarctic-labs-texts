Pseudomonas aeruginosa gene expression changes during established biofilm infection in a cystic fibrosis lung model
Authors: Niamh E. Harrington, Freya Allen, Ramón Garcia Maset, Freya Harrison
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe opportunistic pathogen Pseudomonas aeruginosa forms biofilm infections in the lungs of people with the genetic condition cystic fibrosis (CF) that can persist for decades. There are numerous P. aeruginosa lifestyle changes associated with chronic biofilm infection that are cued by the CF lung environment. These include a loss of virulence, metabolic changes and increased antimicrobial tolerance. We have investigated P. aeruginosa PA14 biofilm infection over 7 days in an ex vivo pig lung (EVPL) model for CF, previously shown to facilitate formation of a clinically relevant P. aeruginosa biofilm structure with expression of key genes comparable to human infection. We have compared P. aeruginosa gene expression between sequential time points: 24 h, 48 h and 7 days post-infection, and investigated tolerance to polymyxins. Our results demonstrate that the EVPL model can maintain a P. aeruginosa biofilm population, which exhibits increased antibiotic tolerance, for at least 7 days. Differential expression of antimicrobial resistance-associated genes was not observed; however, there was significant upregulation of sulphur metabolism and maintenance of a structured biofilm. Our findings demonstrate that 7 days is a viable time point for studying established, chronic biofilm infection in the EVPL model and provide insight into the accompanying gene expression changes.

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa chronic biofilm infections of the cystic fibrosis (CF) lung are characterized by their physiological adaptations to the environment. This is driven by environmental pressures, such as osmotic, nitrosative and oxidative stress, as well as interactions with the commensal microbiome and co-infecting pathogens, and antibiotic exposure [2]. Lifestyle changes have been shown to improve P. aeruginosa persistence in the lung, including loss of swimming motility, impaired pyoverdine production, increased adherence and formation of a biofilm matrix that protects the bacterial cells from not only the host immune response but also antibiotics [34]. Quorum sensing (QS) largely controls the phenotypic changes observed in the biofilm population and is estimated to influence expression of ∼10% of all P. aeruginosa genes [5]. Initial physiological changes that induce a chronic, antibiotic-tolerant biofilm phenotype are followed by evolutionary changes in the population over years of infection. Late-stage infections exhibit reduced phenotypic diversity and loss of virulence compared with early-stage infections [6]. The reduction in virulence factor production is linked to a decline in expression of QS-related genes [7]. Once established, chronic P. aeruginosa infections are almost impossible to eradicate due to high levels of antibiotic resistance and tolerance, which are contributed to by the protective biofilm matrix.

Studies have shown that current in vitro models do not accurately represent critical aspects of P. aeruginosa chronic infection; in particular, QS-associated gene expression in CF sputum is not well captured [8]. However, our previous work has indicated that our ex vivo pig lung (EVPL) model may more accurately capture in vivo QS-regulated gene expression over a short infection time (48 h) [9]. Our study focused on P. aeruginosa gene expression at 24 and 48 h post-infection (PI) in the EVPL model compared with in vitro synthetic cystic fibrosis sputum media (SCFM) growth. The data set collected for this study included RNA from EVPL sections cultured for 7 days, but as in vitro SCFM cultures did not survive for 7 days, no comparison could be made; therefore, data from this time point were not explored. We found that the EVPL bronchiolar tissue-associated biofilm formed at 48 h exhibited key characteristics of in vivo chronic infection not observed in vitro [9].

Here, we have built upon our previous findings to determine whether the EVPL model is viable for an extended infection time of 7 days, as a longer infection period may more accurately replicate chronic CF infections. We previously showed P. aeruginosa biofilms to have a clinically realistic, mature structure in the model at 7 days PI [10]. The structural integrity of the lung tissue was retained over 7 days of P. aeruginosa infection, and we did not observe noticeable decomposition of the tissue [10]. In this work, we have performed differential expression analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis. This confirmed that the biofilm population was maintained at 7 days with limited stress and allowed us to better understand expression changes in established biofilms and further knowledge of P. aeruginosa gene expression in later stage infections. We have re-analysed our transcriptomic data and identified changes in several genes and pathways, including upregulation of sulphur metabolism and biofilm maintenance, that accompanied increased antibiotic tolerance over 7 days. Our findings provide new insight into P. aeruginosa gene expression changes in an established biofilm infection and their potential implications for treatment success, as well as highlighting a viable extended time point for use of the EVPL model to study chronic P. aeruginosa biofilm infection in CF.

Methods

Bacterial strain

The P. aeruginosa PA14 WT strain used for this work was originally from the University of Washington [1112] and was grown on Lysogeny Broth (LB) agar (Melford Laboratories) for 24 h at 37 °C prior to all experiments.

SCFM

SCFM was made based on the original recipe by Palmer et al. and is now commonly referred to as ‘SCFM1’ [13]. Glucose was removed from the recipe as previous work using the EVPL model demonstrated that, in the presence of lung tissue, the absence of glucose did not affect P. aeruginosa growth but did facilitate endogenous lung bacterial species growth [14].

EVPL dissection and infection

Pig lung bronchiolar tissue was dissected and subsequently infected following the previously published method [910 15]. Two independent lungs were used per experiment, with three individual tissue pieces used per lung. All lungs used for this work were provided by Steve Quigley and Sons Butchers Ltd. (Cubbington, UK) as a waste product from the meat industry and were dissected and infected on the day of arrival from the abattoir. In the UK, veterinary antibiotics are prescription-only medications, and their usage in agriculture is strictly regulated; any antibiotic treatment of livestock must be followed by a withdrawal period prior to slaughter for consumption.

EVPL antibiotic treatment

Two different antibiotics were tested: colistin and polymyxin B, both prepared as master stocks as follows: colistin (Acros Organics) was prepared to 10 mg ml−1 in distilled water, and polymyxin B (Sigma-Aldrich) was prepared to 50 mg ml−1 in distilled water. The master stocks of each antibiotic were further diluted in SCFM in 15 ml Falcon tubes to the desired concentration of 256 µg ml−1 for use with the EVPL model.

The EVPL-grown biofilms were treated with each antibiotic as previously described [15]. Following incubation at 37 °C for either 24 h, 48 h or 7 days, P. aeruginosa PA14-infected pig lung tissue pieces were removed from the individual wells of a 24-well plate using flame-sterilized forceps. Each tissue piece was washed in 500 µl PBS in individual wells of a 48-well plate to remove non-biofilm-associated bacterial cells. The tissue pieces were then transferred to individual wells of a 48-well plate, each containing 500 µl of either 256 µg ml−1 colistin or 256 µg ml−1 polymyxin B. A replicate control, non-antibiotic-treated set of tissue pieces from each lung per experiment was transferred to individual wells of a 48-well plate, each containing 500 µl PBS. All plates were covered with a UV-sterilized Breathe-Easier® sealing membrane (Diversified Biotech) and incubated under stationary conditions at 37 °C for 24 h.

EVPL biofilm recovery and determination of bacterial load

Bacteria were recovered from the EVPL tissue pieces, and the bacterial load was determined following the published method [910 15]. Briefly, following the final incubation for both RNA extractions and antibiotic treatments, tissue pieces were transiently washed in 500 µl PBS in individual wells of a 48-well plate to remove non-biofilm bacterial cells. Each tissue piece was then transferred into individual, sterile homogenization tubes (Fisherbrand) containing 1 g of 2.38 mm metal beads (Fisher Scientific) and 1 ml PBS. The tissue-containing bead tubes were placed in a FastPrep-24 5G (MP Biomedicals) and bead beaten for 40 s at 4 m s−1 to recover bacterial cells from the lung-associated biofilm.

The lung homogenate was then used to determine the biofilm bacterial load associated with the EVPL tissue. An aliquot of each homogenate was serially diluted in PBS in a 96-well plate to final volumes of 200 µl. The dilutions were then plated in triplicate on LB agar and incubated overnight at 37 °C. The number of colonies of the two highest dilutions that were countable was determined and used to calculate the c.f.u. per lung for each sample.

RNA extraction and sequencing, and relationship to previously published work

All RNA samples used for this work were collected as part of a bigger study, and thus, the protocol for extraction, precipitation, DNA removal and sequencing is outlined in the original publication [9]. RNA quality prior to sequencing was confirmed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system with the RNA 6000 Pico kit (Agilent).

RNA-sequencing bioinformatic analyses

All reads were quality-checked using FastQC v0.11.8 [16], initially and following every processing step. Read trimming was performed using Trimmomatic v0.38 [17] and a minimum read length threshold of 25 bp [8]. Residual eukaryotic and bacterial rRNA were filtered out of the reads using SortMeRNA v2.1b [18], and then the remaining reads were mapped to the pig genome (Sus scrofa: National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), GCF_000003025.6) using HISAT2 v2.1.0 [19]. All reads that aligned were removed using Seqtk v1.3-r106 [20], and the remaining reads were aligned to the P. aeruginosa UCBPP-PA14 genome (NCBI, GCF_000014625.1) using the Burrows-Wheeler Alignment tool (BWA v0.7.17-r1188) with the MEM (maximal exact matches) algorithm [21]. The reads mapped to coding sequences were counted using ‘featureCounts’, part of the Rsubread v2.0.1 R package [22], against the P. aeruginosa UCBPP-PA14 annotation from pseudomonas.com [2]. Count data were normalized using the rlog transformation function, part of the R package DESeq2 v1.26.0 [23]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was then performed using the ‘plotPCA’ function within DESeq2, using genes (n=5,829), and 95% confidence ellipses were added using ggpubr v0.4.0 [24].

Differential expression analysis comparing the three time points, 24 h, 48 h and 7 days, was performed using the R package DESeq2 [23]. The threshold for significant differential expression was an adjusted P-value of <0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate) and log2-fold change (|log2FC|) ≥1.5. A Venn diagram of the numbers of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was produced using the VennDiagram v1.7.0 R package [25]. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was then performed using the DEGs with the ‘enrichKEGG’ function from the clusterProfiler v3.14.3 R package [26] and the organism KEGG code ‘pau’ for P. aeruginosa UCBPP-PA14. A KEGG pathway was considered significantly enriched if the adjusted P<0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg procedure). The antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes studied were sourced from pseudomonas.com [2] using the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) predictions [27]. Genes associated with the specific pathways, QS and biofilm formation, were sourced from the KEGG database [2830].

Scanning electron microscopy

Following EVPL dissection, infection with P. aeruginosa PA14 and incubation at 37 °C for either 24 h, 48 h or 7 days, lung tissue samples were fixed in 0.5 ml 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution in PBS in a 24-well plate at 4 °C overnight. Uninfected tissue was also prepared as a negative control for P. aeruginosa infection.

Subsequently, the 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution was removed, and the tissue samples were rinsed with PBS three times. Each tissue piece was then transferred to individual wells of a new 24-well plate and passed through an ethanol gradient at the following concentrations (v/v): 20%, 50%, 70%, 90%, 100% and a further 100%, each for 1 h. The samples were transferred to individual wells of a further 24-well plate, and 0.5 ml hexamethyldisilazane (HDMS) (electronic grade, 99+%) (VWR International Ltd) was added. The plate was incubated at room temperature for 3 h, then the HDMS solution was discarded. The tissue pieces were transferred to a new 24-well plate and left to dry in a laminar flow cabinet for 1 h. Double-sided copper tape (Agar scientific) was added to the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) sample holders (Agar scientific), then the tissue samples were placed on top. All samples were then sputtered with carbon by using a carbon coater (SC7620 Mini Sputter Coater/Glow Discharge System) and immediately imaged using a Zeiss Gemini scanning electron microscope with an InLens detector at a voltage of 1 kV. The data were analysed using Omero5.6 [31].

Data presentation and statistical analyses

All bar graphs were produced using DataGraph v4.7.1 [32] on Mac OS Mojave v10.14.6. ANOVA was performed on antibiotic treatment c.f.u. data and post-hoc Tukey HSD (honestly significant difference) tests. All statistical analyses, including ANOVA and differential expression analysis, were performed using RStudio v1.2.1335 (https://www.rstudio.com/) on Mac OS Mojave v10.14.6.

A full list of all genes included in the analysis and the DEG analysis results from the three contrasts reported are provided as a data supplement [1]. The raw sequence data are available at the European Nucleotide Archive (accession no. PRJEB48552), and the individual sample accessions are shown in the table above (Table 1).

Results

As reported in our previous study [9], we grew P. aeruginosa PA14 as a biofilm associated with the surface of EVPL bronchiolar tissue from two independent pig lungs for 24 h, 48 h and 7 days, and performed RNA sequencing from the recovered tissue-associated biofilm population. Our previous work confirmed that PA14 was still metabolically active 7 days PI, using an ATP-based viability assay [9] (Fig. S3 (Supplementary Material 1)), and the bacterial load was maintained; recovered c.f.u. per lung 7 days PI were comparable with 24 and 48 h PI [9] (Fig. S4 (Supplementary Material 1)). In the present study, we have extended our previous work to investigate P. aeruginosa PA14 gene expression changes in the EVPL tissue-associated biofilm at 24 h, 48 h and 7 days PI [P<0.05, |log2FC| ≥1.5]. For all comparisons, the earlier time point was used as the baseline.

Expression of P. aeruginosa genes significantly changed over 7 days in the EVPL biofilm

PCA of PA14 gene expression identified distinct transcriptomes at 24 h, 48 h and 7 days PI (Fig. S1, (Supplementary Material 1). There was no clear difference between samples from different lungs, indicating that variation between time points was much larger than between-lung variation. P. aeruginosa grown for 24 h was the most distinct from the other time points, supported by hierarchical clustering and Pearson’s correlation coefficient analyses (Figs S1 and S2, (Supplementary Material 1)). Differential expression analysis similarly showed the greatest difference at 24 h vs 48 h or 7 days PI, with the smallest number of significantly DEGs arising between 48 h and 7 days (Fig. 1).

Overall, more genes were found to be significantly overexpressed than underexpressed between sequential time points (Fig. 2a), suggesting that gene expression increased over time in the EVPL biofilm. While four genes showed changes in expression over all three time points, there were also unique DEGs identified for each time point comparison (Fig. 2b). This indicates that there were different dynamics in gene expression changes across the three time points.

To further investigate the gene expression changes over time in the EVPL-associated biofilm, expression profiles were determined based on shared, unique and non-significant changes between time point comparisons. We classified the DEGs into seven profiles based on the direction and magnitude of their change in expression 24–48 h and 48 h to 7 days, as outlined in Table 2 (see data supplement [1] for full gene lists).

Sulphur metabolism was upregulated over 7 days in the EVPL-associated biofilm

As expression changes between 24 h and 7 days appeared to have the greatest functional implications, KEGG enrichment analysis was performed on all significant DEGs that arose in this comparison. There were no P. aeruginosa PA14 pathways significantly enriched between 24 and 48 h, and those found between 48 h and 7 days, as shown in Fig. S3 (Supplementary Material 1) (phenazine biosynthesis, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, QS), were comparable to expression profile 6 (Fig. 2c). Four pathways were significantly enriched between 24 h and 7 days in the EVPL-associated biofilm (P<0.05): sulphur metabolism, ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, degradation of aromatic compounds and nitrogen metabolism (Fig. 3).

Each bar shows the percentage of genes associated with each pathway that were significantly differentially expressed (P<0.05, |log2FC| ≥1.5). The greatest percentage of associated significant DEGs was for sulphur metabolism (Fig. 3): 47.06% of sulphur metabolism genes were significantly overexpressed in the EVPL biofilm between 24 h and 7 days, suggesting this pathway was upregulated. This included sulphate transport genes cysA, cysW and cysT (3.04, 2.87 and 3.14 log2FC, respectively). There was only one associated gene significantly reduced in expression at 7 days: the sulphite/nitrate reductase PA14_10550 (−1.56 log2FC). The majority of the significantly differentially expressed ABC transporter genes were involved in sulphur metabolism. However, three phosphate-specific transporter genes were also significantly overexpressed: pstB (1.52 log2FC), pstA (1.83 log2FC) and pstC (1.88 log2FC).

Nitrogen metabolism was the only significantly enriched pathway where more significant DEGs decreased in expression between 24 h and 7 days than increased (Fig. 3). Expression of genes in the P. aeruginosa nitrogen metabolism operons responsible for a shift towards anaerobic respiration in CF sputum (nor, nir, nap and nos [36]) was investigated, and all log2FC values are shown in Table S2 (Supplementary Material 1). However, there was no clear, shared pattern of upregulation or downregulation observed (Table S2 (Supplementary Material 1)).

Antimicrobial tolerance increased in the P. aeruginosa PA14 EVPL-associated biofilm over 7 days despite limited AMR gene expression changes

Our previous work highlighted significant differential expression of AMR genes in the P. aeruginosa PA14 EVPL-associated biofilm compared with in vitro growth at both 24 and 48 h [9]. Therefore, we next aimed to investigate whether any of the changes in gene expression found over 7 days were linked to AMR (see Table S3 (Supplementary Material 1)).

Three AMR genes were found to be significantly reduced in expression over time in the EVPL biofilm (Table 3), and none were found to be significantly increased. The full AMR gene list investigated is shown in Table S3 (Supplementary Material 1), alongside the log2FC values for every time point comparison. The brc-1 gene, encoding a major facilitator superfamily (MFS) transporter associated with bicyclomycin resistance [37], was significantly reduced in expression between 24 h and 7 days. Bicyclomycin is synthesized by P. aeruginosa [38], suggesting that production may be reduced over the 7 days. The other two significant DEGs: mexG and arnA, which encode a transmembrane protein associated with multiple efflux pumps and a bifunctional uridine diphosphate-glucuronic acid decarboxylase/uridine diphosphate-4-amino-4-deoxy-l-arabinose formyltransferase [2], were significantly reduced in expression 24–48 h. The enzyme encoded by arnA is involved in lipid A modifications, linked to cationic antimicrobial peptide (CAMP) resistance [3940], and was previously shown to be significantly overexpressed in the EVPL-associated biofilm compared with in vitro SCFM growth at 24 h [9].

To determine whether reduction in expression of the CAMP resistance-associated gene arnA had a phenotypic effect, P. aeruginosa PA14 was grown as a biofilm on the EVPL tissue for 24 h, 48 h or 7 days, and exposed to each of two clinically relevant CAMPs, colistin and polymyxin B, for a further 24 h(Fig. 4) . P. aeruginosa grown as a biofilm for 24 h could not be recovered following treatment with 256 µg ml−1 colistin (Fig. 1a). However, after 48 h of initial growth, PA14 was inconsistently recovered (Fig. 1b), potentially caused by emerging genetic resistance. By 7 days, all biofilm populations were able to tolerate 256 µg ml−1 colistin (Fig. 1c). This pattern was comparable with the response to 256 µg ml−1 polymyxin B (Fig. 4). The increased polymyxin tolerance was a contrast with the limited changes in AMR gene expression observed and the significantly reduced expression of arnA. This may be linked to persistence of non-dividing cells in the biofilm population. As we do not know the rate of P. aeruginosa division in the EVPL model, it may be possible that there is an early increase in efflux pump and arnA expression that modifies the cell surface to a CAMP-tolerant phenotype, which is maintained. Alternatively, there may be other phenotypic factors driving increased tolerance, such as the established biofilm providing protection against CAMPs by reducing the final concentration that reaches bacterial cells [41]. This is consistent with the higher surface coverage and likely increased biofilm depth observed using SEM at 7 days (Fig. 5) compared with 48 h (Fig. 6).

A mature, structured P. aeruginosa biofilm was maintained for 7 days on the surface of the EVPL tissue, associated with reduced expression of Pel biosynthesis

Therefore, to further explore the increase in antimicrobial tolerance observed over 7 days, gene expression changes in QS (Fig. S4, (Supplementary Material 1)) and biofilm formation pathways over time in the EVPL were investigated. Both are involved in the establishment of antibiotic-tolerant, chronic P. aeruginosa infections in the CF lung, and the EVPL biofilm has been shown to capture these aspects of in vivo infection at 48 h PI [9].

Our previous study demonstrated that the relative expression of QS-regulated genes controlled by the las regulon at 48 h PI in the EVPL-associated biofilm vs SCFM in vitro was comparable with the relative expression in human CF sputum vs in vitro [20]. In the present analysis, there were no temporal differences in QS gene expression in the EVPL in any of the time point comparisons (Figs 7, S4–S6). This indicates that a biofilm infection representative of human infection was likely maintained for 7 days (Fig. S5, (Supplementary Material 1)).

Other genes associated with P. aeruginosa PA14 virulence factor production were also investigated, including those involved in motility, type III secretion, type VI secretion and alginate biosynthesis; however, no significant differential expression over time was found.

In contrast, all genes in the operon for biosynthesis of the biofilm exopolysaccharide Pel were found to be significantly reduced in expression 24 h to 7 days (Fig. 7), five of which were also significantly reduced between 24 and 48 h (Fig. S6, (Supplementary Material 1)). This suggests that these biofilm genes were most highly expressed in the early establishment phase of the biofilm, and their expression decreased at the later time points. To visualize the biofilm formed, we used SEM. Uninfected tissue was imaged as a negative control (Fig. S7, (Supplementary Material 1)). A dense, structured P. aeruginosa PA14 biofilm was clear at 48 h (Fig. 6). The biofilm population was maintained at 7 days, where a very dense biofilm of P. aeruginosa PA14 cells was observed (Fig. 5), which was absent from the uninfected tissue (Fig. S7, (Supplementary Material 1)).

Discussion

We have used RNA sequencing data [9] to investigate P. aeruginosa biofilm infection in the EVPL model over 7 days and improve understanding of temporal changes in expression during chronic infection. We identified upregulation of several non-stress-related pathways, including sulphur metabolism, and demonstrated increased antibiotic tolerance. SEM confirmed that a dense, mature biofilm was formed by 48 h and was maintained at 7 days PI, with no visible indications of stress. Our findings show that the EVPL model provides an environment for P. aeruginosa biofilm formation that, once a clinically relevant biofilm has formed at 48 h [9], can be maintained as a chronic-like infection for 7 days and displays characteristics of clinical importance.

Of all the significantly enriched KEGG pathways over time, sulphur metabolism was associated with the highest percentage of significant DEGs. More specifically, all genes in the sulphur transporter operon associated with cysteine biosynthesis, cysTWA, were found to be significantly overexpressed between 24 h and 7 days. In Escherichia coli, upregulation of this operon has been linked to sulphate starvation [42]. However, investigation of the sulphate starvation response in P. aeruginosa PAO1 and a cystic fibrosis isolate found that cysteine biosynthesis genes were only weakly upregulated when sulphate was limited [43]. P. aeruginosa PA14 upregulation of sulphur metabolism in SCFM has actually been linked to a shift towards anaerobic respiration [44], which is associated with increased antibiotic tolerance [45], also observed in our model. Additionally, links have been made between iron starvation and sulphur metabolism. However, during iron starvation, proteins involved in cysteine biosynthesis and sulphur assimilation are typically reduced [46]. Therefore, it may be most likely that P. aeruginosa upregulation of these genes in the EVPL model was driven by a change in respiration. This further supports the progression of P. aeruginosa biofilm infection in the EVPL model as capturing the lifestyle changes observed in CF.

However, it must also be considered that the pig lung tissue was no longer alive, and the action of bacterial proteases and other virulence factors may degrade the tissue, releasing sulphur-containing amino acids that could potentially impact P. aeruginosa sulphur metabolism. To further investigate the interaction between P. aeruginosa and the pig lung tissue, and the underlying mechanisms driving upregulation of sulphur metabolism, sulphur and oxygen availability in the EVPL model and tissue-associated biofilm should be determined in the future. Additionally, future experiments specifically focused on sulphur metabolism (e.g. knockout or inhibition studies) are required to further validate our findings.

Our previous work demonstrated that the EVPL biofilm may more accurately recapitulate P. aeruginosa QS gene expression in CF sputum than other in vitro models [9]. Therfore, P. aeruginosa PA14 changes in expression of genes in the three QS pathways over 7 days were investigated: LasI/R (3-oxo-C12-homoserine lactone system), RhlI/R (C4-homoserine lactone system) and PQS (Pseudomonas quinolone system). No significant changes were found. Other regulatory pathways involved in lifestyle changes, including the switch from acute to chronic infection, were also investigated; however, no clear differences in expression were found. There were also no significant changes in virulence factor gene expression. It is known that, as part of adaptation to the CF lung and formation of a chronic infection, virulence factor production is significantly reduced [47]. Therefore, P. aeruginosa may have already switched off virulence factor production by 24 h in the EVPL model as it formed the established biofilm visualized at 48 h; hence, no change in gene expression was observed. This is supported by the findings in our previous study, in which we found that two of the major protease-encoding genes, lasA and lasB, were significantly underexpressed in the EVPL-associated biofilm compared with SCFM in vitro growth at both 24 and 48 h [9]. This may also explain why no significant differential expression was observed in regulatory pathways, including QS. Additionally, adaptation to the CF lung is also linked to mutations in P. aeruginosa virulence pathways [47]. Future work looking at the accumulation of mutations over 7 days in the EVPL-associated P. aeruginosa biofilm would provide further insight. However, these results combined support the EVPL as a model for chronic infection, where virulence factor production and QS gene expression are consistently low.

In contrast, significant changes in the expression of biofilm-associated genes were found in the EVPL PA14 biofilm population over 7 days. Pel biosynthesis was significantly downregulated between 24 and 48 h, then maintained at the same expression level between 48 h and 7 days, which resulted in significant downregulation between 24 h and 7 days. This indicated that Pel production was reduced after 24 h of infection and then maintained from 48 h onwards. Pel is a biofilm exopolysaccharide essential for PA14 biofilm development and is only expressed during biofilm growth [4849]. Whilst our results do not confirm that there was not any Pel being produced at the later time points, they indicate less than the initial stage of infection in the EVPL model. This may have been caused by upregulation of Pel biosynthesis as the biofilm was established at 24 h, which was reduced once the biofilm was formed at 48 h and maintained at this lower level over the remaining 5 days. This hypothesis was supported by SEM images of the biofilm, which showed a dense, structured P. aeruginosa biofilm already present at 48 h.

Taking this into account, following formation of a biofilm, gene expression analysis indicated that the focus may have shifted from production of the biofilm matrix to biofilm maintenance and maturation between 48 h and 7 days. Significant upregulation of two lectin genes was also observed in the lung-associated biofilm between 24 h and 7 days, and between 24 and 48 h. Both have been associated with a mature biofilm; lecA is indicated to play a role in the formation of a mature biofilm architecture [50], and lecB has been shown to be involved in increased cell and exopolysaccharide retention in biofilms [51]. This retention may also have been associated with the reduction in Pel production, as less new Pel would be required if the exopolysaccharide already produced was being retained. The proposed maintenance of a mature biofilm structure was also accompanied by increased antimicrobial tolerance. Additionally, inhibitors of both LecA and LecB have been proposed as a novel, non-antibiotic therapeutic to tackle P. aeruginosa infections in CF [52]. Our findings further support this as a potential treatment target for established biofilm infection.

We have also previously found that the EVPL growth environments cue differential expression of a number of AMR genes compared with in vitro growth, despite no antibiotic exposure, at both 24 and 48 h [9]. However, only three genes involved in AMR were differentially expressed over 7 days in the EVPL biofilm, suggesting that differential expression of resistance-associated genes cued by the environment is unlikely to be dynamic and remains consistent throughout 7 days of infection. The significantly differentially expressed AMR genes that were found included significant reduction in expression of two individual antibiotic resistance genes: arnA and bcr-1, associated with resistance to CAMPs [40] and bicyclomycin [37], respectively.

Despite these findings, increased tolerance to colistin and polymyxin B was observed at 7 days, compared with 24 and 48 h. It is hypothesized that the formation of a biofilm in the EVPL model, in which the matrix facilitates antibiotic resistance [3], may have resulted in other mechanisms of antibiotic resistance being downregulated as they were not required. This would provide an explanation as to why the expression of AMR genes was reduced once a biofilm had formed, and the matrix was no longer being rapidly produced. This finding would further support the use of biofilm disruptors to improve treatment regimens, as the P. aeruginosa cells within the biofilm may not be expressing genes associated with antibiotic resistance and are thus more susceptible to treatments.

However, it must also be considered that there were other indirect mechanisms of antibiotic resistance that were found to be significantly overexpressed in the EVPL biofilm, which are associated with potential environmental pressures. Phosphate transporters were significantly overexpressed from 24 h to 7 days, indicative of phosphate stress. Under phosphate stress, P. aeruginosa has been shown to undergo lipid modifications that confer resistance to polymyxin B. There is a transition from phospholipids to glycolipids, which confer protection against polymyxin B [53]. Therefore, this differential expression may also be responsible for, or involved in, the increased antibiotic tolerance observed. Future investigation of P. aeruginosa lipid modifications in the EVPL model environment would further our understanding. This analysis has, however, indicated that these pathways may be induced; thus, investigation of lipid modifications cued by the EVPL model may highlight novel treatment targets.

In summary, we have demonstrated that there are changes in the P. aeruginosa lung-associated biofilm transcriptome in the EVPL model over 24 h to 7 days of infection. We have identified an increase in polymyxin tolerance between 48 h and 7 days that is accompanied by upregulation of sulphur metabolism and phosphate transport, rather than AMR-associated genes. Whilst further work is required to fully determine the chronic-like phenotype formed by 7 days PI, and any potential impact of tissue-invading cells, our results highlight a key further time point to address future research questions related to chronic lung infection using the EVPL model. Future work with larger sample sizes and the introduction of other factors that influence P. aeruginosa gene expression in the CF lung, such as other microbes, may reveal other changes in the biofilm population over time that facilitate survival in these conditions in vivo. Further understanding of these mechanisms will provide a better understanding of how to eradicate or prevent chronic P. aeruginosa infections in the CF lung.
