Evolutionary insights from the mitochondrial genomes of invasive scale insects (Hemiptera: Coccomorpha): large-scale transfer RNA gene truncations and tandem repeat-driven intraspecific gene rearrangements
Authors: Fei Ye, Jia-Yi Zhou, Zhi-Qiang Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoccomorphan mitochondrial genomes (mitogenomes) exhibit a suite of unconventional genomic features. However, the insufficient mitogenomic data from Coccomorpha, which includes numerous economically important plant pests, hampers not only the investigation of mitogenome evolution but also the molecular identification and population origin tracing of pest species.ResultsHere, we sequenced the complete mitogenomes of five invasive coccomorphans and compared the performances of short-read- and long-read-based assemblies. Moreover, the newly generated and 16 previously reported mitogenomes were subjected to comparative genomic and phylogenetic analyses. We found that an extremely high A + T content, large-scale tRNA gene truncation and frequent gene rearrangement were ubiquitous among the coccomorphan mitogenomes. Notably, our data reveal that massive tRNA arm loss and gene rearrangements provide informative phylogenetic characteristics within Coccomorpha. The rapid evolution of ATP8, driven by relaxed purifying selection, has resulted in divergent ATP8 gene lengths and relative genomic positions. Furthermore, long-read sequencing data revealed the heteroplasmic landscape of the repeat sequence copy numbers in two Icerya species, indicating that repeat sequences drive rapid intraspecific evolution of genome size, gene content, and gene rearrangement and increase mitogenomic plasticity.ConclusionsOur study supports the use of long-read sequencing as an efficient and useful approach for resolving complex genomic architectures and heteroplasmy. This study also identifies several potential genome-level phylogenetic markers and advances our understanding of mitogenome evolution within Coccomorpha.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12250-0.Keywords: Mitochondrial genome, Gene rearrangement, Transfer RNA secondary structure, Repetitive sequence, Coccomorpha

Background

Mitochondria are semiautonomous organelles that play crucial roles in many significant biological processes, such as oxidative phosphorylation, cellular metabolism, apoptosis, and the stress response [1]. Most bilaterian mitochondrial genomes (mitogenomes) are small (~ 14–20 kb) double-stranded circular DNA molecules encoding 37 genes, specifically 13 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 22 transfer RNA (tRNA) genes and two ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes. In addition to these 37 genes, the compact mitogenome includes several noncoding regions (NCRs), among which the longest region is generally the control region (CR) involved in genome replication and transcription [2]. Due to the high substitution rate compared with nuclear DNA, maternal inheritance, limited recombination and conserved gene contents, mitochondrial genes and mitogenomes are among the most common molecular markers used for species delimitation, phylogenetics, phylogeography, and palaeogenomics [3, 4].

PCR-based Sanger sequencing has been traditionally used for mitogenome sequencing for decades [5], but this approach has sometimes faced challenges such as extreme base composition bias, large-scale genome duplications, and various repeats [6, 7]. With the rapid development of sequencing technology, next-generation sequencing (NGS) has become the main approach for mitogenome sequencing over the last decade [8]. Although NGS has made mitogenome sequencing easier and less expensive, there remain several potential limitations of short read (~ 100–300 bp)-based assemblies, especially related to tandem repeat sequences [9]. Recently, key advances in genome sequencing have been provided by single-molecule sequencing platforms from Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) and Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT), which can generate long reads (> 10 kb) that cover at least half the length of most bilaterian mitogenomes. These long-read sequencing methods can overcome the limitations of Sanger sequencing and the NGS methods mentioned above and provide more evidence through which to explore mitogenomic structure, gene content, rearrangement and heteroplasmy [10–12].

To date, the mitogenomes of more than 11,000 bilaterian species [13] including more than 5800 insects, have been sequenced [8], but the taxonomic coverage of these mitogenomes is quite limited and disproportionate across lineages. Sternorrhyncha, a suborder of the fifth largest insect order (Hemiptera), comprises more than 18,000 described species in four major groups: Aphidomorpha (aphids), Coccomorpha (scale insects), Aleyrodomorpha (whiteflies) and Psylloidea (psyllids) [14]. Among the Sternorrhyncha, Coccomorpha is the most species-rich and morphologically diverse lineage, comprising ~ 8500 species in 56 families [15], but it has the fewest reported mitogenomes (< 20 species). The first glimpse of the coccomorphan mitogenome, provided by a combination of NGS and Sanger sequencing of the Ceroplastes japonicus mitogenome, revealed several rare genomic features, such as extremely high A + T content, massive gene rearrangements, and truncated tRNAs [16]. Subsequent studies confirmed that these features, which are quite distinct from those of other hemipteran insects [17], are present in several neococcoid families [18] and archaeococcoid Matsucoccidae [19]. In addition, gene loss has been reported in some neococcoid families [18]. All these unconventional and diverse features imply that coccomorphan mitogenomes have been shaped by unusual evolutionary processes, and despite substantial efforts devoted to morphological, single/multi-locus, and phylogenomic analyses, the deeper phylogeny of Coccomorpha remains incompletely resolved, with critical uncertainties surrounding the monophyly and phylogenetic placement of several families, such as Coccidae and Eriococcidae [20–22]. Therefore, systematic comparative mitogenomic analyses with expanded sampling, particularly within archaeococcoids, based on unambiguous phylogenetic relationships are required for a more detailed understanding of these evolutionary scenarios.

Scale insects include many serious agricultural and horticultural pests that damage host plants by sucking plant sap, transmitting plant pathogens and excreting large quantities of honeydew [23, 24]. In addition, scale insects are the most invasive group of insects, and many of them, such as Icerya aegyptiaca, Phenacoccus solenopsis, and Ceroplastes rusci [25–27], are global invasive pests listed on quarantine lists in many countries. However, no mitogenome sequences are available for many invasive scale insects, and even the universal barcode gene COI is quite limited in GenBank for these species, which has hindered rapid species identification, the resolution of population genetic structure, biogeographic inference, etc. Therefore, there is a clear need to obtain more plentiful mitogenome data specifically tailored for research on these invasive agricultural and horticultural pests.

In this study, we sequenced the mitogenomes of five invasive scale insects: Icerya purchasi and I. aegyptiaca (Monophlebidae) were sequenced using long-read sequencing and NGS, respectively, and C. rusci (Coccidae), Phenacoccus madeirensis and P. solenopsis (Pseudococcidae) were sequenced using NGS coupled with Sanger sequencing. After accurate assembly and precise annotation of these mitogenomes, we conducted comparative genomic analyses focused on gene arrangement, tRNA secondary structure and tandem repeat sequences, and reconstructed the phylogeny of Coccomorpha. Our analyses revealed atypical mitogenomic features, including novel gene rearrangements, massive tRNA arm loss, and high levels of tandem repeats and intraspecific gene order heteroplasmy, improving our understanding of mitogenomic evolution in Coccomorpha.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and DNA extraction

Five invasive scale insects (I. aegyptiaca, I. purchasi, C. rusci, P. solenopsis, and P. madeirensis) were collected from different sampling sites in Guangdong Province, China (Table S1), and then preserved in 100% ethanol at −20 ℃. Specimens were identified according to their morphological features and confirmed via COI sequences. Total genomic DNA was extracted from 1–15 individuals of each species using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide method [28]. All the voucher samples were deposited at the Institute of Zoology, Guangdong Academy of Sciences, Guangzhou, China.

Genome sequencing, assembly and annotation

For the NGS method, independent short-insert (350 bp) libraries for all five species were constructed and then sequenced with a 150 bp paired end (PE) on the DNBSEQ platform at the BGI (Shenzhen, China). The raw DNBSEQ reads were filtered by removing reads containing adaptor contamination or poly-Ns (> 5 bp Ns) and PE reads with more than 20% low-quality bases (scores < 20) using fastp v0.24.3 [29]. For the long-read sequencing, total genomic DNA was sheared to between 15 and 20 kb using the Megaruptor system. Single-molecule real-time (SMRT) bell libraries were constructed for I. aegyptiaca and I. purchasi, and then sequenced on the PacBio Revio platform at the BGI (Shenzhen, China). The raw SMRT data were processed using SMARTLink to generate high fidelity (HiFi) reads. After quality control and preprocessing of the raw data mentioned above, we obtained 7.92 Gb (I. purchasi) and 10.29 Gb (I. aegyptiaca) of PacBio HiFi data and 3.28–3.62 Gb of clean DNBSEQ data per species. In addition to the NGS and long-read sequencing, the COI and gap regions between NGS assemblies were amplified by PCR employing the universal primers LCO1490/HCO2198 [30] and specific primers (Table S2) designed using Primer Premier v5.0 (Premier Biosoft, California, USA) and then subjected to direct bidirectional Sanger sequencing or clone-based Sanger sequencing (NCR of two Phenacoccus species).

The NGS data were de novo assembled using NOVOPlasty v4.3.1 [31] (with a default k-mer size of 33 and COI or other mitochondrial genes as seed sequences) and SOAPdenovo-Trans v1.03 (k-mer size = 55) [32] followed by BLAST against local databases containing 10 known Coccomorpha mitogenomes using the program BLAST + v2.9.0 [33]. The resulting mitochondrial sequences from both assemblers were aligned using Muscle v5.1 [34], as implemented in Geneious Prime v2024.0.2 (Biomatters, Auckland, New Zealand), and then, all aligned sites with base differences were verified by PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing, as performed for the gap regions mentioned above. The final mitogenomes were assembled using Geneious Prime based on NGS contigs and Sanger sequences. The PacBio HiFi data were assembled following the modified MitoHiFi strategy [35]. Initially, we mapped the PacBio HiFi reads of Icerya spp. to the corresponding mitochondrial contigs assembled from the NGS data using Minimap2 v2.24 [36] and then filtered the aligned HiFi reads to exclude the reads with < 80% A + T content to avoid nuclear-mitochondrial DNA segments (NUMTs). The filtered HiFi read set was assembled using Hifiasm v0.19.6 [37], followed by BLAST against the corresponding mitogenome reference with BLAST +. Finally, the gap region of the major haplotype (with the highest proportion of reads) from four haplotypes and another rare haplotype with only a few HiFi reads of I. aegyptiaca were independently assembled using Geneious Prime according to the mapped reads spanning the corresponding regions.

The mitochondrial genes were roughly annotated using MITOS v2.1.8 [38], and the accurate boundaries of the PCGs and rRNA genes were determined by alignment with homologous genes in published Coccomorpha mitogenomes using Muscle. The tRNA genes and their secondary structures were additionally predicted by tRNAscan-SE v2.0 [39] and ARWEN v1.2.3 [40]. Furthermore, all the intergenic regions (> 20 bp) were aligned with reference tRNA genes from closely related species to identify undetected tRNA genes. These newly sequenced mitogenomes were deposited into GenBank under accession numbers PV744417–PV744421.

Mitogenome analyses and phylogenetic inference

Base composition and codon usage were analysed using MEGA v11 [41], and the base composition skews were calculated using the formulae AT-skew = (A − T)/(A + T) and GC-skew = (G − C)/(G + C) [42]. To evaluate the PCG evolutionary rate, the rates of nonsynonymous substitutions (Ka) and synonymous substitutions (Ks) and the Ka/Ks ratios for 13 PCGs were calculated using KaKs_Calculator v3.0 [43]. The amino acid hydrophobicity profiles of ATP8 for scale insects, aphids, whiteflies and psyllids (Table S3) were analysed using Phobius v1.01 [44] and TMHMM v2.0 [45]. Tandem repeat sequences were characterized using Tandem Repeats Finder v4.07 [46] and MISA-web [47]. The secondary structures of the repeat units were predicted using Mfold [48]. The collinearity analysis of newly sequenced mitogenomes was conducted using Easyfig v2.2.5 [49]. Gene rearrangement analyses for the newly sequenced mitogenomes were conducted using CREx [50] and the same sets of genes. The ancestral character reconstruction of gene arrangement of PCGs and rRNA genes was conducted using the likelihood reconstruction method with the Markov k-state 1 parameter model implemented in Mesquite v3.81 [51]. Considering the difficulty and uncertainty in the annotation of some tRNA genes of coccomorphan mitogenomes, aligned tRNA genes with low sequence similarity (< 50%) were excluded from investigations of the evolution of tRNA secondary structure to avoid the inclusion of potentially nonhomologous tRNA genes.

Phylogenetic relationships within Coccomorpha were reconstructed using Bayesian inference (BI) and maximum likelihood (ML) methods on the basis of 13 PCGs and two rRNA genes from the mitogenome. A total of 24 terminal species were included in the phylogenetic analyses, of which 21 coccomorphans were included in the ingroup and two aphidomorphans and one aleyrodomorphan were used as outgroups (Table S3). Both protein and nucleotide sequences were individually aligned using Muscle. These individual alignments were subsequently filtered using Gblocks v0.91b [52] to remove ambiguously aligned sites. All 15 genes were concatenated into three matrices, namely, AARNA, NT12RNA, and NT123RNA. The AARNA matrix comprised the amino acid sequences of 13 PCGs and nucleotide sequences of two RNA genes. The latter two matrices contained the nucleotide sequences of the 13 PCGs and two RNA genes, with the NT12RNA matrix excluding the third codon positions of the 13 PCGs. The best-fit substitution model and partitioning scheme (Table S4) were examined using ModelFinder [53] and PartitionFinder [54] implemented in IQ-TREE v2.2.2.6 [55]. The BI and ML analyses were performed using MrBayes v3.2.7 [56] and IQ-TREE, respectively. For the BI analyses, two simultaneous runs of 10,000,000 generations were conducted, with sampling every 500 generations and a burn-in of the first 25% of generations. Convergence was confirmed by the values of an average standard deviation of split frequencies < 0.01. For the ML analyses, node support values were assessed using the ultrafast bootstrap approach (UFBoot) with 2000 replicates.

Results

Mitogenome assembly and general features

In this study, we determined the complete mitogenomes of five invasive scale insects belonging to Monophlebidae, Pseudococcidae, and Coccidae. Mitochondrial contigs assembled using both the NOVOPlasty and SOAPdenovo-Trans methods were generated from a total of 22.8–159.6 k mitochondrial short reads per sample. Overall, NOVOPlasty produced better results than did SOAPdenovo-Trans, especially for the repeat sequences (Table 1, Fig. S1), and both assemblers failed to produce circular mitogenomes, with 1–3 gaps or ambiguous regions for each species. The complete mitogenomes of C. rusci, P. solenopsis, and P. madeirensis were finally generated from the consensus sequences of the NGS contigs produced via the two assembly methods and Sanger sequences overlapping the gap regions. However, PCR amplification of the gap regions between NGS contigs failed for I. aegyptiaca and I. purchasi because these were large-scale repeat sequences. Subsequently, the entire mitogenomes of I. aegyptiaca and I. purchasi were assembled from 420 and 236 PacBio HiFi reads respectively, and the main haplotype was selected as the representative for mitogenome description and analyses.

The lengths of the newly determined mitogenomes ranged from 14,286 bp (P. madeirensis) to 21,925 bp (I. aegyptiaca). Only the C. rusci mitogenome contained the 37 typical mitochondrial genes, while a total of 35, 36, 41, and 69 mitochondrial genes were found in the P. madeirensis, P. solenopsis, I. purchasi, and I. aegyptiaca mitogenomes, respectively (Figs. 1 and S2, Tables S5–S9). The tRNA gene content varied among the latter four mitogenomes, of which trnC was not predicted in P. madeirensis and P. solenopsis; trnV was not detected in P. madeirensis; extra trnNs were found in I. purchasi; and extra trnAs, trnRs, trnS1s, and trnEs were annotated in I. aegyptiaca. These five mitogenomes presented strong base-composition bias, with extremely high A + T contents, ranging from 86.9% (I. purchasi) to 90.0% (P. solenopsis). The A + T content of coding genes was stable, and that of tRNA and rRNA genes was greater than that of PCGs, while the range of the A + T content was the largest in the CRs, reaching 14.7% (Fig. S3). The majority strands of five mitogenomes displayed different levels of A bias and a strong C bias.

Protein-coding genes: truncated ATP8

A full complement of 13 PCGs was retrieved from the sequenced mitogenomes. All PCGs started with the standard start codon ATN and finished with the common stop codon TAA/TAG or incomplete stop codon TA/T. Codons consisting solely of A and/or U (UUU, AUU, UUA, AAU, AUA, UAU, and AAA) were the most prevalent (63.7%–68.3%), whereas GC-rich codons, such as GCG, CGC, and CGG, were less common or even absent (Fig. S4). As shown in Fig. 2, the average Ka/Ks ratio values of the 13 PCGs in 21 coccomorphan mitogenomes ranged from 0.02 (COI) to 0.75 (ATP8), indicating that all these PCGs were under purifying selection. Apart from ATP8, the Ka/Ks values of three genes encoding NADH dehydrogenase subunits (ND2, ND4L, and ND6) were greater than 0.1, and those of the remaining nine genes were less than or equal to 0.1.

The newly sequenced and annotated ATP8 genes of P. solenopsis and P. madeirensis were composed of only 99 and 105 nucleotides, encoding 32 and 34 amino acids respectively, which was similar to the ATP8 gene of Matsucoccus matsumurae (108 bp in length encoding 35 amino acids) but markedly shorter than those of the remaining sequenced coccomorphans (Fig S5). The amino acid alignment of ATP8 revealed that the first four amino acids were not well conserved in scale insects and that more than 10 amino acids at the C-terminus were truncated in P. solenopsis and P. madeirensis, and M. matsumurae (Fig. 2). The hydrophobicity profiles of the aligned ATP8 amino acid sequences were similar, with transmembrane regions in the N-terminal half of the sequences after a short hydrophilic sequence.

Secondary structure of tRNA

The secondary structures of tRNAs from the five scale insects are shown in Figs. S6–S10. Most of them could not be folded into the typical cloverleaf secondary structure with four arms. The arm loss patterns were similar within closely related species but quite distinct among the three families. For C. rusci, only four tRNAs (trnI, trnK, trnL2, and trnM) produced the classical cloverleaf structure, whereas 11 tRNAs lacked either the DHU arm (trnL1, trnN, trnQ, and trnY) or the TψC arm (trnD, trnE, trnF, trnG, trnH, trnP, and trnT), and the remaining seven tRNAs lacked both the DHU and TψC arms. For two Phenacoccus mealybugs, 17 tRNAs presented a similar secondary structure pattern, namely, eight tRNAs (trnE, trnF, trnG, trnH, trnI, trnL2, trnM and trnT) with the typical cloverleaf structure; seven tRNAs (trnA, trnK, trnL1, trnN, trnQ, trnS1, and trnS2) lacking the DHU arm; and two tRNAs (trnD and trnW) lacking the TψC arm. However, three tRNAs with different secondary structures in these two mealybugs, viz. trnP and trnY in P. madeirensis and trnR in P. solenopsis each have a full set of four arms; the trnR in P. madeirensis and trnY in P. solenopsis lack the DHU arm; and trnP in P. solenopsis lacks the TψC arm. Only trnV in P. solenopsis lacked both DHU and TψC arms. The similarity of secondary structure patterns between the two Icerya species was greater than that between the two Phenacoccus species. Similar stems and loops were found in all tRNAs in the two Icerya species, except for trnP. Among them, the typical cloverleaf structure was predicted in seven tRNAs (trnE, trnI, trnK, trnL1, trnL2, trnM, and trnY). The TψC and DHU arms were missing for six tRNAs (trnD, trnF, trnG, trnH, trnT, and trnW) and three tRNAs (trnN, trnQ and trnR) respectively, and both arms were missing for five tRNAs (trnA, trnC, trnS1, trnS2, and trnV). In I. aegyptiaca, trnP lost the TψC arm, but this lost arm was replaced by a short minimal arm with a one-pair match stem in the corresponding tRNA of I. purchasi. Similar short stems were also found in trnR.

Integrated phylogenetic and secondary structure analyses of the 15 evaluated tRNAs revealed the following results: (1) arm-less tRNAs were ubiquitous in both archaeococcoid and neococcoid mitogenomes; (2) the secondary structure patterns of 15 tRNAs were relatively conserved within the same family and distinct among families/clades; and (3) arm loss in the secondary structures of various tRNAs indicates diverse evolutionary processes.

Control region organization and repeat sequences

The location, length and characteristics of CRs varied among the newly sequenced mitogenomes. The CRs of the two Icerya species shared the same location between trnV and ND4, and those of C. rusci and P. solenopsis were located downstream of rrnS; however, the P. madeirensis mitogenome had two relatively large intergenic regions, i.e., NCR1 (531 bp) between trnS2 and ND1 and NCR2 (176 bp) between rrnS and trnM. The length of NCR1 and the location of NCR2 were similar to those of the CR of P. solenopsis (551 bp). The CR lengths of the other mitogenomes were 1972 bp in I. purchasi, 2816 bp in I. aegyptiaca, and 3670 bp in C. rusci. The CRs of insect mitogenomes are usually called the A + T rich region due to their high A + T contents. In general, the A + T content of CR is greater than that of coding regions in many insect lineages. While only I. aegyptiaca and P. madeirensis exhibited this trend, I. purchasi CR presented the lowest A + T content (80.4%) among the different partitions, which is associated with differences in CR organization.

As shown in Fig. 3, the CRs of the five mitogenomes were enriched in repetitive sequences. The C. rusci CR contained five short tandem repeats and a 2658-bp long tandem repeat (repeat unit 1 = 129 bp). Both P. solenopsis CR and P. madeirensis NCR1 presented similar distribution patterns of repeat sequences, with short tandem repeats throughout the CR and relatively longer tandem repeats at the end of the CR (repeat unit 2 = 21 bp in P. solenopsis, repeat unit 3 = 10 bp in P. madeirensis), whereas the P. madeirensis NCR2 contained only an 18-bp mononucleotide repeat. A total of 11 and nine tandem repeats were found in I. aegyptiaca CR and I. purchasi CR respectively, among which six tandem repeat units (repeat units 4–9) were equal to or greater than 10 bp in size. The last repeat in I. purchasi CR (repeat unit 9 = 21 bp, 528 bp) exhibited a strong G + C bias (57.1%, Fig. 3) and correspondingly a relatively low A + T content. Apart from the tandem repeat sequences, the alignment of two Icerya CRs revealed a conserved sequence block (CSB1 in I. aegyptiaca, CSB2 in I. purchasi) adjacent to trnV and another duplicated block (CSB3) in I. purchasi. All three of these CSBs could form stable stem-loop secondary structures, although the insert fragments in CSB2 and CSB3 formed two additional stem-loop structures compared to CSB1. We also predicted the secondary structures of units 1–9 and found four stem-loop structures in unit 1, two stem-loop structures in unit 9 × 2, and one stem-loop structure in units 2 × 2, 4 × 2 and 5 (Fig. 3).

Compared with other regions of mitogenome, the CR often accumulates more nucleotide substitutions, deletions, insertions, and duplications. Therefore, we evaluated the intraspecific heteroplasmy of CR in four scale insects. Sanger sequencing of the P. solenopsis CR cloned from one individual revealed a 551-bp CR and a 494-bp CR variant with a 57-bp deletion spanning (AT)10–(AT)28. Variants of a similar length were also detected in the PCR amplification product of NCR1 from one P. madeirensis individual, but we were unable to sequence the cloned variant because it contained long stretches of short tandem repeats. PacBio HiFi reads provided more detailed information on the two Icerya CRs without amplification bias. Five and 68 HiFi reads covering the entire CR revealed that the length of the CR varied from 2847 to 3206 bp in I. aegyptiaca and from 1957 to 2089 bp in I. purchasi, respectively. The alignment of these CRs revealed that length variation was due mainly to copy number variation (CNV) of the tandem repeats. The length variation and CNV of the repeats were measured in a total of 64 (I. aegyptiaca) and 73 (I. purchasi) HiFi reads covering whole or partial CRs. As shown in Fig. 4, I. aegyptiaca generally exhibited greater length variation among the tandem repeats than did I. purchasi. The largest length variations were found in repeat unit 4 (685 bp, I. aegyptiaca) and repeat element (ATAAA)n (121 bp, I. purchasi). Except for unit 7 in I. purchasi, all the remaining tandem repeats had variants with different copy numbers, e.g., (A)13 adjacent to CBS3 in I. purchasi had alternative alleles (A)9, (A)11, (A)12, (A)14, and (A)15. In addition, tandem repeats (repeat unit ≧ 2 bp) with copy numbers greater than 10 had more variants in most cases, but the last repeat in I. purchasi (unit 9 × 25.5) had only two variants with one or two additional repeats. In addition, mononucleotide repeats presented limited variation, although they were generally present in copy numbers greater than 10.

Phylogenetics

All ML and BI analyses based on three matrices revealed the same topology with high bootstrap and posterior probability values for most nodes (Fig. 5). Six neococcoid families were grouped together as monophyletic lineage that clustered with the archaeococcoid Monophlebidae as the sister group, and another archaeococcoid family Matsucoccidae occupied the basal branch position. Among the neococcoids, Pseudococcidae was recognized as the sister group to the other neococcoids. The monophyly of Coccidae was not supported, as two Aclerdidae species were nested within the Coccidae clade.

Gene duplication and rearrangement

Coccomorphan mitogenomes show great diversity in terms of gene arrangement. The newly sequenced mitogenomes exhibited five different gene orders, among which the gene rearrangement of C. rusci was identical to that of other reported Ceroplastes species, and the remaining four gene orders contained novel and unique gene rearrangements in Coccomorpha (Fig. 1). Two gene blocks, COI-trnL2-COII-trnK-trnD-ATP8-ATP6-COIII-trnG-ND3 (block I) and ND1-trnL (block II), were conserved among all the sequenced coccomorphans. Within the rearranged region, rRNA gene rearrangements with the same pattern were found in two Icerya species, and PCG rearrangements were detected in C. rusci, P. solenopsis and two Icerya species. The rearrangement of tRNA genes occurred with the highest frequency in all five species. Omitting tRNA genes, the arrangement of PCGs and rRNA genes exhibited only five distinct patterns in all sequenced coccomorphans, among which four were rearranged patterns occurring in Albotachaedina sinensis, P. solenopsis, two Icerya species, and all Aclerdidae and Coccidae species. The results of ancestral character reconstruction revealed that the translocation of ND2 and extensive rearrangements between ND5 and ND2 originated from the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of Coccidae (including Aclerdidae spp.) and two Icerya species, respectively (Fig S11). Excluding duplicated or lost genes, CREx analyses revealed that C. rusci and P. madeirensis had undergone several transposition and tandem duplication random loss (TDRL) rearrangement events, whereas the other three species presented more complicated rearrangement scenarios, including inversion, transposition, and TDRL events.

Gene loss was detected in two Phenacoccus mitogenomes, but we could not confirm the detailed process underlying their rearrangement scenarios. However, both Icerya mitogenomes retained large-scale tandem repeat sequences, which provided useful information for understanding the corresponding gene duplications and rearrangements. In fact, the HiFi data revealed different haplotypes for the I. aegyptiaca and I. purchasi mitogenomes, and all of them displayed various gene numbers and gene orders below the species level (Figs. 6 and S1). Two haplotypes of the I. purchasi mitogenome shared the 729-bp repeat unit spanning the tRNA gene cluster between ND3 and trnY, and a shorter 625-bp repeat unit covering a portion of ND3 and the five downstream tRNA genes was found in five haplotypes of the I. aegyptiaca mitogenome. For I. purchasi, 729-bp tandem repeats were present 5 times and 4 times in 67.5% and 30.2% of the HiFi reads spanning the repeat region respectively, and the remaining three HiFi reads had 6 or 7 repeats. A greater degree of heteroplasmy was observed among the 221 HiFi reads spanning the repeat region of I. aegyptiaca, among which the 625-bp tandem unit was repeated 9 times in 85 reads and 6–8, 10–15, or 19 times in the remainder of the reads. In all of the haplotypes of two Icerya mitogenomes, the intact tRNA gene cluster of the first repeat was fully functional, but some of the tRNA genes of other repeats underwent pseudogenization events with nucleotide mutations and/or deletions in tRNA stems or anticodon loops. For example, the nucleotide mutations in the trnA pseudogenes produced an extra mismatch in the acceptor stem and reassigned the anticodon (Fig. 6). In addition, a rare haplotype of I. aegyptiaca assembled from four HiFi reads contained a more complex and broader repeat region spanning from ND3 to ND1 with a total of ten tRNA genes and three PCGs. All of these examples of gene duplication and pseudogenization provide solid evidence for the TDRL-based rearrangement history of the genes in this region.

Discussion

Animal mitochondria possess relatively small and simple genomes with some lineages displaying high variability in genomic characteristics such as chromosome number, gene content, and gene arrangement, which can be leveraged to provide unique insights into genome and taxon evolution [2, 57]. The generation of a complete mitogenome can improve the accuracy of comparative analyses, especially for noncoding regions, genome size, and gene rearrangements. Our assembly results revealed that no whole mitogenome could be recovered using an NGS dataset alone, without Sanger or HiFi reads, and the two NGS assemblers used showed distinct repeat sequence assembly performance. Notably, the 14,914-bp mitochondrial contig of I. aegyptiaca (assembled by SOAPdenovo-Trans) contained the typical set of 37 mitochondrial genes but did not include any repeat sequences between ND3 and ND1, confirming that the accuracy and completeness of short-read assembly are quite limited for repeat sequences. Similarly, missing sequences including repeats or duplicated genes have also been reported for at least 15% vertebrate mitogenomes [10] and many invertebrate mitogenomes [58] assembled from short reads. Considering the differences in repeat sequence assembly performance among assemblers, potential repeat regions in putative complete mitogenomes generated on the basis of short reads should be verified with different assemblers and/or other sequencing data. Although Sanger sequencing has long been regarded as the gold standard for DNA sequencing, PCR amplification failure (e.g., in the large-scale gene duplication region and CR of Icerya species), a lack of specific primer sites, long stretches of mononucleotide or dinucleotide repeats, and other unfavourable features common in repeat regions may increase the difficulty of verification via PCR-based Sanger sequencing. In this study, complete Icerya mitogenomes were assembled from HiFi data, showing that long-read sequencing effectively overcomes these experimental challenges, particularly those associated with repeat sequences, and improve the accuracy and completeness of mitogenome assembly. In addition, previous studies revealed that mitogenome sequences generated from Sanger and NGS data respectively, from a single insect individual showed significant systematic differences [59], e.g., 148 different base sites scattered throughout 10 PCGs, two rRNA genes, two tRNA genes, and the CR in the Gigantometra gigas mitogenomes. The mitogenome sequences assembled from the HiFi and NGS data shared 99.98% (I. aegyptiaca) and 99.99% (I. purchasi) identity, excluding the CR and repeat regions, with only one singleton transition and two singleton indels in the I. aegyptiaca mitogenome and one singleton transversion and one singleton indel in the I. purchasi mitogenome. All these indels occurred within homopolymer tracts. Such high sequence identity reduces obstacles to using the mixed data of coding sequences generated by PacBio HiFi and NGS methods.

Metazoan mitogenomes are generally characterized by extremely compact organization and conserved gene content, resulting in a relatively stable genome size across most lineages [2, 8, 60]. The newly sequenced mitogenomes broaden the scope of known coccomorphan genome size, with P. madeirensis and I. aegyptiaca having the smallest (14,286 bp) and largest (21,925 bp) mitogenomes, respectively. The mitogenome sizes of the two Phenacoccus species reported here are smaller than those of all sequenced species in other families thus far. The length of the coding region accounting for the vast proportion of the mitogenome varies among coccomorphan mitogenomes, but the smaller noncoding region, especially the CR, in Phenacoccus relative to other species is the main reason for the reduced size of Phenacoccus mitogenomes (Fig. S2). As an important source of genome size variation, the CR length of the I. aegyptiaca mitogenome is actually greater than that of most reported coccomorphans, except for C. rusci, while the 5572-bp repeat sequence involving several coding genes is another key factor contributing to its larger mitogenome size. By assessing the length variations in the CR and long repeat sequences among different HiFi reads and mitogenome haplotypes of both Icerya species, we found that long repeat sequences presented a greater range of variation in size than did CRs and were the major contributors to intraspecific genome size variation. Mitogenome inflation has also been reported in many metazoan groups, such as Champsocephalus esox (22,372 bp), Dynastes neptunus (28,021 bp), Schistosoma haematobium (33,418 bp), and Polypodium hydriforme (∼93,000 bp), and most of these cases involve the expansion of long repeat sequences, suggesting that repeat sequence expansion is one of the primary drivers of mitogenome gigantism [12, 61–63].

The nucleotide compositions of the five newly reported mitogenomes do not differ from those reported for other scale insects [17–19], all of which exhibit conspicuously high A + T contents (81.1–91.1%), evident C-skew (−0.573–−0.267, majority strand) and variable degrees of A-skew (0.014–0.244, majority strand), except for Apiomorpha munita, which has a weak T-skew. The average A + T content of coccomorphan mitogenomes is the highest within Sternorrhyncha [64], reaching 86.7%. Base composition bias can also affect PCG codon usage [65]. This strong A + T-biased base composition resulted in a trend towards a low frequency of GC-rich codon usage, which is common in coccomorphan mitogenomes [17]. In addition to the effect of nucleotide composition, mitochondrial PCGs are generally driven by purifying selection with strict functional constraints [66]. According to our selection pressure calculations, most PCGs of coccomorphan mitogenomes underwent strong purifying selection, whereas ATP8 was under relaxed purifying selection, with the highest average Ka/Ks value (0.75). This phenomenon drove the high divergence of ATP8 across coccomorphans, including pronounced variation in gene length (99–159 bp) and in relative position to adjacent genes. Conventional-size ATP8 was presented in only two Icerya species, and most coccomorphans contained a slightly short ATP8 (46–47 amino acids), whereas Pseudococcidae and Matsucoccidae species presented the shortest ATP8 sequences, particularly P. solenopsis, P. madeirensis, and M. matsumurae, in which the ATP8 gene was severely truncated. Amino acid alignment and hydrophobicity profiles revealed that these truncated ATP8 sequences contained mainly functional domains. Although the ‘MPQL’ motif at the N-terminal domain, a common feature of metazoan ATP8 proteins [67, 68], was not found in the coccomorphan ATP8, the first four amino acids of P. solenopsis and P. madeirensis (IIQM) were identical to those of their functional counterparts in other species, e.g., Nipponaclerda biwakoensis. In addition, the predicted transmembrane domain and C-terminal domain with positively charged amino acids involved in the assembly and function of F0 component of ATP synthase [69, 70] were observed in these truncated ATP8 sequences, implying that these genes should be functional. Indeed, truncated ATP8 has been reported in several metazoan lineages, such as tunicates [68], bivalves [71] and planthoppers [72], and also actively transcribed in turbellarians [73]. ATP8 often overlaps with ATP6 on the same strand in metazoan mitogenomes [2]. In coccomorphans, most species retain the typical 7-bp overlap between ATP8 and ATP6, while ATP8 and ATP6 were separated by an intergenic space of 1–35 bp in six species belonging to four families (Fig S5). Phylogeny, these diverse patterns of ATP8 gene length, and the relative positions of ATP8 and ATP6 across distinct groups indicate that ATP8 truncation and decoupling from ATP6 occurred rapidly and independently multiple times in the evolutionary history of Coccomorpha. Particularly, severe truncations of ATP8 genes in Pseudococcidae and Matsucoccidae represent clear convergent evolution, while these truncations and decoupling from ATP6 do not appear to be highly associated.

Reductions exist not only in the PCGs but also, even more frequently, in the tRNA genes of coccomorphan mitogenomes. These truncated tRNAs cannot be folded into the typical cloverleaf secondary structure and at least lack one of the DHU and TψC arms. The most extremely simplified tRNAs are 40 bp (trnS2 of Ceroplastes floridensis) and 42 bp (trnS2 of I. aegyptiaca) in size, which are among the shortest tRNAs, and possess only acceptor and anticodon arms [74, 75]. Large-scale mitochondrial tRNA truncation has also been documented in other metazoan lineages such as nematodes [76], spiders [75], mites [77], and gall midges [78]. The long-term conservation of tRNA arm loss, e.g., in coccomorphan trnN, and diverse combinations of arm-less tRNAs span the evolutionary history of these lineages, implying that these arm-less tRNAs maintain normal functionality and are under selective constraints.

The conserved tRNA cloverleaf secondary structure and its corresponding L-shaped tertiary structure are commonly considered prerequisites for efficient interactions with ribosomes and other proteins [79]. Actually, the L-shaped-like tertiary structure has been proposed to be retained by DHU or TψC arm-less tRNAs, allowing them to perform their normal functions as canonical tRNAs [80, 81]. Several tRNAs lacking both DHU and TψC arms, e.g., mitochondrial trnR and trnI of Romanomermis culicivorax, have been found to adopt a stable hairpin secondary structure and boomerang-like tertiary structure instead of the classic tRNA structures [81], and the 3’-CCA addition, an essential region for aminoacylation and charged tRNA positioning in the ribosome, was found in these transcripts, proving their functionality [74]. The β-turn element of R. culicivorax tRNA nucleotidyltransferase enhances its substrate affinity to offset the loss of the conventional tRNA binding region (the TψC arm) and ensures the efficient addition of CCA to arm-less tRNAs [82]. Furthermore, the translation elongation factor Tu (EF-Tu), which normally binds with the acceptor arm and TψC arm, has evolved another specialized variant (EF-Tu1) to interact with aminoacylated TψC arm-less tRNAs in nematodes [83]. Both types of EF-Tu also exist in Drosophila melanogaster mitochondria without TψC arm-less tRNAs, and its EF-Tu1 also has the ability to bind aminoacylated TψC arm-less tRNAs [84]. For trnS1, a common DHU arm-less tRNA in nearly all metazoan mitochondria [85], the corresponding aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase mSerRS evolved a bimodal readout mechanism to recognize the distinct structures of trnS1 and trnS2 in human mitochondria rather than the canonical identity element, namely the long variable loop [86, 87]. These findings of coevolution and preadaptation between tRNAs and interacting proteins involving tRNA maturation and protein synthesis provide another line of evidence for the potential full functionality of arm-less tRNAs across metazoan lineages. Moreover, it has been well demonstrated that nucleus-encoded tRNAs can be imported into mitochondria to replenish missing tRNAs in mitochondria [88], implying that they may compensate for or replace the function of arm-less tRNAs. The functional confirmation of large-scale truncated tRNAs and their coevolution of interacting factors in Coccomorpha should be further investigated in future studies.

The systematic investigation of how tRNA secondary structures mapped onto the coccomorphan phylogeny revealed diverse arm-loss patterns across lineages. For example, trnN and trnL1 presented highly conserved DHU arm-less structures in all coccomorphans and neococcoids respectively, implying that DHU-arm losses in trnN and trnL1 may be synapomorphies for the respective taxa, whereas trnK without the DHU arm occurred in only the Kerriidae and Pseudococcidea, suggesting that multiple independent DHU-arm losses of tnrK occurred within the Coccomorpha. Additionally, the overall arm-loss patterns of the 15 evaluated tRNAs were similar within families, indicting that combined arm-loss patterns of multiple tRNAs could be regarded as specific characteristics at the family or other ​taxonomic category levels. Interestingly, Aclerdidae and Coccidae presented identical combined arm-loss patterns, implying a close relationship between these two families, which supports the phylogenetic analysis results. Taken together, these findings indicate that tRNA arm loss in coccomorphan mitogenomes represents another valuable genomic feature (in addition to gene rearrangement) that can be used to infer phylogenetic relationships, as also demonstrated in arachnids [89].

Extensive gene rearrangements have been detected previously in Coccomorpha, such as rearrangements involving ND2 in Aclerdidae and Coccidae, ND6-CYTB in Albotachaedina sinensis, and tRNA genes across all sequenced families [18]. Compared with tRNA gene rearrangements, both PCG and rRNA gene rearrangements exhibit significantly lower frequencies in Insecta, with the exception of a few lineages, such as Hymenoptera, Thysanoptera, and Psocodea [3]. In addition to the reported ND2 rearrangement in C. rusci, three novel gene orders in P. solenopsis, I. aegyptiaca and I. purchasi contain rare PCG and/or rRNA gene rearrangements, which narrows the previously proposed boundaries of conserved gene blocks [18]. Within the rearranged regions, ND2 and its flanking tRNA gene clusters are a rearrangement hotspot, exhibiting divergence from the ancestral insect gene order in all coccomorphans. Another two tRNA gene clusters, trnA-trnR-trnN-trnS-trnE-trnF and trnT-trnP, also display relatively high rearrangement rates, which has also been frequently reported in other hemipteran lineages [90, 91]. Mitogenome gene arrangement analysis has been recognized as a powerful tool for resolving phylogeny [92]. However, frequently rearranged tRNA genes may form convergent gene rearrangements in different lineages [93], increasing the complexity of mitogenome rearrangement and potential bias in phylogenetic analyses. After filtering out tRNA genes, the gene arrangements of the remaining PCGs and rRNA genes in Coccomorpha became significantly simpler and clearer, with only five different patterns. Matsucoccidae, Pseudococcidae, Eriococcidae, and Cerococcidae retained the ancestral insect gene arrangement pattern of PCGs and rRNA genes, with the exception of P. solenopsis, which has an autapomorphic ND6-CYTB inversion. Actually, the global gene arrangement of M. matsumurae—occupying the phylogenetically basal lineage in recovered trees—may represent the ancestral gene arrangement of Coccomorpha, which is nearly identical to the insect ground pattern of gene arrangement with only the trnY-trnC rearrangement. All the other families presented lineage-specific gene arrangement patterns. Notably, the identical rearrangement pattern, which originated from the MRCA of Coccidae (including Aclerdidae spp.) and is shared by all Aclerdidae and Coccidae species, as also revealed by their tRNA secondary structure features, strongly supports their close phylogenetic relationships.

In insect mitogenomes, most gene duplications and rearrangements arise through the TDRL mechanism [94], whereas gene inversions are attributed mainly to recombination mechanisms [95]. Furthermore, the few gene rearrangements that cannot be easily and effortlessly explained by one of the abovementioned mechanisms may be derived from other rare mechanisms, e.g., tandem duplication nonrandom loss (TDNL) [96] and tRNA remoulding [97]. Among them, TDRL remains the dominant mechanism for explaining gene rearrangements in coccomorphan mitogenomes, such as the local rearrangements trnW-trnY-trnC in M. matsumurae, trnT-ND6-trnP in Eriococcidae and Cerococcidae, and trnP-trnT-ND6 in Kerriidae. Intriguingly, the two Icerya mitogenomes present a distinct architectural signature that is absent in other sequenced scale insects, namely polarized clustering of codirectional genes bisecting the mitogenome irrespective of noncoding regions. Although multiple TDRL and/or recombination events, as predicted in the CREx results, could produce analogous arrangements, preferential retention of specific duplicated copies (i.e., nonrandom loss) might represent a more parsimonious evolutionary scenario. This hypothesis requires verification through expanded mitogenomic sampling within Monophlebidae.

Mitochondrial heteroplasmy, mainly as single nucleotide variants or indels has been widely documented in diverse metazoan lineages [59, 98], whereas gene order changes remain uncommon, with confirmed cases restricted to a few species, such as parasitic lice [99], squamates [100], and frogs [101]. HiFi read-based mitogenome assembly paves the way for elucidating organizational plasticity below the species level. The most pronounced differences among intraspecific haplotypes for the two Icerya mitogenomes were gene copy number and gene order divergence caused by long tandem repeats. Three haplotypes in I. aegyptiaca differ in terms of tRNA gene copy number, and pseudogenized tRNA genes are consistent across duplicated repeats, mirroring the two haplotypes in I. purchasi, which account for the majority of HiFi reads. Gene order divergence between I. aegyptiaca and I. purchasi is confined to the ND3-ND5 tRNA gene cluster, with divergent tRNA arrangements in repeat units between both species and conserved sequences across repeats for each species, strongly suggesting that these long tandem repeats postdate species-specific rearrangements. Another haplotype exhibits both tRNA gene copy number variations and divergent pseudogenized tRNA genes within duplicated repeats, resulting in a different gene order. The rarest haplotype harbours strand-switching gene repeats (majority-strand repeat unit: six genes; minority-strand repeat unit: seven genes) alongside multigene pseudogenization, yielding large-scale ND3-ND1 repeats and the most complex gene order, which diverges from the conserved nonalternating majority/minority-strand gene clustering prevalent in HiFi reads. High gene order heteroplasmy in the two Icerya mitogenomes, as indicated by the variety of haplotypes, reflects the prevalence of the TDRL mechanism and rapidly recent and ongoing evolution. These haplotypes and their proportions within HiFi reads also reveal greater complexity of organizational variation in I. aegyptiaca than in I. purchasi, suggesting potential species-specific differences in the degree of gene order heteroplasmy.

The mitogenome CR commonly harbours tandem repeat sequences and is characterized by high heteroplasmy [102]. Our data also portray a complex picture of tandem repeats and CNV in coccomorphan CRs. The type, abundance and distribution of tandem repeats within the CR exhibit taxon-specific patterns. Tandem repeats with short repeat units, such as mononucleotide and dinucleotide repeats, are universal across all sequenced coccomorphan CRs. In contrast, tandem repeats with long repeat units appear in the CRs of only a few species. HiFi reads have elucidated varying degrees of heteroplasmy in terms of both copy number and length among these tandem repeats. Due to replication slippage, shorter tandem repeat units tend to exhibit higher levels of CNV [103, 104]. This pattern is pronounced in the CRs of Icerya spp., particularly in dinucleotide repeats (AT/TA)n, which exhibit higher prevalence and greater variability. However, stretches of mononucleotide repeats exhibit limited heteroplasmy, which is consistent with observations in other metazoans [105, 106], with significantly reduced copy number variability compared with most other short-unit repeats. Actually, some repeat sequences, including these poly-A/T stretches [107, 108], function as regulatory elements of transcription and replication, and may be under stronger functional constraints, restricting extreme CNV [109]. Conversely, biologically silent tandem repeats may have accumulated more CNVs during the rapid evolution of the CR. In addition, the accumulation of CNVs in repeat sequence also influenced by the nuclear genome [110].

The length heteroplasmy of tandem repeats, resulting from the combined effects of repeat unit length and copy number variability, differs markedly between the two species. Overall, the I. aegyptiaca CR demonstrates significantly greater heteroplasmy than that of I. purchasi in terms of both the number and length of tandem repeats. As the HiFi data were generated from multiple individuals of both Icerya spp., we cannot definitively determine whether the heteroplasmy in tandem repeats or gene order originates from inter- or intraindividual variations. However, PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing confirmed the intraindividual heteroplasmy of tandem repeats in P. madeirensis and P. solenopsis CRs respectively, suggesting that this phenomenon may be prevalent in coccomorphan mitogenomes. Our results represent the first glimpse into repeat sequences of coccomorphan mitogenomes; a more systematic investigation of repeat sequences and hypervariable CRs at the intraindividual, interindividual, and interpopulation levels should be integrated in future studies to elucidate their evolutionary mechanisms and dynamics and explore their potential applications in population genetics and phylogeography.

Conclusion

In this study, the complete mitogenomes of five invasive scale insects were characterized and compared with those of 16 other coccomorphans. Comparative analyses corroborate the ubiquity of extremely A + T-biased base composition, large-scale tRNA gene truncation, and frequent gene rearrangements within coccomorphan mitogenomes. The phylogenetic analyses revealed that the diverse tRNA arm-loss patterns and gene rearrangement patterns could serve as clade-specific molecular synapomorphies. In addition, we found that the relaxed purifying selection on ATP8 facilitates its accelerated sequence evolution, resulting in significant divergence in both gene length and its position relative to ATP6. PacBio HiFi sequencing of two Icerya species revealed strong heteroplasmy of repeat sequence CNVs, driving rapid intraspecific evolution marked by dynamic changes in genome size, gene content, and gene rearrangements. Our study provides novel insights into the plasticity of coccomorphan mitogenomes and enhances the understanding of their evolutionary dynamics.
