Tailoring hybrid flipped learning course design: exploring motivation, personality, and experiences of students and faculty
Authors: Ali M. Alshareef, Mona Alhasani, Nabeel Qamar, Aiman Alhazmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHybrid flipped learning is increasingly used in health professions education; however, students differ in how they experience and engage with this model. Individual characteristics, such as personality traits, may influence motivation and learning experiences, yet limited evidence exists on how hybrid flipped learning courses can be designed to accommodate diverse learners. This study investigates the relationship between personality traits and students’ intrinsic motivation in a hybrid flipped medical terminology course and explores student and instructor perspectives to inform adaptive course design.MethodsAn exploratory convergent mixed-methods study was conducted in an undergraduate medical terminology course delivered using a hybrid flipped learning model. At the end of the term, students completed an online survey measuring intrinsic motivation (interest and enjoyment, perceived competence, effort and importance, and value and usefulness) and the Big Five personality traits. One-sample t-tests compared motivation scores against the scale midpoint. Structural equation modeling was used to examine associations between personality traits and motivation outcomes. Feedback collected from students and instructors was analyzed thematically to provide contextual insights.ResultsA total of 149 student responses were analyzed. Mean scores for all positive motivation dimensions were significantly above the scale midpoint, suggesting positive perceptions of the hybrid flipped learning design. Several personality traits were significantly associated with motivation outcomes. Conscientiousness was positively associated with interest and enjoyment, effort and importance, and perceived competence, while openness was negatively associated with perceived tension. Extraversion was negatively associated with interest and enjoyment, while agreeableness was positively associated with value and usefulness, effort and importance, and perceived competence. Neuroticism was positively associated with perceived tension. Qualitative findings supported these patterns: students with higher extraversion expressed a preference for greater in-person interaction, whereas conscientious and open students valued structure, autonomy, and self-paced learning. Instructors (n = 10) highlighted the importance of flexible instructional strategies to support diverse learner needs.ConclusionsPersonality traits are associated with students’ motivational experiences in a hybrid flipped learning design. Findings from this single-site male undergraduate cohort suggest that hybrid flipped learning designs that account for learner differences may better support engagement and learning experiences within similar educational contexts. These findings offer preliminary insights for developing more adaptive and inclusive instructional designs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09570-4.

Introduction

The rapid growth in student enrollment, combined with increasingly demanding academic schedules for both students and faculty, has prompted higher education institutions to re-evaluate traditional teaching models. To address challenges related to flexibility, accessibility, and engagement, many universities are adopting technology-enhanced instructional approaches [1, 2]. This transition was accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic, which forced an abrupt shift to online learning and demonstrated the potential of technology-mediated education to improve access and learning continuity [3, 4]. As a result, hybrid learning models that integrate face-to-face (F2F) and online instruction have become increasingly common [5].

Hybrid learning, which integrates traditional in-person teaching with online components, offers a promising solution to the challenges posed by increasing enrollment and scheduling constraints. By leveraging the flexibility of online learning and the engagement of F2F interactions, hybrid models can accommodate diverse student needs while optimizing faculty time and resources. Furthermore, the incorporation of flipped learning elements, where students engage with pre-recorded lectures or materials before attending in-person sessions, has been shown to enhance student engagement, promote self-directed learning, and improve academic outcomes [6, 7]. Studies have demonstrated that flipped and hybrid learning models not only increase student satisfaction but also foster deeper understanding and retention of course material [8, 9]. Despite these documented benefits, evidence suggests that students do not experience hybrid flipped learning environments uniformly. Individual differences, particularly personality traits, may be linked to differences in motivation, engagement, and learning experiences in different educational settings [10, 11].

The Big Five personality traits (extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, neuroticism, and openness) have been widely examined in educational research. Conscientiousness and openness have consistently been linked to higher intrinsic motivation and academic success, while neuroticism has been associated with anxiety and reduced engagement [12, 13]. Extraversion has been shown to support learning in collaborative and F2F environments but may hinder adaptation to asynchronous or self-paced formats [14]. Similarly, students high in openness tend to adapt more easily to novel learning methods, whereas neurotic students may struggle in less structured environments [15, 16].

Recent research has extended this work by examining the mechanisms through which personality traits influence learning. Kara et al. found that self-regulated learning mediates the relationship between personality and student engagement in online contexts, with conscientiousness and openness emerging as key predictors [17]. However, most prior studies focus on either fully online or traditional F2F settings and rarely consider hybrid flipped learning environments. Intrinsic motivation is another critical factor in student engagement and persistence. The Intrinsic Motivation Inventory (IMI) has been widely used to assess interest, perceived competence, effort, and value in educational contexts [18]. Studies using the IMI have demonstrated that perceived competence and task value are strong predictors of engagement in blended and online learning [19]. Yet the interaction between personality traits and intrinsic motivation in hybrid flipped courses remains underexplored.

To address these gaps, this study investigates the relationships between personality traits, intrinsic motivation, and learning experiences in a hybrid flipped undergraduate medical terminology course. By integrating the Big Five personality framework with the IMI, and incorporating perspectives from both students and instructors, our research provides insight into how individual differences shape engagement in hybrid flipped learning environments. Unlike prior work that examines personality or motivation in isolation, this study explores their combined influence within a single instructional context.

The study aims to answer the following research questions:RQ1: Do students have a positive experience with the hybrid flipped learning course design?RQ2: How are different student personality types associated with intrinsic motivation and learning experiences in a hybrid flipped learning environment?RQ3: What are the benefits and challenges experienced by students and faculty in the hybrid flipped learning course design?

The findings of this study aim to inform the design of more inclusive and adaptive hybrid flipped learning courses and offer practical guidance for educators and institutions seeking to better support diverse learners. To address these research questions, quantitative and qualitative analyses were conducted to examine students’ experiences, personality-related motivational associations, and perceptions of the hybrid flipped learning design.

Methodology

This study employed an exploratory convergent mixed-methods, cross-sectional design to examine associations between personality traits, intrinsic motivation, and learning experiences within a hybrid flipped undergraduate medical terminology course. Quantitative and qualitative data were collected during the same study period, analyzed separately, and integrated during the interpretation of the findings. Quantitative data were collected using validated self-report instruments, while qualitative data were gathered through open-ended survey questions from both students and instructors to provide contextual explanations and deeper insight into the quantitative findings. To address RQ1, one-sample t-tests were conducted to evaluate students’ motivational experiences with the hybrid flipped learning design. To address RQ2, PLS-SEM was used to examine associations between personality traits and intrinsic motivation factors. To address RQ3, thematic analysis was conducted on open-ended responses from students and instructors to explore perceived benefits, challenges, and recommendations related to the course design.

The study was conducted in a second-year medical terminology course (TERM 201) at King Saud bin Abdulaziz University for Health Sciences. The course was redesigned and implemented as a hybrid flipped learning model on the male campus, as a preliminary implementation to evaluate its effectiveness prior to potential wider implementation.

Course design and intervention

TERM 201 is a two-credit-hour foundational course required for several health professional programs. The course aims to develop students’ ability to analyze, define, and apply medical terminology by recognizing prefixes, suffixes, root words, and anatomical and clinical language.

Previously delivered through traditional face-to-face lectures [20], the course was redesigned using a hybrid flipped learning approach. In this model, students engage with pre-recorded lectures and digital learning materials before attending in-person sessions. Classroom time is then dedicated to interactive and application-based activities, including discussion, clarification, and problem-solving, allowing students to actively apply the content they studied in advance.

The course is supported through the Blackboard Learning Management System, which provides continuous access to lecture recordings, PowerPoint slides, course guides, and assessment materials. All resources are available online 24/7 to promote flexible and self-regulated learning. Students are expected to review the assigned recordings prior to class, while face-to-face sessions focus on deeper engagement with the material. Optional bi-weekly interactive sessions further support student–instructor communication and reinforce active learning.

Typically, students reviewed one to two pre-recorded lectures per week through Blackboard prior to attending the bi-weekly face-to-face sessions. The recorded lectures focused on foundational medical terminology concepts and were accompanied by PowerPoint slides and supplementary learning materials. The bi-weekly face-to-face sessions emphasized interactive and application-based learning activities, including instructor-led discussions, question-and-answer sessions for concept clarification, terminology exercises, and collaborative learning activities designed to reinforce students’ understanding and application of medical terminology. Participants and data collection

Ethical approval (NRR24/055/11) was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (KAIMRC). Data were collected using online surveys administered at the end of the academic term. During the study term, a total of 302 students were enrolled in TERM 201 at the male campus, and all enrolled students were invited to participate through the course learning management system. A QR code was provided, which directed interested participants to the online survey. A total of 183 students responded to the survey, yielding a response rate of 60.6%. After excluding incomplete responses, 149 valid student responses were included in the final analysis. Incomplete survey responses were manually removed during the data cleaning process prior to conducting the statistical analyses.

Similarly, all ten faculty members teaching the course were invited via email to complete a teaching experience survey at the end of the term. All 10 faculty members responded, yielding a response rate of 100%. Participation was voluntary and anonymous for both surveys.

Measurements

The measurement instruments used in this study are described in the following subsections. The full student and faculty survey instruments developed for this study, including all closed-ended and open-ended items, are provided in Supplementary File 1.

Intrinsic Motivation Inventory (IMI) scale

The IMI is a tool used to assess various dimensions of intrinsic motivation and related self-regulation in a specific activity [18]. This multidimensional scale, developed within Self-Determination Theory (SDT), is commonly used in educational and experimental contexts to evaluate learning motivation, interest, and engagement. The IMI scale examines factors contributing to intrinsic motivation, including personal interest, perceived competence, effort, and perceived value. The primary factors the scale captures are shown in Table 2. The scale consists of 24 items on a 5-point Likert scale, from 1 = Strongly Disagree To 5 = Strongly Agree. Subscale scores for the IMI were computed as mean scores for each factor. Reverse-coded items (IE5, EI4, PT1, PT3, and PC5) were reverse scored in SPSS prior to calculating the subscale means. Incomplete survey responses were removed during the data cleaning process prior to analysis, and no missing items were permitted within subscales included in the analysis.

The big five scale

The Big Five Factor Model (FFM) is the most widely accepted model for classifying personality and organizing individuals into five distinct personality dimensions [21], see Table 3. Its popularity stems from its ability to provide a standardized taxonomy that predicts variations in people’s thoughts, feelings, behaviours, and actions. The Big Five scale consists of 10 items on a 5-point Likert scale, from 1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree. Each Big Five personality trait was computed as the mean of two items, including one reverse-coded item, following the standard scoring procedure for the BFI-10 [21]. Reverse-coded items (EXT2, AGR2, CON2, NEU2, and OPE2) were reverse scored in SPSS prior to analysis.

Statistical analysis

This section outlines the methods used to analyze the data, employing various established analytical tools and procedures.

Descriptive statistics, including frequency, mean and standard deviation, were calculated to summarize the data.The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) was utilized to reverse score the relevant items, calculate the mean score for each IMI scale, and perform one-sample t-tests to compare the results with the neutral midpoint of 3 on a 5-point Likert scale. The one-sample t-tests were conducted to determine whether students’ motivational experiences differed significantly from a neutral response, thereby providing an overall indication of students’ perceptions of the hybrid flipped learning design. Prior to conducting the analyses, the distributions of the IMI mean scores were examined using histograms to assess approximate normality and were considered acceptable for parametric testing. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied, as our analyses were exploratory in nature.Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) was conducted using SmartPLS version 4.1.1.6 to explore the relationships between personality traits and IMI factors (see Fig. 1). The standard PLS algorithm was used for model estimation, and bootstrapping with 5000 resamples and bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) confidence intervals was performed to assess the significance of the path coefficients. PLS-SEM was selected because it is well suited for exploratory research involving relatively small sample sizes and complex relationships between multiple latent constructs [22]. Compared with traditional multiple regression or covariance-based SEM approaches, PLS-SEM is more appropriate for predictive and exploratory analyses and does not require strict assumptions regarding data normality [22].

Measurement validation

To assess the suitability of our data for factor analysis, we conducted the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) Measure of Sampling Adequacy and the Bartlett Test of Sphericity. Our results indicated a KMO value of 0.83, which is well above the recommended threshold of 0.6, confirming sampling adequacy. Additionally, the Bartlett Test of Sphericity was statistically significant (χ²(561) = 2997.55, p < .0001), further supporting the suitability of our data for factor analysis [22].

After confirming the appropriateness of the data, the measurement model was evaluated to assess construct reliability and validity prior to estimating the structural model. Following the criteria suggested by Chin [23] for PLS-SEM, the assessment included indicator reliability, convergent validity, discriminant validity, multicollinearity assessment, and model fit evaluation.

Indicator reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability, with all constructs exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.7 [23]. Convergent validity was assessed using Average Variance Extracted (AVE), and all constructs surpassed the recommended threshold of 0.5 [23], indicating adequate convergent validity. Table 4 presents the construct reliability and convergent validity results, including Cronbach’s alpha, composite reliability, and AVE values. Discriminant validity was examined using the Heterotrait-Monotrait (HTMT) ratio of correlations, with all HTMT values remaining below the recommended threshold of 0.9 [23], see Table 5.

Thematic analysis

The feedback provided by students and faculty through open-ended questions was analyzed using inductive thematic analysis. A total of 149 student responses and 10 faculty responses were included in the qualitative analysis, as all participants were required to complete the qualitative component of the survey. The coding process was conducted independently by two researchers using Microsoft Excel. The analysis followed the thematic analysis framework outlined by Kawulich and Holland [25]. First, each unit of data was assigned a code that captured its meaning. New codes were generated when existing codes did not adequately represent new data, ensuring continuous comparison between newly coded and previously coded responses. The codes were then organized into categories based on shared properties and dimensions. Finally, the themes identified by both researchers were compared, refined, and consolidated into overarching themes representing the dataset. Any discrepancies between the researchers were resolved through discussion and consensus. This process was used to enhance the consistency and trustworthiness of the qualitative analysis. The qualitative findings were reported as thematic categories and supported by anonymized participant quotes to illustrate key points while ensuring participant privacy.

Results

After excluding incomplete student survey responses, a total of 149 completed responses were included in the analysis. All respondents were male students, as the course was delivered at the male campus as part of a preliminary implementation to examine the effectiveness of the hybrid flipped model. Participants were aged 18–21 years. In addition, all ten faculty members teaching the course completed the teaching experience survey.

The following sub-sections present students’ overall experiences with the hybrid flipped learning design, followed by findings on the relationships between personality traits and students’ motivational experiences. The section concludes with the results of the thematic analysis of open-ended responses from both students and faculty.

Student experience in the course design

To measure students’ experiences with the new course design, we utilized the IMI, a scale that evaluates factors influencing intrinsic motivation. Specifically, the IMI assessed students’ enjoyment, perceived usefulness of the course design, effort invested in engaging with the course, perceived competence, and tension experienced during engagement. To address Research Question 1 (RQ1), we calculated the average scores for each IMI factor. As illustrated in Fig. 2, all scores exceeded the neutral midpoint of 3 on the 5-point scale, except for perceived tension (M = 2.55, SD = 0.93). A one-sample t-test confirmed that these scores significantly differed from the neutral midpoint, as shown in Table 8.

The analysis identified several significant associations between personality traits and aspects of intrinsic motivation. For Extraversion, a significant negative relationship was found with Interest/Enjoyment (β= -0.15, p ≤ .05). Agreeableness was positively associated with Value/Usefulness (β = 0.24, p < .01), Effort/Importance (β = 0.28, p < .01), and Perceived Competence (β = 0.17, p ≤ .05). Conscientiousness showed significant positive relationships with Interest/Enjoyment (β = 0.24, p < .01), Effort/Importance (β = 0.22, p ≤ .05), and Perceived Competence (β = 0.31, p < .01). In contrast, Neuroticism was positively associated with Perceived Tension (β = 0.31, p < .01), while Openness displayed a significant negative relationship with Perceived Tension (β= -0.20, p < .01). These findings highlighted the associations between personality traits and students’ intrinsic motivation, particularly in the context of transitioning from traditional face-to-face lectures to a hybrid flipped learning design.

Discussion

Students generally reported positive experiences with the hybrid flipped learning design, describing them as both enjoyable and beneficial. They also perceived themselves as highly competent, with scores exceeding the neutral midpoint across all measured factors. Additionally, most students experienced significantly lower tension while learning in the new course format, suggesting that the design contributed to a more comfortable and engaging learning experience. However, a deeper analysis using the structural equation model revealed nuanced relationships between students’ personality traits and their intrinsic motivation in the hybrid flipped learning environment. These findings align with and extend existing research on the interplay between personality, motivation, and educational design. In the following sections, we explore these relationships in greater detail, integrating qualitative insights to provide a context for the quantitative results.

Extraversion and interest/enjoyment

The negative association between Extraversion and Interest/Enjoyment (β = -0.15, p ≤ .05) suggests that extroverted students may find hybrid flipped learning less engaging than traditional face-to-face settings. Extroverts typically thrive in dynamic, social environments where they can engage in spontaneous discussions, group activities, and real-time interactions [14]. In contrast, the hybrid flipped format, often characterized by asynchronous learning and limited in-person engagement, may not adequately fulfill their need for social stimulation. Our finding aligns with research indicating that extroverts tend to report lower satisfaction in online courses due to reduced opportunities for peer interaction [13]. Previous research in flipped and blended learning environments has similarly emphasized the importance of collaborative and interactive learning opportunities in supporting student engagement and satisfaction [9, 10]. Student feedback reinforced this perspective, with one extroverted participant expressing, “I felt the hybrid way of teaching was a bit hard, as I truly needed a lot of face-to-face interactions for my questions.” Another shared, “The online classes were very boring and long; I watched the first few and didn’t find any benefits.” Such remarks underscore how the lack of dynamic, real-time engagement in hybrid learning can diminish motivation and enjoyment for extroverted students.

Agreeableness, conscientiousness, and motivation

Agreeableness was positively associated with Value/Usefulness (β = 0.24, p < .01), Effort/Importance (β = 0.28, p < .01), and Perceived Competence (β = 0.17, p ≤ .05). Given their cooperative and empathetic nature, Agreeable students may perceive hybrid flipped learning as valuable and engaging due to opportunities for both independent learning and collaborative interaction [12]. This finding is also consistent with flipped learning research suggesting that supportive and cooperative learning environments may positively influence students’ satisfaction and engagement in collaborative learning activities [10, 26]. Similarly, Conscientiousness exhibited strong positive relationships with Interest/Enjoyment (β = 0.24, p < .01), Effort/Importance (β = 0.22, p ≤ .05), and Perceived Competence (β = 0.31, p < .01). Conscientious students, known for their self-discipline and goal-oriented behaviour, are likely to thrive in hybrid learning environments due to their ability to manage time effectively and adhere to self-directed learning schedules [27]. Student reflections echo these findings, with conscientious learners highlighting the benefits of autonomy. One participant shared, “The hybrid format gave me flexibility to manage my schedule and review materials at my own pace. “Another noted, “I learned to learn by myself, and I expect that I gained the skill because I got a satisfactory grade.” These statements illustrate how conscientious students leveraged the hybrid flipped format’s flexibility to optimize their learning experiences.

Agreeable students also emphasized the importance of collaboration, suggesting interactive features to enhance peer engagement. One participant proposed, “Add a comment section where students can ask questions and discuss the information within the slide,” reflecting their preference for cooperative learning. These qualitative insights further support the quantitative results, underscoring how personality traits shape students’ motivation and engagement in hybrid flipped learning. To cater to these differences, educators could integrate structured collaborative activities for agreeable students while maintaining flexible, self-paced components to support conscientious learners.

Neuroticism, openness, and perceived tension

Neuroticism was positively associated with Perceived Tension (β = 0.31, p < .01), suggesting that students prone to anxiety or emotional instability may struggle with the uncertainties of hybrid flipped learning, such as technical issues, inconsistent schedules, or reduced instructor presence. This aligns with previous research indicating that individuals high in neuroticism experience greater stress in less structured environments [16]. This interpretation is also consistent with research in online and flipped learning environments demonstrating that anxiety, social stress, and reduced readiness for flipped learning may negatively influence students’ engagement and learning experiences [28]. Several students expressed frustration over difficulties in maintaining focus, the timing of lectures, and a lack of social interaction. For instance, one participant shared, “I find it difficult to learn because I can’t stay focused for more than five minutes.” Such challenges highlight the need for more structured support mechanisms, such as guided study plans or interactive engagement strategies, to mitigate the stress experienced by neurotic learners.

Conversely, Openness to Experience exhibited a negative relationship with tension (β = -0.20, p < .01), indicating that curious and adaptable students may experience lower tension when engaging with hybrid flipped learning environments. Their inclination to explore new tools and flexible learning methods aligns with research showing that openness correlates with resilience in technology-mediated education [15]. Some students embraced the hybrid model, emphasizing its benefits for fostering independence and self-directed learning. One participant noted, “It was a great choice to make it hybrid because I had to depend fully on myself.” Another open-minded student proposed an innovative solution: “Dividing the video into sections with a comment feature for discussions.” Such proactive approaches highlight the role of openness in facilitating engagement and reducing tension in hybrid learning environments.

Hybrid course design could better accommodate extroverted learners by incorporating more synchronous activities, such as live discussions, breakout rooms, or collaborative projects [29]. Research suggests that extroverts thrive in socially rich, interactive environments [14], making structured engagement essential. Some students emphasized the need for scheduled interactions beyond optional interactive sessions, highlighting the importance of maintaining a consistent study routine. To address this, hybrid courses could include at least one mandatory in-person or live virtual session each week, focusing on group problem-solving or debates, which have been shown to enhance engagement and learning outcomes [9].

Given the observed association between neuroticism and perceived tension, structured schedules, clear deadlines, and proactive instructor check-ins may help better support neurotic learners in hybrid courses. Students expressed frustration with inconsistent lecture timing and unclear expectations, highlighting the need for greater predictability. Research indicates that structured environments help mitigate anxiety in neurotic individuals [16]. Implementing automated deadline reminders and weekly progress reviews with instructors may provide additional support and may help improve students’ learning outcomes [19].

Hybrid courses may better support engagement for conscientious learners by offering optional advanced modules or self-paced challenges that align with their strong self-regulation skills. Students recognized improvements in their ability to study independently, reinforcing research that conscientious individuals thrive in self-directed learning environments [27]. Providing “bonus” assignments or curated resources beyond core content can further support their motivation and encourage deeper learning [30].

Instructor and institutional support

Based on qualitative feedback from instructors on their experience with the hybrid flipped medical terminology course, we identified key areas of support needed to enhance course effectiveness. To improve hybrid flipped course designs, institutions should provide comprehensive support for instructors and encourage cross-disciplinary collaboration. One critical area for improvement is pre-session engagement. Instructors noted that “most students didn’t read materials before class,” diminishing the effectiveness of in-person sessions. This issue can be addressed by requiring students to complete brief comprehension checks, such as three-question quizzes, before attending live sessions. Research indicates that pre-class preparation significantly enhances engagement and learning outcomes [6]. Additionally, training in hybrid pedagogy is crucial for helping instructors balance synchronous and asynchronous content while fostering a sense of online community. Instructors in the study highlighted challenges in “optimizing engagement” in hybrid flipped learning settings, underscoring the need for professional development. Workshops on hybrid teaching strategies can enhance instructors’ effectiveness and confidence in delivering engaging, student-centered courses [7].

Technical and structural adjustments

Based on student feedback, we recommend several technical and structural adjustments to enhance the effectiveness of hybrid medical terminology courses. One key improvement is video design. Lectures should be structured into shorter segments (10–15 min) with embedded quizzes or discussion prompts. Students criticized “long, boring videos” and expressed a preference for “shorter, more engaging content.” Research supports that segmented videos improve retention and engagement [32]. Platforms like Edpuzzle can be used to integrate interactive questions directly into video timelines, enhancing student interaction [33]. Scheduling optimization is another important consideration. Live or in-person sessions should be scheduled during peak focus hours, such as mornings, while avoiding late-day slots. Feedback highlighted “horrible timing” as a major barrier, with neurotic students particularly struggling with inconsistent schedules. Research indicates that well-timed classes improve attendance and student engagement [34]. Technical support also plays a crucial role in ensuring course quality. Institutions should provide dedicated IT assistance and e-learning specialists to help instructors improve recording quality and troubleshoot technical issues. Instructors reported difficulties in “finding the right recording settings,” while students noted problems with “audio issues” and “confusing slide designs.” Effective technical support is essential for delivering high-quality hybrid courses [8].

Limitation

While this study offers valuable insights into the interplay between personality traits, intrinsic motivation, and learning experiences in a hybrid flipped learning environment, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design captures data at a single point in time, limiting causal inferences and restricting the findings to correlational associations. Longitudinal studies could provide deeper insights into how these factors evolve throughout a course or academic program. Moreover, the study did not account for other potentially relevant factors, such as prior academic performance, previous experience with online or hybrid learning, or concurrent academic workload, which may have influenced students’ motivational experiences. Furthermore, the study relied on self-reported survey measures, which may be subject to response and social desirability biases. Second, the study was conducted in a Saudi Arabian context, where cultural and institutional factors may influence student and faculty experiences with hybrid learning. Additionally, the sample included only male students, as the hybrid flipped design was piloted at the male campus, which limits the generalizability of the findings across genders and educational contexts. Findings may not generalize to female students, other disciplines, or different institutional and cultural settings. Future research should include female students to examine potential gender differences in hybrid flipped learning experiences. Finally, while the findings highlight the need for faculty training and technical support, the study does not explore specific training programs or resources. Further research should investigate best practices for faculty development to enhance the implementation and effectiveness of hybrid flipped learning.

Conclusion

This study examined the relationship between students’ personality traits, intrinsic motivation, and experiences in a hybrid learning environment, providing insights into how course design can better accommodate diverse learner needs. The findings suggest that personality traits may be associated with differences in engagement and satisfaction. Extroverted students reported lower enjoyment due to limited social interaction, while conscientious learners thrived in self-directed settings. Neurotic students experienced heightened tension, whereas open-minded individuals adapted more easily to the flexibility of hybrid learning. These findings from a single-site undergraduate cohort suggest that hybrid flipped learning designs may benefit from considering personality differences and diverse engagement preferences. Qualitative feedback further underscored both the benefits and challenges of hybrid learning. Students valued flexibility and autonomy but struggled with maintaining focus in asynchronous settings and expressed a desire for more in-person interaction and structured feedback. Instructors, meanwhile, stressed the importance of enhanced technical support and cross-disciplinary collaboration to improve course delivery. These insights align with existing research suggesting that balancing flexibility with structured support may help sustain motivation and academic success. Within similar hybrid learning contexts, course designs may benefit from incorporating strategies that address diverse student needs. Strategies such as incorporating synchronous activities for extroverts, structured schedules for neurotic students, and advanced modules for conscientious learners may help support engagement and motivation. Additionally, improving technical infrastructure, refining video content, and fostering interdisciplinary collaboration can help overcome common barriers, ultimately supporting a more engaging and inclusive learning environment. Future longitudinal and intervention-based studies are needed to determine whether personality-informed hybrid flipped learning designs lead to measurable improvements in student engagement, motivation, and learning outcomes.
