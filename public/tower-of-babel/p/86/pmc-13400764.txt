Unique presentation of urosymphyseal fistula and conservative management
Authors: Cristian J. Salazar V., David A. Woodrum, Maximilian Staebler, Matvey Tsivian, Lance A. Mynderse, Brian J. Davis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A urosymphyseal fistula is an abnormal connection between the lower urinary tract (typically bladder or proximal urethra) and pubic symphysis. This is an uncommon late complication of prostate cancer treatment, often occurring after pelvic radiation and urethral instrumentation. We report a 79-year-old man who, after external radiotherapy, salvage brachytherapy, and transurethral bladder neck incision, developed a urosymphyseal fistula with urinary tracking into the anterior thigh compartment. Given comorbidities, he was managed conservatively with urinary diversion, percutaneous drainage, and antibiotic therapy. Serial imaging demonstrated nonoperative resolution. This case highlights an unusual clinical presentation and suggests selected patients may avoid extirpative surgery.

Introduction

Urosymphyseal urinary fistulas with associated pubic osteomyelitis are rare but highly morbid complications following treatment for prostate cancer. As described by Chrouser et al., fistula formation after pelvic radiation is frequently associated with prior surgical or endoscopic manipulation of the urethra or bladder outlet.1 Radiation-induced tissue ischemia and fibrosis impair healing, rendering previously irradiated tissues susceptible to breakdown following instrumentation. Pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the modality of choice for diagnosis, demonstrating fluid tracking through the pubic symphysis on heavily T2-weighted sequences, low T1/high T2 marrow signal consistent with osteomyelitis, and characteristic symmetric pectineus, obturator, and adductor edema on coronal T2 sequences (the 'Lorax sign').2 Clinical presentation is often nonspecific, contributing to delayed diagnosis. While definitive management commonly involves cystectomy and urinary diversion, management strategies in medically frail patients remain challenging.

Case presentation

A 79-year-old man with a medical history notable for hypertension, hyperlipidemia, stable thrombocytopenia, carotid artery stenosis, significant cardiovascular disease, prior sigmoidectomy, and transurethral resection of the prostate was diagnosed with prostate adenocarcinoma. Biopsy revealed Gleason score 4 + 3 = 7, clinical stage T1cN0M0 disease, with a prostate-specific antigen level of 4.5 ng/mL. Given cardiovascular risk factors, he was not considered a surgical candidate and underwent definitive external beam radiotherapy (75.6 Gy in 42 fractions) with a four-month course of androgen deprivation therapy.

Three years later, the patient developed biochemical recurrence. Prostate biopsy confirmed locally recurrent Gleason 3 + 3 disease without evidence of metastatic spread. He declined long-term androgen deprivation therapy, was not considered a candidate for salvage prostatectomy, and declined salvage cryotherapy. Salvage iodine-125 brachytherapy was performed (120 Gy prescribed, D90 = 151 Gy) with a four-month course of neoadjuvant androgen deprivation therapy.

Following brachytherapy, the patient developed progressive lower urinary tract symptoms. Cystoscopy demonstrated a bladder neck stenosis that prevented passage of the flexible cystoscope; retrograde urethrogram confirmed narrowing at this level (Fig. 1A). The bladder neck was incised at the 12-o'clock position with a Collins knife under electrocautery, followed by limited loop resection of the proximal third of the prostatic urethra and bladder neck (3- to 9-o'clock); no adjunctive agents were used (Fig. 1B). Four months after the procedure, he presented with perineal pain, worsening urinary symptoms, bilateral lower extremity swelling, and an enlarging mass in the left thigh. A multidisciplinary team involving urology, orthopedic surgery, and infectious diseases evaluated the patient. Given advanced age, medical comorbidity, and operative risk, he was deemed a poor candidate for cystectomy and urinary diversion. A conservative treatment strategy was pursued. The thigh collection was drained percutaneously; aspirate cultures yielded polymicrobial growth (enterococci, coagulase-negative staphylococci, Corynebacterium, and Lactobacillus), which informed therapy. Prolonged suppressive oral antibiotic therapy was initiated with amoxicillin 500 mg TID, later changed to cephalexin 500 mg TID because of concomitant allopurinol use. The planned duration was tentatively set at one year and later extended in consultation with Infectious Diseases and Orthopedic Surgery, given good tolerance and concern for recurrent pubic osteomyelitis.

Complete urinary diversion was achieved with a transurethral (Foley) catheter and bilateral 10F occlusion nephroureteral catheters, each carrying a 13-cm ureteral extension positioned to mechanically occlude the ureter and drained externally (Fig. 4). Non-occlusive drainage had been followed by persistent urinary leakage; conversion to occlusion nephroureteral catheters achieved complete urinary diversion, confirmed by cystogram showing no transfistular leak. The catheters were then exchanged for standard percutaneous nephrostomy tubes and intermittently capped to confirm antegrade bladder flow before removal.Fig. 4Urinary diversion.Prone fluoroscopic image showing bilateral occlusive nephroureteral drainage tubes with the distal ends occluded to prevent urine flow to the bladder. Serial imaging demonstrated progressive closure of the fistulous tract. After five months, the nephrostomy tubes were removed. Three months later, repeat MRI demonstrated resolution of the fistula, allowing removal of the Foley catheter (Fig. 5). Fistula resolution was confirmed with two contrast-enhanced CT urograms. Surveillance consisted of CT of the abdomen and pelvis every 6 months; MRI at 3 years demonstrated no abnormality. No recurrence of fistula-related symptoms was observed. Discussion

This case illustrates a severe manifestation of urosymphyseal fistula following multimodality prostate cancer therapy. Chrouser et al. demonstrated that urinary fistulas developing after radiation therapy are frequently preceded by urethral stricture, transurethral resection, or other endoscopic manipulation, supporting a multifactorial pathogenesis that includes radiation-induced tissue injury and subsequent mechanical disruption.1

The sequence of EBRT, salvage brachytherapy, and a subsequent transurethral bladder neck procedure has been documented in a limited number of prior reports of urosymphyseal fistula. Bugeja et al. described a 79-year-old man with this complication following EBRT, salvage LDR brachytherapy, and channel transurethral resection who ultimately required cystectomy with ileal conduit diversion,3 while Andrews et al. reported a series of patients with this fistula treated with urinary reconstruction and pubectomy, including some previously treated with EBRT, salvage brachytherapy, and lower urinary tract procedures.4 Navaratnam et al. reported an 80-year-old man with a similar treatment history who underwent robot-assisted cystectomy and holmium laser symphyseal debridement.5 Extrapelvic extension into the thigh has been described,2 but the 34-cm collection traversing the anterior adductor compartment exceeds prior reports.

Most reported cases of urosymphyseal fistula following radiation require cystectomy with urinary diversion, often with concurrent symphyseal debridement or pubectomy.3, 4, 5 In the systematic review by Patel et al., conservative management failed in 96% of irradiated patients, and 86% ultimately underwent cystectomy with diversion.6 However, our case suggests that a carefully monitored trial may be more reasonable in highly selected patients with localized, drainable collections, infection amenable to culture-guided antimicrobial therapy, no uncontrolled sepsis, and anatomy allowing complete urinary diversion. When source control and close multidisciplinary surveillance are feasible, conservative management may not be futile despite prior radiation.

Conclusion

Urosymphyseal fistula should be considered in patients with a history of prostate radiation who present with pelvic pain or lower extremity symptoms, particularly following urethral instrumentation. While surgical intervention remains standard therapy, conservative management may be effective in select high-risk patients, provided that complete urinary diversion and source control can be achieved, infection can be managed with culture-guided antimicrobial therapy, and close multidisciplinary clinical and radiographic surveillance is feasible.
