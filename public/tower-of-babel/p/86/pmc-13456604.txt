Messenger RNA From Canine Cytology Lymph Node Specimens Can Be Utilised for Gene Expression Profiling Using Digital RNA Counting
Authors: McKenzie Olsen, Emily Rout, Kelly Hughes, Janna Yoshimoto, Anne Avery, R Adam Harris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGene expression profiling (GEP) is being used with increasing frequency to characterise canine tumours, but canine samples are often not collected in a medium to preserve RNA. Cytology samples from human tumours have been shown to provide adequate RNA for GEP using NanoString nCounter technology, suggesting a similar approach might be viable for canine samples. Determine the quantity and quality of RNA extracted from canine cytology slides, evaluate the time frame for RNA integrity and assess the utility of performing GEP with extracted RNA. Cytological specimens were derived from reactive lymph nodes (n = 10) and lymphoma cases (n = 34) and characterised via flow cytometry or PCR for antigen receptor rearrangements. RNA was extracted from stained and unstained lymph node cytology slides using a RNeasy Mini Kit and subsequently evaluated for quality and quantity. GEP was carried out using nCounter technology with the NanoString Canine IO Panel. Extracted RNA from lymphoma and reactive cytology samples generated concentrations ranging from 27.5 to 543.9 ng/μL (median: 168.5 ng/μL; interquartile range (IQR): 108.15–275.95 ng/μL). RNA integrity numbers (RINs) ranged from 1.0 to 7.2 (median: 2.9; IQR: 2.10–4.45) and DV200 values ranged from 50.5% to 93.2% (median: 81.65%; IQR: 71.95%–87.85%). RIN and DV200 values were highest for extractions within 7 days of sample collection. GEP revealed overexpression of 17 genes in lymphoma samples such as MYC, TERT and BRCA2. Canine lymph node cytologic specimens provide sufficient RNA quality for GEP. The nCounter Canine IO Panel demonstrates the utility of cytology specimens for evaluating gene expression.Keywords: dog, gene expression, lymphoma, NanoString, nCounter, reactive

Introduction

Gene expression profiling (GEP) has emerged as a groundbreaking tool in cancer diagnosis. This technology allows researchers and clinicians to stratify tumour subtypes and evaluate the expression of prognostically significant molecular targets. For example, one of the first successful applications of GEP in humans was the PAM50 gene panel, which utilised limited gene panels to subtype breast cancers and provided prognostic information to clinicians [1]. The PAM50 gene panel is comprised of 50 genes that have been implicated in breast cancer biology and can be measured with microarray, mRNA‐sequencing, qRT‐PCR and most recently, NanoString nCounter technology [1]. Since FDA approval of NanoString's nCounter technology for molecular diagnostics, additional downstream applications using nCounter methodologies have been proposed for GEP of other tumour types, including lymphomas and leukaemias [1, 2, 3]. Altogether, these panels highlight that clinically prognostic information can be determined from GEP approaches with a limited panel of genes [1, 2, 3]. If clinically significant gene expression signatures are identified for subtyping tumours in veterinary patients, similar approaches could be applied in veterinary diagnostics to stratify patients with different prognoses.

The goals of this study were to (1) determine the quantity and quality of RNA that could be extracted from cytology slides, (2) evaluate the time frame for RNA integrity and (3) assess the feasibility of performing GEP via NanoString nCounter technology on extracted RNA from lymphoma and reactive samples. We hypothesised that RNA derived from cytologic specimens would be acceptable for the NanoString nCounter platform, which allows for lower quality RNA [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. We chose to use lymphoma samples for this proof‐of‐concept study because reactive and neoplastic nodal cytologic samples were readily available and could be clearly classified via existing methodologies (e.g., flow cytometry or histology). GEP could provide an additional level of classification in the future, as described with the lymphoma subtyping test for human diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma [2]. Furthermore, one group recently utilised the NanoString nCounter Canine Immuno‐Oncology (IO) panel to identify features predictive of therapeutic responses and outcomes in lymph node aspirates from dogs with diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma [8]. Our group has also previously shown the utility of using NanoString nCounter technology to distinguish acute leukaemia subtypes with a limited number of genes [9]. While these studies highlight the utility of GEP for distinguishing and/or identifying biomarkers to distinguish lymphomas/leukaemias in dogs, additional studies are warranted to assess its applications in the diagnostic workflow.

Before proceeding with a GEP‐based assay, a key course of action is to determine the quantity and quality of RNA that can be extracted from samples collected processed for cytology from veterinary patients. RNA has been successfully extracted from canine formalin fixed tissues, liquid biopsies, fine needle aspirates and/or blood samples [8, 9, 10]. In addition, cytology samples from human tumours have been shown to provide adequate RNA for GEP using NanoString nCounter technology [11]. Determining the adequacy of RNA extracted from veterinary cytologic specimens could allow for an additional source of biological material for future research. Once validated, this approach could enable more convenient and rapid GEP from routinely collected samples.

Methods/Materials

Case Selection of Cytologic Specimens

No samples were specifically solicited for this study, and institutional policies exempt studies using leftover clinical material from IRB approval. Databases from the Colorado State University's Clinical Hematopathology laboratory were queried for cases of lymphoma and reactive lymph nodes, respectively, from September 2019 to January 2024. Lymphoma cases with concurrent cytologic specimens were utilised. All cytologic specimens were stained with Modified Wright‐Giemsa (ELITechGroup) and initially reviewed by a board certified veterinary clinical pathologist at Colorado State University’s Veterinary Diagnostic Laboratory, with subsequent corroboration by a board‐certified veterinary pathologist (RAH) (Figure 1A).

Slides were selected based on the subjective assessment of sample quality by a board‐certified veterinary clinical pathologist (RAH). While no strict quantitative thresholds were applied, general inclusion criteria included samples with moderate to high cellularity (minimum of two slides), a predominance of intact lymphoid cells and minimal background blood or non‐lymphoid contamination. Specimens with extensive cellular rupture, excessive blood, or poor staining quality were excluded.

Cytologic specimens from 34 lymphoma samples were collected. Thirty‐one cases were immunophenotyped via flow cytometry, including one T‐cell lymphoma and 30 B‐cell lymphomas. Flow cytometry was not available for three cases, so the PCR for antigen receptor rearrangements (PARR) assay was performed, which revealed a clonal T cell receptor rearrangement (supporting T cell‐lineage) in 1 case and a clonal immunoglobulin rearrangement (supporting B cell‐lineage) in two cases. Cytologic specimens from 10 reactive lymph nodes with no clinical suspicion for haematopoietic neoplasia were also utilised. To assess RNA degradation over time, cytology slides were prepared from peripheral blood samples of two B‐cell chronic lymphocytic leukaemia cases. Case selection was indifferent to breed and sex. Signalment and physical exam data were obtained from submission forms or medical records, with descriptive statistics calculated. Cases with missing data were excluded from analysis.

Cell Line Validation Statement

No cell lines were used in the current study.

PCR for Antigen Receptor Rearrangements and Immunophenotyping by Flow Cytometry

All lymphoma cases were first evaluated and diagnosed by cytology, with ancillary testing (i.e., flow cytometry or PARR) performed to further characterise the disease. The [REDACTED] routine PARR assay was performed and interpreted as previously described [13, 14]. Flow cytometry‐based immunophenotyping was performed at the Clinical Hematopathology laboratory, according to the methods described by Seelig et al. [15]. First, red blood cells were lysed with ammonium–chloride–potassium (ACK) lysis buffer (0.15 M H4Cl, 1 M KHCO3, 0.1 mM Na2EDTA prepared in‐house). Cell pellets were then subsequently stained for surface antigens. Samples were acquired over 52 months, and the staining panel was changed during this time. Samples submitted prior to 13 November 2019 were stained with antibody Panel 1 and samples submitted after 13 November 2019 were stained with antibody Panel 2 (Table S1). These panels do not detect intracellular antigens. Samples were analysed on a three laser Coulter Gallios flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) and the resulting data were reviewed using Kaluza Analysis Software (Beckman Coulter).

T‐cell lymphoma and B cell lymphoma cases confirmed by PARR had a definitive diagnosis of lymphoma via cytology and a clonal T‐cell receptor or immunoglobulin gene rearrangement as detected by PARR, respectively. The T‐cell lymphoma case diagnosed by flow cytometry had > 90% CD5 + CD45− T‐cells and was consistent with T zone lymphoma [15]. The B cell lymphoma cases diagnosed by flow cytometry had either > 90% small sized CD21+ B cells (n = 5) or > 80% large sized CD21+ B cells (n = 25, Figure 1B) [16]. B‐cell chronic lymphocytic leukaemia cases were identified using previously established criteria [17]. Briefly, cases were selected if they: (a) > 5000 lymphocytes/μL on CBC, (b) > 60% of lymphocytes expressed CD21, (c) no cells expressed CD34 and (d) B‐cells were small‐sized, defined by B‐cell: neutrophil median forward scatter ≤ 0.60 [17].

Extraction of RNA From Cytologic Specimens

Two to three stained cytological slides from reactive (n = 10) and lymphoma (n = 34) cases were ‘rehydrated’ by applying 300 μL of RTL buffer (Qiagen) onto the slide surface and mixed by pipetting up and down until cellular material lifted from the slide. Cells were then transferred to a clean 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube containing 20 μL Proteinase K (Qiagen) by pipetting and razor blade scraping. Contents were then centrifuged and incubated at 56°C for a minimum of 25–30 min. RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's instructions, although the centrifugation step was performed at 12 000g for 30 s. On‐column DNA degradation was performed using 80 μL PureLink DNAse (ThermoFisher) and RNA was eluted in 30–50 μL of RNase free water, pending the subjectively determined cellularity of the slides. Extracted RNA was stored at −80°C until further use. An overview of our methodology for sample processing is provided (Figure 1C).

Extraction of RNA From Cell Pellets

To compare RNA quality and yield between stained cytologic slides (Modified Wright‐Giemsa) and cell pellets, we extracted RNA from both sample types in a subset of lymphoma cases (n = 6; S5, S15–S20). These paired specimens were collected at the time of aspiration, shipped together and processed concurrently upon arrival. Aspirates were collected in a no‐additive tube with saline and 10% patient serum, stored at 4°C and RNA was extracted within 72 h of sample collection. Red blood cells were lysed with ACK lysis buffer using the same protocol as used for preparing cells for flow cytometry. After red cell lysis, leukocytes were pelleted and total RNA was isolated from the leukocyte pellet using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) as described above. RNA concentration and integrity were assessed (described below).

Single Time Point Assessment of RNA

Extracted RNA samples from cytologic specimens (n = 44, all cases) and cell pellets (n = 6, S5, S15–S20) were analysed for concentration (ng/μL) and 260/280 ratios using a Nanodrop 2000c (ThermoFisher). Quality of RNA was determined by the RNA integrity number (RIN) and percentage of RNA fragments > 200 nucleotides (DV200) values using standard RNA ScreenTape (Agilent Technologies) analysis. For downstream analysis of RNA integrity metrics (RIN and DV200), cytologic specimens were stratified into four groups based on the time from original sample collection to RNA extraction: < 7 days, 7–14 days, 15–30 days and 31–365 days (Table S2).

Longitudinal Assessment of RNA

To create replicate samples and longitudinally assess RNA degradation over time, cytology slides were prepared from peripheral blood samples from two B‐CLL cases (S45 and S46). For this experiment, EDTA peripheral blood was lysed with ACK lysis using the same protocol as used for preparing cells for flow cytometry. Briefly, 500 μL of peripheral blood was utilised for these experiments. After red cell lysis, leukocytes were pelleted and resuspended in 250 μL of phosphate buffered saline. Twenty‐five microlitres of sample was aliquoted into the cytocentrifuge funnel and cells were pelleted onto slides. Five stained (Modified Wright‐Giemsa) and five unstained slides of each sample were made. RNA was extracted as described above from each pair of slides at initial processing, 1, 3, 5 and 7 days post‐cytology preparation for each sample. Slides were stored at room temperature during this time.

Performing GEP

Digital RNA counting was performed at the Colorado State University’s Veterinary Diagnostic Laboratory using a nCounter Digital Analyser (NanoString Technologies, Seattle, WA) with the nCounter Canine Immuno‐Oncology Panel (NanoString, Cat. No. XT‐CIO‐12). The panel consists of 800 genes and the following gene groups were analysed for expression: Cytokine and Chemokine Signalling (305 genes), Interferon Signalling (47 genes), Checkpoint Signalling (67 genes), Complement Cascade (33 genes), Immune Cell Abundance (52 genes), Tumour Immunogenicity (74 genes), Inhibitory Tumour Mechanisms (94 genes) and Stromal Factors (101 genes).

Probes were designed by NanoString for detection of mRNA using the known Canine lupus familiaris annotated gene sequences from the CanFam3.1 assembly. Sample processing and hybridisation were performed according to the nCounter XT CodeSet Gene Expression Assay protocol. Each hybridisation reaction included 3 μL Reporter CodeSet, 5 μL hybridisation buffer, up to 5 μL (100–200 ng) of sample RNA, and 2 μL of Capture ProbeSet for a 15 μL volume reaction. No amplification was required. Samples were then incubated at 65°C for 16 h on a thermal cycler. Samples were subsequently loaded onto the nCounter Pro Analysis System Prep Station, where purification and immobilisation onto the internal surface of the sample cartridge, coated with streptavidin, occurred. Approximately 3 h later, the sample cartridge was transferred to the Digital Analyser for imaging and analysis by counting fluorescent barcodes to determine RNA quantity of individual samples.

ROSALIND NanoString Gene Expression Methods

The following methods described were adapted from ROSALIND's website (https://rosalind.bio/). Data was analysed by ROSALIND, with a HyperScale architecture developed by ROSALIND Inc. (San Diego, CA). Read Distribution percentages, violin plots, identity heatmaps and sample multidimensional spacing (MDS)plots were generated as part of the QC step. Normalisation, fold changes and p‐values were calculated using criteria provided by NanoString. ROSALIND follows the nCounter Advanced Analysis protocol of dividing counts within a lane by the geometric mean of the normaliser probes from the same lane. Housekeeping probes to be used for normalisation are selected based on the geNorm algorithm as implemented in the NormqPCR R library [18]. Fold changes and p values are calculated using the fast method as described in the nCounter Advanced Analysis 2.0 User Manual. p‐Value adjustment is performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method of estimating false discovery rates (FDR). Clustering of genes for the final heatmap of differentially expressed genes was done using the PAM (Partitioning Around Medoids) method using the fpc R library that takes into consideration the direction and type of all signals on a pathway, the position, role and type of every gene, etc. Hypergeometric distribution was used to analyse the enrichment of pathways, gene ontology, domain structure and other ontologies. The topGO R library was used to determine local similarities and dependencies between GO terms in order to perform Elim pruning correction [19]. Several database sources were referenced for enrichment analysis, including Interpro, NCBI, MSigDB, REACTOME and WikiPathways [20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. Enrichment was calculated relative to a set of background genes relevant for the experiment.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed through the Rosalind platform for nCounter (NanoString Technologies) analysis as described above. Differentially expressed genes were defined as having an adjusted p ≤ 0.05 with fold change parameters set at 2 and −2. Apart from gene expression data, continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test; as most variables were non‐Gaussian, a Mann–Whitney test was used to evaluate these variables for differences between groups. For ordinal variables, a Fisher's exact test was conducted. Statistical analysis for these variables was performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software Inc).

Results

Cohort Demographics and Flow Cytometric Descriptive Statistics

For our initial experiments, RNA was extracted from a total of 10 canine reactive lymph node and 34 canine lymphoma cytologic specimens (Table 1). The median age of dogs with lymphoma was 10.4 years (range: 4.8–13.0 years) and of dogs with reactive lymph nodes was 8.3 years (range: 1.8–12.0 years). Fifty per cent of the dogs were male, two of which were not castrated. Twenty of the 22 females were spayed. The cohort consisted of the following breeds: 15 Mixed breed dogs, five Golden retrievers, four Labrador retrievers, two Border collies, two Doberman pinschers and one of each of the following: Basset hound, Bichon Frise, Border terrier, Brussels griffon, Chesapeake Bay retriever, Chihuahua, English springer spaniel, German shepherd, Great Dane, Great Pyrenees, Mastiff, Miniature pinscher, Pit bull terrier, Puggle, Viszla and Yorkshire terrier. Two B‐CLL cases were utilised for longitudinal assessment of RNA over time. One sample was from a castrated male Shih tzu and the other sample from a spayed female mixed breed dog. The dogs were 9 years 9 months and 10 years 9 months old, respectively, at the time of diagnosis.

RNA Extracted Within 7 Days From Cytology Slides Exhibits Highest Integrity and Quantity

Extracted RNA from cytology preparations of lymphoma (n = 34) and reactive samples (n = 10) generated concentrations ranging from 27.5 to 543.9 ng/μL (median: 168.5 ng/μL; interquartile range (IQR): 108.15–275.95 ng/μL) and RIN values ranging from 1.0 to 7.2 (median: 2.9; IQR: 2.10–4.45). The 260/280 ratios of the samples ranged from 1.99 to 2.12 (median: 2.08; IQR: 2.05–2.10) and DV200 values ranging from 50.5% to 93.2% (median: 81.65%; IQR: 71.95%–87.85%). No statistically significant differences were observed in the concentration (p = 0.21), RIN value (p = 0.84), or DV200 values (p = 0.57) between lymphoma and reactive lymph node samples (Table 1).

Samples were grouped according to time of RNA extraction post initial sample collection: < 7 days, 7–14 days, 15–30 days and 31–365 days (Figure 2A). Samples processed within 7 days showed the highest RIN and DV200 values (Table 2). RIN values, quality of RNA as previously determined, progressively decreased from a median of 4.1 within 7 days to 1.7 for samples processed after 31–365 days. A similar trend was observed for DV200 values, which were highest within 7 days (median 87.8%) and declined over time. Both one‐way ANOVA for RIN and Kruskal–Wallis test for DV200 values indicated significant effects of extraction timing, with pairwise comparisons confirming substantial differences between early and delayed extractions (p < 0.001 and p = 0.003, respectively) (Figure 2A). Individual data points associated with each case are provided in Table S2.

RNA Yield From Cytologic Specimens and Cell Pellets Is Comparable

RIN values for cytology slides ranged from 3.7 to 5.2 (median: 4.2, IQR: 3.9–4.4), while cell pellets had RIN values ranging from 1.6 to 8.8 (median: 7.7, IQR: 5.1–8.4). DV200 values for cytology slides were 87.3%–92.8% (median: 88.5%, IQR: 87.6%–91.7%), and for cell pellets, they ranged from 70.7% to 94.6% (median: 92.5%, IQR: 86.7%–93.2%). Although cell pellets had higher median RIN values, this was not statistically significant (p = 0.09). Furthermore, the median DV200 values were comparable between cytology specimens and cell pellets at 88.5% vs. 92.5%, respectively (p = 0.70) (Figure 2B).

The Quality of RNA Extracted From Cytology Slides Progressively Decreases Over 7 Days

Next, we performed a longitudinal study using the two B‐CLL cases (S45–S46) to assess the stability of RNA values between initial sample intake to 7 days. Initial RIN values obtained were 8.7 (S45) and 9.3 (S46), respectively. We observed that the RIN value progressively decreased over time for both stained and non‐stained conditions to 4 and 4.7, respectively, 7 days after sample collection and preparation (Figure 3A). The DV200 value was determined upon initial sample preparation at approximately 91% for both stained and unstained samples. With time, the DV200 values for non‐stained specimens (76%) remained higher than stained samples (40%) 7 days after sample preparation (Figure 3B). In summary, both RIN and DV200 values exhibited a downward trend over time for both stained and non‐stained cytologic specimens. Despite this decline, DV200 values theoretically remained sufficiently high for downstream use with Nanostring nCounter methods.

RNA Extracted From Cytology Slides Is of Sufficient Quality and Quantity for GEP

We next performed a proof‐of‐concept experiment in which we utilised RNA from stained cytology samples that was extracted less than 7 days post collection from five B cell lymphoma samples (Study ID S1–S5) and six reactive lymph node samples (Study ID S6–S11) for downstream NanoString nCounter applications with the Canine Immuno‐Oncology (IO) panel. Upon quality control inspection, one sample (Study ID S11) was noted to discreetly separate from the rest of the groups (Figure S1A). This sample (labelled as Reactive 6) yielded multiple quality control warnings and was therefore omitted from downstream analysis. The sample correlation heatmap generated by Rosalind revealed that the lymphoma cases clustered separately from the reactive lymph node samples (Figure S1B and Table S5). Furthermore, the remaining 10 samples (Study IDs S1–S10) had comparable gene counts, providing further evidence that they could be utilised for downstream GEP (Figure S1C). Notably, the counts generated from the reactive lymph node specimens (Study IDs S6–S10) were statistically (p < 0.0001) higher than the lymphoma samples (Study IDs S1–S5).

Using unsupervised hierarchical clustering, the 10 remaining samples (Study ID S1–S10) discreetly separated by disease state (i.e., reactive vs. lymphoma) (Figure 4A). Differential gene expression analysis revealed that 323 genes were differentially expressed between lymphoma and reactive lymph node cases using a fold‐change threshold of > 2 and < −2 with a p‐adj. value < 0.05 (Figure 4A,B). Raw and normalised gene expression data can be found in Tables S3 and S4, respectively. Seventeen genes were considered overexpressed in lymphoma samples including: CD1C, CD70, CMKLR1, CD1A6, TERT, TNFRSF13B, MYC, CCR2, LOC606890, RIPK2, LOC475935, CR1L, CCR6, LY86, CXCR5, BRCA2 and TYMS. A subset of these genes have been implicated in tumour growth promotion/survival (e.g., MYC and TERT) [25, 26]. Specifically, TERT which encodes the catalytic subunit of telomerase (telomerase reverse transcriptase), was found to be overexpressed in lymphoma samples almost fourfold compared to reactive samples (p‐adj. = 4.12e−3) [27]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the MYC protein is often overexpressed in canine B‐cell lymphomas, and our results corroborate this finding at the gene expression level (Figure 4C) [28]. Tumour suppressors such as FAS and TNFAIP3 were found to be under expressed in lymphoma samples [29, 30, 31]. Additionally, CD36, recently identified as a prognostic factor in canine B‐cell lymphomas, was also observed to be underexpressed (Figure 4C) [8].

Finally, we performed gene set analysis using the Rosalind platform and results are provided in Table S6. No gene sets were considered enriched in the lymphoma group (Study IDs S1–S5). However, 41 gene sets had a directed enrichment score < −2. The top three gene sets considered downregulated in the lymphoma group were the Immune Cell Adhesion and Migration, TNF Superfamily and T‐cell Functions pathways (Figure 4D). In the Immune Cell Adhesion and Migration pathway, no gene sets were considered statistically significant within the MSigDB Hallmark, BioCarta, or Protein Interaction database collections.

Discussion

This study sought to investigate the quality and quantity of RNA that can be extracted from cytology slides, and to assess whether cytologic specimens can serve as an efficient and non‐invasive source of material for GEP. Here, we show that RNA can be extracted from slides submitted for routine cytologic evaluation. We also demonstrate that this RNA can be utilised for downstream applications for GEP with NanoString nCounter technology. We found that RNA steadily degrades over time, but the DV200 remains acceptable for downstream applications up to 365 days. These findings suggest that extracting RNA within 7 days of processing will result in the highest RIN and DV200 values. Additionally, we showed that RNA extracted from paired cell pellets and cytologic specimens is comparable. Overall, our data indicated that GEP would be feasible given the RNA integrity and quantity values from our lymphoma and reactive samples, regardless of staining condition.

Our study demonstrated that RNA derived from cytologic specimens enables researchers to identify genes previously implicated in canine lymphoma via NanoString nCounter technology, which allows for lower quality RNA samples [32, 33]. According to Matsubara et al., the DV200 values are considered more useful than RIN values when deciding to move forward with gene expression analysis, particularly when samples have undergone fixation [34]. The DV200 represents the percentage of RNA fragments greater than 200 nucleotides in length and serves as a better predictor of success for hybridisation‐based assays like digital RNA counting with nCounter technology. In contrast, the RIN reflects overall RNA integrity based on ribosomal RNA ratio and is more relevant to enzymatic steps such as reverse transcription and amplification, both of which are utilised for standard short‐read bulk RNA‐sequencing library preparation. NanoString recommends RNA from (FFPE) samples have a DV200 value > 50%, regardless of RIN value [35, 36, 37]. This is because the nCounter platform uses direct digital detection of RNA through hybridisation, bypassing reverse transcription and amplification steps that are more sensitive to RNA degradation. This contrasts with traditional next‐generation sequencing (NGS) approaches, such as those used on Illumina platforms, which require higher RNA integrity to ensure successful fragmentation, cDNA synthesis and library preparation [37]. Therefore, when working with cytologic or otherwise suboptimal samples, DV200 should be prioritised over RIN as a quality control metric for NanoString assays.

Another utility of NanoString nCounter technology is that gene panels targeting ~50–800 genes can be designed for tumour subtypes or other disease conditions [1, 2, 9]. A recent study that compared a targeted Nanostring panel to RNA sequencing in the analysis of T zone lymphoma demonstrated that the methods give similar gene expression results [31]. Furthermore, this technology requires hands‐on time of 30 min and results can be acquired within 24 h after sample hybridisation has begun. This could allow for veterinary diagnostic laboratories to offer more in‐depth methods for profiling beyond traditional immunocytochemistry or immunophenotyping techniques, once gene targets have been investigated and prioritised. It is important to note that all lymphoma cases used for NanoString nCounter GEP in this study were B‐cell lymphomas, whereas the reactive lymph nodes comprise a variety of lymphoid cells, including T cells. This underlying difference in cellular composition may account for some of the differentially expressed genes observed, including those involved in T‐cell signalling pathways (e.g., CXCR5). While our primary objective was methodological, recognising the differences in cell composition between groups is important for contextualising gene expression results and guiding future studies.

As previously mentioned, our primary goal was to investigate whether RNA obtained from lymph node cytologic specimens is of sufficient quality for GEP. While we demonstrated that these samples can support GEP and identify upregulated genes in canine lymphoma, our analysis was limited to a relatively small number of specimens and to lymphoid tumour types. Therefore, it remains unclear whether these findings are generalisable to other tumour types or cytologic sample sites. Additionally, we utilised Nanostring's Canine I/O panel, a pre‐designed panel specifically developed to assess 800 tumour and immune‐associated genes in dogs. This panel is not specific to lymphoma, and additional studies are warranted to assess if there are specific biomarkers or gene panels that can distinguish neoplastic from reactive processes and if GEP can distinguish other subtypes of tumours. This approach could reveal biologically and prognostically relevant markers for future clinical applications.

Upon initial quality control analysis, one sample was flagged (Study ID S11) for binding density, positive control linearity, housekeeping gene normalisation and limit of detection factors. Notably, this specimen did not appear overtly different from the others based on initial RNA quality metrics (concentration = 167 ng/μL, RIN = 4.9, DV200 = 93.2). Binding density values outside the recommended range may reflect insufficient or excessive RNA input, degraded RNA, inhibitory contaminants or technical variation during assay setup. Because multiple quality control parameters were affected, we suspect a combination of pre‐analytic and technical factors contributed to these findings. Visualisation of this sample using multidimensional scaling revealed that this sample discretely separated from the others (Figure S1), and these issues led to the removal of this sample for further analysis. To mitigate this in future studies, stricter RNA input standardisation, careful sample handling and consideration of repeat testing for samples that fail recommended quality control thresholds are warranted. Further investigations using GEP in larger cohorts are warranted to assess the frequency of such quality control issues and their potential impact on the utility of GEP in clinical applications.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate that RNA can be extracted and utilised for GEP from cytologic specimens. This approach shows that, with validation, these approaches could be integrated into clinical diagnostics. This study also highlights that GEP identifies biomarkers that have been implicated in canine cancer. Further exploration of the genes identified is warranted and could lead to improved classification schemes of tumours.
